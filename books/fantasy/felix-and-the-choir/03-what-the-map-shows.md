## 3. What the Map Shows

The garret smelled of tallow and old smoke, the way it always did after dark, when the kitchen fires below burned down to embers and the chimney drew wrong. Tamsin bolted her door — a habit she'd kept for eleven years, though no one had ever once tried it — and set the stub of a candle on the sill where its light wouldn't show through the oiled window.

Then she unrolled the map.

It was smaller than she'd expected. She'd carried it against her ribs since the salt gate, folded into quarters inside her bodice, and she had half convinced herself on the walk home that it would turn out to be nothing. A laundry list. A love letter. A debtor's tally. Something that would let her fold it back up, put it in the stove, and go back to being a woman whose only remarkable quality was that poison didn't kill her.

It was not nothing.

The vellum was soft as cloth at the folds, handled so many times by so many hands that the edges had gone translucent. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood in places, but someone had re-inked the central lines within the last few years — she could tell because fresh iron-gall sat proud of the surface while the older work had sunk into the skin of the vellum like a bruise into flesh.

And there were no roads on it.

That was the first thing that stopped her breath. Every map she'd ever seen — and she'd seen dozens, memorized most, because a food-taster who traveled with the king's household learned geography the way other people learned gossip — every map was roads. Highways and byways, drovers' tracks and river fords, the cartographer's whole art being the honest confession of how people got from one place to another.

This map had none. Instead, running across Thornfell like veins under skin, were lines of notation.

She knew music the way any palace-raised person knew it: enough to recognize a mass from a motet, enough to know Merrin Vale's voice before he'd been disgraced, enough to hum. But this wasn't court notation. The staves ran at angles, climbing the drawn contours of the mountains themselves, and the notes weren't round. They were sharp, angular marks, some stacked two and three deep, some trailing tails like insect legs, and between them were symbols she had never seen — circles quartered like pies, spirals broken mid-turn, small hooked characters that looked almost like letters in a script she couldn't read.

She turned the vellum toward the candle. Faint watermarks ghosted up: beneath the ink, an older layer. She tilted it again, held it close, and saw that the angular notation had been written *over* something else — an even older script, worn nearly away, that looked less like writing than like the growth rings of a tree seen from above.

*The Choir remembers.*

The stranger's words came back to her with his blood still on them, warm through her glove. She looked down at her hands. She'd scrubbed them twice at the gatehouse pump and once more in her room, and they were clean, and they didn't feel clean.

Tamsin Thackeray had survived forty-one deliberate poisonings. She could recite each one: the arsenic in the ambassador's quail, the foxglove in the queen mother's cordial — that one had taken three days off her life and taught her the exact taste of her own heartbeat slowing — the hemlock-laced honey, the antimony in the wedding wine, the slow lead in the winter beer that no one had ever confessed to. She knew what fear tasted like on other people's tongues. She knew what lies tasted like. She had built a life on the principle that if she could just identify a thing precisely enough, name it exactly, she would be safe from it.

She could not name what she was looking at.

That frightened her more than the blood had.

* * *

She worked the way she'd been trained, because training was the only rope she had when the floor went out from under her. Start with what you know. Name what you can.

The mountains were Thornfell; she was sure of it. The cartographer had drawn the range with loving accuracy — the Three Sisters, the Widow's Tooth, the long saddle of the Grey Stair where the king's road crossed — and then, in the passes between them, where every honest map showed either the king's road or nothing at all, the notation began.

There were seven marked lines. Seven paths threading through country she knew intimately, country she had crossed eleven times with supply trains and once, memorably, alone in a whiteout with a dead mule and a sack of saffron. And not one of those lines followed a route that existed.

Except.

Her finger stopped on the third line, where it crossed the Grey Stair near the old tollhouse. The notation bent there, dipped below the contour line of the mountain itself, and continued — underground? Under the notation, faint as breath on glass, the older script repeated, and where the path dove beneath the stone, the old script formed a word-shape she couldn't read but could *feel*, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see.

She had stood on that spot. Two years ago, waiting out a rockfall on the king's road, she'd heard something through the mountain — a hum, low and layered, like voices singing in a church too far away to see. The soldiers with her had heard nothing. She'd told herself it was wind in the scree, or her own pulse, or the beginning of a headache. She had told herself a great many things over eleven years. It was practically her profession.

Now she counted the symbols along that third line and found, recurring at intervals, the same hooked character — and beside it, in newer ink, a tiny annotation in a human hand, cramped and hurried:

*rest here — it listens*

Someone had walked this route recently. Someone who understood the notation well enough to annotate it, and badly enough to need to.

She sat back on her stool. The candle guttered, and she trimmed the wick with a fingernail without thinking about it, the way she did everything without thinking about it, and understood suddenly that she was shaking.

Not from fear, exactly. Fear she knew; fear had a flavor, bitter and metallic, and she could hold it in her mouth all day if she had to. This was different. This was the feeling of standing in a familiar room and having the walls move.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on: the stranger had come to *her*. Out of everyone at the salt gate — the gatemen, the merchants, the guards, the crowd — a dying man had looked past all of them and pressed this into her hands. He'd known her face. Everyone in the palace knew the taster's face; it was part of the job, being visible, being a warning. But knowing her face wasn't the same as choosing her. Choosing her meant he'd known something else about her. Something worth dying to deliver to.

*The Choir remembers.* Remembers what? Remembers whom?

And underneath that question, quieter and worse: why had the spymaster's men arrived *just* after the stranger died, and not a moment before? Master Aldous's network was the finest in the nine kingdoms. Aldous himself had taught her that coincidence was a luxury for people who hadn't been trained. If the man had been carrying something worth killing him for, Aldous's people should have had him before he ever reached the gate.

Unless they'd wanted him to reach the gate. Unless they'd wanted to see who he gave it to.

She made herself stop there. Speculation without data was poison of a kind too, and she'd spent her life learning to spit out the first taste of anything doubtful.

* * *

What she should do — the careful thing, the sane thing, the thing the woman she had been yesterday would do without a second thought — was carry the map to Master Aldous at first light. Lay it on his desk. Say, *a man died at the salt gate and gave me this, I don't know what it is,* and watch his face, and let the machinery of the crown grind forward without her. That was her place. She was a tool, and tools don't choose what they're used on.

She got as far as wrapping the map back into its quarters.

Her hands stopped.

Why? she asked herself, and made herself answer honestly, because lying to yourself was the one poison no taster could detect. Why not take it to Aldous?

Because of the timing. Because of the way Aldous's runner had looked at her at the gate — not surprised to find her there, *placed*, like a piece on a board. Because the stranger had bled out whispering a word she'd only ever heard in nursery rhymes about the old days, the bad old days, the war nobody talked about except to say *thank the gods it's over.* Because in eleven years of tasting death for the crown, no one had ever once asked her whether she wanted to.

She wrapped the map differently instead — rolled tight around a splint of kindling, tied with thread, and slid it up inside the hollow leg of her bed frame, behind the wadding, where she kept the one thing she owned that mattered: her mother's ring, which fit no finger she had, because her mother had died when she was four and the crown had raised her after, and raised, in the crown's vocabulary, meant *used.*

She lay down on the bed without undressing and stared at the ceiling beams.

Sleep didn't come. What came instead was memory, unbidden: the kitchens at night, when she was small, before she'd been promoted from pot-girl to taster's apprentice. Old Marta singing over the bread troughs — low, wordless, a tune that bent in the middle in a way no court song bent. Tamsin, six years old, humming it back without thinking, and Marta going still, flour to the wrists, and saying: *Where did you hear that, child?* And Tamsin saying nowhere, everywhere, it was just *there*, and Marta never singing it again.

She had forgotten that entirely until tonight. The map pulled it up out of her like a splinter.

*It listens,* the annotation said. Rest here — it listens.

* * *

The knock came at the worst possible hour: past midnight, when no honest visitor knocks, and soft — two knuckles, twice, the knock of someone who doesn't want to wake the neighbors.

Tamsin was upright before the second tap, heart going hard. She blew out the candle first — instinct, darkness is cover — then thought better of it and relit it, because a dark room said *guilty* louder than anything. She tucked the bedframe's loose board flush with her heel as she passed it, smooth as breathing, and opened the door on the second round of knocking.

Inquisitor Veyne filled her doorway like a crow on a perch. She knew him slightly — everyone in the palace knew Corvane's inquisitors slightly, the way you know a dog that might bite. He was young for the office, neat, with the patient eyes of a man who enjoyed waiting. Behind him, at the stair's head, stood a palace guard she didn't recognize, which was itself an answer to a question she hadn't asked yet.

"Mistress Thackeray." He had a pleasant voice. They trained them pleasant. "Forgive the hour."

"Inquisitor." She kept her face at its working setting: attentive, mild, slightly tired, nothing to see. Eleven years of tasting had given her a face like a locked pantry. "Is there trouble?"

"There was a disturbance at the salt gate this evening." His eyes moved past her shoulder, unhurried, cataloguing. Garret: one bed, one stool, one chest, one candle. A poor room, correctly poor for her station. "A man died there. You were present."

"I was coming up from the market." True. "There was a crowd. A man had fallen." Also true. Careful now. "I didn't see what happened to him."

"You were seen kneeling beside him."

"I'm a taster, Inquisitor. When someone falls in the street, we kneel. It's habit." She let a little dryness into it, the exact amount a tired servant would permit herself with authority. "I checked whether he'd swallowed something. Old habit. He had."

Something flickered in Veyne's face — interest, quickly banked. "He had?"

"Bitter almonds," she said, and hated herself for how smoothly it came. "Cyanide, or a cousin of it. Whoever he was, he'd already dosed himself before he reached the gate. I've tasted enough of it to know the smell on a man's breath." All true. Every word true. That was the trick she'd learned watching liars at court: build your lie entirely out of truths, and stack them leaning the direction you want.

Veyne considered her. She let him. Her pulse was a drumline and her face was a wall, and both were excellent at their jobs.

"Did he say anything?"

"He was past words." The lie. First one. It left her mouth feeling like a coin she'd stolen — small, cold, already spent. "Some of them get a last swallow down and some don't. He didn't."

"And he gave you nothing? No token, no message?" A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Dying men sometimes press things into nearby hands."

"Nothing," Tamsin said. "His hands stayed fists. Whatever he was holding onto, he held it." That much was true too, and it steadied her — the best lies are load-bearing truths.

Veyne nodded slowly. Then, mildly, as if asking after her health: "You're the king's taster. Eleven years' service. You must have a remarkable constitution."

"I have a remarkable tongue, Inquisitor. The constitution is mostly luck."

"Mostly." He smiled with exactly half his mouth. "Duke Corvane values loyalty above luck. Remember that, Mistress Thackeray. These are times when ordinary people find extraordinary things in their keeping, and the wise ones bring such things to their betters promptly." He stepped back. "Good night. Sleep soundly."

She closed the door. She stood with her forehead against the wood and listened to his boots descend — slowly, at an inspector's pace, pausing once on the landing below, where her neighbor's door also waited.

They didn't believe her. Of course they didn't believe her; Veyne hadn't come believing, he'd come to *watch her lie*, to see how she did it, to file the shape of her face doing it. The visit wasn't an interrogation. It was a marking. She'd been weighed tonight, and found either innocent or useful, and she wouldn't know which until the trap closed.

And still — she turned and looked at the bedframe, at the innocent board hiding a dead man's map and a living woman's future — still she did not go get it and run after him.

She understood, standing there in the candle-smell, that she had just crossed a line she could never cross back. Not when she lied. Lies could be explained, forgiven, forgotten. The crossing happened now, in this quiet minute, when she chose to keep the secret *knowing* they suspected her. Everything before this had been instinct. This was intent.

Panic had chosen for her, maybe. But panic was hers. Panic was the first thing in eleven years that had been entirely, privately hers.

Tamsin Thackeray snuffed the candle, lay down dressed, and did not sleep. Above the rooftops, over the black shoulder of Thornfell, the moon was three nights from full, though she didn't know yet why that mattered, and wouldn't for weeks.

At dawn, she would draw her wages, pack her one bag, and be gone before the kitchens lit their fires — down the stair, out through the tannery lane, onto the smuggler's road toward Marrowgate, following the third line west.

But that was dawn. For now, in the dark, she lay listening — really listening, for the first time in her careful life — to the mountain.

Somewhere far beneath the city, so faint she could tell herself it was her own blood in her own ears, something was humming.
