## 4. Down the Smuggler's Road

She was gone before the bells.

Tamsin had learned long ago that a city tells you when it's safe to move, and the hour before dawn — after the watch changed, before the bakers rose, while the night-soil carts still rattled through the tannery lane — was the one hour Marrowgate's sister city of Carrowmere belonged to no one. She moved through it like smoke: one bag, one knife, the map folded against her ribs where her heartbeat could keep it warm.

The kitchen gate guard didn't even look up. Why would he? Tamsin Thackeray had left the palace at odd hours a hundred times on the king's errands — fetching rare salts, meeting caravans at the salt gate, tasting at merchants' feasts where the crown needed an unpoisoned opinion. Her face was a key that opened doors without questions. That had always been the point of her.

She wondered, walking out, if it would keep being the point. Or if it had already turned into something else.

* * *

The smuggler's road began where the pilgrim road ended, which was to say nowhere in particular — a goat track behind a shrine to some forgotten shepherd saint, dropping off the shoulder of Thornfell like water off a roof. No map in any royal cartographer's office showed it. It existed only in the memory of people who moved things that shouldn't be seen moving: brandy, silk, fugitives, and once, years ago, a coffin full of stones and a very alive woman inside it.

Her father had carried her over this ground on his shoulders when she was six. He'd named every stone.

"That's the Widow's Tooth," he'd said, tapping a leaning slab of granite. "Lean on it wrong and it'll take your ankle as toll. And there — see how the pines bend east? Wind comes down the gully every evening same as a bell rings. You learn what the mountain says regular, and then you'll know when it's saying something new."

She'd thought he meant weather. She'd spent thirty years thinking he'd only ever meant weather.

Now she climbed with the map's third line burning in her mind — *west by the singing notch, two days' hard walk* — and found herself listening the way she listened to a suspicious broth. Not looking. Listening. The mountain said its regular things: wind in the gullies, water under scree, a fox complaining somewhere above the tree line. And beneath all of it, so faint she could still pretend it wasn't there, that hum. Like a note held under a door.

She ate cold sausage and hard bread at noon without stopping, because stopping was for people who weren't hunted yet, and she intended to stay merely *fleeing* for as long as possible.

It lasted until dusk of the first day.

* * *

She saw them from the Widow's Tooth: four riders on the pilgrim road below, moving fast, and moving wrong. Pilgrims spread out. Merchants traveled heavy and slow. These four rode in a line, tight as beads on a string, and they stopped at the shrine — the shrine, the nothing-shrine behind which the smuggler's track dropped away — and one of them dismounted and walked around it twice.

Looking for the entrance.

Tamsin went still against the stone, breath shallow, doing the arithmetic of distance and light. They hadn't seen her. But they knew where the road began, and that meant —

That meant someone had told them.

Not just that she'd fled. Anyone could guess a food-taster might run after a stranger died at her gate whispering nonsense. But the *road*. The shrine. The goat track that existed in maybe forty living heads, most of them smugglers who'd sooner cut their own tongues out than sell a route. Someone had told them exactly where she'd gone, and there were perhaps five people inside the palace walls who knew Tamsin Thackeray well enough to guess her first steps, and every single one of them had taught her, fed her, or paid her wages.

Master Aldous had taught her to read a pursuer's line of travel. *They ride to where they think you'll be, not where you are,* he'd said, a lifetime ago, over a game of stones in the spymaster's office. *So be where they can't imagine.*

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she did what he'd taught her: she stopped being where they imagined. She left the high track and dropped into the gully — the bad one, the one her father had forbidden, the one with the waterfall and the wet climb and the way that came out a half-day west with no backtrack visible. Let them find the shrine and read the mountain all night. By morning she'd be a rumor.

The climb was misery. The waterfall soaked her to the bone and the rock took a strip of skin off her palm as toll, and she paid it without complaint, because pain was just information and the information was good: she could still feel things, therefore she was still alive, therefore the plan held.

But lying that night in a hollow under an overhang, wringing out her stockings by feel, she let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the shrine.

Someone sold my name.

And underneath it, quieter, worse: *I don't even know which voice I'm hoping it isn't.*

* * *

The second day the mountain got serious.

Thornfell in autumn was a country of contradictions — gold larches blazing on the lower slopes, and above them grey stone and the first dirty snow, and between them the smuggler's paths threading like stitches through a wound. Tamsin knew three ways across the range. The pilgrim passes, watched now, certainly. The mining roads, watched always. And the old ways — the ones her father had learned from his father, back when the family had been something other than palace servants, back when Thackerays had been *mountain people* before they'd been anything else.

She took the old ways.

There was a place at midday where the path crossed a shelf of bare stone, and her father's voice in her memory said, *Sing here.* Not sing anything. Just make noise, loud, and listen. Because the shelf was hollow underneath, and if the hollow answered differently than it used to — if rockfall had opened a way down, or closed one — you wanted to know before you trusted the path.

Tamsin stood on the shelf and, feeling like a fool, sang a bar of nothing. A kitchen song. Something about a girl and a goose.

The mountain hummed back.

Not echo. Echo comes back shaped like what you sent. This came back *shaped like itself* — a low, patient tone, two notes where she'd sent one, hanging in the stone under her boots like a hand resting palm-up. Waiting.

She stood there a long time. Long enough for the wind to change. Long enough to be afraid in a completely new direction.

Then she walked on, faster, and told herself the story she'd been telling herself since the salt gate: *strange man, dying words, fever-dreams, maps drawn by madmen.* The story was getting thinner every day. She could see through it in places now, like cloth worn to gauze.

* * *

She nearly walked into the checkpoint at dusk.

It shouldn't have been there. That was the whole problem with it. The old way came down through a fold in the hills called the Shepherd's Pocket, and the last time Tamsin had come this way — seven years ago, running a quiet errand for Aldous, though she hadn't known then what kind of errands she was really running — there had been nothing but a shepherd's hut and a very rude dog.

Now there was a hut, a dog, and six men in grey-and-black livery with a brazier, a rope across the track, and a banner she recognized from proclamations: the black tower of Duke Corvane.

Corvane's men. In the Shepherd's Pocket. On a road that officially did not exist.

She lay in the larch shadow and counted, the way she'd count courses at a suspect feast. Six visible. Probably a seventh minding horses. Brazier meant they planned to hold the position through the night, which meant orders, which meant someone specific was expected through this fold — not random travelers, because random travelers don't get a fixed post in the middle of nowhere. *They ride to where they think you'll be.*

They weren't hunting a fugitive. They were waiting for *her*, by name, at a crossing only her father's dead generation had known.

The exhilaration she'd been carrying for two days — the wild, shameful lightness of finally moving, finally choosing, the open sky and the burning legs and the sense that for the first time in her careful life she was going somewhere instead of standing somewhere — curdled right there in the larch shadow, cold and quick as milk left out.

Because you couldn't wait for someone by name unless you had their name. And you couldn't have her name and her road unless the name and the road had come from the same mouth.

She backed out of the fold without a sound, the way Aldous had drilled into her until it was reflex — heel first, weight never committed, the body always ready to be wrong — and she sat down against a pine trunk in the dark and made herself think it through like a dish.

What do I actually know?

One: the dying man had chosen her. Out of everyone at that gate, he'd pressed the map into *her* hands. Either chance, or he'd known what she was. Both possibilities were bad in interesting ways.

Two: Corvane's inquisitors had reached the salt gate within minutes of the death — too fast for rumor, right for a summons. Someone had summoned them *before* the man died.

Three: the palace kitchens had served a dish that should have killed her, and the spymaster had looked at her survival the way a man looks at a lock that opens too easily.

Four: someone inside those walls had sold her name along with her road, and the list of someones was short, and every name on it hurt to say.

She sat with that fourth item the way she'd once sat with a bowl of soup that smelled almost right — almost, but with a thread of bitterness underneath, the kind that meant bitter almond or worse. You couldn't unsmell it. You couldn't unknow the poison was there. All you could decide was whether to swallow anyway.

She decided no.

* * *

She went around.

It cost her half a night and a scramble along a streambed that tried to claim her boot and nearly succeeded, but she came down out of the hills on the far side of the checkpoint as the sky went the color of pewter, and there, spread below her in the valley mist, was Marrowgate.

The free city. Last of the three that still flew their own banners. A huddle of slate roofs and smoking chimneys around a deep river gorge, walled on the landward side and cliff-hung on the river side, with the great Salt Bridge spanning the gorge on arches older than any kingdom Tamsin had sworn loyalty to. Even from here she could hear it waking — hammers, gulls, the river's constant mutter, a city that had never quite agreed to belong to anyone.

Her father had taken her once, when she was small. Bought her honey-bread on the bridge and told her: *Free means nobody's word is law here but the ones you choose yourself. Remember that, Tam. Whatever else happens.*

She'd remembered. She just hadn't understood it until now, standing exiled on a hillside with blistered feet and a stolen future, watching a city she'd visited perhaps ten times in her life feel more like home than the palace she'd given thirty years to.

That was the thing about exile, she was learning. Nobody banishes you. You just wake up one morning and realize every door behind you has quietly become a wall.

The map's first landmark was in Marrowgate. The third line ended at a mark she'd studied until she could draw it blind: a small circle, and beside it notation she couldn't read — a cluster of strange symbols, stacked like chords — and beneath them, in the same cramped hand as everything else, a single word:

*Tavern.*

Helpful. Wonderful. There were perhaps two hundred taverns in Marrowgate, and she had a circle and some musical gibberish, and somewhere behind her an organized force with her name on a list was tightening the net.

But the mark sat near the river on the map, down in the gorge district — the dockside warrens where the salt barges moored and the tide of humanity ran thickest and a face like hers, tired and hooded and paying cash, would be one among thousands.

She could do thousands. Thousands was comfortable. Thousands was anonymous.

She started down toward the city as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and the light came up the valley and caught the Salt Bridge and set it briefly ablaze, and for one stupid moment — exhausted, hungry, palm scraped, life burned down to one bag and one knife — Tamsin Thackeray felt *lucky*. Felt like the woman on the hill in the ballads, walking toward her own story with the wind at her back.

The feeling lasted until the gate.

* * *

Marrowgate's landward gate was called the Throat, and it earned the name at market hours, when the road funneled a river of carts and beasts and shouting humanity through a stone gap wide enough for four abreast. Tamsin joined the flow an hour past dawn, hood up, head down, one more tired traveler among hundreds.

The queue shuffled forward. She was twenty yards from the arch when she saw the table.

It stood just inside the gate, in the shadow of the arch itself, and behind it sat two figures in grey-and-black, with a ledger open between them and a third man standing at their shoulder wearing the flat, patient expression of a professional. As each traveler passed, some were waved through. Some were stopped. Questions. Names written down.

Checking names. At the gate of a *free city*, which should have bristled at the insult of it — except the gate wardens stood aside and let it happen, and Tamsin remembered the talk in the taverns, the whispers she'd filed away as tavern-talk: Corvane's coin buying wardens, Corvane's men collecting debts, the free cities free mostly in the way a drowning man is free to decline the rope.

She calculated turning back. The queue behind her was thick; the ditch beside the road was thin; but a lone traveler leaving a queue that led to a name-check was a traveler announcing herself.

So she did the thing she was built for. She became unremarkable. Shoulders down, face slack, the walk of a woman with sore feet and no opinions. She drifted with the current toward the arch, past the table, eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing interesting lived.

"—name?" The flat-voiced man had stepped into her path. Not grabbed. Just *there*, the way a wall is there.

"Anwen," she said. "Cooper's widow. Up from the valley villages to sell my husband's stock." It came out smooth. Thirty years of practice, lying with her face if not her tongue; today the tongue finally got its turn.

"Cooper," he repeated, writing. "Which village?"

"Brambleholt." Small enough to check slowly, far enough to check never.

He looked up at her then, and she watched his eyes do their work — cataloguing hands, boots, accent, the quality of her bag — and she gave him nothing, because there was nothing to give. Poverty was the best disguise in the world. Nobody searches a poor woman; poverty explains everything.

He waved her through.

She walked under the arch, into the gorge-shadow, into the roar and stink and glory of Marrowgate, and she let herself breathe again only when the Throat was fifty paces behind her and the crowd had swallowed her whole.

Then she stopped at a corner, bought a meat pie with a trembling hand she disguised as eagerness, and stood eating it while she consulted the map in her mind. Down. Everything on the map pointed down — toward the river, toward the docks, toward the circle and the chord-symbols and the word *tavern*.

She was three streets into the descent, following the slope of the lanes toward the smell of fish and tar, when she heard it.

Faint. Underneath the city's noise — the hammers, the gulls, the hundred human sounds of a working morning. Underneath, the way a second voice sits underneath a melody.

Humming.

Coming up out of the ground. Out of the gorge itself, out of the ancient stone the Salt Bridge stood on, rising through the soles of her boots like warmth through a floor.

And this close, for the first time, it wasn't just a hum.

It was a tune. Half-remembered, half-heard, patient as stone — and threaded through it, unmistakable, impossible, the exact shape of the chord-symbols stacked beside the little circle on a dead man's map.

Tamsin stood in the crowded lane with a half-eaten pie going cold in her hand, and every instinct Aldous had ever trained into her screamed that a landmark that announced itself was a landmark that could betray her.

She went toward it anyway.

Down, past the fish-stalls, past the ropewalk, to a crooked lane at the bottom of the gorge where the river thundered under the street and a tavern leaned against the bridge's oldest pier like a drunk leaning on a friend. Its sign creaked overhead: a painted bird, faded to a ghost of blue.

*The Wren.*

Above the door, scratched into the stone lintel in a hand gone silver with age, someone had carved a line of those same stacked symbols — chords, notation, whatever it was — worn nearly smooth by centuries of shoulders brushing past without seeing.

Tamsin stood in front of the door and listened.

Inside, faintly, a man was singing.
