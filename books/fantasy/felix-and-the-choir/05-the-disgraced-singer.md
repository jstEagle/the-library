## 5. The Disgraced Singer

The Salt Larder smelled of tar, spilled ale, and the particular despair of men who had given up on better rooms. Merrin Vale had learned to love it, or at least to stop resenting it, which was nearly the same thing.

He stood on a crate behind the long table because the tavern had no stage, and he sang for copper and the occasional silver, and he sang small. That was the trick of it now — sing small. A voice like his drew eyes, and eyes drew questions, and questions in Marrowgate these days came with grey cloaks attached.

So he flattened his tone, roughened his edges, sang dock songs about drowned sailors and unfaithful wives, and let the tavern's noise swallow the thing underneath: a trained breath that could hold a note across four measures without wavering; an ear that caught every flat pitch in the room like a splinter under the skin; a range he kept folded up inside him like a letter he no longer dared open.

Three years ago he had sung before the court at Vael Sarn, and duchesses had wept into their wine, and the old king himself — dying, half-blind, cruel in his dotage but not yet usurped — had said, *that boy's throat is worth a regiment.* Three years ago he had been the finest voice in six kingdoms, and everyone told him so until he believed it, which was the beginning of his ruin.

Because then Corvane had come.

Merrin finished the set with something bawdy and forgettable, collected eleven coppers and a button from the pass-around hat, and retreated to his corner by the rain-streaked window with a cup of small beer he didn't want. The Salt Larder's regulars went back to their arguments. Outside, the harbor fog pressed against the glass like something trying to get in.

He thought about the night of the ascension, as he did most nights, in the way a man worries a loose tooth.

They had summoned him to the great hall three days after the old king's funeral — too soon, everyone knew it was too soon, the body barely cold and already the banners were being changed — and Master Aldous himself, the king's spymaster, soft-spoken and terrible, had stood in the doorway while Corvane's herald explained the program. Coronation anthem. New words. Words praising the Duke's *swift assumption of necessary authority*.

"New words," Merrin had repeated.

"The old words celebrate the old line," the herald said. "The old line is concluded."

And Merrin — twenty-six years old, arrogant, certain that his voice belonged to beauty and not to politics — had looked at Master Aldous, expecting the spymaster to intervene, because Aldous had always been kind to him, had once said *you are the truest thing in this palace, boy*, and instead Aldous had simply watched him with those patient grey eyes and said nothing at all.

"I won't sing it," Merrin said.

The silence afterward had lasted perhaps two seconds. It felt like years.

He remembered the exact quality of it — the way sound died in that hall, the way the stone seemed to lean in. He had spent his life inside acoustics the way fish spend their lives inside water, and he had never heard a silence with teeth before. He heard one that day.

They hadn't killed him. That would have made a martyr of a popular man, and Corvane was careful. Instead they had ruined him quietly: a charge of sedition whispered rather than shouted, a trial conducted in a room with no windows, a sentence commuted to exile on condition of silence. *You will not perform in any hall of the crown,* they told him, *and you will not speak of why.* His name was struck from the conservatory rolls. His students were reassigned. His patroness burned his letters where he could watch.

He had come to Marrowgate because it was the farthest free city from Vael Sarn and the closest to forgetting, and he had discovered within a month that you cannot forbid a man to breathe, and singing was breathing, had always been breathing, for him. So he sang in dock taverns, small and roughened, and took coin from fishermen, and told himself this was freedom.

Some nights he almost believed it.

Tonight was not one of those nights. Tonight the fog had come in thick off the water, and the tavern door opened on a gust of cold salt air, and a woman walked in wearing a travel-stained riding coat and the expression of someone who had been looking over her shoulder for days and had decided, finally, to stop pretending she wasn't.

She scanned the room the way he'd seen soldiers scan rooms — corners first, exits second, faces third — and then she did something that snagged his attention like a hook: she looked at *him*. Not the way patrons looked at entertainment. She looked at him the way you look at a locked door when you're being chased.

Then she ordered ale, sat with her back to the wall, and pulled up her hood, and Merrin decided she was none of his business.

He was wrong, of course. He was wrong the way a man is wrong about weather coming in off the sea — visibly, unavoidably, and with plenty of warning he chose to ignore.

* * *

She approached him at the end of the second set, when the tavern had thinned to drunks and diehards.

"You're Vale," she said. "Merrin Vale."

"That depends who's asking." He kept his voice low and his hands visible. In Marrowgate, being recognized as Merrin Vale was either an invitation or a death sentence, depending entirely on the asker.

"Tamsin." No family name offered. Her eyes flicked to the window, the door, back. "I need to show you something. Not here."

"Nothing about you says *not here* works," Merrin said. "You've got the look of a woman with grey cloaks behind her, and I have a strict policy about grey cloaks, which is that I am nowhere near them."

"Five minutes." She set a silver piece on the table — real silver, worn smooth, the kind of coin that had passed through many desperate hands. "There's a notation on a map. I'm told you read music better than anyone alive."

That landed harder than she could know. He laughed, short and bitter. "Whoever told you that is three years out of date."

"They said you were the finest voice the court ever produced."

"The court produces nothing anymore but decrees." He should send her away. Every instinct honed by three years of careful smallness said *send her away, finish your beer, climb to your rented room above the chandler's, be nobody until morning.* But there was something in her voice under the words — a strain, a held tension, like a singer sustaining a note past comfort — and Merrin Vale had spent his whole life listening beneath the surface of things.

"What kind of notation?" he asked.

Her jaw tightened. "Not the kind I understand. Lines and marks, but not roads. Someone told me—" she stopped herself, recalibrated. "Someone told me you were the only person in Marrowgate who might read it."

"Someone is risking my life with their confidence." He looked at the silver. At her face — sharp-boned, tired, a scar through one eyebrow old enough to have gone white. Hands that were steady in the way of hands that had learned steadiness deliberately, through practice, through discipline, the way his own voice had learned its smallness. He knew damage when he saw it. He saw it in mirrors daily.

"Back room," he said at last, hating himself. "Five minutes. Then you leave me out of whatever this is."

* * *

The back room of the Salt Larder was a storage closet with pretensions: casks, a broken chair, one candle stub on a barrel head. Tamsin closed the door, listened at it for a moment — actually listened, ear tilted, the way he might check a tuning — and then unwrapped the oilcloth bundle she'd carried against her ribs.

The map came out in a breath of smell that stopped Merrin cold. Old vellum, yes, and road dust, but under it something else — something green and cold, like the air off a cave mouth in high summer. Like the memory of a place he'd never been.

He'd expected smuggler's charts. He got something else entirely.

The map showed the Thornfell mountains, roughly, in faded ink — but threaded through and around the honest passes were other lines. Lines that didn't follow any terrain he knew. And along them, dense and deliberate, ran markings: staff-lines drawn freehand, curved like contour lines on a surveyor's map, crowded with notes. Not decoration. Composition. Whole movements of it, winding through the mountains like rivers.

"This isn't a map," he said. His voice came out strange. "Or it's not only a map."

"It's supposed to lead somewhere. The Choir." She said the word carefully, like handling something hot. "A stranger gave it to me. He was dying. He said—" her throat worked. "He said *the Choir remembers*."

Merrin wasn't listening anymore. He was reading.

His finger traced the first staff-line, and the notes rose under his touch, and something happened in his chest that he had no decent name for. He had studied every tradition the conservatory possessed — court anthems, harvest rounds, the plainsong of the southern monasteries, even the quarter-tone modes the eastern traders brought. This was none of them and all of them. The intervals were wrong in a way that was *right*, tuned to something between the notes, microtones his teachers had dismissed as impossible to sing. The phrasing didn't resolve the way human music resolved. It circled. It *called*.

And underneath all of it, faint as a watermark, he could see it — the structure. He'd spent ten years analyzing music until analysis was reflex, and the architecture here was unmistakable: three voices. Always three. A ground line steady as bedrock. A high line that moved like wind through leaves, ornamented past anything human lungs could manage. And between them, a middle voice — the bridge, the carrying voice, the one that made the other two into harmony instead of noise.

He knew the shape of it the way you know your own heartbeat.

"Covenant-song," he whispered.

Tamsin went very still. "You know what it is."

"My grandmother sang fragments of it." The words came from somewhere older than his training. "Before she died. She was from a village up in the Thornfell foothills, and she used to say the mountain used to *sing back*, and we all thought it was an old woman's fancy. She'd hum these little phrases that never resolved, and when I asked her what song it was, she said it wasn't hers. She said it was *everyone's*, once." He looked up. "This is that. This is exactly that, written down, whole."

"What does it do?"

"It's not a *what*, it's a—" He stopped. Started again. "There are stories. Old ones, the kind the conservatory called peasant nonsense. That the mountain courts and the human realms made a pact, ages ago, and the pact wasn't signed, it was *sung* — both sides together, three voices, and as long as the song continued, neither realm could harm the other. Protection. Mutual. Binding." He tapped the map. "And then a generation ago it stopped. Everyone knows it stopped — the border raids started, the disappearances, the reason there are soldiers on every Thornfell road now. Nobody ever knew why it broke."

"And this map—"

"Is either the song itself," Merrin said slowly, "or the road to wherever the song has to be sung." He turned a page of the vellum and his breath caught. There, at the heart of the mountains, marked not with an X but with a chord — a single sustained chord, three notes stacked — was a symbol like a well, drawn in ink that shimmered faintly when the candle flame guttered.

"The Deepwell," he murmured. "Grandmother said that word too. Said the song lived in the deep of the mountain, in a well that wasn't for water."

Tamsin sat down on the broken chair, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings. Up close, in candlelight, he could see how exhausted she was — the bruised skin under her eyes, the tremor she was holding out of her hands through sheer will. A woman running on discipline alone. He recognized that engine. He'd been running on it for three years.

"I stole bread from a lord's kitchen when I was seven," she said, to the candle, seemingly to no one. "It was the first time I'd ever taken anything. My hands shook for an hour after. Do you know what I've figured out since? It wasn't guilt. I wasn't sorry. It was fear — good, clean fear, the kind that keeps you alive." She looked up at him. "I haven't felt it properly in years. Until this week. And now everything frightens me and I keep going anyway, and I don't know if that's courage or if I've just run out of choices."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you looked at that map the way I look at food I've been told is safe." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "Like something you'd stopped believing existed."

Merrin sat down across from her, the map between them like a campfire.

"I'll tell you something I've never told anyone," he said, "since we're trading wounds. Three years ago I refused to sing at Corvane's ascension. One sentence. *I won't sing it.* Eight seconds of courage, and it cost me everything — my name, my students, my right to stand in any hall in the crown's reach. And do you know the worst part?" He laughed, and it hurt. "Some nights I'm not sure I did it because it was right. Some nights I think I did it because I was arrogant, because I couldn't bear to lend my voice to something ugly, because my *art* —" he made the quotation marks with tired fingers. "And some nights I think that distinction doesn't matter. Right thing, wrong reason. Wrong thing, right reason. Out here in the world it all comes out the same: you're in a storage closet with a stranger and a forbidden map, and the grey cloaks are coming either way."

"Are they?" Tamsin asked quietly. "Coming?"

"For you? Almost certainly. For me?" He shrugged. "I've been dead to them for three years. But I know what happens to people who help fugitives in Marrowgate lately. There was a chandler on Harrow Street. Helped a fae-blooded family hide in his cellar. They found him floating by the breakwater with his tongue—" He stopped. "They make examples. Corvane's inquisitors are thorough."

"So you won't help me."

He looked at the map. At the three-voiced chord at the heart of the mountains. At the middle line, the bridge, the carrying voice — the part a singer would take, the part that had been missing from his life for three years like a removed organ, some essential function he'd learned to live without and had never once stopped grieving.

He thought about singing small for the rest of his life. About dying in this tavern town with eleven coppers and a button to his name, having used the finest voice in six kingdoms to sing about drowned sailors.

"No," Merrin said. "Apparently I will."

Tamsin blinked. "Just like that?"

"Not just like that. I've thought about it for the entire conversation, and I'm ashamed to say the deciding factor is pure vanity." He rolled up the map with hands that were, he noticed, not shaking at all — steadier than they'd been in years. "That middle voice on this map. The bridge line. I can hear it, Tamsin. I can hear how it goes. And I have not been able to *hear* anything in three years — really hear — and I would walk into Corvane's own dungeon to chase that sound." He met her eyes. "Besides. You looked at me tonight like I was a door worth knocking on. Nobody's looked at me like that since the palace. I find I missed it terribly."

Something loosened in her face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would go, cleared of rubble.

"There's a boat," she said. "Downriver, tomorrow, before dawn. A friend of a friend. After that I don't know — the map wants us in the mountains, and the mountains want blood lately."

"Then we'd best be interesting enough to survive," Merrin said, and blew out the candle, and in the dark of the storage closet, very softly, so soft that only she could hear it — so soft that even he could pretend it wasn't happening — he hummed the first phrase of the covenant-song, the opening interval, the call.

And from somewhere impossibly far away — below the floorboards, below the harbor mud, below the bedrock of the world — something hummed back.
