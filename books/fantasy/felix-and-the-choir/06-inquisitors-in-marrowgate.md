## 6. Inquisitors in Marrowgate

Marrowgate smelled of fish, tar, and old lies, and Tamsin had been in it less than a day before she learned to hate all three.

The city clung to the river like a barnacle to a hull — crooked, stubborn, and impossible to scrape off. She and Merrin had come in with the morning tide, two travelers among hundreds, and by noon she'd found the landmark the map promised: a chandler's shop on Rope Walk with a carved lintel of three interlocking circles. The map showed a notation beneath it. Merrin had hummed it once, quietly, and the chandler — a stooped woman named Odile with ink-stained fingers — had gone white as tallow.

"You shouldn't have sung that here," Odile had said. "Not in Marrowgate. Not now."

"Why not now?" Tamsin asked.

"Because the Duke's men arrived on yesterday's tide." Odile had turned back to her candles, voice dropping. "Six black cloaks at the customs house. They're calling it a census. Nobody believes that for a moment."

That had been noon. Now it was dusk, and the bells of Marrowgate were ringing wrong.

Tamsin heard it from the window of the room they'd taken above the Drowned Rat, a tavern down in the stink of the harbor district where nobody looked at anybody twice. Three bells, then silence where the fourth should be. Then three again. She'd grown up in a palace full of signals disguised as courtesies; she knew what a pattern meant.

"That's not a festival," she said.

Merrin was sprawled on the narrow bed, boots off, tuning a battered lute he'd bought from a pawnbroker who hadn't met his eyes. He'd paid too much for it and pretended not to care. At the sound of the bells his hands went still.

"No," he agreed. "It's a sweep."

"A what?"

"When I still sang for the court," he said, sitting up, and there was something in his voice Tamsin couldn't name — not bitterness exactly, more like an old scar being pressed, "Corvane ran them through the capital every season. Soldiers seal a district, inquisitors go door to door with a ledger of names. Anyone who can't account for themselves gets accounted for." He swung his legs down. "They'll start at the gates at dawn and work inward. By tomorrow night this whole quarter will be standing in line like sheep."

"Then we leave tonight."

"We leave tonight," he agreed, "but not through the gates. They'll have those first." He was already moving, stuffing the lute into its case with quick, practiced hands. "Harbor district has other exits. Give me a moment and I'll show you how a disgraced court singer leaves a city without using doors."

Tamsin watched him work and felt the strange, uncomfortable thing she'd been feeling since the salt gate: that she trusted him. Not completely — trust was a currency she'd never had enough of to spend freely — but more than she trusted anyone else alive, which was a low bar and yet somehow felt enormous.

She went to the window instead of saying any of this. Below, the harbor lane was filling with people drifting toward their doors, the way water drifts toward a drain. A woman was pulling in laundry. Two boys were racing home ahead of the dark. Somewhere a dog barked, and kept barking, and then stopped all at once, which was worse.

"Merrin," she said quietly. "Look at the tavern."

He joined her at the window. Below, the Drowned Rat's common room was emptying fast — except for one man. Broad-shouldered, weathered, sitting alone at the corner table with a cup he wasn't drinking. Tamsin had noticed him when they'd checked in. She'd told herself he was just a dockhand drinking off a shift.

"He hasn't touched that cup since we came up," she said.

"No, he hasn't." Merrin's jaw tightened. "I know him. That's Cobb. He runs messages along the quay — or he did. He used to carry word between singers' lodgings when I was at court. Cheap, cheerful, and utterly reliable." He paused. "Which is why it worries me that he's sitting there like a man waiting for a payment."

"Or a man who's already taken one."

Merrin didn't answer, but his silence had a shape, and the shape was yes.

* * *

They came down the stairs quiet as smoke, packs already shouldered, and crossed the common room toward the kitchen door. The landlord was barricading his shutters and didn't look up. The man called Cobb didn't move either — until Tamsin passed his table.

"Tamsin Thackeray," he said.

Her whole body went still, the way it went still when she tasted something wrong on her tongue. Not panic. Assessment.

"I don't know you," she said.

"No," Cobb said, and now he did look up, and his eyes were sorry about it, genuinely sorry, which somehow made everything worse. "But I know your face from a description, and I know your friend's name from a list, and I know there's forty crowns in it for whoever says where you sleep tonight." He spread his hands. "I'm sorry, miss. Truly. But forty crowns is forty crowns, and my girl's got the lung-rot, and the physickers want coin I don't have."

The kitchen door was ten feet away. Between them and it stood nothing but air, and behind them stood the truth: he'd already sold them. The only question was whether the inquisitors were outside yet.

"They're not here yet," Cobb said, reading her the way she read a dish. "I'm to keep you in sight till the bell rings four. You've time to run if you run now. That's not part of the bargain. That's just me being sorry."

Tamsin's hand had drifted, without her permission, to the little knife at her belt. She was aware of it the way she was aware of poison — a fact, a weight, a choice not yet made. Forty crowns. A sick daughter. A man doing arithmetic with other people's lives because the sums were cruel.

She knew that arithmetic. She'd lived her whole life inside it.

"Where are the inquisitors staging?" she asked.

Cobb blinked. "The customs house, but—"

"How many? How are they armed? Do they have dogs?"

"Miss—"

"*Answer.*"

And something in her voice must have carried, because he answered. Six men, crossbows, no dogs yet, dogs coming at dawn from the garrison. Staging out of the customs house on the high quay. Led by a tall one with a burned cheek whom the dockhands called the Lantern, because wherever he went, questions got lit up and somebody burned.

Tamsin nodded slowly. Then she took three crowns from her purse — nearly everything she had — and set them on Cobb's table.

"For the lung-rot," she said. "Not for the information. The information you stole."

Cobb stared at the coins like they might bite. "You're mad."

"I'm a food-taster," Tamsin said. "Madness is occupational." And she walked out through the kitchen with Merrin at her heels, leaving the man sitting in the wreckage of his own conscience.

* * *

"What in the name of every god was that?" Merrin hissed, once they were in the alley behind the tavern, breath steaming in the cold. "He sold us. He'd have sold us again tomorrow. You gave him our escape money."

"I gave him three crowns so that tonight, when he tries to sleep, he remembers that the woman he betrayed paid for his daughter's medicine." Tamsin was already moving, scanning rooftops, counting distances between eaves. "People remember things like that, Merrin. It makes them slower to sell the next person. It's the only weapon I've ever had that works on everyone."

"That's not a weapon, that's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's the most exhausting philosophy I've ever heard."

"It's kept me alive."

"Has it?" He caught her arm at the mouth of the alley, and his grip was gentle but his eyes weren't. "Because from where I stand, it's what put you in front of six crossbows."

"The crossbows aren't for killing me. Didn't you hear Cobb? They want me *alive*. Which means every man holding one is carrying a weapon he's afraid to use properly." She pulled free, gently. "That's not a small thing, Merrin. That's the whole battle."

He looked at her for a long moment. "Who taught you to think like that?"

"A spymaster," she said, before she could stop herself. Then, quieter: "We'll come back to that. Right now — the harbor. You said you knew a way out."

Something flickered across his face — the old wound, the court, whatever had driven him from it — and then he nodded and led her down toward the water.

* * *

The way out, it turned out, was a chimney.

"Not a real chimney," Merrin said, stopping before a tannery whose back wall faced the harbor mud. "A flue. Old smokehouse, disused. Runs up through the building and comes out under the eaves on the quay side. From there it's a drop to the pilings, a walk along the harbor wall, and a ladder down to the waterline where the fishing boats tie up." He glanced at her. "I hid in it once, years ago, after a performance I'm not proud of and a patron I'm even less proud of. It smells like death, incidentally."

"Everything tonight smells like death," Tamsin said. "At least this death is familiar."

The flue was exactly as bad as advertised — a vertical stone throat slick with old grease and older smoke, barely wide enough for her shoulders. She climbed it in the dark by touch and memory, counting handholds the way she counted ingredients, and came out gasping under the eaves with the whole gray harbor spread below her.

And froze.

Lanterns. Dozens of them, moving along the high quay in a slow, deliberate line. Not six men. Twenty at least, and among them figures in black cloaks moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the night. At their center walked a tall man with a lantern held high and a burn scar down one side of his face, and even at this distance Tamsin could feel the quality of his attention — the way it moved over the harbor like a tongue over a dish, tasting for wrongness.

"The Lantern," she breathed. "He moved early."

"Cobb," Merrin said grimly, hauling himself up beside her. "Forty crowns buys a fast conscience."

Below them, the line of lanterns reached the Drowned Rat. They saw the door open. Saw figures go in. Saw, a few heartbeats later, a man dragged out into the light — broad-shouldered, protesting, a girl with a cough held weeping against his side.

"Oh no," Merrin said. "Oh, no, no—"

"Don't." Tamsin's voice was flat and hard, and it surprised even her. "Don't look at it as guilt. Look at it as information. They took Cobb because he lost us. Which means the Lantern doesn't fully trust his informants — which means he doesn't have another lead on us in this city. We are ghosts for exactly as long as we stay ghosts." She was already moving along the eaves, low, fast. "Boats. Now."

They dropped to the pilings and went along the harbor wall in the shadow of the moored hulls, and it was there, wedged between a gutting shed and a rotting sloop, that they found her: a river skiff, maybe twenty feet, flat-bottomed, with a single lug sail furled and a pair of oars shipped. Her paint was peeling and her name — *Sweet Mercy* — was half-scraped away, but she sat at her moorings like an answered prayer.

"She's not ours," Merrin said.

"Neither is the night," Tamsin said, stepping aboard. "Borrow it."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, the first real laugh she'd heard from him — and cast off the line.

* * *

They made the river mouth before the alarm bells began, and by the time the Lantern's lanterns were swarming the quays behind them, the tide had them in its fist and was pulling them out past the breakwater into open dark. Tamsin shipped the oars and let the skiff swing with the current, chest heaving, hands burning from the lines.

Merrin sat amidships with the lute case hugged to his chest like a child, watching Marrowgate's lights shrink astern.

"They'll question everyone Cobb ever spoke to," he said quietly. "Everyone I drank with these last two days. The landlady. The pawnbroker. By morning the Lantern will have my whole life in that ledger of his, and none of it will matter, because we'll be thirty miles downriver."

"Then why do you sound like a man at a funeral?"

"Because I *liked* it here." His voice cracked slightly, and he covered it with a shrug that fooled neither of them. "Two days of singing in a dockside tavern and nobody cared who I used to be. That's the most freedom I've had in six years, and I've just spent it."

Tamsin shipped the oars and sat down across from him. The river was wide and black and smelled of silt and distance, and somewhere ahead of them, if the map was honest, there was a smuggler who owed her father a debt, and beyond him a mountain full of half-alive music.

"Merrin," she said. "When this is over — if it ends well, which I grant is a large *if* — you can sing anywhere you want. In any city. Under any name."

"And until then?"

"Until then you sing for us." She nodded at the lute case. "Play something. Quietly. I want to hear what Corvane tried to silence."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't. The old fear was in his face — the flinch of a man who'd been punished for the crime of having a gift. But then his hands moved, almost without him, and the lute came out, and he began, very softly, to play.

It was nothing grand. A river song, an old one, the kind boatmen sang to keep stroke. But underneath the simple melody his voice wove a second line, low and strange, and Tamsin felt the hair rise on her arms — because she'd heard that second line before. On the map. In the notation that wasn't roads.

"Merrin," she whispered. "Play that again. The lower part."

He played it. The river seemed to lean in around them.

"That's covenant-song," she said. "Isn't it. That's what the map is written in."

His hands had gone still on the strings. In the dark his eyes were wide and wet and afraid and lit from within, all at once.

"Tamsin," he said. "I think I've been singing pieces of it my whole life. I just never knew what they were *for*."

Behind them, Marrowgate's lights guttered and shrank. Ahead, the river ran north into the hills, toward smugglers and hedge-witches and a mountain that remembered how to sing.

Tamsin took up the oars.

"Then let's find out," she said, and rowed.
