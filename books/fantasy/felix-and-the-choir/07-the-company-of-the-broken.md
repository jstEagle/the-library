## 7. The Company of the Broken

Merrin Vale had spent three years learning how to be small.

It was a discipline, like any other. He'd been the finest voice in the royal court once — trained since boyhood, praised by ambassadors, wept over by duchesses. Now he knew exactly how to stand in a dockside tavern so that no one's eyes caught on him. How to pitch his songs just below the noise of the crowd, so they washed over the room like weather instead of demanding to be heard. How to drink one ale for the length of three songs and look like he was on his fourth.

He was singing in Marrowgate now, in a tavern called the Drowned Bell, because it was far enough from the capital that nobody remembered his face and close enough to the river that a man could leave quickly if he needed to. A man always needed to be able to leave quickly. That was the first lesson of disgrace: keep your boots by the door.

The song was an old one, a hauling chantey about a wife who waited on the cliffs until she turned to stone. He sang it low, half-swallowed, letting the room do most of the work. And that was when he saw her.

She came in with the rain still dripping off her, hood back, moving like someone who had learned to read a room the way he read sheet music — entrances, exits, which corner held the danger. She took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered nothing at first, just listened. To him. Not the way patrons listened, which was barely at all, but the way a person listens when they're checking something against something else.

When he got to the third verse, her hand went to her pocket. When he hit the refrain, she pulled out a folded square of paper, opened it flat on the bar, and turned it toward him across two stools and a spilled cup of cider.

Merrin kept singing. It cost him something. The notation on that paper was like nothing he had ever seen — not roads, not rivers, no cartographer's marks anywhere. Just lines of symbols stacked like verses, climbing up the shape of a mountain drawn in a single unbroken stroke.

He finished the song. He let the tavern swallow the applause. Then he picked up his tankard, wandered down the bar as if looking for a better seat, and sat beside her.

"You're staring at my map," Tamsin Thackeray said.

"Your map is staring at me." He kept his voice under the room's noise, the way he did everything now. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me at the Salt Gate."

Something went through Merrin then, cold water down the spine. He'd heard about the Salt Gate stranger — half of Marrowgate had, gossip traveling faster than any boat. *The Choir remembers.* Everyone assumed it was a madman's last words.

"The notation," Merrin said carefully. "These aren't directions. They're music."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here. I've spent four days asking every musician in this city what these marks mean. Most of them laughed. One threw me out. A woman who tunes harps said her grandmother used marks like them for lullabies her grandmother claimed came from under the mountain." She looked at him directly, and Merrin felt seen in a way that was frankly inconvenient. "You didn't laugh. You went pale."

"I went pale," Merrin agreed, "because my old master used to say the court archives held fragments of covenant-song, and that if anyone ever brought him notation like this, I should burn it and forget their face." He turned the tankard in his hands. "I refused to sing at Corvane's ascension. You know what happened after that."

"Everyone knows what happened after that."

"Then you know I'm not a safe person to know."

"I'm not a safe person to know either," Tamsin said. "I think that makes us even."

* * *

They talked until the tavern keeper started stacking chairs, and then they talked in the alley behind it, out of the rain's reach under a sagging awning, and by the time the bells rang midnight Merrin had told her things he hadn't told anyone in three years.

How the summons had come, beautifully worded, requesting his voice for the Duke's ascension ceremony. How everyone else had sung. How he had stood in front of the assembled court and said that his voice belonged to the king's service, and the king was living, and he would not lend it to a usurper. How Corvane had smiled — that was the part people never believed, the smile — and stripped his title, his post, and his name from the rolls, and how the punishment for a silenced singer was supposed to be silence, except nobody could actually stop him singing, so instead they made sure no respectable hall would ever book him again.

"They couldn't take the voice," Merrin said. "So they took everything around it. You spend years building a life as a musician, and then one day your whole world is a dockside tavern and men who want to hear sad songs about drowned wives." He shrugged, aiming for lightness and missing by a mile. "I've gotten very good at being small."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the awning above them.

"I taste poison for a living," she said. "Did you know that? King's food-taster. Every meal, every banquet, every suspicious gift of candied fruit from foreign courts. I've been poisoned eleven times on purpose and survived all of them, and there are people in the palace who can't look at me without wondering what I am." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I ran because a dying man gave me a piece of music and I hid it from the spymaster who raised me. First lie of my life. I don't even know why I lied. Instinct, maybe. Or maybe some part of me already knew that if Aldous saw this map, I'd never see it again."

"Aldous. The spymaster?"

"My teacher. Practically my father." Her jaw tightened. "And someone in the palace sold my name to Corvane's riders before I was even out of sight of the walls. So either my teacher betrayed me or my teacher failed me, and I honestly don't know which would be worse."

Merrin studied her — this sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued woman who tasted death for breakfast and lied as rarely as other people breathed — and recognized something. Not her situation; his own. Two instruments that had been played by other people's hands for so long they'd almost forgotten they were instruments at all.

"The marks on your map," he said. "I've only ever seen fragments. But the structure — the way the lines interweave, the way each line has its own rhythm and they lock together—" He exhaled. "It's not a song written *for* voices, Tamsin. It's a song written *as* voices. Three lines, braided. Mortal, fae, and something between. If I'm reading it right, this isn't a map of passes through Thornfell. It's a score. The mountain itself is the instrument."

"And the Choir," Tamsin said slowly, "isn't a place."

"It might be both. Places remember songs. Songs remember places." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Listen to me. Three years of dockside chanteys and I'm talking like a conservatory lecture. My master would box my ears."

"Your master sounds wise."

"He was. He also died believing I'd thrown my gift away out of stubbornness." Merrin folded the map along its creases with the care of a man handling a relic. "Here's what I can tell you for certain. This first line here—" he tapped a cluster of notation near the mountain's base, "—that's a starting note. A place where the song begins. And it's not in the capital, and it's not in Corvane's territory. It's here. In Marrowgate. Somewhere near the river."

Tamsin stared at him. "You're telling me the first landmark is in the city I fled to."

"I'm telling you," Merrin said, "that either fate has a sense of humor, or the dying man at the Salt Gate knew exactly what he was doing when he pressed that map into your hands."

* * *

The first landmark was a chimney.

Not a grand one — a leaning stone chimney attached to a ruined smokehouse downriver of the salt warehouses, half-swallowed by ivy, the kind of structure every city has ten of and no one looks at twice. But when Merrin hummed the opening phrase of the map's first line against its stones, the chimney *answered*. Faintly. A resonance, deep in the masonry, like a bell struck somewhere far below the earth.

Tamsin had gone very still. "Do that again."

"Don't ask me to do that again," Merrin said, but he did, and this time the answer lasted longer, a low harmonic humming up through the ground and into the soles of their feet before fading into the ordinary noises of the riverfront.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Gulls argued over the mudflats. Somewhere a fishwife was screaming about herring prices.

"It's real," Tamsin said finally. Her voice had gone strange. "All of it. The Choir. The covenant. It's real, and it's *here*, under our feet, and it's been waiting—"

"Merrin Vale." The voice came from behind them, gravel-deep and amused. "Singing to chimneys now. The conservatory really does lower its standards."

They both spun. Standing at the mouth of the alley was a dwarf built like a door, gray-streaked beard braided with iron rings, arms crossed over a leather coat that had clearly stopped more than one knife. He leaned on a walking staff that was probably also a weapon, and he was grinning.

"Brannoc," Merrin said. "I thought you were north."

"I was north. Then I heard a rumor that the court's lost nightingale was in Marrowgate humming at architecture, and I thought, well, that's worth the walk." Brannoc's grin faded into something sharper as his eyes moved to Tamsin, then to the map in her hands. His whole manner changed — the humor dropping away like a mask. "That's a covenant-map. Where did you get a covenant-map?"

"You know what it is?"

"I know what it is because your father drew maps like it," Brannoc said to Tamsin. "Thackeray. You're Thackeray's girl. You have his chin and his terrible habit of standing where the light won't reach." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped. "Your father carried me out of the Thornfell passes on his back with two arrows in him when I was a young fool running ore for the wrong people. He died owing me nothing and me owing him everything. So before you answer another question, girl — who's hunting you, and how many?"

"Inquisitors. Corvane's. At least six that I know of, possibly more."

"Six." Brannoc nodded slowly. "Good odds. For them." He spat thoughtfully into the mud. "Right. Here's what happens now. You're coming off the streets, both of you, because inquisitors pay informants by the head and Marrowgate's docks have more ears than the sea has fish. There's a woman up on Cindervine Hill who owes me a favor and keeps a house nobody finds unless she wants them to. Wren, they call her. Hedge-witch, herb-woman, and — though she'll deny it with a stick — one of the last people alive who remembers the old rites." He fixed them both with a look. "And before you ask: yes. I think it's time somebody remembered them."

* * *

Wren's house sat at the top of Cindervine Hill like a hen settling onto eggs: squat, brown, and apparently composed entirely of things other buildings had discarded. Wind chimes made of spoons. A roof patched with three kinds of tile. A garden that seemed to be mostly thorns and things that moved slightly when there was no wind.

"She's expecting us," Brannoc said, knocking a pattern on the door that Merrin suspected was less a knock and more a password. "Which means she's known since yesterday and has been insufferable about it ever since."

The door opened on a small woman, sixty-odd, with iron-gray hair escaping its braid and eyes the color of wet slate. She looked Tamsin up and down, then Merrin, then the map, and snorted.

"So it's finally happened," she said. "Someone's gone and found the thing I've spent forty years hoping nobody would find." She stepped aside. "Well? Come in before the hill notices you. It gossips."

Inside, the house smelled of drying herbs and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something mineral and green, like a cave after rain. Bundles hung from every beam. A fire burned in a hearth shaped like a cupped pair of hands. And sitting cross-legged on the floor by the fire, sorting dried flowers into piles with fierce concentration, was a child.

Maybe ten years old. Thin, dark-haired, with the kind of stillness children only have when they're listening to something adults can't hear. The child looked up as they entered, and Merrin felt the air change — a faint pressure, a faint brightening, like the moment before a chord resolves.

The child's eyes went straight past the adults. Straight to the map.

"You found it," the child said. Their voice was soft and clear as a struck glass. "I heard it wake up. Four days ago. I was asleep and then I wasn't, and the mountain was singing, and Mama said stop talking nonsense, so I left." They tilted their head at Tamsin. "You're carrying it. Why are you carrying it? It's loud."

"Sef," Wren said warningly.

"What? It *is* loud. It's been getting louder all week." The child scrambled up, barefoot, and approached Tamsin with the fearless directness of the very young and the very strange. "Can I see? I promise I won't break it. I never break things. Things break *near* me sometimes, but that's different."

Tamsin glanced at Wren, who gave a shrug that managed to convey both *your funeral* and *I told you nothing*. Slowly, Tamsin knelt and unfolded the map between them.

Sef looked at it for perhaps three seconds. Then the child began to hum.

Merrin's skin prickled. It was the same melody as the chimney's answer — the same deep harmonic he'd felt through his boot-soles — but sung by a child's voice it was transformed, clarified, *finished*, the way a sketch becomes a painting. Sef hummed eight bars, flawless, note-perfect, music the child could not possibly have learned because it had been woken from under a mountain four days ago.

Then Sef stopped, and looked at Tamsin with enormous, ancient eyes.

"That's the first line," the child said. "There are three. Do you know the others?"

"No," Tamsin said hoarsely.

"Well." Sef sat back on their heels, satisfied, as if the matter were settled. "We'd better find them, then. Before the bad men do."

Nobody asked which bad men. Everybody knew.

* * *

They stayed that night, because the rain turned hard and Wren refused to let anyone travel in it, and because — though no one said it aloud — none of them wanted to separate yet. Wren fed them barley soup with wild garlic and bread baked in a pot, and grumbled the entire time about uninvited guests and the state of the world, and refilled every bowl before it was empty.

Afterward, while Sef slept curled in a chair like a cat, Wren spread the map on her table and weighted its corners with river stones, and told them what she remembered.

Her grandmother had been a rite-keeper, back when rite-keepers were tolerated. The covenant, she said, wasn't a treaty the way humans wrote treaties — signed, sealed, filed away. It was a song, sustained. Sung at the Deepwell beneath Thornfell by three voices in harmony: mortal, fae, and a bridge between. As long as the song continued, the border between the realms stayed open and gentle, and neither side could raise a hand against the other without the mountain itself knowing.

"A generation ago," Wren said, "the singers were killed. Humans did it — or men claiming to act for humans. The song stopped. The border closed hard. And everything since — the usurpation, the inquisitors, the machines they're building to shatter fae-song — it's all one long echo of that murder." She tapped the map. "This isn't instructions. It's the *score*. Whoever drew it wanted the Choir rebuilt. And whoever killed the stranger at your gate, girl, wanted exactly the opposite."

Merrin looked at the fire. "Three voices," he said quietly. "Mortal, fae, bridge. I can carry a mortal line, if my voice hasn't rotted in the taverns. The child—" he glanced at Sef's sleeping form, "—may be fae-blooded enough for the second. But the bridge? Who sings the bridge?"

Wren and Tamsin exchanged a glance that Merrin couldn't read, and Wren said, "One disaster at a time, singer."

Later, unable to sleep, Merrin climbed to the loft where Tamsin lay awake on a pallet, staring at the rafters.

"You should sleep," he whispered.

"I'm listening," she said. "Under the house. Under the hill. There's something. Like a note held so long you stop hearing it — until it stops."

Merrin lay back and listened too. And there it was, faint as memory: a sustained tone beneath the mountain, patient beyond all human patience, like a choir holding its breath.

"We'll need more than the three of us," he murmured. "Voices, supplies, roads in and out. People who can move unseen."

"I know one smuggler," Tamsin said.

"I know a witch," Merrin said.

From the chair below, without stirring, Sef said sleepily, "I know a secret way under the river. Probably. I haven't tried it yet."

Merrin smiled in the dark. It felt strange on his face, that expression — rusty, underused, like a door long shut. Warmth, after all that flight. He'd forgotten what it was like, wanting to be found by the morning.

Down in the hills toward the south, torchlight was moving. Too orderly for travelers. Too numerous for a patrol.

Brannoc appeared at the foot of the stairs, silent as smoke, and his voice was grim as grave-dirt.

"Riders on the Cindervine road," he said. "Inquisitor green. They're not searching the city anymore, friends. They've narrowed it to the hill."
