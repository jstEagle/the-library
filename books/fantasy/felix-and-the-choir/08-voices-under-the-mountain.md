## 8. Voices Under the Mountain

They left Marrowgate by a door that did not exist.

That was Brannoc's phrase for it, and he was proud of it in the way only smugglers are proud of their lies. The door was a drainage arch behind a tannery on the hill's north face, bricked over generations ago and forgotten by everyone except the people whose business required forgetting things professionally. Brannoc had the key to the forgetting — three turns of an iron pick, a shoulder of practiced weight, and the bricks swung inward like a mouth deciding to speak.

"Mind the drop," he said. "And mind the smell. Both are worse than they look."

Tamsin went first, because she always went first now. It had happened without anyone deciding it. Somewhere between the salt gate and the tavern cellar where they'd spent the last two days breathing through wet cloth while inquisitors knocked on doors above them, she had become the point of the arrow, and the others had arranged themselves behind her like feathers.

Merrin came second, Sef tucked against his side with the child's hood up and their small hand buried in his sleeve. Old Wren came last, muttering wards under her breath as she sealed the arch behind them — not locked, exactly, but *confused*, so that anyone following would find the passage turned around inside itself and walk out again believing they'd never entered.

"Will that hold?" Tamsin asked.

"Against a clever man? An hour." Wren's teeth were very white in the dark. "Against an inquisitor? Less. They carry iron and doubt, and both cut through hedge-magic like a hot knife through lard. Walk fast."

They walked fast.

The drain became a culvert, the culvert a ravine, the ravine the grey northern slopes of the Thornfells, where the pines grew crooked and the fog lived like a tenant who paid no rent. By the time the sun hauled itself above the ridgeline they were three miles from Marrowgate and climbing, and Tamsin allowed herself one look back.

The city sat in its bowl of hills, smoke rising straight and ordinary from ten thousand chimneys. It looked peaceful. It looked like nothing was wrong at all. She thought about the tavern keeper who'd sold them to the green riders for eleven silver marks, and about how she couldn't even hate him properly, because eleven marks was half a year of honest work and honesty had never once kept a roof over anyone's head in Marrowgate.

"They'll burn the tavern," Brannoc said beside her. He'd followed her gaze without seeming to look at all. "The one we slept in. That's how they do it. Not the whole street — just the building. Everyone learns: harbor rats, lose your house."

"You've seen it before."

"I've *been* it before." He said it lightly, the way men say heavy things when they've carried them long enough for the weight to wear smooth. "Corvane's not new, girl. He's just finally stopped pretending."

Tamsin took out the map.

She'd been taking it out every hour since the salt gate, unfolding it in snatched moments — in alleys, in cellars, once in the privy of a river barge — and every time it looked slightly different than her memory of it. Not the ink. The ink was stable enough, brown-black lines on skin that might have been calfskin or might have been something else entirely, something with pores too fine for any animal she knew. What changed was her understanding of it. Each hour she saw more.

It showed the Thornfell mountains, but not as any cartographer drew them. There were no roads. Instead there were lines of notation — small hooked symbols, stems and flags and rests — running along the valleys and up the shoulders of the peaks like rivers of writing. And threaded among them, fainter, almost shy, were markings she could only call *passes*: gaps in the ridgelines that she knew for a fact did not exist. She had grown up in these mountains. She had crawled through every crack a goat could squeeze through, and half the ones a goat couldn't. These passes were strangers to her.

Except.

She stopped walking.

"What?" Merrin said, instantly alert, hand going to the knife at his belt.

"No. Look." She held the map flat between them. "This mark here. This little cluster of notes at the head of the Greywater valley. I know this place. There's nothing there — a scree field, a dead waterfall, a hollow under the cliff. I sheltered in it once during a whiteout when I was fourteen. But the map puts a *door* there. A big one. See the double line? Doors get double lines. Passes get single."

"And you trust the map?"

"It hasn't lied yet." She looked up at him. "Has anything else?"

He didn't answer that, because the answer was yes — everything else had lied, everyone else had lied, the whole architecture of her careful poisoned life had been one long lie — and they both knew it, and some things didn't need saying twice.

Sef tugged her sleeve. The child hadn't spoken much since the cellar; fear sat on them like a coat too heavy to shrug off. But now Sef pointed up the valley, past the scree fields, past the dead fall of the Greywater where it spilled white bones of ice down the mountainside, and said, in a voice thin with something that wasn't fear anymore:

"It's louder here."

Tamsin heard nothing. Wind in the pines. Water under the ice. Her own pulse, which she monitored the way other people monitored the weather.

But Wren had gone still in the way old women go still — completely, deliberately, like a heron deciding whether the pond is safe. "Louder than what, child?"

"The singing." Sef's eyes were enormous. "I thought it was the sea. In Marrowgate I thought it was the sea under the docks. But there's no sea here."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Wren said, gently, "How long have you heard it?"

"All my life?" Sef shrugged, small and defensive. "Doesn't everyone?"

"No," said Wren, and Merrin said, "Gods," and Brannoc said nothing at all but his hand found a hammer at his belt that wouldn't help against anything, and gripped it anyway.

Tamsin folded the map with hands that wanted to shake and made them not. "Then we go where it's loudest," she said. "Because that's where the map says the door is, and I'm done being led by people who lie."

* * *

The hollow under the cliff was exactly as she remembered it: a shallow amphitheater of stone scooped from the mountain's flank, floored with scree, walled by a curtain of rock from which the dead waterfall descended in frozen ropes. In a whiteout it had saved her life. In afternoon light it was just a cold place where wind came to die.

But the map's double line ran along the base of the frozen fall, and when Tamsin knelt and put her palm against the ice — because she was the one who tasted everything, who tested everything, who put her own body between the company and whatever came next, because that was the only way she knew how to be worth anything to anyone — the ice was not cold.

It was warm. Faintly, impossibly, like a cheek.

"Wren," she said.

The old woman crouched beside her, laid her own palm flat, and closed her eyes. Her lips moved. Whatever she felt, it took a long time to come up through her, like a diver surfacing from depth.

"Well," Wren said at last. "Well, well, well." She laughed, a dry rustle with no humor in it and something close to awe. "Fifty years I've dowsed for the old ways, and I find more of it in one dead waterfall than in forty years of standing stones. Girl, do you know what you're kneeling on?"

"A door."

"A *throat*." Wren rapped the ice with one knuckle, and the sound it gave back was wrong — deep, resonant, a bell-note that went on far longer than ice should allow, rolling away up the valley and coming back changed. "This whole mountain is hollowed out like a honeycomb, and every chamber of it was built to sing. This isn't a wall. It's the rim of an instrument."

Merrin had gone pale. Tamsin watched him listen — really listen, the way he must have listened once, in a court that had since learned to flatter lesser voices. His head tilted. His breath slowed.

"I hear it," he whispered. "Under the ice. Under the stone. It's—" He stopped. Started again. "It's a chord. Held. It's been held for—"

"Since the breaking," Wren said. "A generation ago. Maybe longer. A chord held that long by singers who are either dead or worse than dead, sustained by nothing but the mountain remembering them." She settled back onto her heels and looked at each of them in turn, and Tamsin realized the old woman had decided something, had weighed them all in her hedge-witch scales and found them, if not sufficient, then at least present.

"Sit down," Wren said. "All of you. If I'm going to tell you why half the world wants you dead, you may as well be comfortable."

* * *

They built no fire. Wren wouldn't allow it — light draws eyes, and warmth draws things older than eyes — so they huddled in their cloaks against the warm-cold stone while the light failed, and Wren told it the way she told everything: slowly, in pieces, like dealing cards she wasn't sure she wanted to give up.

"Before your grandfather's grandfather," she began, "there were two courts in this land, and everyone knew it. The human realms — your kings, your dukes, your fat merchants and hungry peasants. And below, in the mountain's roots, the fae courts. Ash and Thorn and the drowned court under the lakes, and others besides, names that don't sit well in a human mouth. Two peoples. Two territories. One border, and the border was war, for longer than anyone could count. Every generation, raid and reprisal. Children stolen both ways. Wells poisoned both ways." Her eyes flicked to Tamsin, briefly, meaningfully, and moved on. "Both ways. Remember that."

"We remember," Tamsin said quietly.

"Do you? Your histories don't. Your histories say the fae were monsters and the heroes drove them underground, and maybe that's true the way a coin is true — one face of it. Anyway. At some point, someone on both sides got tired. Or wise. Accounts differ. And what they built instead of a treaty — because treaties are paper and paper burns — was a song."

"A song," Merrin repeated. He sounded like a man being handed back something stolen from him years ago.

"The Choir." Wren spread her hands toward the mountain, the whole massif of it, black against the last violet of the sky. "Not one singer. Hundreds. Thousands, at the height of it. Humans and fae together, in chambers like this one, singing a covenant into the bedrock itself. The terms weren't written anywhere because they didn't need to be — the song *was* the terms. Mutual protection. Open borders at the crossing-places. Peace enforced not by armies but by harmony, because when the Choir sang true, neither people could raise a hand against the other without hearing what they were about to break. You cannot swing a sword, child, when your whole body is busy making beauty. Try it sometime."

"And the notation on the map," Merrin said. His voice had gone strange, reverent, stripped of the tavern-toughness he wore like armor. "Those aren't directions. They're—"

"The score. Parts of it. What survived of the score." Wren nodded at the leather case where Tamsin kept it. "Roads for people who travel by song. And the passes marked along it — the ones that shouldn't exist? They exist when the Choir sings. They're *made* by the singing. Crossings that open between the realms where the harmonies touch. When the covenant broke, the crossings closed, and the world forgot they'd ever been there. Except the mapmaker. Whoever he was."

"Felix," Tamsin said. The name came out of her before she decided to say it. She hadn't spoken it aloud since the salt gate. "The man who gave me the map. He said—" Her throat tightened around it, the way it always did. "He said the Choir remembers."

Silence, except the wind, except — faintly, so faintly Tamsin might have invented it — something underneath the wind. A held note. A chord that refused to die.

"So it does," Wren said softly. "So it does. Here's the grief of it, children, and I'll only say this part once. Twenty-two years ago, the king's court — advised by men whose names I know and will carry to my grave — decided the covenant was a leash and the fae were dogs. There was a faction that had always hated the peace. Called it weakness. Called the open crossings an invitation to invasion. And they found the flaw in the whole beautiful structure, the same flaw in every structure ever built by two peoples trusting each other." She paused. "It depends on the singers."

"The massacre," Merrin said. "My grandmother sang of it. We thought it was legend."

"It was Tuesday," Wren said flatly. "It happened on a Tuesday, in autumn, in six halls at once. The king's soldiers came for the mortal singers first — quietly, family by family, so no hall would warn another. Then they sealed the fae chambers and burned something called quicksilver-salt in the vents, and the fae voices that didn't die were driven mad or driven silent. And the Choir broke mid-chord, all across the mountain at once, and the sound of it—" For the first time, the old woman's voice failed her. She pressed her lips together, breathed through her nose, tried again. "I was young. I heard it from thirty miles away. The mountain screamed, and then it went quiet, and it has been quiet ever since. Quiet the way a house is quiet after the funeral."

Sef was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've learned crying loudly costs something. Merrin pulled the child against his side.

"And Corvane," Tamsin said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her, because underneath the levelness was a furnace. "The duke. The inquisitors. The instruments they carry — the ones designed to shatter fae-song. He's finishing it. Whatever the massacre started, he means to end it. No covenant. No fae. Nothing left below but ore to dig and dark to fill."

"Now you see it," Wren said. "Now you see why a dying man bled out at a salt gate to put a scrap of skin in a food-taster's hand. Because the Choir remembers, yes. But memory alone sings nothing. Someone has to carry the tune."

* * *

Full dark came down, and with it the thing none of them were prepared for, though Wren had perhaps suspected, because she had chosen this hollow and not another.

It began as a change in the wind. Then beneath the wind. Then beneath the stone.

A harmony. Faint as breath on a winter window, rising out of the deep places of the mountain — not one voice but many, braided, holding that impossible chord that had been held for twenty-two years, and tonight, tonight of all nights, with five living bodies pressed against its throat and a fragment of its own score lying folded in a taster's pack, the chord *moved*. Shifted. Resolved, a single step, toward something that had been unresolved for a generation.

Merrin made a sound Tamsin had never heard a grown man make. Sef stood up, arms out, face lifted, tears freezing on their cheeks, and hummed — a third above the deep voices, perfect, instinctive, a child answering a call older than kingdoms.

And Tamsin Thackeray, who had tasted poison in a hundred dishes and never once been able to hear music without flinching, because music meant court and court meant masks — Tamsin listened, and for the first time in her life heard a thing underneath a thing, the second voice beneath the first, and understood that she had been trained for this her whole life without knowing it. To taste what others couldn't. To hear what others couldn't. To stand between.

The chord faded. The mountain settled. Sef sat down again, dazed and shining.

"It knows we're here," the child whispered.

Far to the south, down in the lowlands where the torchlight had marched two days ago, a horn blew. Once. Twice. Then many horns, many camps, the sound carrying up the valleys on the cold air with terrible clarity.

Brannoc stood, and spat, and said what they were all thinking:

"That's not a patrol anymore. That's an army."

Above the treeline, the moon was three days from full.
