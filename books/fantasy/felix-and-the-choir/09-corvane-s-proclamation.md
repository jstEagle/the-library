## 9. Corvane's Proclamation

The news came up the river on a barge, the way bad news always did in Marrowgate — faster than any rider could carry it, because bargemen traded gossip like coin and spent it just as freely.

Merrin heard it first from a fishwife at the Saltwater Stairs, her baskets of silver-scaled dace abandoned as she gripped the rail and shouted down to the deckhands. "Corvane's declared! The free cities are in rebellion — all of them, every one, by his own decree!" Her voice cracked across the morning fog. "He's marching north!"

For a moment the whole quay went still, the way water goes still before something rises through it.

Then it broke into noise — shouting, cursing, a man laughing with the hollow edge of someone who doesn't believe what he's hearing, a woman gathering her children close with hands that shook. Gulls screamed over the rigging. Somewhere a bell began to ring, and then another, and then all of them, the harbor bells of Marrowgate tolling out of rhythm with each other like a heart gone arrhythmic.

Merrin stood at the top of the stairs with a jug of cheap wine under his arm and felt the words land in his chest like stones dropped down a well.

*Rebellion.* That was the word Corvane had chosen. Not *secession*, not *defiance* — *rebellion*. Which meant treason. Which meant that every soul in Marrowgate, in Vell's Landing, in the salt-towns and river-holds, was now legally a traitor to a crown most of them had never sworn to. It meant there would be no negotiation, no parley, no terms. It meant armies.

It meant that everything Tamsin had been running from had just grown teeth.

He found her an hour later in the room above the Drowned Bell, the tavern where Brannoc's cousin kept rooms for people who didn't want their names written anywhere. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the bedframe, the map spread open across her knees, and she didn't look up when he came in.

"You've heard," she said.

"The whole city's heard." He set the wine down untouched. "Fishwife at the Stairs. Bells are ringing."

"Bells." Tamsin laughed once, without any humor in it. "I used to think bells were beautiful. In the palace they rang them for coronations, for births. You'd stand in the kitchens and feel it in your chest." She traced one finger along the map's impossible notation, those curling marks that were roads and songs at once. "Now they sound like counting. Like someone tallying how much time we have left."

"How much do we have?"

She did look up then, and Merrin saw something in her face he'd only seen once before — the night she'd told him about the tasting, about the years of sipping death in small measured doses so that some fat lord could eat his pheasant in peace. It was the look of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"Armies march slowly," she said. "Especially Corvane's. He'll want to make a show of it. Banners, supply trains, the whole theater of power. Call it three weeks to reach the Thornfell foothills if he pushes hard." A pause. "But he won't send the army after us. Not yet. He'll send the inquisitors ahead. Fast riders. They move light and they don't stop to sleep."

Merrin sat down across from her, the floorboards cold through his trousers. "And us?"

"We keep moving. Up-country, toward the first notation on the map." She folded the map with the crisp economy of someone who'd learned never to leave anything important unfolded where eyes could find it. "Wren says the harmonies are strongest near the old shrine-stones above Harrow Vale. If we can hear the Choir properly — really hear it, not just echoes — maybe we can understand what we're supposed to do with it before—"

"Before the world ends," Merrin finished.

"Before the world *changes*," Tamsin corrected quietly. "Those aren't the same thing. One of them you survive."

There was a knock at the door — two short, one long, Brannoc's signal — and then the dwarf himself shouldered in without waiting, which was also very Brannoc. His beard was braided tight for travel, which meant he'd already made his decision about something.

"River's watched," he said without preamble. "Two boats at the ferry crossing asking questions, and neither one's fishing. Wren sent Sef up the chimney to listen at the tavern below — don't give me that look, the girl *likes* chimneys — and there's men down there talking about a taster and a singer. Descriptions. Rewards." He spat into the corner, an old habit he refused to apologize for. "Your faces are worth more than my boat now, and my boat was worth a lot."

Tamsin rose in one motion, already moving, already packing. Merrin watched her and thought, not for the first time, that she moved like water finding its level — no wasted motion, no hesitation, every part of her aimed at the next necessary thing. He wondered if she knew how rare that was. He wondered if anyone had ever told her.

"Then we go tonight," she said. "North road or goat tracks?"

"Goat tracks. North road's got eyes on it." Brannoc grinned, and there was no joy in it. "Good news is, nobody follows goats willingly. Bad news is, goats don't carry provisions, so we'll be eating like goats too."

Merrin almost smiled. Almost. Then the harbor bell tolled again, long and low, and the smile died somewhere on the way to his mouth.

Outside the window, Marrowgate was changing around them. He could see it from the glass: merchants hauling shutters closed, a militia sergeant nailing a proclamation to the customs-house door, a knot of people reading it in silence. Even from here Merrin could see the Duke's seal at the bottom — black wax, a raven stamped in it, wings spread wide over a broken crown.

*A raven over a broken crown.* Corvane had chosen his heraldry the day he'd taken power, and everyone had pretended not to understand what it meant. Nobody was pretending anymore.

"I sang at his ascension," Merrin said suddenly. The words came out of him before he could stop them, the way blood comes from a cut you've been pressing on too long. "Did I ever tell you that? Not the ceremony itself — I refused that, that's why I'm here — but the feast after. Before I understood what he was. Everyone sang that night. The whole court. And Corvane stood at the window with his back to all of it, watching the mountains, and I remember thinking: *there's a man listening to something else.*"

Tamsin stopped packing. "Listening to what?"

"I didn't know then." Merrin looked at his own hands, the hands that had once held courts spellbound, now roughened by dock work and cheap gin. "I think I know now. He wasn't ignoring the music. He was measuring it. Working out how loud it would have to be to drown."

* * *

They left Marrowgate two hours past midnight, slipping out through the tanner's quarter where the smell covered everything and even inquisitors preferred to breathe through their mouths. Wren led them through lanes older than the city itself, paths that existed only in the memories of people who'd needed them, and by dawn they were in the high pasturelands with the smoke of the harbor behind them and nothing ahead but hills and weather.

Sef walked beside Merrin. The child had been quiet since the news came — quieter than usual, which for Sef was saying something. Now, with the city safely distant, they finally spoke.

"Merrin." Their voice was small in the big morning. "What happens to fae folk if Corvane wins?"

Merrin considered lying, which was a kindness sometimes, and decided against it, because Sef always knew. "Nothing good."

"That's what I thought." Sef kicked a stone off the path with great precision. "Old Wren told me stories. About before. When the courts were open and the singing crossed the border both ways." A pause. "Were you ever happy? Before?"

Merrin thought about the conservatory, about the years of training, about the first time he'd opened his mouth in the great hall and felt two hundred people forget to breathe. He thought about the moment he'd learned what his voice was being used *for* — the softening of crowds, the anesthetizing of dissent, the way a beautiful song could make people accept things they'd have died resisting otherwise.

"I was happy in the wrong way," he said finally. "Happy like a bird in a gilded cage is happy. I didn't know the cage was there until I tried to fly and hit the bars." He looked down at Sef, who was watching him with those unsettling fae-colored eyes. "You're asking because you're scared."

"I'm asking because I can hear something." Sef pointed north, toward the mountains, toward the haze where Thornfell's shoulders rose against the sky. "Since last night. Since the bells. There's a new sound underneath everything. Low. Like..." They frowned, searching for the word. "Like a string being tightened."

Merrin felt the hair rise on his arms. "The Choir?"

"No. Something pushing *against* the Choir. Or trying to." Sef's small hands curled into fists. "Wren says instruments are coming. Bad ones. Made to break song instead of make it."

"She said that?"

"Sef hears things." The child shrugged, elaborately casual, the way children do when they're terrified. "People talk on docks. Boatmen talk loudest of all." They kicked another stone. "They say the inquisitors are carrying something called a *shatterbell*. Iron and silver, tuned wrong on purpose. When they ring it, anything fae within earshot feels like their bones are being unstrung. And anything human who's been... touched by fae-song..." Sef glanced up at Merrin, and their meaning was plain. "...it hurts them too."

Merrin's throat went dry. He thought of his voice — the thing he'd been born with, trained for twenty years, ruined for five, and somehow, impossibly, mending now that it had a purpose again. The thing the map needed. The thing the whole mad quest rested on.

"And if one of those bells rings while I'm singing?" he asked. "While any of us is singing?"

Sef didn't answer. They didn't have to. The silence stretched between them, filled with wind and distance and the tightening string only Sef could hear.

* * *

They made the shrine-stones above Harrow Vale on the third evening, and it was there, with the sun going down red behind Thornfell, that Old Wren confirmed what the whole company had been dreading.

She'd climbed to the tallest of the leaning monoliths — stones raised by hands no history remembered, carved with spirals that matched the notation on Tamsin's map — and stood there with her palm flat against the rock, listening the way she listened, with her whole body. When she came down, her face was gray.

"It's true," she said. "All of it. The proclamation, the march, the instruments." She lowered herself onto a fallen stone with the care of a woman whose joints had opinions. "A fish-merchant came through the vale this afternoon. Talked my ear off while I bought onions. Corvane's not just marching — he's *proclaiming* as he goes. Every town he takes, he reads the same decree from the steps of whatever passes for a hall. Rebellion. Treason. Seizure of property. Conscription of all able-bodied persons between fifteen and fifty." She snorted. "Fifteen. Sef's age, near enough."

"He wouldn't," Tamsin started.

"He would, and worse. That's the point of him." Wren dug in her pack and produced a strip of dried fish, chewed it thoughtfully. "But here's the part your fishwife didn't know, and the dock-talk only half-got right. The shatterbells are real. I've seen one. Thirty years ago, back when the covenant was fresh-broken and the Crown was still pretending to be sorry about it. A prototype. Iron cast around a silver core, tuned to the frequency of a breaking promise." Her eyes went distant. "When it rings, the air itself turns hostile to song. Notes die mid-flight. Voices crack. Fae-blooded folk double over like they've been gutted." She looked at Merrin directly. "You'd survive it. But you'd never sing again afterward. Not properly. Not ever."

The fire crackled. Somewhere downslope, a nightjar called, and the sound seemed fragile, temporary, like everything else.

"So that's the shape of it," Merrin said at last. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened. "We're racing an army to a hole in a mountain, carrying the one weapon they've built specifically to destroy, and our plan hinges on a disgraced tavern singer whose voice they can end with a bell."

"When you put it like that," Brannoc said, "it sounds grim."

"When I put it like *any* way, it sounds grim."

"But—" Brannoc held up a finger. "Here's what your fishwife and your onion-seller and your dockside gossips don't know, because they weren't smugglers during the last war. Corvane's army is big, yes. Ten thousand, maybe twelve, with baggage train and siege gear. But it has to stay *together*. An army that scatters dies. So it moves along the valleys, the river roads, the places where grain can be requisitioned and wagons can roll." He tapped the map where Tamsin had unrolled it across her knees. "Which means the high country is empty. All of it. Every pass, every track, every goat-path from here to the Deepwell — no soldiers, because soldiers can't climb. Only inquisitors ride ahead, and inquisitors travel in sixes and eights, not hundreds."

"So we race the army," Tamsin said slowly, "by going where it can't follow."

"We race the army by going where it *won't look*," Brannoc corrected. "Different thing. Better odds."

Wren nodded slowly. "And there's this. The shatterbells are few. Expensive to make, dangerous to carry — the tuning's unstable, and a badly-rung bell kills its bearer as gladly as its target. Corvane won't waste them on villages. He'll save them for the Deepwell, for the moment he thinks the covenant might actually be reforged." Her gaze swept the circle of faces around the fire. "Which means, children, that as long as we're unknown, we're safe-ish. The moment he learns what we're attempting..."

"Then we don't let him learn," Tamsin said.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting very straight, the firelight catching the fine scars on her forearms — the old taster's marks, needle-thin, dozens of them, a lifetime of small poisons survived. Merrin had seen them a hundred times now and still hadn't gotten used to what they meant: that this woman had been poisoned deliberately, systematically, since childhood, and had endured it, and had come out the other side with her mind intact and her will unbent.

"They know I exist," she went on. "They know I have the map. They don't know what the map *is*, or they'd have burned the mountain down already looking for it. So we stay small. We stay fast. We stay *quiet*." She looked at each of them in turn — Brannoc, Wren, Sef, and last Merrin, holding his gaze a beat longer than the others. "No fires after this one. No names in towns. No songs sung aloud until we're past the last inhabited valley. Merrin—"

"I know." He managed something like a smile. "The voice stays holstered."

"The voice stays holstered," she agreed. "Because the moment you sing — truly sing, the way you sang in the great hall — every fae-blooded thing within ten miles will feel it, and so will every instrument built to hunt it. Your voice isn't just a key anymore. It's a beacon."

"A beacon they can put out with a bell," Merrin said.

"A beacon they can't ring a bell at if they never hear it," Tamsin countered. "We choose the ground. We choose the moment. That's all any fighter gets, in the end — the choosing of the ground." She paused, and something flickered across her face, some old memory surfacing and sinking again. "Someone taught me that. Someone I trusted."

Merrin heard the name she didn't say — *Aldous* — and let it lie. Some wounds shouldn't be prodded in company.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn and climbed.

The high country took them the way the sea takes swimmers — gradually, then completely. The pastures gave way to scree, the scree to bare granite, the granite to a world of wind and lichen and the occasional defiant pine bent horizontal by decades of weather. Brannoc led, reading the mountain like a ledger, calling out holds and hazards in his gravel voice. Sef scrambled after him like the mountain was a game invented for their benefit. Wren rode Brannoc's pack-mule — a sour-tempered animal named Duchess who tolerated exactly one person and it wasn't her owner — and muttered wards under her breath the whole way, keeping the worst of the cold off the company.

Merrin walked beside Tamsin, and for a long time neither of them spoke. There was no need. The mountain did the talking — groaning in the heat of the day, ticking as stones settled, whispering in the passes where the wind funneled through. And beneath it all, fainter than breath, Merrin could almost hear it now: the thing Sef heard clearly. The string, tightening.

On the second day of the climb, they crested a shoulder of rock and saw the Thornfell massif proper for the first time — the whole vast spine of it, snow-crowned, running north beyond sight, with the free cities' last territories sprawled green and vulnerable in the valleys below. From this height Merrin could see the roads. The real ones, the wide ones. And on the northernmost road, a dust-plume.

Not weather. Dust doesn't move in columns.

"Army," Brannoc confirmed, squinting. He passed Merrin a battered brass spyglass. "Count the banners."

Merrin counted. Lost track. Counted again. "More than the fishwife said."

"Fishwives round down. Armies round up." Brannoc took the glass back. "That's the vanguard. Main force is days behind, strung out along the river road. But the vanguard's got the inquisitors attached. Fast movers." He lowered the glass and looked at Tamsin. "Three days, maybe four, and they reach the foothill towns. After that, the high country stops being empty."

"Then we have three days to get past them," Tamsin said.

"Or we get clever," Brannoc said, grinning his terrible grin, "and use the one advantage we've got that they don't."

"Which is?"

Brannoc jerked a thumb at the child crouched at the cliff-edge, head cocked, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear. "We've got a listener. And I've got a cousin who runs sheep through the high passes this time of year, who owes me more money than he's ever admitted, and who knows a route down into Harrow Vale that drops out behind the shrine-stones." He shouldered his pack. "Question is whether we spend our lead running *from* the storm — or positioning ourselves to watch it break."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the dust-column crawled north, patient as doom. Above them, the peaks of Thornfell caught the afternoon light and burned white-gold, hiding in their deep places the half-dead harmony of a broken covenant and whatever remained of the courts that had made it.

"We watch it break," she said at last. "But from higher ground. And if the inquisitors turn up the valley toward the shrine-stones—" her jaw set, "—we're gone before they crest the first ridge. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Brannoc.

"Agreed," said Wren, from atop Duchess.

Sef simply nodded, still listening, still frowning at the tightening string.

Merrin looked north one last time. The dust-plume had thickened. Somewhere inside it, men were marching under a raven-and-broken-crown banner, and somewhere behind them rolled wagons carrying iron-and-silver bells tuned to the frequency of a breaking promise, and somewhere ahead of them all lay the Deepwell, and the Hollow Court, and the song that had to be sung before the moon turned.

His voice, he thought. His ruined, mending, impossible voice. Holstered. A beacon. A key. Maybe a grave.

"Well," he said aloud, to no one in particular, to the mountain, to the gathering dark. "Let's give them something to march about."

And far below, faint but unmistakable, carried up on the evening wind from the direction of the vanguard's camp — Sef's head snapped up, eyes wide — came the first thin, wrong note of a shatterbell, ringing out across the valley like the opening bar of a funeral.

* * *

They descended through the night, taking Brannoc's cousin's route down into Harrow Vale, and by gray morning they were established in a shepherd's bothy halfway up the valley's western wall, with a view of the shrine-stones below and the valley mouth beyond. From there, over the next two days, they watched the war arrive.

It arrived the way weather arrives — first the outriders, single figures on the far road, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who own everything they survey. Then patrols, in pairs and fours. Then the main column, a brown-and-black river of men and wagons flowing up the valley road toward the foothill towns, trailing its dust-plume like a scar across the green.

And with the column, on the second afternoon, came the inquisitors.

Merrin knew them instantly, though he'd never seen one before. They didn't look like soldiers. They rode apart from the ranks, in charcoal-gray cloaks, and they carried their instruments wrapped in oilcloth slung across their backs like rifles — long, narrow cases that could hold nothing but bells. They didn't speak to the troops. They didn't camp with the troops. At dusk they pitched their own tents in a precise circle, and Merrin, watching through the spyglass from the bothy's doorway, saw one of them unwrap an instrument briefly to check it, and even at that distance, even through glass, the sight of it made his teeth ache.

"Tuned wrong on purpose," Wren murmured beside him. She'd seen him flinch. "Everything about them is wrongness given form. That's the craft of it. You can't counter-song a wrong note; it doesn't play fair."

Below, the vanguard reached the largest foothill town — Thornden, a market town of perhaps two thousand souls — and made camp outside its walls rather than storming them, because there was nothing to storm. Thornden had no walls. Thornden had a militia of forty farmers with pikes and a mayor who would read whatever proclamation was put in front of him, because the alternative was watching his town burn.

At dusk, the inquisitors marched into Thornden's square in their precise circle, and set up their instruments in front of the town hall, and rang.

Even at four miles' distance, even through stone walls, Merrin felt it.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It was the *absence* of sound — a pressure, a hollowness, as if the air itself had been scooped out and replaced with something dead. The evening birdsong cut off mid-note. The wind died. For three heartbeats the whole valley was silent in a way that silence should never be, and Merrin's chest seized around his lungs, and beside him Sef made a small sound and doubled over, and Wren grabbed the child and held on, muttering, muttering, her wards flaring faintly blue around them both.

Then it stopped. The birds resumed, raggedly. The wind returned. Sef breathed.

But in Thornden's square, the mayor had come out to meet the inquisitors, and the proclamation was read, and the town's bell — its old covenant-bell, cast generations ago, rung at weddings and harvests and the turning of the year — was taken down from the tower by soldiers while the whole population watched in silence, and loaded onto a wagon, and hauled away south.

"They're collecting them," Wren said softly. Her face was terrible. "The old bells. The covenant-bells. Every town they take, they take the bells. Melt them down, probably. Feed them to the shatterbells." She looked at Merrin, and her eyes were wet and furious. "This is what I feared, child. This is why I came. It's not an occupation. It's an *exorcism*. He means to unmake every trace of the old covenant from the land itself — every bell, every stone, every song — so that when he builds his counter-choir, there'll be nothing left to answer it."

Merrin stared down at the valley, at the tiny distant figures of soldiers loading a bell onto a wagon while its town watched, and felt something in him go very still and very cold.

He had spent five years believing his voice was a vanity. A pretty thing, ruined, best forgotten. He had drunk to forget it and sung in dockside taverns to spite himself and told everyone, including himself, that the court had taken nothing from him that mattered.

Watching that bell come down, he understood, finally and completely, that he had been lying.

They were going to take everything. Every bell, every song, every voice that wouldn't kneel. And the only thing standing between Corvane and a world with no music in it but his own was a smuggler, a witch, a fae-blooded child, a poison-eater — and a disgraced singer who had better decide, very soon, exactly what his voice was worth.

That night, after the others slept, Merrin sat alone at the bothy door with the map's notation copied onto a scrap of paper, and hummed — barely, barely, a thread of sound below the wind's threshold — the first phrase of the covenant-song.

The mountain answered.

Far above, faint as memory, a harmonic bloomed in the stone and faded, and Merrin sat in the dark with tears on his face and his hand pressed flat against the earth, feeling the land hold its breath, waiting — the way he was waiting, the way they were all waiting —

— for the moon to turn.
