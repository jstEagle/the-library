## 10. The Tollhouse Ambush

The tollhouse sat where the smuggler's road narrowed to a single cart's width, wedged between a cliff face and a drop into the grey throat of the Vale of Sorrows. It had been abandoned for years — Brannoc said so twice, and Brannoc did not say things twice unless he believed them.

"Been empty since the old toll-keeper died," he said, spitting over the edge of the path. "Nobody collects on this road. Nobody *travels* this road, except the kind of folk who don't pay tolls."

"Which is us," Sef said brightly.

"Which is why I don't like it," Brannoc said.

Tamsin stopped twenty paces out and made herself look at the building properly, the way Aldous had taught her to look at a plate of food. Not at what it was. At what it might be hiding.

It was a low stone box with a slanted roof, a door hanging by one hinge, windows like closed eyes. Smoke-stain above the hearth but no smoke now. The road in front of it was churned mud from the recent rain, and the mud told her everything the building tried not to.

Cart tracks, yes — old ones, softened by weather. But under them, fresher, half-washed: boot prints. Dozens. Coming up from the valley side and going nowhere else. Boots that had stood still a long time in one place, weight shifting, the way men shift when they've been waiting hours for something to walk into their trap.

She raised a hand. The company stilled behind her — Merrin going quiet as held breath, Wren's staff tapping once against stone and then not again, Sef's small face going serious in that uncanny way the child had, as if they'd heard something on a frequency the rest of them couldn't reach.

"Tollhouse is occupied," Tamsin murmured. "Has been since before dawn."

Brannoc squinted. "I don't see—"

"You're not supposed to. That's rather the point of an ambush." She crouched, touched the mud, rubbed it between her fingers the way she'd rub sauce between thumb and forefinger to read its fat content. "They've been standing in the lee of the east wall, out of the wind. Eight, ten men, maybe more inside. They came up the pilgrim stair from the valley — see the scuff marks? They didn't want tracks on the road itself."

Merrin crouched beside her. Up close, in the flat mountain light, he looked older than he had in Marrowgate — the taverns had been kind to him in their way, letting him be nobody, and four days of climbing had stripped that away. "Inquisitors?"

"Who else camps in the Thornfell waiting for five travelers on a road that officially doesn't exist?" She kept her voice level. Fear wanted to make it shake; she'd spent her whole life training fear out of her voice, because a taster whose hands trembled got people killed. "They knew we'd come this way."

"Nobody knew we'd come this way." Brannoc's voice dropped to gravel. "I picked this road *because* nobody knows it. I've used it eleven years and never—"

"Somebody sold us," Tamsin said quietly. "Again. We'll deal with that later. Right now we deal with them."

* * *

Wren laid out their options on a flat rock with pebbles and twigs, which would have looked charming if the options hadn't all been variations of dying.

"We go back," she said, moving a twig down the slope. "Two days' retreat to the fork at Cold Larch, then the long way round through the Wyrm's Teeth. Adds a week. Costs us the high pasture shelter, and there's snow coming — I feel it in my knees, and my knees have never lied to me about weather or men."

"Or?" Tamsin said.

"Or we go through." Wren moved the twig straight along the road, past the pebble that was the tollhouse. "Past them. There's a goat track above the house — I can see it from here if I squint and lie to myself about my eyes. It rejoins the road a mile beyond. If we're quiet, we pass above their heads and they never know."

"And if we're not quiet," Merrin said.

"Then we find out how many of them there are," Wren said, "the loud way."

Sef was staring at the tollhouse with their head tilted, the way they did when the Choir-echoes under the mountain got loud. "There's iron in there," the child said. "A lot of iron. And something else. Something that hums wrong."

"Instruments," Merrin said softly. Everyone looked at him. He was rubbing his own throat without seeming to notice, a habit he fell into whenever the subject turned to Corvane's inquisitors. "They carry instruments now. Tuning forks set in iron collars. I saw one demonstrated, before — before I left court." His jaw tightened around whatever memory he'd bitten off. "They use them to break song. To shatter it in the air. A singer caught in the ring of those things loses the note halfway out of her mouth. It doesn't hurt exactly. It's worse than hurting. It's like being *unmade* mid-word."

Tamsin watched his hands and understood, not for the first time, that Merrin Vale had not fled the court only because he refused to sing at Corvane's ascension. Something else had happened. Something with iron and tuning forks. He would tell her when telling wouldn't cost him more than he had. She was learning to wait for people. It was a new skill, and she was bad at it.

"The goat track," she said. "We go quiet. Weapons out, but nothing drawn unless drawn on. Brannoc takes point because he knows stone. Sef holds Wren's hand so the child doesn't wander toward any humming. Merrin—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because he deserved plainness, "if they have the forks, you get behind me. Whatever happens, you get behind me."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, and under it a rawness he covered fast with a crooked almost-smile. "The famous taster's shield," he said. "What do you taste your way out of, crossbow bolts?"

"I haven't yet," she said. "But I'm told I'm a quick study."

* * *

The goat track was a lie the mountain told to goats. It traversed the cliffside forty feet above the road, wide enough for one careful foot at a time, crumbling at the edges into the same grey drop that waited below the tollhouse. Wind came up the valley in slow cold pushes, carrying pine resin and woodsmoke — woodsmoke, faint, from the tollhouse chimney after all, banked low and hidden. They had been patient. That was the worst part. Someone had planned this the way Tamsin planned a tasting: methodically, with contingencies.

Brannoc went first, huge and improbable, moving over broken stone with a delicacy that always startled her. Then Sef, hand in Wren's. Then Wren herself, muttering wards under her breath like a woman complaining about prices. Then Merrin. Tamsin took the rear, because she trusted her own eyes to watch their back trail better than anyone trusted theirs.

They were thirty feet past the tollhouse — close enough that Tamsin could see, below and behind, the glint of a helmet rim carelessly left in the sun — when the child stopped.

Sef stood absolutely still on the track, head cocked, and said, in a clear carrying voice that had no business being calm:

"They know we're here. They've known since the bend. The humming changed."

Everything happened very quickly after that.

A horn sounded from the tollhouse — two notes, flat and ugly, the second note deliberately sour, and Tamsin thought with a strange detached corner of her mind: *even their signals are tuned to wound.* Men boiled out of the building and up the slope on both sides, and she counted them the way she counted ingredients — eight below, six above, two more at the treeline with the long black cases that could only hold the iron forks. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, and on every breast the sigil of Duke Corvane: a black key crossed with a quill. The lock-and-ledger. *Everything opens. Everything is owed.*

"Above!" Brannoc roared, and then the rocks started coming down.

Not arrows. Rocks — fist-sized, hurled with soldierly economy, meant to drive them off the track and onto the road, into the ring. Tamsin flattened against the cliff as a stone cracked off the wall where her head had been. Below, the eight were spreading into a crescent across the road, unhurried, professional. One of them unslung a case and began assembling something that gleamed dull grey even in the flat light.

"Wren!" Tamsin shouted. "Can you ward the track?"

"For about as long as it takes you to regret asking!" The old witch planted her staff and began to speak words that made the air thicken, and a shimmer crawled across the goat track like heat-haze. A thrown rock struck it and burst apart into grit and a sound like a snapped harp string. Wren grunted as if she'd taken the blow herself. "That cost me! Decide something!"

Tamsin decided.

Going forward meant the treeline and the forks. Going back meant the bend and whoever had been left watching it — and there would be someone; there was always someone covering the door. Staying meant standing on a warded shelf of crumbling rock until Wren's strength ran out, which by the grey look of her would be soon.

So Tamsin did the thing that was hardest and most obvious, the thing a careful person would never do, and went *down* — straight down the cliff face toward the road, toward the eight, toward the center of the trap, because the center of a trap is the one place built on the assumption that no one walks into it on purpose.

She heard Merrin shout her name. She heard Brannoc begin an oath in dwarvish that was probably anatomically ambitious. She climbed down hand over hand through scrub and loose shale, faster than sense, and hit the road in a crouch with shale dust smoking off her shoulders, ten feet from the nearest inquisitor.

He was young. That was her first stupid, useless observation. Young, square-jawed, startled — he'd expected quarry driven into his blade, not a woman dropping out of the sky into his personal space. He recovered well, all things considered; he drew and lunged in one motion, and the blade was not a killing blade.

She knew that in the half-second it took to move, because Tamsin Thackeray had spent nineteen years learning to read intent faster than action, and the blade told her everything. Short, narrow, double-edged, and — she caught the smell of it as it whispered past her cheek — coated. Oily, sweet-bitter, a resinous reek she knew the way other people knew their mothers' cooking. *Thessaly root and dreamshade,* her mind catalogued, cool as a kitchen ledger. *Enough to drop a horse. Enough to stop a heart in nine breaths.*

Meant to capture her alive.

The realization should have frightened her. Instead it opened a cold clear space in her head, the space she cooked in, the space where the world slowed down and became a problem with an answer.

She wasn't afraid of the drug. That was the joke of her life, the punchline nineteen years in the making: she was the one person on this mountain who did not need to fear a poisoned blade. Every dose Aldous had ever fed her, every escalating cup and crumb and carefully laced wine, had built in her blood a fortress against exactly this. Whoever these men were, whoever had sent them, they had done their research — and their research had missed the single most important fact about her.

The blade grazed her forearm as she turned inside his guard. A thin line of fire, the drug sliding into her blood like a guest through an unlocked door — and finding the doors barred, barred, barred, every one, nineteen years of locked doors.

He stared at her. She felt the moment he realized something was wrong: she hadn't staggered. Hadn't swayed. The graze should have put her on her knees by his count of three, and instead she was looking at him with the mild professional interest of a woman inspecting a suspect dish.

"That's not the good stuff," she said, and hit him in the throat with the edge of her hand.

He went down hard. She took his knife out of his belt before he finished falling — a habit, taking the weapon, the way other people said a prayer — and turned to face the rest.

The seven remaining inquisitors had gone very still. The man with the iron instrument had stopped assembling it. Somewhere above, on the goat track, Wren's ward shimmered and Brannoc was roaring down the cliff face like an avalanche with opinions, and Merrin's voice cut through it all, high and clear and *singing* — a single sustained note that made the air shiver, that made two of the grey-cloaks flinch and grab at their ears.

And Tamsin stood in the middle of the road with stolen blood on her sleeve and someone else's knife in her hand, and understood, with terrible clarity, that everything had just changed.

They hadn't come to kill her. Killing her would have been easy — arrows from the treeline, a rockfall, a blade in the dark on some lonely stretch of road. They had come with a drugged blade and a plan to take her breathing, because someone wanted her alive. Wanted her specifically. Wanted her enough to burn a dozen trained men on a mountain pass for the privilege.

*Why?*

The question rang in her like a struck bell, and under it, fainter, another sound — Sef's voice, from far above, thin with distance and something else, something urgent: "*Tamsin!*"

One of the inquisitors broke the stillness. He was older than the others, scarred at the jaw, and he didn't rush her the way the young one had. He circled, blade low, and said, "Stand down, Thackeray. No one needs to die today. My orders are to bring you in gentle. Don't make me amend them."

"Gentle," Tamsin repeated. Her arm burned where the blade had kissed it, and beneath the burn her blood moved slow and thick and utterly unbothered, processing the dreamshade like a course of weak tea. "You stab a woman with enough poison to kill a horse and call it gentle."

"Orders," he said, and there was something almost like regret in it, which somehow made it worse.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I've heard a lot about orders lately."

He came at her then, and he was good — better than the boy, better than any tavern brawler, trained in the same school of economical violence she'd been taught in the palace cellars. But he was expecting a drugged opponent, a softening target, a woman whose limbs would start lying to her any moment now. And his arms and legs kept waiting for a collapse that was never coming, and that expectation, that tiny borrowed second of confusion each time she stayed upright, stayed fast, stayed *hers* —

That was enough.

She fought the way she tasted: reading, adjusting, committing late and completely. She let his third strike open her left side and stepped through it, drove the stolen knife into the meat of his sword shoulder, and twisted free as he screamed. First blood. *Her* first blood given, not taken, not swallowed, not survived — *given*, in defense of herself and the strange fragile family scattered on the cliff above her, and she felt it land somewhere in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The wounded inquisitor staggered back. The others wavered — and then the iron instrument finally sang.

The man with the case struck it, and the sound that came out was not a sound. It was an absence shaped like a sound, a hole in the air, and Tamsin saw Merrin, forty feet up on the track, fold forward as if punched, his beautiful note severed mid-breath. Wren cried out. Even Sef doubled over, clutching their chest.

Rage arrived in Tamsin fully formed, wearing no disguise at all.

She crossed the road in six strides while the fork-ringer struggled to strike a second tone, and she did not kill him — later she would take a fierce, complicated pride in that — but she broke the instrument across her knee, iron and wire shrieking, and threw the pieces into the gorge, and stood over him breathing hard with the knife in her fist and her poisoned-and-unpoisonable blood singing in her veins.

"Tell whoever sent you," she said, and her voice came out low and steady and stranger to her than any voice she'd owned before, "that the next time they want me alive, they should send someone who asks nicely."

Above, Brannoc came down the cliff like judgment, and the inquisitors broke. Professional to the last, they withdrew in order, dragging their wounded, fading into the treeline with the discipline of men who would report everything and be believed. In ninety seconds the road was empty except for the groaning, the blood, and the five of them.

* * *

Afterward, they regrouped a mile on, in a hollow of granite where the wind couldn't find them. Wren cleaned and bound the gash on Tamsin's arm without a word about how shallow it was, though her eyes said she'd noticed — noticed the graze, the coating, the fact that Tamsin stood pale but steady as a kitchen post when she should have been dead three times over.

"He used dreamshade," Wren said at last, quietly, testing the words. "Thessaly base. I smelled it from the track."

"I know. I tasted it in the air." Tamsin flexed her fingers. They obeyed. Everything obeyed. "It doesn't work on me."

"I know it doesn't work on you." Wren tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels, and looked at Tamsin for a long, long moment with an expression the taster couldn't read — and Tamsin could read almost anything, having spent her life reading faces across plates of food that might kill her. This expression was older than the others. Sadder. Like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish.

"What?" Tamsin said.

"Nothing," Wren said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and neither of them said anything else about it.

Merrin sat apart, wrapped in his cloak, working his jaw silently, chasing a note that had been torn out of the air. Sef leaned against him, and for once the child looked less than certain of everything. Brannoc stood watch on the rocks, axe across his knees, and when Tamsin joined him he spoke without turning.

"Eleven years," he said. "That road has been mine for eleven years, and today it had a dozen of Corvane's hounds sitting on it, waiting for us by name." He spat. "Somebody sold us, girl. Somebody close."

"I know."

"They wanted you breathing."

"I know that too."

Brannoc was silent a moment. "Doesn't that frighten you more than the alternative?"

Tamsin looked down at her bandaged arm, at the blood seeping rust-brown through the linen, at the body that had drunk poison meant to fell a horse and asked politely whether there was more. Somewhere behind her, Merrin was humming fragments, rebuilding his shattered note piece by piece; Sef was listening to something under the ground; Wren was watching Tamsin with that unfinished arithmetic in her eyes.

"No," Tamsin said slowly, and found, to her surprise, that it was true. "It makes me angry. There's a reason they need me alive, Brannoc. A reason worth a dozen blades on a dead road. And I'm done waiting for someone to tell me what it is." She turned toward Wren. "Old mother. You looked at me like you already know. So you're going to tell me — tonight, by the fire, every word of it. What am I? What did they *make* me?"

Wren met her gaze, and the arithmetic finished behind her eyes, and the answer she gave was only three words long, and every one of them landed like a tolling bell.

"Come sit, child."
