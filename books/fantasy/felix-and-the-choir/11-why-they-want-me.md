## 11. Why They Want Me

Wren made me take off my shirt.

Not in the way a healer asks — gentle, apologetic, eyes elsewhere. She said it like a woman who had already decided what she'd find and wanted to be proven wrong for once. "Off," she said, and set her lamp on the tollhouse table so its light fell across my arms, my throat, the ladder of old scars down my ribs.

We'd barricaded ourselves in the upper room of the ruined tollhouse since the ambush. Brannoc was below with Sef, wedging the last of the shutters and muttering about sightlines. Merrin sat by the cold hearth with his arms around his knees, pretending not to watch. The drugged blade's residue still itched under the bandage on my shoulder where the inquisitor's knife had kissed me — a kiss that should have put me under for a day and a half, and instead had faded inside an hour.

"Sit," Wren said. "And don't talk while I work. Talking is how people lie to healers."

"I don't lie."

"You lie beautifully. That's worse." She pulled up a stool, cracked her knuckles one at a time like an old door opening, and took my wrist.

Her fingers were dry as paper and twice as strong. She turned my arm over, traced the veins from elbow to palm, and pressed here and there with the flat of her thumb, watching my face each time. I gave her nothing. Twenty years in the king's kitchens teaches you to give nothing — not when the broth is bitter, not when your tongue goes numb, not when the man across the table is watching to see if you flinch.

"Hm," Wren said.

"That's not encouraging."

"It wasn't meant to be. Hold still." She leaned close and sniffed along my forearm, then my throat, then — without asking — behind my ear, and whatever she smelled there made her sit back sharply, as if I'd bitten her.

"What?"

"Quiet." She was already rummaging in her pack: a stub of chalk, a clay cup, three stoppered vials that clinked like teeth. She poured a measure of something clear into the cup, dipped the chalk in it, and drew a line down the inside of my forearm.

The line went black.

Not gray. Not smudged. Black as ink, black as the water at the bottom of a well, and it sank into my skin as she watched, vanishing beneath the surface like something diving.

Wren did not say anything for a long moment. In the silence I could hear Merrin stop breathing by the hearth.

"Wren," I said. "What is that?"

"A truth-tincture. Chalk and silverroot. It turns dark in the presence of certain substances in the blood." She set the cup down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Mostly it's used to detect poisons. In a healthy body the line stays pale or fades in a minute."

"And mine?"

"Yours drank it."

* * *

She made me tell her everything after that. Not the map, not the Choir — she didn't care about any of that yet. She wanted the kitchens. She wanted the beginning.

So I told her, because by then I think we both understood that whatever she suspected, she was going to dig it out of me one way or another, and better with my consent than without.

I was brought to the palace at four years old. I never knew from where. There was a woman who handed me over at the servants' gate — I remember her hands, rough as bark, and the smell of woodsmoke in her shawl — and then she was gone, and Master Aldous was there instead, kneeling down to my height with that patient spymaster's smile, saying, *You have an important job now, little one. You're going to keep the king safe.*

They fed me first. That's what I remember most clearly, absurdly: they fed me before anything else, a bowl of barley porridge sweetened with honey, and I ate every bite because I was four and starving, and only afterward did Aldous explain that some of what I'd eat from now on would not be kind to me.

"They started small," I told Wren. My voice came out flat, recital-flat, the way I'd told it to myself a thousand times in the dark of the garret without ever calling it what it was. "Bitterleaf, first. A drop in milk. Then nightshade tincture, diluted. Then things I never learned the names of — Aldous kept the ledger, not me. By the time I was seven I could taste arsenic in wine at concentrations that killed the kitchen cat. By ten I'd survived eleven full doses of things that had no business being survivable."

"The scars?" Wren asked.

"Some from the early years, before the immunities took hold properly. The rest—" I touched the long seam along my ribs. "Testing. Twice a year, a physician would come and open a vein and introduce measured doses directly, to confirm the tolerance held. They called them my 'assessments.' I thought everyone had assessments. I thought everyone grew up learning which deaths tasted like almonds and which like copper."

Merrin made a sound low in his throat. I didn't look at him. If I looked at him I would stop talking, and I needed to finish, because Wren's face had gone very still and very old, and old faces going still is how you know the worst is coming.

"And the records," Wren said quietly. "You said the spymaster kept a ledger."

"He kept everything. Doses, dates, reactions. I saw it once, when I was sixteen — he left it out on his desk and I read it upside down, the way he'd taught me to read documents across a room. It wasn't just a record. It was a recipe. Every entry built on the last. He wasn't training an immunity, Wren. He was *brewing* one. Compound by compound, year by year, like a tincture steeped in a child instead of a jar."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's what I thought." She got up, went to the window, checked the dark outside through a crack in the shutter — habit, or a need to move — and came back. "Now hold out your arm again. This next part matters."

* * *

What she did next, she did in silence, and I let her because I understood we were past the point where questions helped.

She opened one of her vials — the contents smelled of crushed ferns and something colder underneath, something mineral, like snow on stone — and painted a thin line of it along my forearm with a feather. Then she held my arm toward the lamp.

Where the salve touched my skin, my veins rose up visible. Not the way veins do in a hot bath — this was different, wrong, luminous. For a heartbeat I could see the whole map of myself: the blue rivers running from wrist to shoulder, and threaded through them, fine as wire, a second pattern. A darker current moving inside the blood, slow and deliberate, like smoke underwater.

"There it is," Wren breathed. "There it is, by all the dead gods' patience. Look at it, girl. Look."

"I'm looking. What am I looking at?"

"Thorn-essence. Fae-blood poison, refined. It doesn't occur in any human pharmacopoeia — humans can't even harvest it safely; it has to be taken from the verges where the fae realm bleeds through, and it kills ordinary collectors within the year." Her finger hovered above my skin without touching. "It's in every vein you have. Diluted past lethality, layered over twenty years until your body learned to carry it the way a riverbed carries a river. You're not immune to poison, Tamsin. You're *infused* with it. You are, quite possibly, the most toxic human being alive."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the shutter. Somewhere below us, Brannoc dropped something heavy and swore at it in two languages.

"Toxic to whom?" I asked. And I knew. Even as I asked it I knew, because the pieces had been assembling themselves since the tollhouse, since the blade meant to capture rather than kill, since the stranger at the salt gate dying with a map in his fist and the words *the Choir remembers* — but knowing a thing in your bones and hearing it said aloud are two different wounds, and only one of them bleeds.

"Fae," Wren said. "Toxic to the fae. Their blood is song; yours is the note that breaks it. One scratch from you, one drop of yours in their wine, in their wells, in the air of their courts — and a fae sickens. Prolonged exposure kills. Your blood is a weapon against the oldest peoples of this mountain, girl, and someone spent twenty years and no small fortune making sure of it."

I looked at my arm. The luminous map of my veins was fading now, the salve wearing off, the dark thread sinking back where I couldn't see it. But I could feel it. That was the horror of it — I could feel it everywhere, suddenly, the way you feel your own heartbeat only when someone names it. Twenty years of tasting. Twenty years of numb tongues and assessment scars and surviving, and I had called it discipline. I had called it *duty*. I had been proud of it.

Aldous teaching me to read upside down. Aldous kneeling at the gate. Aldous keeping the ledger, entry by entry, brewing me like a tincture in a jar, and all the while calling me *little one* in that patient voice, and me believing — gods help me, believing to the age of thirty-one — that the crown had taken in an orphan out of charity.

"They didn't raise me," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "They *manufactured* me. I'm not a food-taster who survived. I'm a delivery system. I'm a poisoned dart they taught to walk and pour wine."

"Tamsin." Merrin, from the hearth. Soft. Careful, the way you're careful near a drawn bow.

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't be kind to me right now. I need to not be handled for a moment."

He nodded and looked away, and I loved him a little for it, and hated that I noticed loving him, because noticing anything felt like a betrayal of the girl in the ledger — the four-year-old with the honey porridge, who hadn't done anything to anyone except be small and available.

* * *

Wren let me sit with it. That was her gift, I was learning — she knew when to speak and when to let silence do the surgery. She cleaned her chalk and stoppered her vials and moved around the room putting things right, and I sat at the table with my shirt in my lap like a child, watching the lamp flame, doing sums.

Here is what the sums said.

Corvane's inquisitors carried instruments designed to shatter fae-song. Corvane marched on the free cities. Corvane wanted me alive — the blade at the tollhouse had been a captor's blade, dosed to sleep me, not kill me, and the inquisitor had aimed for the shoulder, not the heart. And the covenant between the realms lay broken, and the fae courts of Thornfell had withdrawn behind their verges for a generation, grieving their slaughtered singers.

You didn't brew a weapon against the fae unless you intended to use it against the fae.

You didn't spend twenty years steeping a child in thorn-essence for a war you planned to avoid.

"They're not hunting me because I stole a map," I said at last. "The map is an excuse. A convenience. They've always known what I am. Aldous built me. Corvane inherited me the way you inherit a sword from a dead man's estate. The question was never whether they'd come for me. The question was when I'd finally be useful enough to deploy."

"And the stranger at the gate?" Merrin asked. "Felix. If that's his name. Why hand the map to *you*, of all people? Did he know?"

"I don't know." But I remembered the weight of his hand on mine, the terrible precision of a dying man choosing where to put his last strength. *The Choir remembers.* Not *run*. Not *hide it*. Remember. As if he'd known exactly what I was. As if the map hadn't been given to a smuggler or a scholar or a loyalist, but to the one person in all the human realms whose blood could matter to the fae — the bridge nobody had planned on, the weapon that might refuse to fire.

Or maybe he'd simply been dying and I'd been nearest. Hope does that. It takes the smallest coin and mints it into a destiny.

"Your father," Wren said abruptly.

I looked up. "What about him?"

"Brannoc says your father ran the passes. Smuggler, guide — a man who knew every fold of Thornfell better than the fae themselves. And you were raised in the palace from four years old, with no record of where you came from." Her eyes were sharp as awls. "Girl, who do you suppose arranged for a smuggler's daughter to be standing at a servants' gate with a woodsmoke-smelling woman and no past? Who benefits from a child with mountain blood and no kin to ask questions?"

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. I gripped the table's edge.

"You think my father sold me."

"I think," Wren said gently, "that someone did. And I think your father spent the rest of his life running those passes for reasons he never explained, and owing debts he never named, and that Brannoc's loyalty to his memory might be worth more than gold before this is done. But that's a wound for another day. Tonight you have a nearer one."

She was right. She was horribly right, and I set the thought of my father down carefully beside the thought of Felix and the thought of the honey porridge, a little row of griefs I couldn't afford to carry all at once.

"So what am I?" I asked. "Truly. Say it plainly. I've had enough of plain things arriving dressed as kindness."

Wren came and stood over me, and for the first time since I'd met her she looked uncertain — an old woman weighing how much truth a person can hold before it becomes a different kind of poison.

"You're the reason the covenant can't simply be reforged," she said. "And you may be the only reason it can be reforged at all. The fae will smell what you are the moment you cross the verge. To them you'll reek of thorn-essence and human treachery — a walking atrocity, brewed in a palace kitchen to murder their kind. They'll want you dead on sight, and they'll be right to." She paused. "Unless. Unless the thing that makes you deadly also makes you necessary. Poison and medicine are the same root, girl. It's only the dose and the intent that differ. Whether your blood closes the door forever or becomes the key—" she spread her dry hands, "—that depends on choices not yet made. Yours. Theirs. Maybe the dead stranger's."

Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell with a sound almost like voices — a long, low harmonic moaning through the broken tollhouse eaves, and I saw Merrin tilt his head toward it the way a man turns toward a name being called.

Below us, boots on the stairs. Brannoc, two steps up, stopping. His voice came through the door, gruff and stripped of all its usual joke:

"Tamsin. You'll want to see this. Riders on the north road — six of them, lanterns hooded, moving quiet. And they're not riding like soldiers." A pause. "They're riding like they know exactly where we are."

Wren and I looked at each other across the lamp.

"Then someone told them," I said, and reached for my coat.
