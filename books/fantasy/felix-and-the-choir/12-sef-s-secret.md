## 12. Sef's Secret

Sef heard the mountain singing before anyone else woke.

It came up through the soles of their feet first, a hum in the stone like a cat purring under a blanket. Then it climbed — through the bones of their ankles, their knees, the hollow of their chest — until it sat behind their teeth like a word they'd forgotten they knew.

They lay still on their bedroll between Brannoc's snoring bulk and Old Wren's patchwork cloak and listened to it properly, the way you listened for footsteps when you weren't supposed to be anywhere at all. The song had been getting louder every night since the tollhouse. Three nights louder now. It wasn't words. It was better than words. It was the feeling of being called by name by someone who had known you your whole life and whom you had never once met.

*Come down,* it said, without saying anything at all. *Come home.*

Sef pressed their palms flat against the cave floor and let the hum run up into their fingers. Warm. The stone was warm here, though the night outside was cold enough to crack a bucket. Everything below was warm. Everything below was *waiting*.

That was the problem.

They had been careful. They were always careful. Careful was how you stayed alive when you were small and alone and your ears did things other people's ears didn't. You kept your eyes dull and your mouth shut and you never, ever told anyone that you could hear the second voice underneath the first one — the voice under the fire crackle, under the river, under a person talking about the weather. Everyone had two voices if you listened right. Most people just didn't know it.

But this wasn't a person's voice. This was the mountain itself, and it was getting louder, and last night Wren had caught them humming along.

Wren hadn't said anything. She'd just looked at them over the fire with her milky old eyes, and she'd gone very still, the way animals go still, and Sef had known: *she knows.* And this morning Brannoc had asked Tamsin why the map's marks made Merrin go quiet, and Tamsin had looked at Sef too, quick as a knife going back in its sheath, and Sef had understood that everyone knew, or nearly everyone, and that the secret had gotten too big to carry alone.

Secrets got heavy. That was another thing nobody told you. You carried one long enough and it started carrying *you*, dragging you places you didn't want to go.

So Sef decided, sitting up in the dark with the mountain humming through their ribs, that tonight they would put it down.

* * *

They waited until after supper, because supper mattered. Brannoc had shot something fat and stupid out of the pines, and Merrin had made a sauce from nothing — a handful of berries, some pine needles, a spoon of honey he'd been hoarding like a dragon — and for a little while the cave smelled like a home instead of a hiding place. Even Tamsin had smiled, the small crooked smile she did, like smiling was a skill she'd learned late and practiced badly.

Sef watched them all across the firelight and tried to memorize it. Brannoc mending a strap with his huge clumsy-careful hands. Wren dozing upright, her wards strung around the cave mouth like washing on a line, faintly glowing whenever the wind touched them. Merrin humming without noticing he was doing it — he was always humming, low, half-swallowed, like a man who'd been taught that music was contraband. And Tamsin, sitting closest to the entrance because she always did, tasting the air the way she tasted food, checking it for poison.

*These are mine,* Sef thought, and the thought scared them worse than any inquisitor. Things you owned could be taken away. That was the whole lesson of Sef's entire life.

"Right," they said, before they could lose their nerve. Their voice came out smaller than they wanted. They cleared their throat and said it again, bigger. "I have to tell you something, and you're going to be angry."

The fire popped. Brannoc's needle stopped mid-stitch. Tamsin turned from the cave mouth, and her whole body went quiet in that particular way of hers, everything gathered inward, listening.

Wren opened her eyes. "Say it, child."

"The mountain." Sef wrapped their arms around their knees. "You keep asking what I hear. When we stopped at the falls, and when we crossed the scree, and you keep looking at me when the map does its — its thing. So. I'll tell you. But you have to let me finish before anybody decides anything, because people decide things fast about me and then I get left places."

Nobody spoke. Merrin set down his bowl. Tamsin came and sat cross-legged across the fire from Sef, close enough to talk low, far enough not to loom. She was good at distances, Tamsin. Somebody had taught her that somebody else's space was a kind of food you shouldn't touch unasked.

"I can hear it," Sef said. "The Choir. Not the way you mean — not echoes, not old songs in the stone. I hear it *now*. It's still going. Down there. Under us. It never stopped, it just got thin, like a fire down to coals." They swallowed. "And it knows me. It's calling me. By my — not my name exactly. By whatever I am. It says come home."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow on a roof.

"It's been calling since the tollhouse," Sef went on, faster now, words tumbling. "Louder every night. And I know what you're thinking, because everybody thinks it eventually, so I'll say it myself: I'm fae-blooded. Part-fae. I don't know how much or from who — I was left at a shrine when I was four with a note that lied about my age, which is a funny thing to lie about, but people lie about me a lot. My ears. My eyes do the thing in the dark. I heal fast. And I can hear the second voice under everything." They lifted their chin. "There. Now you know. Now you can start deciding whether I'm useful or a burden, and I'd rather be useful, but I want it on record that those aren't the only two choices."

Brannoc broke the silence first, because Brannoc always did. He set down the strap and the needle and rubbed his beard, and his voice came out rough as gravel. "Who's been lying about ye, lad? Names. I'll want names later."

"That's not the point—"

"Aye, it's not the point *yet*." He pointed the needle at Sef, almost gently. "Go on. Finish."

Merrin hadn't moved. He was staring at Sef with an expression Sef had never seen on him — hunger, almost, the look of a starving man watching bread go by. "You hear it *live*," he said quietly. "Not the notation. Not the memory of it. The actual—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, hands closing on his knees. "Sorry. Go on."

"And," Sef said, and this was the hard part, the part they'd chewed on all day like gristle, "it's getting louder because we're getting closer, and I think — I think it's not just calling me. I think it's calling *through* me. Like I'm a door someone left open. Last night I woke up standing at the cave mouth facing downhill and I don't remember getting up. Wren's wards buzzed when I crossed them. I'm sorry, Wren. I didn't mean to."

"I know ye didn't," Wren said. Her voice was very soft. "The mountain doesn't ask, child. That's the oldest thing wrong with it."

"So that's it." Sef spread their hands. "That's the secret. I can lead you down there faster than any map, because the map is just writing down what I can hear. But I also might not be able to stop walking when it tells me to walk. And I wanted you to know both halves before anybody started treating me like a compass instead of a person." They looked around the fire, at each face in turn, making themselves do it. "That's all. You can be angry now."

* * *

Nobody was angry. Somehow that was worse.

Tamsin spoke first, and her voice was careful, level, the voice of someone handling something that might go off. "Thank you for telling us. Truly." A pause. "How long have you known what you are?"

"Known? Since the shrine woman pulled my ears and crossed herself. *Suspected* since forever." Sef shrugged. "Does it change anything?"

"No," Tamsin said — too fast, Sef thought. "No. It doesn't."

But it changed the shape of the room. Sef could feel it, the way you felt weather coming. Brannoc had gone from *lad* to *lad-who-is-also-a-key*. Merrin had gone from friend to — no, not that, Merrin would die before he did that, but there was a new carefulness in him, a man handling a instrument worth more than his life. Even Wren, who had probably guessed days ago, had rearranged herself somehow, sitting straighter, watching Sef the way she watched her own potions.

Only Tamsin moved closer instead of further. She shifted around the fire and sat down next to Sef, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and said nothing at all, and that was the most comforting thing anyone had done all night.

It was Merrin who broke the new silence, and he broke it carefully, feeling his way. "If you can hear the living song — the actual line, the true melody under all the notation — then you're not just useful. You may be *necessary*. The map gives us the road. You'd give us the music." He hesitated. "What does it sound like? The call. If you can describe it."

Sef considered. Describing it was like describing the color red to someone in a cellar. "Like being remembered," they said finally. "You know how nobody remembers you, ever, your whole life, and then one day something knows you — completely, all the way down, even the parts you hide — and it's glad?" They hugged their knees tighter. "It's like that. Every night. All night. It's the best thing I've ever heard and it scares me stupid."

"Why does it scare you?" Tamsin asked.

"Because it doesn't care what happens to the rest of me," Sef said. "Only the fae part. It wants *that* part home. The rest of me is just — packaging." They heard Merrin inhale sharply. "Don't look like that. It's fine. I've known I was packaging my whole life. Shrines take in fae-blooded kids because we're lucky charms. Towns feed us because we're good luck. Bad men chase us because our blood sells. Nobody's ever wanted the packaging." They glanced sideways at Tamsin. "Well. Almost nobody."

Tamsin's jaw did the tight thing. She reached over and, very briefly, squeezed Sef's shoulder — one squeeze, hard, then done, like a signature.

Then Brannoc ruined it, in the way Brannoc ruined things, which was by being honest. "So we use it," he said heavily. "Aye?"

The word landed in the fire and burned there.

"We've a war coming down on us," Brannoc went on, staring into the flames, refusing to look at Sef while he said it, which Sef appreciated more than he knew. "Corvane's host is marching, the free cities are pissing themselves, and the only chance any of us has is reforging this covenant before the moon turns. And the child can hear the way. I'm not saying we spend him. I'm saying we'd be fools not to *use* what the gods gave us. There's a difference, and I'd like someone to tell me where the line sits, because I can't see it from where I'm standing."

"You're looking at it," Wren said. "The line. You're sitting next to it."

"I know where he's sitting, old woman. I'm asking about the *other* line."

"There's only one line," Wren said, "and it runs through the middle of every one of us. You cross it the first time you stop asking and start taking. Ask him —" she nodded at Sef — "*with* him, every step, and you haven't crossed it. Decide for him once, and you have, and no amount of good intentions walks you back over."

Brannoc grunted. It wasn't agreement. It was the sound of a man putting a hard thing on a high shelf to deal with later.

Merrin leaned forward. "There's a third option nobody's named yet." His voice had changed — the tavern-warmth stripped out of it, leaving something harder underneath, the court singer who had once refused a duke to his face. "We could turn back. Take Sef somewhere the call can't reach — south, past Marrowgate, over the sea if it comes to that — and find the Deepwell ourselves off the map alone. Slower. Maybe too slow. Maybe impossible; the notation's incomplete without a live ear. But it keeps him whole."

"And hands Corvane the mountain while we walk," Brannoc rumbled.

"Maybe. Or maybe we arrive late but *arrive*, with all our souls intact, which is more than Corvane can say."

"And if we're too late?" Tamsin asked quietly. "If the passes fall while we're being honorable at a walking pace?"

"Then we'll have lost," Merrin said, "but we'll know exactly what we paid for it."

Sef listened to them argue over their head — over their head, around their head, their head the table the argument was held on — and felt something twist slowly in their chest. It wasn't anger, exactly. It was older than anger. It was the shrine feeling again, the feeling of being discussed in the third person by adults who meant well, the feeling of being a *question* instead of an answer.

"Stop," they said.

They didn't raise their voice. They didn't need to. Something in it must have carried, some second voice of their own, because all four of them went silent and turned.

"You're doing it already," Sef said. "All of you. Deciding. Brannoc's decided to use me and feels bad about it. Merrin's decided to protect me and feels noble about it. Wren's decided to referee. And Tamsin—" they faltered, because Tamsin was the one whose opinion actually hurt, "—Tamsin hasn't decided anything, which means she's waiting to see which way the wind blows, which is what tasters do, I guess."

Tamsin flinched. Actually flinched, small and fast, like a struck animal. Sef hadn't known she could.

"But here's the thing none of you asked." Sef stood up. They were shaking a little, and they let themselves shake, because hiding it took energy they needed for words. "I'm coming. All the way down. To the Deepwell, to whatever's down there, to the end of it. Not because the mountain calls me — the mountain can shout itself hoarse. Because *you're* down there. Because for the first time in my whole rotten lucky-charm life, there are people I'd walk toward instead of away from." Their voice cracked on the last of it and they hated that and pushed through anyway. "So don't vote on me. Don't weigh me. Just — ask me. Going forward. Every time. That's all. That's the whole price. Ask me, and mean it, and I'll walk into the dark with you gladly."

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines, and beneath it, faint and patient, the mountain hummed its long warm note.

Brannoc cleared his throat. "Can't promise I'll always like yer answers, lad."

"Don't have to like them. Just have to ask for them."

"Aye." He stuck out one enormous hand, solemn as an oath. Sef shook it; it swallowed theirs whole. "Asked proper, then: will ye guide us down, knowing what it costs ye?"

"Yes," Sef said. "Gladly."

Merrin rose too. He didn't offer a hand; he offered a bow, deep and formal, the bow he must once have given kings, and it was so absurd and so sincere that Sef laughed despite everything. "Will you teach us what you hear, as we go?" Merrin asked. "So we're not following blind?"

"Yes."

Wren simply nodded, once, as if a debt had been settled that the others hadn't known existed.

Which left Tamsin. She stood, and for a moment she seemed to be fighting for words the way she'd once fought for breath after a poisoned cup. Then she knelt — knelt, on the cold stone, eye to eye with Sef, which was ridiculous and unbearable and exactly right.

"Sef," she said. "Do you want to go home?"

And Sef understood, suddenly, that it was a real question with two real answers, and that whichever one they gave, she would stand guard over it.

"Not the mountain's home," they said slowly, feeling their way. "Not the call. But — wherever this ends up. Wherever you all end up. If there's room." They wiped their nose with their sleeve, fiercely. "Is there room?"

"There's room," Tamsin said. "There was always room. I just didn't know it until recently myself."

She stood, and the matter was settled, and Sef felt lighter than they had in years — the secret handed over, the price named, the family asked and answering.

It lasted until Wren stiffened.

The old witch had risen halfway, one hand pressed flat against the cave wall, her head cocked, her clouded eyes wide and white in the firelight. Her wards at the mouth of the cave had begun to glow — not the soft wind-touch flicker, but a hard steady burn, all of them at once, like a scream held in colored glass.

"What is it?" Tamsin's hand went to her knife. "Inquisitors?"

"No." Wren's voice came out thin. "No, girl. Worse, and stranger." She turned her head slowly toward Sef, and her face was full of something Sef had never seen directed at them before: fear. "The mountain's stopped calling, child. It's stopped calling because something down there has started *answering* — and it's using your voice to do it."
