## 13. The Hollow Court

The map stopped making sense a mile before they found the door.

Merrin had learned to read it by then — not the ink, but the notation beneath the ink, the little runs of marks that Wren called *covenant-script* and that looked, to his eye, like nothing so much as a melody flattened onto parchment. He'd spent eleven years reading music for a living and three more reading rooms full of strangers for his supper. The map did both at once. It wasn't telling them where to go so much as *singing* where to go, each phrase resolving toward the next like a line of a song he almost knew.

But here, in the high shoulder of Thornfell where the pines gave way to bare grey stone, the notation simply ended. A rest. A long held silence across half a page.

"It's wrong," Tamsin said, crouched over the parchment with her jaw set the way it set whenever she was about to be right about something unpleasant. "There should be a pass here. I've run this ridge twice with Brannoc's father's cargo. There's nothing but cliff."

"There's never been nothing but cliff," Brannoc rumbled behind her. He had his axe out, which he did when he was nervous, though he'd have denied it under torture. "There's been a door."

Sef had gone quiet an hour ago — quieter than usual, which for Sef was saying something. The child stood at the cliff face with one palm flat against the stone, head tilted, hair stirring in a wind that Merrin couldn't feel. Their lips moved without sound.

"Sef?" Tamsin said.

"They're singing," Sef said. "Down there. Slow. Like they're asleep." The child looked up, and their eyes had gone the color of deep water. "It's cold down there. But it isn't empty."

Wren leaned on her staff and squinted at the rock with the expression of a woman reading a bill she suspected she couldn't pay. "Covenant doors don't open for hands," she said. "They open for voices. Someone has to ask."

Everyone looked at Merrin.

He'd known they would. He'd felt it building for days, the way you feel a note approaching that sits just past the top of your range — dread and hunger braided together. He was the singer. That was the whole reason Tamsin had pulled him out of a Marrowgate tavern with a bottle still in his hand and a debt on his name. His voice had opened doors once before. It had also closed the most important one he'd ever been offered, when he'd refused to sing Corvane's ascension and traded a court appointment for a dockside stool and the slow erosion of everything he'd built.

"Right," he said. "Someone has to ask."

He stepped up to the cliff face. Up close, the stone wasn't stone the way he'd expected. There were lines in it — faint, deliberate, running in curves too regular for weather. Carvings, worn nearly to nothing, or perhaps never meant for eyes at all. He put his hand where Sef's had been. Cold came up through his palm, but underneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something *moved*.

"What do I ask?"

"You don't ask for anything," Wren said. "That's the trap everyone falls into. You offer. The opening verse is a gift, not a request. Sing what you'd bring to the table, and let the door decide if it's hungry."

Merrin laughed — a short, ugly sound. "I've got nothing to bring. My voice is worth four coppers a night and a room that smells of fish."

"Then sing that," Wren said, unsmiling. "Truth opens doors better than beauty. Ask any god."

He closed his eyes. He thought about the first time he'd sung in front of anyone who mattered — nine years old, voice like a struck bell, and the whole court leaning forward, and old Queen Isolde saying afterward, *that boy will carry our words further than our armies ever marched.* He thought about how it had felt to be carried instead of carrying. He thought about the night he'd said no to Corvane, alone in a corridor that smelled of lilies and fear, and how the word had come out of him like the last true note he'd ever sing.

He breathed the way he'd been taught at eight years old: from the floor of himself.

And he sang.

Not the court songs. Not the ascension anthem he'd refused, not the lament he'd written for his own ruin. He sang the thing Wren had asked for — the truth of him, stripped down. Four coppers and a fish-smelling room. Eleven years of silence dressed up as principle. The hunger that had never died, only gone underground, feeding on tavern noise and cheap wine. And underneath all of it, the thing he'd never said aloud to anyone: that he had refused Corvane not because he was brave but because some part of him had understood, even then, that a voice given to the wrong master stops being yours forever — and he would rather be poor and whole than rich and hollow.

His voice cracked on the last phrase. He let it crack. He'd stopped performing years ago; this wasn't performance.

The silence after was enormous.

Then the stone answered.

Not with sound — with *absence* of sound, a hush so complete it pressed against his eardrums like water. The carved lines under his palm began to glow, faintly, the color of moonlight through fog. And the cliff face — the solid, unyielding, geologically indifferent cliff face — opened.

Not swung. *Unmade itself*, the way mist unmakes, folding aside into a corridor of pale stone that sloped gently down into the dark. Air breathed out of it, cold and mineral and old, and it smelled — Merrin's skin prickled — it smelled like the inside of a cathedral after centuries of incense, like something that had once been holy and was now only waiting.

"Well," said Brannoc. "That's never happened before."

"It happened once," Wren said quietly. "A generation ago. This is how the singers went down to the Hollow Court." She looked at Merrin with an expression he couldn't parse — awe, or pity, or both. "You asked properly. That's more than I could have done."

Merrin stared at his own hands. They were shaking. *Carry our words further than our armies,* the dead queen had said. He'd thought that was flattery. He was beginning to suspect it had been a job description.

* * *

They went down in single file, torches guttering in air that moved without wind. The passage descended in long spirals, and the walls changed as they went — rough-hewn at first, then dressed, then carved, and the carvings grew stranger and more beautiful with every turn.

Merrin walked last, because someone had to watch the dark behind them, and because he couldn't stop looking at the walls.

The carvings showed people. That was the first shock — he'd expected fae shapes, the antlered and the many-eyed of old woodcut nightmares. Instead: humans. Ordinary ones. Farmers with wheat in their arms, weavers at looms, a woman holding up a baby. And beside them, woven into every scene, figures that were *almost* human — taller, thinner, their outlines blurring into branches and rivers and weather, but unmistakably standing *with* the mortals, not above them. In panel after panel, the two kinds faced each other with mouths open, and between their mouths ran lines — carved ribbons of script that Merrin recognized with a jolt that went through him like cold iron.

"Notation," he whispered. "They carved the music into the walls."

"The whole covenant," Wren said from ahead, her voice strange in the echoing dark. "Every verse. Every term. Sung into the stone so no king could burn the treaty. You can't rewrite a song that lives in a mountain."

Tamsin had stopped at one panel, torch raised. Merrin came up beside her. The carving showed a circle — dozens of figures, mortal and fae alternating, hands joined, and above them a shape that might have been a hall's vaulted ceiling rendered in cross-section. At the circle's center stood two figures slightly larger than the rest, one crowned with antlers, one crowned with gold, and between them, held jointly, a small glowing thing the sculptor had rendered as a star.

"The Choir," Tamsin said. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but Merrin had learned to hear the second voice under her first — the skill he'd begun teaching her on the road, though she didn't know yet that's what he was doing. Under the flatness was something raw. "This is what it was. Not a spell. Not a wall. A *choir*. People and fae, singing together, holding something up."

"Holding what?"

"I don't know." Her torchlight moved across the star. "But look at their faces."

He looked. The sculptor had been good — good enough that even in shallow relief, worn by centuries, the faces read clearly. Joy. Not solemnity, not duty, not the grim righteousness of war memorials. Joy. Mortals and fae caught mid-phrase, mouths open, eyes bright, leaning toward one another like dancers.

Merrin had sung for courts. He knew what commissioned joy looked like, and this wasn't it.

"Come on," Brannoc growled, further down. "Before I start feeling things."

* * *

The passage opened without warning, and Merrin's torchlight fell into a space so vast that the flame seemed to shrink rather than illuminate it.

The Hollow Court.

He'd imagined thrones — the map's final notation had resolved, in his mind, into some grand seat of power. What he hadn't imagined was the scale, or the shape, or the terrible *rightness* of it. The hall was a perfect oval, its floor of seamless black stone that reflected their torches like still water. Tiered galleries rose around it in curves — and he understood within three heartbeats that the tiers weren't seating. They were *choir stalls*. Row upon row upon row, curving up into darkness, built to hold hundreds of voices arranged in ascending harmony. The acoustics of the place were engineered into its very bones; he could tell by the way his own breathing came back to him a half-instant late, softened, gathered, as if the hall were listening.

At the far end stood the thrones. Two of them.

One was alive — or had been. It grew from the living rock, a great chair of white wood and pale stone, its back a fan of branches that had once, perhaps, borne leaves. It was beautiful and it was empty, and dust lay on it like snow.

The other was worse. It was made of gold and granite, human workmanship, fine workmanship, and it had been *broken*. Deliberately. The backrest was split down the middle, the armrests toppled, the whole thing shoved sideways off its dais as if by a blow. It lay in pieces like a corpse.

Nobody spoke. Even Brannoc had nothing.

Merrin walked out onto the black floor alone. His footsteps rang and faded, rang and faded, and the hall took each one and gave it back diminished, as if tasting it and finding it wanting. He stopped at the center, where the carved star had marked the covenant's heart, and he turned in a slow circle, and the galleries rose around him like frozen waves.

He had performed in the Grand Basilica of Marrowgate. He had sung in the Amber Hall, whose acoustics were famous across three kingdoms. This place made them both look like barns. Whoever had built it had understood something about the marriage of voice and space that human builders had never even approached — that a hall is an instrument, that architecture can be a kind of music, that a choir does not merely fill a room but *completes* it.

And it was dead. All of it. Dead as the broken throne.

"Merrin." Tamsin's voice, from the edge of the floor. Careful. "What do you need?"

He almost laughed. What did he need? He needed forty years and a hundred trained voices and the fae courts returned from wherever they'd gone. He needed the world of a generation ago, before the slaughter, before the breaking, before a duke with soft hands learned that fear was cheaper than song.

"I need to try the opening verse," he said. "Properly this time. With the hall helping."

Wren limped down onto the floor, staff tapping, and studied the black stone. "The Deepwell notation," she murmured, unrolling a copy of the map she'd traced. "The first phrase. It's meant to be sung from the center, and the hall carries it outward, and the galleries..." She trailed off, looking up at the tiers. "The galleries answer. That's the design. One voice asks, the hall remembers, and the answer comes back from the stones themselves. The Choir isn't people, exactly. It's people *and place*, singing to each other."

"And if the place has forgotten?" Tamsin asked.

"Then we'll know," Wren said.

Merrin set down his torch. He rolled his shoulders. He planted his feet on the black stone at the heart of the Hollow Court, in the exact spot where, a generation ago, two monarchs had held a star between them while hundreds of voices rose in tiers around them, and he sang the opening verse of the covenant.

He sang it better than he had ever sung anything. He poured in everything the road had given him — the terror of the tollhouse, the warmth of the winter cave, Sef's small hand in his during the bad nights, Tamsin's stubbornness, Brannoc's gruff constancy, Wren's fierce fading strength. He gave the hall his truth again, deeper this time, shaped now by the notation's architecture, each interval placed where the map said it belonged. His voice climbed the tiers and filled the oval and the black floor drank the sound and the vaulted dark received it, and for a handful of seconds — he would remember this always — the Hollow Court *sang*.

The hall took his voice and multiplied it and gave it back from every tier at once, a chord of echoes, vast and mournful and almost — *almost* — alive.

And then it faded. And the silence that followed was not the listening silence of before.

It was the silence of a grave.

Nothing answered. No glow in the walls, no breath of waking stone. The echo died in the highest gallery and did not return, and the dark came back in, patient and total, and Merrin stood alone on the black floor with his arms slowly lowering and his heart going out like a lamp.

He tried again. He told himself he'd misplaced a third, that the hall needed warming, that any instrument needs breath before it speaks. He sang the verse a second time, and a third, pushing harder, reaching for notes he hadn't touched since his prime, and his voice frayed and cracked and the hall swallowed every attempt and gave back nothing but the ghost of his own sound, diminishing, diminishing, gone.

On the fourth try his voice simply failed. It quit mid-phrase like a snapped string, and what came out instead was a sound he hadn't made since he was a child — a thin, cracked, human noise, no craft in it at all.

He stood there shaking. The torches hissed. Somewhere above, water dripped with the regularity of a clock counting down to nothing.

"Merrin." Tamsin crossed the black floor to him. She didn't touch him — she wasn't a touching person, he'd learned that — but she stood close, close enough that he could see her face in the torchlight, and her face was doing something he'd never seen it do. It was open. "The hall answered you," she said. "The first time. We all heard it."

"It answered and then it *stopped*." His voice was wrecked and he didn't care. "Don't you understand? It's not broken the way a door is broken. It's grieving. Something in here was killed, and the stones remember, and they're not going to sing for us. Not for me. Maybe not for anyone." He gestured at the shattered throne. "Look at it. They didn't just break the covenant. They broke the *seat*, and left it lying there like a warning. And the other throne—" he turned to the white chair, dusted with centuries, "—the other one's been waiting so long it's turned to a tomb."

Sef came down the tiers then, small feet silent on the black stone, and stood beside the white throne and looked up at it for a long moment.

"She sat here," the child said.

"You can't know that," Wren started.

"I can hear it." Sef laid their cheek against the dusty armrest, eyes closing. "The stone keeps the shape of whoever sat in it longest. She's still... the shape of her is still here. She was sad at the end. She was singing when they came, and she kept singing, and the last thing the stone heard was—" The child's face crumpled. "She didn't stop. That's what broke the throne over there. Not an army. *Her.* She sang so hard at the end that the human throne couldn't hold the truth of it and it came apart."

The silence after that was different from the other silences. It had weight. Merrin found he couldn't breathe in it.

"So it's hopeless," Brannoc said at last, heavily. "We came all this way to sing at a grave."

"No," Wren said.

Everyone turned. The old witch had lowered herself painfully onto the bottom tier, and she was looking at the black floor with an expression Merrin had never seen on her — not wry, not weary, but lit from inside, like a coal remembering fire.

"You're all hearing failure," she said. "I'm hearing a diagnosis. The hall answered him. Once. Fully. Which means the voice of the place isn't dead — it's *bound*. Something is holding the Choir shut from below, the way a fist holds a bird. The Deepwell notation doesn't end at this hall, children. Look at your maps again. The last phrase points *down*." She tapped the black stone with her staff, and the sound rang out into the dark, and this time — faint, distant, deep beneath them — something rang back.

Sef's head came up like a deer scenting wind.

"That," Wren said, "was not an echo."

Far below the Hollow Court, down whatever throat of the mountain the covenant had been drowned in, something ancient shifted in its sleep — and began, very slowly, to listen.
