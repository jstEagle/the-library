## 14. The Traitor's Name

The letters came out of a dead man's coat.

They had taken the tollhouse back in the first hour of the ambush — taken it, held it, and buried its keeper in the scree behind it, because the keeper had been no toll-keeper at all but a Corvane agent with a crossbow and a signal mirror, and Merrin's arrow had found him before his mirror could find the valley below. Afterward, while Brannoc dragged the body and Sef sat white-faced by the fire pretending not to look at it, Tamsin had gone through the man's effects the way she went through everything: methodically, thoroughly, looking for poison.

She hadn't expected correspondence.

Now, two days later, camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite where the wind couldn't reach them, she sat with the oilcloth packet open on her knees and read by the last grey light of evening. Seven letters. Sealed with wax that had been broken and re-sealed carelessly, as if the courier had never imagined dying with them on him. The hand was small and precise, the ink brown-black, the paper good — court paper, the kind that came from the chancery stores under the palace, the kind Tamsin had carried a hundred times between offices when she was young enough to be useful for errands and not yet old enough to be useful for tasting.

She read the first letter twice before the name at the bottom stopped meaning nothing.

*Your servant in the kitchens performs admirably,* it said. *The girl's constitution continues to exceed expectation. The ninth compound produced no visible distress. Recommend we proceed to the tenth, per the original schedule.*

It was signed with a single initial, pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

*A.*

Tamsin read it a third time. The words didn't change. That was the trouble with words; she had spent her whole life trusting them to stay where she put them.

"Tam." Brannoc's voice, low, from the edge of the hollow. "Food's on. You eating or brooding?"

"Eating," she said, and folded the letter along its original crease, and did not eat.

* * *

She waited until Sef was asleep — truly asleep, not the light half-doze the child fell into whenever adults lowered their voices, which was most of the time — before she took the letters to Wren.

The old woman was awake, of course. Wren was always awake now, or seemed to be, propped against her pack with her hands folded over her walking stick like a queen holding audience with the dark. Her eyes were bad in the firelight and worse in the dusk, so Tamsin read the letters aloud to her, one by one, keeping her voice flat the way she kept everything flat, and watched Wren's face while she did it.

Wren didn't interrupt. She listened the way she did everything — completely, without hurry, as though time were something other people worried about.

When Tamsin finished, the silence lasted long enough for the fire to settle twice.

"You knew this man," Wren said finally. "Aldous."

"He wasn't a man to me. He was weather." Tamsin turned the last letter over in her fingers. "He was there when I was brought to the palace. I don't remember before him. I've tried. There's a kitchen, and a smell of onions, and Aldous telling me I'd been given a great honor, and after that there's just — years. Lessons. He taught me to read a room the way you read a page. He taught me which poisons numbed the tongue first and which lied. He taught me that fear is a flavor and a taster who can't taste her own fear is worth ten who can't taste arsenic." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "He used to say I was the finest instrument the crown ever tuned."

"And he was tuning you for this," Wren said. Not unkindly. Just true.

"For this." Tamsin looked at the letter again. *The ninth compound.* She tried to count backward through her life and find the ninth winter, the ninth birthday, the ninth whatever-it-was, and discovered she couldn't, because Aldous had never let her know what she was counting. Every illness she'd survived had been a lesson. Every night she'd spent curled around a bucket in the taster's cell had been a *result*. "I thought I was strong. All those years I thought I was strong, and I was just — dosed. Scheduled. *Per the original schedule.*"

"Aye." Wren poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "And here's the question your gut's already asked and your head won't answer: how did Corvane's men know your name at the salt gate? How did they know which road you'd take? How did they find the tollhouse?"

"They're inquisitors. Finding people is what they do."

"Mm." Wren looked at her over the fire, and her eyes in the ember-light were the color of old honey gone dark. "Or someone told them. Someone who knew your routes better than you do, because he built them. Someone who taught you every smuggler's pass through Thornfell — and so knows every place a runner might stop, every safehouse, every tollhouse, every friend."

Tamsin said nothing. The fire popped.

"I'm not telling you anything you haven't already swallowed," Wren said. "I can see it sitting in you like a stone. But hear the rest of it, because the rest matters. A man doesn't spend twenty years raising a weapon and then lose track of it. If Aldous wanted you dead, girl, you'd be dead. Those riders at the tollhouse wanted you *alive*. Whatever Corvane needs from you, he needs it breathing. Which means somewhere ahead of us, there's a conversation waiting that you don't want to have."

"I've had it," Tamsin said. "In my head. About forty times since sundown."

"And?"

"And I keep arriving at the same place." She fed the last letter to the fire and watched the initial curl and blacken. "He taught me everything I am. And everything I am is a lie someone else wrote."

"No," Wren said, sharp as a slap. "That's the lie talking, and it's *his* lie, and you'll not carry it into the Deepwell. He brewed your blood, maybe. He trained your tongue, certainly. But he didn't teach you to hide a map from a spymaster with your heart hammering and your face still as a pond. He didn't teach you to choose strangers over crown. That was you, Tamsin Thackeray, alone, in the space of one bad minute at a gate. He made an instrument. Instruments don't run away."

Tamsin stared at the burning paper until it was ash.

"Instruments don't get to be angry either," she said quietly.

"Oh, they don't." Wren settled back against her pack and closed her eyes. "But you're not one anymore. So be angry. It'll keep you warm till morning."

* * *

Merrin found her at second watch, sitting up with her knees drawn in, doing the thing she did instead of sleeping: cataloguing. Exits. Water sources. Sight lines. The habits of everyone in the camp, including him. She heard him coming — she always heard people coming, another gift from Aldous, another piece of the curriculum — and didn't turn.

"The letters," he said. "Brannoc told me. I wanted to say—" He stopped. Sat down beside her, careful of the distance, the way he always was. In the dark he smelled of woodsmoke and cheap tavern wine he'd sworn off three weeks ago and apparently missed. "I refused to sing at Corvane's ascension. One refusal. One afternoon of courage, and it cost me everything I had. You spent your whole life being poisoned by the man you loved like a father, and you only found out yesterday, and you're still walking north."

"Don't make it noble. I ran because I was scared."

"Everyone runs scared. It's what you do *after* that—" He exhaled. "I keep composing speeches for you in my head. They're all terrible. I'm a singer, not a comforter."

"That makes two of us."

They sat. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like distant voices, and both of them tilted their heads toward it at the same moment, and neither commented, because commenting would have meant admitting they'd both heard it.

"What did he feel like?" Merrin asked at last. "Finding out. I've wondered. What does it feel like, learning the person at the center of your life was the trap all along?"

Tamsin considered the question honestly, because he had earned honesty and because lying to Merrin had started to feel like lying to herself.

"It feels like tasting wine," she said. "You know how it is — the first sip tells you nothing. You hold it. You let it sit on the tongue. And then the truth comes up underneath, late, the way the heat of a pepper comes late, and by then you've already swallowed." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I swallowed twenty years of him, Merrin. Every kindness. The time he sat up all night with me when I was sick — I thought that was love. It was observation. The time he gave me my mother's ring — I thought that was grief. It was bait. He knew exactly what a rootless child needed to hear, because he was the one who'd made sure I had no roots to need anything else."

"Do you hate him?"

"Yes." It came out fast and clean, and then, slower: "And I'd still take his advice tomorrow. That's the worst part. Twenty years of listening to a man doesn't come out of your ears just because you learn he was paid to talk."

Merrin nodded slowly. "My first teacher was like that. Not cruel — worse, kind, in the way that keeps you obedient. When I left the court I hated her for a year. Then I caught myself teaching dockside kids the way she taught me, word for word, and I understood I'd never leave her at all. She was in my mouth." He glanced sideways at her. "So the question isn't whether Aldous is in yours. He is. The question is whether you sing his notes or write your own."

"I can't sing at all."

"Not yet," Merrin said, and there was something in his voice when he said it — a certainty, a promise, a threat, she couldn't tell which — that made her look at him properly for the first time that night.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around the coldest hour she gave up pretending and walked the hollow's rim, checking the pickets out of habit, and found Brannoc awake at the eastern post, whittling something that was going to be either a spoon or a knife handle depending on how the wood felt about it.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said, without looking up.

"I don't really do it anymore. Sleep. It's a professional hazard."

"Aye, well." He turned the stick. "Your da slept like a stone. Anywhere, anytime. Said it was the smuggler's advantage — the ones who can't rest die tired." He smiled at some private memory, then let it go. "Word from the tollhouse papers, then. Bad news?"

"Worse than bad. Familiar."

"Aldous." He said the name easily, and she realized he'd known — Wren had told him, or he'd read the letters himself, and nobody had thought to warn her that the whole camp already knew her wound was open. She must have shown something on her face, because Brannoc set down the stick. "He knew your father too, you know. Before. Back when your da ran the high passes and the crown still pretended the mountain was free."

"I know. That's how Aldous got me. My father trusted him. Dying words, practically — *take the girl to Aldous, he'll see her right.*" She laughed, short and bitter. "He saw me right, all right. Right into a taster's cell."

"Or out of a worse one." Brannoc shrugged, heavy shoulders rolling. "I'm not defending him. Man sold the passes to Corvane, near as I can tell, and that's beyond forgiveness in my book. But your da chose him too, Tam. Smart man, good man, and he chose wrong. Doesn't make you a fool for trusting him. Makes you his daughter."

She looked out at the dark mountains, black against the less-black sky, and thought about her father, whom she remembered mostly as a smell of pine resin and a pair of hands lifting her onto a mule. Dead fifteen years. Betrayed, probably, by the same man, in ways she'd probably find in these letters if she read them again — and she would read them again, because that was what she did, she read things until they confessed.

"Brannoc. If it had been you — if you'd learned your whole life was somebody's design—"

"I'd be furious," he said simply. "And then I'd get up tomorrow and walk north anyway, because the walking's the only part that was ever mine." He picked the stick back up. "Get some rest, girl. Tomorrow we hit the high trail, and the high trail doesn't care whose daughter you are."

* * *

Dawn came up thin and gold. They struck camp in the practiced silence of people who had gotten good at leaving places quickly, and Tamsin stood a moment at the mouth of the hollow with the letters' ash sifted into the dirt under her boots, and made herself say it inside her own head, plainly, the way she'd say *this dish contains hemlock*:

Master Aldous betrayed me. Master Aldous has always served Corvane. Everything else is weather.

It didn't hurt less. But it held still, and a thing that holds still can be worked around.

She shouldered her pack and turned to call the others — and stopped.

Sef was standing at the hollow's far edge, facing the mountain, absolutely still, head tipped as though listening to something very far away. The dawn light caught the child's face, and on it was an expression Tamsin had never seen there before: not fear, not the careful blankness Sef wore around adults, but something older. Recognition.

"Sef?" Tamsin kept her voice gentle. "What is it?"

The child didn't turn. When they spoke, their voice was thin and strange, like a thread pulled tight.

"It's louder here," Sef said. "Under the mountain. It's been getting louder every day since the tollhouse." A pause. "And this morning — Tamsin — it said my name."
