## 15. Winter Camp

The snow came down like the sky had given up on holding anything back.

Brannoc Ironhand had wintered in worse places — three years in the Kettlepits, one memorable season in a wrecked river barge with eleven other dwarves and one goat — but he'd never wintered anywhere that mattered this much. The cave sat high on Thornfell's western shoulder, behind a curtain of ice he'd known about since he was a lad running his father's routes, and it was deep enough to hold six people, two pack mules, and a fire that didn't smoke if you built it right. He built it right. He always built it right. It was the one thing in his life you could count on.

Outside, the world was white noise and wind. Inside, the company of the broken was doing what broken things do when nobody's watching: they were knitting themselves together, stitch by stubborn stitch, and mostly without noticing.

He watched them from his corner by the woodpile, whittling a spoon he didn't need, because a dwarf's hands wanted work even when his heart wanted rest.

Tamsin was mending socks. That was a thing about her that still caught him sideways — the king's taster, the woman who'd stared down an inquisitor's blade at the tollhouse, darning wool by firelight like any grandmother in Marrowgate. She'd taken over the mending on the third day without asking anyone's leave, the way she took over most things: quietly, completely, as if she'd been doing it all her life and couldn't remember who'd taught her.

Nobody had, he suspected. That was the thing about Tamsin Thackeray. Everything she knew, she'd learned alone.

Sef lay curled against the mule's warm flank, pretending to sleep the way children pretend — eyes shut too tight, ears wide open. The fae-blooded child had been quieter since the Hollow Court, since those empty thrones and dead acoustics, and Brannoc didn't blame them. He'd walked into that hall himself and felt something old and sad press down on him like a hand on the back of his neck. For a child who could *hear* the mountain singing, it must have been like walking into a house where everyone they loved had died at table.

Merrin Vale sat cross-legged near the fire, teaching Wren's fingers a chord on a battered lute he'd carried out of Marrowgate wrapped in oilcloth like a relic. Which, Brannoc supposed, it was. The hedge-witch's hands were stiff from cold and her wound still seeping under its bandage, but she laughed when she fumbled the change, and Merrin laughed with her, and for a moment neither of them looked like people running from anything.

And Old Wren — well. Old Wren was dying. She knew it, and Brannoc suspected Tamsin knew it, and the witch herself treated the fact the way she treated weather: inconvenient, worth planning around, not worth weeping over. She'd stopped hiding how much the wound cost her. She spent her strength like a miser spending his last coin — carefully, deliberately, on things that mattered.

Right now what mattered was the chord.

"No," Merrin said gently, for the fourth time. "You're reaching for it. Don't reach. It's already there. You're just letting it out."

"Easy for you to say," Wren grumbled. "You were born with a throat full of silver."

"I was born with a throat full of nothing, actually. Same as you. I just got yelled at by better teachers."

Brannoc smiled into his whittling. Two weeks ago these two had been strangers circling each other like cats. Now look at them.

The storm had trapped them here four days ago, and Brannoc had privately decided it was the best luck they'd had since leaving Marrowgate. Armies didn't march in this. Inquisitors didn't ride in this. Even the Ash Queen's folk kept to their verges when the snow flew. For four days, nobody had been hunting them, and it turned out that when nobody was hunting them, they didn't quite know what to be.

So they told stories.

It had started the first night, half-accidental. Sef had asked — in that direct, unblinking way of theirs — why Brannoc had come along at all. Owed Tamsin's father, that was the official answer, and true as far as it went. But the child had looked at him with those too-bright eyes and waited, and waiting was a weapon Sef wielded without knowing it, and eventually Brannoc had heard himself saying things he hadn't said aloud in twenty years.

* * *

"Tamsin's father saved my life twice," he'd said. "First time, he pulled me out of a rockfall on the Low Pass. Second time was slower." He'd turned his mug in his hands. "I was drinking myself into the ground after my wife died. Your da — Tam's da — he didn't say a word to me about it. Just started showing up at my door every morning at dawn with work. Carrying jobs, mapping jobs, anything that needed doing before the sun cleared the ridge. Couldn't drink and climb. Had to choose." He'd shrugged. "Took me a month to figure out what he was doing. Took me ten years to thank him properly."

"And then?" Sef had asked.

"Then Corvane's men came through the passes looking for smugglers to make examples of. Her da moved the whole winter's cargo out of their reach — three nights in a blizzard, carrying twice what any man should carry. Caught a fever doing it." Brannoc had looked across the fire at Tamsin, who'd gone very still over her mending. "The crown buried him in a pauper's row and docked his pay for the lost cargo. So aye, lass. I owed him. But I'd have come anyway."

Tamsin hadn't said anything. She'd just bent her head lower over the sock in her lap, and Brannoc had seen her blink twice, fast, and had pretended not to, because that was a courtesy between people who understood what it was to owe the dead.

Tonight, though, the stories came easier. The fire was good, the storm was loud enough to make the cave feel like the last room in the world, and something had loosened in all of them — some knot of vigilance that even four days of safety hadn't undone until now.

It was Sef who started it, because it was always Sef now. The child had stopped flinching from questions somewhere on the far side of the Hollow Court, and had begun instead to ask them.

"How did you learn to taste poison?"

The question landed on Tamsin like a thrown stone. Brannoc saw her shoulders square, saw the reflexive glance toward the cave mouth — old habit, checking exits — and then, remarkably, she let it go. She set down the sock.

"I was bought," she said. "That's the short version."

"There's a long version?" Sef asked.

"A very long version." Tamsin fed a stick to the fire. "But I suppose you've earned it. All of you have." She glanced around the circle, and Brannoc braced himself, because he'd known this woman for weeks now and he knew what it cost her to open a door.

"I don't remember my mother. Or father, or — anyone. Aldous says I was found. Aldous is the spymaster; he raised me, more or less, once the kitchens did. What I remember is the kitchen. I was small. There was a woman called Mistress Hobb who ran the pastry ovens and snuck me honeycakes when the steward wasn't looking, and there was a stool by the hearth that was mine, and I thought I lived there." A thin smile. "I didn't live there. I was being kept. Like a knife is kept."

"The tasting started when I was seven. Small things first — a drop of this, a crumb of that. They'd watch my eyes, my skin, my breathing. Most children would've sickened. I didn't. I thought I was strong. I thought I was *lucky*." Her voice stayed level, but Brannoc had hauled cargo up scree slopes in high wind and he knew strain when he heard it. "By twelve I could name nine poisons by taste alone. By fifteen I was tasting for the king's own table. Every meal, I went first. Every feast, I drank from the cup before he did. And every time I survived something that should have killed me, Aldous would write it down in a little book, and I'd feel proud." She laughed, short and bitter. "Proud. Of being a lock they could test keys against."

Silence held the cave. Even the wind seemed to lean closer.

"Mistress Hobb figured it out before I did," Tamsin went on. "She's the one who told me I'd never once been invited to eat *with* anyone. Fifteen years in that palace and I'd never shared a table. I ate alone, after everyone, off whatever was left — because what if the taster died? You can't have the taster sitting next to the heir when she drops." She looked up, and her eyes found Brannoc's, then Merrin's, then Sef's, moving around the circle like someone counting coins they couldn't quite believe were real. "She told me that, and I remember thinking — oh. Oh, that's what's wrong. Not the poisons. The table."

"You have a table now," Sef said simply.

Tamsin's face did something complicated. She looked down at the mending in her lap and didn't answer, and didn't need to.

* * *

Merrin picked up the thread an hour later, when the fire had burned low and Wren had dozed off sitting up, which she did now more often than anyone liked to mention.

"My turn, I suppose," he said. "Fair's fair. You've all heard me sing. You're owed the story of why I stopped."

Brannoc settled back. He'd wondered. They all had. A man threw away the finest career in the kingdom rather than sing at one coronation — there was a story in that, and Merrin had kept it buttoned up tight as a money pouch.

"I was five when they found me," Merrin said. "Not bought — found. Singing on the harbor steps in Vailmoor for coppers, and a passing choirmaster said he'd never heard pitch like it in his life. My parents took the stipend and I went to the conservatory, and I won't lie to you: it was a golden cage, but it was gold. I loved it. I loved the work, the discipline, the feeling of a hall going quiet when I opened my mouth. By twenty-five I was first voice of the royal chapel. The king — the old king — used to say my high C could calm a riot." His mouth twisted. "It nearly caused one, in the end."

He tuned a peg on the lute without seeming to notice he was doing it. Nerves, Brannoc realized. The man still had nerves after all this time.

"When Corvane made his move — and it was a slow move, years of it, buying magistrates and marrying widows and putting his men in every post that mattered — the chapel was one of the last places that stood apart. We sang for the realm, not for whoever sat the throne. Corvane wanted that changed. Wanted us singing his ascension mass, wanted the whole country to hear the choir bless him as if God himself had signed the paperwork." Merrin's jaw tightened. "The master of the chapel knelt. Most of them knelt. And I stood up in front of forty singers and said no."

"Brave," Wren murmured, half-asleep.

"Foolish," Merrin corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is usually about six months' hindsight. They didn't kill me. Killing the court's favorite voice makes martyrs, and Corvane doesn't make martyrs, he makes examples. So they did something cleverer." He held up his hands, flexing them. "They didn't cut my throat. They cut my *place*. Struck me from the rolls. Blackened my name — quietly, so quietly, little whispers in the right ears. Said I'd taken bribes. Said there was a scandal involving a magistrate's daughter, which there wasn't, but try proving a negative in a city where your enemy owns the magistrates. Within a season I couldn't get hired to sing at a funeral. Within two, I was in dockside taverns taking coin from sailors who mostly wanted a familiar song and didn't care whose mouth it came out of."

"And the voice?" Tamsin asked softly. "Is it still there?"

Merrin was quiet a long moment. "That's the strange thing," he said finally. "Yes. That's what I couldn't forgive them for, at first. They ruined me and left the one thing intact, like — like cutting down a tree and leaving the roots. For years I thought it was mercy. Then I thought it was cruelty. Now—" He looked at the map case lying beside Tamsin's bedroll, the dead stranger's map with its roads written in music. "Now I think maybe it was always meant for something else. Maybe everything I was, was meant for this. The covenant-song isn't court music. It's older. Deeper. Maybe the conservatory was just the training ground and I never knew it."

"Maybe," Brannoc rumbled, "you're allowed to just be good at something without the universe having a plan about it."

Merrin blinked at him, then laughed — really laughed, the first full laugh Brannoc had heard out of him. "Aye. Maybe. Thank you, dwarf. Keep me humble."

"Somebody has to. You preen when you hit the high notes."

"I do not preen."

"You absolutely preen. Sef, tell him."

"He preens," Sef said instantly, without opening their eyes, and the cave dissolved into laughter, and Brannoc sat in the middle of it feeling something he hadn't felt since before his wife died, which was simply glad, plain and undecorated as bread.

* * *

Later — much later, when Wren slept properly under blankets and Merrin had taken first watch out of habit even though there was nowhere to go — Sef sat up by the embers, and Tamsin moved to sit beside them, and Brannoc, who was pretending to sharpen an axe that needed no sharpening, listened the way he did everything: quietly, thoroughly, and without letting on.

"Tell me about running away," Tamsin said. "If you want. Only if you want."

Sef pulled their knees up to their chin. In the ember-light their eyes had that faint silver sheen that Brannoc had stopped finding unsettling and started finding, frankly, beautiful — like moonlight caught in water.

"I had a mother," Sef said. "In the verge-settlements, past the border. Human father, fae mother — or the other way round, I never learned which, and it doesn't matter, because the settlements don't care which way the blood runs, only that it runs mixed. There were thirty families. We farmed the twilight crops. Mushrooms, mothsilk, the berries that ripen in dusk." The child's voice was steady, but their hands weren't; they were pleating the blanket hem over and over. "Corvane's inquisitors came two winters ago. They said mixed blood was corruption. They said the covenant was dead and anything left of it was filth to be burned out. They burned the mothbarns first, so we couldn't run at night — the light would show us. Then they—"

Sef stopped. Pleated. Started again.

"I ran. Mother pushed me into the root cellars and told me to walk until the dark ended, and I did, and I kept walking, and the dark never ended, and I never went back. I don't know if she's alive. I don't know if anyone is. I hear the Choir sometimes — I've always heard it, a little, the way you'd hear a river through a wall — and lately it's been calling louder, and I think maybe it's calling *me*, and I'm scared that if I answer it I'll find out what happened to everyone, and I'm scared that I won't."

Tamsin put an arm around the child. Sef let themself be gathered in, stiff at first, then not.

"You know what I keep thinking?" Tamsin said. "Everyone in this cave was thrown away by somebody. Bought, silenced, exiled, orphaned. And somehow we all ended up in the same hole in a mountain, sharing one pot of stew." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Maybe that's what family is. Not the people who were supposed to keep you. The ones who did."

Sef was silent a moment. Then, very quietly: "Are we family? Actually? Because I've had foster-mothers and they always said it and then the coin ran out."

Brannoc saw Tamsin go still. Saw her take the question the way she took everything — seriously, completely, weighing it like a draught she might have to survive.

"Yes," she said. "Actually. I've never had one before, so I might be bad at it. But yes."

"Me neither," Sef said. "So we'll both be bad at it together."

"That's generally how it works."

From his corner, Brannoc cleared his throat loudly. "Oi. If we're doing declarations, someone should wake the witch, she'd hate to miss it."

"She'd hex you," Sef said.

"She'd hex me fondly. There's a difference."

* * *

Brannoc took the middle watch, as he always did, because he slept least and trusted himself most in the dark hours. The storm had spent itself sometime past midnight; the wind had dropped to a mutter, and through the ice-curtained mouth of the cave he could see the sky clearing, stars coming out hard and bright over Thornfell's white shoulders.

That was when he heard it.

At first he thought it was wind in the pass — but wind didn't harmonize. Wind didn't hold a note and add another above it, low and vast and patient, rising from somewhere deep beneath the snowpack, from the bones of the mountain itself. He'd heard the Choir's echo once before, on the march up from the Hollow Court, faint as memory. This was not faint. This was close. This was *underneath them*.

He woke Tamsin with a hand on her shoulder, careful, the way you wake a soldier.

She came awake instantly, fully, no fog of sleep — another thing about her that never stopped unnerving him. She listened. Ten heartbeats. Twenty.

"It's stronger," she whispered. "Than at the Hollow Court. Why is it stronger *here*?"

Beside her, Sef had sat up without being woken at all, head tilted, eyes shining silver in the dark.

"Because we're almost there," the child said. "Don't you feel it? It's not echoing anymore. It's *waiting*."

Far below, deep in the singing dark, the note changed — and Brannoc, who had spent his whole life reading mountains the way other men read letters, felt the hair rise on his arms, because the sound wasn't drifting upward anymore.

It was climbing.
