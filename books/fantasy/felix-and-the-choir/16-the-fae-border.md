## 16. The Fae Border

The last human landmark was a cairn.

It wasn't much to look at — a heap of grey stones no higher than Brannoc's shoulder, furred with lichen, half-swallowed by the hillside it had been raised on. But Wren stopped before it the way a sailor stops at the edge of a charted sea, and when she spoke, her voice had the flat carefulness of someone reading terms aloud.

"Past this stone, nothing is promised. Not the light. Not the hours. Not your own name, if you say it wrong." She looked around at each of them in turn. "I'll ward us as best I can. Best isn't certain. Understand that before you take another step."

Sef stepped over the cairn first.

Of course they did. The child moved with the easy certainty of someone walking into their own house, and where their bare feet touched the ground, the frost that silvered the grass did not form. They turned back and looked at the rest of them with the particular impatience of the young waiting for slow adults to finish being afraid.

"It's not scary," Sef said. "It's just *different*."

"Different is how scary introduces itself," Brannoc muttered, but he hefted his pack and crossed anyway, one boot after the other, jaw set like a man wading into cold water.

Merrin went next. Then me.

I remember the exact moment of crossing because my body told me about it before my mind caught up. One stride I was standing on Thornfell's northern shoulder, in the blue hour before dawn, with snowmelt soaking through my left boot. The next stride the light was wrong.

Not dark. Not bright. It was the light of an hour that couldn't decide what it wanted to be — the gold of late afternoon layered over the violet of dusk, both of them true at once, neither of them winning. The sky above the peaks was the color of deep water seen from underneath. And the air — the air tasted of something I had no word for. Green things growing in the dark. Iron left out in rain. A sweetness underneath it all, like honey held too long on the tongue until it turns.

My tongue, trained since childhood to catalog every flavor that might mean death, offered its report: *unknown. Unknown. Unknown.*

For the first time in my life, that report didn't frighten me. It almost felt like a greeting.

"All right," Wren said behind me, breathless. "Wards on. Everyone stay inside my shadow's reach. If you can see the edge of the light I'm casting, you're safe. If you can't—" she didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

We walked.

* * *

The verge did not behave like land. That was the first lesson, and Sef taught it without meaning to.

The child ranged ahead of us the way they always did, quick and curious, but here their wandering bent strangely. Twice we watched Sef walk straight away from us for what felt like a hundred paces and arrive back at our side from the front, as though the world between us had quietly folded. The third time, Sef came back older.

Not much older. An hour, maybe two — enough that the child who'd skipped ahead suddenly stood eye-level with my chin instead of my shoulder, their sleeves too short, their face carrying a new sharpness at the cheekbones that hadn't been there that morning.

Brannoc saw it before anyone else. He stopped dead in the trail and made a sound low in his chest like a dog deciding whether to growl.

"Sef." His voice was very gentle, which from Brannoc was more alarming than shouting. "Come here, little one. Let me look at you."

"I'm fine," Sef said, and their voice had dropped half a tone too. They looked down at their own hands, flexing them, and I watched the wonder and the fear chase each other across their face. "It's just — time goes soft here. Like butter in the sun. I could feel it happening and I couldn't tell which way it was going."

"That's why the wards exist," Wren said tightly. Sweat stood on her brow despite the cold; her fingers never stopped moving, knotting and unknotting a length of red thread she'd produced from somewhere, muttering under her breath in a language that made my teeth ache faintly, like a note pitched too low to hear properly. "That's why nobody wanders. Nobody strays. Nobody *naps*, gods help us, or we'll wake to find them a grandfather."

"So we stay close," Merrin said. He'd been quiet since the cairn — quieter than usual, which for Merrin meant nearly silent, his eyes moving constantly across the landscape as if reading it. Now he fell into step beside me and lowered his voice. "Tamsin. Are you seeing this?"

"Seeing what?"

He nodded ahead, off the trail, to our right.

I looked. At first I saw only trees — birches, white-barked and slender, exactly the kind that grew on this side of Thornfell. Then I saw that their shadows fell the wrong way. All of them, every trunk, cast shadows toward the light instead of away from it. And in the shadows, if I let my eyes unfocus, there were shapes. Tall ones. Still ones. Watching the way herons watch water: with a patience that predated hunger.

"I see them," I said.

"Don't look directly," Wren said without turning around. Her thread-knotting had sped up. "And don't count them. Counting is asking."

"Asking what?"

"Asking them to be counted."

I decided I would not count them.

* * *

We made camp — or rather, Wren chose a place and declared it a camp, drawing a circle in the frost-grey grass with salt from Brannoc's stores and ash from her own pouch, and informing us that within the circle, the hours would hold still and behave. Outside the circle, she said, was anyone's guess.

I took first watch. I always took first watch; sleep had never come easily to me, and the others had long since stopped arguing.

The verge at night was worse than the verge by day, or better, depending on how you measured such things. The impossible light faded but didn't die, sinking into a twilight that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, so that shadows lost their meaning entirely. The watching shapes among the birches drew closer — or perhaps the trees themselves leaned in. Once, far off, I heard singing.

I want to be precise about this, because everything that followed began with that singing.

It was not Merrin's kind of song. Merrin sang lines, melodies, beginnings and endings. This was harmony without any melody beneath it — voices stacked on voices, rising and falling in patterns that never resolved, that seemed less composed than *grown*, the way a forest grows. It was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. My skin prickled. My heartbeat tried to follow it and kept losing the rhythm, stumbling, catching itself.

And then, without my choosing it, I hummed back.

Just a fragment. Three notes, picked out of their pattern, matched to it the way a taster matches a poison to its antidote — by instinct, by some deep training I'd never known I'd received. The distant harmonies stopped.

The silence afterward was enormous. It had attention in it.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice, sharp with alarm, from the circle behind me. "What did you just do?"

"I don't know." My mouth had gone dry. "They were singing. I answered. It seemed — polite."

"*Polite.*" Wren struggled upright, joints cracking, and came to stand beside me at the circle's edge, squinting out into the half-dark. For a long moment she said nothing. When she finally spoke again, her voice had changed — gone careful, gone old. "They stopped because they tasted you."

"What does that mean?"

"It means, girl, that everything in this realm can taste blood the way you taste poison. It's how they know each other. How they know *us*. And yours—" she exhaled through her nose. "Yours must be like biting into a hot coal."

I thought of the birches and their backward shadows. Of the silence with attention in it. "So they'll avoid me."

"They'll *notice* you," Wren said. "There's a difference, and it matters. Nothing here forgets a coal."

* * *

The second day, the land began to recoil.

I noticed it first as a strangeness in the trail: wherever I walked, the frost did not melt under my boots the way it melted under everyone else's. Instead it retreated — visibly, a handspan ahead of each step, the grass greening and then browning in a wave that fled before me like water before a hot iron. Behind me I left a seam of dead ground, narrow as a footpath, stitching itself slowly closed.

By midday it was worse. Flowers closed as I passed. A stream we crossed went silent downstream of me, its small noises stopping as if the water itself were holding its breath. Birds — if they were birds; nothing here was quite anything — lifted from the branches in a widening ring, keeping always the same distance from me, a distance that never shrank no matter how fast or slow I walked.

Sef walked beside me for most of that day. I don't think it was an accident. I think the others arranged it, trading places subtly, so that the child would never walk alone near the things that flinched from me. But children notice arrangements, and near evening Sef looked up at me with those eyes that had seen too much for their years and said, with the devastating directness of the young:

"The ground doesn't like you here."

"No," I said. "It doesn't."

"Why?"

And because it was Sef, and because lying to that child had become a thing I found myself physically unable to do, I told the truth. "Because of what's in my blood. What they brewed into me when I was smaller than you. Everything alive in this realm can feel it, and everything alive in this realm knows it's meant to hurt them."

Sef considered this with great seriousness, walking along, kicking a stone that was probably not a stone.

"But you've never hurt anyone with it," they said. "You've only ever drunk the poison so nobody else had to."

"Yes."

"Then the ground is being stupid," Sef decided, and took my hand, and did not flinch, and we walked on together through a country that leaned away from me, while one small warm anchor of it leaned in.

I am not ashamed to say I had to blink hard a few times, and blamed the wind.

* * *

The wards broke on the third night.

Not all at once. Wren felt it coming — we all felt it coming, in the way the twilight thickened and the watching shapes pressed closer and the distant harmonies crept back into audibility, nearer each hour, hungrier each hour. Wren sat in the center of her salt-and-ash circle working her red thread until her fingers bled, retying knots that unraveled even as she tied them, and she was saying, over and over, very quietly, "Hold, hold, hold," the way you'd speak to a dying fire.

They didn't hold.

I woke — or came back to myself; in the verge I was never sure which — to find the circle's boundary glowing a sputtering orange, like a rope burning through from both ends, and beyond it the shapes had come out of the trees. They stood at the circle's rim in a loose ring, tall and thin and crowned with what I first took for antlers and then understood were branches, still growing, budding pale flowers in the impossible light. Their faces were faces the way a mask is a face: shaped for expression, holding none.

Wren was on her feet, grey-faced, spent. "I can't renew it," she said. "Not without days I don't have. If they want in—"

"They don't want in," Merrin said.

He was standing at the circle's edge, and he was doing the thing I had watched him do once before, in a Marrowgate tavern, when a drunk sailor grabbed Sef: going very still, gathering himself around his voice the way a fist gathers around a knife.

"They want to *listen*," he said. "Look at them. Look at where they're facing."

They weren't facing Wren. They weren't facing the wards, or the salt, or the child whose blood sang loudest of any of ours. Every masked face in that ring was turned toward me.

"No," I said, before I understood why I was saying it. "No, they've got it wrong, I'm the one thing here they should—"

"You're the one thing here they've never smelled before," Merrin said. "Poison, yes. Death, yes. But a *taster*? Someone built to carry death and live?" He shook his head slowly. "You're not a weapon to them, Tamsin. You're a riddle. And fae—" his mouth twisted, "—fae cannot leave a riddle alone."

One of the branch-crowned shapes took a single step forward. The burning circle guttered. Wren made a sound like tearing cloth.

And I did the mad thing. The thing that in any sane life I would never have done. I stepped past the ward-line, out of the circle, into the open dark, and stood before them with my empty hands visible and my traitor heart hammering, and I hummed the three notes again. The fragment I'd given them on watch. The answer to their unfinished question.

The ring went still in a way that made the stillness before seem loud.

Then the nearest shape — the tallest, its crown of branches heavy with those pale buds — reached out one long grey finger and touched me, once, on the hollow of the throat, where a taster's pulse lives.

Cold. Cold like well-water, like winter stone. And through that touch I felt it *reading* me — tasting me, as Wren had said, the way I tasted a dish: the bitterness of the brews, the iron of the scars, the years of deliberate swallowing, and underneath all of it, threaded through it like wire through wax, the thing the king's alchemists had built me for and never once explained.

The finger withdrew. The masked face tilted. And though it had no mouth that moved, I heard it — heard it inside the bone behind my ears, in the same register as the Choir's distant harmonies:

*"Coal-blood. Bridge-blood. The Deepwell remembers what you are."*

Then the whole ring folded backward into the birches like smoke returning to a doused fire, and the twilight eased, and the pressure lifted, and I was standing alone outside a broken ward with four people staring at me as if I'd grown a second head.

"What," said Brannoc, "in the name of every buried god, was that."

I opened my mouth to say I didn't know.

But I did know. That was the terrible part. Somewhere beneath the fear, in the place where my tongue had always lived — the place that knew poison from broth, truth from lie, friend from threat — something had recognized the touch, and answered it, and filed it away. *The Deepwell remembers what you are.*

"Wren," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "How far to the Ash Queen's court?"

She was looking at me strangely, hugging her ruined thread to her chest. "Two days. Why?"

"Because I think," I said, "they just sent word ahead of us."
