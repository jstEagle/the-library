## 17. Audience with the Ash Queen

The border did not announce itself. That was the first thing Merrin noticed — no gate, no wall, no line drawn in the earth. One moment they were climbing through grey scree and stunted pine, Wren's wards humming faintly at their wrists like struck tuning forks, and the next the light changed.

It went gold. Not sunlight-gold; something older, thicker, the color of honey held up to a candle. The air tasted of woodsmoke and apples out of season. Brannoc's beard had gone from iron-grey to silver in the space of a single step, and Sef — Sef was *glowing*, faintly, along the edges of their ears, the way a lamp glows through a shade.

"We're through," Wren said quietly. "Mind your words now. Here, words are things. You drop one carelessly, it stays where it falls."

Merrin had sung for kings. He had stood in the Marble Hall of Vail with three thousand eyes on him and felt nothing but the shape of the phrase in his throat. But walking into the fae twilight, he found his hands shaking, and he could not have told you which note of fear it was.

They climbed for what felt like hours and might have been minutes. Time here moved like water finding its level. Twice Merrin caught himself mid-stride, unable to remember whether he'd been walking toward something or away from it. Tamsin kept her hand on his elbow without seeming to notice she'd done it, and he let her, because the alternative was admitting he needed steadying.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

The Hollow Court — no. Not the Hollow Court. That ruin lay deeper in, where the covenant had been sung and broken. This was the living court, and it was carved into the face of the mountain itself: arches of black stone hung with banners that were not cloth but woven light, stairways that spiraled upward into a sky the color of late dusk even though the sun stood somewhere behind them still. And everywhere, everywhere, the sound.

Not music, exactly. Not yet. A hum, a resonance, the mountain holding a note it had been holding so long it had forgotten it was a note. Merrin's trained ear caught the pitch of it — a low C, deep as a heartbeat — and beneath it, threads. Dozens of threads. Voices, or the memory of voices, frayed and thin.

*They're singing to themselves,* he thought. *A choir with no conductor. No audience. Just keeping the flame alive.*

"They know we're here," Tamsin murmured. "They've known since the border."

As if she'd spoken an invitation, the great doors at the mountain's base — doors tall as cathedral spires, banded in silver that moved like liquid when you looked too long — swung inward without a hand touching them.

Cold air rolled out. It smelled of ash, yes, but also of winter roses and hot iron and something sweetly rotten, like fruit left too long in a cellar. Sef made a small sound and pressed closer to Brannoc.

"Steady, sprout," the dwarf rumbled. "Nobody eats anybody before supper."

"That's not funny," Sef whispered.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "But it's true, and truth's better than silence."

They went in.

* * *

The throne room — if that was the word for a cavern whose ceiling vanished into darkness full of drifting lights, each one perhaps a star, perhaps an eye — was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. The cold came off the courtiers.

They lined the approach in ranks: tall figures in grey and white and the pale gold of old bone, faces beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, precise and inhuman and already melting into something else if you stared. Some had antlers. Some had crowns of thorn or wire or living moth. None of them blinked. Merrin counted forty of them before he made himself stop counting, because counting was a way of pretending they were countable, and they were not.

At the far end, on a dais of black glass, sat the Queen.

She was smaller than Merrin expected. That was the trick of it, he realized — everything here was built vast to make you look up, and then the Queen herself was slight, almost delicate, seated with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for tea. Her hair was the white of paper burned at the edges. Her gown was grey silk shot through with threads of ember-light that dimmed and brightened with her breathing. Her face —

Her face was grief that had been worn so long it had become architecture.

"You come into my court," she said, and her voice filled the cavern without effort, the way a bell fills a church, "wearing the shapes of thieves."

Wren stepped forward first, which took courage Merrin didn't grudge her, and sank into a bow that was half curtsy, half collapse. "Ash Queen. We come under the old forms. We come asking parley."

"The old forms." Something moved at the corner of the Queen's mouth. It was not a smile. "The old forms were broken, hedge-witch, by your side of the border. Speak your names, since you remember how."

"Wren, of the Thornfell hedges. Brannoc Stonehand, free of any guild. Sef—" the child hesitated, and the Queen's ember-lit gaze slid to them and stayed, and Sef finished in a whisper, "Sef. Of nowhere."

"Merrin Vale," Merrin said, and heard his own voice crack on the name, and hated himself for it. He bowed the way he'd been taught at court, low and controlled. "Once of the king's chapel."

"Once." The Queen turned the word over like a coin she suspected was clipped. "And the last one. The quiet one at the back, who has not spoken. Step forward, taster."

Tamsin stepped forward.

Merrin watched the court react. It was subtle — a ripple, like wind over wheat — but every fae face turned, and every fae face went colder, and two of the nearest courtiers actually drew back a half-step, hands rising as if against a smell.

They could feel it. Whatever was brewed into Tamsin's blood, whatever the crown had done to her before she could walk, these beings could sense it across twenty feet of stone floor, and it read to them the way plague reads to a city.

"You know what I am," Tamsin said. Flat. Steady. Merrin marveled at it. He'd seen her hands shake over a map, seen her flinch at kindness, and here she stood before a queen of the elder world sounding like a signed treaty.

"I know what you are," the Queen said. "I knew it when you crossed the verge. My land recoiled from your feet, child. The grass will not grow where you slept last night. Do you know why?"

"I've been told my blood is poison to your people."

"Told." The Queen rose. She was taller standing than sitting, but still not tall; the dais did the work. "Your masters fed you death in a spoon from the time you could hold a cup, and you call it being *told*. Yes, taster. Your blood is poison. Your blood is the reason we are having this conversation in a hall instead of a garden. Your blood is the reason my court is called Ash."

Silence. Even the drifting lights seemed to hang still.

"Tell them," the Queen said, and now her voice dropped, and somehow that was worse — the vastness contracting down to something intimate and terrible. "Tell them what happened at the last reforging. Since your human histories have evidently misplaced it."

No one spoke. Wren's face had gone the color of tallow.

"Sixty years ago," the Queen said, "the covenant frayed, as all bindings fray, and needed renewal. It required singers. Three lines, mortal and fae and bridge, as it always has. The fae sent theirs. We sent our best — the Cantors of the Deepwell, voices that had taken a century to train. And your kingdoms sent soldiers."

She descended one step. The ember-threads in her gown flared.

"There was a faction at your court — the same lineage of ambition that produced your Duke Corvane, I do not doubt — that had decided the covenant was a leash and meant to break it rather than renew it. They invited our singers across the border under truce-flags. They gave them wine. And in the wine was a distillation very much like the one running in this girl's veins, refined past all mercy. Our Cantors died choking on their own voices. Every one. And the men who did it sang the *counter-song* over their bodies, and the covenant shattered, and the passes closed, and the blight came into the high valleys, and my people began to burn from the inside with a sickness we named after ourselves."

One more step. She was close enough now that Merrin could see her eyes properly: grey, ringed with ember, and bottomlessly tired.

"So when you ask me for alliance," she said, "understand what you ask. You ask the burned to shelter the arsonist's daughter."

"She didn't choose it," Sef burst out, and immediately clapped both hands over their mouth.

The Queen looked at the child for a long moment. Something flickered behind the grief-architecture of her face — recognition, maybe. Fae-blood, runaway, alone. Merrin saw the Queen see it, and saw her set it aside like a letter to be opened later.

"No," the Queen said at last. "She did not choose it. That is the tragedy's cruelest verse, little one. Neither did my Cantors."

* * *

They were given a chamber to wait in — a round room of black stone with a skylight onto the false dusk, and a table, and chairs, and absolutely no indication of how long they would be permitted to keep any of it. Wine appeared. Nobody drank it. Brannoc looked at it the way he'd look at a rope bridge with missing planks.

"Well," Brannoc said finally. "That went about as well as a funeral."

"It went exactly like a funeral," Wren said. She was rubbing her wrists where the wards sat, and Merrin noticed her hands weren't steady. "Because it is one. We're at a wake, and none of us brought flowers."

"She's going to ask for Tamsin's head," Merrin said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had simply arrived, fully formed, the way the worst sentences do.

Nobody argued. That was the horror of it. Tamsin just sat there, turning her cup slowly on the table, watching the wine shiver.

"You knew," Merrin said. "Coming in. You knew what she'd see when she looked at you."

"I guessed." Tamsin's voice was even. Too even. "You don't spend your life around poison without learning how it moves through a room. I could feel them feeling me. Like walking into a kitchen where everyone knows there's a dead rat somewhere and you're the only one who can't smell it."

"That's a horrible image," Brannoc said.

"I'm a food-taster. All my images are horrible. It's practically the guild motto."

It should have been funny. It wasn't. Merrin found he couldn't look at her, and couldn't stop looking at her, and the two impulses ground against each other until Wren said, gently, "Merrin. Breathe."

"I refused to sing for Corvane," Merrin said, to the table, to the wine, to nobody. "One refusal. One evening. I lost everything worth having and I called it a sacrifice, and I've spent three years drinking about how brave I was. She was poisoned into a weapon before she could *walk*, and she's sitting here deciding what to do about it while the rest of us rehearse our griefs." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm the disgraced singer. I'm supposed to be the tragic one. It's unbearable being outdone at your own tragedy."

"Merrin." Tamsin's voice, quiet. He looked up. She wasn't smiling, exactly, but something in her face had shifted — the thing he'd started noticing on the road, the thing that happened when she stopped performing competence and just *was*. "For what it's worth, your tragedy bought us the boat out of Marrowgate. And taught me the notation. And — " she paused, and he watched her decide to say the rest, watch her weigh it like a dose, "— and I've never had anyone to be outdone beside before. So. There's that."

Brannoc made a noise like a kettle deciding not to whistle. Sef grinned. Wren studied the ceiling with tremendous interest.

The doors opened before Merrin could find anything to say back, which was probably merciful, because he had a suspicion whatever he said would be true, and he wasn't ready for true.

* * *

The Queen received them again in a smaller hall — a garden, actually, or the memory of one, trees of black bark bearing blossoms of pale fire, a fountain that ran upward. Courtiers ringed the walls. The Queen stood rather than sat, which Merrin understood, correctly, as a concession: equals stand.

"You have discussed," she said. It was not a question.

"We have," Wren said.

"Then hear my terms, and hear them once. The covenant can be reforged. The Deepwell still holds its shape; the counter-song has eaten much but not all. If it is sung again — truly sung, three lines, whole — the blight recedes, the passes open, and my host will stand between the free cities and Corvane's madness. That is the alliance you came for."

Hope went around the circle like a lit taper. Merrin felt it catch in his own chest and hated how easy it was.

"And the price?" Tamsin asked.

The Queen's ember-ringed eyes settled on her, and the garden grew very quiet, and Merrin understood with sickening clarity that everything before this — the hall, the history, the waiting — had been courtesy. This was the actual audience.

"The covenant broke," the Queen said, "because human ambition killed fae voices. It cannot be reforged while the instrument of that killing walks and breathes. Your blood is a weapon forged against my people, taster. Weapons do not get to be allies. They get to be broken, or melted down, or buried." She lifted her chin, and for one unguarded instant Merrin saw not a queen but a woman who had buried singers and counted the years since. "The price of my alliance is your death. Freely given, cleanly done, by my own hand and no other. Your companions walk free. Your cities receive my host. And the thing in your veins that kills my children ends with you."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped down a well.

Merrin's mind did the thing it always did under catastrophe: it went musical. He heard the sentence as a cadence, a descending line, the dominant falling toward a resolution that was Tamsin's death, and some detached part of him noted that it resolved *beautifully*, and the rest of him wanted to scream.

"No," he said.

"Merrin—" Wren started.

"No." Louder. His voice cracked again and he let it. "No. You want to talk about the covenant? The covenant is *song*, and song is — " he groped for it, reached for the oldest thing he knew — "song is the art of making separate voices agree without erasing any of them. That's the whole craft. That's all it is. You kill her and you're not reforging anything. You're writing another verse of the same song that killed your Cantors. Different key. Same song."

The courtiers stirred. The Queen did not move.

"She did not choose the poison," Merrin said, quieter now, because the loudness had spent itself. "But neither did you choose the burning. And if the only answer either of you has is that someone must die for what was done to them — then Corvane has already won, and he hasn't even marched yet."

Silence. The upward-running fountain made its impossible sound.

Tamsin hadn't spoken. Merrin risked a glance at her and found her looking at the Queen — not afraid, or not only afraid, but *thinking*, working the problem the way she worked a suspect dish, isolating notes.

"Your Majesty," Tamsin said. "May I offer a counter-term?"

The Queen's eyebrows moved a fraction. "Bold, for a condemned woman."

"I'm told I'm immune to most things. Boldness may be among them." She stepped forward, into the space between the company and the throne, and Merrin's heart went with her whether he willed it or not. "My blood kills fae. I've heard that twice today, and I believe it. But Wren says the covenant needs a bridge — a third voice that belongs to neither side and both. What is my blood, if not that? Poison to fae, brewed by humans, carried in a body that belongs to neither world anymore. You want the weapon melted down. I'm offering to make it a bell instead. Same metal. Different purpose."

"You propose to *sing* the covenant," the Queen said, "through veins full of death."

"With training, yes. With help. Before the moon turns, if that's the term."

The Queen was silent for a long moment. Around the garden, the fire-blossoms pulsed slowly, like the mountain thinking.

"If you fail," the Queen said at last, "if the moon turns and the Deepwell stands unsung, I will not offer this bargain twice. My host comes over the passes regardless, and it will not distinguish gently between the realms of men. And if you succeed—" something moved in her face, something older and sadder than threat— "then you will live, taster, and living may prove the harder price. The covenant does not leave its bridges unchanged."

"I've been changed my whole life," Tamsin said. "By other people. Let me try choosing one."

The Queen regarded her for the length of three heartbeats — Merrin counted, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

"One chance," the Ash Queen said. "Reforge the Choir at the Deepwell before the moon turns, or be hunted by my host and Corvane's both, and learn what it is to have no realm at all." She turned away, dismissal and benediction in the same motion. "Go. And take your singer with you. If she means to attempt the bridge-line, she will need teaching — and there is no one left alive who remembers it better than the last voice your court threw away."

Merrin felt the words strike home like a hand laid flat on his sternum.

They walked out through the ranks of unblinking courtiers, through doors tall as cathedrals, out into the gold-thick twilight where the mountain hummed its long unfinished note. Only when the doors had sealed behind them did Brannoc exhale like a man surfacing.

"Well," he said. "We've got a deadline, a death sentence on layaway, and a singing lesson to schedule."

"Aye," Wren said grimly, flexing her ward-marked wrists. "And one moon to do what sixty years couldn't."

Merrin looked at Tamsin. She was already looking at the mountain, jaw set, listening to that deep C with the expression of a woman tasting something and refusing to spit it out.

"Right," she said. "Teach me to sing."
