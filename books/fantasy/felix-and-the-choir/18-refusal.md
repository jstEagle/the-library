## 18. Refusal

The Ash Queen's court did not breathe.

Tamsin had noticed it first in the antechamber, and now, standing on the floor of the great hall beneath the mountain's roots, she could not stop noticing. The fae did not shift their weight. They did not cough or clear their throats or fidget the way human courts did, all that restless flesh barely contained by ceremony. They simply *were*, ranks of them rising into the dark like pale trees in a forest that had learned to watch. Their eyes caught what little light there was — a greenish glow seeping from veins of mineral in the walls — and gave nothing back.

The silence was worse than any crowd she'd ever faced. She had tasted before kings. She had stood in the royal kitchens with a spoon of suspect broth at her lips while forty cooks watched to see if she'd fall over dead, and she had never once let her hand shake. But this silence pressed against her eardrums like deep water, and somewhere in it was the knowledge that every creature here could smell her blood — and hated it.

The Ash Queen sat upon a throne of black stone veined with grey, as if the rock itself had been burned from within and never quite gone out. She was not beautiful the way Merrin was beautiful, all line and longing. She was beautiful the way frost is beautiful: precise, indifferent, and absolutely certain of her own rightness. Her hair fell white as ash, and her gown moved when there was no wind, and when she spoke, her voice arrived inside Tamsin's skull rather than her ears.

*You are the one whose blood kills.*

Tamsin kept her eyes where Wren had told her to keep them — on the Queen's collarbone, never the eyes, never the crown. "I am Tamsin Thackeray," she said. "Food-taster to the crown of Vellmark. And I didn't ask for this blood. It was brewed into me before I could walk."

*A distinction without a difference.* The Queen's gaze slid past her, to Sef, who stood between Brannoc and Old Wren with their chin up and their small fists clenched. The child had refused to stay behind, and none of them had been fool enough to argue. *You come into my court wearing poison in your veins, attended by a child who fled my realm, a smuggler who has robbed my roads, and a witch who traffics in half-remembered rites. And you ask for alliance.*

"I ask for the covenant," Tamsin said. "The one your people made with ours. The one that broke."

Something moved through the assembled fae then — not a murmur, because they did not murmur, but a stillness that somehow deepened, the way a room goes quieter when grief enters it.

*The covenant did not break,* the Queen said. *It was murdered. Do you know how, taster?*

"Old Wren has told us some of it."

*Then hear the rest.* The Queen rose. She was taller than Tamsin had judged, impossibly so, and the light in the walls dimmed as if the mountain itself deferred to her. *A generation ago, your Duke Corvane — then merely an ambitious man with a grudge — came to the verge with soldiers and silver-tongued advisors. He asked to hear the Choir. We sang for him. We opened the Deepwell and showed him the place where our two worlds touch, because we trusted the old words, the old bonds.* Her voice did not rise, but the temperature of the hall seemed to drop. *And when the singing was done, his men put blades through the throats of every singer present. Eleven of mine. Three of yours. The bridge-voices, the ones who carried the covenant between realms. He cut out the middle and left the ends to bleed.*

Merrin made a sound beside her, low and involuntary. Tamsin did not look at him. She kept her eyes on the Queen's collarbone and thought: *Corvane. Of course Corvane. The man who wants the free cities under his boot has been at this his whole life.*

"The singers who died," Tamsin said carefully. "They were the third voice. The bridge."

*There were three lines in the covenant,* the Queen said. *Mortal. Fae. And the voice between, which belonged to neither and both. Your kind killed the bridges. Mine sealed the passes and withdrew, and the world above forgot us, and the world below forgot you, and Corvane spent twenty years building instruments to shatter what song remained.* She descended one step from the throne. The whole court leaned inward without moving. *Now he marches north with machines that scream, and he needs only one thing to finish what he started — a living bridge he can corrupt. A child of mixed blood would serve.*

Sef went rigid. Brannoc's hand settled on the child's shoulder like a door being closed.

*Or,* the Queen continued, *a woman whose poisoned blood was engineered to be lethal to my kind. Corvane bred you for this, taster. Did you know? Your immunity was never training. You were a weapon walking around in the shape of a girl, and now you have walked into my court carrying the very thing designed to destroy us.*

"We know," Tamsin said. "Wren found the records."

*Then you understand why your request is not merely refused but obscene.* The Queen reached the bottom step. This close, Tamsin could see that her eyes were not eyes at all but pools of slow grey fire, banked and patient. *You stand in the house of the people your blood was brewed to kill, asking us to fight beside the realm that made you. Give me one reason — one — why I should not have you taken to the verge's edge and bled.*

The hall waited. Hundreds of fae faces, centuries old, waiting for her to beg.

Tamsin discovered something about herself in that moment, something she might have found funny if she weren't about to die: she had never begged in her life. Not when the spymaster's men held her down with funnels as a child. Not when the poisons got worse each year and no one told her why. Not when Aldous smiled at her across the map table and she trusted him with everything she had. Begging required believing someone might show mercy, and Tamsin Thackeray had stopped believing that before she was old enough to name the feeling.

So instead of begging, she laughed. One short, hard sound that rang off the stone like a dropped coin.

"You want to bleed me?" she said. "Get in line. Corvane wants me alive. Your court wants me drained. My king's spymaster — the man who raised me — sold my name to the man who built me. Everyone I have ever met wants a piece of my body for their own purposes." She took a step forward, and heard Wren's sharp intake of breath behind her, and took another anyway. "I've been drunk, dosed, dissected on paper, and hunted across three provinces. So yes. Bleed me. Add your name to the list. But you'll be doing exactly what Corvane does — treating a person as an ingredient."

*Careful,* the Queen said, and though her voice did not change, the grey fires in her eyes flared. *You presume much, mortal.*

"I presume nothing. I know." Tamsin spread her hands. "Here's what I know. Your covenant needed three voices. Mortal, fae, and bridge. Corvane killed the bridges because without them, the song can't hold — and without the song, your realm and mine drift apart like rafts on open water. Am I wrong?"

The Queen said nothing, which Tamsin had learned, in a life spent reading powerful faces, was its own kind of answer.

"And here's what else I know." Her heart was hammering and she let it hammer; she'd long ago stopped pretending fear away and started using it, letting it sharpen her the way a whetstone sharpens a blade. "My blood kills fae. That's true. Wren tested it. One drop in a warding circle and the circle screams. But poison is just medicine in the wrong dose — I've known that since I was six years old, tasting belladonna tonics measured out grain by grain. Arsenic heals the skin. Foxglove steadies the failing heart. Everything is a question of measure." She lifted her chin. "So don't drain me. *Sing through me.*"

The silence that followed had texture. Tamsin felt it settle over her skin like cold silk.

*Explain,* the Queen said at last.

"The covenant is a song. Songs need carrying. If the bridge-voices were people whose nature belonged to both realms —" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the whole impossible mess of her engineered body — "then maybe the bridge doesn't have to be born. Maybe it can be made. My blood is death to fae, yes. But it's also *of* both worlds — brewed from fae reagents by human hands, if the records Wren read are true. I'm not mortal. I'm not fae. I'm the exact thing Corvane tried to build and never understood: a crossing. He wanted a weapon. He got a door, and he never knew the difference."

Old Wren stepped forward, leaning on her staff, her voice cracked and human and suddenly very loud in that inhuman hall. "She's right, Majesty. I've read the old rites. The covenant was sung *through* bodies — that's why they chose bridge-singers, folk who could hold both natures without tearing. The blood isn't the barrier. The blood is the instrument. Poisoned strings still make music, if the player knows the tune."

*If,* the Queen repeated.

"If," Tamsin agreed. "I'm not going to lie to you. I can't sing. Merrin says I'm the worst student he's ever had, and he once taught a deaf fishmonger's son to hold a drone. I have maybe a moon's turn of learning in me, not twenty years. And I don't know if it will work. But I know what happens if it doesn't." She pointed upward, toward the surface, toward the north. "Corvane marches with screaming machines and stolen voices. He'll corrupt whatever's left of the covenant and use it as a chain. Your realm will be his instrument, my realm will be his footstool, and the last free thing in either world will be a runaway child hiding in a smuggler's cave. That's the future you're buying if you bleed me today. Cheap. Quick. And worthless."

She stopped. Her legs were shaking now that she'd stopped feeding off the anger, and she locked her knees and waited.

The Ash Queen regarded her for a long time. Around the hall, the fae had gone from watching to something more intent — Tamsin couldn't have named it, but she recognized its shape from kitchens and council chambers: the look of people recalculating.

*You speak boldly for a woman standing in her own grave,* the Queen said.

"I've eaten worse than this moment," Tamsin said. "Believe me."

Something happened to the Queen's mouth then. It was so slight that Tamsin almost missed it, and she would spend years afterward wondering whether she'd imagined it: the corner of the grey lips twitched, upward, briefly, like ice cracking in spring.

*Corvane brewed you to be a blade,* the Queen said. *It appears he failed to sharpen the edge he should have feared most.* She turned, ascending back toward her throne, and her voice filled the hall without effort. *Hear me, court of ash. This mortal asks not for mercy but for a chance, and there is a difference, and the difference matters. The covenant was reforged once before at the Deepwell, in the days of the first kings, when the worlds were new to each other. It can be attempted again — once.*

Murmurless, the court absorbed the pronouncement. Tamsin felt Merrin go very still beside her.

*These are the terms,* the Queen said. *Before the moon turns — one full passage, no more — you will stand at the Deepwell and sing the covenant entire: three lines, three voices, the binding sung through the bridge. If you succeed, the covenant stands renewed between my realm and the free cities, and my host will answer when the passes are threatened. If you fail—*

She paused, and the temperature dropped again, and every vein of green light in the walls guttered.

*—if you fail, then the bargain is void, and my hunt rides. Not only for you, taster. For the child. For the singer. For the witch and the dwarf and every soul who aided you. You will be hunted by my court and Corvane's army at once, from both sides of the veil, until nothing remains of your company but stories. Do you accept these terms?*

Tamsin looked at her people. Brannoc, arms crossed, jaw set — already agreeing, she could tell, already planning routes. Wren, pale and steady, giving her the smallest nod. Sef, wide-eyed, gripping Brannoc's sleeve, and Tamsin's chest tightened, because the terms named the child too, and she had brought Sef into this hall, into this danger—

But Sef wasn't afraid. Sef was looking at the Queen with an expression Tamsin had never seen on the child's face before: recognition. Longing. Like someone hearing a language they'd dreamed in.

*Tamsin.* The Queen's voice cut into her skull again. *Your answer.*

"Yes," Tamsin said. "We accept."

*Then go.* The Queen settled onto her throne, and the audience was ending the way a door closes — total and immediate. *Go, and learn to sing, taster. You have one moon. And know this:* the grey fires fixed on her one final time, *I do not grant chances. I spend them. Spend yours well.*

The hall began to dim around them, the fae ranks dissolving backward into the dark like a forest absorbing its own trees, and the last thing Tamsin saw before a grey-cloaked usher gestured them toward a side passage was the throne, empty-seeming and vast, and the faint crack of amusement at its owner's mouth already smoothed away.

* * *

They walked out in silence, guided along corridors of living stone where the green light pulsed slow as a heartbeat. It was Brannoc who finally spoke, his voice pitched low even though no fae were near enough to hear.

"One moon," he rumbled. "To teach a lass who cannae carry a tune in a bucket to sing a covenant older than the mountains. Through poisoned blood. At the bottom of a haunted well." He shook his head, beard bristling. "I've run cargo through war zones with better odds."

"Thank you, Brannoc," Tamsin said. "Very comforting."

"I dinnae do comfort. I do honesty." But his hand landed on her shoulder, brief and heavy, and stayed a moment longer than it needed to.

Wren walked with her staff tapping double-time, muttering — and Tamsin realized, with a lurch, that the old woman was crying without any change in her expression, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her cheeks. "One moon," Wren whispered. "The rites take a season to teach properly. A year. I'll have to strip it to the bone, teach you only the spine of it, and pray the Deepwell remembers the rest—" She stopped herself, drew a breath, and looked at Tamsin with red-rimmed eyes that were, underneath the fear, fiercely alight. "You mad, glorious girl. *Sing through me.* Where did you find that?"

"In a kitchen," Tamsin said. "Everything I know is from a kitchen."

Only Sef seemed untouched by the weight of it. The child trotted alongside, turning to walk backward, facing Tamsin, and their eyes were bright as coins.

"You stood up to her," Sef said. "Nobody stands up to the Ash Queen. The stories say even the first kings knelt."

"I knelt on the inside," Tamsin said. "It just doesn't show."

"What's the Deepwell like?"

"I don't know yet."

"I do," Sef said quietly, and stopped walking. "I've been dreaming about it since we crossed the border. There's water at the bottom that isn't water. And a ring of stones where the singers used to stand. And one stone is broken, and nobody's fixed it, and it's *waiting* for something." The child's voice dropped. "It's waiting for a voice, Tamsin. I can hear it from here."

The corridor hummed faintly around them, and Tamsin thought of the harmonies they'd heard beneath Thornfell weeks ago, the Choir still half-alive in the dark, and felt the enormity of what she'd just accepted settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

One moon. One chance. Two armies hunting them if she failed.

Behind them, far back in the dark of the court, something began — very faintly — to sing. A single high line, wordless, winding up through the stone like smoke. Sef spun toward it, transfigured. Merrin closed his eyes as if struck.

"That's a bridge-line," he breathed. "That's the *third voice*. Tamsin — the Queen still has bridge-singers. She's had them all along, hidden. Which means she knows exactly what the covenant requires." He opened his eyes, and there was something new in them, something hard and bright. "Which means she expects us to fail. Unless—"

"Unless what?" Tamsin asked.

Merrin looked at her, and then at Sef, and his face went carefully blank in the way Tamsin had learned meant he was protecting her from a thought.

"Unless the third voice she's hiding," he said slowly, "is meant to be a spare. In case hers runs out."

And far ahead, at the end of the corridor where the twilight of the verge spilled gold and grey through a crack in the world, the singing stopped — mid-note, like a door closing — and did not begin again.
