## 19. Aldous Catches Up

Aldous Vane had spent thirty years learning that the world told you everything, if you knew how to listen. The wind through the verge's edge told him the girl was close. The birds had gone quiet in a pattern he recognized — not the silence of empty country but the held breath of hidden things. And the moss on the northern stones was bruised where boots had crossed it, hours old, moving fast.

Four of them. Maybe five. A dwarf who walked like a man used to carrying weight. A child whose footprints were too light and too far apart. An old woman leaving a scent trail of herbs and woodsmoke. And Tamsin Thackeray, walking the way she always walked — heel to toe, silent, a habit he had drilled into her himself when she was nine years old and still flinched at loud voices.

He knew her stride better than he knew his own daughter's. He had never had a daughter. He had had a project, and then somewhere along the way — he could no longer name the year — the project had become the closest thing to a child he would ever permit himself.

It changed nothing. He reminded himself of that as he signaled his men into position among the standing stones. It changed nothing, and if he told himself it enough times, perhaps by morning he would believe it.

"Remember," he said quietly to the six inquisitors crouched around him. "The map is the prize. The singer is secondary. The witch and the dwarf are expendable. The child—" he paused. "The child is for the Duke. Alive."

"And the taster?"

"Tamsin comes to me unharmed or not at all." He said it flatly, a logistics instruction, nothing more. "If she dies, every one of you will wish Corvane got to you first."

* * *

He had prepared this moment for weeks. That was the trouble with loving your work: you rehearsed even the parts that hurt.

Aldous had built Tamsin from raw material. He had found her at seven, an orphan of the border wars with a tongue too clever and a stomach that refused to quit, and he had seen what she could become. He had fed her the first poison himself, holding her hand while she retched, telling her it was medicine. He had taught her to read a man's intentions in the set of his shoulders, to taste fear in wine, to lie with her face while telling truth with her mouth. Every skill she was using against him now, he had given her. There was a symmetry to it that he almost admired.

Almost.

The reports from Marrowgate had troubled him more than he admitted. She had escaped the inquisitors there with a stolen boat and a disgraced singer, which was luck. But the tollhouse ambush — that was not luck. She had survived a drugged blade calibrated to drop a warhorse, walked out of a burning building, and taken two of his men off their feet before they understood what was happening. His people had trained her to survive poison. They had never trained her to fight like that.

Which meant someone else was teaching her now. Which meant she was growing in directions he had not planned.

That was the word for it, he realized, watching the treeline where she would soon appear. Not betrayal. Growth. A plant bending toward a light he hadn't provided.

He hated the light. He intended to cut it down.

* * *

They came out of the trees an hour past dusk, strung along a deer path toward the shimmer in the air ahead — the verge, the place where Thornfell stopped being entirely the world of men. Aldous let them get within forty paces of the stones before he stepped out into the open, alone, hands visible, an old man in a plain grey coat.

"Tamsin."

She froze. Even at distance, in the last grey light, he saw the whole argument happen in her body — the half-step forward, the hand catching herself, the stillness she wore like armor.

"Aldous." Her voice was steady. He'd taught her that too. "You came yourself."

"You thought I'd send boys?" He spread his hands. "You're the finest thing I ever made. I don't delegate my finest work."

Behind her, the dwarf shifted, and Aldous catalogued him without looking: Brannoc Stonehand, smuggler, owed a debt by a dead man named Thackeray who had never been any relation to the girl at all — a fiction Aldous had constructed and maintained for fifteen years. The witch stood slightly ahead of the others, one hand inside her cloak, and he gave her the respect of attention; hedge-witches died hard and took people with them. The singer hung back, useless, frightened, exactly as the files said. The child watched him with eyes that were the wrong color for a human child, and something in that gaze made the back of his neck prickle in a way he hadn't felt since his first year in the field.

"I'm not here for a fight," Aldous said. "I'm here to offer you a way home."

"There's no home left for me there."

"There's a pardon. Full and sealed under the king's own signet — such authority as the king still has." He let that barb show, just a little. "Corvane drafted it himself. Whatever you think you've learned about me, about the taster program, about why your blood does what it does — none of it needs to matter. Walk away from these people. Give me the map. Go south, take a house on the coast, take a new name, live to be old. You've earned old, Tamsin. You of all people know how rarely anyone earns old."

She said nothing. Good. Silence meant she was listening. He pressed on.

"You think I betrayed you. I want you to hear the truth, because you deserve truth even if you'll hate it. Everything I did, I did because the alternative was worse. Your immunity wasn't cruelty — it was survival, engineered before you could be killed by the very world we were sending you into. And yes, your blood is a weapon. Do you know what else is a weapon? A hammer. A fire. Anything depends on the hand that holds it. I held you carefully, girl. Ask anyone I've ever handled carelessly whether they'd trade places with you."

"You lied to me about my father."

The words landed harder than he expected. He kept his face still.

"The man you called your father was a fiction I wrote to give you something to hold onto. Children need a dead parent more than they need a living handler. It's kinder. Grief has dignity. Handler-hunger doesn't."

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said to me," Tamsin said, "and it's monstrous."

"It's true," Aldous said. "I've never once lied to you about anything that mattered. I've only ever told you truths late. There's a difference, and someday, when you're older and the bleeding stops, you'll understand it."

* * *

Behind her eyes, he could see the war. He knew every expression she owned; he had commissioned most of them. This one — jaw tight, breath shallow through the nose, gaze fixed slightly left of his throat — was the one she wore when she was tasting something bitter and deciding whether to swallow.

He played his last card gently.

"Look at them, Tamsin. Truly look. A smuggler who'll sell you the moment the price beats his sentiment — and it will, I've already sent the offer. A witch older than the treaty, who remembers the covenant well enough to know what it cost the last time humans sang it. A tavern tenor who couldn't protect himself from a dockside brawl, let alone from what's coming. And a fae-blooded child who is, right now, the single most valuable object in the known world, being dragged toward a mountain by people who need it more than they love it." He softened his voice. "You've spent your whole life being used, so you've learned to see use everywhere. But ask yourself: what do these people need you for? The bridge-line. The blood. The song. You're not their friend, girl. You're their instrument. The only difference between me and them is that I'm honest about holding the handle."

For a moment — one long, cold moment — he thought it had worked. He saw her glance back at them, saw the doubt move through her like a shadow of a bird crossing a wall.

Then the child stepped forward and took her hand.

Small fingers, wrong-jointed, lacing into hers without hesitation, without calculation. The child looked up at her, and whatever passed between them was not in any report Aldous had ever read, because it could not be written down. It was the oldest language there was: *I am small and I have chosen you.*

Tamsin turned back to him.

"No," she said.

Just that. No speech, no justification. He had expected anger, argument, the chance to counter her point by point. Instead she gave him a single syllable, and he understood, with the clarity that only arrives too late, that he had lost the argument before it began — because she was not arguing. Arguing means you still believe the other person can be persuaded. She had simply closed the account.

"Tamsin—"

"You asked once," she said, "why I never flinched when the poisons got worse. Do you remember what I answered?"

He remembered. *Because you told me it was medicine.* He said nothing.

"It was never the poison I survived," she said. "It was believing you. I'm done surviving that."

* * *

He gave the signal because there was nothing left to say, and because a man who talks instead of acting dies talking.

The inquisitors rose from the stones in a practiced crescent, crossbows leveled, and the night tore open. Brannoc bellowed and hauled the child behind a fallen monolith; the witch was already moving, both hands full of light now, wards blooming around the company like frost-flowers of gold. The first bolt shattered against her shield with a sound like a struck bell. The second found the gap before the shield closed.

Wren went down without crying out. That was the worst of it, Aldous thought later — the old woman folded like a coat dropped from a hook, and the sound she made was almost polite, an apology for the inconvenience.

Then Tamsin moved, and he watched his own work come back at him. She used the singer as cover — pushed Merrin Vale bodily into the crossbows' sightline for half a heartbeat, long enough to break their aim, an act of ruthless arithmetic he had drilled into her at fourteen. She came through the smoke low and fast, put a knife into the nearest inquisitor's thigh with a taster's precision — the great vessel, bleed but don't kill, though he suspected she no longer cared about the distinction — and rolled behind the monolith where the dwarf was roaring and throwing stones the size of loaves.

"Fall back!" Aldous snapped. "Fall back, don't pursue —"

But his men were young, and young men chase. Two of them broke cover after her, and the witch, bleeding out on the grass, lifted one hand and spoke a word that made the ground itself refuse them. They stumbled as if the earth had tilted. The dwarf's thrown stone caught one across the temple. The other made the mistake of reaching for the child, and Tamsin was simply *there*, the way water is there when a fire needs it, and the man learned in one bright instant of pain why the Duke had wanted her alive.

"Enough!" Aldous's voice cracked across the field like a whip, and to their credit, his people obeyed. He had built them too. They withdrew to the stones, breathing hard, three wounded, one unconscious, and watched the company gather their witch and melt backward into the shimmer of the verge.

* * *

Tamsin stopped at the boundary. Of course she did. She looked back at him across twenty paces of darkening grass, Wren's arm over her shoulders, the child pressed against her side, and Aldous saw that she was waiting — giving him one last chance to say something that mattered, or perhaps wanting to see his face when she crossed.

"Your witch is dying," he called. "I have surgeons. Come back, and I'll save her."

It was cruel, and he knew it was cruel, and he said it anyway because it was also true and because some stubborn part of him still believed truth was the highest currency he possessed.

Tamsin's answer was to lift Wren higher against her shoulder and step backward into the twilight, the verge closing around her like water around a stone. The last thing he saw was her eyes, and they held no hatred at all. That was the part that would keep him awake. Hatred would have meant he still counted. What he saw instead was the look she gave a dish she had tasted and found merely harmless — a professional assessment, filed and dismissed.

She had weighed him and found him safe to walk away from.

* * *

They made camp at the stones because pursuit through the verge at night was suicide, and Aldous sat apart from his men with a map he no longer needed to read, doing the arithmetic he had been avoiding all evening.

Corvane would want a report. *Engaged the fugitives at the verge's edge. Recovered: nothing. Casualties: four. The map remains at large. The taster refuses terms.* Four lines that would end his career, unless he brought something more.

He turned the evening over again, scene by scene, the way he turned over every failure, hunting the flaw. Where had he miscalculated? The offer had been generous. The leverage had been precise. The psychology —

The psychology had been his own.

That was the flaw, and seeing it was like swallowing ice. He had assumed Tamsin feared abandonment, because he had raised her on abandonment, rationed affection like a drug, kept her grateful and hungry. He had assumed she would come back to the hand that fed her, because every creature he had ever handled came back to the feeding hand eventually.

But she had spent her whole life eating from his hand, and she had finally noticed she was starving.

And the child — the child had done what he could never have predicted, because it had done the one thing he had never once done in thirty years of tradecraft. It had offered something for free.

Aldous looked at his hands in the firelight. Steady. Always steady. They had held a seven-year-old's hair back while poison emptied her, and they had signed the orders that made the poison necessary, and they had never once shaken, and he had told himself for decades that steadiness was the same thing as strength.

Across the fire, one of the young inquisitors — the one whose thigh she had opened, bandaged now, feverish — muttered, "Why didn't we take her? We had numbers. Why did you call us off?"

"Because she'd have killed three more of you getting free, and we'd have gained a corpse," Aldous said. Then, because the boy deserved better than a lie: "And because I wanted her to choose it. I thought — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought I was the thing she'd choose."

The boy stared at him, uncomprehending, and Aldous almost laughed. Yes. Exactly that. Thirty years of reading every soul in the kingdom, and he had misread the only one he'd written himself.

* * *

Near midnight, the witch's blood on the grass began to glow faintly, and the verge itself shivered along its whole length, a ripple like wind over wheat, running north toward the mountain. His sentries reported it in whispers: the shimmer was *moving*, thickening, drawing itself toward Thornfell like a tide answering a moon.

Aldous stood at the boundary for a long time, an old man alone at the edge of two worlds, listening.

Beneath the wind, beneath the earth, faint as memory, he heard it: singing. Many voices, deep under the mountain, waking.

He had spent his life believing that everything could be bought, bred, broken, or bargained with. Standing at the verge's edge with his career in ashes and his one creation gone beyond his reach, Aldous Vane heard the old covenant stirring in the dark like a heart beginning to beat again — and for the first time since he was a young man, he did not know what to do next.

Behind him, the wounded inquisitor groaned. Ahead, the mountain sang.

Aldous turned back to his men and gave the order that would define whatever remained of him: "Break camp. We ride for the Deepwell — and send a bird ahead. Tell the Duke the taster isn't running anymore."
