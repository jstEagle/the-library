## 20. What Wren Knows

The wound had a smell to it. Old Wren knew that smell the way she knew her own hands — yarrow and iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a dark room. Inquisitor steel. They coated their blades with something out of Corvane's laboratories, something meant not to kill quick but to kill *certainly*, slow as rot through bread.

She'd been dressing it with comfrey and willowbark for three days now, down in the smuggler's cave while the snow piled up outside like a second mountain. She knew what it was doing inside her. She'd watched it do the same thing to a poacher once, back when she was young enough to think poultices could argue with poison. Eleven days, that man lasted. He'd been strong.

Wren was seventy-one years old and mostly held together by spite and nettle tea.

So she did what any sensible hedge-witch would do with eleven days left, give or take: she made a list.

*What they need to know. What I can still teach. What I can't fix.*

The list was short, which was the worst part of it. Everything important always was.

* * *

She found Tamsin at the cave mouth just past dawn, standing where the firelight gave out and the snow-light began, watching the passes the way a hawk watches a hedgerow. The girl never truly rested. Even asleep she lay like a drawn bow.

"Sit," Wren said. "You've been up all night listening for riders."

"I sleep."

"You *doze*. There's a difference, and the difference is you." She lowered herself onto the flat stone beside her — slower than she liked, her hip complaining — and pressed a clay cup into Tamsin's hands. "Drink. It's just chamomile. If I wanted you dead I'd have saved myself the trouble of three days' nursing."

Tamsin drank. That was one thing about her; she trusted Wren's cups without question, which was either wisdom or exhaustion, and Wren suspected it was both.

For a while neither of them spoke. The snow came down soft and enormous, filling the world's edges. Somewhere behind them in the cave, Sef was talking in their sleep — little half-sung fragments, notes that didn't belong to any human scale. Brannoc snored in counterpoint. Merrin, who claimed he couldn't sleep without a roof, slept anyway, curled around his own silence like a man hugging an old scar.

"The wound's talking to me," Wren said finally. "And I've decided to listen."

Tamsin went very still. Tasters were good at stillness; it was the first thing the trade taught you, before flavors, before survival. Don't react. Your face is the king's face. But this wasn't court stillness. This was a girl bracing.

"How long?"

"Long enough. A fortnight if I'm lucky, less if I'm stupid, and I've never been lucky." Wren waved off whatever was building behind Tamsin's teeth. "Don't waste my time grieving. Grief is heavy and I'm done carrying things. I've got teaching to do instead, and you're the only student left who can learn it."

"Brannoc—"

"Knows roads. Sef knows songs they can't explain yet. Merrin knows the old music but not why it works. None of them know the *structure*, Tamsin. Nobody alive knows it but me, and I learned it from a woman who learned it from a stone, if you can believe that, and I only half believed it myself until I heard the harmonies under Thornfell and felt my own bones agree with them." She pulled her cloak tighter. "So. Lesson one. And then lesson two, and however many lessons your stubborn skull can hold before the moon turns."

"The moon turns in nineteen days."

"Nineteen days. Good. I'd hate to run short." Wren smiled, and knew from Tamsin's expression that it wasn't a comforting smile. "Fetch me the map. And the charcoal. We're going to draw the covenant the way it actually is, not the way the ballads say it is. Ballads are wrong about everything except the tune."

* * *

They spread the dying stranger's map on the floor of the cave's inner chamber, weighted at the corners with four smooth stones Wren had chosen carefully — river-stones, each one sung over in its day, because paper this old deserved courtesy. The musical notation crawled across the vellum in place of roads, those strange floating figures Merrin had wept over the first night he saw them.

"Now," Wren said, "forget everything the ballads told you. Forget 'the Choir binds realm to realm in friendship everlasting.' Friendship everlasting doesn't leave empty thrones in a hollow under a mountain. Here's what the covenant actually is."

She drew in the ash-dust with her finger, because drawing in dust made people listen; it was an old trick and it worked.

"A covenant is a song with three voices. Not metaphorically. Literally. Three lines of music, braided. The first voice is mortal — human lungs, human breath, human time. It carries the *promise*: what we give, what we ask, what we'll hold to. The second voice is fae — and don't let anyone tell you the fae sing like us, because they don't, they sing like weather, like the sound a season makes turning. They carry the *answer*: what they give, what they ask, what they'll hold to. Two voices make a bargain. That part's simple. Any two creatures with voices can strike a bargain."

"But two voices aren't enough," Tamsin said slowly. Her eyes had gone to the notation on the map, tracking it the way she tracked poison in a cup — reading the structure beneath the surface.

"No. Because bargains drift. Mortals forget. Fae interpret. A promise made in spring gets read differently in winter, and both sides are sure they're keeping it, and that's how wars start. So the covenant has a third voice." Wren tapped the center of the map, where the notation gathered densest, knotting itself into patterns no single throat could carry. "The bridge. A voice that belongs to neither side and both. It doesn't carry the promise or the answer. It carries the *remembering*. Every phrase the other two voices sing, the bridge holds in between — holds the mortal's words in fae understanding and the fae's words in mortal understanding, so the bargain never drifts, never curdles, never gets forgotten by accident or misread on purpose. The bridge is the covenant's memory. Kill the bridge voice, and the whole song falls apart within a generation."

"And that's what happened." Tamsin's voice was flat. "A generation ago."

"That's what happened." Wren let the silence sit a moment, because some truths needed weight. "Your king's father — may his ghost be quieter than he was in life — got it into his head that the fae courts were plotting. Maybe they were; courts plot, it's what they're for. But instead of sending envoys, he sent soldiers to the Hollow Court during the singing. Killed every singer they found. Humans and fae alike, and worst of all—" she tapped the knotted center of the notation again, "—they killed the bridges. All of them. There weren't many. There never are. A bridge voice isn't born every year, or every decade. It takes a particular blood, a particular ear, a particular willingness to stand in the doorway between two houses and be claimed by neither."

"And without the bridge, the covenant unraveled."

"Like a rope with the middle strand cut. The mortal line kept being sung — badly, in bits, by people who remembered fragments. The fae line too, somewhere under the mountain; you heard it yourself, that first night at the first notation. Fae are long. They can hold a note for a hundred years waiting for the harmony. But nothing joined them. Nothing remembered for them. So the realms drifted apart, and the passes closed, and the free cities lost the protection they'd forgotten they had, and men like Corvane grew fat in the space where the song used to be." Wren coughed, deep and wet, and spat into the fire. "Corvane isn't trying to destroy the covenant, girl. It's already destroyed. He's trying to make sure it stays destroyed — and to build something else in its place. Something with only one voice. His."

* * *

Tamsin sat with that for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, in the voice she used for hard questions in front of kings: "Then what am I?"

Wren had been waiting for that question since the tollhouse. Since the drugged blade. Since the moment she'd peeled back the girl's sleeve and seen the scars — not the honest scars of a working life, but the neat, spaced, deliberate marks of someone bled on a schedule, starting far too young.

"Come here," she said. "Give me your arm."

Tamsin gave it. Of course she did. That obedience was going to get her killed someday, Wren thought, unless it got her killed in nineteen days instead, and there was no use saying so.

Wren turned the arm over in the firelight. The old taster's scars ran up the forearm like a ladder — the small punctures of a childhood spent swallowing death in measured doses. But beneath them, fainter, older, were the others. The ones Tamsin had never asked about, because children don't question the medicine they're given, and by the time they're old enough to question it, the story has grown over the truth like bark over a nail.

"These," Wren said, touching the faint ones. "Tell me what you were told these were."

"Tonic doses. Immunity training. Every taster gets them." Tamsin's jaw tightened. "That's what Aldous said. That's what they all said."

"Aldous said." Wren snorted. "Aldous says a great many things. No, child. I've brewed tonics for forty years, and I've brewed immunity courses for tasters, and I know what they look like and what they cost and what they leave. This isn't that. Look at the spacing. Look at the compounds — you can still see the discoloration, if you know herbs, and I know herbs. Wolfsbane and silverleaf and a fae reagent I couldn't name until last month, when I saw the same scarring on a fae captive Corvane's people had been *feeding* something to." She looked up and met the girl's eyes, and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the last kindness left in her kit. "This is bridge-blood brewing. Someone took a child born with the makings of a bridge voice and dosed her from infancy with everything poisonous to the fae, until the poison became her. Until her blood itself became a weapon against the very realm she was built to speak for."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, Sef murmured another fragment of impossible melody.

"They made me a weapon," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. It was a wall going up, brick by brick, the way it always did.

"They made you a *key*," Wren said, "and then they threw away the door. Think about it properly, and stop flinching. Why would Corvane's people want a taster whose blood kills fae? A taster! Poison-tasting is a fine trade, but it doesn't need engineered blood; it needs a strong stomach and a short memory. No. You were bred and brewed for the covenant, Tamsin. You were meant to be the third voice. Someone at court — before the old king died, before Corvane — was trying to rebuild what the soldiers tore down. Trying to grow a new bridge. And then the plans changed hands, or the plans changed minds, and the weapon they'd built got filed away in a kitchen and forgotten."

"Forgotten," Tamsin repeated. Her voice had gone thin. "I tasted for six years. I nearly died four times. I was *good* at it. And the whole time I was—"

"—a bridge nobody sang across. Yes." Wren took the girl's hand, old fingers over young ones, and felt the tremor she would never acknowledge. "I know what it is to find out your whole life was somebody else's plan. I found out at fifty that the woman who taught me herbs had marked me for the same purpose and thought better of it. Thought better of it and said nothing, and let me live small and safe and ignorant. I was furious for a decade. Then I understood. Fear makes cowards of the kind ones too." She squeezed. "But here's the thing your fury won't let you hear yet, so I'll say it anyway and you can hate me later: what they brewed into you is exactly what the covenant needs. The poison and the bridge are the same substance, girl. Blood that belongs to neither realm and can touch both. They meant it to kill. It can remember instead. It can *hold*."

"If I can learn to sing," Tamsin said bitterly. "Which I can't. Merrin says I hum like a kettle."

"Merrin says that because Merrin teaches the way conservatories teach, God rest his patience, all breath and posture and terror of the wrong note." Wren laughed, and paid for it with a coughing fit that took a full minute to put down. When she could speak again, she said, "You don't need to sing *well*. Do you understand? The bridge line isn't beauty. It's accuracy. It's holding two truths at once without dropping either. And you have spent your entire life doing precisely that — tasting the king's food while knowing it might be your death, loving a mentor who was lying to you, walking between the crown's law and the smugglers' mercy. You've been singing the bridge line your whole life, Tamsin Thackeray. You just haven't been making noise while you did it."

* * *

They worked until the fire burned low. Wren taught the way she brewed: measure twice, taste once, write everything down. She made Tamsin copy the covenant's structure into the margins of the map — three staves, not one; the mortal line running straight and steady, the fae line curling like smoke, and between them the bridge, a thread that touched both and belonged to neither, holding every phrase in suspension like a hand catching falling water.

She taught her the intervals. Not with a harp — hers had gone to a debt years ago — but with cups of water, struck with a spoon, tuned by adding drops. Tamsin's ear, Wren discovered, was obscene. Perfect. Of course it was. A tongue that could detect hemlock at four parts per thousand was never going to miss a quarter-tone.

"She hears it," Wren murmured to herself, watching the girl adjust a cup by three drops of water without being told it was sharp. "Sixty years I've waited to see an ear like that."

"What was that?"

"Nothing. Again. Third interval. Hold both pitches in your head at once before you pour."

By midday Tamsin could strike the opening of the bridge line unerringly — humming it, barely voiced, a thread of sound that made the fire lean and made Sef sit bolt upright in their blankets, eyes huge, whispering, *"that's the missing one, that's the one that goes underneath."*

Wren felt something loosen in her chest that had nothing to do with the wound. Nineteen days. It could be enough. It might be enough. It would have to be.

* * *

In the afternoon the pain came, as it always did, riding in on its slow tide, and Wren sent Tamsin out to tend the horses partly for the errand and mostly for the mercy of not being watched. She sat alone with her list and added to it in her head.

*What they need to know:* done, or near enough. Structure, intervals, the Deepwell's requirements. The rite itself — the actual binding — she'd teach tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that until the words lived in the girl's spine instead of her notebook.

*What I can still teach:* tomorrow. And maybe the day after.

*What I can't fix:* everything else. The armies coming. The moon turning. The fact that a bridge voice, once sung true, is sung *through* — that the covenant doesn't borrow the bridge's blood, it spends it. Wren had read that in the stone-carvings forty years ago and hoped to God she'd mistranslated. She hadn't. She'd checked three times.

The old king's soldiers had known it too, she suspected. It was easier to murder singers when you told yourself the alternative was asking them to die.

Well. Tamsin would learn it soon enough, and she'd learn it from Wren, plainly, with time to be angry about it — time Wren herself had never been given. That was the whole point of teaching, in the end. You handed down not just the knowledge but the chance to refuse it. Nobody had offered Wren that chance. She intended to offer it, even knowing — knowing the way you know weather in your knees — that the girl wouldn't take it. Some people were built to walk into doorways. It was a terrible thing to build someone to be, and a magnificent one.

Tamsin came back in with snow in her hair and wood for the fire, and paused in the entrance, looking at Wren sitting straight-backed against the stone despite the pain, and Wren watched her do the arithmetic — the limp, the gray lips, the smell of the wound — and arrive at the number Wren had already arrived at, and choose, visibly, to set it aside and keep working. Like a taster setting aside fear. Like a bridge holding two truths.

"Lesson two?" Tamsin said.

"Lesson two," Wren agreed, and reached for the charcoal. "Tonight we learn the rite of opening. And Tamsin — before we start, fetch Sef. Whatever comes next, the child deserves to hear it. They're not cargo. They're the second voice, and they've been waiting their whole short life for someone to tell them so."

Outside, far down the valley, a crow lifted off a dead pine and flew north, fast, like something carrying word.

Wren watched it go and thought: *nineteen days, and the enemy already knows where we are.*

She picked up the charcoal anyway.
