## 21. Learning the Song

Merrin Vale had taught voice to duchesses and to the king's own choristers, and he had never once been afraid of a student.

He was afraid of Tamsin Thackeray.

Not of her — that was the strange part. He'd watched her gut a man at the tollhouse with a drugged blade still in her arm, and fear hadn't entered into it. What frightened him was the teaching itself, because he could already see what she was going to be, and he knew exactly how much it was going to cost her to get there.

They made their school in a hollow below the high passes, a fold of stone where a stream ran cold and clear over slate, and the water's sound covered them from above. Wren had chosen it before she grew too weak to walk far — "the mountain listens here," she'd said, "and forgets to tell anyone" — and now it was theirs: Brannoc off checking the eastern roads, Wren sleeping propped against her pack with her grey braid over her shoulder like a rope someone had abandoned, Sef crouched on a flat rock with their chin on their knees, watching everything with those eyes that caught light wrong, green going gold at the rim.

And Tamsin, standing in the shallows in her boots because she refused to take them off even to learn to sing, arms crossed like a woman awaiting sentencing.

"You have to breathe," Merrin said.

"I am breathing."

"You're *bracing*. There's a difference, and it's the whole art." He stepped closer, circling her the way he used to circle nervous choirboys. "You taste poison for a living. You hold your body like a shield. Every muscle you own is standing between you and the world."

"That's kept me alive."

"I know." And it had; he'd seen the scars when Wren dressed the blade-wound, the lattice of old marks down her forearms, a map of every dish that might have killed her. "But a shield doesn't sing. A shield gets hit. You have to put all of that down for three minutes at a time. Can you do that?"

Tamsin looked at him the way she looked at unfamiliar food — assessing, weighing, deciding whether this was the kind of thing that killed people. Then she uncrossed her arms. Not relaxed. Uncrossed. It was, he would think later, the bravest small thing he ever saw her do.

* * *

The trouble — and he'd known it from the first night by the fire, when she'd hummed half a bar without noticing she was doing it — was not her voice.

Her voice was a scandal. It was low and dark and slightly rough at the edges, like a bell that had been rung in weather, and when she spoke plainly it did nothing, but underneath the speaking there was a resonance that made the hair on his arms stand up. The bridge-line of the covenant needed a voice that could carry both weight and truth, and hers had been sitting inside her all these years like a coin at the bottom of a well, untouched, unspent.

The trouble was that no one had ever asked her for anything she wanted to give.

"Again," he said. "Just the first phrase. Don't reach for the note. Let it come to you."

She reached for the note. It came out thin and strangled and she stopped immediately, jaw tight, and he saw it happen — the flinch, the retreat behind the shield. Waiting to be told she'd failed.

"Tamsin."

"I can do it again."

"That's not what I was going to say." He crouched by the stream, wet his hand, flicked water at her — childish, deliberate, and it worked, because she blinked out of the flinch and glared at him. Good. Glaring was honest. "When you were a girl, in the palace kitchens. Did anyone ever teach you a song?"

The question landed harder than he'd meant it to. He watched her decide whether to answer.

"The cooks sang," she said finally. "Working songs. About bread and bad husbands." A pause. "I wasn't allowed to join. Tasters eat apart. Everything about me was kept separate — my food, my cups, my room. If I'd learned to sing with them, I'd have been..." She searched for the word.

"One of them," Merrin said quietly.

"Yes."

"And wanting that would have hurt more than not wanting at all. So you stopped wanting things."

She said nothing, which was its own confession. Behind her, Sef had gone very still on the rock, the way they went still when adults were saying true things near them.

"So we're not going to start with notes," Merrin said. "We're going to start with want. Sing me something you're not supposed to know."

"What?"

"A working song. A bread song. A bad-husband song. Something the cooks sang when they thought no one important was listening. You memorized them anyway, didn't you? Even though you weren't allowed."

Her ears went pink. It was, he thought, the most human he had ever seen her.

"They're stupid songs," she said.

"Sing me one anyway."

* * *

She sang him a song about a miller's wife who drowned her husband in his own ale barrel, and it was stupid and bawdy and rhythmically a disaster, and halfway through the second verse something happened to Tamsin Thackeray's face.

It started around the eyes. The permanent sentry-posture of them softened. Her mouth kept trying not to smile and losing, and by the last line — where the miller's wife rolls the barrel into the river and tells the neighbors he's gone to market — she was laughing, actually laughing, a startled ugly snorting laugh she clearly hadn't heard from herself in twenty years, and Sef was giggling on the rock, and even Wren opened one eye and pronounced, "That song got two men hanged in Dunmarrow, and worth it," before going back to sleep.

"There," Merrin said, when the laughter had burned down to embers. "That. Do you feel where that lived? In your belly. Low and loose. That's your instrument, Tamsin. Not your throat. Your throat is just where it comes out."

"It felt—" She stopped. Frowned, as if the feeling were an unfamiliar flavor she needed to identify. "Easy. It felt easy."

"Because you wanted to sing it. Wanting carries the note. Now — same feeling, new melody." He held up a hand. "Don't panic. It's only four notes."

It was not only four notes. It was the opening phrase of the covenant-song as Wren had reconstructed it from memory and fragment, the mortal line, the foundation the other voices would stand on. Merrin sang it once, slow, breaking it into pieces small enough to swallow.

Tamsin got it wrong six times. On the seventh try she got it right, and the stream went quiet.

Not literally quiet — the water kept running. But the sound of it seemed to lean aside, the way a crowd parts, and for the space of one held note the hollow beneath the high passes listened to a king's food-taster singing four notes of a dead covenant, and approved.

Sef slid off the rock. "Do it again," they whispered, urgent, grabbing at her sleeve. "Do it again, it liked it, it *liked*—"

"Sef." Merrin's voice was gentle but firm. "Breathe. We go slowly. The mountain has waited a generation; it can wait another day."

But Sef was staring up at Tamsin with an expression Merrin recognized from mirrors, from years ago, from the conservatory before Corvane — the look of a child hearing the thing they were born for. And Tamsin was looking back at them, and then at her own hands, as if they belonged to someone else.

"It didn't feel like the kitchen songs," she said. "Those felt like me. This felt like..." She shook her head.

"Like being used?" Merrin asked carefully.

"No." She was quiet a long moment. "Like being *answered*. Nobody's ever answered me before. They've tested me. Watched what I survived. But nothing's ever come back."

"Well," Merrin said, and found his throat unexpectedly tight. "Get used to it. That's what a choir is. It's just a lot of people answering each other until nobody's alone anymore."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the morning worked on them.

He built her voice the way he'd been trained to build any voice, but gentler, because she broke differently than choirboys broke. Choirboys flinched from criticism. Tamsin flinched from praise — actually flinched, shoulders dropping half an inch, when he told her the seventh phrase was beautiful. He learned to say less and mean more. He learned that she practiced in secret, that if he walked away for ten minutes and came back she'd have a passage perfect and would pretend she didn't know how. He learned that she could hold a pitch longer than anyone he'd ever trained because holding steady under pressure was the entire architecture of her life.

And Sef — Sef was the miracle he hadn't dared hope for.

The child couldn't read notation and barely carried a tune alone. But when Tamsin sang the mortal line, Sef lay flat on the warm rock with their eyes closed and *answered*, wordless, a thread of sound that slipped between and around the melody like water finding cracks in stone. It wasn't harmony as Merrin had been taught it. It was older. It was the fae line of the covenant doing what it had done since before humans wrote music down: not accompanying, but conversing.

When the two lines locked together for the first time — truly locked, mortal ground and fae air meeting in the middle — the stream ran backward for the length of a breath.

Merrin sat down hard on a stone.

"Was that supposed to happen?" Tamsin asked, alarmed, watching the water resume its senses.

"No," Merrin said. "Yes. I don't know. Wren!"

Wren woke with the ease of a woman who had only been pretending to sleep, which was most of the time. She looked at the stream, then at the two singers, then at Merrin, and smiled with all five of her remaining teeth.

"Third time in recorded history," she said. "First time it happened to people who weren't trying to be famous about it." She levered herself upright, wincing, one hand pressed below her ribs where the wound Aldous's men had given her still wept slow poison that her own craft held at bay but could not cure. "Keep doing that. Whatever it costs, keep doing it."

"Wren, you should rest—"

"I'll rest when the moon turns or when I don't, boy, don't fuss." But her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned away to hide it, and Merrin let her, because some mercies are silence.

* * *

At midday Brannoc came back down the eastern road with snow in his beard and news in his fists, and the news was bad, because the news was always bad now: Corvane's columns moving in the valleys, beacon-smoke on three peaks, refugees on the smuggler paths with bundles and dead-eyed children. Marrowgate's name spoken in the tense way people speak a name they're afraid will soon be a ruin.

The company gathered around the fire and ate hard bread and said little.

Merrin watched Tamsin listen. She'd changed in the weeks since the tollhouse — not softer, exactly. Aimed. She took reports the way she'd once taken a suspect dish: a small sip, held on the tongue, every component identified. And he saw her do the arithmetic she always did, the terrible quiet arithmetic of a woman who had spent her whole life as an instrument of other people's survival, weighing how many strangers a song was worth.

"Don't," he said, quietly, when the others had drifted to their tasks.

"Don't what?"

"Do the sums. I can see you doing the sums. One voice against an army, what's the exchange rate." He poked the fire. "I did the same sums for years, sitting in that gilded cage at court. My voice against Corvane's patience. Whether one refusal was worth my whole life. And you know what I learned?"

"What?"

"The sums are a lie. They make it look like you're choosing whether to spend yourself. You're not. You're choosing who you are while you spend yourself, and that part's free." He glanced up. "Also, you're not one voice. You haven't been since the stream ran backward."

Tamsin was silent. Then, so quietly he almost missed it: "When I was eight, I wanted a wooden flute. A peddler sold them in the yard. I saved bread for a month — traded my portions, hid the crusts. Aldous found the flute and burned it. Said a taster's hands shouldn't learn pretty things. Said wanting made me predictable." She fed a stick to the fire. "So I stopped. For twenty years I stopped. And now you're all standing there acting like it's normal to want things, and I don't—"

Her voice failed. Not the trained failure of a singer losing a note — the human kind.

Merrin didn't touch her. He'd learned that too; she tolerated touch like medicine, in measured doses, from few hands. Instead he picked up the melody, the four notes, the mortal line, and sang it soft and imperfect into the firelight, and after a moment Sef's thread rose to answer it, and after another moment — stiff, rough, wanting — Tamsin's voice joined them both.

Three voices in a hollow under the war. Somewhere north, drums. Somewhere south, a burning city's future. Here, slate and cold water and a hedge-witch humming along with her eyes closed, keeping time with one finger.

When the phrase ended, Tamsin wiped her face briskly, businesslike, the way she did everything.

"Again," she said. "From the seventh phrase. I nearly had it."

* * *

They worked until the light went blue and then grey, and Wren called a halt only when Tamsin's voice began to fray — "Rest it or lose it, girl, even swords get sheathed" — and then the company made supper of barley and dried plums and a trout Brannoc had bullied out of the stream, and for one hour the hollow was almost a home.

Merrin sat apart afterward, tuning nothing, thinking.

He had lost his voice once — not literally, but the use of it, the meaning of it, the night he refused Corvane's ascension ceremony and was escorted from the conservatory with his music burned in the yard. He had thought that was the worst thing that could happen to a singer. He had been wrong. The worst thing was what Corvane was doing right now, somewhere in the southern camps: building a counter-choir out of captured throats, stolen fae voices bent to a corrupted song. Merrin had heard rumors through Brannoc's road-network, and each rumor was worse than the last — cages, coercion, instruments of iron designed to shatter what they could not compel.

They were racing an army to a wellspring, armed with a taster, a child, and a dying witch.

And yet.

He looked across the fire at Tamsin, asleep sitting up as soldiers sleep, her head tipped against the stone, and even in sleep her lips moved faintly, running phrases. Sef was curled against her side like a cat, and she had not moved away. Brannoc was mending a strap. Wren was — genuinely asleep this time, breath shallow but steady, and Merrin counted her breaths the way he'd once counted bars of rest, praying for one more, and one more.

*Tenderness amid the march of war,* he thought. *Someone should write a ballad.* He almost laughed. Someone would, someday. That was the thing about songs — they outlived the terror that interrupted them.

He was still awake when Sef stirred, sat up, and went rigid, head cocked toward the deep rock of the mountain the way a dog points.

"Merrin," the child whispered. "Merrin, wake up. It's singing."

"Sleeping, probably. Echoes—"

"No." Sef's eyes were wide and gold-rimmed and terrified and lit with joy all at once. "It's singing *back*. From underneath. It's learning our song." A pause, and then, smaller: "And there's something else. Underneath the singing. Footsteps. A lot of footsteps. Coming up the pass."

Merrin was on his feet with his hand on Tamsin's shoulder before the words finished, shaking her awake, the tenderness folding itself away like sheet music into a pocket — because the war, as it always did, had found them again.
