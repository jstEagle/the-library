## 22. Marrowgate Burns

Brannoc Ironhand had carried a lot of things down the smuggler's roads in his long and mostly dishonest life — kegs of Thornfell brandy, bolts of Corvane silk that never saw a customs house, a live snow leopard once, which he did not care to discuss — but he had never carried news like this.

It came up the mountain on the feet of a girl.

She was maybe fourteen, barefoot, her soles cut to ribbons from the shale, and she walked into the winter camp at dusk with the particular straight-backed walk of someone who has decided that if she stops moving she will fall apart. Behind her came others. A dozen at first. Then more. By full dark there were forty-odd souls huddled at the mouth of the cave where the company had made its camp, and more coming up the switchbacks behind them, torchlight strung along the mountain path like a rosary of embers.

Brannoc met the girl at the fire line because somebody had to, and because he was the biggest thing between her and the dark.

"Name," he said, gentle as he knew how, which was not very.

"Petra." Her voice was ash and gravel. "Marrowgate. The Salt Quarter."

He crouched to her level, which for a dwarf meant barely crouching at all. "Marrowgate what, lass? What happened in Marrowgate?"

She looked at him as if the question were obscene. As if he'd asked her to describe the color of the sky.

"It burned," she said. "They burned it. All of it. They said—" and here her voice cracked clean through, and she pressed on anyway, the way you do when stopping would kill you, "—they said it was punishment. For harboring traitors. They nailed the proclamation to the harbor chains. *The city that shelters the taster burns with her name upon it.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Brannoc, Sef went very still, the way the child always went still when the world said their name without saying it.

Brannoc stood up slowly, his knees announcing their opinions as they always did these days, and looked out past the firelight toward the south, where the mountains stepped down in long grey shoulders toward the coast, toward the free cities, toward Marrowgate, where he'd drunk more bad wine and sung more bawdy songs than any city had a right to host. Where Old Marta kept the ferry house and watered nothing but her flowers. Where the fishmongers' guild hall had a cellar Brannoc himself had dug, thirty years back, wide enough to hide a wagon team.

Ash, now. Probably all of it.

"Get them food," he said over his shoulder, to nobody in particular, meaning everybody. "Blankets. Wren's willowbark tea if she's got it, those feet need cleaning before they fester." He put a hand on Petra's shoulder, careful, the way you touch something already broken. "You're safe here, lass. Whatever else is true tonight, that much is true."

She nodded. She didn't believe him. He could see she didn't believe him, and he couldn't blame her, because he wasn't sure he believed it either anymore.

* * *

Tamsin found him an hour later at the camp's edge, standing watch over the switchback road out of habit rather than necessity, because his hands needed something to hold besides a mug.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard."

She came and stood beside him. In the starlight she looked thinner than she had a month ago, cheekbones sharpening, eyes too big for her face, but steady. She was always steady. It was one of the things about her that frightened people who didn't know her, and reassured the ones who did.

"How many?" she asked.

"Forty-one in camp. More coming. Petra says the inquisitors gave the city three days' warning to surrender 'the taster and her company.' Nobody surrendered them, because nobody knew where you were, and so—" He made a gesture, short and brutal, that finished the sentence better than words.

"They burned a city," Tamsin said quietly, "to send me a message."

"Aye. And here's the part that'll keep you up nights: it worked. Message received." He turned to face her fully. "I've been doing sums since sundown, lass, and I don't like the total. Every soul on this mountain came up here because of us. Because of the map, the song, the whole mad covenant business. Forty-one refugees and counting, and every one of them is a debt with our names on it."

"That's not how debts work, Brannoc."

"Isn't it?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I've spent fifty years keeping accounts. Smuggler's arithmetic. You take a cargo, you owe a delivery. You take a favor, you owe a favor. You take a man's trust—" he tapped his own chest, twice, hard, "—you owe him back his life if it comes to that. That's the whole trade. That's the only law I ever kept. And by that law we are ruined, Tamsin Thackeray. Ruined past any recovering."

She was quiet a moment. Below them, the refugee fires dotted the mountainside like fallen stars.

"And what does your arithmetic say we should do?" she asked. "Walk down there and give ourselves up? Would that unburn Marrowgate?"

"No." He said it flatly, because it was true and truth deserved flatness. "Nothing unburns Marrowgate. But I'll tell you what my arithmetic does say. It says Corvane thinks you're worth a city. Which means either he's a fool who burns cities for nothing—" Brannoc spat, "—which no one who's met him believes, or he knows exactly what you are and what you can do, and he is terrified. Terrified men make mistakes, lass. Terrified men burn things they should have left standing, and spend soldiers they'll wish they'd kept, and show their whole hand to anyone patient enough to watch."

"So we watch."

"We watch, we count, and we make every life on this mountain worth what it cost." He looked at her. "That's not despair talking. That's the ledger."

Tamsin held his gaze, and then she did something she almost never did: she reached out and gripped his forearm, dwarf-fashion, warrior-fashion, the grip of equals.

"Then teach me the ledger," she said. "Because I've been keeping accounts my whole life too, Brannoc. Poison accounts. What I could swallow, what I couldn't, what it cost me to survive another meal. And I am done surviving. I want to *owe* something. Properly. To people who are owed."

He grunted, which from a dwarf of the Ironhand line was roughly equivalent to weeping openly.

"Come inside first," he said. "There's a woman down there asking for you by name. Says she has kin among the dead. Says she walked two days uphill to find you specifically." His jaw worked. "Lass, you'd best hear what she has to say. But I'm warning you now — it's not kind."

* * *

The woman's name was Odile Ferran, and she had been the harbormistress of Marrowgate for eleven years, and she delivered her report sitting upright on a crate by the fire with a bandaged arm and the bearing of a general accepting terms, because some people cannot be broken, only relocated.

"The fleet came at dawn on the third day," she said, when Tamsin sat across from her. "Nine ships flying Corvane's black gull. They didn't even bother with the harbor chain — they ran a fireship into the granary wharf and let the wind do the work. By noon the Salt Quarter was gone. By evening the temple district. The inquisitors went street to street with their instruments, those horrible tuning-forks of theirs, shattering anything that sang. There was a choir school on Lantern Row." Her voice did not waver, which somehow made it worse. "Sixty children learning winter carols. The inquisitors called it a nest of covenant-sympathizers."

Nobody spoke. Sef had both hands pressed over their ears, though there was nothing left to shut out; Merrin knelt beside the child with one hand hovering, not quite touching, the way you hover over a wound you're afraid to probe.

"Merrin Vale was named in the proclamation too," Odile went on. "And the dwarf. And the witch. And the child." Her eyes found Sef, and softened by perhaps a hair's breadth. "Corvane wants all of you. But most of all he wants the taster, dead or alive, alive preferred. The rest of us were just the envelope the message came in."

"Why tell me this?" Tamsin asked. Her voice was level. Brannoc, watching, saw the effort it cost — saw the way her hands stayed flat and still on her knees, deliberately, the discipline of a woman who had learned young that trembling hands got you killed at table. "Why climb two days through winter passes to say it to my face?"

"Because the council of the free cities meets at Highmoor in nine days," Odile said, "and half of them want to surrender you to Corvane and sue for peace, and the other half want to fight and die stupidly, and neither half knows what actually happened at the Deepwell or what the covenant means or why the Ash Queen's scouts have been seen on the southern slopes for the first time in a generation." She leaned forward. "You're going to that council, taster. And when you go, you're going to speak for Marrowgate. For the sixty children on Lantern Row. For my ferry house and my fishermen and old Marta who never hurt a soul. You're going to stand up in front of every general and merchant-prince too cowardly to act, and you're going to tell them what a city looks like when it burns for sheltering hope. Because I carried the message up the mountain, and by every god old and new, you will carry it the rest of the way."

The fire cracked. Snow ticked softly against the canvas overhead.

"All right," Tamsin said.

Just that. Two syllables, quiet as a knife going back in its sheath. But Brannoc had watched this woman taste death forty times and swallow it without blinking, and he knew her silences, and this one had iron in it.

Odile studied her a long moment, then nodded once, the nod of one professional acknowledging another, and lay back against her pack, and within minutes was asleep — the sleep of the truly spent, deep and graceless and complete.

* * *

Later, when the camp had gone quiet, Brannoc sat by the low fire with Merrin and Wren and did what dwarves do instead of grieving, which is plan.

"Roads first," he said, scratching lines in the ash with a stick. "We've got forty-one mouths and more coming, and this cave holds maybe sixty before it turns into a tomb. There's the Hollowfork galleries two days north — abandoned copper workings, dry, defensible, room for three hundred if we cram. I can have the first party moving at first light."

"Corvane's patrols?" Merrin asked. The singer's voice was hoarse; he hadn't sung since the news came, and everyone had noticed, and nobody had said anything, because some silences are load-bearing.

"Two routes avoid them entirely. One goes through the Underfell, which is ugly but empty. The other—" Brannoc hesitated. "The other runs close along the verge. Fae borderland. Wren's wards could manage it, maybe, but after last time—"

"After last time the land itself recoiled from Tamsin," Wren finished. The old witch sat wrapped in three blankets, and even by firelight Brannoc could see how much greyer she'd gone since the tollhouse, since whatever it was she'd spent of herself that couldn't be gotten back. "It can be done. It will cost. Everything costs; that's the first law older than song." She poked the fire. "But hear me, all of you. The burning of Marrowgate isn't just cruelty. It's strategy. Corvane knows the covenant needs voices, and voices need people, and people need somewhere to stand. He is burning the ground out from under the song. Every free city that falls is a verse that can never be sung again."

"Then we get faster," Brannoc said.

"You can't outrun an army, dwarf."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "But I've spent fifty years learning every crack and cranny of these mountains, and I'll tell you what armies can't do. They can't hold a pass they can't find. They can't blockade a road that moves. And they can't burn a city that's already emptied itself into the stone." He tapped the ash-map, and the lines under his stick multiplied — galleries, tunnels, old smugglers' caches, the secret circulatory system of Thornfell that his father had taught him and his father's father before that. "Let Corvane burn what's standing. We'll be underneath it. All of us. And when the moon turns and it's time to sing at the Deepwell, we won't come crawling out of some cave begging his mercy. We'll come up out of the ground itself, everywhere at once, like water finding every crack in a dam."

Merrin looked at the ash-lines a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had something new in it — not hope exactly, but the raw material hope gets smelted from.

"You sound like you've fought a war before, Brannoc."

"I've smuggled through three," Brannoc said. "Same skill set. You just swap the cargo." He looked into the fire, and let himself say the thing he'd been carrying since sundown, heavy as any keg. "Old Marta kept the ferry house. Did I ever tell you? Watered nothing but flowers, fed every stray cat in the Salt Quarter, and once hid me and eleven kegs of untaxed brandy from a customs raid by stuffing us all in her flower cellar and telling the inspectors the smell was compost. She'd be ninety-one this spring." His jaw tightened. "If she didn't get out, then this stopped being a war about maps and songs and thrones somewhere around suppertime today. Now it's a war about Marta. About every Marta. And I intend to win it, gentlemen, lady, and I intend to win it *thoroughly*."

Wren reached over and laid her spotted old hand on his wrist.

"Then we'd best sleep," she said, "because thorough takes time."

* * *

Brannoc took the middle watch, as he always did, because he slept badly and saw no reason to inflict it on others. He stood at the cave mouth with his axe across his knees and watched the mountain breathe.

Snow fell fine and steady, filling tracks, erasing the day. Down the slope the refugee fires had banked to coals. Somewhere among them a baby cried, briefly, and was hushed. Above, the peaks of Thornfell stood black against the stars, and far off — so far he might have imagined it, except that Sef had heard it too, days ago, and gone pale as milk — the mountain hummed. Low. Patient. A note held under the world like a finger under a bird's wing.

The Choir. Half-alive, Wren said. Waiting.

Brannoc was no singer. His voice was a gravel-slide and his ear was tuned to false bottoms and breaking seals. But he'd grown up in these mountains, and he knew — the way smugglers know things, in the bones, below argument — that the stone remembered. That everything under Thornfell remembered. And he thought: let Corvane burn every city on the coast. Let him burn the ports and the temples and the granaries and the pretty palaces. Stone doesn't burn. Song doesn't burn. And a debt—

A debt always comes due.

Footsteps behind him. Small ones. He didn't turn; he'd know that tread anywhere now, light and quick and trying too hard to be quiet.

"Can't sleep either, sprout?"

Sef climbed onto the rock beside him and pulled their knees up, fae-bright eyes reflecting the coals. For a while the child said nothing. Then:

"She was real. The ferry lady. Marta."

"Aye. Real as rain."

"And the children on Lantern Row."

"Aye."

Sef was quiet again. Then, in a small fierce voice that made Brannoc's chest ache: "When we sing at the Deepwell — when the Choir comes back — will it bring them back?"

And there it was. The question under all the questions, the one Brannoc had been dodging since sundown, and he found he could not dodge it with a child, not this one, not honestly.

"No, sprout," he said gently. "Dead's dead. Even the fae don't unpick that knot."

Sef's chin trembled, and Brannoc put his big scarred hand carefully on the child's shoulder.

"But here's what it *will* do," he said. "It'll mean they didn't die for nothing. It'll mean the next Marta keeps her ferry house, and the next choir school on the next Lantern Row sings loud enough to shake the rafters, and no black-gull fleet ever comes up that harbor again. That's not bringing them back. But it's the only thing we've got that's anywhere near as good." He squeezed. "Revenge is a cold supper, sprout. Remembrance is a feast. We're cooking the second one."

Sef wiped their nose on their sleeve, nodded, and leaned against his side, and within a few breaths was asleep, trusting as a stone settling into earth.

Brannoc sat a long while longer, holding the warmth of the child and the weight of the axe and the ledger of the dead, watching the snow fill the road behind them.

Which was why he was awake, and the only one awake, when the runner came pounding up the switchbacks an hour before dawn — a lean, frost-bearded man with a smuggler's badge knotted round his wrist, one of Brannoc's own network, half-dead on his feet and shouting before he was even in earshot.

"Brannoc! Brannoc Ironhand!" The man skidded into the firelight, lungs heaving. "Word from Highmoor — the council — they've sent riders, all directions—" He caught his breath, and what he said next dropped through Brannoc like a stone through deep water.

"Corvane's army is marching on the passes. Twenty thousand, maybe more. And the Ash Queen's host has come down off the high snows to meet it." He swallowed. "Both armies, Brannoc. Both of them, converging on Thornfell. And the council sends word: whatever the taster means to do at the Deepwell — she'd best do it fast. Because in six days, there won't be a mountain left to sing in."
