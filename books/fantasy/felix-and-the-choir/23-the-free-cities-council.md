## 23. The Free Cities' Council

The council chamber of Vellamere had been a granary once, before the free cities needed somewhere to argue in. You could still smell the grain in the walls — dry and sweet and old — and I found that comforting in a way I couldn't explain. Maybe because everything else in the room smelled like fear.

Fear has a scent. I'd learned that young, standing at the king's elbow with a silver spoon while courtiers watched to see if I'd fall over. Fear smells like vinegar and cold sweat and, underneath it, something faintly metallic, like a blade kept too long in its sheath. The chamber was full of it. Forty-odd delegates from nine cities — what was left of nine cities, now that Marrowgate was ash — packed onto benches built for grain sacks and patience.

Brannoc stood at my shoulder, arms crossed, doing his best impression of a door that had opinions. Sef sat behind me on the floor, cross-legged, humming so quietly only I could hear it. Wren's absence was a shape in the room. She was back at the camp above Thornfell, spending herself drop by drop on the rite that would hold the Deepwell open, and every hour we spent here was an hour she didn't have.

So when the marshal from Kettering stood up and said, "We should discuss terms of surrender," I did not have time for courtesy.

I stood up first.

* * *

I want to be honest about how that moment felt, because the ballads get it wrong. They say the taster rose like a flame and the room went silent at her courage. What actually happened was this: my knees were shaking, my mouth tasted like the road dust of three days' hard riding, and the silence that fell wasn't awe. It was the silence of forty people looking at a woman they'd heard of in dispatches and deciding whether she was worth listening to or throwing out.

Marshal Dree Halloway of Kettering was a broad woman gone grey at the temples, with a voice like a landslide. She'd been arguing surrender since before I arrived, and she had arithmetic on her side. "Corvane has thirty thousand under arms," she said, tapping a map weighted with candlesticks. "He has siege trains. He has those instruments — the shatter-horns, whatever they are — and he has demonstrated he will use them. Marrowgate held for six days. Six. We cannot hold one moon against that. We can barely hold one week."

"And if you surrender?" I said.

Halloway looked at me the way generals look at people who ask inconvenient questions. "Then some of us live."

"Then tell me what lives look like." I walked to the map. My legs steadied as I moved; they always did. A kitchen is a dangerous place, but it is never still, and I had learned long ago that motion was safer than standing. "Show me Corvane's terms. Because I've read them — Brannoc's couriers pulled copies off a dead quartermaster at the tollhouse. There are no terms. There is a list." I put my finger on the parchment someone had nailed beside the map. "Every singer. Every hedge-witch. Every child born with fae blood, and every child who might carry it — which means any child whose grandmother ever saw a light in the woods. Every smuggler who ever ran a pass. That last category includes most of the men in this room's payrolls, Marshal, including yours."

Murmuring. Halloway's jaw set. "Exaggeration."

"It's a transcription. Count the line items yourself." I let them count. Some did. The murmuring changed pitch.

A delegate from Saltmere — fat rings, merchant hands — said, "Even granting your reading, madam taster, what do you propose? Faith? You're asking us to believe in a covenant older than anyone living, broken a generation ago, restored by—" he gestured at me, at my travel-stained coat, at everything I was not, "—a servant girl and a tavern singer."

"A servant girl," I agreed. "And a tavern singer. And a dwarf who knows every smuggler's road in Thornfell better than Corvane's cartographers know their own. And a hedge-witch who is currently dying to keep the Deepwell open for you. And a fae-blooded child." I turned to face the benches. "You keep saying faith like it's a coin I'm asking you to spend blind. I'm not. I'm telling you what I've seen with my own eyes, and I'll tell you now, and then you can decide."

* * *

I told them about the harmonies under the mountain — how the Choir was half-alive, how the stone itself remembered. I told them about the Ash Queen's court, and the bargain struck: reforge the covenant at the Deepwell before the moon turned, and the fae hosts would stand with the free cities; fail, and both armies would hunt us all. I told them about Marrowgate, though I didn't need to — half of them had seen the smoke from their own walls.

And I told them the thing I'd been carrying since Wren first explained it, the thing that made my voice go quiet without my meaning it to.

"The covenant isn't a treaty. Treaties are paper. Paper burns — Corvane proved that. The covenant is a song, sung by three voices together: mortal, fae, and the bridge between. While it holds, the mountain itself protects both realms. Corvane's whole strategy — the shatter-horns, the stolen singers, the caged children — it's all aimed at singing a counterfeit version, a corrupted choir with stolen voices, so that he holds the binding instead of the realms sharing it. If he succeeds, there will be no more free cities. There will be no more anything except what he permits." I paused. "If we succeed, his army breaks. Not loses — breaks. The mountain turns hostile to him. His stolen voices walk away from him mid-note. I've seen what happens to men whose instruments abandon them."

"You claim to know a great deal," Halloway said, "for someone who fled the capital three weeks ago."

"I claim to know poison," I said. "Twenty years of it. And I'm telling you, Marshal, that Corvane's plan is poison — slow, sweetened, meant to be swallowed whole. I know what poisoned things look like from the inside. This is one."

Silence again. But it was a different silence. Fear smells one way; doubt smells another, sharper, like a door starting to open.

Then Lord Cassel of Dunmark stood, and I learned what despair looks like when it puts on armor. He was old, upright, decorated with the honest scars of the border wars. "I don't dispute the lady's conviction," he said. "I dispute her odds. Say the covenant is real. Say the Deepwell opens. We must still hold the passes for however long this takes — against thirty thousand men — with what? Militia? Smugglers?" His mouth twisted around the word. "Deserters?"

"Yes," I said.

That got a laugh — one bitter bark from the back of the room. I let it land.

"Lord Cassel, I grew up in the palace kitchens, and the first thing they taught me was this: the strongest lock means nothing if the cook leaves the postern open, and the weakest wall holds fine if nobody finds the door. Corvane doesn't know the passes. His maps show roads that end where smuggler roads begin. For three weeks, his columns have been bleeding men into ravines and circling back the way they came, because the people who know Thornfell are on our side. Not because they love me. Because Corvane burned Marrowgate, and Marrowgate fed half of them." I tapped the map, on the passes, one by one. Grey Gap. The Kettle Stair. Widow's Chimney. Names like old friends. "Hold these for one moon's turn. Don't win. Just don't lose. Make every mile cost him a day. That's all I'm asking. One moon."

"One moon," Cassel repeated. "And if your song fails?"

"Then I'll be dead at the Deepwell, and you can surrender to whoever's left."

Nobody laughed at that.

* * *

They argued for four hours. I want to record that honestly too, because the ballad compresses it into a single dramatic beat, and the truth was four hours of heat and fear and arithmetic, and me standing at that map like a pin holding a garment together, answering every objection I could and enduring the ones I couldn't.

Halloway wanted numbers. I gave her what Brannoc's couriers had gathered: Corvane's supply lines stretched thin, his forage failing in the high country, desertion climbing — the tollhouse ambush hadn't just been an ambush, it had been evidence that his own soldiers were tired of burning towns. She grunted at that but didn't concede it.

Cassel wanted guarantees. I told him the truth: there weren't any. "I can't guarantee the song works. Wren believes it does. Merrin Vale — the finest voice the court ever trained, the man who refused to sing at Corvane's ascension and paid for it — believes it does. Sef hears the Choir calling from under the mountain, clear as a bell. Three independent witnesses, Lord Cassel, and none of them stand to gain anything by lying to you. In my old trade, that's called corroboration, and it's more than most poisons ever get."

The merchant from Saltmere wanted to know what it would cost the cities in coin. I nearly laughed. "Everything, if you lose. Nothing you wouldn't spend on a lost war, if you win."

A young delegate from the river towns — I never learned her name — asked the question that actually mattered, the one underneath all the others: "Why you? Why should we stake everything on you?"

And I didn't have a good answer, so I gave her a true one.

"Because I spent twenty years being used by powerful people and calling it loyalty. Because my immunity was brewed into me before I could walk, because I was made into a weapon against the fae without my knowledge or consent, and because when I found that out, I chose — not obedience, not survival, but choice — to finish a dying stranger's song. I'm not asking you to trust me because I'm special. I'm asking you to trust me because I'm exactly as ordinary as your militia, and I'm still going to stand at that well and sing. If I can do it, so can the people holding your passes."

Sef's humming behind me had shifted key at some point. I felt it in my spine before I understood it in my ears — a low thread of harmony winding through the chamber, unnoticed by everyone except, perhaps, the fae-blooded child making it. Later Brannoc told me half the delegates had gooseflesh and blamed the draft. Good. Let them blame the draft. The draft was on our side.

* * *

It came down, in the end, to Halloway.

She dismissed the council for a recess — "so we can shout at each other privately," as one delegate muttered — and instead of leaving, she walked over to me at the map. Up close she smelled of horse and iron and, faintly, of the vinegar of controlled fear. A commander's smell. I knew it from the palace guards.

"Dree Halloway," she said, offering no hand. "Kettering garrison, twenty-two years. I held the ford at Anselm's Crossing with four hundred men against two thousand, and I held it because I believed the reinforcements were coming. They weren't. I lost three hundred and eleven of them learning that belief is not a fortification." Her eyes were pale and level. "Convince me you're not asking me to learn that lesson twice."

"I can't convince you," I said. "Anyone who convinces you with words alone is selling something. Ask your scouts. Ask them whether Corvane's columns move like men who know the ground or men who fear it. Ask the refugees streaming out of Marrowgate whether the inquisitors' horns sound like music or like something breaking. Ask them what the smoke smelled like." I held her gaze. "You're a soldier, so you'll hate this answer, but the intelligence is the argument. I'm not asking for faith. I'm asking for one moon of holding actions based on the best available information — and the best available information says Corvane can be broken at the Deepwell, and cannot be survived anywhere else."

She studied me for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, almost reluctantly: "My daughter was in Marrowgate. Guild quarter. We haven't found her name on any list, dead or alive."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and knew how small it sounded.

"So am I." She turned back toward the benches. "One moon," she said loudly, to the room. "Kettering holds Grey Gap and the Kettle Stair. Not a day longer, taster. If your song fails, I will personally come down that mountain and surrender what's left of us, and I will not apologize for it."

"One moon," I said. "That's all the song needs."

Dunmark followed — Cassel gave me a nod that contained, somewhere deep beneath the armor, a flicker of hope he clearly resented. Saltmere held out for another hour of haggling over grain levies and then folded when Brannoc, who had been silent all day, finally spoke exactly nine words: "My roads feed your cities. Choose wisely." The rest came in ones and twos after that, the way ice goes off a pond — slowly, then all at once.

By dusk we had it: nine cities, or the shards of nine, pledged to hold the passes of Thornfell for one turn of the moon. Fragile as spun sugar. Real as bread.

* * *

Afterward, in the granary's side room, with Sef asleep against Brannoc's shoulder and the delegates' voices fading down the corridor, I let myself lean against the wall and shake. Just for a minute. Nobody saw. Or rather, somebody saw, and pretended not to, which is its own kind of family.

"You did well," Brannoc said quietly.

"I lied to them."

"You didn't."

"I told them one moon was all the song needs. I don't know that. Wren doesn't know that. Nobody knows that."

Brannoc considered this, rocking slightly, Sef's weight warm against him. "Your da told me once — the year he pulled me out of a Kettering debt-cell — that a leader's job isn't to know. It's to decide. Knowing's for afterward." He shrugged. "Seems a hard trade. Seems the only one on offer."

Outside, the wind was rising off the mountains, and somewhere in it — very faint, very far, threading through the wind like a wire through wool — I thought I heard singing. Not Sef's humming. Something older. Something patient.

One moon. Twenty-nine days. Wren was spending hers drop by drop, and Corvane's columns were already turning north.

I pushed off the wall and went to wake the others. We rode for the Deepwell before dawn.
