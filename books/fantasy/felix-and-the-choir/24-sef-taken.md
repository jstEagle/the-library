## 24. Sef Taken

Sef woke before dawn because the mountain told them to.

That was the thing nobody else understood — not Tamsin with her poisoned tongue, not Merrin with his ruined voice, not even Old Wren, who knew more about the old rites than anyone left living. The mountain spoke all the time. It hummed under the snow, it murmured in the roots of the pines, and lately, since they'd come down from the verge with Wren bleeding and the Ash Queen's bargain hanging over them like a blade on a thread, it had started saying one word over and over:

*Soon.*

Sef lay in their bedroll in the smuggler's cave and listened to it, the way they'd listened their whole life without knowing what they were listening *to*. The word came up through the stone floor like warmth from a hearth. It wasn't a warning exactly. It was more like a hand reaching down through the dark, asking.

*I'm coming,* Sef thought back at it, the way they always did. *I'm not done here yet.*

Because that was the truth of it, the truth they hadn't said out loud, not even to Tamsin, especially not to Tamsin: the Choir needed three voices, and Sef was going to be one of them, and being one of them might mean standing at the Deepwell when Corvane's counter-song came down like an avalanche. Wren had explained it in her cracked, patient way — how a bridge holds two shores together, and how bridges are the first things armies burn.

Sef was eleven years old and had been running since they could walk, and for the first time in their life they had people who'd chosen them, and the mountain wanted them soon, and all of those facts sat together in Sef's chest like stones in a sling.

They got up quietly. Brannoc snored by the fire pit, his beard full of ash, his axe within reach of his hand even asleep — Sef had watched him sleep enough times to know he never really slept, not all the way. Tamsin was on watch at the cave mouth, a small dark shape against the grey light, still as a heron. Sef padded over and sat down next to her because sitting next to Tamsin was the closest thing to safe Sef had ever known.

"You should be sleeping," Tamsin said, without turning.

"The mountain woke me."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. She did that every time Sef mentioned the mountain's voice — a little flinch, like the words cost her something. Sef had figured out why, or most of why: Tamsin's blood made the fae land recoil, and now she was supposed to be the bridge, and bridges were supposed to reach both shores, and hers only reached one. Sef could hear the second voice beneath everything. Tamsin couldn't hear any of it. That had to feel like being handed a song written in a language you'd been taught to hate.

"What's it saying?" Tamsin asked.

"Same thing. *Soon.*" Sef hugged their knees. "It's not scary, though. It sounds like—" They searched for it. "Like my mother, maybe. If I'd had one. Like someone who's been waiting a long time and isn't angry, just tired."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. "When this is over," she said, "you'll go through. To the other side."

It wasn't a question. Sef looked at her profile, sharp against the dawn, and felt the first cold drop of something they'd been refusing to feel for weeks.

"I don't know," Sef said. "Maybe. Maybe both. Wren says the covenant means there *is* a both. Doors instead of walls."

"Doors," Tamsin repeated softly, like she was tasting the word. Testing it for poison.

That was when the dogs started.

Not real dogs. Sef heard them a heartbeat before anyone else — heard the wrongness of them, the hollow ring under the baying, like bells struck inside empty barrels. Inquisitor-hounds. Wren had described them once: beasts bred in Corvane's kennels with a drop of something cold in their blood, trained to scent fae-touched flesh across miles of stone and snow.

And Sef was the most fae-touched thing in these mountains.

"*Tamsin.*" Sef's voice came out very small. "*Hounds.*"

Tamsin moved so fast she was already up before the sound reached the cave behind them. "Brannoc! Wake up! Riders on the switchback road — Sef, how many?"

Sef closed their eyes and let the mountain tell them, because the mountain felt everything that moved across its skin. "Six hounds. Nine horses. And—" Their stomach turned over. "Something else. Something big and quiet, walking behind. Iron all around it."

"A censer-bearer," Brannoc rasped, already rolling to his feet, axe in fist. "They brought a censer-bearer for a child." He spat into the fire. "Corvane wants you bad, little one."

Behind them, Wren stirred on her pallet, and the smell of her wound — honey and rot and burnt herbs — filled the cave. She'd been dying slowly for days, spending herself teaching Tamsin the covenant's bones, and she dragged her eyes open now with the effort of a woman hauling a door that weighed more than she did.

"How far?" she asked.

"Half a league," Sef said. "The hounds are fast."

* * *

There wasn't time for a plan, so there was a fight instead, and fights were the one kind of plan Sef understood completely, because they'd been in them since before they could count.

Brannoc wanted to run — down the back scree, into the deep passes where six horses couldn't follow. But Wren couldn't run. Wren could barely sit up. And Tamsin stood at the cave mouth with her short blades out and said, in the flat voice Sef had learned meant she'd already decided, "We don't leave her. We hold the narrows and make them pay for every step."

So they held the narrows.

The cave opened onto a shelf of rock above a ravine, with a neck of stone barely wide enough for two men abreast, and Brannoc filled that neck like a wall fills a gate. Sef crouched behind him with a sling they'd made from a bootlace and a river stone, because Brannoc had taught them that even a child can crack a skull if the child is patient and the skull is stupid enough to come close.

The hounds came first, streaming up the switchbacks with their bell-voices ringing, and Sef saw them properly for the first time: grey, gaunt, too many joints, eyes like chips of winter sky. When the lead hound caught Sef's scent it stopped dead and *shook*, all over, like a wet dog — fear, Sef realized. It was afraid of them. Good. Let it be afraid.

Sef put a stone through its eye at forty paces. Brannoc had said patience; Sef preferred accuracy.

Then the riders came, black-and-silver, Corvane's sunburst stamped on their breasts, and the narrows became a butcher's corridor. Brannoc's axe rose and fell and rose. Tamsin fought low and mean, the way she did everything, slipping inside guards like she was slipping poison into wine — quick, precise, deniable until it was too late. An inquisitor screamed. Another. Sef slung stones until their arm burned.

For one bright, stupid moment, Sef thought they were going to win.

Then the big quiet thing came around the bend, and Sef understood what iron-all-around meant.

It was a man, or had been. Now he was a walking cage — bands of black iron riveted from wrist to shoulder, throat to hip, and between the bands, his skin showed grey as ash. A censer-bearer. Wren had named them once in a voice gone thin: men who'd burned out everything human in themselves except obedience, whose touch unraveled wards and whose presence smothered song. He didn't draw a weapon. He just walked forward into the narrows, and where he walked, the morning went dull, and the mountain's voice — the warm *soon* that had lived under Sef's ribs since waking — went silent.

The silence hit Sef like a slap. They'd never been alone in their own head before. They hadn't known silence could be this loud.

"No," Sef whispered. "No, no—"

The censer-bearer's head turned. Under the iron brow-band, his eyes found Sef across the melee with terrible calm, and he raised one banded hand, and the world tilted.

Later, Sef would try to remember what happened next and fail, because it happened too fast and too slow at once. Brannoc roaring. Tamsin screaming their name — actually screaming, Tamsin who never wasted breath. Wren dragging herself upright at the cave mouth with her hands raised and blood seeping fresh through her bandages, pouring the last of herself into a warding that bloomed gold for one heartbeat and then guttered like a candle in wind.

And the censer-bearer walking through all of it untouched, because you can't cut a man who has nothing left to lose, and Sef's sling-stone bouncing off iron with a sound like a struck coffin.

His hand closed on Sef's collar.

The iron was cold. Not winter-cold. *Empty*-cold, the cold of a place where nothing had ever grown. Sef felt the mountain's voice vanish entirely, felt their own strange hearing fold up like wings, and for the first time since they'd run away from the village that had called them changeling and devil, Sef was just a small, ordinary, terrified child in a strong man's grip.

"It's all right," the censer-bearer said, and his voice was the worst thing yet, because it was gentle. "You're wanted. You've always been wanted. Someone finally knows what you're for."

Sef bit him. Kicked him. Screamed for Tamsin, for Brannoc, for anyone, and heard Tamsin answer — heard her fighting toward them through three men with no care at all for what steel found her, heard Brannoc bellowing, heard Wren's ward flare one last desperate time —

And then a hood came down over Sef's head, and the world became wool and rope-smell and the ringing of hound-bells, and the mountain was gone, and everyone they loved was gone, and Sef was carried down the switchbacks like a sack of grain while the company's voices tore themselves ragged behind them.

*Someone finally knows what you're for.*

Sef cried into the hood, silently, furiously, the way you cry when you learned young that crying loudly gets you abandoned faster.

Then they stopped crying, because Sef had survived six years on roads and rooftops by learning one lesson better than any other: nobody is coming to save you, so you save yourself, and you start by paying attention.

Under the hood, Sef listened. Counted hoofbeats. Felt the road turn — south, then east, toward the army camps in the valley. Memorized the rhythm of the march, the jingle of the harness, the cough of the man riding beside their captor. Filed it all away in the place where they kept everything, the hoard-place, the survival-place.

And deep under the hood, deeper than the iron man's silence could reach, Sef found one small thread of the mountain's song still humming — faint as a hairline crack of light under a door.

The censer-bearer could smother the song outside himself. But Sef's blood was the song. It had always been the song. He could carry the silence with him all he liked.

He was carrying a choir into Corvane's camp, and it was listening to everything.

* * *

They kept Sef in a wagon cage, which was almost funny, because the bars were iron and iron did nothing to Sef — it was the censer-bearer sitting outside the cage day and night, breathing his dead-air silence, that kept Sef's gift folded shut. Sef ate what they gave them, drank what they gave them, answered questions with the flat stare of a child who has been called a devil by adults before and knows exactly how much adults hate being stared at.

From the soldiers' talk, careless around a caged child the way soldiers are careless around anything that isn't armed, Sef built the picture piece by piece.

The Duke was coming north. Not marching — *coming*, the way weather comes. Ten thousand men, supply trains stretching back to Marrowgate's smoking ruins, and with them wagons of instruments Sef heard described in whispers: long horns of black iron, drums headed in something that wasn't skin, a great frame harp strung with wire that made the soldiers' teeth ache when it was unloaded. A counter-choir. Captured singers. Sef heard a guard mention a tenor — a court-trained tenor, taken in a raid, who sang when they made him and went silent as a stone when they didn't, and who had tried to hang himself with his own bootlaces and been prevented, because his voice was worth more than his life.

Merrin. Sef's chest clenched. *Merrin.*

And the purpose of it all, spoken by a lieutenant to a captain within earshot of the cage, in the bored tone of men discussing grain counts: the Deepwell. Corvane meant to sing his corrupted covenant at the Deepwell itself, on the night the moon turned, using fae-blood and stolen voices to bind the mountain's power to himself. For that he needed fae-blood that ran true and strong.

He needed Sef.

On the third night, the censer-bearer came to the cage bars and sat down across from Sef, cross-legged, patient as stone, and took off his hood.

Up close, without the iron shadowing his face, he looked almost ordinary. Grey-skinned, hollow-cheeked, with the eyes of a man looking at you from the bottom of a well. But there was something else, buried so deep Sef almost missed it — and Sef missed nothing, that was the whole point of Sef.

Grief. Old grief, worn smooth as river glass, the way grief gets when you carry it so long it stops hurting and starts being furniture.

"They'll ask me to hurt you eventually," the censer-bearer said. "Bloodletting, probably. The Duke's chirurgeons are thorough." He said it without menace, the way you'd report the weather. "I want you to know that I remember what it is to be taken. I was taken too. Sixty years ago. I was fae-blooded, like you."

Sef stared at him.

"There is no rescue coming," the man continued. "Your friends are four, and one of them is dying, and the Duke's vanguard is nine thousand. Whatever hope you're holding — set it down. It's heavy, and you'll need your strength for what's ahead." He rose, rebanded, hooded, a walking silence again. At the edge of the firelight he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the song never fully leaves us. That's the cruelty of it. I have spent sixty years wishing it would."

He walked away into the dark, and Sef sat in the iron cage under the strange southern stars, and did not set the hope down. Hope was the one thing Sef had never once put down in eleven years, not in the hedge-bottoms, not in the workhouse, not in the snow, and they weren't going to start for a man made of iron and old grief.

Instead Sef did what Sef did best. They listened.

All night, they listened past the hounds, past the sentries, past the groaning wheels and the restless thousands, down through the earth to the thin bright thread of the mountain's song, still humming, still waiting, still saying *soon.*

And threaded inside the song now — faint, frantic, unmistakable — Sef heard something new. Voices moving fast through the high passes. Four heartbeats' worth of fury and fear, driving north along the smuggler roads, following the trail Sef was leaving them: every stone Sef touched, every bar Sef gripped, singing their location into the mountain's memory like a message scratched on a wall.

*Tamsin,* Sef thought down the thread, with all the force of a child who had once moved a whole village's cattle with a thought they never knew they had. *I'm leaving you a road. Come find me.*

Far above, in the frozen passes, Sef felt the thread tremble — felt someone hear.

* * *

In the grey hour before dawn, the wagon train halted at a river crossing, and for twenty minutes the censer-bearer walked the bank alone, checking the ford, his back to the cage.

Twenty minutes of silence-without-the-silencer. Sef's hearing unfolded like a fist opening.

They didn't scream. Screaming brought guards. Instead Sef pressed both palms flat against the iron bar and poured everything down through it into the mountain — the layout of the camp, the count of the wagons, the black-iron horns, the broken tenor in the singer's wagon, the name of the ford, the hour of the crossing, *everything*, a child's whole hoard of stolen knowledge given to the stone.

Then, because there was time, and because hope was heavy but so was love, Sef added one more thing. A message, shaped as carefully as they knew how, sent down the singing thread of the world to whoever was climbing north through the snow after them:

*Don't come for me straight. He wants you to. He's building a trap with me for bait — I heard the lieutenants say it. Go to the Deepwell. Finish it. I'll be there. I'll get myself there. I'm good at getting places I shouldn't be.*

A pause. Then, smaller, the part that wasn't strategy at all:

*Tell Tamsin I chose this. Tell her it doesn't count as leaving. Tell her doors, not walls. Tell her —*

The censer-bearer's boots crunched back across the shingle, and the silence closed over Sef like water.

But the message was gone. Sent. Racing up through root and rock toward four people and a dying witch, toward the passes, toward the Deepwell, toward whatever came next.

Sef settled back against the cold bars, closed their eyes, and began, silently, under the silence, to practice the first line of the covenant-song — the one Wren had taught them, the fae line, the line that ran in their blood like a river runs in its bed.

If Corvane wanted a fae-blooded child to power his counter-song, he was welcome to try.

He had no idea what he'd captured.

The mountain said *soon.*
