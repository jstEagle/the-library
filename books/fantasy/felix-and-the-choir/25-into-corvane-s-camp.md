## 25. Into Corvane's Camp

They told her no. All of them, in turn, and then all of them together around the fire at the mouth of the smuggler's cave, with the rain coming down outside like a judgment.

Brannoc said it first, arms crossed over his barrel chest: "Ye walk into ten thousand men, lass, ye don't walk out."

Wren, gray and fading against her bedroll, said it gently: "Your face is known now. The tollhouse sketches went out weeks ago."

Merrin said it last, and worst, because he said it quietly: "You're the bridge, Tamsin. If they take you, there is no song. There's nothing left to fight for."

She listened to all of it. She let them finish. And then she packed her knife, her taster's kit, and three days of hard bread, and she waited for dark.

Because here was what none of them understood — what none of them *could* understand, having never been a thing that was owned: Tamsin Thackeray had spent her whole life being useful to people who saw her as an instrument. A tongue. A tool. She knew exactly how instruments were stored, guarded, and spent. Corvane had taken Sef because the child's blood could corrupt a covenant. Which meant Corvane would keep Sef alive, close, and careful — caged like a rare bird, not thrown into a ditch.

And Tamsin knew supply roads the way priests knew psalms.

"You can't stop me," she said, shouldering her pack. "You can only decide whether I go alone."

The argument lasted another hour. She won it the way she'd won every argument since the salt gate: by being already moving before anyone agreed.

* * *

Corvane's army did not march like an army. It flowed, the way a flood flows — down the river valleys, along the old king's roads, filling every low place between Thornfell and the free cities. From the ridge above the Vale of Harn, Tamsin lay on her belly in the wet heather and watched the camp spread below her like a city built overnight: tent-lines in perfect rows, cookfires by the hundred, picket lines, horse lines, the great banner of the black crow hanging limp in the still air.

Ten thousand men. Maybe more. Her eyes did what they had been trained to do since childhood — catalogued, measured, looked for the poison in the pattern.

And found it quickly enough. Armies are bodies, and bodies have throats. Every throat has a supply road.

The army ate from the south. She could see the wagon trains crawling up the river road, could see the butchers' quarter off to one side with its reeking smoke, the grain wagons stacked under canvas, the water carts going back and forth to the river like patient beetles. And where food goes in, everything else goes too: fodder, firewood, ink, rope, cages.

Her father — the smuggler who'd died owing debts and leaving her his maps — used to say: *every fortress is a stomach, and every stomach has an appetite.* You didn't break walls. You came in with the flour.

She watched for two days. That was the discipline of it, the part Merrin would never have believed she had in her: two days flat in the heather, cold, barely eating, memorizing the rhythm of the place. Watch rotations changed at dusk and again past midnight. The supply gate on the south side never fully closed while wagons moved; a bored sergeant checked seals and waved drivers through. Wagons came from Marrowgate's direction — from the burned city — and the men driving them had the hollow faces of pressed laborers, prisoners given a job instead of a rope.

That was her way in. Not courage. Arithmetic.

On the third night she cut a dead sentry's cloak from a laundry line near the pickets — the sentry himself was very much alive and asleep, which she counted as a good omen — and walked down to the river road with her hood up and her gait changed, shoulders rounded, one hand held stiff, a limping carter with bad teeth and no story worth asking about.

The forgotten face. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she carried like a stone: everyone remembered the king's taster as a function, a fixture, a pair of lips at the high table. Nobody remembered her *face*. Aldous had made sure of it — "you're furniture, girl, furniture doesn't get noticed" — and for once his training served her against him.

At the supply gate, a sergeant with a lantern squinted at her seal-less wagon handcart of turnips she'd liberated from a farmstead two miles back.

"Where's your papers?"

"Press-gang took 'em," she rasped, and coughed wetly. "Took my brother too. Says I drive or we both hang."

He looked at her — really looked, three full seconds, long enough for her heart to count itself down like a fuse — and then waved her through, because ten thousand mouths do not care about papers when the turnips are turning soft.

* * *

Inside, the camp swallowed her.

That was the strange mercy of armies: nobody sees anyone. Ten thousand men each see their own fire, their own boots, their own misery. She pushed her cart down the lane between the tents and became invisible in the way she had always been invisible — by being beneath notice, by smelling of turnips and river mud.

She worked. That was the trick of staying somewhere you shouldn't be: have a task. She delivered her turnips to the butchers' quarter, got shouted at, got redirected, got shouted at again. By midnight she had a splinter of chalk in her pocket and a route in her head marked with the things that mattered:

*The cages are north.* She'd seen them on her second day of watching — a stockade near the command tents, wagons with barred beds under guard, the kind of transport used for something valuable rather than something merely dangerous. Prisoners of rank went in the stockade. Caged wagons meant cargo that had to breathe.

Getting near the command tents was another matter. The lanes narrowed there, guards thickened, and the quality of the silence changed — the silence of men who were afraid of someone nearby. She abandoned the cart behind the farrier's line and went forward on foot, head down, carrying an armload of firewood she hadn't been asked to deliver.

A voice stopped her twenty yards from the stockade.

"You. Wood for the Duke's fire?"

She turned slowly, keeping her face stupid. An officer — young, tired, the kind of officer who delegated — gestured at a tent where light spilled gold onto the mud. "In there. Stack it by the flap. Don't touch anything."

So Tamsin Thackeray, who had tasted death for a king, carried firewood for a usurper.

She stacked the wood with a carter's clumsy care, and through the gap in the tent flap she heard voices — and one voice she knew.

Merrin's.

Not singing. Speaking, low and frayed, and then a sharp word from someone else, and then — worse than anything — Merrin's voice rising into song anyway, a scale, a fragment of melody, forced out of him like a tooth being pulled. It broke off. Someone laughed.

Tamsin's hands went still on the woodpile. Cold moved through her, slow, from the spine outward.

*They have him. They've had him since the rescue.*

She had known it — known it in the way you know a wound is deep without looking at it. Merrin had stepped into the open at Corvane's camp with his hands raised and his voice shouting *run*, and she had run, because running was the only thing that saved Sef, and she had told herself every night since that he'd escaped, that a man who knew every dockside alley in Marrowgate could slip ten thousand men.

He hadn't slipped. He'd been *kept*. Because a coerced tenor is worth more than a corpse.

"Move along," the young officer said, and she moved, and inside her chest something closed its hand around a blade.

* * *

The cage wagons stood in a row of four. Three were empty, doors gaping. The fourth was not.

Sef sat inside it like a small gray bird in a trap, knees drawn up, and even across forty yards of torchlight Tamsin saw what captivity does to children: it makes them smaller without making them younger. The child's fae-blood showed faintly even here — the ears, the way torchlight seemed to bend just slightly toward those watching eyes — and the guards around the wagon stood farther back than guards usually stand, and one of them wore a leather glove on his right hand and kept flexing it, and Tamsin thought: *they're afraid of touching the cargo.*

Good. Fear of the cargo meant rules about the cargo. Rules were doors, and doors had hinges.

She drifted to the edge of the torchlight, became a shadow shifting wood, and let her eyes do their work. Four guards visible, two more walking a circuit. The wagon's lock was new iron, army-issue — good against thieves, useless against a taster's kit, because tasters' kits contained things that opened locks quietly if you'd practiced on Aldous's desk drawers as a bored teenager, and she had, and he'd never caught her, which in retrospect should have told her something about both of them.

But not yet. Not with six guards and a camp waking toward dawn. Patience. Patience was the first thing they'd ever taught her — *swallow, wait, watch the king's face* — and she had been patient for twenty years.

What she needed tonight was information. So she did what she did best: she listened, and she tasted the air.

The officers' mess tent glowed fifty yards beyond the stockade, and voices carried in the cold. She positioned herself at the woodpile, ostensibly restacking it, and let the words come to her in pieces, the way poison comes to the tongue — fragments first, then the shape of the whole.

"—the Deepwell, yes, my lord, the sappers say the chamber will hold eight hundred—"

"—the captives sing the true lines, the choir sings the inversion, and the mountain opens or the mountain breaks, either serves—"

"—the fae bitch's covenant runs on consent, my lord duke says, so we will *consent* it to death—"

Tamsin went very still among the logs.

She had imagined many things Corvane might want with Sef's blood. A weapon. A hostage. A key turned backward. But this — this was worse and cleaner than any of it. He wasn't going to corrupt the covenant from outside. He was going to *sing it wrong from inside*, at the Deepwell itself, using stolen fae voices to force the covenant's own machinery into his service. The Choir bound human and fae realms in mutual protection. A counter-choir sung at the Deepwell with captive blood would bind them in mutual *destruction* — or bind them to him, which amounted to the same thing.

And Merrin's voice would be in it. Forced. Inverted. The finest singer of the realm made into a crow's instrument.

*Taste it,* Aldous used to say, when she was nine and trembling before a dish of quail. *Don't flinch. Name the poison. Naming is power.*

She named it now, silently, precisely, the way she named arsenic and nightshade: *Corvane means to unmake the world's oldest promise and wear it as a crown. The counter-song needs the Deepwell. The Deepwell needs the moon's turn. We have eleven days.*

Eleven days. One child in a cage. One singer in chains. One bridge standing in the mud with an armload of other people's firewood.

* * *

She almost left then. That was the honest truth of it, the truth she would carry afterward like a scar: she got clear of the stockade, got back to the farrier's line, got her hand on her knife and her feet pointed south toward the hills and Wren and Brannoc and the plan they'd bled to build — and she stopped.

Because Sef had looked up.

Across the torchlight, across forty yards of mud and armed men, the child's eyes had lifted from the straw floor of the cage and found her — found her the way fae-blooded eyes find things, straight through hoods and shadows and the ordinary blindness of the world. And Sef had not cried out. Had not lunged at the bars. Had done nothing but look at her, steadily, with an expression no child should have to know how to make: *I see you. I won't give you away. Take your time.*

Take your time. As if Tamsin were the one being rescued.

She stood in the dark between the woodpiles and felt the choice close around her like a hand. She could run south with information and a plan and a clean conscience, and the company would strike at the Deepwell with everything they had, and Sef would be standing in a cage wagon in the middle of Corvane's counter-choir when it happened — the bridge's blood called for, the child's blood corrupted, the whole design needing both of them, which meant Corvane would keep them both alive until the last possible moment, which meant—

Which meant there was a window. Ugly, narrow, guarded, but real.

Or she could go back now, tonight, half-cocked, and probably die in the mud forty feet from a child who would then watch her do it.

Patience. Swallow. Wait. Watch the king's face.

She went back to the stockade's edge one more time, and this time she counted everything: the changing of the circuit guard (four hundred heartbeats), the position of the lanterns (one guttering, one steady, one blind spot behind the wheel of the third wagon), the depth of the mud by the cage wheels (ankle-deep, sound-swallowing, a gift). She fixed the lock's shape in her memory so Wren could talk her through picking it blind. She noted which tent the cage keys hung outside of — a canvas square with a bored-looking corporal asleep in front of it, keys on a nail, because armies are stomachs and stomachs are lazy.

Then she melted back into the camp's grey hours and began, methodically, to build herself a reason to stay.

It took her a day. She became the carter with the turnip cart permanently attached to the butchers' quarter, the one nobody questioned because everybody had seen her yesterday. She learned the names of three sergeants and the drinking habits of two. She learned that the counter-choir rehearsed at dusk in a long pavilion near the command tents, and she stood at the woodpile during rehearsal and listened to eighty stolen voices sing the covenant's opening verse *backward*, and the sound of it made her teeth ache and her scars itch, and she understood with total clarity why Wren had gone gray describing it.

And on the second night, delivering slops to the guard detail at the stockade, she passed close enough to the cage wagon to speak — one breath, under the clatter of the bucket, words shaped for fae ears alone:

"Eleven days. Hold on. I'm coming back with the whole sky."

Sef didn't move. Didn't look up. Just smiled, very slightly, at the straw.

* * *

She slipped out of the camp before dawn on the third day, in the stream of dung-carts and dead horses that left through the east gate, and she climbed the ridge above the Vale of Harn with the army's thousand cookfires smoking below her like a field of gray flowers.

Merrin was alive. Caged, coerced, but alive — and singing, which meant they needed his voice intact, which meant there was still a version of this where he walked out. Sef was alive and afraid and holding on. The enemy's whole design rested on the Deepwell and the moon's turn, and she knew its shape now, every beam and joint of it, the way she knew the shape of poison on her tongue.

She also knew, with the same cold precision, what the others would say when she told them the plan forming in her chest. Brannoc would swear. Wren would calculate odds and hate the answer. Merrin wasn't there to argue, which was perhaps the only mercy in it.

They'd need to take the cage wagon on the march — Corvane couldn't leave the camp static forever; the moon was turning and the Deepwell was calling him north, up the very passes Tamsin knew better than anyone breathing. An army moves its treasures when it moves. Treasures on wagons. Wagons on roads. Roads through Thornfell.

*Every fortress is a stomach,* her father had said. *And every stomach has an appetite.*

Corvane's army was about to march into the mountains, hungry, along roads that Tamsin Thackeray had been mapping since she was small enough to ride her father's shoulders — roads with narrows, and cliff-shelves, and one particular gorge where a single smuggler with a rope and a grudge could stop a column of ten thousand men.

She turned south and ran for the cave, and the plan sharpened in her with every stride, fine and bright and terrible, like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath.
