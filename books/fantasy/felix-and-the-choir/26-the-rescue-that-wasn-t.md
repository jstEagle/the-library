## 26. The Rescue That Wasn't

Merrin had spent eleven years learning to read a room by its silences. A tavern that went quiet a beat too long when he walked in. A patron who stopped mid-sentence and didn't resume. The particular quality of hush that meant someone was listening for his voice — the voice they'd banned from every court stage from Marrowgate to the sea.

So when the camp of Duke Corvane's army went quiet around their cookfires at dusk, Merrin felt it in his teeth.

He lay flat on the ridge above the encampment, belly in cold pine needles, watching ten thousand fires bloom across the valley floor like a pox of orange stars. Somewhere down there, in a cage or a tent or worse, was Sef. And somewhere down there, moving through the supply lines with a stolen quartermaster's coat and a face no one remembered, was Tamsin.

*She'll be fine,* he told himself, and did not believe it.

Beside him, Brannoc shifted his bulk with the care of a man who'd learned that mountains gave nothing away for free. "Third watch changes at moonrise," the dwarf murmured. "That's your window. After that, the pickets double."

"I know."

"You don't like it."

"I don't like any of it." Merrin kept his eyes on the camp. "I don't like her going down alone. I don't like that we're crouched on a hillside instead of three days gone with the child already safe. I don't like that Aldous knew about the tollhouse before we did, and the river crossing, and—" He stopped. Breathed. "I don't like being the one who waits."

Brannoc grunted. In the dark, his beard made him look like something carved out of the mountain itself, all stone and iron and one pair of eyes catching firelight from below. "Waiting's the job," he said. "Her father used to say it. *The road does the running; the runner does the waiting.* Tamsin knows these roads better than Corvane's whole quartermaster corps. She'll walk out of that camp like smoke through a keyhole."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then we go down after her," Brannoc said simply, "and we die badly, and Wren's sacrifice is worth nothing, and Sef stays in a cage. So let's both be very good at waiting."

Merrin almost laughed. It came out as something else — a small, cracked sound that Brannoc politely ignored.

Below, the camp changed shifts. Torches moved along the perimeter in slow procession, and the noise of ten thousand men settling into evening rose up the valley — laughter, a fiddle somewhere, the clang of mess tins. An army sounded, from a distance, almost like a city. Almost like home.

Merrin found himself thinking of the conservatory he would never build now, the students he would never teach, the songs he'd hoarded through the silent years like a miser's coins. He thought of Tamsin's face two nights ago, in the cave, when he'd finally coaxed her through the bridge-line without her voice cracking — the naked astonishment on it, as if joy were a language she'd only just learned she could speak. He had watched her discover music the way you watch someone discover sunlight after a lifetime underground.

He was not going to lose that. He refused.

"Signal," Brannoc said.

A light flickered twice in the trees below the eastern picket line — once, pause, twice. Tamsin's signal: *inside, clear.* Then, after a long moment, a single flash. *Found them.*

Brannoc exhaled through his nose. "The child's alive."

"The child's alive," Merrin agreed, and felt something in his chest unclench and immediately re-clench tighter, because *found* was not *out*, and between *found* and *out* lay half a mile of sentries, horse lines, and the command tents where Aldous Vane kept his meticulous, patient, terrible mind.

They waited. The moon climbed. The fiddle below played a tune Merrin knew — an old Marrowgate dock song, which meant some soldier there was a long way from home, and Merrin hated him a little for having a home to be far from, and hated himself more for the pettiness.

Then the eastern treeline flashed again. Twice. Pause. Twice. Pause. Twice.

Three pairs. Not a signal they'd planned. Merrin's stomach dropped.

"What's that mean?" Brannoc asked.

"It means she's moving," Merrin said slowly, working it out, "and it means she wants us to move too. Meet her at the fallback point." He paused. "It also means she's improvising, because we never agreed on a third signal."

"Ah," said Brannoc. "Improvising."

"She's good at it."

"So are dead people, occasionally. By accident."

They packed the camp in silence and started down through the trees toward the stream-cut gully that served as their fallback — a fold in the land where the mountain's runoff ran shallow over slate, quiet enough to talk, exposed enough to see anyone coming. Merrin took the lead because his night eyes were better; Brannoc took the rear because Brannoc always took the rear, on the principle that anything chasing them would have to go through four feet of dwarf first.

The gully received them with the smell of wet stone and cold water. No Tamsin. No Sef.

Merrin held up a hand. They waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. The moon cleared the ridgeline and turned the world silver and black, and the water talked to itself over the slate, and Merrin counted his own heartbeats because counting was better than imagining.

At the twenty-third minute, he heard footsteps. Two sets. Light ones, quick ones, and heavier ones dragging slightly — and then Tamsin came around the bend of the gully with Sef on her back, and Merrin's relief lasted exactly as long as it took him to see her face.

She was gray. Not tired-gray. *Wrong*-gray. Her lips had a bluish cast, and there was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers, and her eyes had the fixed brightness of someone holding themselves together by will alone.

"Sef first," she said, before anyone could speak. "Sef first, questions later."

Brannoc lifted the child off her back as if lifting something made of glass. Sef was conscious — that was the first mercy — but their eyes were unfocused, and there was a raw band around one wrist where a manacle had been, and when they tried to speak only a whisper came out.

"They drank from me," Sef whispered. "Every day. The duke's men. Into little cups. They said it was for the counter-song." Their gaze drifted, unfocused, to the dark sky. "I can hear it starting. Under the ground. It's wrong, Merrin. It's all wrong notes."

Merrin crouched to the child's level. "We're getting you away from here. You won't hear it anymore."

"You don't understand." Sef's hand closed on his sleeve with surprising strength. "It's not under the ground here. It's coming from the Deepwell. They're already marching there. And the Queen's host is coming from the other side, and when they meet—"

"Later," Tamsin said. Her voice was steady. Her body, Merrin noticed, was listing slightly to the left, as though the left side of her had stopped taking instructions. "Sef drinks this." She pressed a flask into the child's hands. "Wren's recipe. All of it."

"That's your last dose of the fever-tincture," Brannoc rumbled. "You're supposed to drink it yourself. You look like something a cat dragged out of a culvert."

"I look *fine*."

"You look like a corpse with opinions."

"Then I'll die with opinions. Sef drinks it."

There was a short, hopeless silence, the kind that families have when they know they've lost an argument before it starts. Sef drank. Tamsin sat down heavily on a rock as if her strings had been cut, and Merrin knelt beside her and finally got a proper look at her in the moonlight.

The blood on her sleeve wasn't hers. But the bruise blooming along her jaw was, and so was the deep gash above her ear, and when he pulled back her collar he saw the edge of a mark he recognized from the tollhouse: the round burn of a drugged blade's kiss.

"They tagged you," he said quietly.

"Aldous's new toys. Sleep-venom, not killing-venom." She managed a crooked smile. "Lucky me. They still want me breathing."

"How did you get out? The east picket line was doubled. We watched it. There was no gap."

"There was no gap," she agreed. "So I made one. Started a small fire in the fodder stores. Horses hate fire. Horses make excellent arguments for rearranging picket lines." She swayed. Merrin caught her shoulder. "But it turns out Aldous doesn't post his best men where the fire is. He posts them where the fire *isn't*. Where the quiet is. He had two men on the stream path. Knew I'd come for water for the child. Because he taught me, you see. He taught me that a runner always thinks about water."

Her voice stayed level through all of it, and that levelness frightened Merrin more than sobbing would have.

"He let you go," Brannoc said grimly. "Partway."

"He let me get to the tree line. Then he stepped out of the dark like he'd been waiting for a dinner invitation, and he said—" Tamsin stopped. For the first time since the gully, her composure flickered. "He said, *You've grown into your mother's stubbornness, little bird.* And then he whistled, and six men came out of the rocks, and I had maybe ten seconds and a sleeping child on my back."

"And?" Merrin prompted, though his mouth had gone dry.

"And I remembered what you told me. About how a decoy works. How the eye goes to the loudest thing." She looked up at him, and in the moonlight her expression was terrible and tender at once. "You told me that in Marrowgate, the night the inquisitors swept the taverns. You put yourself in the doorway and sang, and everyone looked at you, and I walked out the back with the map. You said, *the loudest thing in the room buys the quietest thing its life.*"

Merrin felt the shape of what was coming before she said it, the way you feel a storm's pressure change before the first drop falls. "Tamsin."

"There's a ford downstream," she said. "Half a mile. Shallow water, loud water — you can't hear footsteps in it. If we split, if one of us goes loud and visible along the open bank while the others cross quiet—"

"No."

"—then the loud one draws every man Aldous has on this side of the valley, and the quiet ones are over the mountain before the nets close—"

"*No.*" Merrin stood. His voice cracked on it, embarrassingly, the old performance instinct turning refusal into theater whether he wanted it or not. "No. Find another way. Go back up the gully. Circle wide. Something."

"There's no time to circle. The fever-tincture was the last of Wren's stores, and Sef needs a healer, and the Deepwell deadline is in five days, and Aldous has six men and probably thirty more within shouting distance." She said it all flatly, the way she recited poison lists, fact by fact, no feeling permitted to slow the arithmetic. "And there's one of us who can't fight, one of us built like a barrel and loud as one, and one of us who spent eleven years learning how to be looked at."

"Merrin," Brannoc started.

"One of us," Merrin said over him, "spent eleven years learning how to be looked at, and is *not volunteering*, Tamsin, because the last person who volunteered for something like this was Wren, and Wren is dying in a cave two valleys back, and I am not—"

He stopped. Because Tamsin had reached out and taken his hand, and her grip was stronger than her body looked, and her eyes were bright and fierce and full of the exact same terror he was feeling.

"I know," she said softly. "I know what I'm asking. I'm asking it anyway, and I'm asking it knowing you'll say yes, and I hate myself for knowing that, and I'm going to spend whatever time I have left hating myself for it. But Sef heard the counter-song starting, Merrin. Under the ground. Five days. Everything — Wren, the Queen's bargain, the free cities holding the passes, all of it — comes down to whether we get to the Deepwell with three voices. You trained my voice. You can't train it from a cage."

"And you think I can outrun six soldiers in the dark?"

"I think you're the finest singer I've ever heard, and I think sound carries strangely in this valley — Wren said so, the night we heard the harmonies under Thornfell. I think if you stand on that open bank and sing, every man in a mile will turn toward you, and men who turn toward a voice forget everything else. I think you can lead them on a merry chase down the river and lose them at the old millrace, where the smuggler's crawl lets out past the falls. You know the crawl. Brannoc showed us."

"I know the crawl," Merrin said. His heart was hammering. "I also know that Aldous has heard me sing. At court. Before. He knows my voice the way I know yours."

"Then don't sing like yourself." Tamsin leaned closer. "Sing like the Choir. Sing the notation from the map — the opening verse, the one the stone didn't answer at the Hollow Court. Sing it loud enough that the whole valley hears it. Because I promise you, Merrin Vale, that when those notes go out over Corvane's camp, things are going to happen that neither of us can predict — fae-blooded soldiers waking up, horses panicking, the mountain itself listening. Every eye in that army will be on you. And no one — *no one* — will be looking at the stream."

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The water ran. Somewhere far off, a sentry's horn sounded the hour.

"This is madness," Merrin said.

"Yes," said Tamsin.

"Complete, suicidal madness."

"Yes."

"And if I'm caught—"

"Then we come back for you," Tamsin said. "Whatever it costs, however long it takes. I came into Corvane's camp tonight for Sef. I'll come into hell for you. That's not a promise, Merrin. That's just what this family does."

*Family.* The word hit him harder than any argument could have. He thought of the winter camp, the stories, the way Sef slept curled against Brannoc's side like a kitten against a stove. He thought of Wren's laugh, thin as paper now but still laughing. He thought of Tamsin's face when she'd first sung, really sung, and the sun coming out in her.

He thought: *if I don't do this, I will never forgive myself, and worse, I will never hear the end of it from her ghost.*

"All right," he said. "All right. But I want it noted, formally, for posterity, that this plan is garbage."

"Noted," said Brannoc solemnly. "Chiseled into the mountain as we speak."

Merrin laughed despite everything — a real laugh, startled out of him — and then it caught in his throat, because Sef had risen unsteadily to their feet and was looking at him with those huge, luminous fae-struck eyes.

"You'll come back," Sef said. Not a question. A command, almost. The certainty of a child who had heard the future under the ground and decided it contained Merrin Vale, alive.

"I'll come back," Merrin said, and was surprised to find he believed it — or wanted to believe it so hard that belief and wanting had become indistinguishable, which was, he suspected, the definition of faith.

* * *

They moved fast after that.

Brannoc carried Sef. Tamsin walked point, favoring her left leg but refusing help, navigating by stars and memory down the stream path toward the ford. Merrin followed, rehearsing the opening verse under his breath, feeling the notes settle into his body like old friends returning from exile.

At the ford, the plan assembled itself in whispers. The water ran shin-deep and loud over its bed of stones, and beyond it the open bank stretched pale in the moonlight, a natural stage if ever there was one.

"Tamsin." Merrin caught her arm as she turned away. "When this is done — when the Choir is reforged and Corvane is dust and we're all impossibly old and insufferable — I'm building that conservatory. In Marrowgate. And you're going to teach there. Listening. You're going to teach children how to listen for the second voice beneath the first."

She stared at him. In the moonlight, something moved behind her eyes — hope, terror, and something else, something she'd been carrying since the winter camp and hadn't yet found words for.

"Merrin—"

"Later," he said, using her own trick against her. "Questions later. Go."

She went. One moment she was there, gripping his arm; the next she was across the ford and into the shadows on the far bank, with Brannoc and Sef, and the darkness folded around them like a page closing.

Merrin stood alone on the near bank, the open ground stretching before him, the enemy camp a glow of embers to the south. He rolled his shoulders. He flexed his hands. Eleven years ago, he had stood on the greatest stage in the realm and opened his mouth, and the whole court had gone silent, and he had learned that silence could be a kind of power.

Now he would learn what kind.

He stepped out onto the open bank, lifted his face to the moon, and began to sing.

Not his own voice. Not the polished court tenor they'd banned and broken. He reached past all of it, down to the notation on the map, down to the covenant-song that predated every kingdom and every ban, and he let it pour out of him across the valley — the opening verse, the one the Hollow Court's stone had refused, sung now at full strength under the open sky.

The effect was immediate and strange.

The camp below erupted. Horns blew confused and contradictory signals. Men poured from tents, pointing, shouting, torches streaming toward the bank like sparks toward flame. Somewhere in the command tents, Merrin knew, Aldous Vane was standing very still, listening, and recognizing, and understanding exactly what this was.

*Come on,* Merrin thought, walking backward along the bank, drawing them, leading them, the notes rolling out of him like a tide. *Come on, then. Look at me. Look at the loudest thing in the room.*

Torches crested the ridge to his left. Soldiers fanned out across the open ground, cautious, superstitious, crossing themselves against fae-work. Above the din, Merrin heard — or imagined he heard — other voices rising faintly from the camp itself, ragged and wondering, soldiers with fae blood in their veins answering the verse without meaning to. Horses screamed in the picket lines. The mountain seemed to lean in.

He backed toward the millrace, singing, and the net closed behind him exactly as Tamsin had promised it would — every torch, every blade, every eye, all of it flowing toward the loudest thing in the room.

He was three steps from the smuggler's crawl when the torchlight ahead of him resolved into a figure, and the figure into a man, and the man into Master Aldous Vane, spymaster, traitor, the closest thing Merrin had ever had to a father-in-law of enemies — standing calmly in the narrow path with six crossbows leveled at Merrin's chest and an expression of genuine, almost paternal regret.

"Master Vale," Aldous said, over the fading last note. "What a *performance*."

Merrin stopped singing.

Behind Aldous, far across the dark valley, the stream ran on unheard, carrying three fugitives toward the mountain and the Deepwell and the five days that remained. Merrin thought of Sef's certainty. Of Tamsin's promise. Of the second voice beneath the first.

He raised his chin, and smiled the smile he'd once used to bring kings to their knees.

"You should have seen the reviews," he said.

The crossbows didn't waver. Aldous studied him for a long moment — and then, unexpectedly, gestured, and the bolts lowered.

"Take him alive," Aldous said quietly. "The duke wants his tenor intact." His eyes lingered on Merrin's face, searching for something — fear, perhaps, or hatred — and finding, instead, a calm that seemed to trouble him. "You knew we'd be here."

"I hoped," Merrin said. "There's a difference. Hope is a plan with better lighting."

Something flickered across the spymaster's face — doubt, or its shadow — and was gone. "Bind his hands. Gently. He's worth more than the rest of you put together."

As they bound him, Merrin looked back once, deliberately, toward the dark line of the stream — letting Aldous see him look, letting the old spy read whatever he wanted into it. Let him wonder. Let him lie awake tonight doing the arithmetic of what had crossed that ford while his men chased a song.

Let the loudest thing in the room buy the quietest things their lives.

They marched him down toward the campfires, and Merrin Vale, disgraced singer, walked into captivity with his head high and a verse lodged in his chest like a coal — ready to burn through anything they built around it.

*Five days*, he thought. *Hold the note, Tamsin. I'll find the harmony.*
