## 27. The Counter-Song

The tent was pitched over a hollow in the earth, and the earth remembered.

Aldous felt it the moment he stepped inside — a wrongness in the ground's hum, like a struck bell that had been muffled with cloth. Corvane's engineers had chosen this spot deliberately. The Deepwell's roots ran beneath the whole eastern shoulder of Thornfell, and where they ran closest, sound behaved strangely. Notes lingered too long. Silence had texture. It was, Aldous supposed, exactly what you wanted if you intended to build an instrument out of a mountain.

He intended no such thing himself. He only intended to watch, and to count, and to keep his face arranged into the expression of a man whose certainty was absolute.

Forty-two voices stood on risers inside the great pavilion, arranged by pitch the way Corvane's choir-master had arranged them for three weeks now: fae captives in the center ranks, human conscripts flanking, and at the front, alone on a small dais of black oak, the tenor.

Merrin Vale.

Aldous had heard him sing once, years ago, in the high gallery of the royal chapel — before the ascension, before the refusal, before the disgrace. Even then he had understood why the old king kept the man close. There were voices that performed music, and there were voices that *were* music, and Merrin Vale's had been the second kind. Listening to him had felt like being told a truth you already knew but had never dared say aloud.

Now his wrists were iron-cuffed behind him, and a collar of worked silver sat against his throat — one of the inquisitors' instruments, tuned to punish any note that strayed from the score. When he sang correctly, it did nothing. When he faltered, or when he tried to bend the melody toward something the choir-master hadn't written, it bit. Aldous had seen the burns on the man's neck that morning. Thin lines, precise as script.

*They are writing on him,* Aldous thought, and then put the thought away, because thoughts like that were cracks, and cracks were how men like him died.

"Again," said the choir-master, a soft-bellied man named Fessik who had once directed cathedral music in Corvane's capital and now directed this. "From the seventh bar. Master Vale — the ascent. *Higher.* You are not begging the mountain. You are commanding it."

Merrin lifted his chin. His voice, when it came, was still beautiful. That was the horror of it. Three weeks of captivity and coercion and silver at his throat, and the beauty hadn't been beaten out of him; it had only been bent to purpose, the way a river is bent to turn a millwheel. The corrupted line climbed — a covenant-verse turned inside out, its harmonies inverted so that instead of binding two realms together it would pry them apart at the seam — and forty-one other voices rose beneath it, and the ground under Aldous's boots shivered like a dog hearing its name.

It should have satisfied him. It was, after all, his design.

Not the song itself — that was Fessik's craft and Corvane's ambition — but the architecture around it. Aldous had spent thirty years learning that wars were not won by armies but by information, and the information here was simple: the free cities would fight for their walls, but they would not fight for a mountain. Let Corvane march north and bleed against Marrowgate's gates all winter, and the war would end in stalemate, and stalemate favored the crown. But give the duke a counter-song, a way to break the old covenant at its root and turn Thornfell's own deep magic against the fae courts — that changed everything. The fae would retreat beyond the verge rather than be unmade. The free cities, seeing their oldest ally abandon them, would sue for terms by spring.

Clean. Bloodless, almost. Aldous had built a career on almost-bloodless.

So why did the ground's shivering feel like a warning?

* * *

After rehearsal, Corvane summoned him to the command pavilion, and Aldous walked through the camp with his hands clasped behind his back and his eyes doing what they always did: counting.

Eleven thousand infantry along the river flats, in good order. Two thousand horse picketed on the rise. Siege train half-assembled, the trebuchets still crated. Forage sufficient for six weeks, eight if they stripped the valley. And everywhere, threaded through the ordinary machinery of an army, the inquisitors — grey-robed, silent, carrying their instruments in long cases like lute-players going to a funeral. Forty-two voices in the choir. Sixty-one instruments in the inquisitors' armory. He had counted both twice.

Corvane received him standing over a map table, which was how he received everyone; the duke believed that height conferred authority, and so had built a dais under the map table's legs. A small vanity. Harmless. Most vanities were harmless. That was the thing about men like Corvane that Aldous had understood for thirty years and was only now, at the end of his career, beginning to question: they were rarely monsters in the ways that mattered. They were vain, and impatient, and certain, and certainty did more damage than cruelty ever had.

"The taster," Corvane said, without looking up.

"Still at large."

"That is not an answer I pay for."

Aldous let the silence sit for exactly as long as insolence could masquerade as deliberation. "She went to ground after the tollhouse. My riders found blood and boot-prints heading up-valley toward the old shepherd tracks. She knows those tracks better than anyone alive — better than I do, and I taught her half of them." He paused. "She'll make for the Deepwell. If she means to answer your choir, that's where she must stand."

"And you will be there to take her."

"I will be there."

Corvane finally looked up. He had a handsome face going soft with middle age, and eyes that held nothing you could argue with, because there was nothing in them to argue against — just appetite, smooth and patient as standing water. "You trained her, Aldous. You picked her out of the gutter kitchens yourself. Tell me honestly — can she actually do it? Sing the covenant? She's a food-taster. She has no voice worth the name."

"She has something better than a voice." Aldous heard his own tone go flat, careful. "She has blood that the fae cannot touch and the covenant will recognize. Wren's records were clear before we burned them. The bridge-line doesn't require beauty. It requires a body that belongs to neither realm and both. Tamsin Thackeray was brewed to be exactly that, twenty-six years ago, by men who understood the old rites better than anyone since. Whatever else she is, she is the key."

"Then we take the key before the lock turns." Corvane returned to his map. "Double the watch on the verges. And Aldous—" the duke's finger tapped the Deepwell's marked position, once, twice, "—if it comes to choosing between taking her alive and stopping her, stop her. The choir doesn't need her. It needs her *absent*."

"Yes, my lord."

He bowed and left, and only when he was three tents away did he permit himself to notice that his hands were not entirely steady.

That was new. Thirty years of service — interrogations, executions, the quiet removals that kept kingdoms running — and his hands had never shaken. He had watched men die for less reason than Corvane had just given, and slept well after. *Stop her.* Two words. Why should those two words sit in his chest like a swallowed stone?

Because she was mine, he told himself. Because I made her. Because a craftsman does not like to see his work destroyed.

It was even true. It simply wasn't the whole truth, and Aldous had survived thirty years by never lying to himself about anything important.

* * *

He went to see the captives that evening, as he did every evening, though he told no one and justified it to himself as inspection.

They were held in a stockade of grey oak down by the water, sixty-three souls — he had counted them every night for three weeks, and every night the number was smaller by whatever the collar-instruments had broken beyond use. Tonight it was sixty-one. The fae did not weep, mostly. They sat in the trampled grass in their ragged finery and looked through the palisade at the mountains with expressions Aldous had no name for, though he suspected the name was *homesickness*, and that homesickness, for beings who might live five hundred years, was a wound that never scabbed.

One of them, an old woman with bark-grey skin and moss in her hair, looked up as he passed the fence. She always looked up. She had done it the first night, and every night since, and she never spoke, and he never spoke, and somehow this had become the most honest conversation of his life.

"You count us," she said tonight.

Her voice was dry leaves. Aldous stopped. In three weeks she had broken the silence first, and he found he could not pretend he hadn't heard.

"I count everything," he said. "It's the trade."

"And what does the counting tell you?"

"That your people are dying slowly in a ditch, and that the man who ordered it sleeps well, and that I have served him for nine years without once asking myself whether he should."

The words were out before he could weigh them. That had never happened either. He was a man who weighed everything; weighing was the whole of his discipline. But the old woman's eyes held his, and they were patient the way mountains are patient, and something in him tipped over like a cart on a bad road.

She studied him for a long moment. Then: "You are the one they call the spymaster. The taster's teacher."

"I am."

"She escaped your riders at the tollhouse. Drugged blade, meant to take her sleeping. She walked out of it in an hour." Something moved at the corner of the old woman's mouth — not quite a smile. "Your poison-masters built her well."

"They built her for a different purpose than the one she's chosen."

"All purposes are chosen late," the fae woman said. "Even yours." She turned her face back to the mountains. "Count us again tomorrow, spymaster. Count us until the number shames you. That is all the counting is *for*."

He walked away along the riverbank in the blue dusk, and the stone in his chest had grown heavier, and he could not for the life of him remember why he had ever thought it was a virtue to carry things without noticing their weight.

* * *

The escape attempt came four nights later, and Aldous knew about it before it happened, because he had arranged for it to happen.

Not the rescue itself — the girl Sef's capture had been genuine, a piece of luck Corvane's raiders hadn't earned — but the response. He had read Tamsin Thackeray for twenty years. He knew she would come. She had been raised in his school, and the first law of his school was *you do not leave your people*. He had drilled it into her alongside cipher-work and the taste of arsenic in almond cream. Loyalty as leverage. He had called it training. Watching the trap he'd laid for it now, from the ridge above Corvane's camp, he called it what it was.

He had expected her to come alone, or nearly. He had expected stealth, misdirection, the smuggler's tricks she'd learned from her father before Aldous ever found her. What he had not expected — what no model of her behavior had predicted — was the singer.

Merrin Vale walked out of the choir pavilion at the third hour past midnight wearing an inquisitor's stolen robe, crossed forty yards of open ground between two patrols that should have seen him, opened the stockade lock with a strip of wire, and put the child Sef into Tamsin Thackeray's arms while the alarm bells were still being untied. It was clean. It was beautiful. It was, Aldous thought with a sensation like ice water finding a seam in a wall, exactly the kind of impossible thing that happened when people fought for each other instead of for a plan.

And then it went wrong, because he had built the wrongness in. The second ring of guards closed. Torches bloomed along the palisade. Tamsin got the child over the stockade's blind side and down to the river path — he watched her do it, small and quick and utterly certain, the girl he'd pulled out of a kitchen at nine years old moving through the dark like water finding its level — and then the gap began to close behind her, and there was no time, and Merrin Vale turned around and walked back into the torchlight with his hands raised.

Loudly. Deliberately. Drawing every eye, every shout, every running boot, so that the small shape slipping away along the river with a child on her back went unwitnessed by anyone except the one man on the ridge who had taught her that trick too.

Aldous stood in the dark and did not raise the horn.

He had the horn. It hung at his belt. One blast and thirty riders would sweep the river path and take her, and Corvane's problem would be solved, and Aldous's thirty-year record would remain unblemished. It was the correct move. Every calculation he had ever trusted said so.

He watched the torches swallow the singer. He watched the singers' voice — that voice, the one that had been music itself — fill the night with a false trail of shouts pointing east while Tamsin went west. He watched the river path empty, and the dark take her, and the child with her.

Then he took out the horn, and blew it — late, a full minute late, long enough that any rider following the sound would find cold trail and confusion — and stood listening to the camp erupt around a prize that had already gone.

*Why?*

He asked it honestly, walking back down through the chaos he had half-made, and the honest answers came one after another like stones dropping into a well. Because she was mine. Because a craftsman does not destroy his work. Because the fae woman counts us and the number shames me. Because I have watched a man put a silver collar on the finest voice in the kingdom and call it necessity. Because she was nine years old in a kitchen and I told her loyalty was a tool and she believed me, and she has spent her whole life paying for my belief, and tonight she risked death for a child she has known two months, and I could not blow a horn against that. Because—

Because somewhere in thirty years of clean, almost-bloodless work, I stopped checking whether the work was clean, and only checked whether it was bloodless, and it was never bloodless, and I knew, and I counted everything except that.

At the edge of the torchlight, an inquisitor captain hurried toward him, breathless. "My lord — the prisoner. The singer. He gave himself up so the others could run. Shall we—"

"Put him back in the choir," Aldous said. His voice was steady. It cost him more than the horn had. "Unharmed. If a single new burn appears on him, I will know whose hand held the iron."

The captain blinked. "My lord?"

"You heard me."

He walked on, past the stockade, past the river, and stopped where the bank curved and the mountains stood black against the stars. Somewhere up there, in the passes he had mapped and the girl he had trained knew better, Tamsin Thackeray was carrying a child toward the Deepwell, toward the covenant, toward the song that would undo everything he had built.

He should feel dread. He felt instead something far more dangerous: relief, thin and cold and unmistakable, like the first thread of light under a door.

The old fae woman's voice followed him up the dark. *Count us again tomorrow, spymaster.*

Sixty-one, he thought. Tomorrow it would be sixty, or fifty-nine, unless he did something.

For the first time in thirty years, Aldous began to consider what doing something would look like — and what it would cost, and whom it would save, and whether a man could spend a lifetime building a machine and then, at the very end, refuse to be one of its parts.

Above him, faint on the wind off the mountain, so faint he might have imagined it, the ground hummed — and for half a heartbeat, the hum sounded less like a muffled bell than like a choir tuning.
