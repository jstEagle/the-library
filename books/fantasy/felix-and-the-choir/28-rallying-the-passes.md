## 28. Rallying the Passes

Brannoc had spent forty years learning which roads were worth dying on, and he'd never found one until now.

He stood at the mouth of the Kettle, the old smuggling cave three days' climb above Marrowgate's ashes, and watched Tamsin Thackeray draw lines in the soot of a hearthstone with a charred stick. She drew fast, like she was running out of time, because she was. The moon had four nights left to turn.

"Every pass," she said. "Every road, every goat track, every drainage tunnel under every tollhouse. We hold them long enough for me to get to the Deepwell and sing."

"Hold them against what?" asked a voice from the back — one of the Marrowgate survivors who'd come up the mountain with nothing but the clothes they'd fled in and the ash still in their hair.

"Against an army," Brannoc said. "Two armies, if the fae decide we're lying."

Silence. He let it sit. He'd learned that from Aldous, back when Aldous was worth learning from: let people sit inside the size of a thing before you ask them to carry it.

Then a woman near the fire — a cooper's widow, Brannoc thought, or maybe a fishwife; half of Marrowgate had been both by the end — stood up and said, "My son is dead because that duke wanted two people caught. I've got nothing left to lose but my feet, and my feet know these passes better than his soldiers ever will."

Brannoc felt something turn over in his chest, old and heavy, like a keel coming about.

"Aye," he said. "That'll do to start."

* * *

The plan was simple, because plans built for desperate people have to be.

Tamsin knew the Thornfell roads the way a spider knows its web — not as lines on a map but as living things, each with its own moods and seasons and betrayals. She split them into threads. Brannoc took the western passes, the ones he'd run since he was a boy small enough to fit through the Widow's Crack with a full pack of untaxed brandy. Sef went east with two of Wren's old wards stitched into their coat hem, carrying word to the fae-touched villages where the old rites were never quite forgotten. Merrin — no, Merrin was gone, taken, and Brannoc made himself not think about it, made himself set the grief aside like a pack too heavy to carry up scree. Later. Grief later. Work now.

Tamsin herself would go down the center roads, the dangerous ones, the ones the army already held, because she had a face nobody remembered and a body that laughed at poison, and because she refused — flatly, furiously, in the voice of someone who had decided and would not be re-decided — to ask anyone else to walk roads she wouldn't walk herself.

"You're the singer," Brannoc told her. "You should be resting your voice."

"I'm the taster," she said. "I taste first. That's the whole job."

He couldn't argue with that. He'd watched her do it a hundred times in another life, in a palace kitchen, lifting a spoon to her lips without flinching while grown men sweated. Some people were built for going first. It didn't make it easier to watch.

They scattered at dawn at the Kettle's mouth, and Brannoc clasped her forearm and said the thing he'd been chewing on all night: "Your da ran these roads with me when we were both idiots. He'd be proud past bearing."

She looked at him for a long moment with those grey eyes that gave away nothing, and then she said, "He'd say I'm doing it wrong," and almost smiled, and was gone down the trail before he could answer.

That was Tamsin. You got half a smile and the whole truth, and you were grateful for both.

* * *

The western passes were Brannoc's country, and he worked them like he'd worked them all his life, because he had.

First stop: the Drover's Rest, a waystation squatting under a rock overhang where smugglers had watered horses and lied to each other for six generations. The keeper was a one-eyed woman named Hesk who owed him money and knew it.

"We're done running loads," Brannoc told the room — eleven smugglers, three deserters from Corvane's supply train, and a shepherd who'd wandered in out of the snow and stayed because the ale was warm. "New cargo. We hold the passes. Four nights. Every soldier Corvane sends up this mountain finds a rockslide, a washed-out bridge, a wrong turn into a ravine. We don't fight fair. We don't fight much at all. We make the mountain itself slow him down."

"And after?" Hesk asked. "After the moon turns?"

"After the moon turns, either there's a song that ends a war, or there isn't, and none of us are here to worry about it." He let that land too. Then he added, gentler than he meant to: "But if it works — if it works, Hesk, the roads open. Both sides of the mountain. Real trade, real crossings, no more of us dying in ditches over casks of brandy. A whole new world to smuggle in."

That got a laugh, and laughs in a room like that were worth more than oaths. Smugglers didn't swear loyalty. They weighed odds. So Brannoc gave them odds.

By the time he walked out of the Drover's Rest, he had nine volunteers, two wagons' worth of rope and oil, and the shepherd, who turned out to know a way through the high scree that wasn't on any map, including Tamsin's.

* * *

It went like that, stop after stop, day after day, and what surprised Brannoc — what kept surprising him, like a bruise he kept pressing — was how easy the asking was.

He'd expected argument. Bargaining. The old calculus of the roads, where everything cost and everyone counted. But Marrowgate had changed the arithmetic. These were people who had watched a city burn because a duke wanted two fugitives, and they understood now what Brannoc had spent his whole life pretending not to understand: that staying out of it was also a choice, and the mountain did not care whether you chose.

At the Salt Lick caves, a band of deserters — boys mostly, farm lads who'd walked away from Corvane's levies with frostbitten feet and no plan beyond walking — listened to him in silence, and then their sergeant, a grey-whiskered man named Odo, said, "We deserted once already. Second time's easier."

At the shrine of the Roadmother, where smugglers left offerings for safe passage, Old Wren's name still opened doors even though Wren was dying in the Kettle with a wound in her side that no poultice could argue with. An ancient keeper pressed a bundle of ward-cords into Brannoc's hands and said, "For the singers. Tell her the Roadmother remembers her debts."

And at the Broken Stair, where the path climbed through a gorge so narrow two men couldn't stand shoulder to shoulder, Brannoc found a hundred villagers from the fae-side hamlets already digging a rockfall onto the lower approach, working with picks and bare hands, organized by nobody, led by a girl of perhaps fifteen who took one look at his beard and said, "Are you the dwarf who owes Tamsin Thackeray's father?"

"That's me."

"She said you'd come. She said, tell Brannoc the gorge needs timber for a barricade and he'll grumble but he'll bring it." The girl grinned. "Did you grumble?"

"I grumbled the whole way up," Brannoc admitted, and the whole gorge laughed, and he found himself laughing too, for the first time since Marrowgate burned.

* * *

On the third night he stood on a ridge above the southern passes and looked down at what they'd built, and it took his breath away.

Fires dotted the mountainside like fallen stars. Not campfires of an army — there was no drill, no order, no line — but the scattered lights of people who had chosen to be exactly where they were. Smugglers rolling boulders onto switchbacks. Deserters teaching farmers how to spoil a road with a spade and an hour's work. Fae-blooded villagers hanging Wren's ward-cords across the passes so that any soldier who crossed them would walk in circles until sunrise, cursing, lost, harmless. A cooper's widow directing traffic at a junction with the authority of a general and a ladle she'd brought from the ashes of her house for reasons she declined to explain.

Nobody commanded any of it. That was the miracle and the terror of it. Brannoc had run cargoes his whole life on trust — trust that the man ahead would signal, that the man behind would keep watch, that the whole chain would hold because every link chose to hold. This was that, scaled up past sense. A thousand links. A thousand choices, renewed every hour, in the cold, against an army.

An army was coming. He could see its fires too, down in the valley: orderly, vast, a grid of light like a city laid out on the plain, and the sound of it reached him faintly on the wind — hammers, hooves, ten thousand men clearing their throats. Corvane had learned about the passes. Of course he had. And tomorrow, or the day after, that grid of light would start moving uphill.

Brannoc spat over the edge of the ridge, an old habit, an offering to nothing in particular.

"Come on, then," he told the valley. "The mountain's ours tonight."

* * *

The message came up from the south near midnight, passed hand to hand along the chain of watchers the way contraband used to travel, whispered from fire to fire: riders under a parley flag, asking for the one called Brannoc.

He went down expecting a knife and found instead a boy in Corvane colours, barely older than the sergeant's deserters, holding out a folded paper with a shaking hand. Brannoc read it by torchlight. He read it twice.

It was written in a fine courtly hand he recognized, because he'd seen it on manifests and pardons and one memorable forged bill of lading thirty years ago. Master Aldous. It offered terms: the passes opened, the company surrendered, and every soul on this mountain pardoned — every soul but the singers. Those terms were not negotiable. Below the offer, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, almost human:

*You taught her the roads, Brannoc. You know what she is walking into. I am offering you the chance to spare her. Take it.*

Brannoc folded the paper slowly. The boy watched him, terrified, waiting to be killed for the crime of delivering mail.

"Tell your master I'll answer myself," Brannoc said. "Wait there."

He climbed back up to the ridge fires, and he gathered whoever was nearest — the widow with the ladle, Odo the deserter, the fifteen-year-old from the gorge, a dozen others drawn by the strange gravity of his face — and he read them the letter aloud, all of it, even the last part, especially the last part.

"He thinks fear will open these passes," Brannoc said. "He thinks we're a cargo that can be bought off." He held up the paper between finger and thumb, like something dead. "I've been a smuggler forty years. I know a bribe when I'm handed one. And I know what my answer is."

He dropped the letter into the nearest fire and watched Aldous's fine courtly hand curl and blacken.

"But it's not only my answer, is it?" he said. "It's yours. Every one of you can still walk down that mountain tonight and take his pardon. Nobody here would think less — well." He glanced at the widow. "I'd think less. But nobody would stop you."

The widow snorted. Odo laughed. The fifteen-year-old picked up a stone and threw it down the slope toward the distant enemy fires, which was tactically useless and emotionally perfect, and then they were all throwing stones, shouting into the dark, a hundred small defiant voices rattling down the black mountainside toward ten thousand men.

Brannoc stood in the middle of it, beard full of snow, heart full of something he didn't have a name for, and thought: *your da would be proud past bearing, Tam. Both of us.*

* * *

Near dawn, a second runner came up the southern chain, this one one of Sef's fae-village folk, half-frozen and breathless, and the news she carried emptied Brannoc's lungs.

"The Deepwell," she gasped. "There's movement at the Deepwell. Corvane's vanguard — they're not waiting for the passes. They found another way in. And they've got prisoners with them, chained, in carts — folk with pointed ears and—" her voice cracked, "—they're making them sing, master. Marching them up singing. The whole mountain can hear it."

Brannoc closed his eyes. Somewhere below, muffled by miles of stone and snow, he could just catch it on the wind if he strained: a thread of coerced melody, thin and wrong, voices bent to a purpose they were never meant for. Merrin's range was in there somewhere. He was sure of it. He'd have known that tenor in a hurricane.

He opened his eyes and turned to the fires behind him, to the smugglers and deserters and widows and children who had chosen to stand, and he raised his voice the way he'd once raised it over storm-winds on the Widow's Crack, and every head turned.

"Right, you beautiful bastards," Brannoc roared. "They've gone for the Deepwell early. Which means every pass, every track, every cursed goat path between here and that mountain's heart buys her one more minute — and minutes are the only coin that matters now. Dump the rocks. Cut the ropes. Spoil every road you ever loved. Move!"

And the mountain, all at once, began to move with him.
