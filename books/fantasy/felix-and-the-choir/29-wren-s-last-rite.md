## 29. Wren's Last Rite

Wren had been dying for three weeks, and she'd decided it was going well.

Not the dying itself — that part was tedious, the way the wound in her side had gone from hot to cold to a kind of deep, patient ache that woke her every hour before dawn. But the dying *well*, in the sense that a job done properly is done well. She had taught Tamsin the bridge-line until the girl could hum it in her sleep. She had drawn the covenant's structure in charcoal on every flat surface of the cave, and some of the curved ones. She had made Brannoc cry twice, which she counted as a personal triumph over a man who claimed to be carved from granite.

What remained was the rite.

She'd known since the tollhouse, when the blade went in and came out wrong, that this was how it would end. Hedge-witches know their own bodies the way tasters know poison: intimately, without illusion. The steel had nicked something that didn't forgive nicks, and no poultice in Thornfell would knit it. She could have told them straight away. Instead she'd spent three weeks teaching, because a dying woman's lessons land deeper than a living one's, and she was not above using that.

Now the moon was four nights from turning, and there was one thing left to do.

She woke before dawn, as she always did now, and lay listening to the cave breathe. Sef's small snores from the alcove. Brannoc's deeper rumble by the fire pit, banked to embers. Somewhere above, wind moving through the pines like a slow exhale. And beneath all of it — fainter each day as her strength drained, but still there if she pressed her ear to the stone — the Choir. Half-alive. Waiting.

*Hurry up, old woman,* the mountain seemed to say. *We haven't got forever.*

"We've got exactly one moon's turn," Wren muttered, "and I intend to spend mine usefully."

She dressed slowly. That was the worst of it now, the slowness. Her hands still worked but they worked at the speed of an old woman's hands, and she had never in eighty-one years thought of herself as old. Old was other people. Old was a category she'd cheerfully mocked at every funeral she'd ever attended, of which there had been many, most of them her own doing in one way or another.

The rite required preparation. She'd gathered what she needed over the past week under cover of ordinary herb-work, because if she'd told them what she was gathering for, Brannoc would have carried her down the mountain to a healer against her will, and Merrin would have played sad songs at her, and neither of those things was going to change the outcome. They'd only have cost her time.

Time was the whole coin she had left. She meant to spend every last piece of it.

* * *

Tamsin found her at the cave mouth an hour past dawn, grinding ashroot into paste.

"You're up early," Tamsin said. "Even for you."

"I'm always up early. You're all up late. The young sleep like the dead." She smiled at her own joke. It wasn't a very good joke. "Sit down, girl. I want to talk to you about tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?"

"The moon turns in four nights. But we walk to the Deepwell tomorrow. It's a day's climb through the verge, and you'll want a night on the threshold to steady yourselves before you sing." She tapped the mortar. "Which means today is the last day I'm useful, and I intend to be extremely useful."

Tamsin sat. She did that now — sat when Wren told her to, listened when Wren talked — and Wren had watched it happen over three weeks, the slow softening of a woman who had spent her whole life being nobody's student, nobody's daughter, nobody's anything. It was the best work Wren had ever done, and she'd once raised a barn roof single-handed during a storm, so the competition was stiff.

"The rite," Tamsin said.

So she'd guessed. Of course she'd guessed. The girl tasted truth the way she tasted arsenic — instantly, and without mercy.

"You knew," Wren said.

"I suspected. You stopped eating meat two weeks ago. You only do that when you're saving your strength for something." Tamsin's voice was level, which cost her; Wren could see what it cost. "I hoped I was wrong."

"You weren't wrong. Sit still and let me finish." She kept grinding. The rhythm helped. "Here's what happens. The Deepwell isn't a door, whatever the ballads say. It's a wound. When the covenant broke, the wound started closing — the mountain knitting itself shut around the place where human song and fae song used to meet. Corvane means to pry it open with his counter-song, and his way leaves scar tissue. My way keeps it open long enough for the true Choir to pass through clean."

"And your way costs—"

"My way costs what it costs." She looked up, met the girl's eyes. Grey eyes, sharp as a taster's tongue. "Don't you dare make me say it twice."

Tamsin said nothing. Her jaw worked once. Then, in the voice of a woman who had learned, late and painfully, to ask for things: "Is there another way? Any other way? I'll take ugly. I'll take desperate. I've lived my whole life on ugly and desperate."

"There is no other way," Wren said gently. "There's only this way, done well or done badly. Done badly, I die anyway and the Well closes and you sing into solid rock while Corvane's army eats the free cities. Done well—" she spread her hands, floury with ashroot, "—done well, I die exactly the same amount, and you get your door."

"That's not fair."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's not. Fairness is for people with more time." She reached out and took Tamsin's hand — the poisoned blood, the engineered weapon, the girl who flinched from touch and had stopped flinching from hers somewhere around week two. "Listen to me. I'm eighty-one years old. I buried a husband, two children, and a man I probably shouldn't mention to Brannoc. I've been waiting forty years for someone to come ask me about the old rites, and when they finally did, it was you — a runaway taster with a murderer's map and a singer who can't sing. If I'd designed my own death, I couldn't have improved on this one. Dying for something that works?" She squeezed the hand. "That's the luxury, girl. Most people don't get it."

Tamsin's eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them. Progress.

"What do you need from us?" she asked finally.

"Now you're asking the right question." Wren released her hand and stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Go wake the others. We've got a day's work and I mean to spend it loudly."

* * *

They argued, of course. She'd budgeted an hour for it.

Brannoc led with volume. "There are healers in Marrowgate—"

"Marrowgate burned," Wren said. "You watched it burn. Next."

"Sef knows fae craft, maybe—"

"Fae craft doesn't mend mortal steel wounds, and Sef's craft is for the Well, not for me. Next."

Merrin tried the quiet approach, which she respected more: he simply sat beside her and said, "Please," and nothing else, and for a moment she almost wavered, because his voice even broken was a lovely instrument and he aimed it at her like a man aiming his last arrow.

"Sing at the Well instead," she told him. "Save the please for the stone. It'll listen better than I do."

It was Sef who ended it, the way children end things adults cannot — by cutting straight through. The child climbed into Wren's lap, which hurt, and looked up at her with those too-old fae eyes, and said:

"Will it hurt?"

"Some," Wren admitted. "Less than you'd think. The rite spends me all at once, like emptying a purse. After that it's mostly quiet."

"Will you hear us? After? When we sing?"

Wren considered lying, decided against it — the child heard lies the way Tamsin tasted them. "I'll be in the song, little one. That's what the rite does. It doesn't just hold the door. It puts a voice in the doorway. Mine." She tapped Sef's chest, right over the heart. "When you sing the fae line, listen underneath it. There'll be an old woman humming along, badly, and off-key, and you'll know I made it."

Sef nodded, solemn as a judge, and slid off her lap, and that was that. Brannoc turned away and did something loud with firewood. Merrin bowed his head. And Tamsin — Tamsin looked at Wren across the fire and gave her a single nod, taster to witch, professional to professional: *understood, terms accepted.*

Wren had raised difficult children before. None of them had ever made her this proud.

* * *

The rite took place at dusk, at the cave mouth, facing the mountain.

She'd chosen the spot deliberately. From here you could see down the valley to where Marrowgate's smoke still smudged the horizon, and up to where Thornfell's peaks caught the last red light, and between those two things — the burning city below, the singing mountain above — was everything the fight was actually about. She wanted to die looking at both.

Brannoc built the fire ring without being asked, laying the stones in the old pattern, seven and seven. He remembered. She'd shown him once, twenty years ago, in a tavern in Marrowgate, drunk on plum brandy, and he'd carried it all this way like a coin in his pocket. Dwarves were like that. Sentimental hoarders, the lot of them.

Merrin brought her water. Sef brought her the ashroot paste and helped her paint the signs — on her own wrists, her throat, her sternum — small cool hands steadying her shaking ones. Tamsin stood at her shoulder and asked, quietly, "What do you need me to do?"

"Nothing," Wren said. "That's the hardest part. This one's mine alone. Your part starts tomorrow, at the Well, and it's bigger than either of us." She took a breath. It scraped. Everything scraped now. "But I'll tell you what I need after. I need you to remember me accurately. Not as a saint. I was a hedge-witch and a nuisance and I stole a horse in '61 and never gave it back. Remember me like that. Saints are useless to the living. Nuisances get things done."

"I can do that," Tamsin said. "It's the easiest lie I'll ever tell."

"It's not a lie if it's true, girl. First lesson. Honestly, have you learned nothing?"

And then, because the sun was touching the peaks and the light was right and she had spent enough of her remaining hours on talk, Wren stepped into the fire ring and began.

The rite was older than the kingdoms. Older than the breaking. It had been sung at the founding of the covenant by women whose names the mountains kept and men forgot, and Wren had learned it at her grandmother's knee in exchange for a promise never to use it lightly. She had kept that promise for sixty years. She intended to keep it perfectly, once, at the end.

She sang the opening line, and the mountain answered.

Not with sound — with attention. She felt it turn toward her the way a great animal turns, vast and slow and curious, the half-dead Choir stirring in its sleep beneath miles of stone. Her voice cracked on the second phrase and she let it crack; the rite didn't want beauty, it wanted truth, and her voice was truer broken than whole. She sang the names of the thresholds. She sang the debt of the doorway. She sang the old bargain — *a voice for a voice, a life for a life, held open for the singers to come* — and with each line she felt herself growing lighter, as though the song were drawing her out through her throat like thread from a spool.

The company knelt at the ring's edge. She hadn't told them to. Brannoc's head was bowed, his big hands flat on the stone. Merrin's lips moved silently, following the structure, learning it even now, even tonight — the man could not stop learning music, and she loved him for it. Sef watched her with wide unblinking eyes, and Tamsin —

Tamsin stood. Wouldn't kneel. Stood at the ring's edge with her fists at her sides and her grey eyes fixed on Wren's face, taking it in, memorizing it, refusing to look away, and Wren thought: *yes. That one. That's the one I'd have chosen, if choosing had been mine.*

The third verse was the spending. She felt it go — warmth first, a false and golden warmth flooding out from her heart to her fingertips, and then the cold behind it, honest cold, the body's ledger closing. Her knees buckled. Brannoc moved to catch her and she snapped, mid-verse, "Don't you *dare* break the circle," and he froze, and she finished the verse kneeling, and the fourth verse she sang mostly lying down, propped against Sef's shoulder, her voice gone to a whisper that the mountain leaned down to hear.

The last line was a promise, and promises must be spoken clear. She gathered what was left — one breath, one good breath, she'd saved it, she'd been saving it all day — and she gave it everything:

*"Hold, door. Hold for the singers. Hold for the child. Hold for the taster. Hold until the song is sung."*

The mountain said: *held.*

She felt it take. Felt the rite sink down through the stone like a root, down and down to the Deepwell itself, and there — she felt it with the last of her senses, the witch-sense going out like a lamp — the wound in the world stop closing. Stop. Hold. A doorway braced open by one stubborn old woman's entire life, spent in a single evening, the best purchase she'd ever made.

Then the senses went, one by one, like windows darkening. Smell first — pine smoke, ashroot, the iron tang of her own blood, gone. Then sight, the sunset fading to grey to nothing, though she could still feel the last warmth of it on her face, and that was kind. Hearing lasted longest. Of course it did. She was a hedge-witch; hearing was the whole trade.

She heard Sef crying, small and fierce, trying not to. She heard Brannoc's breath hitching in a chest built like a bellows. She heard Merrin begin, very softly, to hum — not a lament, she noticed; a working tune, one of the covenant's lines, already turning grief into material, because that was what singers did, and bless him for it.

And she heard Tamsin lean close, and take her hand, and say — clearly, steadily, in the voice of a woman who had finally stopped being afraid of wanting things:

"We'll finish it. I swear it on my tongue. You'll hear every note."

"Oh, I know," Wren whispered. The words cost almost nothing now; she was nearly all spent, and what remained was light. "I know, girl. I'll be listening from underneath. Try to stay in tune. It's the least—"

The sentence didn't end so much as dissolve. The last window closed. And what was on the other side of it was not darkness, exactly. It was more like the moment before dawn, when the whole sky holds its breath — and beneath it, faint and patient and half-alive, a harmony waiting for its missing parts.

*Oh,* she thought, with the last flicker of the old mischief. *So that's what it sounds like from in here.*

*Well then. Let's see if they can sing.*

* * *

They buried her at dawn where she'd asked: at the cave mouth, facing the mountain, wrapped in her own cloak with a sprig of ashroot in her folded hands. Brannoc dug the grave himself and let no one help, and when it was done he stood over it a long time and said, gruffly, to no one in particular: "Plum brandy. In '61. She never told me about any horse." And he laughed, wet and rough, and wiped his face with the back of his hand, and that was the eulogy, and it was perfect.

Merrin sang. Not a lament — a working tune, the covenant's second line, the one she'd drilled into him across a hundred cold evenings. His voice cracked in the middle and he let it crack, because Wren had taught him that too, in her way: truth over beauty, always, always.

Sef laid a stone from the fire ring on the grave, seven-and-seven pattern complete.

And Tamsin stood last, longest, saying nothing at all — just standing the way Wren had stood at the ring's edge, fists at her sides, refusing to look away, memorizing. When she finally spoke, it was one sentence, quiet enough that only the grave heard it:

"Nuisance. Saint. Both. I'll remember you accurately."

Then she turned to the others, and the grief was still on her face, but underneath it — visible now, unmistakable, banked like Brannoc's coals — was something harder and brighter. Purpose, transmuted clean out of mourning, the way Wren had taught her grief could be if you refused to waste it.

"The moon turns in three nights," Tamsin said. "The door is held. Wren bought us our chance, and I don't intend to waste a copper of it." She shouldered her pack. "We leave for the Deepwell within the hour. Singers' pace — we arrive rested, fed, and ready."

Brannoc nodded and fell in. Sef took Tamsin's hand without asking, the way the child had learned to. Merrin lingered one moment at the grave, touched two fingers to his lips and pressed them to the turned earth — an old court gesture, a promise given to a patron — and then followed.

Behind them, high on the mountain, the wind moved through the pines like a slow exhale.

And far below, deeper than roots, deeper than memory, at the bottom of a wound in the world, an old woman's voice settled into the doorway — humming, badly, and off-key, and waiting for the choir to come.
