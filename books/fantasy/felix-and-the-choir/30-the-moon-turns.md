## 30. The Moon Turns

The moon began to turn at dusk, and Tamsin watched it happen from the mouth of the Deepwell with a cup of cold tea going untouched in her hands.

It was a thin moon, a paring of bone against a sky the color of a bruise, and it hung low over the western shoulder of Thornfell as if it had come to look down into the mountain's open throat along with everyone else. Below her, far below, the valley floors were filling with firelight. She could see the free cities' host strung along the northern passes — cookfires and watch-fires and the small moving lights of pickets, an army that wasn't really an army at all but smugglers and shepherds and deserters holding ground they'd sworn to hold for one moon's turn. And beyond them, south, where the land flattened toward Marrowgate's ashes: Corvane. His campfires were denser, whiter, laid out in the neat geometries of a man who believed order was the same thing as right. Between the two hosts the mountain itself rose black and indifferent, wearing its snow like a shroud it hadn't decided to put on yet.

And above — above, spilling down the high verges in rivers of pale light that were not torchlight — came the Ash Queen.

Tamsin had seen fae before. She'd seen Sef every day for months; she'd walked the twilight verge until her eyes learned its rules. But she had never seen the host of the court, and nothing had prepared her for the silence of it. Thousands upon thousands descending the mountain's hidden faces without one footfall, without one jingle of harness, their banners moving like smoke underwater. They did not camp. They arranged themselves on the high slopes in ranks that caught the moonlight and gave it back wrong, and they waited, the way winter waits.

"They're watching," Sef said quietly. The child stood at her left hand, wrapped in three cloaks because fae-blood ran cold and Wren's rite had cost them all warmth. "All of them. Every eye in the court."

"I know."

"They'll see if we fail."

"I know that too." Tamsin set down the tea. Her hands were steady. She'd trained them to be steady through worse than this — through cups that might kill her, through dishes meant for kings that she swallowed first so that someone else would live to eat. Steady hands were the one inheritance Aldous had given her that she intended to keep. "Sef. Are you afraid?"

The child considered it honestly, the way Sef considered everything, turning the question over like a stone found in a stream. "Yes. But it's a good fear. It's the kind you get when something matters." A pause. "Wren said fear is just your body knowing a moment is important."

"Wren said a lot of things." Tamsin's voice caught on the name and she let it, because hiding grief from Sef had never once worked. "I wish she were here."

"She is here." Sef touched the stone of the Deepwell's rim, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "Can't you feel it? She spent herself keeping it open. The whole well is full of her."

Tamsin reached out and laid her palm flat against the stone.

She felt it. Of course she felt it — her hearing had been remade at the covenant's reforging-in-progress, attuned to both realms whether she wanted it or not, and the Deepwell was no longer just a shaft of rock going down into dark. It *sang*. Low and vast, a note held beneath hearing, the mountain's own voice sustained by an old woman's last strength, fraying now at the edges like a rope worn thin. Wren had bought them this night with everything she had. Somewhere below, in the dark of the well, the rite was still burning, and it was guttering.

"How long?" Tamsin asked the stone.

The answer came back not in words but in pressure, in the way the note trembled. Not long. Hours, perhaps. Less.

* * *

Merrin should have been there.

That was the thought that kept circling back, a hawk over a field. Merrin should have been standing at her right hand the way Sef stood at her left, and instead he was somewhere in Corvane's white-lit camp below, caged or coerced or worse, his tenor — the finest mortal voice in a generation, the voice the whole plan depended on — bent to sing a counter-song against everything he loved. Brannoc had gone down after him two nights ago with six of the steadiest smugglers, and had come back at dawn gray-faced and empty-handed, with a report Tamsin still couldn't make sit straight in her chest.

"He knew we'd come," Brannoc had said, hunched by the fire, his beard crusted with ice. "Aldous had the cage moved before our people even reached the perimeter. Left a message, too. For you." He'd handed over a slip of paper, and Tamsin had read it by firelight with her taster's tongue gone dry as ash:

*You taught me to read a table, girl. I'm reading this one. Come alone, or don't come, and I'll spend the singer however Corvane pleases.*

Alone. Always alone. That had been the shape of her whole life before the company — alone in the kitchens, alone in the garret, alone with her careful tongue and her careful lies — and Aldous knew it, because Aldous had built it. He was offering her the oldest shape she knew and calling it safety.

She had burned the note. But the shape of it stayed.

Now, as the moon thinned overhead and the Deepwell hummed its failing note beneath her feet, Tamsin made herself say it out loud, because Sef deserved truth and because saying things aloud had always been how she kept from drowning.

"The song needs three voices. Mortal, fae, bridge. You're the fae line. I'm the bridge — my blood, my voice, whatever Wren built me into." She looked west, down toward the distant white geometry of Corvane's camp. "And the mortal line is down there, in a cage, being used against us."

"Then we go get him," Sef said, simply, the way children solve problems adults have already given up on.

"It's a trap."

"Yes," Sef agreed. "But the moon doesn't care. It turns whether we're ready or not." The child looked up at the sky, at that thin paring of bone, and added, with terrible gentleness: "How much time do we have?"

Tamsin did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all day. The moon would complete its turn near midnight — Wren's rite would fail shortly after, and the Deepwell would close, and the chance would be gone for a generation, maybe forever. Corvane's counter-choir would sing at the same hour; she'd heard the rehearsals carried up the wind, hundreds of stolen voices droning a corrupted harmony that made her teeth ache. To reach the camp, find Merrin, and return before midnight was impossible. Even for her. Even with every smuggler's pass in Thornfell folded inside her memory like letters in a drawer.

Unless.

Her eyes went to the verge — the shimmering boundary where the fae realm bled through, visible now from the Deepwell's mouth as a faint silvering of the air along the high slopes. The fae paths didn't obey distance. Wren had said so. The Ash Queen's host had descended those paths in an afternoon what should have taken a week.

The Ash Queen's host was right there.

"You've gone quiet," Sef observed. "You go quiet like Brannoc does when he's about to do something expensive."

"I need to ask the Queen for something," Tamsin said slowly. "And she's going to hate it."

* * *

They came down from the Deepwell together, Tamsin and Sef, walking out onto the slope where the fae ranks stood like a forest of frost, and the host parted for them without being asked — which was either courtesy or contempt, and Tamsin suspected she knew which. The land recoiled from her steps; it always did, the grass graying where her boots fell, the air pulling back like a hand withdrawn. Her blood, brewed poisonous before she could walk. The living weapon, walking willingly into the court that had every reason to let her die.

The Ash Queen waited at the center, on a throne of black ice that had not been there a moment before, and her face was beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful.

"You stand in my host," the Queen said, "on the night of the testing. Speak."

"The third voice is taken," Tamsin said. No preamble; the Queen despised preamble. "Corvane holds our singer. Without him the Choir fails tonight, your bargain dies, and Corvane's counter-song takes the Deepwell. You know this. You can see it as clearly as I can."

"I can." The Queen's eyes were winter. "And?"

"Your paths don't obey distance. Give us the roads. Let me go down and bring him back before the moon completes its turn."

Silence, long enough that Tamsin heard her own heart, heard Sef's quick breathing, heard — beneath everything — the Deepwell's sustaining note beginning, very faintly, to fray.

"You ask my host to lend its roads," the Queen said at last, "to the creature whose blood kills my people. You ask me to arm the weapon made to end us, and point it at the man who would use it." Something moved behind the winter of her face — not softness, never softness, but a kind of terrible attention. "Why would I do this?"

"Because I'm not asking for myself." Tamsin took one step closer, and the ground grayed beneath her, and she let it. "I'm asking for the same thing you are. The Choir restored. One mortal voice, carried back up your roads in time to sing it. That's all. If I fail, you lose nothing you weren't already losing. If I succeed—" she spread her empty hands, "—you get your covenant back, and I stand at the bridge between our worlds for the rest of my life, belonging fully to neither. You know that's the price. I accepted it before you ever offered it."

The Queen regarded her for a long moment. Then her gaze shifted, fractionally, to Sef.

"And the child vouches for you?"

"With my life," Sef said, without hesitation, and the words went through Tamsin like a blade — that a child would spend themselves so easily on her account, that they'd learned sacrifice from watching adults too closely.

"So be it." The Queen rose, and the whole host breathed, one vast motion like wind through a frozen forest. "One road, opened once. Two travelers. If you are not at the Deepwell when the moon completes its turn, the road closes behind you and the bargain is void, and my host will withdraw and leave your kind to Corvane's mercy." Her lips curved, without warmth. "Which is none."

"Understood."

"One more thing, taster." The Queen descended one step from the ice throne, close enough that Tamsin smelled cold iron and old snow. "Your mentor waits for you at that camp. The man who brewed you. My seers have watched him for a generation, and I will tell you what they see, freely, because it amuses me: he is already cracking. Certainty is a vessel, and his has a flaw running through it. When you meet him tonight — and you will meet him — remember that cracked vessels can be filled with anything." She straightened. "Even doubt."

Then she raised one hand, and the air beside them tore open like silk, silver light spilling through, and a road that was not a road lay revealed, winding down through the world's skin toward the fires of Corvane's camp.

"Go," said the Ash Queen. "The moon is turning."

* * *

The fae road was like walking through the memory of distance.

One step covered a hundred yards; another, a mile. The world smeared past in ribbons — trees, scree, starlight, the white sprawl of the enemy camp swelling from a scatter of coins to a city in the space of twenty strides. Sef gripped Tamsin's hand hard enough to hurt, and Tamsin fixed her eyes ahead and did her work, because this too was a kind of tasting: reading the landscape as it blurred, marking the layout of the camp, the stockades, the choir-tent at its center glowing wrong, lit from within by something that wasn't fire.

They stepped out of the road into shadow behind a supply wagons' park, and the ordinary world snapped back around them — mud, horse-smell, the drone of the counter-choir rehearsal rolling over the stockade walls like a tide of spoiled honey. Somewhere in that tent, stolen voices sang Corvane's theft. Somewhere among them, Merrin.

"Perimeter's thinner here," Tamsin murmured, all business, because feeling anything would come later or never. "Supply road, the one Brannoc mapped. We go in as quartermasters' runners. Nobody looks at a runner's face."

They moved. Through gaps in the pickets, past sentries warming their hands, past wagons stacked with grain and, on one flatbed under canvas, something that made Tamsin's stomach clench: instruments of black iron, long and jointed, designed to shatter song. Dozens of them, being oiled by lamplight for midnight.

The choir-tent squatted at the camp's heart like a tumor. Around it, cages — actual cages, wagon-built, and in the nearest one, huddled shapes with the too-graceful stillness of captured fae. And in the second cage, chained to the frame, sitting upright with his ruined dignity gathered around him like a cloak—

"Merrin," Tamsin breathed.

He looked terrible. His wrists were raw; his voice, when he saw her and shaped her name without sound, was surely worse. But his eyes found hers through the dark with the same clarity that had once cut through a dockside tavern's noise, and he shook his head — sharply, twice — *trap, trap* — and then jerked his chin toward the tent.

Too late. Lanterns bloomed along the stockade line, unhurried, deliberate, and out of the light stepped Master Aldous, spymaster of the crown, the man who had taught her every trick she knew, holding an unlit pipe and looking very tired.

"Tamsin." His voice was gentle, and that was worst of all. "Right on time. The Queen's roads — yes, I thought she might lend them. She always did love a wager." He sighed, and gestured, and soldiers closed in around them with practiced calm, no blades drawn, because they wanted her alive and everyone present knew it. "Come and sit by my fire, girl. We have until midnight, and I find I want to talk."

"I have nothing to say to you."

"No," Aldous agreed mildly. "But I have something to say to you, and you're going to listen, because that's what I trained you to do." He turned and began walking toward a firelit hollow at the camp's edge, and the soldiers' ring tightened around Tamsin and Sef like a closing hand, and above the mountains the moon slid, thin and white and merciless, toward the top of the sky.

Overhead, the Deepwell's distant note — the note only she could hear — wavered, and dimmed, and held.

Barely.
