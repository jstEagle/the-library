## 31. Aldous Chooses

Aldous Vane had spent forty years learning to read a room by its silences, and the silence at the mouth of the Deepwell was the loudest he had ever heard.

It was not empty silence. It was the silence of a held breath — the mountain itself waiting, the way a court waits before a verdict, the way a kitchen waits after the taster lifts the spoon. Below him, the great shaft of the Deepwell dropped away into blue-dark, its walls carved with a thousand years of notation, and the notation was *moving*, faint lines of light crawling along the grooves like worms waking in spring. Above him, the moon hung one hand's width from full. One night. The Queen had given them one night, and this was it.

And between him and the girl — between him and everything — stood thirty paces of black stone and thirty years of his own decisions.

He had come up the pilgrim stair alone. That had been deliberate. Corvane's vanguard was two hours behind him at the tollhouse, and the corrupted choir was being marched up the eastern switchbacks with their throats wrapped in iron gauze, and none of that mattered to Aldous just now. What mattered was that he had read the reports, run the numbers, done the cold arithmetic he was famous for, and the arithmetic said that everything Corvane had built would come apart in the next hour unless one specific girl failed to sing.

So he had climbed ahead of the army, alone, because there were things a man should look at with his own eyes before he ended them.

She was already there.

Of course she was. Tamsin Thackeray had never once in her life been where Aldous expected and always been exactly where she needed to be. She stood at the well's rim with the child Sef beside her — the little fae-blooded wraith, hands pressed flat to the stone as though listening for a pulse in it — and behind them, laid out on a bier of pine branches, was Old Wren. The hedge-witch's face was already the face of the dead, or nearly. Aldous had seen that face before, on agents who had spent their last strength buying an hour for someone else.

Tamsin turned when his boot scraped the stone. She did not reach for a weapon. That was the first thing he noticed, and it cut him worse than a blade would have. She had decided, somewhere on the road, that he was not worth a weapon.

"You came yourself," she said. Her voice was hoarse. She had been singing — rehearsing, he guessed, though the mortal line of the covenant needed three voices and she had only the child. "No guards. No inquisitors. Just you."

"You'd have killed the guards."

"I'd have tried."

"And the inquisitors would have gotten in the way of what I came to say." He walked forward, slowly, hands open at his sides, an old habit from negotiating with men who shot first. The stone under his feet hummed faintly. He had never in his life stood on ground that hummed. "You look terrible, Tam."

"You look old."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound that echoed down the well and came back changed, harmonized with something, as though the mountain had opinions about laughter. "Fair. Both fair."

Sef had gone very still, watching him with those eyes that were a shade too large and a shade too bright. Aldous had read every file on the child twice. Runaway. Fae-blooded. The linchpin of Corvane's whole design — the pure voice that would let the Duke's counter-song take root in the covenant's bones. The inquisitors had orders to take the child alive and unmarked. The orders for Tamsin had been different, once, and then had changed, and then had changed again, and Aldous had signed each version without letting himself read the words too carefully.

That was the trick of it, he thought now, looking at her. You never read the words too carefully. You taught yourself to skim. Forty years of skimming, and here was where all those unread words had been piling up, waiting for him at the bottom of a hole in a mountain.

"You know why I'm here," he said.

"I know why you're *supposed* to be here."

"Then let's not insult each other." He stopped ten paces from her, close enough to see the poison-scars on her forearms where the sleeves had worn thin, the fine white lattice of a childhood spent being brewed against. Gods, she'd been six when the first regimen started. He'd carried her to the taster's chair himself. She'd cried and asked if she'd done something wrong, and he'd told her she was being made strong.

He had believed it, too. That was the unforgivable part. He had *believed* it.

"Corvane's choir reaches the well in two hours," he said. "The army holds the passes — or thinks it does; your smuggler friends are making that expensive for him. The Duke's counter-song needs the child's voice and the well's resonance and a moon near full. You have none of the things you need. You have a dead witch, a stolen boat's worth of ragtag friends scattered down the mountain, and no third voice. I've read Wren's notes. Three voices, Tam. Mortal, fae, bridge. You're the bridge. The child is the fae line. And the mortal line—" He spread his hands. "The mortal line is standing in front of you wearing a spymaster's coat."

Her eyes narrowed. "You're offering to defect."

"I'm offering to *sing*."

The silence that followed was long enough that he heard the Deepwell breathing — a slow, vast intake from far below, like the mountain dreaming. Sef's head came up sharply. The child had heard something in it. Of course the child had.

"That's not possible," Tamsin said. "You don't have the voice. You told me once you couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. You said—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—you said that's why you needed tasters and singers and spies, because you were a man with no music in him."

"I said that," Aldous agreed. "I said a great many things."

He reached into his coat — slowly, showing her his hand the whole way, because trust was a currency he had bankrupted with this girl and he knew it — and drew out a folded square of vellum, soft as cloth with handling. He did not give it to her. Not yet.

"Your father sang," he said.

Tamsin went white.

"Not well," Aldous went on, because the only mercy left to him now was the truth, delivered whole and uncut, and he intended to spend every coin of it. "Not like your Merrin Vale. But he sang. Dock songs, mostly. Smuggler's rounds. He'd sing the passes to me as we walked — that's how I learned the Thornfell routes, Tam, did you think I walked them all myself? I learned them from a man who loved them, humming, and I wrote them down, and thirty years later I sold them to the Duke's cartographers so his army could march on the free cities." He watched the words land, one by one, like stones dropped down a well. "He sang to me the night we took you in. Held you on his knee and sang a round — three parts, you only need two people for a three-part round if you stagger it right. And I sat there with my wine and my ledger and I thought: this man is a liability, and this child is an asset, and I can use both."

"Aldous—"

"Let me finish. I've been not-finishing for thirty years and I find I've run out of time for it." The vellum trembled slightly in his hand. He steadied it. He had not survived this long by having shaking hands. "Your father didn't die of fever. He found out what the taster's program was. What *you* were. He came to me furious — I'd never seen him furious — and he said he'd go to the King, and I believed him, because he was the only honest man I ever employed. And honest men are the most dangerous things in a palace." He swallowed. "The fever was mine. I brewed it. I used the same apothecary who brewed your immunity, because I knew his work, and I knew it would look like fever, and I signed the order myself and I skimmed past my own name on it, the way I'd taught myself to do."

The Deepwell hummed. Somewhere below, the notation-light was brightening, crawling faster, as though the mountain could taste what was coming.

Tamsin had not moved. Her face had gone very still — the stillness he had trained into her, the taster's stillness, the face you wore while your tongue decided whether you were about to die. He had put that face on her when she was eight years old. He was looking at his own work.

"You're telling me this," she said at last, each word placed like a coin on a counter, "because you think it will make me trust you."

"No. I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it before you decide whether to let me die for you. Those are different calculations. I've spent my life confusing them and I won't do it one last time." He held out the vellum. "This is the mortal line. Wren wrote it down — she sent it to me, actually, six weeks ago, the sly old bird, before you ever reached her. She knew I'd been at the first covenant's breaking. She thought—" his mouth twisted, "—she thought there might still be something in me that remembered how to be a man. The mortal line isn't hard, Tam. That's the point of it. It was written to be sung by anyone. Farmers. Soldiers. A smuggler with a bad voice and a worse heart. It's not a performance. It's a *promise*, set to a tune simple enough that a promise-keeper can hold it."

"And if I take it," Tamsin said, "you walk away. That's the offer. You betray Corvane, hand me the song, and vanish, and I never have to look at you again."

"I suspect," Aldous said, "that you'd prefer I vanish *before* handing over the song. But no. That's not the offer. You've misread me one final time, and I take full credit for teaching you to."

He stepped to the well's rim.

The drop was enormous. The blue-dark below was not entirely dark anymore — the notation had begun to glow in earnest now, lines of light climbing the shaft like ivy, and from somewhere beneath them came a sound that was almost sound: the Choir's ghost, the residue of a thousand years of singing, still echoing at the bottom of the world the way heat lingers in a hearthstone after the fire dies.

"Corvane's choir arrives in two hours," he said. "When it does, the Duke's counter-song will try to take the well the way a thief takes a house — by forcing the locks. The child's stolen voice is his key. Your voice is the bridge. But a bridge needs an anchor, Tam, and the anchor is the mortal line, sung by a mortal throat, *freely given*. That's the whole architecture of the covenant. It was never magic. It was consent, made musical." He looked back at her. "Corvane's singers are coerced. Iron gauze on their throats, poison in their water, their families in his cells. Coerced song can break a lock. It cannot *make* one. He will fail tonight — unless he fails *at* you, unless his counter-song arrives while your covenant is still half-built and shatters it in the cradle. You need time. Opening bars. A minute, perhaps two, of the mortal line held alone against everything he can throw at it."

"And you'll sing it."

"I'll sing it."

"You can't. You told me—"

"I lied about a great many things." He said it lightly, and then stopped, because it wasn't light. He set the vellum down on the stone between them, weighting it with a pebble, an old courier's habit. "Here is the truth of my voice, since we're spending truth tonight. I could always sing. I sang as a boy — choir school, before the palace took me. I was good. I was good at everything, that was the problem; a boy who is good at everything gets collected by powerful men and turned into a tool, and the first thing they file off is the parts of you that want things. So I told the world I had no music. It was the only room in my house I kept locked. Even from myself." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, worn smooth as a river stone. "Wren knew. That's why she sent it to me. The old witch could smell a locked room through a mountain."

Tamsin looked at the vellum. Then at Wren's body on its bier. Then at Sef, who was watching Aldous with an expression no child should own — the expression of someone who can hear the second voice beneath the first, and was hearing, beneath the spymaster's flat careful words, something enormous and breaking.

"You'll die," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. "The counter-song. If you hold the line alone against it, with no bridge beneath you, it will—"

"Tear the mortal line out of me, yes. Wren's notes are quite specific. I read them the way I should have read everything, all the way through, for once." He straightened his coat. Forty years of coats, and this was the last one. "I've arranged my affairs. There's not much. A house in Marrowgate I never lived in — it's yours, or the conservatory's, or whoever needs walls. And a letter for the King, which says things I should have said twenty years ago, and which will accomplish nothing, and which I wrote anyway."

"Aldous." Her voice cracked on the name, and he heard, beneath it, all the other names she wasn't saying. *Mentor. Jailor. Father-thing. Murderer.* "I don't forgive you."

"I know." And gods, it was a relief, to hear it said plainly, no court mercy, no absolution bought cheap. "Don't. Forgiveness is for people who can make amends. I can only make a down payment." He looked at the child. "Sef. When I sing, you'll hear the line clearly — the fae blood hears true. If I falter, don't wait for me. The bridge goes in whether the anchor holds or not. Do you understand?"

Sef nodded, solemn as a small priest. Then the child did something Aldous had not expected and would carry with him wherever he was going: crossed the stone, took his hand, and held it, briefly, the way you'd steady a man on a high ledge.

"You're scared," Sef said.

"Terrified," Aldous said. "I've been terrified since 1973—" no. He caught himself. "Since I was younger than you. It's an old friend. We're going to walk together for a few minutes, and then it can have the rest."

Tamsin was crying. She did it silently, the trained way, tears without sound, and he hated that he had taught her that too. She picked up the vellum. She read it — all the way through, he noted; she'd always been the one who read things all the way through — and her lips moved over the notes, and her voice, untrained, rough, *wanting*, tried the first phrase in a whisper.

The Deepwell answered her.

The light in the shaft flared. The hum became a chord. Far below, something that had been waiting a generation turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.

"That's it," Aldous said softly. "That's it, Tam. It knows you. Now — Corvane's vanguard will be at the stair in minutes; I heard the horns at the tollhouse. When his counter-song comes up that shaft, it will come like a flood. You and the child go down to the second gallery — Wren's notes show it, the singing ledge — and you build the bridge. I stand here, at the mouth, and I hold the door." He took his place at the rim, facing down into the light, and rolled his shoulders like a man preparing for a long day's work. "One thing more. When this is over — if you ever tell this story, and you will, because stories are the only thing that outlives us — don't make me noble. Make me *late*. I knew the right thing for thirty years. I just did it at the last possible moment. Let the children sing it that way. It's a better lesson."

"Aldous—"

"Sing, Tamsin."

And far below, and far above, and coming up the pilgrim stair with the smell of iron and forced throats, the counter-song began.

He heard it first — of course he did; the locked room had been open for weeks now, ever since Wren's letter, and everything he'd shut away came flooding back with the tuning of a boy's ear. It was ugly, Corvane's choir. Voices bent out of true, dragged along by the child-key they meant to steal, a sound like a rope bridge under too much weight. It climbed the shaft. The notation-lights shuddered.

Aldous Vane, who had no music in him, who had never had music in him, who had told the world so for forty years, opened his mouth.

The first note was rough. The second found its neighbor. By the third, the Deepwell had caught him up the way a river catches a leaf, and the mortal line — the simple, stubborn, anyone-can-hold-it promise of it — came out of him whole, and it was not beautiful, and it did not need to be. It was *true*, and the mountain took truth the way dry ground takes rain.

Behind him he heard Tamsin gasp — heard the chord change as she and the child dropped to the ledge and began to build. Ahead of him the flood came roaring up the well, and he planted his feet on the stone of his last hour and held the line.

It cost exactly what Wren had written it would cost.

He felt it take his voice first, then his warmth, then the long ledger of his years, and as the counter-song broke against him like water against a seawall — as the opening bars rang out below him, Tamsin's rough wanting voice and the child's bright inhuman one, weaving the first strands of a covenant reforged — Aldous Vane thought, with the last of his clarity, that his father had sung too, once, in a choir school by the sea, and that somewhere in the flood there was a note with his boy's name on it, and he went toward it singing.

* * *

Tamsin felt him go the way you feel a keel leave the water — a lurch, a lightness, a terrible new freedom of motion.

The mortal line did not stop. That was the miracle Wren had promised and Tamsin had not dared believe: the line, once given freely, *held*, hanging in the shaft like a bridge with its builder fallen from it, anchored in stone and consent, humming with the shape of a dead man's last honesty. She sang the bridge-line into it with blood that burned like swallowed starlight, and Sef's voice rose beneath hers, silver and sure, and the covenant began to close its circuit — mortal, bridge, fae, mortal—

And from the dark of the shaft, faint and fading and unmistakably his, Aldous's voice carried one final phrase before the mountain took it: not the covenant's words at all, but a dock-song round, three parts, the kind a smuggler hums to his child on his knee.

Tamsin sang the next bar through her tears, and the Deepwell blazed, and far below, in Corvane's own choir, a hundred stolen throats began — one by one — to remember whose voices they were.
