## 32. The Choir Reforged

The Deepwell was not a well. That was the first thing Tamsin understood as the light rose around her — it was a throat.

The shaft plunged beneath her feet like a singer's open mouth, and the stone walls curved inward in perfect rings, each one carved with lines of notation that had been dark her whole life and were now kindling ring by ring, following Aldous's voice down into the dark. The air itself had weight. It pressed against her eardrums the way deep water presses against a diver's chest, and every breath she drew tasted of struck flint and cold iron and something sweeter underneath, something green, something that belonged to a world she had been built to kill.

Sef's hand found hers. Small fingers, cold from the mountain night, gripping hard enough to hurt.

"Don't stop," Sef said. "Whatever happens. Don't you dare stop."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

Tamsin almost laughed. The child knew her too well already — three months on the road, and Sef could read her silences better than Aldous had ever read her words. She squeezed back. "Sing your line."

Above them, at the rim of the Deepwell, the Ash Queen's host stood silent along the galleries — a thousand shapes of silver and ash-grey, still as carved figures, their faces turned downward to watch whether the humans below would honor the bargain or break it a second time. Tamsin did not look up at them. Looking up was how you lost the note. Wren had taught her that in the last weeks of the old woman's life, hands too weak to gesture, so she'd taught with her eyes: *the song lives where you send it, girl. Send it down.*

She sent it down.

* * *

It began in her blood.

That was the part no one could have prepared her for, though Wren had tried, there in the snowbound cave, holding Tamsin's wrist and listening to her pulse with the expression of a woman reading a letter she already knew was bad news. *Your blood is the bridge,* Wren had said. *But bridges are walked on, child. Nobody asks the bridge how it feels.*

Now Tamsin knew how the bridge felt.

The covenant-song entered her through the soles of her feet, through the living stone, and met her blood somewhere behind her navel — and burned. Not like poison. She knew poison; poison was cold, poison was a slow grey tide climbing the limbs, poison was her oldest acquaintance and her most patient teacher. This was fire with intent. It climbed her spine vertebra by vertebra, and at each bone it left something behind: a word of the old tongue she had never learned, a memory that was not hers — a harvest festival where human children and fae children wove crowns from the same wheat, a treaty-fire on this very mountain when the world was younger, a name — *Felix* — spoken in a voice like morning.

Aldous's voice. Still falling. Still singing.

She caught the phrase he'd given her and carried it forward, and the bridge-line opened under her like a road.

This was the line Merrin could never have sung, though he'd tried until his throat bled in the practice caves. The mortal voice held the foundation; the fae voice held the light; but between them, binding them, there had to be a third thing — a voice that belonged to both realms and neither, a voice made of the wound between worlds. Corvane had spent twenty years trying to build that bridge out of stolen throats and corrupted blood. He'd never understood that the bridge was not something you took. It was something you were.

Something someone had made Tamsin into, before she could walk, without ever telling her why.

Her immunity. Her engineered, brewed, poisoned-perfect blood. Every dish she'd ever tasted for the crown, every quiet survival the court had praised as skill — all of it had been preparation for this. Not a weapon against the fae, as the taster's records claimed. A tuning fork. A living string stretched between two worlds, waiting for someone to draw a bow across her.

Wren had known. Wren had spent her dying strength keeping the Deepwell open precisely so that this moment could exist.

Tamsin sang, and her blood answered, and the burning became music.

* * *

Sef's voice came in above her like dawn coming in over water.

The child had been afraid to sing since Marrowgate — since the night they'd hidden in a cellar while inquisitors dragged a fae-blooded dyer into the street, since the night Sef learned what happened to voices like theirs in Corvane's world. But fear had gone out of them somewhere on the climb down, replaced by that fierce, terrifying clarity children carry like a lantern. Sef stood at the Well's rim with their bare feet on the notation-rings, and the fae line poured out of them — high, wild, ancient — and every syllable of it was in a language Sef swore they didn't know until the moment they sang it.

The galleries stirred. Tamsin felt it more than heard it: a thousand fae bodies shifting at once, like wind moving through a forest that has decided, at the last possible moment, not to fall.

Because here was the truth the Ash Queen had come to see with her own cold eyes: a human child of the blood, singing the fae line, unforced, unchained, unafraid. Not a captive choir. Not a coerced tenor. A volunteer.

The covenant had never needed perfection. Wren had said that too, near the end, her voice fraying like rope. *It needs willingness. That's all it ever needed. That's why it broke — not because the song failed, but because the willing stopped.*

The willing had started again.

* * *

And then, from far below — from the depths of the shaft where Corvane's counter-choir had been rehearsing for nine days in a cavern of stolen voices — the hundred began to rise.

Tamsin heard them the way you hear rain starting on a distant roof: one drop, then another, then a sound like the sky opening. Captured singers. Fae-blooded villagers taken in the purges, dock-singers seized off Marrowgate's quays, a whole village choir from Thornfell's southern valleys hauled north in wagons. Corvane had meant to bend them into his counter-song, to turn the covenant's own instrument against it. But a stolen voice is still a voice. And Aldous's last phrase — that stupid, tender dock-song round, three parts, the kind a smuggler hums to his child — had done what no incantation could do. It had reminded them whose throats these were.

One by one, then ten by ten, then all together, the hundred below stopped singing Corvane's corruption and started singing the covenant.

The Deepwell blazed.

There was no other word for it. Light came up out of the shaft like water reversing its fall, gold-white shot through with green, and the notation-rings ignited in sequence, and the whole hollow throat of the mountain filled with a sound that was not loud so much as *total* — a sound that did not enter the ear but replaced the air, so that breathing and singing became the same act. Tamsin's bridge-line ran through the center of it like a spine through a body, and she could feel every other voice touching hers: Sef's bright thread, the hundred rising from below, the galleries above beginning — hesitantly, then not — to answer, fae voices joining human ones for the first time in a generation.

And in the middle of it, impossibly, she heard Merrin.

He was supposed to be in Corvane's camp. He was supposed to be a prisoner, a decoy, a sacrifice — the price they'd paid to pull Sef out of the cages. But his tenor came threading down the shaft from the north side of the mountain, ragged and half-broken and unmistakably alive, singing the mortal foundation-line from wherever Corvane's people were holding him, and Tamsin understood with a lurch that the freed captives hadn't escaped alone. Somewhere down there, Merrin had done what Merrin always did. He'd taught them the song. Even in chains. Especially in chains.

*Merrin,* she thought, pouring everything into the thought because there was no breath to spare for words. *Hold on. We're coming.*

His voice wavered — laughed, maybe — and steadied.

* * *

The pain peaked at the third turning of the verse.

Later, she would remember almost nothing of it clearly — only fragments, the way you remember a storm from inside a shuttered house. She remembered that her blood felt like molten glass in her veins, and that the glass was being blown into a shape, and that the shape was a door. She remembered that her hands had gone white on Sef's shoulder, and that Sef had gripped back just as hard, the two of them anchoring each other to the world while the song tried to pour them both through it. She remembered tasting copper and starlight on her tongue at the same time, which should have been impossible, and understanding dimly that impossibility was rather the point.

She remembered wanting to stop.

Gods, how she wanted to stop. Her body begged for it the way it had begged during her first poisoning at seven years old, curled in the taster's cot, Aldous sitting beside her all night with a wet cloth, saying *again tomorrow, little one, again until it can't touch you.* She had survived every poison the crown ever fed her by refusing to let it finish. Now the covenant itself was the poison, and the only way through was the opposite of everything she'd ever learned: not to resist it. To let it finish. To let it remake her.

*You were made a weapon,* Wren had told her, angry on her behalf, furious at the crown that had done it. *So be a weapon. But choose the target.*

Tamsin chose.

She chose the wall between the realms — the wall Corvane had built higher every year with burnings and purges and stolen choirs, the wall that had made a child like Sef run from their own blood, the wall that had made an old hedge-witch spend her last winter teaching a taster to sing. She aimed everything she was at that wall and sang it open.

The bridge-line broke through.

* * *

What happened next, the ballads would get wrong, and Tamsin would let them, because the truth did not fit in verses.

The truth was that the Deepwell became a place where two skies touched. The shaft filled with light that was not light — or not only light — and through it, for the space of eleven heartbeats, the twilight verge and the mortal mountain were the same place. The galleries of the Ash Queen's host looked down into a hall that was also a hillside. The hundred captives in Corvane's cavern looked up into a sky that was also stone. And the song passed between the worlds like a flame passing between candles, taking nothing, losing nothing, lighting everything.

Tamsin stood at the center of it, burning, and wept, and sang, and had never in her life been less alone.

She felt her father in it — dead twelve years, the smuggler who'd taught her the passes, humming somewhere in the mortal line. She felt Wren, who had no voice left to give and gave something else instead, some final ember of will from whatever room beyond the world the dead keep. She felt Felix — the stranger at the salt gate, the man who'd bled out against her shoulder whispering *the Choir remembers* — and his presence was not grief anymore. It was gratitude. It was *finish it*, and she finished it.

On the eleventh heartbeat, the last ring of notation ignited at the bottom of the shaft, and the covenant closed around itself like a hand closing around a spark — gently, completely, forever or until broken again, and the difference between those two things was now written in living blood and would not be broken cheaply.

The light went out all at once, the way a struck bell goes silent — not fading but completing.

Tamsin fell.

* * *

She woke on her back on the Well's rim with Sef's coat folded under her head and the taste of iron and honey in her mouth, and the first thing she saw was stars — real stars, mortal stars, the ordinary cold constellations over Thornfell — and the second thing she saw was Sef's face, tear-streaked and grinning and lit from below by a glow that was coming, she realized slowly, from her own skin.

Faint lines traced her forearms where her sleeves had fallen back. Notation. The same script carved into the Deepwell's rings, inked into her flesh in pale gold, visible only when the light caught it — and the light catching it now was coming from inside her.

"It doesn't hurt," Sef whispered. "Does it? You're not hurting?"

"No." She flexed her fingers, astonished. The fire was gone. What remained was warmth — banked, settled, permanent, like a hearth in a house someone intended to live in. "It's — quiet. Everything's quiet." She sat up too fast and the world tilted, and Sef caught her, and over the child's shoulder she saw the galleries.

Emptying. The Ash Queen's host was withdrawing up the mountain paths in perfect silence — but not departing. She understood that with a taster's certainty, the same certainty that read a dish and knew what lived in it. They were withdrawing the way an audience withdraws after the performance they came scorning, and leave changed. Some of them, passing the rim of the Deepwell, bowed their heads toward her. One — tall, ash-pale, wearing the Queen's own silver — pressed a hand briefly to its chest, the old gesture, the covenant-gesture, the one from the carvings in the Hollow Court.

Then the galleries were empty, and the mountain was theirs.

"The hundred," Tamsin said, struggling upright. "Merrin — the captives —"

"Coming up," Sef said. "Listen."

She listened. Her hearing was changed — she'd known it would be, had felt the covenant settle into her ears along with everything else — and what she heard now was not sound so much as *texture*. Far below, footsteps on stair-cuts that hadn't existed an hour ago, dozens of them, weary and stumbling and ascending. And threaded through the footsteps, exhausted, hoarse, half-laughing, half-sobbing, the freed hundred were singing the dock-song round as they climbed. Three parts. Badly. Wonderfully.

And one voice ahead of the rest, leading them home.

Tamsin got to her feet. Her legs shook. Her blood sang. At the top of the stairs, torchlight bloomed, and the first of the freed captives came up out of the Deepwell into the open air — a fae-blooded woman with a dyer's stained hands, blinking at the stars — and behind her more, and behind them, gaunt and chained-wristed and grinning like a fool, Merrin Vale.

He saw her and stopped. The whole column stopped behind him, a river of voices dammed by one look.

"You're glowing," he said.

"You're late," she said.

He crossed the rim of the Deepwell in six strides and held her the way you hold something you'd already mourned, and around them the freed hundred came up into the starlight singing, and Sef threw themselves into the middle of all of it laughing, and far to the south, down in the dark where the counter-choir had died mid-note, Duke Corvane's great machine was even now beginning to understand that it had lost.

Tamsin held on, and glowed, and let herself be held.

Then, over Merrin's shoulder, her new ears caught it — faint, urgent, wrong — the sound of horns from the southern passes, and running feet, and someone shouting a word that froze the marrow of everyone on the mountainside:

"Marrowgate."
