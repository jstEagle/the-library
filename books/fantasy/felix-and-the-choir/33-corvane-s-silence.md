## 33. Corvane's Silence

Merrin had spent his whole life learning what a voice could do to a room. He knew how a single held note could make a tavern go quiet, how a wrong one could start a brawl, how grief sounded different in a throat than joy even when the melody was identical. He had thought himself an expert in voices.

He was not prepared for what it meant when ten thousand of them stopped at once.

He stood on the shoulder of Thornfell with Sef's small hand gripping his, and below him Corvane's army filled the valley like water filling a bowl — ranks of pike and crossbow, supply wagons, the great gilded pavilion of the Duke himself pitched within bowshot of the Deepwell's mouth. And rising out of that pavilion, out of the long wooden gallery Corvane had built around his captured singers, came the counter-song.

It was a hideous thing. Merrin heard it the way a jeweler hears paste: every note correct, every interval measured, and none of it alive. Corvane's choir-masters had taken fae voices — actual voices, torn from throats in ways Merrin did not let himself imagine — and bound them into an engine of sound designed to do what the covenant did, but inverted. Where the Choir wove realms together, the counter-song would cut. It would sever the mountain from its deep places, seal the verges shut forever, and leave the fae courts stranded and starving on the far side of their own border while Corvane's armies picked the free cities clean.

It should have worked. Wren had died believing it might not — had spent her last breath making sure it might not — but no one had known for certain. The covenant had not been sung whole in a generation. No living person had heard it finished.

Now Merrin heard it.

It began, as all such things begin, quietly. A thread of light rose from the Deepwell's mouth — he saw it as much as heard it, a gold-white filament climbing through the dark air like smoke deciding to be a rope — and then Tamsin's voice came up inside it.

Merrin had trained her for six weeks. Six weeks of cracked notes and fury and her stubborn, poisoned body refusing to do what he asked until suddenly, without warning, it didn't refuse anymore. He had told Brannoc once, drunk on plum wine in the winter cave, that teaching her was like watching someone learn to swim who'd been thrown in rivers all her life and never once been allowed to float. She fought the song because everything she loved had been used against her. Then she stopped fighting it, and the river carried her.

That voice rose now over the valley, thin and human and absolutely unbreakable, carrying the bridge-line Wren had taught her with her dying weeks. Beside her, Sef lifted the fae line — a child's voice, high and strange, harmonizing with something no human ear could fully track. And from the Deepwell itself, from the freed captives Corvane had caged in his own choir-gallery, other voices began to answer.

The counter-song faltered.

Not much. A half-beat, a hesitation in the third measure where there had never been a hesitation before. But Merrin felt it pass through the army below like wind through wheat — ten thousand soldiers shifting at once, hands tightening on weapons, eyes going to the pavilion.

"They hear it," Sef whispered. The child's face was streaked with ash and tears they weren't aware of shedding. "They can't help it. Nobody can help hearing it."

* * *

What happened next, Merrin would spend years trying to describe, and never quite succeed.

The stolen voices reclaimed themselves.

He felt it first as a change in the counter-song's texture — the way a chord changes when one instrument goes silent mid-phrase. One of the fae voices, somewhere in that wooden gallery, simply stopped obeying. Not stopped singing; stopped being *used*. There was a difference, and the difference rang across the valley like a bell cracking.

Then another. Then three at once.

Below, in the gallery, Merrin could see movement now — figures rising from their benches, silhouettes against lantern-light, and the choir-masters in their grey hoods rushing down the rows with their binding-rods raised. He saw a rod come down. He saw the singer it struck — a slight figure, fae-pale even at this distance — turn and look at the man holding it, and Merrin saw the choir-master take a step back.

Because the voice looking back at him was no longer captive. It was only angry.

"It's the covenant," Sef said, and the child was crying openly now, both hands pressed flat against the rock as if to hold themselves to the world. "When it's whole, it doesn't just bind the realms. It binds the *singers*. Everyone who ever sang it is part of it, forever. Those voices were sung into the covenant once, long ago — their grandmothers' grandmothers. Corvane stole them and thought they were things." The child's voice broke. "They were never things. They were members."

One by one, then in groups, then all together, the stolen voices turned on their masters.

The counter-song did not shatter. It *unraveled* — mid-note, exactly as the old hedge-witch had promised it would if the true Choir ever rose against it, because a lie cannot hold its shape in the presence of the truth it imitates. The great inverted engine of sound came apart the way a woven basket comes apart when you find the end of the wrong thread: one strand, then another, then the whole structure sighing loose into nothing.

And in the silence it left behind, Tamsin's voice went on alone for one impossible, naked moment — the bridge-line hanging in the air over ten thousand armed men — before the freed voices came home to her like birds to a roost, and the Choir stood up in the world again, whole.

* * *

The army broke in under a hundred heartbeats.

Merrin watched it happen and understood, with the terrible clarity of a witness, that no battle had been lost here at all. Battles require two sides willing to fight. What happened in the valley below was more like a fever breaking, or a spell ending, or a crowd waking from a dream it had been ashamed of.

The counter-song had done more than threaten the fae. Merrin understood that now, hearing the silence where it had been. For months it had been pouring out of that pavilion day and night, a low subsonic drone beneath the threshold of hearing, and it had been doing to Corvane's own soldiers what fear does — narrowing them, hardening them, making the man beside them look like an obstacle and the enemy look like vermin. It had been the sound of the Duke's will made audible, and every soldier in that valley had been breathing it since the march north began.

Now it was gone, and the Choir was in its place, and the Choir said something entirely different. It said: *you are not alone. You have never been alone. The thing you were told you must destroy is the thing that has been holding you up all along.*

A pikeman near the front rank dropped his weapon. Merrin saw it fall, saw it land in the trampled snow, saw the man stand looking at his own empty hands as though he'd never seen them before. Then a second soldier. Then a whole company, arms clattering down in a ragged cascade, and after that it wasn't a decision anymore, it was weather.

Corvane's guard tried to hold the line around the pavilion. Merrin gave them credit for trying — they were the Duke's own men, fed on better lies than the rest, and for perhaps thirty seconds they formed a wall of steel around the golden tent while inside it, faintly, Merrin heard shouting.

Then the mountain joined in.

Old Wren had told them about this, in the last week of her life, propped on furs in the smuggler's cave with her wound gone black and her mind still sharp as a frost edge. *The covenant isn't a contract between people,* she'd rasped. *People signed it, but the mountain countersigned. Stone remembers. When the Choir stands, the mountain stands with it.*

The mountain stood.

It was not dramatic, at first. A tremor underfoot, nothing more — the kind of shudder that might have been artillery or avalanche. But then the passes began to close. Merrin felt it rather than saw it, a grinding groan rolling through the rock beneath his boots, and down in the valley the retreat routes — the narrow defiles through which Corvane's army had come north — started to come down in controlled, deliberate slides, stone shearing from stone with the unhurried precision of a door being shut by someone who owns the house.

Not on the soldiers. That was the horrifying, merciful part. The rockfalls sealed the *roads*, not the men. Every soldier still had open ground before them, away from the Deepwell, away from the pavilion, down the long southern slopes toward the lowlands. The mountain was not killing anyone.

It was evicting them.

"Look," Sef breathed.

At the tree line above the eastern passes, lights were coming down. Not torches — lights, moving with purpose and grace, dozens of them, then hundreds: the Ash Queen's host descending through the twilight verge in full glory, spears like frozen lightning, banners that hurt to look at directly. They came down the slopes in perfect silence and took positions on the high ground overlooking the valley, and they did not attack, and they did not need to. Their presence was a sentence already passed.

Corvane's army looked up at the fae host, looked at the closing passes, looked at the golden pavilion — and chose, almost to a man, to walk away.

* * *

Merrin did not see the Duke die. He wanted to say, later, that he hadn't cared — that a man who burned Marrowgate deserved whatever end found him in that tent. But the truth was stranger and sat heavier in him.

The pavilion guards went last. Even they walked, eventually — laid down their halberds in the snow in a neat row, which somehow was worse than throwing them, and joined the long grey stream of men flowing south out of the dying campaign. And then the tent flaps parted, and Duke Corvane came out alone.

He was not running. Merrin would grant him that until his own dying day. The Duke walked out into the open ground before his empty camp, looked up at the fae host on the ridgeline, looked at the Deepwell's mouth where the light of the reforged covenant poured upward like a fountain that sang, and stood very still.

The Ash Queen's herald descended. Merrin saw the exchange from too far to hear it — a tall figure in ash-grey, stopping ten paces from the Duke, speaking words that glittered in the cold air. Whatever was said took less than a minute.

Then Corvane knelt.

Not to the herald. Merrin watched carefully, and the angle of the man's body was wrong for surrender to a fae court. He knelt facing the Deepwell — facing the Choir — and he bowed his head, and his lips moved, and whether it was prayer or apology or simple exhaustion Merrin never learned, because the herald touched his shoulder once, lightly, and the Duke of Corvane folded down into the snow like a coat slipping off a hook, and did not move again.

"He's not dead," Sef said quietly. The child's ears, fae-sharp, caught what Merrin's could not. "The Queen took his voice. All of it. He'll live a long time and never speak another word, and everyone who meets him will know why, because it'll be written on him." Sef wiped their face with a grimy sleeve. "She said — I heard her say — that a man who steals songs should learn what silence costs."

Merrin found, examining himself, that he could not summon triumph. He thought of Merrin Vale of the court, twenty-six years old and beautiful-voiced and terrified, standing in Corvane's receiving hall refusing to sing at the ascension, and the price he'd paid for that refusal — the years of dockside taverns, the whiskey roughening the instrument, the certainty that he had thrown away the only thing he'd ever been good at. He had survived his own silencing. He knew what it carved out of a person.

"He'll wish she'd killed him," Merrin said. "In about a year."

"Good," said Sef, with a child's unsentimental cruelty, and Merrin did not argue.

* * *

They came down into the valley as the sun set, and the sight of the abandoned camp was one Merrin knew would follow him to his grave. Cook-fires still burning with no one to warm. Banners lying in drifts. Wagons of siege equipment abandoned mid-road, oxen grazing placidly where their drivers had loosed them. An army of forty thousand had marched north behind a stolen song, and the same song, taken back, had marched them home again without a drop of blood spilled on either side — except the blood already paid, Wren's blood, and the blood of the singers whose voices had been caged, and the blood of Marrowgate, which no covenant could give back.

Tamsin stood at the Deepwell's mouth, and Merrin went to her.

She looked emptied. Not wounded — the poison-blood had burned through her and the covenant had taken it and remade it, and she was alive, impossibly, gloriously alive — but hollowed, the way a bell is hollowed, so that everything afterward would ring through her. Her hands were shaking. Her eyes were fixed on the middle distance where the last of Corvane's soldiers were disappearing down the southern roads.

"Merrin," she said, when she saw him. Her voice was raw, ruined, barely there. "Did we—"

"You did," he said. "We did. It's done."

"I can't stop hearing it." She pressed a hand to her sternum. "The song. It doesn't stop. It's in the rock and the trees and the—" She laughed, a small broken sound. "I spent my whole life tasting things for other people. Making sure nothing dangerous got past my tongue. Now I can taste the whole mountain and it's *singing*."

Merrin took her hands. They were cold. Around them, the freed singers were emerging from the wrecked gallery, blinking in the open air, and the fae host had begun to descend from the ridgeline in a slow ceremonial flood, and somewhere Brannoc was roaring laughter loud enough to echo off three peaks at once. None of it seemed to reach her yet. Shock, he thought. The body's oldest mercy.

"Come inside," he said. "There are people who want to see you. People who *chose* you, Tamsin. All of them. Let them."

She let him lead her. And as they walked, past the fallen pavilion, past the kneeling shape of the silenced Duke already going stiff in the snow, past the neat row of surrendered halberds glinting in the last light — Tamsin stopped.

"What?" Merrin asked.

She was looking at the ground. At a small object half-buried in the trampled snow where the Duke had knelt: a signet ring, thrown down or dropped, its crest crushed deliberately flat.

"No," Tamsin murmured, and her ruined voice went strange. "No, that's not his ring."

She knelt and picked it up, and turned it in the fading light, and Merrin saw her face change — saw recognition and horror arrive together, hand in hand.

"Tamsin? Whose is it?"

She looked up at him, and the singing mountain seemed suddenly very loud, and very far away.

"It's Aldous's," she said. "And it was crushed *before* the Duke knelt. Someone was here. Someone got to him first."
