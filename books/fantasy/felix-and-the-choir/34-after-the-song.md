## 34. After the Song

She woke to birdsong that was not birdsong.

Tamsin lay still and listened, because listening was the first thing she had ever learned to do well. There were thrushes somewhere above her — she could place them by the wet stone smell of their ledge — but beneath their song ran a second line, thin as wire: a counting, a keeping of time, the mountain's own pulse ticking through the roots of Thornfell like a heart under floorboards.

*The Deepwell is awake,* said the second voice, in no language at all. *The Deepwell is awake. The Deepwell—*

"Stop," she whispered, and it didn't stop. Nothing stopped anymore.

She opened her eyes. She was lying on a pallet of cloaks in a chamber of pale stone, and the first thing she understood was that the chamber was singing too — not audibly, not the way Merrin would have sung it, but in the way a bell keeps ringing after you've touched it. Every surface held a note. The walls hummed at a frequency just below hearing, the floor at one just above, and between them her own body sat like a tuning fork struck and never allowed to go quiet.

Sef was asleep beside her, curled small, one hand fisted in her sleeve even in sleep. The child's skin had gone faintly luminous since the Well — or perhaps Tamsin was simply seeing differently now, seeing the way both realms saw at once, the mortal flesh and the fae light laid over each other like two drawings on tracing paper.

She remembered everything. That was the cruelest part. She had expected — what? Blankness? Mercy? Instead the memory of the singing came back whole and unblurred: the bridge-line rising through her like water through a wick, her poisoned blood catching fire vein by vein, burning clean, burning *out*, and somewhere past the pain a moment when she had heard every voice in the world at once — human, fae, stone, river, the dead ones too, Wren's low rasp and Aldous's cracked tenor still holding his bar of the song with his last breath — all of it braided through her chest, and then silence, and then this.

She sat up. The chamber swam, steadied. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that much of her training had survived the remaking of her blood.

There was a cut on her palm from the rite, already closed. She turned her hand over and looked at the old scars beneath it, the hundreds of needle-marks from twenty years of tasting death for kings, and found — carefully, the way you probe a tooth — that they didn't hurt anymore. Not because they'd healed. Because the poison that lived in her blood, the poison brewed into her before she could walk, was gone. Not burned away exactly. *Spent.* Given to the covenant, note by note, until nothing toxic remained in her veins at all.

She was, for the first time in her life, an ordinary woman's blood in an extraordinary body.

And her body was neither one thing nor the other.

She felt it as soon as she stood. The floor of the Deepwell chamber accepted her weight, but slowly, reluctantly, the way ice accepts a footstep it has decided not to crack. The mortal world pulled at her from one direction — bread, smoke, cold water, the honest weight of iron — and the fae realm breathed against her from the other, sweet and vast and indifferent, and she stood precisely between them like a coin balanced on its edge, belonging to both, owned by neither.

"Oh," she said quietly, to no one. "Oh, that's the price."

* * *

Merrin found her an hour later at the mouth of the cavern, where the twilight verge thinned into ordinary grey morning air.

He looked terrible. Ten days in Corvane's cages had left him hollow-cheeked, his throat wrapped in linen where the inquisitors' gag-chains had torn it, and his voice — when he spoke — came out sanded down to half its former grain. But he was walking, and he was free, and when he saw her sitting on the stone watching the sun come up over the passes, he stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

"You're awake."

"I'm awake."

"We thought—" He crossed the distance fast and then, oddly, slowed in the last few steps, the way people slow near something they're afraid might break. "Three days, Tamsin. You've been three days under. Sef wouldn't leave you. Brannoc carried you up from the Well himself and cried the whole way and will deny it to his grave."

"Is Corvane—"

"Gone." He said it simply, and the word went out over the valley and did not echo, because the mountain caught it and kept it. "His counter-song unraveled mid-note, just like Wren promised it would. His stolen voices woke up and walked out of him. Half his army deserted before sundown. The rest are straggling south, and the Ash Queen's host let them go — watched them go, rather, the whole way, which I think frightened them worse than killing them would have."

"And the free cities?"

"Holding the passes. Brannoc's smugglers have been carrying word for three days — your word, actually. You talked in your sleep. A lot." A ghost of his old crooked smile. "Half of it was orders. Half of it was swearing."

She almost smiled back. Then the wind shifted, and she heard what lay underneath the morning — the whole layered world of sound she could never again shut off — and her face must have done something, because Merrin's smile died.

"What is it?" he said. "What's wrong? You won. Everyone's saying you won."

"I don't know if I won." She looked at her own hands again. "Listen to me, Merrin. Really listen. Can you hear the mountain?"

"The mountain?"

"Underneath. The note it holds. The covenant — it's running through everything now, stone and river and border-ward, and I can hear all of it. All the time. Both sides." She pressed her fingertips to her temples. "When I stand here, I can hear Marrowgate's bells, and I can hear the Ash Queen's court singing vespers forty miles inside the verge, and I can hear the space between them, the seam where the worlds are stitched, and it is *loud*, Merrin. It never stops. And when I walk into a human town, the town will know I'm not quite of it. And when I walk into the fae court, the court will know I'm not quite of them either. My blood is part of the covenant now. Which means I'm part of the seam. And seams don't get to live on either side of the cloth."

Merrin sat down beside her on the cold stone. For a while he said nothing, which was one of the things she had learned to love about him — that he knew when a silence needed filling and when it needed keeping.

"Wren told me once," he said finally, "that every gift has a tollhouse on its road. She said it like a joke. It wasn't one."

"No. It wasn't."

"Does it hurt? Right now, does it hurt?"

"It's loud," she said. "It isn't pain. It's more like — you know how after years in the palace kitchens I couldn't taste a stew without naming every spice in it? Couldn't just eat. Just *knowing*, all the time, whether I want to or not."

"That sounds lonely."

"It is completely lonely," she said, and laughed, because it was absurd — she had spent thirty years being lonely in crowds and now she was lonely inside a miracle, and there was no version of complaining about it that anyone alive could pity. "I asked for this, Merrin. At the Well, when the third voice failed and Aldous was dying and Sef's line was slipping, I took the bridge-line knowing what it might cost. I'd do it again. I want to be clear about that. But knowing the price going in doesn't make the bill smaller when it arrives."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't."

Below them, far down the valley, the refugee road out of the mountains was coming alive — carts, banners, the free cities' militia drilling in a field, and over all of it the thin bright thread of the covenant humming through the ground like a plucked string that refused to fade. She could hear individual voices in it. She could hear a woman in Marrowgate singing to her baby, and a fae child in the verge singing the same tune without ever having heard it, and the seam between those two songs was her, was *her name*, stitched through the middle of the world.

"Sef's awake," Merrin said. "Been asking for you."

"How are they?"

"Changed too. Less frighteningly. They say the calling has stopped — whatever was pulling them home below the mountain, it's satisfied now. They belong to themselves, they said. First fully-formed sentence I've heard out of them." He paused. "They also said something else. About you."

"What?"

"They said you're the only person in either world who can hear both sides tell the truth." He turned to look at her, and his ruined voice dropped lower. "The council's sent riders. The free cities and the Ash Queen both. They want terms, Tamsin. An accord. And every envoy on both sides is already lying, because that's what envoys do, and nobody — not one soul in either realm — can hear the lie under the words."

"Except me."

"Except you."

She closed her eyes. Behind them, the seam sang.

* * *

They brought her food, and this was how she learned the second cost.

Brannoc brought it himself — black bread, hard cheese, smoked fish, a pot of honey, the kind of smuggler's feast he'd promised her half a lifetime ago in a tavern that no longer existed. He set it down before her with enormous ceremony and then hovered, arms folded, watching her eat with the anxious intensity of a man who had carried her unconscious body up a mountain and needed evidence the effort hadn't been wasted.

The bread tasted of wheat, and of the field it grew in, and of the rain that fell on the field, and of the farmer's mood when he harvested it, and of the miller's grief for his dead wife, and of the covenant-thread running through the yeast like a silver wire. The honey tasted of ten thousand bees and the particular flowers they had chosen and the queen bee's long slow satisfaction, and underneath it, faintly, of the vergewood hives that had supplied it, which meant the honey had crossed the border, which meant someone in the free cities was already trading with the fae, which meant—

"Tamsin." Brannoc's voice, careful. "Lass. You've gone white as flour."

"I can taste everything," she said. "Everything the food *is*. Where it's been. Who made it. Whether they were happy." She set down the spoon. "Twenty years I trained my tongue to catch poison in a hundred dishes a day. Now my tongue catches truth. In everything. Forever."

Brannoc lowered himself onto the bench across from her, joints cracking, and studied her the way he studied a lock he intended to pick.

"My da had a saying," he rumbled. "For dwarves who work the deep seams and come up deaf from the blasting. 'You gave your ears to the mountain, lad. Mountain'll give 'em back different.' Never met a miner yet who regretted it. Missed the old ears, mind. But the new ones heard things worth having."

"This isn't deafness, Brannoc. It's the opposite."

"Aye. Worse luck." He pushed the honey pot toward her. "But hear me out, because I'm old and I've earned a speech. You spent your whole life being used, lass. King used your tongue. Aldous used your trust. Corvane wanted to use your blood. Every gift you ever had, somebody else decided what it was for." His eyes, dark and shrewd under the grey brows, held hers. "This one's yours. Nobody handed it to you. Nobody bred you for it. You *built* it, at the Deepwell, out of your own stubborn poisoned hide. So it's loud, and it's lonely, and you can't ever sit in a kitchen and just eat a bloody stew again. Fine. But it's the first thing you've ever owned outright. Don't you dare grieve it like it was taken from you."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment.

Then she picked up the spoon, dipped it in the honey, and ate it — deliberately, tasting all ten thousand bees and the farmer and the rain and the seam of the world, every layer at once, refusing to flinch.

"It's very good honey," she said.

"Aye," Brannoc said gruffly, and got up to fetch more bread, and if his eyes were wet he blamed the mountain draft, loudly, twice.

* * *

Sef came to her at dusk.

The child climbed onto the ledge beside her without asking, the way family does, and sat with their legs swinging over the drop, and for a while they simply listened together — Tamsin to the doubled world, Sef to whatever single clear channel the child now owned entirely.

"You're sad," Sef said.

"I'm adjusting."

"You're sad because you can't go back to being only human. And you're scared because the fae side hasn't decided what you are yet." Sef swung their feet. "I can hear it too, a little. What things think of me. The stones like you. Did you know that? The stones think you're the best thing that ever happened to them."

"The stones are welcome to me."

"But the *court*—" Sef wrinkled their nose. "The court is confused. The Ash Queen's people keep arguing about you. Some of them call you the Bridge. Some of them call you the Poisoner Still. Old names. From before the breaking."

"Which do you call me?"

Sef considered this with the gravity the child brought to everything.

"I call you Tamsin," they said. "That's why it works."

She put her arm around the child and drew them in against her side, and Sef came willingly, boneless with trust, and the two of them sat in the doorway between worlds while the first stars came out — stars that Tamsin could now hear, faintly, each one holding its own long note, the whole sky a choir that had apparently been singing all along and had merely been waiting for anyone mortal enough to listen.

"Merrin says there are envoys coming," Sef said into her shoulder. "From both sides. For the talking-fire."

"I know."

"They're all going to lie."

"Some of them will."

"Not to you, though." Sef tilted their head back to look up at her, and in the twilight the child's eyes held both colors at once, mortal brown and fae silver, and Tamsin understood that Sef could see her exactly as she now was — the seam itself, the living stitch, the woman who belonged to both worlds and would never be at home in either. "Nobody lies to the seam. The seam knows."

Down in the valley, torchlight was moving along the refugee road. Two parties, she realized, listening: one riding up from the free cities with the council's seal, one descending out of the verge with the Ash Queen's white banners, converging on the same fire-circle in the same field, both carrying words that had been polished smooth of all truth before they ever left home.

Both sides wanted an accord. Both sides wanted it on their own terms. And in three days, at a fire between armies, someone would have to stand between them and hear what was actually being said — every hidden clause, every buried fear, every lie dressed as a gift — and speak it aloud to enemies who had every reason to silence her.

Her tongue had been a weapon for kings. Then a weapon against the fae. Now, at last, it might be a tool for peace — if the peace survived her honesty, and if she survived theirs.

"They're coming for the parley," Sef said.

"Yes."

"Are you afraid?"

Tamsin listened — to the armies, the mountain, the sky, the seam that was herself, singing.

"Terrified," she said honestly. "But I've been poisoned by better than this."

Sef laughed, high and bright, and the sound went down into the valley ahead of the envoys, and the mountain carried it, and both realms heard it, and neither one could claim it.
