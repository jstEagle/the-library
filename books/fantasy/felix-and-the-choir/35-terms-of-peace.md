## 35. Terms of Peace

The negotiating fire was built in the shape of a doorway.

Tamsin noticed that first, before the heralds finished their speeches, before the Ash Queen's retinue came down the mountain in a procession of grey silk and bone-white antlers. The free cities' masons had raised a hearth on the flats below Thornfell's southern shoulder — no walls, no roof, just a ring of black stone with a lintel beam laid across two pillars — so that whoever sat at it sat *in* a threshold rather than beside one. Old Wren would have known what it meant. Wren was ash now, scattered at the tollhouse with a spymaster's signet, but Tamsin had learned enough in the last year to read the shape of things: this fire was meant to be walked through, not around. A door both sides could claim to have entered honestly.

She took her place at the fire's edge with a cup in her hand and nothing in her stomach, because that was how she had always worked best. Empty tongue, empty belly, senses stripped down to wire. Twenty years of tasting other people's food had taught her that truth had a flavor — bitterness under honey when a man lied about his loyalties, copper-heat when a woman spoke words she'd rehearsed until they stopped being hers. She could not explain it to anyone who hadn't spent a childhood being poisoned on purpose. But today, for the first time, she wasn't there to swallow danger for someone else.

Today she was the instrument.

* * *

The delegations faced each other across thirty feet of trampled snow: nine lords and mayors of the free cities on one side, gaunt from Marrowgate's burning and the winter of holding the passes; the Ash Queen's court on the other, still as carved figures, their eyes catching the firelight like coins at the bottom of a well. Between them, the fire cracked and spat, throwing sparks up through the doorway of its own smoke.

The talks began badly, which Tamsin had expected. What she hadn't expected was how quickly they went wrong in ways nobody else seemed to notice.

Lord Casserly of Vell opened for the cities, his voice pitched to carry, all reasonableness and grievance. "Your people burned our fields in the war's last days. Grain stores at Harrow Ford. We have the ash to prove it."

"They were Corvane's granaries," said the fae envoy, a tall figure called Lissande whose voice sounded like wind through reeds. "Stolen harvests. Taken from your own farmers and marched south to feed an army that meant to enslave us."

"Then you burned *our* grain."

"We burned *his*."

Tamsin watched the lords stiffen. Watched the fae delegation go colder still. And she tasted the air between them — not literally, though her tongue pressed against the roof of her mouth out of habit — and understood that both sides were telling the truth, and that the truth was going to kill the accord before noon.

She stood. Every eye swung to her, and she felt the old familiar flinch, the taster's instinct to be small, to be furniture. She put it down like setting aside a heavy pack.

"Before anyone speaks again," she said, "I want to taste what's being served."

Murmurs. Lord Casserly frowned. Lissande tilted her head with the slow curiosity of a heron.

"The custom is old," Tamsin went on, walking to the fire. "A king's taster stands between the crown and poison. I held that post for twenty years. I know what it is to be the thing that dies so someone else can eat safely." She crouched at the fire's edge and lifted the ladle from the communal pot — a peace-pot, both sides had contributed to it, venison from the mountain and barley from the valley, cooked over wood gathered by human hands and kindled with a coal carried down from the fae verge. "So I will taste first. Not for poison in the food." She sipped the broth, held it on her tongue, let them watch her do it. "For poison in the words."

"That's not—" Casserly started.

"It is exactly what you brought me here for," Tamsin said. "You asked me to stand at this fire because my tongue reads truth where others hear only noise. So let me work." She swallowed, and looked at him. "Lord Casserly. When you say 'our grain,' what do you actually mean?"

He hesitated. She watched the lie assemble itself behind his eyes — the reflex of a politician, half spite and half habit — and then, because she had asked plainly and he was too tired for anything else, it crumbled.

"Grain I taxed," he admitted. "Grain I took from farmers who are now starving. I want it back because losing it shamed me." A ripple went through the delegation. Someone coughed. Casserly looked, briefly, like a man relieved of a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "That's the truth of it. The burning hurt my pride more than my people. My people were starving before the fires."

"Thank you." She turned to Lissande. "And you? When your people say the granaries were stolen — is that grief or grievance?"

Lissande was silent a long moment. Then, in that reed-and-wind voice, quieter than anything she'd yet said aloud: "Grief. The harvest was sung over. Our singers blessed those fields for three hundred years, even after the covenant broke, because the singing does not stop just because the listeners did. When they burned, we heard every song end at once." Her hands, folded in her lap, tightened. "We did not burn them to wound you. We burned them because we could not bear to hear them."

Silence around the fire. Tamsin tasted the words — grief, true grief, bitter as gentian root — and nodded once.

"You see?" she said to the whole assembly. "Two truths. Both real. Neither one a weapon, once you've tasted it properly. This is what I can do. This is why you should let me stand between you."

* * *

They made it formal by sundown, because institutions move fast only when they're frightened, and everyone at that fire was frightened.

*Taster of Intentions*, the provisional accord named her — a title Merrin later swore he'd invented on the spot and Brannoc insisted came from some smuggler joke neither of them would admit to. Whatever its origin, it stuck. Before any term was spoken aloud, it passed through Tamsin first. She would listen, taste, and name what lay beneath: fear dressed as principle, pride dressed as justice, genuine injury dressed as policy. She didn't judge the hidden thing. Judging would have ended everything by morning. She only named it, plainly, the way she'd once named nightshade in a soup — *this dish contains something you should know about before you swallow.*

It nearly broke the talks twice.

The first time was over the passes. The cities demanded the Thornfell crossings be garrisoned by human soldiers; the fae court refused absolutely; voices rose; hands went to sword-hilts on the human side and to things that were not swords on the other. Tamsin stood into the shouting and tasted it, and what she found underneath surprised even her.

"Neither side wants soldiers on those passes," she announced. "Lord Casserly, you're asking for garrisons because Marrowgate burned and you cannot bear another city undefended — but you don't trust your own generals to keep those garrisons from becoming occupiers of fae roads. And Lissande, your court refuses because you remember what armed men did to your singers — but you know, you *know*, that a pass with no travelers on it starves both realms." She paused. "What you both actually want is someone on those roads whom everyone trusts. Which is why the accord you'll sign tonight puts the passes in the hands of couriers. Smugglers. People like me, who've walked them all our lives and owe both sides nothing and both sides everything."

Brannoc, sitting with the free cities' delegation as unofficial advisor and very official skeptic, barked a laugh. "Ha! The Thieves' Road, run honest. Never thought I'd see it."

"You haven't seen it yet," Tamsin said. "You're going to help build it."

The second near-break was worse. A fae noble — old, cold, introduced only as the Warden of Roots — rose and demanded, in a voice like frost forming, a term of restitution: that the descendants of the men who slaughtered the covenant singers three generations ago be named before the court and made to kneel.

Tamsin tasted the demand before she answered it, and found, beneath the frost, something that stopped her breath. Not vengeance. *Longing.* The Warden wanted to see human faces bent low not from cruelty but from hunger — hunger for a reckoning that might finally make the loss feel real, feel *finished*. And beneath that, fainter, almost unbearable: the fear that nothing ever would.

She could have named it cruelly. Instead she crossed the snow, stood before the ancient fae, and said quietly: "You don't want their knees. You want the killing to have *meant* something. You want to look at the grandchildren of the men who did it and see that they know what was lost — not as a story, as a wound."

The Warden's eyes fixed on her. Cold as well-water. Then, slowly, the frost cracked. "Yes," the old fae whispered. "Gods below. Yes."

"Then ask for that instead," Tamsin said. "Ask for the names to be sung, not the bodies bowed. Ask the conservatory in Marrowgate to teach the children what happened, in song, every year, so no one forgets — and so the forgetting never has room to grow into doing it again." She glanced toward Merrin, who sat with the cities' delegation looking like a man watching his life's work get requisitioned in real time, tears standing unashamed in his eyes. "He'll write it. He's good at turning wounds into music. It's rather his whole tragedy."

Someone laughed — a wet, broken laugh, human and fae together — and the moment turned, and the accord survived.

* * *

Near midnight, with the terms agreed and only the signing left, the Ash Queen herself spoke for the first time since the fire was lit.

She had sat through everything in silence, grey and luminous, and Tamsin had felt her attention like a hand resting lightly on the back of her neck. Now the Queen rose, and the fire itself leaned toward her, flames bending as if in wind.

"One matter remains," the Queen said. "Not a term. A question." Her gaze found Tamsin. "The bridge-line you sang at the Deepwell was written into no covenant and composed by no court. You made it. Out of poisoned blood and desperation and — if the reports I am given are true — joy. Such a voice does not belong to a title. It belongs to a choice." She descended the few steps to the snow, and stood before Tamsin, close enough that Tamsin could smell cold stone and night-blooming flowers. "So I ask you, Taster of Intentions, not as a subject asks a queen, nor a queen a servant: what do *you* want? Not the cities. Not my court. You."

Every face at the fire turned to her. Merrin's, steady and warm and carefully not hoping. Sef, half-asleep against Brannoc's shoulder, blinking awake. The lords, the envoys, the Warden with his cracked frost.

And Tamsin stood in the doorway-shaped firelight and reached for the answer the way she'd once reached for a suspect dish — empty-tongued, wire-strung, honest.

"I want a door that opens both ways," she said. "I want to walk the passes carrying letters and songs instead of secrets and blades. I want the conservatory full, and the taverns loud, and the mountains safe enough that Sef can stop looking over their shoulder. I want—" and here her voice caught, because wanting was still new to her, still a muscle learning its use, "—I want to be trusted with my strange gifts instead of being used by them. That's all. That's everything."

The Ash Queen studied her for a long moment. Then she extended one grey hand, palm up, and in it lay a small cup of hammered silver, steaming faintly.

"Then drink," the Queen said. "Once more, and freely chosen. A draught of the verge, brewed for oath-keeping. It will mark you — both courts will know your tongue anywhere, in any hall, and know it clean. No king can command it. No queen either. It binds you to nothing except the truth." A pause, and something moved behind the Queen's ageless face that might, in a younger creature, have been a smile. "Consider it the last poison you will ever taste. And the first gift."

Tamsin looked at the cup. Twenty years of swallowing death for strangers, and here at last was a cup offered *to* her, for *her*, by someone who knew exactly what her tongue could do and honored it instead of spending it.

She drank.

It tasted of gentian and honey and snowmelt and, underneath everything, faintly and unmistakably, of song. Warmth spread through her chest and settled somewhere behind her ribs, next to the scar the Deepwell had left. When she exhaled, her breath fogged silver in the cold air, and every ear at the fire — human and fae alike — heard, for one clear instant, a second voice beneath her own, harmonizing.

The accord was signed by dawn. Nine seals of wax and ink from the free cities; one print of a grey hand, glowing briefly on the parchment, from the Ash Queen. The passes would belong to the couriers. The singers would be remembered in song. The verges would open at the turning seasons, and no army would cross them.

As the delegations broke apart into exhausted, disbelieving groups — men and fae sharing the peace-pot's last broth, Casserly and Lissande already arguing about grain tariffs with something dangerously close to friendliness — Merrin came to stand beside Tamsin at the dying fire.

"Well," he said softly. "You fed them the truth, and they ate it."

"They were hungry," Tamsin said. "That's the secret of it. Everyone's hungry for it. They're just afraid of the taste."

Merrin smiled, and opened his mouth to say something else—

—and stopped, because down the southern road, dust rising despite the frozen ground, came riders. Many riders. Flying no banner Tamsin knew, moving fast, and at their head a figure in travel-stained grey whose face, even at this distance, she recognized from a hundred childhood memories of a palace corridor.

Sef was suddenly, fully awake, gripping her sleeve. "Tamsin. The road. Who is that?"

Tamsin's stomach dropped, twenty years of training narrowing to a single point of dread.

"That," she said, "is the King's own marshal. And if he's ridden this hard, the crown has heard about the accord — and has sent someone to tell us what the crown thinks of it."
