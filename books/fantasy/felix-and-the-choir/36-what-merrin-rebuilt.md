## 36. What Merrin Rebuilt

The first thing Merrin Vale did in ruined Marrowgate was sweep a floor.

It seemed a small thing to do with the freedom of a man who had spent months as a prisoner of war, but his hands remembered work. They had always been better at doing than at speaking — which was its own private joke, given what his voice had once been worth. He found a broom in the ash of a chandler's shop and swept the great hall of the old conservatory until the soot lifted and the flagstones showed their original pattern: interlocking circles, human-made stone fitted against fae-grown crystal, neither seam visible. The builders of Marrowgate had known things the city's burning had not managed to unmake.

The conservatory had been Corvane's first target when he sacked the free cities. Not the granaries. Not the armories. The school. Merrin had stood in Corvane's camp and watched the fires on the horizon and understood, with a clarity that still frightened him, that the duke had never feared swords. He had feared songs.

So Merrin swept the floor. Then he pried boards off the windows. Then he sat down in the middle of the empty hall, in the slanting afternoon light, and sang the first note he had sung since Aldous put a knife to his throat and told him what note to sing instead.

His voice cracked on it. That was fine. Cracked notes were honest ones.

* * *

They came slowly, at first, the way water finds a channel.

A girl named Odile whose family had lost everything in the sack of the river quarter, trailing her two younger brothers like ducklings. A stonemason's son who could hold a pitch the way other children held a grudge. Then, within the first month, the fae children began to arrive — shy as deer at first, standing in the doorway with their too-bright eyes and their ears that caught harmonics human ears couldn't name, until one of them, a willow-slim boy called Fen who smelled of rain and green wood, crept in during a lesson and sat at the back and did not leave.

Merrin taught them all the same songs.

That was the whole philosophy, if you could call it a philosophy. It was really just a refusal. In the old court, where Merrin had been the finest voice in three kingdoms before he was nothing at all, the music had been divided the way everything was divided — mortal halls sang mortal songs, and the few fae airs that crossed over were curiosities, performed behind wards, stripped of their second voice the way you'd defang a snake. And in Corvane's camp, the division had become a weapon: captured fae voices bent into a counter-choir, song turned into chains.

Here there was only a circle of children on a swept floor, human and fae and the fae-blooded in between, learning to listen for the part beneath the part.

"Again," Merrin said, tapping the rhythm on his knee. "Odile, you're rushing. Fen — don't correct her. Sing *with* her. If she rushes, you slow, and she'll hear you and come find you. That's how it works. Nobody drags anybody. You make a sound the other person wants to stand inside."

Fen's brow furrowed — the deep, ancient frown of a child perhaps forty years old, which among the fae was barely weaned. But he tried. He slowed his line, softened it, wrapped it around Odile's stumbling melody like a hand around a bruise. And Odile, without knowing why, stopped rushing.

The room settled into harmony. Dust motes turned in the light. Somewhere in the walls, the old covenant-stones — the ones that had survived the fire because they were not entirely stone — gave off a faint warmth, the way a hearth holds heat after the coals die.

Merrin stood very still and let the sound finish, because interrupting it would have been a kind of blasphemy.

*There,* he thought. *This is what it was for. This is what they killed a city to stop.*

* * *

He rebuilt more than the school, though the school came first.

Marrowgate was a skeleton of a city with a stubborn marrow. People filtered back through the autumn — masons assessing what walls could stand, fishwives re-rigging stalls in the salt market, an old harbormaster who had refused to evacuate and now emerged from his cellar blinking like a mole, furious and alive. Brannoc arrived in the second week with three wagons of timber from Thornfell and a beard full of sawdust, took one look at the conservatory roof, and said, "Right. I've built worse drunk," and set his smugglers to work as carpenters, which explained a great deal about the subsequent architecture.

Sef came and went like weather. The child had a throne now, or the beginnings of one, in the reforged courts under the mountain — the youngest voice ever seated in the Ash Queen's circle, and the bridge between realms made flesh, and also, still, irreducibly, a child who liked honey cakes and hated sitting still. Sef appeared at the conservatory most weeks, flopped onto the floor among the students, corrected everyone's technique with the imperiousness of rank, and vanished again before supper. The children adored them. Merrin suspected half of what Sef taught the fae children about humans, and all of what they taught the human children about the fae, happened right there on that dusty floor, in the space between one verse and the next.

And Tamsin came when she could, which was less often than any of them wanted, including her.

She was everywhere and nowhere those first seasons after the Choir. The passes needed walking; the crossings between realms needed mapping before someone else mapped them badly; the negotiating fire between the free cities and the Ash Queen required her tongue, which could taste truth in words the way it once tasted poison in wine. She arrived in Marrowgate smelling of mountain rain and other people's politics, stayed three days, five days, a week, and left again with her pack already buckled, apologizing with her whole body for going.

Merrin never asked her to stay.

He thought about it constantly. He composed the asking the way he'd once composed songs — in the dark, in the pauses, turning phrases over until they shone. *Stay. There's a room above the conservatory, I've already put a bed in it, I put the bed there four months ago and I change the linens anyway.* He knew exactly how he'd say it. He'd spent a lifetime learning how to say things beautifully; it was the one craft the silencing hadn't taken from him.

But he had also spent a lifetime watching what happened when beautiful words became cages. Corvane had asked him to sing, once, in a way that admitted no refusal. Aldous had asked Tamsin to come home, in a voice that loved her and meant to own her. Merrin had sworn, in the ashes of his own captivity, that he would never make a request whose no cost anything.

So he said nothing, and fixed the roof, and taught the children, and kept a room ready above the conservatory with clean linens and a window facing the mountains, and waited for her the way you wait for spring — not by pulling at the ground.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first winter of the new accord, and with it the first Frostfair.

Wren had talked about Frostfairs sometimes, in the cave, in the weeks before her rite — the old joint festivals, human and fae, that had died with the covenant. Merrin decided, with the particular stubbornness of a man rebuilding a world out of spite and hope in equal measure, that Marrowgate would have one. Brannoc hauled ice. The Ash Queen sent an honor guard of willow-wisps who pretended to be decorative lighting and were, in fact, security. Sef judged the snowman competition with scandalous partiality toward their own entry.

On the night itself, the conservatory steps became a stage, and Merrin stood before the whole ragged, frostbitten, miraculous city and sang.

Not alone. That was the point. Odile opened with the children's round, human voices braided with fae ones, and then the crowd took it up — the harbormaster bellowing, the masons' wives harmonizing, a wisp-light choir threading silver above it all — and Merrin stood in the middle of a song that belonged to no single throat and felt, for the first time since the knife at his neck, that his voice was fully his own again. Not because it was the finest voice in three kingdoms. Because it was one voice among hundreds, and none of the hundreds would have been complete without it.

Afterward, in the cold blue dark behind the conservatory, he found Tamsin leaning against the wall, apart from the firelight, watching the fair with the expression she wore whenever joy happened near her — wary, attentive, like a taster checking a dish for poison.

"You're missing it," he said.

"I'm tasting it." She glanced at him. Snow caught in her hair and didn't melt. "Old habit. Look at it first. Wait. See if anyone falls over."

"And?"

"No one's fallen over." A pause. "It's unsettling."

Merrin laughed, breath steaming. Then, because the moment was warm and the night was long and courage is mostly timing, he heard himself say: "There's a room upstairs. Above the hall. It's been ready since autumn."

Tamsin went very still. It was the stillness she'd learned in kitchens — the total absence of motion that meant every sense was pointed at a threat. Except this time the threat was a kindness, and she didn't have training for that.

"I'm not asking anything," Merrin said quickly. "I want to be clear about that. The room exists whether you're in it or not. It has a good window. That's the entire content of my offer — a room with a good window, in a building where children sing, in a city that's coming back. No conditions. No expiration. You can use it twice a year or never, and it stays yours, and I'll keep changing the linens, and I will never once ask you why you're leaving or when you're coming back."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's a strange offer."

"It's the only kind I know how to make anymore." He looked at the firelight, not at her, giving her the mercy of an unwatched face. "I spent years being asked for my voice by people who'd take it if I said no. You spent your whole life being used by people who called it love. I'm not going to be one more person holding out something you want with a hook in it. So: no hook. Just the room. Stay or go. Both answers are allowed forever."

For a long moment Tamsin said nothing. Below them the fair roared and glittered; somewhere Sef was cheating at their own snowman contest; the wisps drifted overhead like patient stars.

"I don't know how to answer that," she said finally, and her voice was rough. "I don't know how to want things yet. I'm learning. It's slow. Everything I ever wanted before, someone put in my hand and told me it was duty." She looked up at him then, and her eyes in the cold light were the clearest thing he'd ever seen. "Ask me again sometime. Not because I need persuading. Because I want to know what it feels like to say yes on purpose."

"Whenever you're ready," Merrin said. "The linens are changed weekly."

And she laughed — really laughed, short and startled out of her, the laugh of a woman discovering an unused muscle — and reached over and squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go, and walked back toward the firelight, and Merrin stood in the snow feeling like a man who had planted an orchard and been told, honestly, kindly, truthfully: *wait.*

He could wait. He was very good at long pieces.

* * *

Spring brought the first graduating class, all nine of them, and a problem Merrin hadn't anticipated: the fae children were growing restless in the human-built hall, and the human children were begging to see the courts below the mountain, and the old question — *where does one world end and the other begin?* — was being asked by eight-year-olds who expected an answer.

He was drafting a letter to the Ash Queen's circle about a joint summer term when the knock came at the conservatory door.

Not Tamsin's knock. Hers was two beats, apologetic, like someone hoping no one was home. This was formal. Three measured raps, the knock of an official.

He opened the door on a courier in the livery of the free cities' council — mud-spattered, windburned, having ridden hard. The young man held out a sealed letter and a verbal message both, and his eyes were wide in a way that made Merrin's stomach drop before a word was spoken.

"From the council, master Vale. And from—" the courier swallowed— "from the pass at Thornfell. The lady Thackeray sent it ahead of me. She said you'd want to know at once."

"What is it?" Merrin asked, taking the letter. "What's happened?"

The courier glanced past him, at the children visible through the doorway, at the fae boy Fen peering around Merrin's leg with luminous eyes — and lowered his voice.

"The crossings are waking up," he said. "All of them. Every pass in Thornfell, all at once, and nobody ordered it. The lady says the mountain is opening doors that have been shut since the first covenant broke." He hesitated. "She says something on the other side is calling them open. And she says—" he fumbled for the exact words, clearly having repeated them to himself the whole ride— "she says the singing started three days ago, and it isn't stopping, and it isn't the Choir. It's something older. And it knows her name."

Merrin stood in his own doorway with the letter unopened in his hand, listening to his students practice scales behind him, and understood that the world he had just finished rebuilding was about to be tested at its foundations.

"Fen," he said quietly, without turning around. "Fetch your coat. We're going to need to send word to the mountain."
