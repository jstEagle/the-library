## 37. The Road Not Closed

The first frost of the season had come down off the peaks overnight, and Tamsin woke to it silvering the mouth of her tent like breath on glass.

She lay still a moment, listening. That was the habit now, and would be for the rest of her life: listening past the first layer of sound to the second one beneath. Wind in the scree. A marmot's alarm, two ridges east. And beneath both, faint as a pulse through a wall, the Choir — the covenant's slow harmony running under the bones of Thornfell, steady as water in a deep well. Six months since the Deepwell, and she still caught herself holding her breath to hear it, the way she'd once held her breath before tasting a suspect dish.

Old habits. Old tongue.

She crawled out into grey light and found Brannoc already up, feeding the fire with larch cones that popped and spat resin.

"You're really going," he said. Not a question. He'd known since the council at Marrowgate had voted to establish courier routes between the realms, and known longer than that, probably, from watching her pack.

"Someone has to map them properly." She crouched by the fire and warmed her hands. "The fae crossings shift with the moon. If we don't chart them, some smuggler's going to walk into the verge on the wrong night and come out forty years old or forty years dead."

"And nobody charts a road like a Thackeray." Brannoc grunted, which was his way of saying most things. "Your father walked these passes with me when we were both stupid and young. He'd have liked this. You, walking them free."

Tamsin didn't answer right away. Free was still a word she was learning to hold without checking its edges. "I'll be back before the deep snow."

"I know." He handed her a wrapped parcel — bread, hard cheese, a jar of Wren's wintergreen salve that someone had thought to save. "Take the high route past Grey Crag. The tollhouse stands, if you want shelter. And Tamsin—" He hesitated, which he almost never did. "You don't have to do this alone. That was rather the point of everything."

"I know," she said. "That's why I can."

* * *

The passes were different now, and not only because of the frost.

Every crossing between realms had a texture to it, she'd learned. The verge bled through at certain places — a fold of shadow under an overhang, a stretch of air that tasted faintly of honey and cold iron, a pool that reflected stars on a cloudless noon. Before the Choir was reforged, those places had been wounds, leaking. Now they were doors, and doors could be mapped.

She traveled light: rope, rations, ink and good paper, a courier's satchel stamped with the twin sigil of the free cities and the Ash Queen's ash-leaf. At each crossing she stopped and did the work. She measured the width of the threshold. She noted the phase of the moon it favored. She sang a single low note — her voice had steadied over the months, though it would never be Merrin's — and listened to how the stone answered, and wrote down what it answered *with*, because the notation was Merrin's gift and the ear was hers, and between them they were building something no realm had possessed since before the breaking: an atlas of the in-between.

On the fourth day, at the crossing above the Larkwater falls, she found fresh tracks on the human side. Small boots, then bare feet, then small boots again — someone crossing and recrossing, testing. A child's stride.

She followed them a half-mile to where they ended at a shepherd boy, maybe ten years old, sitting on a rock and staring at the verge-fold with his whole face.

"It talks," he said, when he noticed her. Not startled. Children who lived near the crossings had stopped being startled months ago; they'd simply grown up in a world with two kinds of neighbors. "It hums at me. Nobody believes me."

"It's not talking *at* you," Tamsin said. She sat down beside him on the rock, deliberately unhurried. "It's singing *with* you. There's a difference. Can you hear what note it wants?"

He frowned, concentrated. Hummed something thin and wavering.

"There," she said, when the fold in the air brightened faintly. "You hear it. Most people need teaching. Some don't." She took out her book and made a mark in the margin — a note for Merrin's conservatory, a name to send for. "What's your name?"

"Davy."

"Well, Davy. In Marrowgate there's a school where they teach exactly this. When I come back through, there'll be a letter for your mother. Fair warning: she'll cry."

He grinned, gap-toothed, and Tamsin walked on feeling something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't joy exactly. It was older than joy. It was the feeling of a door opening because someone wanted it open.

* * *

The tollhouse looked smaller than she remembered.

That was wrong, probably. Places didn't shrink. But she'd first seen it bleeding, with an inquisitor's blade in her side and Sef screaming behind her, and every place seen that way grew enormous afterward. Now it stood quiet in the late afternoon light, its stone walls patched where Brannoc's people had repaired them, its iron gate hanging open because nothing on this road needed locking anymore.

This was where they'd shed first blood for her. Where the drugged blade had met a body it couldn't put down. Where guilt and resolve had first twined together in her chest and never fully untwined since.

And this was where she'd promised herself she'd stop, on the way back. She'd carried two things up the mountain for this exact day, and she took them out of her satchel now and sat with them on the tollhouse step while the sun went long and amber across the valley.

The first was a small clay jar, sealed with wax. Wren's ashes. They'd burned her in the fae manner, on a pyre of rowan and juniper, because Wren had been hedge-witch enough to want both worlds' customs and stubborn enough to say so while dying. Her last instruction about her ashes had been characteristically practical: *"Bury me somewhere with a view of a road. Roads are the only prayers that ever worked."*

The second was a signet ring. Black iron, worn smooth, stamped with a coiled serpent — the mark of the king's spymaster. Aldous's ring. She'd taken it off his hand herself at the Deepwell, after his song had bought her the opening bars with his life, after the covenant had taken him mid-note the way it takes everyone who gives themselves wholly to it. She had meant, at the time, to throw it into the deepest crevasse in Thornfell.

She hadn't. She'd carried it instead, month after month, telling herself she'd know what to do with it when the time came.

She turned it in her fingers now. The serpent caught the low sun.

"He taught me everything," she said aloud, to the empty road, because some things needed saying out loud or they went septic. "How to read a poison by its smell. How to lie with my face. How to survive. And he sold me before I ever knew I was for sale, and then he died buying me back, and I don't—" Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I don't know what to do with that. I don't think I'm supposed to know. I think I'm just supposed to carry it."

The Choir hummed beneath the stone, indifferent and patient. Somewhere in it, she sometimes thought, she could hear a bass line that might be him. Might be wishful thinking. The dead didn't join the covenant; the covenant had simply been built on their bones, all of theirs, the slaughtered singers and the poisoned tasters and the old king's forgotten promises.

She dug the grave at the eastern corner of the tollhouse, where the wall broke the worst of the wind and the morning sun would reach it first. Two feet down, the frost giving way to softer earth. She set the clay jar in first, and then, after a long moment, the ring beside it — serpent-side up, so that if anything of him lingered, it would see the sky.

"Wren," she said, filling the grave, "you get the view. You earned it. Aldous — you get the company. Whether you deserve it is between you and whatever's under the mountain."

She packed the earth flat and set a stone on top, and then, because Wren would have wanted it and Aldous would have hated it, she picked a sprig of juniper growing wild by the gate and laid it on the cairn.

Then she sat on the step until full dark came down, and let herself grieve them properly — not the versions she'd needed to hate or forgive, but the actual people, the witch who'd taught her rites and the spy who'd taught her survival, both of whom had loved her badly and usefully and too late. Grief, she was learning, wasn't a thing you finished. It was a thing you carried lighter, year by year, until it fit in your pocket beside everything else.

When the moon rose she sang the low note over the grave, and the stone answered, and she wrote the answer down.

* * *

She was three days from Marrowgate when the second voice reached her.

She'd camped at the Larkwater crossing again, mapping its autumn alignment, when the harmony beneath the ground shifted — a new thread entering the weave, high and clear and unmistakably fae, coming down out of the northern peaks where no crossing was charted.

Then a second thread joined it. Human. Rough, untrained, but certain.

Then a third, braiding the two together, and Tamsin sat bolt upright in her bedroll with her heart hammering, because a third voice braiding mortal and fae lines was supposed to be *her*. That was the bridge-line. That was the thing the whole covenant hung on, and there was exactly one bridge alive, and she was lying in a valley fifty miles south of whoever was singing.

Unless—

She was moving before she finished the thought, striking camp in the dark, taking the high route north at a pace she hadn't used since fleeing Corvane's inquisitors. The threads strengthened as she climbed. By dawn she could hear words in them, fragments — and the words were wrong. Not covenant-song. Something older, rougher, sung in a language the Ash Queen's court had sworn was extinct.

A language the carvings in the Hollow Court had shown, in the mouths of singers who predated the covenant itself.

Whoever stood at the source of that song had been singing since before the Choir was reforged. Since before the breaking. Maybe since before either realm had names.

Tamsin crested the ridge at sunrise and stopped.

Below her, in a cirque of black stone that appeared on none of her maps, stood a figure robed in mountain-grey, singing to a gathering of lights that drifted up out of a fold in the air — dozens of them, scores, fae-lights rising like sparks off a bonfire, drawn to the voice. And the figure, sensing her, stopped mid-phrase and turned.

Its face was old beyond old. Its eyes were the color of the space between stars.

It looked at Tamsin — at the bridge, the living hinge of the covenant — and smiled with terrible gentleness, and spoke in the common tongue as if it had been waiting centuries for the chance:

"At last. We wondered which of you would hear us first."
