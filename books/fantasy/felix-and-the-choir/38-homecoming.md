## 38. Homecoming

The road into Marrowgate smelled of woodsmoke and new mortar.

Tamsin stopped at the crest of the last hill, the way she always did at thresholds — old habit from a life spent tasting other people's intentions before she swallowed them. Below her, the city spread along the river in its patched and stubborn way: scaffolding on the burned quarter, tarps over half-finished roofs, the salt gate rebuilt wider than it had been because Brannoc had argued that a city which had nearly died should learn to let more of the world in.

A year ago she had fled through that gate with a stolen boat and a man who sang like a broken bell and a child who heard voices under mountains. She had looked back once, at the smoke of the inquisitors' boats, and thought she would never see it whole again.

She had been wrong about a great many things. It was one of the year's few gifts.

"You're going to stand there until dark," said Sef beside her, "and then you're going to say something like *I was just taking in the view*, and everyone will pretend to believe you."

"I was taking in the view."

"You were stalling." Sef was fourteen now, taller by a hand's width, their fae-blood showing more openly these days — a faint silver at the temples, eyes that caught lamplight like a cat's. They had grown up in a season when growing up fast was the only kind available. But they still grinned like the dock-rat Tamsin had pulled out of a burning tavern, and they still saw straight through her, every time.

"Fine," Tamsin said. "I'm stalling."

"Why? It's your city now. Everyone says so. The ballad says so. There are *verses* about you, Tam. Verses about your *tongue*."

"There are verses about my tongue," Tamsin agreed grimly, "because a dying man bled on my boots and I never learned to say no to a map."

But she didn't move yet. Because there was a thing under the stalling, and Sef deserved to hear it before the gate swallowed them both.

"A year ago," she said, "if someone had opened a door for me, I'd have checked it for poison first. Checked the hinges for wires. Checked the person holding it for what they wanted out of me. That's not a flaw, that's training. Twenty years of training. You don't unlearn it because a mountain sang at you."

Sef waited. They had learned patience from Wren, back when Wren still had breath to teach it, and they wore it now the way she'd worn her scars.

"And now?" Sef asked.

"Now I'm standing on a hill trying to remember how to walk through a door like a person who expects it to stay open." Tamsin exhaled. "That's all. That's the speech. Don't put it in a verse."

"Too late," said Sef. "It rhymes beautifully."

She cuffed them lightly across the ear, and they came down off the hill together, into the smell of bread and river and rebuilding.

* * *

The gate guards knew her. That was the first strange thing.

Not as the king's taster — there was no king anymore, not since the accord, and the council that governed in the crown's absence had voted to retire the office of food-taster entirely, on the grounds that it had been invented for murder and should die with its maker. Not as the fugitive whose face had once been chalked on every wall from Marrowgate to the coast. Not even as the woman from the ballad, though the ballad was everywhere, sung in taverns and schoolrooms and, to Merrin's endless professional agony, badly.

They knew her as Tamsin Thackeray, who had come home.

"Hallo, mistress," said the younger guard, a girl with a burn scar down one arm — one of the survivors from the sack, Tamsin realized, one of the faces from the column of refugees she had led into the passes. "Brannoc said you'd come by the coast road. He's had the kettle on since dawn."

"He's had the kettle on since dawn," Tamsin repeated.

"He says tea keeps," the guard said solemnly, "but readiness doesn't."

The gate stood open. Nobody searched her bags. Nobody asked her business. A door, opening because someone on the other side wanted her there, and the world continuing to turn regardless.

She walked through it and made herself feel it — the whole weight of it, the strangeness, the small terrifying luxury of being expected.

* * *

Marrowgate had learned to wear its wounds openly.

That had been Merrin's doing, mostly, though he'd have denied it with his dying breath. When the conservatory rose from the ashes of the dockside quarter, he had refused the council's offer of fresh-cut stone. Build with the burned beams where they could bear weight, he'd insisted. Leave the scorch marks on the ones that couldn't. A voice that has been silenced shouldn't pretend it never went quiet; a building shouldn't either. The children who studied there now learned their scales under rafters that remembered fire, and if any of them found that frightening, Merrin taught them the note that breaks fear — the one Old Wren had used, the one Tamsin had carried out of the Deepwell still ringing in her bones.

She heard the conservatory before she saw it. Of course she did. Her hearing had never gone back to being merely human after the covenant took hold; she lived now with both realms in her ears, the mortal street-noise braided with the fainter music underneath — the hum the land made when it was content, the long slow chord of the mountain two valleys east, where the Deepwell kept its light.

Underneath the ordinary sounds of the city: forty-odd children singing rounds.

Her chest did something complicated. She followed the sound the way she'd once followed the map's notation, without needing to think about it, through streets she was learning to know again, past the tavern where she and Merrin had first read each other's damage across a barroom table, past the shrine the fishwives had raised to Wren — a simple stone with a wren carved on it and a dish for offerings that was always full, because hedge-witches who died buying a city time got remembered whether they'd have wanted it or not.

Merrin was in the doorway.

He must have heard the gate, or felt her coming the way he sometimes claimed he could — *your footsteps have a key signature, Tam, I'm not making it up* — and there he stood, leaning against the frame with his arms crossed, grey threading his temples now, the disgraced court singer of a dead king's reign looking for all the world like a man who had never been anything but exactly where he meant to be.

"You're late," he said.

"The coast road was flooded."

"You're three days late and you hate the coast road and you're going to tell me later, over tea, the real reason, which is that you stopped at the tollhouse again." His voice was gentle under the dryness. He always knew. It was infuriating and it was the thing she loved most about him, and she had told him so exactly once, a month ago, on this same doorstep, and neither of them had needed to say it since.

"I buried some ash," she said. "And I talked to a stone."

"To Aldous?"

"To Aldous." She had taken to telling the signet-ring's grave the news, the way sailors talked to drowned men. *The accord held through winter. Corvane rots in a cell he can't sing his way out of. Your students' students are learning the second voice.* It wasn't forgiveness. Some debts don't resolve into forgiveness; they resolve into understanding, which is colder and lasts longer. But she had stopped hating him somewhere on the road, the way a fever breaks, and what remained was grief with the poison drawn out of it — grief for the man who had taught her everything except how to be loved, and who had finally, at the Deepwell's mouth, paid for that failure with everything he had.

Merrin stepped aside from the doorway. "Come in, then. You're letting the draft in, and Brannoc will claim it ruined the tea."

* * *

The hall was full of children, and the children exploded.

They came off the risers in a wave — human children and fae-blooded children and one or two, Tamsin noted with a pang, who were neither and both, the first generation born under the restored covenant, kids whose ears worked in both worlds the way hers did now, permanently, incompletely, hers. They swarmed her the way they swarmed anyone the ballad mentioned, tugging at her sleeves, demanding to know if the Deepwell really blazed, if the Ash Queen was really seven feet tall (she was, but only when she wished to be), if Tamsin had really tasted poison every day for twenty years and never once died.

"Once a week, usually," Tamsin said. "Dying is mostly a matter of dose."

"That's *horrifying*," said a small boy with delight all over his face.

"It's a profession," Tamsin said. "Most professions are horrifying if you describe them honestly."

Sef had vanished into the crowd the way they always did, adopted instantly by the older students, already deep in some argument about notation — Sef had become the conservatory's authority on fae-line harmonics, a title they wielded with the ruthlessness of a customs official. Tamsin watched them go and felt the old ache that was also, these days, mostly pride: the child who had been hunted for what they were, teaching children to be proud of the same.

Merrin appeared at her elbow with two cups of tea that were definitely not Brannoc's, and steered her toward the side of the hall, away from the swarm.

"Well?" he said quietly. "How was the pass?"

"Quiet. The new crossings hold. The warding-song needs re-tuning at the third marker — the stone's settling faster than we estimated, Wren's calculations were close but the mountain has opinions." He smiled at that; they all still spoke of Wren's calculations as if she might correct them from wherever she was, and privately none of them had ruled it out. "The fae side of the tollhouse wants a proper envoy stationed there. Half-year rotations. The Queen won't say it outright, but she's tired of dealing with smugglers as intermediaries."

"The Queen is tired of dealing with smugglers," Merrin repeated. "The woman who knighted Brannoc."

"She didn't knight him. She gave him a ring and threatened him."

"Same thing, in Brannoc's cosmology."

Tamsin drank her tea. Around them the hall filled back up with sound, Merrin whistling the children to order with two fingers, the round starting over — and she listened, as she always listened now, for the second voice beneath the first. It was there. It was always there now, in Marrowgate most of all: the faint fae harmony threading under the children's song, notes no purely human ear could catch, the covenant audible in miniature every time these children opened their mouths. Merrin had built a school. He had also, without ever saying so, built a choir that rehearsed the world's repair twice a week between meals.

"You're staring at me with an expression," Merrin murmured, mid-verse.

"I'm having a thought."

"Dangerous. Share it."

"I keep waiting for it to close," she said. "The door. Every door. Twenty years of watching hinges. And it doesn't close, and I keep standing in it like a fool, waiting."

Merrin was quiet for a moment. Then, softly, in the space between the children's phrases, he sang four notes — the opening of the bridge-line, her line, the one her poisoned blood had carried and her untrained voice had learned too late and too hard. The line that was hers forever now, written into the covenant, humming faintly in her veins on quiet nights.

"That's the cure," he said. "You don't wait for doors to stay open. You write yourself into the house." A pause. "Also, Brannoc will be insufferable that you arrived while I was teaching. He wanted to be first at the gate. He's been rehearsing a greeting since Tuesday."

"He hasn't."

"He has. It involves lifting you off the ground. I'd brace yourself."

* * *

Brannoc did lift her off the ground.

He met them at the tavern — his tavern now, rebuilt on the old foundations, the sign repainted with a badger instead of the old crossed keys because, he maintained, keys implied lockpicking and he was a legitimate businessman these days, a claim no one in Marrowgate had managed to say with a straight face since. He came through the crowd like weather, beard shot through with more grey than last spring, and gathered her up in arms like forge-tongs and held her there until her ribs complained.

"Three days," he rumbled into her hair. "Three days, and the kettle on since dawn, and do you know what I get? A note from a gate guard. *Mistress Thackeray is come.* I trained that girl myself and that's the message she sends me."

"You could have come to the gate."

"And miss the look on your face when you walked in expecting nobody?" He set her down and held her at arm's length, examining her the way he examined cargo — for damage, for smuggling, for lies. Whatever he found seemed to satisfy him. "You look terrible. Coast road agrees with you about as much as it agrees with anyone. Sit. Eat. Sef, sit. Merrin, stop conducting the kitchen, they can't hear you over the soup."

The tavern was full and loud and warm, and Tamsin sat in the middle of it with her family around a table — Brannoc booming, Sef arguing harmonics with a conservatory student twice their age, Merrin quietly stealing bread off her plate with the practiced ease of a man who had never once in his life bought his own dinner — and she let herself feel the thing she had circled for a year without naming.

She had been raised to be useful. Useful things get kept. That was the whole of the philosophy Master Aldous had fed her, spoonful by spoonful, alongside the poisons: be valuable, and you will be safe. And she had believed it, right up until the day she learned what value meant to the people who held the ledger — that they would spend her blood like coin, that usefulness was just another word for owned.

What she had now was different, and it had taken her a year of roads and graves and gates to learn the difference. Nobody at this table needed her to be useful. Brannoc would love her if she never mapped another pass. Sef would love her if she lost every note of the bridge-line. Merrin — Merrin had watched her fail at singing for a full season with the patience of a saint and the commentary of a devil, and had never once, not once, made her feel that her worth was conditional on the next verse.

The door stayed open because they wanted it open. That was all. That was everything.

"You've gone quiet," Sef observed, kicking her under the table. "Thinking-quiet. Bad-quiet or good-quiet?"

"Good-quiet," Tamsin said, and found, testing the words as she said them, that they were true. "I was just thinking that I used to check every room for exits. This one I checked for chairs."

Brannoc snorted into his ale. "Progress," he declared. "The taster has discovered furniture. Next she'll learn holidays."

"I'll learn holidays when the third crossing is warded."

"The third crossing," said Sef, "can ward itself for a fortnight, and you're staying for the festival, and that's final, the whole city's talking about it—"

"Festival?"

"Marrowgate Rising," Merrin said. "First anniversary of the sack. We decided — the council and I — that we'd rather mark what survived than what burned. There's to be singing in the square. All of it. Every choir in the free cities sent word they're coming, and the fae delegation arrives tomorrow, and the Queen herself—" he paused, savoring it, "—has requested, in writing, formally, through diplomatic channels, that Brannoc not be permitted to give a speech."

"I'm giving a speech," Brannoc said.

"You are not giving a speech."

"I've *written* a speech."

"Gods preserve us," Merrin said, "it rhymes."

And Tamsin sat back and laughed — really laughed, the unguarded kind, the kind that cost nothing and was worth everything — and outside the tavern window the evening came down soft over the rebuilt city, and beneath the lamplight and the argument and the clatter of dishes she could hear it still, faint and steady as a heartbeat: the second voice, under the first, holding.

Tomorrow the Queen would arrive. Tomorrow the square would fill with every voice the free cities could muster, and the fae lines would answer from the verge, and the covenant would be sung in the open air for the first time in a generation — not in a hidden well beneath a mountain, but in a street, among people, where it belonged.

Tonight there was bread, and family, and a chair that was hers.

She intended to enjoy it while it lasted. She had learned, at last, the difference between dread and wisdom — and wisdom, she was fairly sure, allowed for one good night's sleep.

She was almost right.
