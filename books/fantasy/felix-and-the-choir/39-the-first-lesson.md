## 39. The First Lesson

The conservatory had been a granary once, and before that — if Brannoc's stories were to be believed — a smuggler's warehouse where casks of Thornfell brandy waited out the customs men behind false walls. You could still see the seams of it: the bricked-over loading arch at the back, the iron rings in the beams where pulleys had hung, the floorboards worn pale in long diagonal lines where barrels had been rolled, decade after decade, by people whose names no one remembered.

Tamsin remembered now. That was the strange arithmetic of survival — that she carried more dead people's names than living ones, and every year the ledger tipped further.

She stood in the morning light that came slanting through the new windows — Merrin had insisted on glass, real glass, paid for with the first coin the free cities had sent after the accord — and looked out at twenty-three children who were not looking back at her.

They were looking at each other.

That was the whole lesson, really, though it would take her the better part of an hour to say so in a way they'd believe.

Half the class was human. Half was fae. The fae children sat on the left, mostly, because fae children arrived together and arranged themselves like a flock deciding which way the wind blew; the human children sat scattered, because human children sat wherever their mothers shoved them. Between the two groups ran three feet of clean pine floor that might as well have been the Deepwell itself for all the crossing it got.

One girl — human, freckled, maybe eight, with the stubborn set of a child who'd already decided what she thought about everything — kept glancing sideways at the boy beside her. He was fae, willow-thin, with ears that tapered to fine points and skin that held a faint green undertone in certain lights, like leaves seen from beneath. His name was Ilex. Tamsin knew this because Sef had told her, in the conspiratorial whisper Sef reserved for intelligence about other children, *she keeps looking at him but she won't talk to him, he thinks she hates him, she doesn't hate him, she thinks his ears are pretty.*

Sef was fourteen now, or claimed to be; with fae-blooded children you took their word for it and adjusted later. They sat at the back of the hall these days, too old for lessons and pretending not to attend them, sprawled against the wall with a lute across their knees that they never actually played when anyone was watching. Tamsin let them pretend. Everyone needed one place where they could be present without being counted.

"Right," Tamsin said. "Hands."

Twenty-three pairs of hands came up, some eagerly, some grudgingly, one — a small fae boy named Alder — raised only after checking whether anyone else had.

"Put them down. That wasn't a question. It's just easier to start a class when everyone's doing the same wrong thing."

A ripple of laughter. Even the left side of the room. Good.

"Yesterday," she said, "Merrin taught you rounds. Row upon row, voice over voice, everyone singing the same thing at different times. Today we're going backwards. Today I want you to stop singing entirely."

Confusion, beautifully uniform. Twenty-three faces doing the same arithmetic: a music lesson with no music in it.

"Here is what I know about listening," Tamsin said, and began to walk the length of the hall, slow, hands loose at her sides. "I spent nineteen years being paid to listen. Not to music — to food. To wine, to broth, to bread, to anything the king might put in his mouth that someone wanted him dead over. And I'll tell you the secret of that trade, since apparently I tell secrets in classrooms now." She stopped. "The poison is almost never the loud thing. The loud thing is the saffron, the pepper, the honey — all the flavors shouting at you, *look at me, look at me.* The poison hides underneath. Quiet. Patient. Waiting for you to be satisfied with the first taste."

She let the silence sit. Children were better with silence than adults gave them credit for; they lived inside it most of the time, waiting for grown-ups to finish.

"Music is the same," she said. "Every song has a second voice under the first. Sometimes it's another singer. Sometimes it's the room itself — the way stone holds a note after your throat has quit. Sometimes—" and here she paused, because this part was true and heavy and she made herself carry it anyway, "—sometimes it's something older than either. But it's always there. And the world breaks, again and again, whenever people forget to listen for it."

At the back, Sef's hand stilled on the lute strings.

"So. First exercise. Close your eyes."

* * *

They closed their eyes. All except one fae girl who didn't trust the room yet — fair enough, Tamsin thought; there had been years when she wouldn't have trusted it either — and Tamsin knelt by her desk rather than make an example of her, and said quietly, "You can watch. Watchers hear different things. Tell me later what they are."

Then she listened to twenty-three children breathing.

It was, she reflected, the closest thing to peace her life had ever contained. Not silence — she'd had silence, in garrets and prison cells and mountain passes, and silence was just the absence of threat. This was presence. The small wet sounds of children trying very hard to be still. A nose being rubbed. The creak of a bench under a shifting weight. Outside, Marrowgate going about its rebuilt business: cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife's cry two streets over, hammers from the east quarter where they were raising the new market hall on foundations that had been ash four years ago.

"Tell me what you hear," she said. "Not the loudest thing. The thing under it."

"A cart," said the freckled girl, eyes shut tight as if the effort might crack her face.

"The cart's the first voice. What's under it?"

Silence. Then, slowly: "...The wheel squeaks? Only on the left side?"

"There it is. Second voice. Someone should tell that carter his axle's dying." A laugh went around the room, and something loosened in it. "Next."

"My own heart," said Alder, alarmed, as though his heart had been hiding there without permission.

"Good. Yours has been there your whole life and you've never once thanked it. Next."

"I hear the sea," said a human boy near the window, doubtful. "But we're nowhere near the sea."

"You're hearing the wind through the harbor streets. It carries salt and it carries sound, and your ears can't tell the difference yet. Give them time." Tamsin turned. "Ilex. What do you hear?"

The willow-thin boy opened his mouth, closed it. His ears — the ones the freckled girl thought were pretty — moved slightly, the way a deer's do, swiveling toward something the rest of the room couldn't find.

"Under the floor," he said finally. "There's... water? Old water. It goes *down*, somewhere. Like a big empty jar underground."

Tamsin felt the hair rise on her arms and kept her voice level. "Anyone else?"

Nothing. Twenty-two heads shaking, or eyes still closed and frowning with effort.

"That's exactly right," she said to Ilex, and meant six different things by it. "Fae ears hear down. Human ears hear across. Which is why—" she clapped once, sharp, and the class flinched pleasantly, "—which is why neither of you can sing the covenant alone. Down without across is a well with no bucket. Across without down is a road to nowhere. Together—"

"Together it's the Choir," said Sef from the back, flat, and the temperature of the room changed.

The children knew the story. Of course they did; every child in the free cities knew the story, had been sung versions of it in cradles and schoolrooms, some accurate, some absurd, one — the one Merrin was writing, verse by verse, and refusing to show anyone until it was finished — presumably truest of all. They knew that a taster and a singer and a smuggler and a witch and a child had gone up the mountain, and that the mountain had sung back, and that Duke Corvane's army had broken like a wave on rock that had decided, after a thousand years, to be a cliff.

What the songs didn't say — what Tamsin had asked Merrin, gently, firmly, to leave out — was how much of it had been luck, and terror, and Wren dying with her wards lit like a last candle, and Aldous standing at the Deepwell's mouth with his mortal line held steady in a voice that shook, buying thirty seconds with his life. The ballads wanted heroes. The truth had been people, frightened, doing the next necessary thing. Tamsin had decided that children could have the ballad version for comfort and grow into the true one for use.

"Yes," she said now. "Together, it's the Choir. So today, we practice being together. Pairs. One from each side of the room — yes, I see the looks, no, the plague isn't coming back, sit *down*. One of you sings any note you like. Your partner listens underneath it and finds the note the room makes in answer. Don't sing it. Just find it. When you've found it, raise your hand, and we'll see if you're right."

Chaos, briefly. The eternal chaos of children pairing off, negotiations, betrayals, one boy abandoned mid-sentence by a partner with better options. Tamsin watched it with the patience of someone who had watched poisoners at a banquet table and found this considerably less dangerous.

And then, gradually, wonderfully, the noise resolved into something else.

It started ragged. A human girl droning a tuneless C while her fae partner winced. Two boys giggling. The freckled girl — Petra, Sef had named her too — paired at last with Ilex, sitting rigidly apart, both staring straight ahead, singing nothing, because neither would go first.

Tamsin drifted over. "Problem?"

"He wants me to sing first," said Petra.

"She won't sing first," said Ilex.

"And the covenant nearly died of exactly this," Tamsin said dryly, "so one of you had better be braver than the courts of two realms combined." She crouched to their level. "Petra. Sing anything. Truly anything. Bad is fine. I have heard kings sing. Nothing you do today will ever be that bad."

Petra sang. It was a nursery scrap, three notes, wobbly, delivered at the volume of someone apologizing.

Ilex's ears swiveled. His whole body went still in that fae way, the stillness of a creature listening with its bones. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Then his lips parted and — barely, breathily, not quite a sound so much as the shape of one — he answered, a third below her, and the two notes met in the air between them and *locked*, and the granary's old beams picked the interval up and hummed it back, faint as memory, and both children's eyes went wide as coins.

"It buzzed," Petra whispered. "The floor buzzed."

"That's the second voice," Tamsin said. "Say hello."

* * *

By the end of the hour, half the pairs had found it — that moment when two voices stopped being two and became a space between, and the building itself leaned in. The other half were laughing too hard to listen, which Tamsin considered an acceptable intermediate stage. She dismissed them with the promise that tomorrow they'd try finding the second voice in each other's speaking voices, which was harder and infinitely funnier, and stood in the doorway watching them spill into the street — human and fae children, still mostly in their separate flocks, but Ilex and Petra walked out together, arguing about whose turn it was to sing first tomorrow, and Tamsin decided she could retire on that alone.

"They'll talk about this class for forty years, you know."

Merrin. Leaning in the far doorway where he'd been for who knew how long, arms crossed, that look on his face he probably thought was neutral and wasn't. Four years had silvered him at the temples and settled him into himself; the dockside tavern wariness had burned off him like fog, leaving someone quieter and larger somehow, a man who had gotten his voice back and then, deliberately, given most of it away to children.

"They'll talk about how you made them sit in silence for ten minutes and called it music," Tamsin said. "History is cruel to teachers."

"History's cruel to everyone. That's why we get students." He pushed off the doorframe and came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, in the way that had become their native language — closeness without demand, offered daily, accepted daily, never once made into a debt. "The bridge exercise. Where did you learn that?"

"Nowhere. I invented it this morning, washing dishes." She shrugged. "Turns out nineteen years of tasting teaches you that everything interesting is underneath. I just finally found a use for it that doesn't involve vomiting."

He laughed — full, unguarded, the laugh she'd coaxed out of him over months and treasured like contraband. Then it faded into something more careful.

"Sef stayed for the whole lesson," he said.

"I noticed."

"They haven't sat through a lesson since they aged out. They sit at that wall like a gargoyle with a lute." He glanced at her. "Today they watched every pair. Tracked every note. And when Alder couldn't find his partner's answer-note, Sef hummed it — quiet, from the back, just loud enough. Three times."

Tamsin looked out at the emptying hall, at the benches, at the back wall where a lute-shaped shadow had recently been.

"They're thinking about teaching," she said.

"They're thinking about *something*. With Sef it's always seven things at once." Merrin hesitated, and she heard the hesitation for what it was: a man choosing his words like steps across ice. "There's something else. I finished it. Last night. The ballad."

Tamsin went still.

Four years he'd worked on it. Four years of scraps and revisions, verses written and burned, the melody changing every winter as if the song itself were growing the way children grow, awkward then sudden then all at once. He'd shown her fragments — never the whole, never the ending. *Not until it's true,* he'd said, every time. *A ballad lies by leaving things out, and I keep leaving out the parts I can't bear.*

"You're sure?" she asked. "It's true?"

"It's true." He said it simply, the way he said the important things. "All of it. Even the parts about Aldous. Especially those." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that his hands — the beautiful, ruined, court-trained hands — were trembling slightly. "I want to sing it for the first time where it belongs. In this hall. To them. Tomorrow, at the end of your lesson. If you'll allow it."

"Allow it," Tamsin repeated. "Merrin. You don't need my permission to sing in your own conservatory."

"Not yours as headmistress." His voice dropped. "Yours as the one it's about. There's a verse in it, Tam. Near the end. About the taster. What she was made to be, and what she chose to be instead, and—" he stopped, jaw working, "—and I wrote it the way I heard it that night at the Deepwell, when your blood was burning and the mountain was listening, and I need you to have heard it before twenty-three children do. Because if it's wrong — if I got you wrong — I'd rather die privately."

She looked at him for a long moment. This man who had refused to sing for a tyrant and lost everything, and had spent four years rebuilding not his career but a *place* — a granary with glass windows where fae and human children learned to listen underneath. Who loved her in the key of patience. Who was asking her, essentially, to approve her own legend, knowing what legends cost.

"What's it called?" she asked. "The ballad."

He smiled, and there was something old and sad and bright in it, like light off snow.

"Felix and the Choir," he said. "His name was Felix, Tam. The stranger at the salt gate. I checked the palace records — Aldous kept them too well, even in treason. A courier in the old king's service, carrying the map south to find whoever could finish what he'd started. He never learned your name. He gave the map to the first person who knelt down to help him." Merrin's voice roughened. "I think he'd have liked knowing it worked."

Tamsin turned back to the hall. Morning light lay across the worn floorboards in long pale bars, and somewhere under the floor, she could hear it now — Ilex hadn't imagined it — the old water moving in its dark jar beneath the city, patient as a held note, waiting for the next voice to come down and meet it.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "After the lesson. Sing it true, Merrin. They'll carry it longer than we will."

Outside, faintly, from the direction of the harbor streets, a cart went by with its left wheel squeaking — and beneath it, clear as a bell to anyone who had learned to listen, the second voice of Marrowgate rising to greet the day.
