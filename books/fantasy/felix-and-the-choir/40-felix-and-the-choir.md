## 40. Felix and the Choir

The conservatory hall had been a granary once, back before Marrowgate burned and was rebuilt around its own ashes. Tamsin could still see the old grain-chutes in the ceiling where they'd been left for honesty's sake — Merrin's idea, though he'd never admit it was sentiment. *Let them look up,* he'd said, *and remember what we saved.* The chutes were strung now with lanterns and paper birds that Sef folded by the dozen, wings dipped in fae-silver so they caught the light like minnows.

She stood at the door of the teaching hall the way she used to stand at the king's table: listening first.

Twenty years of practice had made her hearing into something the old spymaster would have called a weapon and the Ash Queen called a gift and Tamsin herself had long since stopped calling anything at all. It was simply how she moved through the world. She heard the children before she saw them — forty of them this season, human and fae-blooded and everything between, shuffling on benches built for grown smugglers, whispering, giggling, one small voice in the back practicing scales under her breath because she was nervous and thought no one could hear.

Tamsin heard. She always heard. But she let the girl have her secret.

Merrin stood at the front, silver threading his temples now, his voice still the finest in three realms when he chose to use it and rough as dockside tar when he didn't. He caught her eye across the room and did not stop the chatter, because the lesson hadn't started yet and he believed — with the stubbornness of a man who had once been silenced and never forgot it — that noise was a kind of health.

"You're late," he said, without heat.

"I'm exactly on time," Tamsin said. "You're early. You're always early. You've been early since the day I met you bleeding behind a tavern."

"Someone has to be." He stepped aside, ceding the floor to her the way he always did for this particular lesson, and took up his place against the wall with his arms crossed, watching from the doorway's shadow like a man guarding something he refused to name.

Tamsin walked to the center of the hall and did not raise her voice. She never had to anymore.

"Sit down," she said quietly, and forty children sat as if the floor had been pulled out from under them gently.

* * *

The lesson was always the same, at least at the start. It was the lesson that had saved everything, and Merrin said a teacher should teach the thing that matters most first and decorate afterward.

"Close your eyes," Tamsin told them. "All of you. Yes, even you, Pip — if you keep peeking I'll know, and I will know, because I hear everything, which is rather the point of today."

Giggles. Then quiet. Then the deep, fidgeting quiet of forty children trying very hard to be still, which is its own kind of music if you listen right.

"Now," she said. "Listen to the room. Tell me what you hear."

"The wind," said a fae-blooded boy near the window. "The birds outside."

"The birds are sparrows arguing about the gutter," said another child. "There's a cart in the square. Two wheels, heavy load, the left axle squeaks."

"Good. That's the first voice. The world on top. Now go deeper. Under the cart and the sparrows and Pip breathing through his nose like a startled horse—"

More giggles. Pip went scarlet.

"—there is a second voice. Under every sound there is another sound. Under the wind, the mountain it moves over. Under the mountain, the water in the rock. Under the water—" she paused, and let her own hearing open the way she'd learned to do at the Deepwell, the way that still cost her something every time, a small ache behind the eyes like pressing on a bruise, "—under the water, the song."

The room changed. She felt it change, the way she felt weather change, the way she'd once felt poison change a soup two spoonfuls before anyone else. Forty children stopped being forty separate fidgets and became one held breath.

A girl in the second row — the nervous one, the scale-practicer — raised her hand without opening her eyes. "I hear it," she whispered. "It sounds like... like humming. Like someone humming in the next room."

"That's the Choir," Tamsin said. "It's been humming since before your grandparents were born. Since before mine. And here is the only thing I will ever ask you to memorize, so listen well: the world does not stay whole because of walls or armies or kings. It stays whole because somebody, somewhere, is always singing the second voice. When people forget to listen for it, things break. When people remember—" she shrugged, "—things hold. That's all the covenant ever was. Remembering together."

She opened her eyes. They opened theirs.

"And today," she said, "we learn the ballad. All of it. Every verse. Because it's the anniversary, and because the person it's about is standing right here, and because she is old enough now to enjoy embarrassing herself in public."

"I am not old," Tamsin said.

"You're ancient," said Pip, with the frank cruelty of the young, and Tamsin laughed, because twenty years ago she had not known how to laugh in front of other people, and some days she still marveled at it, the way you marvel at a healed bone.

* * *

They sang it from the beginning, as they always did, and Tamsin sang with them, her voice low and unlovely and steady, because the ballad was not about beauty. Merrin had told her that once, the night they finished writing it. *It's about truth set to a tune so it can't be lost.*

**Oh, Felix came down from the Thornfell high,
with blood on his coat and a map in his hand,
and the salt gate took him as the salt gate must,
and he gave the map to a taster's trust.
Sing low, sing low, the Choir remembers,
sing low, sing low, the Choir remembers.**

The children knew their parts. The fae-blooded ones took the harmony line without being told, the way Sef had once done, the way they all did now — it was in the teaching, woven in, the second voice living under the first even in a tavern ballad. Especially there.

**He said, *the Choir remembers*, and died in the snow,
and the taster who tasted death every day
took the map home and lied to the spy
who taught her her trade and taught her to lie.
Sing low, sing low, the road goes under,
sing low, sing low, the road goes under.**

Tamsin had argued about that verse for a month. *I didn't lie to him out of courage,* she'd said. *I lied out of panic. It wasn't noble.* And Merrin had looked at her with that patient, infuriating tenderness and said, *No. But you kept lying to fear after that, every day, in the direction of hope. That's what the verse means. You just weren't close enough to see it.*

She was close enough now.

**Down the smuggler's road she ran from the crown,
through passes that aren't on any king's map,
to Marrowgate's docks where the singer sang
who wouldn't sing for the man with the black-ink hand.
Sing low, sing low, the broken find each other,
sing low, sing low, the broken find each other.**

Pip sang the next part loudest, because Pip always did, because Brannoc had been his grandfather's friend and Pip considered it a family duty:

**There was Brannoc Broadback with his beard full of frost,
who owed a dead man and paid it in full;
there was Old Wren with her wards worn thin,
who spent her last light to let the song in;
there was little Sef with the stars in their ears
who heard the Choir clear through twenty years.
Sing low, sing low, the company gathers,
sing low, sing low, the company gathers.**

Sef was there today, sitting at the back with the smallest children clustered around them the way ducklings cluster around something calm. Twenty years had turned the runaway child into a tall, quiet adult with silver-shot hair and the best ear in the conservatory, and Sef still folded paper birds, and still heard the Choir more clearly than anyone alive, and still, every year on this day, sat in the back and let the children lean on them.

Sef caught Tamsin's eye during the next verse and did not look away, because neither of them had ever managed to make this verse easy.

**At the Deepwell dark where the dead thrones stand,
where the covenant broke by a traitor's hand,
the Queen of Ash demanded blood for blood:
the taster's heart, whose poison flood
was brewed in a cradle, poured in a cup,
a weapon made before she could walk or sup.
Sing low, sing low, the price is spoken,
sing low, sing low, the price is spoken.**

"But she said no!" shouted a tiny fae-blooded girl, delighted, as she did every year, as if the ending might have changed since last autumn.

"She said no," Tamsin confirmed gravely. "Never die on command. Write that down. It's the second most important thing I'll ever teach you."

"What's the first?" asked Pip.

"Listen for the second voice. Keep up."

* * *

They sang through the middle of it — the tollhouse ambush, the council of the free cities, the burning of Marrowgate, the rescue that failed and succeeded in the same terrible hour, Merrin giving himself to Corvane's choir and singing coerced tenor in a corrupted counter-song until his stolen voice tore free mid-note and walked out of the tyrant's throat like a bird leaving a cage. The children loved that verse best. They acted it out with paper birds. Merrin pretended to be embarrassed and arranged the staging himself.

And then the verses Tamsin always sang with her eyes half-closed, her hands loose at her sides, her breath measured the way she'd once measured poison:

**At the Well's dark mouth where the armies met,
the spy who betrayed her came alone at last,
and Aldous, who taught her tongue and blade,
took up the mortal line and paid
the whole of the price he'd spent her life avoiding,
and bought her the opening bars with his dying.
Sing low, sing low, the last door opens,
sing low, sing low, the last door opens.**

She had buried his signet beside Wren's ashes at the tollhouse, all those years ago, and had gone back twice since, and both times had found wild thyme growing over the stones, which Wren would have found hilarious and Aldous would have found intolerable, and which Tamsin found exactly right.

**Then the taster sang the bridge-line through,
with poison burning her veins like dew
that burns at dawn; and Sef below
carried the fae-line, sweet and slow;
and the freed voices rose from the broken choir,
and the Deepwell blazed like a funeral pyre
that was not an ending but a birth —
and the mountain itself remembered its worth.
Sing high, sing high, the Choir reforged,
sing high, sing high, the Choir reforged.**

Her blood had changed that day. Not cleansed — nothing so simple — but *translated*. What had been engineered as venom became something else, a language her body spoke that both realms could hear. It was why she could stand in a doorway and hear a nervous girl practicing scales and also hear, beneath it, the faint hum of the Deepwell sixty leagues north, keeping time like a heart under the world.

It was also why she belonged fully to neither realm, and why she had needed twenty years to stop minding. Some lessons take longer than wars.

* * *

The ballad ended where it always ended, with the last verse left deliberately unfinished — a rest, a held silence, Merrin's compositional joke and sermon in one. The children knew to stop there. They stopped, and the silence rang, and into it Tamsin stepped forward, as she did every year on this day, to add the verse that was hers.

This was the tradition. This was the whole point. The ballad named Felix, the stranger who started it. The final verse named everyone else — and it was never written down, and it was never the same twice, because the people it honored were not the same either. New names got added. Old names stayed. That was how the Choir worked. That was how remembering worked.

She took a breath, and the hall went absolutely still, forty children leaning forward, and Sef smiling faintly at the back, and Merrin in the doorway with his arms crossed and his eyes bright.

"This year," Tamsin said, "I'm adding two names. Listen, because someday you'll sing this verse to your students, and it will be yours to carry."

She sang it plainly, without ornament, the way she did everything that mattered:

**For the gatekeeper Hesta, who looked at my face
and saw a fugitive, and opened the gate anyway;
for the ferryman Dole, who asked no fare,
who poled us through the inquisitors' flare
and vanished upriver, whistling low —
wherever you drifted, I hope you know:
sing low, sing low, the list grows longer,
sing low, sing low, the list grows longer.**

Two new names. A gatekeeper who had risked her neck at the Marrowgate docks in the bad years and never been sung for until now; a ferryman who had been dead five winters and had once, with a shrug, saved all their lives. Tamsin had found Hesta's granddaughter working in the rebuilt market two weeks ago and promised her this. Promises made to the families of the forgotten were the only taxes she still collected, and she collected them scrupulously.

The children repeated the verse back, as the tradition required, learning the new names into their bones. Then the oldest among them — a gangly human boy with a passable voice and an excellent memory — raised his hand.

"Taster Thackeray? The verse about Felix. The first one. Why him? He's only in one verse. He dies in the first verse. Why is the whole ballad his?"

Tamsin had answered this question many ways over the years. She had given the diplomatic answer, and the tactical answer, and once, tired, the honest-but-unhelpful answer (*because history is unfair, child, and ballads are how we apologize*).

Today she gave the true one.

"Because he was nobody," she said. "That's the whole of it. He wasn't a king or a chosen one. He was a courier — a map-carrier, a runner between worlds, probably hired by someone in the old court who wanted the covenant restored and couldn't do it themselves. He carried a folded piece of hide through the Thornfell passes with inquisitors behind him, and he made it to the salt gate, and he was dying, and he had one job left: put the map in the right hands. He couldn't have known whose hands those would be. He couldn't have known me from any other face in that crowd. He bet everything on a stranger, and he spent his last breath on six words instead of a prayer or a curse." She paused. "*The Choir remembers.* That's what he gave me. Not the map. The words. Everything I did after, I did because a dying stranger trusted me with the most important thing in the world without knowing a single thing about me except that I was there."

She looked around the hall, at the mixed faces, the folded paper birds, the lantern-light in the old grain-chutes.

"So the ballad is his. And the last verse is ours. That's the shape of every good story: somebody starts it who doesn't live to see the end, and everybody else finishes it. Your job — all of you — is to be the everybody else. It's the better part. Trust me. I've had both."

* * *

Afterward, when the children had flooded out into the square in a loud tide of elbows and questions, Tamsin and Merrin and Sef climbed the stairs to the conservatory's roof, where someone — Brannoc, judging by the arrangement — had left bread, hard cheese, and a bottle of the plum wine he pretended was imported and distilled himself in a shed.

The city spread below them, rebuilt twice over, gold in the late light. Beyond it the Thornfell mountains stood blue and enormous, and somewhere inside them, invisible, the Deepwell hummed its long note, and the verge shimmered along the border where the fae realm bled gently through, and the crossings Tamsin had mapped — the new ones, the honest ones, marked in musical notation because some traditions deserved to survive — threaded between the worlds like stitching through cloth.

"Felix's anniversary," Merrin said, pouring. "Forty years. Do you ever miss her? Her. The girl at the salt gate?"

Tamsin considered it honestly, because he deserved honesty and always had.

"No," she said. "She was so lonely, Merrin. She was the best taster in the kingdom and nobody knew her name and she liked it that way because names get people killed. She slept with a knife and counted exits and thought that was a life." She turned the cup in her fingers. "I don't miss her. I thank her. There's a difference."

"And the blood?" Sef asked quietly. "Does it still hurt? On the anniversary?"

"A little. Like an old break in cold weather." She smiled. "Worth it. Every note."

Below them, in the square, the children had begun singing the ballad again without any teacher at all — badly, joyfully, the harmony fraying and reassembling, the second voice wobbling under the first like a fledgling learning the air. Passersby slowed. Someone joined in. Then someone else.

Merrin closed his eyes and listened the way he listened to everything, as if it might be taken from him again.

"They'll still be singing it when we're gone," he said. "All of it. Even your terrible verse."

"Especially my terrible verse."

"Especially yours." He opened his eyes and looked at her — the look he had offered without pressure twenty years ago in a ruined granary, and offered the same way now, unhurried, unspent. "Stay for supper? Stay for the winter? Stay for—" he gestured vaguely at the roof, the city, the rest of a life, "—whatever comes after supper."

Tamsin looked out at the mountains, at the passes she still walked, at the road that had never closed and never would. She thought about the girl at the salt gate, catching a dying stranger's weight, hiding a map out of pure animal instinct, not knowing she was catching her own life too.

Then she looked at the two people on the roof with her — the singer and the star-eared listener, her family, the one that had chosen her back and kept choosing her, year after year, verse after verse.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "To all of it. Whatever comes after supper."

Below, the children reached the unfinished final bar and held the silence together, forty throats holding one rest — and then, unprompted, unschooled, entirely on their own, they began to hum the second voice, and the evening filled with it, and the Choir remembered, and so did everyone who heard.

*Here ends* Felix and the Choir.
