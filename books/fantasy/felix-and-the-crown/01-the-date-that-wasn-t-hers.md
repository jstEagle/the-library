## 1. The Date That Wasn't Hers

The first thing you learn running from a hedge-witch is that hedge-witches don't chase.

They don't need to. Marisol had told me that once, back when I still thought her lessons were kindnesses instead of cage-bars. "A witch doesn't run after what's hers, Isandro. She simply closes the road behind it." Then she'd smiled with all the warmth of a hearth that could just as easily burn your house down, and I'd laughed, because I was fourteen and stupid and she'd given me honey-cakes with my tea.

I wasn't laughing now. I was three days into the Thornfell passes with no food, one boot half-soaked through, and a growing suspicion that every path I chose had been chosen for me.

That was the thing about Marisol's magic. It never looked like magic. It looked like weather. Like a rockslide that happened to block the eastern trail an hour after I turned west. Like a shepherd who happened to mention, in perfect innocence, that the low road was washed out — when I knew for a fact it wasn't, because I'd walked it in spring. Like luck, curdling. The whole mountain rearranging itself around me, patient as a spider, funneling me somewhere I didn't want to go.

So I did the only sensible thing a runaway apprentice can do against a mistress who owns the roads.

I stopped using them.

* * *

There's a smuggler's saying I picked up off a carter in Lowmarket, back when Marisol still let me walk to town alone: *the mountain keeps more doors than the law knows about.* He'd winked when he said it, like he was handing me something valuable for free. Maybe he was. Hedge-witches pay attention to what people give away.

I found the cache on the second night, or rather — and this is the part I keep turning over, even now, even after everything — it found me.

I'd climbed above the treeline to break the funnel, following a goat track so narrow I had to press myself against the rock face and inch along sideways, wind trying to peel me off the mountain like bark off a birch. Below me the valley was going blue with dusk. Above me nothing but scree and the first mean stars. And then the track simply ended at a wall of stone that my eyes kept sliding off of, the way they slide off a word you've read ten times without taking it in.

A glamour. A good one, too — old, layered, the kind of work that takes years and a patience I didn't know anyone in these mountains possessed. Most people would have walked right past it and never known they'd missed anything. But I was raised on Marisol's craft, and the first rule of hedge-magic is that everything hidden wants, a little bit, to be found. Even wards get lonely.

I put my palm flat against the stone and whispered the unbinding Marisol taught me for stuck jar-lids and stubborn cow-knees — small magic, kitchen magic, the kind nobody bothers to ward against — and felt the glamour shiver like a horse bothered by flies.

"Come on," I muttered. "You've been shut up in here longer than I've been alive. Don't tell me you want to stay."

The stone sighed. There's no other word for it. And a seam opened where no seam had been, exhaling air that smelled of lamp oil, old wool, and something else underneath — dust and vellum and time.

I should have turned around. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the world decides they don't matter anymore. Every instinct I had said *this is not yours, this is too easy, easy is Marisol's favorite flavor.* But the wind was knife-cold, my boot was soaked, and the seam was warm.

Warmth wins. It almost always does. Remember that about me. It'll matter later.

* * *

Inside was a smuggler's cache, all right — but a strange one. A low chamber cut into the mountain's bones, maybe eight paces across, stacked with crates gone soft with age and oilcloth stiff as bark. A dead lantern hung from an iron hook. No recent tracks, no droppings, no sign anyone had opened this place in decades. Whatever had been smuggled here had been smuggled and forgotten, and the mountain had folded its hands over the secret and gone to sleep.

Except for the book.

It sat on top of the nearest crate. Not inside a crate, not buried under oilcloth — *on top*, square in the center, positioned with the kind of care that isn't carelessness. Bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, corners worn round, no title on the spine. It lay there in the dark like it had been waiting for exactly one visitor, and had known the date of my arrival better than I had.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, cold air at my back, and told myself the smart things. *It's bait. It's a trap. Marisol reads minds across mountains, everyone knows that, she probably put it here herself.* But Marisol's magic smelled like rosemary and hot iron; this smelled like nothing at all. And besides — and this is the truest thing I can say about myself, the flaw at the center of the stone — I have never once in my life walked away from a closed door.

I lit the lantern. Its oil caught grudgingly, coughing out light the color of weak tea.

The book opened itself before I touched it.

Not dramatically — no wind, no thunder. Just the cover lifting like a hand beckoning, pages falling open to a place near the middle, and I remember thinking, distantly, *that's not how books work,* and then thinking nothing at all, because I was reading.

The page was dense with script in two hands: an older cramped lettering, brown-black ink gone rusty with age, and beneath it, in fresher ink, annotations in a slanting script I recognized down to my stomach.

Marisol's handwriting.

*No,* I thought. *Lots of people write like that. Slanted letters are common. Lots of—*

But I'd copied out two hundred of her receipts in that exact hand. I knew the way her *f*s hooked like fish. I knew the little tick she left at the end of every line, like a stitch pulled tight.

And I knew the name written in the margin beside the older text, underlined twice, in ink that looked — I leaned closer, heart doing something arrhythmic — *recent.*

**Isandro Zhou.**

My name. My actual name, spelled correctly, which almost nobody managed, in a book that had been sealed behind a glamour older than my grandmother's grandmother.

I sat down on a crate because my legs had opinions of their own. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, wind worried at the mountain's scalp, and I heard it the way you hear rain when you're underwater.

*Read it,* said the part of me that had opened every locked door in Marisol's cottage by fifteen. *Whatever it says, it's yours. Nobody else gets to decide what you know about yourself.*

*Burn it,* said the part of me that had watched Marisol turn a poacher into something that still screamed, sometimes, on windy nights.

I read it.

The old text was prophecy — real prophecy, not the fortune-teller mush sold at fairs. It had the cadence Marisol called *deep-verse*, the kind that costs the speaker something. Lines about a gate falling. About snow that forgot which way was down. About a covenant broken on royal blood and three stones scattered like teeth from a shattered jaw. Standard end-of-the-world inventory. I'd catalogued a dozen such doomings in Marisol's library, all of them vague, all of them safely elsewhere, none of them—

None of them dated.

This one was dated. At the bottom of the page, in the old cramped hand, in letters that seemed darker than the rest, as if the ink had been pressed harder or the pen held steadier:

*On the night of the thirteenth frost of autumn, when the moon rides high and horned over the Thornfell passes, the door opens to her.*

And beneath it, in Marisol's hand, fresh enough that the ink might still smudge:

*Tonight.*

I looked up, absurdly, at the dead lantern hook, as if the date might be written there instead. As if some other calendar might apply. The thirteenth frost of autumn. I'd been counting frosts since the first one, the morning I'd found the rime on Marisol's herb-beds and known it was time — the morning I'd packed my mother's iron pendant and Marisol's stolen map and slipped out the dairy door before dawn. Tonight was the thirteenth. I'd counted them myself, scratched little marks into my belt-leather each dawn like a prisoner tallying days.

Thirteen marks. Thirteen frosts. Tonight.

"No," I said aloud, to the book, to the mountain, to whoever had written my name in a century-old margin. "No, that's not — you don't get to do that. You don't get to write me down before I've happened."

The book ignored me, the way important things do.

I flipped forward, fast, defiant, looking for anything — a correction, a footnote, a *disregard previous entry*. The next page held more deep-verse, and here the annotations thickened, Marisol's hand crowding the margins like weeds through flagstones:

*Beware the warden. He is named in the old text as the betrayer.*
*Do not let her grow attached. Attachment complicates the vessel.*
*The vessel must arrive unspoiled. Grief is acceptable. Love is not.*

*Her.* *She.* *The vessel.* The pronouns circled me like crows. I read the lines again, slower, making myself see them, making myself understand that *she* was not some princess in a distant stanza. *She* had my name in the margin. *She* had my crooked spelling corrected in a teacher's neat hand. *She* was sitting on a smuggler's crate with wet feet reading about herself in a dead woman's grammar.

Unspoiled. I turned the phrase over and felt something in my chest go from hurt to hot. So that was the shape of it. All those years of lessons — the small magics, the kitchen charms, the careful rationing of anything powerful — not protection. *Preservation.* You keep a jar sealed until you're ready to open it. You keep a vessel clean until you're ready to fill it.

She hadn't raised an apprentice. She'd been keeping a bottle stoppered, waiting for a date.

And the date was tonight.

* * *

Here is what defiance looks like when it has nowhere to go: it gets very quiet, and it starts planning.

I closed the book. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected shaking. Instead there was a cold clear feeling spreading through me, the same feeling I got the day I ran — not bravery exactly, more like the moment a rope parts and you discover how much of your weight it was really holding.

"Fine," I told the book. "Tonight. A door opens to me. Good. Doors open both ways, and I've been walking away from doors my whole life." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You want a vessel? I'm the leakiest vessel Marisol ever tried to keep. Ask her."

The book warmed under my hands.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. It didn't glow — not at first. It *warmed*, the way a stone warms after a day of sun, and the warmth had a pulse to it, slow and patient, and I realized with a lurch that I was feeling a heartbeat that wasn't mine. Old magic. Deep-verse magic. The kind that costs the speaker something, and apparently, sometimes, costs the reader too.

"Put it down," I whispered to myself. "Isandro. Put it down and walk out and never think about—"

The pages began to glow.

Softly at first, a gold like lamplight through honey, rising up out of the vellum itself, and the words lifted off the page — I swear this, I will swear it on any grave you like — the words *lifted*, hanging above the paper in threads of light, rearranging themselves while I watched. The old cramped script unwove and rewove, and the new pattern it made was simple, and it was addressed to me, and it was not a prophecy anymore. Prophecies are letters sent backward. This was a letter sent *forward*. Someone had known I'd be sitting here, on this crate, at this hour, and had left the light on.

*THE GATE IS DYING,* the light wrote, in letters like frost forming. *IT WILL NOT WAIT FOR THE MOON TO SET. COME NOW OR THE DOOR CLOSES FOR ANOTHER HUNDRED YEARS.*

"I didn't ask for a door," I said. My voice sounded very small in the stone room. "I asked for a way out of the mountains."

*IS THERE A DIFFERENCE?*

And that — that cheap, clever, cruel little question — decided it. Because there *was* a difference, and the difference was that I chose it. Whatever waited behind a dying gate in a mirror-realm full of upward snow and crown wardens, whatever Marisol had planned for her unspoiled vessel, none of it could be worse than being a jar. Than being kept. Than spending the rest of my life on a shelf in a cottage that smelled like rosemary and hot iron, waiting for someone else's date to arrive.

I was tired of other people writing me down before I'd happened.

So I did the reckless thing, the Isandro thing, the thing that warmth always wins. I stuffed the book into my satchel — it fit as if the satchel had been measured for it, which I chose not to think about — and I stood, and the whole chamber exhaled again, that long stone sigh, and the seam in the mountainside began to widen.

Not outward. *Inward.* The rock beyond the wall went grey, then translucent, then thin as fogged glass, and through it I saw a pass that was not my pass — the peaks wrong, doubled, their shadows falling toward the light instead of away from it. Snow drifted up out of the valley below like ash from a fire burning underground. And the sky above those impossible peaks was bruised violet, the color of a healing wound, and it was getting darker in a way that had nothing to do with nightfall.

Somewhere on the far side of that thinning glass, something felt me looking, and looked back.

The warmth in my satchel pulsed once, hard, like a grabbed hand.

"Right," I said, to the book, to the mountain, to Marisol wherever she was listening, and my voice only shook a little. "But I'm keeping the book. Finders keepers. First law of smuggling."

I stepped through the falling gate, and the mountain closed its mouth behind me, and that was the last honest breath of my old life.

What I didn't know — what no margin note had thought worth mentioning — was that on the other side of the dying gate, in the violet dark of a realm that had forgotten spring, men in crown-grey cloaks were already riding toward the place where the air had torn.

They'd read the prophecy too.
