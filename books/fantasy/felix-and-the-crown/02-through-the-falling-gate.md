## 2. Through the Falling Gate

The book did not let go.

Isandro had been running for three days on hard cheese, stolen apples, and the particular adrenaline of a girl who'd just robbed her own mistress, and she told herself — firmly, in the voice Marisol used for correcting sloppy sigilwork — that the heat crawling up her arms was exhaustion. Not magic. Not the book.

The book was glowing again.

She'd stuffed it back into her pack twice already. Both times it had worked its way free, settling on top of the bedroll like a cat that had decided where it lived now. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as old skin, and the sigil pressed into it — a circle broken by a single vertical line — had begun to emit a faint amber light, as if something behind the symbol were breathing against it.

"Absolutely not," Isandro said to it, for the third time that hour. "I don't do portals. Portals are advanced work. I'm a hedge-witch. I do warts and weather-charms and finding lost goats."

The book pulsed, warm against her hip, like laughter.

The pass above her was called the Shepherd's Tooth, though no shepherd had used it in living memory; Marisol's maps marked it with a little inked skull and the annotation *smugglers only, and stupid ones*. Isandro qualified on both counts tonight. Behind her, somewhere down in the valley, lantern-light moved in a pattern she recognized: search-line, four abreast. Marisol's people. Marisol's *discipline*.

Ahead of her, the cache.

Old Tam Vetch had shown it to her two summers ago, drunk on plum brandy and feeling generous: a smuggler's hollow behind a shelf of granite, dry, hidden, stocked once upon a time with rope and lamp-oil and tinned things nobody had eaten since the last border war. She'd been saving it in her head like a coin. Now she climbed toward it with her lungs burning and the book pulsing hotter with every step, and by the time she wedged herself behind the granite shelf and dragged her pack in after her, the glow was strong enough to read by.

She didn't want to read it. That was the truth of it, the ugly little truth she kept shoving down under the bravado: she was terrified. Her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her knees. But terror had never once in her life stopped her from looking, and it wasn't going to start tonight.

She opened the book to where the ribbon marked the page.

The script was old Thornic, dense and formal, the kind Marisol made her copy out fifty times for insolence. But someone — many someones, over years — had annotated the margins in a dozen different hands. And there, halfway down the page, bracketed in fresh-looking ink, brown-black like dried blood:

*On the night of the thirteenth of Frostmoon, the gate at Shepherd's Tooth will fail, and the runaway will be drawn through.*

Below it, in a different hand, smaller, almost apologetic:

*Isandro Zhou.*

Her name. Spelled correctly. Even the *Z*, which everyone got wrong.

Isandro sat very still behind the granite shelf while the wind screamed down the pass and the book lay open in her lap, glowing like a coal. She thought about all the reasonable explanations. A trick. Marisol's doing — a test, a trap, one of the mistress's elaborate lessons. Someone who knew her name writing a prophecy-shaped lure. All reasonable. All comforting.

None of them explained why the words were *warm*.

"Right," she said aloud, because talking made her feel brave even when it was lies. "Here's what happens now. I close the book. I leave it in this cache for the next stupid smuggler. I go find a nice sensible valley where nothing glows, and in ten years I tell this story in taverns and everybody laughs."

She reached out and closed the book.

The light didn't stop. It came up through the leather, through her fingers, through the bones of her hand, and Isandro had one long moment to notice — with the detached, cataloguing part of her mind that always noticed the wrong thing at the wrong time — that the cold in the cache had vanished entirely, replaced by a heat like standing too near a forge. Then the granite shelf behind her hummed. The whole mountain hummed. Somewhere beyond the rock, stone was grinding against stone, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep, and air rushed past her into a crack that hadn't been there a moment before — a crack widening into a seam, the seam into a doorway, the doorway into

light.

"Oh," Isandro said. "Oh, you absolute *book*—"

The cache floor opened like an eye.

* * *

Falling through a waygate, she would think later, is less like falling and more like being *read*. Every part of you turned over, examined, annotated. She felt the passage take inventory of her: the hedge-magic coiled small and stubborn under her sternum, the salt-stink of the smuggler roads in her clothes, the fear, the fury, the name. It lingered longest on the name.

Then it spat her out.

She landed on snow — real snow, cold, wet, gloriously ordinary snow — and rolled, came up in a crouch with her knife out, because the first rule of the smuggler roads was *never land anywhere without a weapon ready*. Old Tam's voice in her head: *Second rule, girl: never trust a door that opens itself.*

The second rule proved immediately relevant, because the door was still there. Behind her, hanging in the air between two leaning slabs of black stone, was a ragged oval of amber light, and through it she could see the inside of Tam Vetch's cache, the bedroll, the abandoned book — no. The book was not abandoned. The book was *here*, lying in the snow at her feet, closed, dark, smug.

And the gate was closing. Not gently. The oval's edges frayed, guttered, spat sparks of amber that fell upward — *fell upward*, she noted, cataloguing again, filing the horror away for later processing — and then the whole thing collapsed inward with a sound like a struck bell dying, and there was nothing but black stone and snow and a silence so complete it rang.

"No," Isandro said. "No, no, no. Open. *Open.*"

She hammered her fist against the stone where the gate had been. Nothing. She tried the few gate-sigils Marisol had let her practice — chalk-work, really, theory dressed up as craft — and felt them slide off the stone like water off grease. Whatever had carried her through had done it on its own authority, and its authority was spent.

Snow drifted past her. Upward. From the ground into the sky, unhurried, like smoke.

She stood in the middle of it and made herself look properly, because looking was the one thing she was good at. The sky was the color of a bruise going bad — deep violet shading to black at the zenith, with stars she didn't recognize arranged in constellations that meant nothing to her. The mountains were the right shape for the Shepherd's Tooth range and entirely the wrong color, black rock veined with something faintly luminous, blue-white, like frost grown inside the stone. The valley below held a frozen river and, far off, a scatter of lights. A town. Lights meant people. People meant information, food, and a way to stop panicking.

Terror masked well with a plan. That was the whole trick of bravado, and Isandro had been performing it since she was six years old.

"Fine," she announced to the empty, upward-snowing, violet-skied wilderness. "New realm. No gate home. Book that thinks it's funny." She picked the book up, weighed it, and shoved it deep into her pack, binding it shut with a strap and a small silencing knot Marisol had taught her for keeping enchanted objects quiet. "You," she told the pack, "are grounded."

Then she checked her supplies with the brisk efficiency of someone who refuses to cry until the inventory is done. Bedroll. Knife. Three days of food if she stretched it. Flint. Chalk. A coil of good rope. Forty-one coppers and a silver bit from the Thornfell side, useless here or priceless, no way to know. Her mother's ring on its cord around her neck, which she touched once, briefly, and then put away.

She started downhill toward the lights.

* * *

The town was called Coldharbour Row, according to a sign whose letters had been half-eaten by frost-lichen, and it was the strangest place Isandro had ever crept through, and she had crept through some places.

Everything was preserved. That was the word that kept coming back to her. The buildings were timber and stone, sound, roofed, intact — and empty. Doors stood ajar. A cart sat in the main street with its load of split firewood still stacked and waiting. Laundry lines hung with cloth frozen stiff as boards. A child's sled leaned against a well. And everywhere, over everything, that same blue-white luminescence veined the walls, the cobbles, the well-head, growing like frost but moving like moss, slow and patient and *alive*.

Nobody. A whole town of nobody.

The long winter, the margins of the prophecy had whispered. She hadn't understood. She was beginning to.

She found the inn because it was the largest building and inns are inns in any world: a sign depicting a boar, a common room with dead hearths, a bar with bottles still ranked behind it. She helped herself to bread from the kitchen — stale as gravel but bread — and a bottle of something amber, and she was reaching for a candle stub when she heard the horses.

Hooves. Many. Coming up the frozen highroad with the disciplined, economical rhythm of riders who patrol for a living, not ride for pleasure. Isandro blew out the candle she hadn't lit, dropped below the window line, and looked out through a gap in the shutter.

They wore grey cloaks over mail, and each cloak was pinned at the shoulder with a badge of office: a crown within a circle. Crown wardens. Twelve riders, torches burning a strange pale gold, fanning through the town with the smooth coordination of a sweep pattern she'd seen Marisol's people run a hundred times. They were searching for something.

Isandro did the arithmetic. New realm. Empty town. Armed riders sweeping building by building. A girl who had arrived through an illegal collapsing gate mere hours ago, carrying a prophesied book that glowed.

"I'm going to die," she informed the bottle, conversationally. "Well. At least I'll die somewhere interesting."

But dying required being caught first, and Isandro Zhou had been dodging catchers since she was nine. She went out the inn's back, over the frozen laundry line — the cloth crackled and snapped like kindling under her boots — through a yard where a plow stood rusting mid-furrow, and into the lichen-glow dark of the streets beyond. The wardens' torches swept the main street behind her. She cut north along a drainage channel, boots wrapped in sacking for silence, moving the way Tam had drilled into her: never in a straight line, never twice the same way, always knowing your next hole before you need it.

It worked beautifully for almost two hours.

What undid her was the frost-lichen. She ducked into what looked like a cellar — sunken doorway, half-buried, perfect — and the moment her boot touched the floor, the lichen *flared*. Blue-white light blazed up the walls in a spreading web, bright as day, and somewhere out in the dark a horn sounded. One note. Then another, answering from the east.

"Oh, you're *kidding* me," Isandro said. "You're the alarm system. This entire world is the alarm system."

She bolted. Out the cellar, up the street, the horn behind her now joined by hoofbeats converging from two directions, and she was fast, she was genuinely fast, but the town was small and the net was tight and when she vaulted a garden wall into the last yard before the fields, there were torches already waiting at both ends of the lane.

Three wardens dismounted. Grey cloaks, crown-in-circle badges, swords loose in scabbards rather than drawn — professional, not panicked. The one in front was young, broad-shouldered, with a face like a closed door and eyes that went from her face to her pack to her hands, cataloguing, exactly the way she catalogued.

"Hands," he said. "Where I can see them."

Isandro raised them slowly, palms out, fingers spread. Her heart was slamming so hard she could feel it in her teeth. So she smiled — the wide, easy, road-tough smile that had talked her out of six arrests and one hanging — and said:

"Evening, officers. Lovely night for it. Bit chilly, though, isn't it? You might want to look into that. Whole winter situation seems, ah, ongoing."

The young warden didn't smile. "Name."

"Depends who's asking, doesn't it? If it's friends, it's Izzy. If it's law—" she shrugged, "—it's whatever gets me a warm cell and a hot meal. Speaking of which, is there a warm cell? Because I have to say, the hospitality in this realm leaves something to be desired. No gate signs. No directions. Snow goes the wrong way. Two stars."

One of the wardens behind snorted despite himself. The young one didn't. He stepped closer, and the torchlight caught his badge, and beneath the crown-in-circle Isandro saw, stitched small in silver thread, a personal mark she would come to know very well indeed: an ash leaf, inverted.

"You came through the gate at Shepherd's Tooth," he said quietly.

"Did I?" said Isandro. "Funny story. I was hiking. Very scenic. Fell asleep behind a rock. Woke up here. Happens all the time."

"The gate collapsed a century ago."

"Then I clearly fell asleep for a very long time, because I am *exhausted*—"

"Search the pack," the young warden said, and stepped back, and it was the calmness in his voice more than anything else that finally frightened her — the calmness of a man following a script he'd rehearsed his whole life. The other warden pulled the pack off her shoulder, cut the silencing knot with a small knife, and reached inside.

The book began to glow through the leather.

All three wardens went still. The youngest one actually stepped backward. And the young warden with the ash-leaf badge stared at the light spilling between his comrade's fingers with an expression Isandro couldn't read — not surprise. Something older than surprise. Something like recognition, and grief, and dread, all wearing the mask of duty.

"Bind her hands," he said. "Not the mouth. I want to hear everything she says."

"That's irregular, Ashgrave," one of the others murmured.

"It's irregular that she's *here*, Corren." He turned those closed-door eyes on Isandro one last time. "Take her to the citadel. Quietly. If anyone asks — we found a smuggler in Coldharbour Row, and she's nobody."

He wheeled his horse away, and Isandro, wrists being bound with cold iron cord, watched him go and thought, with the part of her mind that never stopped noticing:

*Ashgrave knows that book. And he's afraid of it.*

Which meant, she reasoned, as they hauled her onto a horse behind a warden who smelled of wet wool and torch-smoke, that the book was worth something. Which meant she was worth something. Which meant, for the moment, nobody with sense would kill her.

She grinned at her captor's back, teeth chattering, terror howling underneath like wind in a chimney.

"So," she said cheerfully. "Anyone want to tell me what century this is?"

Nobody answered. Ahead, on a black ridge against the bruised-violet sky, a citadel's towers rose crowned in frost-fire, and the snow went on falling upward, patiently, like the world exhaling a hundred years of held breath.
