## 3. The Warden Who Should Have Killed Me

The prisoner was smaller than the prophecy had promised.

Felix Ashgrave stood on the far side of the holding cell's iron grate and studied her the way you'd study a wound you couldn't explain — a girl, maybe nineteen, soaked to the bone with snowmelt that fell upward off her shoulders in slow silver threads. She sat on the bench like it was a throne she'd stolen fair and square, wrists bound in warden's cord, chin up, eyes doing rapid inventory of everything in the room: the grate, the lamp, his sword, the seam in the wall where the old gate-iron had been poured.

Smuggler's eyes. Thief's posture. And underneath all of it, when her gaze snagged on his face for half a heartbeat — fear. Well-hidden, but Felix had spent eleven years reading fear in the faces of people brought into Thornfell's citadel, and he knew its weather like a sailor knows clouds.

"You're staring," she said. Her voice was rougher than a girl's voice should be, sanded down by hard roads. "Stare costs extra, where I'm from."

"Where are you from?"

"Wouldn't you like to know."

"I would, actually. It's my job."

She grinned at him, quick and crooked, and something cold walked down Felix's spine on small feet.

He knew that grin.

* * *

He'd been seven years old the first time. The nightmares hadn't started as nightmares — they'd started as a story his mother told, back when she still told stories, before the name Ashgrave became something said only in whispers and behind doors. *The witch and the warden*, she'd called it. A hedge-witch from beyond the gates, and a crown-sworn man who would break every oath he ever took for her sake. A traitor's story. A story about their family, though his mother never said so out loud, because saying it out loud made it true, and in the Ashgrave house truth was a thing you kept wrapped in wool and buried deep.

Then the dreams came anyway. Dreams of a girl with smuggler's eyes and a crooked grin, standing in snow that fell upward, holding out her hand while the sky bled violet above her. Dreams of himself turning away from her. Dreams of her face when he did — not surprised. That was the worst of it. In the dreams she was never surprised. She looked at him like a woman watching a door close that she'd always known would close.

He'd woken screaming more nights than he'd admit to anyone, least of all the healers, because healers wrote things down, and things written down in Thornfell found their way to the Queen.

And now here she was. Real. Bound in warden's cord, dripping upward snow, grinning at him through the grate like the whole thing was a joke she was in on and he wasn't.

"What's your name?" he asked, and hated how carefully he asked it.

"Isandro." The word came out of her like it surprised her too — like she'd meant to give him a false one and her mouth had betrayed her. Her grin flickered. Died. For a moment she just looked at him, and the fear underneath came closer to the surface than she probably wanted. "Huh," she said quietly. "You look like someone I've seen."

"Where?"

"In a book." She said it lightly, but her hands had gone tight against the cord. "A stupid book. Old. Full of nonsense." She lifted her chin again, defiant, daring him. "Why? You see me somewhere before, Warden?"

*In my sleep,* he didn't say. *Every night for fifteen years.* *Standing in violet light, waiting for me to betray you.*

"No," Felix said.

The lie tasted like iron.

* * *

The protocol was simple. Intruders caught crossing a collapsed waygate were to be questioned, catalogued, and — if they could not demonstrate lawful purpose in the realm — remanded to the Queen's justice. The Queen's justice, this year, meant the ice cells below the citadel, where prisoners were kept until the winter decided what they were worth. Nobody came out of the ice cells. Everybody understood this. It was the cleanest system the crown had ever devised, because it required no executions at all, merely patience.

Felix had delivered nine people to the ice cells. He remembered each of their faces. He expected to remember them for the rest of his life, however long the Queen permitted that to be.

He should have taken Isandro downstairs within the hour. Instead he found himself sitting on the wrong side of the grate, on a stool, lamplight between them, asking questions that weren't on any protocol form.

"The waygate you came through. The one above Greyfall Pass. Did it open for you, or did you force it?"

"It opened." She shrugged, cord pulling taut. "Sort of. It was dying. It grabbed me more than let me through, if that makes sense. Like a drowning man catching a rope."

It made sense. It made horrible sense. The gates had been failing for a century — everyone knew that, everyone had grown up under the long winter and the thinning magic and the slow dimming of the covenant-stones' light. But a dead gate reaching across the veil to *pull* someone through, specifically, deliberately—

"Why you?" he asked.

"If I knew, I'd sell you the answer." But her bravado had a crack in it now, and through the crack he could see the same question eating at her. She'd been asking herself why since she arrived; he could tell. "Look, Warden. Ashgrave." She'd read his badge, then. Sharp eyes. "I'm not a spy. I'm not an army. I'm a hedge-witch apprentice who ran away from her mistress through some mountains and got eaten by a book. Whatever your Queen thinks is happening, I promise you the truth is stupider."

"A book."

"A prophecy book." She watched him say it. Watched his face the way he'd been watching hers, and whatever she saw there made her go very still. "You know it."

"There are a hundred prophecy books in Thornfell. Most of them are frauds. The rest are worse than frauds."

"This one had my name in it." Her voice dropped. "And tonight's date. Written a hundred years ago. So either I'm the punchline of a very long joke, or—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. "Or nothing. Forget it. When do I get out of here?"

"That depends on what you tell the Queen's interrogators."

Something crossed her face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone who has heard about a place the way you hear about a disease. "The ice cells," she said.

"You've heard of them."

"In the Undermarket they say the winter keeps people." She met his eyes. "They say the winter's hungry. They say it's not weather anymore, it's a *creditor*."

Felix said nothing, because there was nothing true he could say that wouldn't make things worse, and no lie good enough to hold against those eyes.

"They're going to feed me to it," she said. Not a question.

"The Queen will decide—"

"The Queen decided a hundred years ago." Isandro leaned forward, cords creaking. "Warden. Listen to me. I don't know what I am to this realm. I don't know why a dead book pulled me through a dead gate. But I know what I read last night, and I know what your face did when I said the word *prophecy*, and I know you've been looking at me like you're checking me against something. Against *someone*. So either you tell me what I am right now, or you march me down to the ice and let the winter do your confessing for you."

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp's small flame and the soft hiss of snow falling upward past the high window.

"You have her hands," Felix said finally. It came out before he could stop it. "The girl in the dreams. She holds her hands like that. Like she's about to pick a lock even when they're tied."

Isandro looked down at her own bound hands as if seeing them for the first time. Then back up. "What dreams?"

"The kind you don't report." He stood. His legs felt foreign, borrowed. "Protocol says I take you downstairs now."

"So take me downstairs."

"Yes," Felix said. He did not move.

* * *

He walked the corridor instead. Ten paces one way, ten paces back, boots loud on stone that had heard a century of wardens walking the same floor making the same decisions. Duty was a simple thing, usually. You held the line. You kept the oath. You did the ugly necessary work so that the realm behind you could pretend the world was kind. Eleven years of it, and he'd been good at it — good enough that they'd given him the Greyfall sector, good enough that men who'd once called him *the exile's whelp* behind his back now called him *sir* to his face.

But duty had never once asked him to walk a girl to the winter knowing her face from his own childhood.

He thought about the dreams again, properly this time, laid out end to end like evidence. The girl in the violet light. The betrayal — always the betrayal, though the dreams never showed its shape, only its weight, the way a nightmare shows you the fall but never the ground. And beneath it, older, half-buried: his mother's voice, low, in the years before the whispers started. *There was a prophecy made about our blood, Felix. About what we cost this world. If anyone ever comes asking after it — anyone with a witch's eyes — you run. Promise me you run.*

He'd promised. He'd been eight.

And now the girl with the witch's eyes was sitting in his holding cell asking him what she was, and every oath he owned was pulling in a different direction like a cart team gone mad.

There was another thing, too. The thing he kept circling and refusing to land on. The way the lamp flame had leaned toward her when she spoke. The way the frost on the cell's window — frost that had not melted in this citadel in ninety years — had gone glassy and wet along one edge, in a neat line, like a finger drawn through it. The stones in the vaults sang sometimes at night; every warden pretended not to hear. But Felix had stood watch over the vault wing enough winters to know the song had changed this week. Had gotten louder. Had gotten *directional*.

Toward Greyfall Pass. Toward the dead gate. Toward her.

"She resonates," he said aloud, to the empty corridor. "God help us both, she resonates."

Behind him, faintly, through two walls and an iron grate, he heard the frost crack.

* * *

When he came back, she was standing at the bars, which she shouldn't have been able to do — the warden's cord was oath-knotted, it held a person seated whether they liked it or not. The cord lay on the bench in two pieces, neatly severed, ends frayed as if burned from inside.

Felix looked at the cord. Looked at her. She had the grace to look slightly embarrassed.

"Hedge-magic," she said. "It's mostly knots and nerve. Your knots were very good, for what it's worth."

"You could have done that hours ago."

"I wanted to see what you'd do first." No apology in it. Smuggler's honesty — the kind that tells you the truth precisely because it costs nothing. "You paced for a quarter hour and talked to yourself about resonance, which, rude, by the way. And then you came back. Wardens don't come back, in my experience. They hand you off and wash their hands." Her eyes searched his face. "So. What are you going to do, Warden Ashgrave? Because I've read a page or two about you as well, and I'll be honest — the pages didn't end well. For either of us."

The corridor lamp guttered. Somewhere far below, in the vault wing, a covenant-stone began — very softly — to sing.

Felix had rehearsed a dozen versions of the next minute on his pacing rounds. Most ended with him drawing his sword. One or two ended with him shouting for the guard. None of them, not one, ended the way it actually ended: with him unlocking the cell door, stepping aside, and pointing down the west stair toward the old servant passages that every warden knew and no map showed.

"Undermarket," he said. "Beneath the frozen city. Find the tinker called Vetch — grey coat, three rings in his left ear — tell him the Ashgrave boy sent you and he owes the Ashgrave line a debt he'll hate paying. Stay off the upper streets. The Queen's hunters fly at dusk." He pulled his cloak from its peg and threw it to her; she caught it one-handed, blinking. "It's cold above. It's colder below. Don't die in either."

Isandro stared at him. "You're letting me go."

"I'm losing my mind," Felix corrected. "Letting you go is a side effect."

"Why?" And there it was — the question under all her questions, stripped of bravado now, bare as bone. "Why would you burn yourself for a stranger? What am I to you?"

*You're the reason I learned to sleep with a knife under my pillow. You're the shape my worst nights have worn since I was seven. You're the person I am prophesied to destroy, and I am hoping — with everything left in me that isn't afraid — that the prophecy is a fraud, like most books, and that I get to find out by never finding out.*

"Because the ice cells are murder," he said, "and I've carried enough of it."

Which was true. Which was maybe a tenth of the truth. She seemed to weigh it, this girl who weighed everything, and then she did the last thing he expected: she reached through the open door and pressed something into his palm. Small, warm despite the cold, humming faintly like a struck bell.

A splinter of pale crystal. Gate-iron, or something older.

"It broke off the waygate when it grabbed me," she said. "It's been warm since I touched it. Warm things are rare here, I'm guessing. Consider it payment." A ghost of the crooked grin. "Or a down payment. Depends how the next chapter goes."

Then she was past him, quick and quiet down the west stair, cloak swallowing her, gone the way thieves go — as if the dark had simply agreed to keep her.

Felix stood alone in the corridor with a burning splinter in his fist and the sound of his own career collapsing around him like snow off a roof. In an hour the night watch would find the empty cell, the cut cord, the missing cloak. By morning the Queen's hunters would have his name. By dusk, perhaps his head.

He found he didn't care. What he cared about was the splinter, which had begun, very faintly, to pull — northward, toward Greyfall, toward the singing stones, toward a road he had no business walking.

What he cared about was that in none of his nightmares had he ever seen himself *follow* her.

Maybe, he thought, sheathing his sword and taking the north stair at a run, that's how the prophecy gets you. Maybe it doesn't need you to turn away. Maybe it just needs you to chase.

The stones were singing louder now. And somewhere ahead, in the frozen dark, a runaway witch was about to discover that the warden who should have killed her was coming after her anyway — and neither of them yet knew which of those facts was the trap.
