## 4. Bargains in the Undermarket

The city above was called Vharenmark, though nobody up there would have told me its name for free. I learned it from a fishmonger's sign, half-buried in ice, as I slipped down a stairwell that smelled of tallow and old secrets. The stair went down farther than any cellar had a right to, and the cold changed as I descended — not warmer, exactly, but *thicker*, the kind of cold that lived indoors, that had made its peace with bodies and breath.

At the bottom was a door made of whalebone ribs lashed together with copper wire, and past it, the Undermarket.

I'd seen black markets before. Marisol used to send me to collect debts from smugglers in the Thornfell passes, back when I still thought she was teaching me herbcraft instead of something else entirely. But those markets had been grubby and human — dice games, stolen brandy, a fence who cried when he drank. This place was grubby and *other*. Lanterns burned blue-green with some fat I didn't want to identify. A woman with antlers filed down to stubs sold bottled breath from a handcart. Two figures argued over a crate of what looked like songbirds frozen mid-flight, each bird still singing, thin and looping, like music remembered by someone who hadn't heard music in years.

Nobody looked at me twice. That was the first mercy this realm had shown me.

I pulled Felix's cloak — no. Not his. The warden's cloak I'd taken off a drying line three streets above, because walking through a strange city in a hedge-witch's smock was asking to be noticed. It hung off my shoulders wrong, smelled of someone else's woodsmoke, and made me look, I hoped, like nobody worth a second glance.

Hunger found me before anything else did. Three days since the gate. Two since I'd last eaten — a heel of bread I'd charmed out of a windowsill using a cantrip so small it was barely a lie. My stomach had moved past growling into a kind of hollow negotiation, and I knew how those negotiations ended if I let them run: you got slow, then stupid, then caught.

So. Work.

I found him near the market's heart, at a stall built from an overturned riverboat, selling passage — or claiming to. His name, according to the chalkboard propped against the hull, was Dreech, and he could get you anywhere in the White Reach for "fair coin or fair trade." He was a squat man with frostbitten ears and eyes like chipped flint, and when I approached, he looked me over the way a butcher looks at a carcass.

"You're not from here," he said.

"Neither is anyone, eventually."

That got a grunt that might have been approval. "What've you got?"

I set my hands flat on the boat's gunwale and thought about what I had left. Hedge-magic isn't much to look at. No fire from fingertips, no storms on command. What it is, is *small true things* — knots that stay knotted, milk that doesn't sour, a fever drawn down into a clay bead. Small true things, traded carefully, keep a witch alive.

"I can mend," I said. "Bones, rope, promises. I can find water under ice. I can make a charm that'll keep your lungs working clean in coal-smoke for a year." I paused. "And I can tell you which of your customers is lying to you."

Dreech's flint eyes flickered. "Last one's worth more than the rest together. Prove it."

I nodded at a man haggling over dried fish two stalls down. "He's shorting you. He counted the coins into his left palm twice — once for you, once for himself. There's a sliver of lead painted silver at the bottom of the stack."

Dreech watched the man leave, then checked the purse. The lead sliver rang dull against the deck when he dropped it. He looked at me differently after that — not warmly, but the way you look at a tool that works.

"Passage where?" he asked.

"Away. South, east, anywhere the Queen's wardens aren't."

"Wardens aren't south. Wardens are *everywhere*." He leaned in, lowering his voice. "But there's a road under the ice roads. Old smuggler tunnels, pre-covenant. Runs toward the Ashfall border. Costs double."

"Double what?"

"Double whatever you were going to pay. And you work the trip. My last guide coughed himself into the ground — smoke-lung. You draw the charm, you keep the crew breathing, we're square."

It was more than I'd hoped for and less than I needed, which I was learning was the standard exchange rate in this world. "Done," I said, and spat in my palm before I could think better of it. He stared at it, then laughed — a dry, rusted sound.

"We don't spit here anymore," he said. "Ice takes the moisture. Word's enough, or it isn't."

We shook on it, dry-handed, and I felt the bargain settle somewhere behind my sternum, the way hedge-bargains do. Binding, but only as binding as both parties' fear of consequences. I'd have preferred the spit.

* * *

I worked the rest of the day at his stall while he arranged the tunnel passage, drawing lung-charms on scraps of birchbark, and that was how I learned about the winter.

Not from Dreech — from his customers. They came and went in ones and twos, hooded and hollow-eyed, buying bottled breath and heat-stones and little wax saints of a god I didn't recognize, and they talked, because people always talk when they think the person serving them is furniture.

"The harvest failed again," said a farmer with hands like tree roots. "Third year. The seed-grain's dead. Not dormant — *dead*. I cracked one open and there was nothing inside. Just more cold."

"My sister's in Thornfell," said another, a woman with a soldier's bearing and a civilian's exhaustion. "Proper Thornfell, the other side. She says it's the same there. Snow won't melt, wells freezing from the bottom up. Same winter, both realms, like the sky forgot which world it belonged to."

"They say the covenant's broken," muttered a third, an old man buying nothing, warming himself at Dreech's brazier without asking. "Say it broke a hundred years ago and everything since has just been the long exhale. Realm's dying. Both of them. Slow, but sure as taxes."

"And the Queen?" Dreech asked, with the careful flatness of a man asking a question he already knew the shape of.

The old man shrugged. "Queen Vess says the covenant is a story for children. Says endurance is the new covenant, and loyalty, and her face on the coin. Meanwhile she's collecting every stone and shard and relic with covenant-marks on it. Buying them, taxing them, taking them. Nobody knows why." He snorted. "Nobody asks twice, neither."

I kept my head down and my charcoal moving, but my blood had gone strange. *Both realms.* The same winter killing both worlds at once, like a single body with two frostbitten hands. And a queen hoarding covenant-stones — stones like, I suspected, the thing I'd felt humming in the drowned chapel before Felix dragged me out of it. Stones like whatever it was Marisol had been training me to hold.

Small true things, Isandro. Start with what's small and true: you are in a dying world, you have sold your labor for a hole in the ice, and the woman who raised you knew more about all of this than she ever told you.

One truth at a time. That was survivable.

* * *

The Undermarket dimmed toward what passed for night — the lanterns burning lower, the crowd shifting from desperate to desperate-and-drunk — when I felt it.

A prickle at the nape of my neck. Hedge-witches don't get grand warnings; we get itches, hunches, the sense of a door opening somewhere behind us. Mine said: *someone is watching you, and they know your name.*

I turned with a lung-charm palmed, ready to press it against whoever came close and watch them spend ten minutes gasping politely in a corner.

Felix Ashgrave stepped out from between two stalls, hood down, unarmed as far as I could see, and holding two bowls of something steaming.

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. *Run. He's a warden. He let you go once, which means either he's incompetent or he wants something, and both mean run.*

But I was tired, and hungry, and the bowls smelled like actual food — barley and marrow and salt — and there was a third instinct underneath the other two, quieter, that said: *he looked at you like a man looking at a ghost he'd been promised. Ghosts don't scare people who plan to hurt them. Ghosts scare people who are afraid.*

"You found me," I said. "That's not comforting."

"It wasn't hard. You charm things loudly." He held out a bowl. "Eat. Then listen. Then decide whether to run."

I took the bowl. Stupid. Reckless. I did it anyway, keeping my body angled toward the alley between stalls, keeping the charm in my other fist. The stew was hot and dense and I hated how good it was, hated the sound I made on the first spoonful, a small animal noise I couldn't swallow back down.

"There's a price on you," Felix said quietly. "Not official. The Queen's hunters don't file reports. But word moves through places like this faster than it moves through palaces. By morning, every fence and fixer in the Undermarket will know a foreign witch is worth more alive than the whole stall row combined."

"Then why warn me? You could sell me yourself. Save the middlemen their cut."

Something crossed his face — quick, quickly buried. "Because the people hunting you don't want to question you. They want to *open* you. There's a witch in the Queen's service who reads people like texts. She'd take everything you know about the other realm out of your head and leave the rest of you for gulls." He paused. "Also, I owe you a debt. You didn't run when the wraiths came, in the chapel. You stood and you *held*, and that's more than most crown-sworn manage. So: eat, listen, live."

"Why do you care whether I live?"

He looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment the warden's composure thinned and I saw something underneath it that looked almost like fear — not of me, but *for* me, which made no sense at all.

"Because the winter is killing both realms," he said. "You heard it today, I'd wager. The covenant broke a century ago and everything since has been the slow bleed. The stones can reforge it — three of them, scattered, and the Queen is gathering them for reasons that have nothing to do with saving anyone. You resonated with the first stone, Isandro. It *sang to you*. I've stood next to that stone my whole life and it never once acknowledged I existed." His jaw tightened. "Whatever you are, whatever Marisol made you into, you may be the only person alive who can touch those stones and survive it. That's why I care. That, and—"

He stopped. Closed his mouth on whatever the *and* was, like a man biting down on a live coal.

"That, and what?" I asked.

"Nothing. Eat your stew."

We ate in silence for a while, shoulder to shoulder at the edge of the market's noise, two fugitives pretending at privacy. The blue-green lanterns guttered. Somewhere a frozen songbird sang its four-note loop, over and over, patient as grief.

"If I run for the tunnels tonight," I said finally, "how long before your hunters pick up my trail?"

"Hours. Maybe less. They have a scent-hound that works on magic, not smell." He set down his empty bowl. "Which is why I'm coming with you."

I laughed. It came out ugly. "You're a crown warden. I'm the intruder you were supposed to arrest. And you want to *travel together*?"

"I want to keep you alive until you understand what you're carrying," he said. "After that, if you still want me gone, I'll walk away. Warden's oath on it." He hesitated. "What's left of it."

I should have said no. Every smart bone in my body said no. Smart bones, though, don't fill an empty stomach, and they don't cross a dying world alone, and they definitely don't explain why a disgraced warden looks at a stranger like she's a debt he's been paying interest on since childhood.

"Dreech's tunnel leaves at first bell," I said. "South, toward the Ashfall border. If you slow me down, I'll charm your boots to walk you into a lake."

"Noted." Almost — *almost* — a smile. "Isandro. One more thing. Before we go. The hunters coming for you — their captain is a man called Renn Vaile. He was my brother-in-arms. If he finds us, don't let him talk. He talks people into surrendering. It's kinder than what follows, and it's still a trap."

"Anything else I should know?"

"Yes." He stood, pulling his hood up, and the lantern-light caught the scar along his jaw. "Everything I've told you is true, and it's still not the whole truth. When you're ready to hear the rest, ask me what my family name was, before Ashgrave."

And he walked into the dark between the stalls, leaving me standing there with an empty bowl, a full head of questions, and the sudden, unwelcome certainty that I was going to follow him anyway.
