## 5. What the Covenant Was

The road out of the Undermarket climbed through a seam in the world that had no business existing, and I tried not to think about how much of my life now fit that description.

Three days since I'd let the witch walk out of her cell. Three days since I'd stood in the warden barracks and lied to Captain Dree's face — *the intruder escaped on her own, sir, slipped the ward like smoke* — and felt something in me crack along a fault line I hadn't known was there. Now here I was, walking beside her instead of dragging her back to it, heading for a chapel at the bottom of a frozen lake because a book of prophecies told us to, and if any of my old brothers-in-arms saw me they'd put a bolt through my throat before I finished saying her name.

Isandro walked ahead of me on the switchback trail, hood up against the wind, and every few steps she'd glance over her shoulder like she still expected me to change my mind. I couldn't blame her. In her place I'd have run from me too.

"Stop doing that," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me like you're doing arithmetic." She turned fully around, boots skidding a little on the frost-slick stone. "You keep adding things up and not liking the total."

She wasn't wrong. That was the problem with hedge-witches. Marisol's training or no training, they read people the way I read terrain.

"I'm trying to decide how much to tell you," I said.

"About what?"

"The covenant." I nodded up the trail, toward the pass that would take us down into the lake country where the first stone waited under black ice. "You're going to be walking into places that want you dead for reasons older than either of us. You deserve to know why."

Something shifted in her face — the smuggler's bravado thinning, just for a moment, into the face of someone who'd found her own name in a dead man's book and hadn't slept right since. Then the bravado came back down like a shutter. "Fine," she said. "Talk while we walk. My feet are freezing and misery loves company."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a waygate ruin — one of the small ones, a standing arch half-swallowed by drifts, its keystone carved with interlocking crowns worn nearly smooth. Isandro ran her bare fingers over the carving while I got a fire going, and when she pulled her hand back she was frowning.

"It's warm," she said. "Barely. Like a hearthstone an hour after the fire dies."

"That's the covenant," I said. "Or what's left of it."

I fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb. Snow fell upward past the tree line, slow as ash off a pyre, and I let the silence stretch until she sat down across from me and pulled her knees to her chest. Waiting. She was good at waiting. Smuggler's patience, she'd called it once, back in the Undermarket, and I'd almost smiled.

"A hundred and six years ago," I said, "there were two Thornfells. This one—" I gestured at the violet bruise of the sky, the upward snow, the cold that never quite killed you but never let you forget it was trying— "and yours. The green one. The one with weather that makes sense."

"My realm doesn't have a name for yours," she said. "Most people don't believe it exists."

"We don't have a name for yours either. The old texts call them the Two Courts. Same land, folded twice." I drew two overlapping circles in the snow with a stick. "Same mountains, same rivers, same cities — but doubled. What grows here withers there. What's built there echoes here. And between them, holding the fold together, was the covenant."

"The pact," she said. "Felix mentioned— sorry. You mentioned. Back at the shrine." She winced. I decided not to enjoy it.

"The pact," I agreed. "Sealed by a king — a true king, crown-sworn, blood-oathed. His blood went into three stones, one for each anchor point of the fold: the chapel in the lake country, the amphitheater in the war vale, and the altar at the crown of the world. As long as the stones sang in harmony, both realms lived. Crops grew in yours. Winters ended here. The gates stayed open and honest."

"And then?"

"And then someone cut his throat."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, a ridge of snow calved off a pine and slid silently down through the falling-up flakes.

"Regicide," Isandro said quietly. "That's the word the market-talker used. Vess's word."

"Everyone's word, eventually. It's the hinge everything else swings on." I turned the stick in my fingers. "Here's what the histories agree on. One hundred and six years ago, the king was murdered at the high altar — the third anchor, the one we're not going to yet. Not poisoned, not stabbed in his sleep. Killed *on* the altar, mid-rite, with the stones singing. Deliberate. Ritual. Whoever did it wanted the covenant to break ugly."

"And it did."

"And it did. The stones shattered — not destroyed, thank every mercy, but cracked, scattered, their song broken into discord. The fold started tearing. Your realm kept most of its life; ours kept most of its winter. The gates began dying. And the cold—" I looked up at the snow drifting toward the stars. "The cold isn't weather. It's the wound bleeding. The long winter is the covenant dying slowly, and it will finish dying, and when it does, both realms go with it. Ours freezes solid. Yours—" I met her eyes. "Yours dries up. Blights. Every river finds its mirror-river here and follows it into the dark."

She was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge entirely. "The prophecy book said the winter is the death of a broken covenant. I thought that was poetry."

"It's engineering. The old kings built the pact so it couldn't be undone by force — only reforged. There are rules. The stones can be remade whole, but only at the original altar, and only by—"

I stopped.

It wasn't even a decision, the stopping. It was reflex, deep as breathing, trained into me before I could write my own name: *never say whose blood.* Because the moment I said it, she'd ask whose blood, and the moment she asked, I'd have to decide whether to lie to her outright, and I was already lying to her by omission, and every hour of that lie sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"Felix." Her eyes had narrowed. "Only by what?"

"By royal blood," I said. "Willingly given. That part of the rite has never changed. It's why nobody's managed it in a century. There hasn't been royal blood to give."

That last part was even true. I clung to that. *There hasn't been royal blood to give.* Ashgrave blood didn't count. Ashgrave blood had been struck from the rolls, attainted, exiled, renamed, buried under a century of warden oaths. When I said *royal*, the word meant a dead dynasty, meant history, meant a story in a book.

It did not mean the man sitting across the fire.

Isandro stared at me, and I watched her turn it over — the witch's instinct again, reading the terrain of my face — and for one horrible second I thought she'd see it. But she was tired, and cold, and carrying her own betrayals, and after a moment she just nodded slowly.

"So the prophecy sent me to collect pieces of a puzzle that can't be solved," she said. "Great. Wonderful. The book lured me through a dying gate to do an impossible errand."

"Not impossible," I said. "Unfinished. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm standing?"

"Yes." I poked the fire harder than it needed. "From where you're standing, everything looks impossible. You crossed between worlds four days ago. You've already survived the Undermarket, the Queen's wardens, and me. Impossible is just a word for work you haven't started."

She snorted — but I saw it land, the way her shoulders came down half an inch. Small mercies. I was getting good at counting those.

* * *

We walked the next two days through country that used to be farmland.

I knew it had been farmland because I'd been raised on the maps of it — the warden academy drilled every league of the realm into us, and the lake country had been the breadbasket of the Court before the Breaking. There were hedgerows under the snow, still standing in their lines after a hundred years, black thorn skeletons marking fields that hadn't grown anything since my great-grandparents' time. A farmhouse roofline broke the drifts to our left, caved in, its door hanging open on darkness. A well. A child's swing, rotted to ropes and a plank, swaying gently in a wind that blew the wrong direction.

Isandro kept looking at all of it. At first I thought it was the same curiosity she brought to everything — the defiant, hungry looking that had gotten her named in a prophecy book in the first place. But by the afternoon of the second day I realized it was grief. She was grieving a country she'd never seen, because she could feel what it should have been.

"This is the part they don't tell you in your realm," I said. "That the winter isn't empty. It's *occupied*. Everything the cold took is still here. Just waiting."

"For what?"

"For the stones to sing again." I said it flatly, because the alternative was saying it like a prayer, and I'd sworn off prayers the day I took the warden oath. "Every frozen field, every dead orchard, every gate that won't open — it's all held in suspension. The covenant didn't just bind the realms together. It bound the *damage* in place. Break the binding wrong and—" I shook my head. "There are scholars who think the Breaking didn't cause the winter. It caused the *pause*. And whatever ends the pause decides what wakes up."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a warden. Cheerful is above my pay grade."

That got a real laugh out of her, short and surprised, and I hated how much I liked the sound of it, and hated more what I was going to have to do about the fact that I liked it. Because here was the arithmetic she'd caught me doing, the sum I didn't like:

The prophecy said I would betray her. I had read it myself, years ago, in the sealed archive, in the version with my childhood nightmares written all over it in a dead archivist's hand. And now I was walking her toward the first stone, and every mile closed the distance between her and the moment the prophecy would come due, and I still didn't know which side of it I'd be standing on when it did.

But I knew this: whoever had shattered the covenant had wanted the winter. And whoever wanted the winter now — Vess, with her hunters and her frozen eternal kingdom — would want the stones, and would want the witch who could carry them, and would want the traitor warden who kept handing her chances to fail.

If betrayal was coming for Isandro Zhou, it would have to come through me first. Whatever shape it wore. Even mine.

Especially mine.

* * *

We smelled the lake before we saw it.

Not water — nothing smelled of water anymore — but a kind of pressure in the air, a hush, the way the Undermarket had gone quiet when the Queen's men walked through. Isandro stopped at the top of the final rise and pulled her hood back despite the cold, and I understood why a heartbeat later.

The lake lay below us like a sheet of smoked glass, three miles of black ice ringed by drowned hills. And beneath it, visible through the clear dark depths as a smear of pale stone, was the chapel.

It hadn't sunk all at once. You could see the story of it in the layers — the bell tower leaning at an angle, the roof ridgeline buckled, gravestones scattered across the lakebed like dropped teeth. The covenant-stone was somewhere under all of it, in the crypt below the nave, still singing its broken note into the water. I could feel it from here, a faint wrongness behind my teeth, like a word spoken slightly out of tune.

"You're making that face again," Isandro said. "The arithmetic face."

"I'm making the *this is a terrible idea* face. There's a difference."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Nothing's wrong with it. That's what's wrong with it." I pointed down at the ice. "The chapel's been sinking for a century and the ice has never cracked. The wraiths nest in the nave and they've never left. The whole site is held exactly the way the covenant held it — paused, preserved, waiting. Which means when we crack it open, we're not robbing a ruin. We're *ending a pause*. And neither of us knows what wakes up."

Isandro looked at the lake for a long time. Wind pulled at her hood. Below us, under three fathoms of black water and older ice, something that had been a place of worship waited with the patience of the dead.

"Okay," she said finally. "So we don't know what wakes up. But I know what stays asleep if we don't do this. Both realms. Everyone in them." She shouldered her pack and started down the slope, boots crunching, and threw the words back over her shoulder with that smuggler's grin that never quite reached her eyes anymore. "Come on, Warden. Let's go commit some blasphemy."

I followed her down onto the ice, and beneath my feet, faint as a heartbeat through the floor of the world, the broken stone sang — and this time, I could have sworn, it sang *louder* the closer she came.

As if it knew her name too.
