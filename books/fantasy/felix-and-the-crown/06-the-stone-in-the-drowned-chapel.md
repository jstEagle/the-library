## 6. The Stone in the Drowned Chapel

**FELIX**

The lake had a name once. Every place in Thornfell did, before the winter ate the words off the maps. Now the wardens' charts just called it Blackwater, which was less a name than an apology.

We crossed it at dusk, when the ice was hardest and the light was dying in ways I didn't like. The surface stretched out ahead of us, grey-white and scarred with old pressure cracks, and somewhere beneath our boots — forty feet down, maybe fifty — sat the chapel of Saint Veyra, drowned a century ago when the covenant broke and the land itself began to grieve.

"You're quiet," Isandro said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *wary* quiet. There's a difference. Wary-quiet is when you're counting exits."

She wasn't wrong. I was counting exits, and there weren't any. Behind us lay two days of open ice and the smoke of Queen Vess's hunters on the southern shore. Ahead lay the chapel, its bell tower just breaking the surface like a drowning man's last knuckle, black stone furred with frost.

"The wraiths start at sundown," I said. "Whatever's left of the congregation. The covenant's death leaks into everything it touched, and that chapel was soaked in it. Consecrated ground, drowned in broken oaths. It makes things. Things that remember being people."

Isandro stopped walking. "Remember being—"

"Don't." I caught her arm. "Don't think about them as people. The moment you pity them, they can use it. That's how they get in."

She pulled her arm free, but gently, and looked at me with those eyes that were too old for her face. "You've fought them before."

"Once." I didn't elaborate. She didn't ask. That was one of the strange mercies of her — she knew when not to ask, even though she was the most curious person I'd ever met. Curiosity like hers was usually a weapon. On her it was just hunger.

The sun went down behind the western ridge, and the ice began to sing.

Not the normal groan and tick of freezing water. This was harmonic, deliberate, a low choir note rising up through our boot soles, and along the eastern shore I saw them come: pale shapes rising out of pressure cracks like steam given bones. They didn't walk so much as *arrive*, one after another, drifting across the ice with their faces tilted up, faces that still held the arrangement of features but none of the meaning. Eyes like frozen puddles. Mouths open on nothing.

"How many?" Isandro whispered.

"More than once."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." I drew my blade — cold-forged steel, the only kind that meant anything to them — and felt the old familiar sickness rise in my throat. "Chapel entrance is a stairwell under the bell tower. We go down fast, we don't fight unless we have to, and we don't look at their faces. Agreed?"

"No," she said. "But let's do it anyway."

* * *

**ISANDRO**

Here's what nobody tells you about walking toward monsters: your body knows before you do. My hands went numb first, then my feet, and by the time we reached the bell tower my heart was hammering somewhere up around my ears, and every hedge-craft trick Marisol ever beat into me was screaming the same lesson: *fear feeds them, fear feeds them, fear feeds them.*

So I did what I did best. I got angry instead.

The stairwell door was oak gone soft as cheese, and it gave way under Felix's shoulder like wet bread. Below, black water had climbed the steps and stopped — just stopped, a wall of lake standing perfectly still in the dark, held back by something that had no business holding anything anymore. Past the waterline, candle niches lined the walls, and impossibly, impossibly, half of them were still lit.

"They burn for the dead," Felix said quietly behind me. "Or the dead burn for themselves. Scholars argue."

"Scholars argue about everything. It's how they stay warm."

I heard him huff — almost a laugh — and then we were past the water-wall and into the nave, and I forgot every joke I'd ever learned.

The chapel was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Vaulted ceilings furred with ice that caught the candlelight and threw it back in colors that don't exist above water. Pews drifted weightless, half-sunk, arranged as if the congregation had simply stood up mid-sermon and walked into the deep. And at the far end, where an altar should have been, the water floor glowed.

It came up through the cracks in the tiles like veins of lightning slowed to a crawl — gold light, warm light, the only warm thing in this whole frozen world, pulsing slow and steady as a sleeping heart.

"There," Felix breathed. "That's it. That's the first stone."

I waded toward it — the water came to my knees, then my waist, and it was cold enough to make my teeth ache but it *parted* around the light, warm where the glow touched me — and when I got close enough, I saw it through the flooded altar: a stone the size of a fist, faceted, gold-shot, sitting in a cradle of fused glass where the altar had melted around it a century ago.

And it was singing.

I'd felt it through the ice for two days, faint as a heartbeat. Here it filled my skull, filled my chest, and here's the part I haven't told anyone, not even Felix, especially not Felix: I knew the song. Not the tune — the *grammar*. Hedge-magic is scraps and stitches, charms for chapped hands and finding lost goats, and I had spent nine years learning its language. And this stone sang in that language. Perfectly. Like it had been written by the same hand.

"Hedge-witch," I whispered, without meaning to. "It's singing hedge-craft."

Behind me, Felix went very still. "What did you say?"

"Nothing. Help me get it loose."

The glass cradle resisted, then didn't — the moment my bare fingers touched the stone, the meltwater around us warmed five degrees, ten, and the glass softened like sugar in tea. The stone came free into my palm, and the song *surged*, and every candle in the drowned chapel flared white—

—and the wraiths came down the stairwell.

* * *

**FELIX**

I put myself between her and the door because that was the job, because that was all I had left of the job, and because some traitor part of me had already decided, two days ago on the ice, that it would keep being the job long after the job ended.

There were seven of them. Then nine. They poured down the stairs like fog with intent, and the cold came with them, a cold that had *teeth*, a cold that reached past my cloak and my skin and found the marrow and squeezed.

"Felix—" Her voice cracked high. "The candles are going out!"

They were. One by one, as the wraiths advanced, the flames guttered and died, and I understood with a lurch that the flames were the congregation — whatever was left of them, whatever hadn't become *that* — and the things in the doorway were what happened when the covenant's death finished its work.

"Isandro." I kept my voice level. Level voices keep you alive; that was the first thing they taught us at the wardens' academy, back when the academy still taught things besides despair. "The stone. Can you carry it and run?"

"I can carry it and *swim* if I have to—"

"Then when I say go, you go up the stairs and you don't stop until the far shore. Don't look back. Don't answer them if they call you in my voice."

"They can do that?"

"They did it to my whole patrol, four years ago. I'm the only reason you know that story." I raised my blade. "Go on three. One—"

"*Wait.*" She wasn't running. She was standing in the flooded nave with the stone clutched to her chest, and she was looking at the wraiths with an expression I couldn't read, and the stone's light was climbing her arms like ivy made of dawn. "Felix. Look at them. Really look."

"I told you not to—"

"I know what you told me." Her voice shook, but she didn't lower the stone. "Look anyway."

Against every instinct the academy had hammered into me, I looked.

The nearest wraith had stopped. Ten feet away, drifting, its frozen-puddle eyes fixed not on me, not on the exit — on the stone. On *her*. And its mouth, that open nothing of a mouth, was moving. Shaping words without sound. And slowly, horribly, I realized I could read the shape of them, because I'd seen dying men mouth the same thing a hundred times in a hundred ditches:

*Please.*

"They're not attacking," Isandro whispered. "They never were. They're praying. They've been praying for a hundred years, Felix, and nobody came."

"That's not—" My voice failed. Restarted. "That's not how the reports describe them."

"The reports were written by people who only saw them attack." She took a step forward. Toward them. My heart stopped, physically stopped, I was sure of it. "Because nobody ever brought one of these."

She lifted the stone.

The light went from gold to white to something past white, and the song that had been building in my skull since the ice broke open like a dam, and I felt it not in my ears but in my *oaths* — the crown-oath carved into my bones at nineteen, the one that had been a splinter in me since the day I swore it, and the song passed over that splinter like a hand over a wound, and for one impossible second the oath was *quiet*.

Every wraith in the chapel sank to its knees.

Not collapsed. Knelt. Nine pale shapes folding down into the floodwater with their heads bowed, and the sound they made — the sound finally, finally audible — was a hundred years of held breath let go all at once. The candles relit, every one, flames standing tall and gold, and the water began to warm around our legs, and the pressure cracks in the ceiling began to seal themselves with new ice, clean ice, ice that grew in patterns like lace instead of scars.

"What did you do?" I asked. My voice came out wrecked.

"Nothing." She turned to look at me, and the stone's light was in her eyes, and she was terrified. Genuinely, completely terrified, more than she'd been on the ice, more than she'd been at the gate. "Felix, I didn't *do* anything. I just held it. It did this. It *knew* — it knew the words, it knew the — hedge-craft isn't supposed to be able to do this. Covenant magic is royal work. High work. Wardens study for decades and touch maybe a tenth of this. I'm a *hedge-witch*. I fix sprains and find lost goats."

"Isandro—"

"Why does it fit in my hands?" Her voice cracked clean through. "Why does it sing in *my* language? What am I?"

And I didn't have an answer. I stood in a warming drowned church surrounded by kneeling ghosts and the woman I was prophesied to betray, and I had no answer, and worse — I had a suspicion. A suspicion shaped like a childhood nightmare, shaped like a prophecy book I'd glimpsed as a boy, shaped like the way the stone had sung louder the closer she came.

*I know why,* I almost said. *I think I've known since the gate.*

Instead I said: "We should take the second stairwell. The east one. Before the warmth draws attention."

She stared at me. "Did you just hear what I—"

"Every word. And I'll help you find the answer, I swear it on whatever's left of my honor. But answers don't keep, Isandro. Hunters do. And right now there are nine of Vess's best crossing the south ice, and they felt that light from thirty miles away."

* * *

**ISANDRO**

He was right. That was the worst part. He was right, and he was hiding something — I'd learned his tells by now, the way his jaw set a half-second before a lie, the way he looked at my left hand when he thought I wouldn't notice — and I let him be right anyway, because he was also correct about the hunters, and because some small stubborn part of me wanted to believe that whatever he was keeping back, he was keeping it to protect me rather than himself.

We climbed the east stairwell as the chapel sealed itself behind us, the candles burning gold in the dark like a congregation finally allowed to rest. At the top, the night air hit my face, and the stars were out — real stars, the bruised-violet sky torn open for once — and the stone sat warm against my sternum where I'd tucked it inside my coat, humming against my heartbeat like it had always lived there.

The wraiths did not follow. But as we crossed the ice toward the far shore, I looked back once — breaking my own rule, because rules were for people whose lives fit inside them — and saw the bell tower standing black against the stars, and in its belfry, faint as breath on glass, a light was glowing. Steady. Golden. Watching us go.

And the stone against my chest pulsed once, twice, in rhythm with my own heart, and then — I would swear this on any grave you like — it pulsed a third time when neither of ours did.

A rhythm that wasn't mine. Wasn't his. Older than both.

Somewhere out on the southern ice, a hunting horn sounded, long and low, and Felix's hand found my elbow and steered me north, and we ran.

But all the way across the lake, through the stitch in my side and the burn in my lungs, one question kept pace with me better than any hunter could:

If the stone knew my name —

what else, in this world, already did?
