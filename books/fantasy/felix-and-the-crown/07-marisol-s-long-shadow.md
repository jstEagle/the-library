## 7. Marisol's Long Shadow

The stone did not want to sleep.

Isandro had wrapped it in three layers of wool and tucked it at the bottom of her pack, and still she could feel it through the canvas and the goose-down of the inn's thin blanket — a hum, low and patient, like a wasp trapped in a jar. The Drowned Chapel lay a day behind them now. Her palms still remembered the cold of it, the way the ice-wraiths had come apart when her magic touched the stone, as if the wraiths had recognized something in her. As if they'd been waiting.

*Why me.* The question had been circling since the lake. She'd shoved it down under practical things — dry socks, food, the route Felix scratched into the frost on the window each morning. But the stone hummed, and the hum said *ask me*, and around the third hour of a night too cold for sleep, she asked.

She didn't touch it with her hands. That would have been stupid. She touched it with the thread — the witch's life-thread every hedge-witch learned to spin out from behind her navel, fine as spider silk, the thing that let you feel weather coming in your teeth and find lost goats and hex a rival's churn so her butter came out grey. Marisol had taught her that. Marisol had taught her everything.

The thread found the stone, and the stone opened like a door.

* * *

Snow first. Thornfell snow, falling upward, but wrong — this was a memory, and memories don't care which way water goes. A study she had never seen, all dark wood and darker books, candles burning with flames that bent sideways toward nothing. And Marisol.

Not old Marisol. Not the stooped hedge-witch with the burn-scarred hands who smelled of tallow and feverfew. This Marisol stood straight, dark-haired, maybe forty, wearing robes cut finer than anything Isandro had ever seen her in — court robes, or something older than court robes. She stood over a table where a book lay open, and even in the memory Isandro knew that book. She'd slept with it under her head two nights ago while its pages glowed against her scalp.

A man's voice, off-frame, dry as paper: "You're certain it's her?"

"The prophecy names her." Marisol's voice — younger, harder, but unmistakably hers. "Isandro Zhou. Born under the split moon. The book has never been wrong."

"Books are written by people."

"This one wasn't." A pause, and then Marisol laughed, and Isandro felt the laugh go through her like a fishhook. She'd heard that laugh a thousand times, aimed at other people, at customers who haggled too hard, at Isandro herself when she botched a cantrip. It had always sounded fond. Here, in the candlelight of a room a hundred years gone, it sounded like a knife being drawn across a strop.

"You understand what I'm offering," Marisol said. "The gate is dying. Both realms are dying with it. Someone sealed the covenant with royal blood, someone broke it, and the only way to reforge it—" she tapped the page, "—is written here. All I need is a vessel. Someone the stones will answer. Someone with enough raw thread to carry the rite and not enough training to ask questions."

"And if she asks them anyway?"

"Then I'll have spent twenty years teaching her not to."

The scene folded like wet paper and dealt another.

* * *

A cottage kitchen. Isandro knew this one — knew the scar on the table where she'd carved her initials at seven and gotten the switch for it, knew the smell of bread and bitterroot tea. But the woman at the table wasn't her. It was another girl, maybe ten, brown-skinned like Isandro, with the same restless hands, and she was crying.

Marisol sat across from her, patient as stone.

"It hurts because you're doing it wrong," Marisol said. "You're pulling the thread from your fear. Pull it from your wanting instead. Wanting holds its shape."

"I want to go home," the girl whispered.

"No, child." Marisol reached across and covered the girl's small hands with her scarred ones, and the tenderness in the gesture made Isandro's stomach turn, because she had felt exactly that gesture, exactly that warmth, a hundred times, and had believed in it completely. "You want to matter. There's a difference. Home is just where you happen to be standing when you figure that out."

The girl pulled the thread. It came out gold and steady, and Marisol smiled, and somewhere above them, faint as a heartbeat, a waygate flickered.

*There were others before me.*

The thought arrived whole, and the vision confirmed it without mercy. The scene shifted again — a boy this time, fourteen, coughing blood after his first attempt to channel a covenant-stone; a twin sister who burned out her thread entirely and spent the rest of her short life unable to feel weather in her teeth; a third, a fourth, a fifth, each one trained, tested, measured against the prophecy's requirements like lambs held up against a butcher's chart. Each one discarded. Some died. One simply walked into the snow one morning and Marisol did not follow.

And then Isandro saw herself — saw herself at six years old, an orphan off a smuggler's cart, snot-nosed and furious, biting the hand of the woman who'd bought her passage. Saw Marisol look down at her and say, softly, to no one: *"Oh, you'll do."*

* * *

Isandro came back to her body all at once, the way you surface from deep water, and the first thing she did was vomit into the chamber pot.

The second thing she did was try to tear the thread out of herself.

She got as far as gripping the invisible line behind her navel with her mind's fingers before some animal instinct slammed the door — because the thread *was* her, pulling it out wasn't surgery, it was suicide, and whatever else Marisol had made her, she hadn't made her stupid. Isandro knelt on the frozen floorboards of the inn room, shaking, sweat freezing at her hairline, and laughed until the laugh turned into something else and she pressed both fists against her mouth to keep Felix from hearing in the next room.

Twenty years. Twenty years of chopping bitterroot and memorizing herb-lore and practicing thread-work until her nose bled. Twenty years of believing she'd been *chosen* — that the meanest, sharpest orphan on the smuggler roads had been picked out by the greatest hedge-witch in the passes because there was something in her worth finding. And the truth was there was something in her worth *using*, and Marisol had known it before Isandro could write her own name, had known it from a book, had built her like a mason builds a wall — course by course, brick by brick, with mortar made of exactly enough love to hold the structure up.

*Enough love to hold it up.* That was the worst part. That was the part that kept her kneeling on the floor long after the shaking stopped. Because the vision hadn't lied about that either. She'd seen it — seen the flicker of something real in young Marisol's face when the six-year-old bit her, something almost like delight, almost like affection. Marisol hadn't been faking twenty years. She'd simply been willing to spend real love on a tool, the way a farrier spends good iron on a horse he means to ride hard.

The stone hummed at the bottom of her pack. Patient. Pleased, almost.

"Don't," Isandro told it. Her voice came out scraped raw. "Don't you dare sound smug. You're a rock. You're a rock that drowned a chapel full of people, and I fished you out because a book told me to, and every single thing that's happened to me since I was six years old has been somebody else's plan."

The hum didn't change. Stones don't apologize.

* * *

Felix knocked at dawn.

She heard him hesitate outside the door — heard the particular quality of his hesitation, the weight shifting from foot to foot, the breath taken and released. He'd been doing that more lately. Since the chapel. Since her magic had lit up the stone like a struck bell and the ice-wraiths had bowed, practically *bowed*, and neither of them had said a word about it on the road.

"Isandro. There's tea. And we should move before the light does."

"Come in."

He looked terrible, which was normal; Felix Ashgrave wore exhaustion the way other men wore livery, fitted to him by years of practice. What wasn't normal was the careful blankness he put on when he looked at her now, the warden's mask sliding down over whatever lived underneath. He set the tea on the table between them and didn't sit.

"You were talking in the night."

Heat crawled up her neck. "I talk in my sleep. Ask anyone I've ever shared a campfire with."

"Not words." He turned his cup a quarter-turn, not drinking. "Singing. Low. In a language the wardens use for covenant-liturgy. I served the crown eleven years and I've heard it twice, both times from dying men." His eyes came up, grey and level. "You were speaking it perfectly."

The tea steamed between them. Isandro thought about lying, and then thought about Marisol's hands covering a crying child's hands, teaching her to pull the thread from her wanting, and decided she was done being managed by people who loved her in useful amounts.

"I went into the stone last night," she said. "On purpose. I wanted answers about why it responds to me, and it gave me answers, and I'd give them back if I could." She wrapped her hands around her cup because otherwise they'd shake visibly, and she'd rather freeze than let him see. "Do you know how I ended up with Marisol? I assumed you'd pieced it together. Runaway apprentice, tragic backstory, et cetera."

"Orphan. Bought off a smuggler's caravan."

"Bought." She laughed, short and ugly. "That's the word I always used too. Bought. Like it was luck. Like the meanest little orphan on that cart got lucky." She told him. Not all of it — she couldn't make her mouth form the discarded children, not yet, maybe not ever — but the shape of it. The book. The vessel. The twenty years of deliberate construction. The waygate flickering over the cottage roof the day a ten-year-old girl pulled her first clean thread.

Felix listened the way he did everything, completely and silently, and when she finished he said, "The prophecy named you before you were born."

"Before I could walk. Before I could *talk*. There was no version of my life where I chose any of this, Felix. She built the choosing out from under me. Every skill I have, every spell, every — " My voice cracked and I hated it. "Every time I pulled the thread from my wanting, like she taught me, I was just — practicing. Rehearsing for a role I never auditioned for."

"And the stones answer you because she trained you to carry them."

"Because she trained me to *be carried by* them. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of those is a person and one of those is a sack." She drained the tea scalding hot, because pain was simpler than this. "You know what the worst part is? I ran away from her because I found tonight's date in that book. I thought I was running *from* the prophecy. I was running exactly along the groove she'd cut for me. Straight to the gate. Straight through. Straight to you."

Something moved behind his eyes at that — the nightmare thing, the thing he'd hinted at twice and swallowed both times. *The warden who betrays her.* She saw him decide, again, not to say it, and she was too empty to push.

"So what now?" he asked quietly.

"Now?" Isandro set down the cup. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the telling, and what had replaced the shaking was colder and much more useful. "Now I stop being a sack. Marisol wanted a vessel that wouldn't ask questions. Fine. I'm asking them. Why do the stones answer me — really? Not because a book said so. The wraiths didn't bow because of a book. Something in me *resonates*, and Marisol didn't put that there, she found it, which means it was mine before she ever got her scarred hands on it." She stood, slung the pack over her shoulder, and felt the stone's hum settle against her spine like a second heartbeat. "She planned my whole life around delivering me to that altar. So my whole life from today is mine. Every step. Starting with the next one."

"Which is?"

"North. You said the second stone sings in a war-camp amphitheater where two armies are pretending to have a truce." She pulled the door open onto a morning the color of dirty pewter, breath smoking, the frozen city waking below them with its bells and its hunger. "Two armies, one stolen stone, and me — a hedge-witch with no right to any of this except that I refuse to be anyone's instrument ever again."

Felix followed her out. At the stairhead he caught her arm, gentle, and for one unguarded moment the warden's mask slipped and she saw what lived under it: fear, and guilt, and something warmer that he kept locked in a room even deeper than hers.

"For what it's worth," he said, "she was wrong about one thing. You're not a vessel. Vessels hold what's poured into them." His mouth twitched, almost a smile. "You pour yourself out. There's a difference."

"There's a difference," she agreed, and meant it, and filed the warmth of it away like contraband.

They took the north road together, and behind them, in the bottom of a wool-wrapped pack, a covenant-stone began — very softly, very patiently — to sing.

It was still singing when the riders appeared on the ridge behind them, black cloaks snapping in the upward-falling snow, and the Queen's colors burning on their shields like frost-fire.

Felix saw them first. He went very still, the way men do when the past finally catches up, and said, "Those are my brother-in-arms. And Isandro — they aren't here to take prisoners."
