## 8. The Queen's Hunters

The border shrine had been a place of worship once. Now it was a place where the snow forgot how to fall.

Felix felt it before he saw it — that wrongness in the air, the hush of a world holding its breath. The shrine stood at the seam between two provinces, a squat stone circle roofed in slate gone green with frost-lichen, its carved saints worn faceless by a century of ice. Snow drifted around it in slow spirals, neither rising nor falling, just hanging, as if the whole landscape were a painting someone had abandoned mid-stroke.

He stopped at the tree line and put out an arm.

Isandro walked into it and glared up at him. She'd been doing a lot of glaring since the drowned chapel — three days of it, punctuated by questions he couldn't answer and silences he didn't want to explain. The covenant-stone rode against her sternum in a sling of braided cord, and even through her coat Felix could feel it humming, a low wrong note like a struck bell that wouldn't finish dying.

"Why are we stopping?" she asked. "You said the ford was half a day."

"The ford is half a day." He nodded at the shrine. "But we're not crossing there."

"Then why—"

"Because I know this place." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "I helped build this checkpoint. Six years ago. Before." He left the *before* hanging there, the way he left most things about his past hanging, and Isandro's eyes narrowed the way they always did when he did that — sharp, suspicious, cataloguing another entry in whatever ledger of grievances she kept against him.

"You helped build a *shrine*?"

"I helped build a *killbox*." He crouched, brushing snow from a flat stone near the tree line. Beneath it, half-eaten by rust, lay an iron ring bolted to bedrock. "Wardens' waystation. Signal relay. There'll be a garrison rotation passing through every nine days, and there's a hunter cell stationed at the ford itself because the border shrines are choke points for smugglers, refugees, and anyone else Her Radiance doesn't want moving north." He looked up at her. "We're all three."

Isandro crouched beside him, breath fogging. "So we go around."

"There is no around. The White Waste starts north of here. East is the Queen's road, west is a glacier field that will kill us slower but just as certainly." He rubbed his jaw, feeling three days of stubble. "We go through. Fast, quiet, and we pray the rotation is late."

"When has praying ever worked for you?"

"It hasn't. That's why I'm a warden."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the stone against her chest pulsed — once, hard, like a heartbeat skipping — and her hand flew to it, and the almost-smile died.

"What was that?"

Felix didn't answer, because he was already looking at the shrine. At the snow hanging motionless above it. At the way the light through the trees had gone thin and silver, the particular quality of light he'd learned, in six years of hunting men across this frozen country, meant one thing only:

*Someone had lit a beacon.*

"They know we're coming," he said quietly. "They've known since the chapel."

---

The beacon was a witch-light set into the shrine's oldest saint, burning violet-white in the hollow where the statue's heart should have been. It threw shadows that moved wrong, lagging behind their owners, and under its light the hanging snow turned to a curtain of suspended glass.

Felix counted the hunters from the treeline: four visible, two positions he could infer from sightlines, and at least one more unaccounted for, which was the number that killed people. They wore no livery — Queen Vess's hunters never did — just grey wool and white furs, faces masked against the cold, crossbows slung with the casual competence of people who had done this so many times the killing had become paperwork.

And at their center, arms crossed, watching the tree line with the patience of a man who had all night and knew exactly what was coming out of it:

Sergeant Dain Vosk.

Felix's stomach dropped through the ice of the world.

He'd known, in the abstract, that it might come to this. He'd told himself stories on the road — that Vess would send strangers, that the hunters would be nameless, that fate would have the decency to make this impersonal. But of course it wasn't. Of course it was Dain. Because the Queen was thorough, and because the Queen remembered which of her wardens had trained together, bled together, buried each other's dead together — and because she understood, better than anyone, that the surest way to catch a traitor was to send the person whose betrayal would hurt him most.

Dain had pulled him out of the Verre Marshes with a rope and a broken arm. Felix had carried Dain eleven miles on his back after the Saltgate ambush, singing army songs to keep him conscious. They had shared a tent for four winters. Dain had been the first person Felix told — drunk, stupid, whispering in the dark — that he sometimes dreamed of a girl made of prophecy, and that the dreams scared him more than any battlefield ever had.

Now Dain stood in the shrine where they'd planted the beacon together, waiting to bring him home in chains or not at all.

"Felix." Dain's voice carried across the frozen clearing, unhurried, almost gentle. He hadn't raised a visor; the mask stayed down, but Felix would have known that voice underwater, in fire, in the dark at the end of the world. "I can see your footprints, Ashgrave. You always were too proud to cover your trail."

Felix stepped out of the trees.

It was, tactically, the worst decision available. Every part of him that had survived six years of warden work screamed about it. But if he stayed hidden, they would sweep the forest, and they would find Isandro, and Isandro — carrying a covenant-stone that sang her name — would be taken to Vess alive, and everything after that was a door he refused to imagine opening.

So he walked into the open, hands loose at his sides, and heard Isandro's sharp intake of breath behind him, and kept walking.

"Dain Vosk," he said. "Sergeant. Still keeping bad company, I see."

A ripple went through the hunters. Crossbows came up — not aimed, not yet, but ready, the way you hold a weapon when your officer hasn't given the word. Dain lifted one gloved hand and the bows held.

"Bad company." Dain laughed, and there was something broken in the laugh, something that had been broken for a long time. "That's rich, coming from you. Do you know what they're saying in the garrisons, Felix? Desertion. Theft of crown intelligence. Conspiracy with a foreign witch." He shook his head slowly. "I told them it was lies. I told them you'd been bewitched. I fought for you in the officers' hall until they threatened to have *me* tested for hexwork." A pause. "Was I wrong? Are you bewitched? Because that would be easier. That I could fix."

"You weren't wrong to fight for me." Felix stopped ten paces out, close enough to talk, far enough that a crossbow bolt would have to travel. "But I'm not bewitched, Dain. I'm awake. There's a difference, and it took me thirty years to learn it."

"Awake." Dain spat, and the spittle froze before it hit the ground. "You sound like the old stories. Like the regicide's apologists. 'The crown is corrupt, the covenant is broken, the winter is the crown's fault.'" He gestured at the hanging snow, the dead shrine, the whole dying country. "You know what I see, Felix? I see a kingdom that survived. Barely. Ugly, cold, starving — but *surviving*. And now you come back with a hedge-witch and a stolen relic, and you want me to believe you're saving us." His voice hardened. "Give me the stone. Give me the witch. Come home, and I swear on the Saltgate dead I'll get your sentence commuted to exile. You'll live. Isn't that what all this running is for? To *live*?"

Behind him, Felix heard Isandro moving — low, quiet, circling through the trees toward the shrine's flank. Good. Smart. He needed maybe ninety seconds of Dain's grief to buy her the angle.

"That's not what this is about," Felix said. "You've read the same reports I have. The Waste is expanding. The thaw-line retreated forty miles last spring. Both realms are dying, Dain, and Vess isn't trying to stop it — she's trying to *own* it. You've seen her new edicts. Eternal winter, eternal crown. She wants the stones so nothing ever changes again."

"And you want them so everything does." Dain's mask tilted. "You always did think you were the hero of the story, Ashgrave. Even in training. Even bleeding in the mud, you'd look up at the sky like it owed you something." He sighed, and the sigh was the saddest sound Felix had heard in years. "I loved you like a brother. That's why I'm asking twice. Drop the sword. Walk to me. Let me save your life the way you saved mine at Saltgate. One last time. Brother to brother."

Every word landed like a nail. Felix let them.

"I can't," he said. "Not because of the witch. Not because of the stone. Because I finally read the whole prophecy, Dain, start to finish, and do you know what it says?" He laughed, short and bitter. "It says I betray someone. My whole life I thought it meant I'd fail her. Turns out the only question was *who*." He spread his arms. "So here I am. Choosing."

Dain went very still. "Choosing what?"

"Who I am when the story ends."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment, in a clearing where even the snow had forgotten how to fall — Felix believed it might end without blood. That Dain would lower the crossbow, or turn away, or simply stand aside the way brothers in stories do when the truth finally reaches them.

Then Isandro broke cover.

She didn't mean to. Later, Felix would piece it together: the stone pulsing again, harder, reacting to the shrine, to the beacon, to something in the ley-vein beneath the border — and Isandro stumbling out of the treeline with her hand clamped over her chest, glowing faintly at the seams like a lantern with a cracked shade.

Every crossbow in the clearing swung toward her.

"No!" Felix drew and threw himself sideways, and the world became very simple.

Bolts. Three of them, fast, professional. One took his cloak; one buried itself in a saint's faceless head; one — the third — flew true toward Isandro's throat, and Felix did the only thing there was to do, the thing he'd been doing since the day he met her, the thing the prophecy had been winding toward all along:

He got in the way.

The bolt hit his shoulder instead of her neck. He barely felt it — later he would, oh, he would — because Dain was shouting orders, and the hunters were reloading, and Isandro was screaming his name in a voice that cracked something loose in his chest, and the stone was *singing* now, a high keening note that frosted the air and made the hanging snow shiver like wind chimes.

And Dain — Dain had a clear shot. Ten paces, broadside, Felix tangled between him and the witch. Any sergeant in the Queen's service takes it. Any sergeant ends this here, collects the reward, goes home a hero of the realm.

Dain hesitated.

One heartbeat. Two. Long enough for Felix to see his eyes above the mask — wide, wet, *horrified* — long enough for Felix to understand that Dain couldn't do it either, that love cuts both ways, that the prophecy's trap had teeth on every side—

Long enough for the third hunter, the one Felix had never accounted for, to step out of the shrine's shadow with a drawn blade and go for Isandro's back.

Felix didn't decide. His body decided, six years of muscle and one week of something deeper than muscle. He crossed the distance in four strides, caught the hunter's wrist, turned the blade — and drove it up under the man's jaw, through the brainstem, clean, the killing stroke he'd taught a hundred recruits in a hundred yards.

The hunter dropped. The mask rolled off. And Felix looked down into the face of Tam Wrenlow, nineteen years old, whom he'd taught to fish, whom he'd promoted himself, whose mother had baked him honey-bread on leaving-day and asked Felix, *asked him*, to look after her boy.

The clearing went silent except for the stone's fading song.

Dain stared at the body. At Felix. At the blood freezing black on the shrine stones. When he spoke, his voice was the voice of a man reading his own death warrant.

"Oh, Felix." He lowered the crossbow — not in surrender, in mourning. "What have you done?"

"What I had to." Felix's shoulder screamed. His hands wouldn't stop shaking. He found Isandro's eyes across the clearing — white-faced, gripping the stone, alive — and held onto them like a rope over a cliff. "What I should have done a year ago."

"This isn't—" Dain's composure fractured, reassembled, fractured again. "There are orders, Felix. Dead or alive. *Dead or alive.* You know what happens to me if I go back and report—"

"I know." Felix stepped over Tam's body, gently, the way you step over a sleeping friend. "That's why you're not going back."

The remaining hunters had frozen, crossbows half-raised, suddenly uncertain — because their sergeant hadn't fired, and their sergeant's hesitation was contagious, and loyalty is a strange animal that dies quickly in cold air. Felix raised his voice, and it came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

"Hunters of the Ninth Cell. Your sergeant knows me. Your sergeant knows what I'm telling you is true: the Queen sent you to die on this border chasing a prophecy she's terrified of. Go home. Tell them Felix Ashgrave killed Tam Wrenlow and vanished into the Waste. Or stay, and find out what a warden does when he stops being polite."

Nobody moved. Then, one by one, like lights going out, the hunters faded backward into the hanging snow, taking their wounded pride and their unspent bolts, until only Dain remained.

Brother and brother-in-arms, ten paces apart, with a dead boy between them.

"You could have run," Dain said at last. "All these weeks. You could have stayed a rumor. Now you're a killer of your own, and there's no coming back from Wrenlow's blood. You understand that. There's no exile soft enough, no border far enough. You've burned it, Felix. All of it."

"Yes," Felix said.

And the terrible thing — the thing he would carry longer than the bolt-scar, longer than Tam's face — was that underneath the grief, underneath the shame, underneath the horror of his own hands, there was a small cold flame of *relief*. Because the choice had been made for him at last, written in blood he couldn't undo, and he would never have to wonder again which side of the line he stood on.

He was done choosing the crown. The crown had chosen for him, and he had answered.

"Take care of his mother," Felix said. "Tell her whatever gets her through the winter. I'll carry the rest."

Dain looked at him for a long time. Something moved behind his eyes — hatred, maybe, or its exhausted cousin, understanding.

"If I see you again, Ashgrave, I have orders."

"Then pray you don't."

Dain shouldered his crossbow, knelt briefly beside Tam's body — one gloved hand resting on the boy's chest, a soldier's farewell, quick and wordless — and walked into the snow without looking back.

---

Isandro found him at the tree line, sitting against a pine with his shoulder bleeding through his fingers, watching the shrine shrink behind them.

"You're hit."

"Bolt graze. Through the meat, missed the bone." He tried to wave her off and failed, mostly because his arm wouldn't cooperate. She knelt anyway, cut his sleeve open with a smuggler's knife, and whistled low at the wound.

"Graze. Sure. You're lucky it's a graze, because I lost my needle-kit in the lake, and my stitching looks like a spider's funeral." She worked as she talked, packing the wound with moss and binding it with strips of his own cloak, her hands steady, her voice anything but. "You idiot. You absolute — you stepped in front of a *bolt*, Felix. For me. Again. Do you have any idea what that does to a person? To watch someone keep deciding you're worth dying for?"

"Better than watching them decide you're not."

She tied the bandage off, harder than necessary, and sat back on her heels. In the strange silver light, with frost in her hair and fury in her face, she looked young and old at once.

"Tam Wrenlow," she said quietly. "You said his name like you knew him."

"I trained him. I taught him to shoot. I taught him to *read maps*." Felix closed his eyes. "His mother gave him to the wardens because she thought the crown would feed him. The crown fed him and spent him, and I held the sword, and somewhere in the middle I called it duty." He opened his eyes. "That's what Vess builds, Isandro. Boys like Tam, stacked like cordwood under a throne of ice. I helped stack them. Today I stopped."

Isandro was quiet for a while. The stone hummed against her chest, softer now, almost gentle — as if it, too, were listening.

"So that's it," she said finally. "No bridge back. No pardon, no exile, no clever bargain. Just us, and the Waste, and two more stones, and a queen who wants you dead." She swallowed. "Are you sorry?"

He thought about lying. He was, apparently, done with that.

"No," he said. "God help me, no. I'm sorry for Tam. I'll be sorry for Tam until I die. But the rest of it?" He looked north, where the horizon had begun to glow with the pale, hungry light of the White Waste. "The rest of it feels like putting down something heavy I've been carrying since I was nine years old."

Isandro studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she reached out and gripped his good shoulder — brief, fierce, gone again before he could decide what it meant.

"Well then, deserter," she said, standing, pulling him up after her. "Since we're both traitors now, I suppose you'd better tell me what's actually in that prophecy. All of it. Tonight. By the fire. Because I'm done learning your secrets from hunters and haunted stones."

Felix looked north. The Waste waited, white and endless, and somewhere inside it, the second stone was singing a song he'd hoped never to hear.

"There's a shepherd's bothy, half a day on," he said. "Old warden cache. We can make it by dark if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

He started walking. "Then we learn what the winter wants from us a little sooner than planned."

Behind them, at the border shrine, the beacon-light guttered and died — and for the first time in a hundred years, in that one small clearing, the snow began, very softly, to fall.
