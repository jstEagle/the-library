## 9. A Night Without Snow

The blizzard stopped at dusk.

Not faded. Not eased. *Stopped* — the way a held breath stops — and the silence that followed was worse than the wind had been. For three days we had crossed the high moor with snow driving sideways and Felix navigating by a compass that spun like a drunk, and now, all at once, the air went still and the flakes hung suspended around us like a held curtain.

"That's not weather," I said.

"No," Felix said. "It's attention."

We ran for the bothy. I'd seen it from the ridge an hour before — a low stone hump with a turf roof, one of the shepherd shelters that dotted this stretch of the Waste like knuckles on a clenched fist. The door was a slab of driftwood on leather hinges, and it groaned when Felix shouldered it open, and inside there was nothing but a hearth, a stool, a pile of old bracken, and four walls that had kept worse things out than cold.

Felix got a fire going from a twist of waxed bark in his pack while I checked the corners out of habit — smuggler's habit, checking caches, checking exits, checking whether anyone had been here before us and left anything behind. There were scratches on the lintel, tallies in some script I didn't know, and a child's mitten frozen stiff in the bracken pile, which I decided not to think about.

Outside, the snow still hung in the air, unmoving, lit faintly violet by the bruised sky. It looked like the inside of a bell jar. It looked like something waiting.

"Is it watching us?" I asked.

"Everything's watching us," Felix said. "The question is whether it's reporting to Vess or to the winter itself. Lately I've stopped being sure those are different employers." He fed the fire another knot of peat someone had left stacked by the hearth, dry as bone after a hundred years under turf. "The stone's quiet?"

I touched my chest where the first covenant-stone rode in its sling against my skin. Since the drowned chapel it had been a presence there, a second heartbeat with opinions. Tonight it was silent as the sky outside.

"Quiet."

"Good. Quiet stones mean it hasn't found us yet." He said *it* the way you'd say a name you didn't want to say.

We ate hardtack and smoked fish and drank water melted over the fire, and neither of us talked, because talking costs warmth and we'd learned that on the moor. But eventually the fire burned down to a good orange core and the bothy warmed enough that my fingers stopped aching, and the silence changed shape — became less like endurance and more like a room two people are sitting in.

That's when Felix said, "You should know what the prophecy says about me."

I'd been half asleep against the wall, the stone humming its low nothing-hum under my palm. His voice came through the drowse like a hook through cloth.

"I'm listening," I said, which was a lie; I sat up.

He was looking at the fire. In its light his face lost the warden's flatness and became just a face — young, tired, with a scar through one eyebrow he'd never explained and I'd never asked about. "There's a verse. It's not one of the famous ones. Wardens get taught it early because it's about wardens — about what happens when the covenant breaks all the way down." He turned his hands toward the fire, palms up, like a man showing he holds no weapons. "It says: *When the vessel crosses the falling gate, the crown-sworn will raise her up, and the crown-sworn will cast her down.*"

The fire popped. Outside, the hanging snow did not move.

"Cast her down how?" I asked, though I already knew. You always already know, with prophecies. That's their whole trick.

"The text is ambiguous," he said. "Death, betrayal, ruin — the scholars argue. But the shape of it is simple. Whoever I am to you, whatever we—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The prophecy doesn't say I'll fail you, Isandro. It says I'll be the instrument of it. And I have known that since I was nine years old, since the first time I dreamed your face standing in a doorway of violet light, years before you crossed any gate at all."

My skin prickled. "You dreamed me. Before."

"Every winter of my childhood. Same dream. A girl in a smuggler's coat, standing in a broken archway, looking back at me like I'd done something terrible." He finally looked at me then, and his eyes were steady in a way that cost him something; I could see the cost, the way you can see a current under ice. "So when I found you in the holding cell at Thornfell garrison, I recognized you before you said your name. And when I let you go instead of executing you — which was my duty, which would have been the sane thing — I told myself I was defying fate. That I could stand between you and the prophecy like a wall."

"And now?"

"Now I've killed a brother-in-arms for you. Now I'm a traitor to the crown I swore to, hiding in a shepherd's hut with the first stone of a dead covenant, and every step we take toward reforging it is a step down the road the verse described." His jaw tightened. "I don't know if I'm defying the prophecy or walking straight into its arms. I genuinely do not know. And you deserve to travel with someone who isn't a loaded weapon pointed at your back."

The honest part of me wanted to say: *I knew. Of course I knew. Marisol's books had verses about the crown-sworn too, and I read them all, and I came anyway.*

But before I could decide whether to be honest, honesty happened to me instead.

"You're not the only one who's been carrying a secret," I said.

Felix waited. He was good at waiting. It was one of the most annoying things about him.

"Marisol didn't take me in off the street," I said. "Everyone assumes that — the hedge-witch with the soft heart who adopted the gutter orphan. She tells it that way herself, when she wants something from people." I pulled my knees up, wrapped my arms around them. The stone against my sternum felt suddenly heavy, like a coin placed on a corpse's eye. "She chose me. Out of maybe forty children in the river districts that year. She tested us — little games, little cantrips disguised as street magic, and she watched how each of us answered. And she picked me because of how I answered. Because my hedge-magic didn't push back against hers. It *resonated*. She told me once, years later, drunk on plum wine, that finding me was like tuning a lute and hearing a second string sing."

"What does a hedge-witch need with a resonant apprentice?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I spent six years thinking I was loved. Then I started reading her private correspondence — smuggler's habit again, never trust a lock — and I found letters to a colleague in the capital. About vessels. About the old covenant, and the theory that a properly prepared witch could act as a living key for covenant-work. About how the resonance had to be cultivated young, shaped, *owned*, before the vessel developed attachments or a will of her own." My voice stayed level, which surprised me. "She wasn't raising an apprentice, Felix. She was growing a tool. Six years of care and training and — and I thought, love — all of it husbandry. And the worst part, the part I can't stop chewing on, is that she was *good* at it. Kind, even. A well-tended tool lasts longer."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the walls, the bothy ticked as the cold pressed in.

"So I ran," I said. "Not because I was brave. Because I read my own price tag and couldn't stand the smell of the shop. And three days later I found a book in a smuggler's cache that had my name in it and tonight's date, and here I am, sitting in a prophecy with a man who's fated to destroy me, carrying a stone that sings to me like I'm its mother." I looked at him across the fire. "Marisol trained me to resonate with covenant-stones, Felix. Whatever the prophecy thinks I am — whatever the *stone* thinks I am — she built it. On purpose. Years before either realm knew the covenant was going to break open."

Felix was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "You could have let me keep believing you were just a runaway."

"You told me your truth."

"Yeah, well." He almost smiled. "Mine was cheaper."

* * *

We banked the fire and laid out the bracken into two beds, and then, without discussing it, dragged them together against the cold — practicality, nothing else, the same arithmetic that had governed every night since the chapel. We lay shoulder to shoulder, backs to the warm stones of the hearth, boots on, knives loose in their sheaths, because neither of us had forgotten how to sleep dangerous even in a warm room.

The hanging snow outside caught the last violet light and held it, thousands of suspended stars that weren't stars.

"Felix," I said, to the ceiling. "If the prophecy comes true. If you cast me down, whatever that means. Will you tell me first? Before you do it? So I'm not standing in a doorway someday wondering when it happened?"

He was silent so long I thought he'd fallen asleep.

"I've thought about that every night since the garrison," he said finally. "And I used to think the answer was to stay away from you. Put distance between us, hand you off to someone else, keep myself dull and far away so the blade never got close." A breath. "Then I watched you walk into a lake full of ice-wraiths to pull that stone out of a drowning chapel, and I realized distance was never actually on the table. Not for me. Maybe not for the prophecy either — maybe that's why the verse exists, because whoever wrote it saw this exact night coming and wanted me to at least have to choose it awake."

"Choose what awake?"

"This." He turned toward me. In the ember-light his eyes were dark and very clear. "Choosing you knowing what I might be. That's the only defiance I've got left, Isandro. Not running from the verse — I tried that, it doesn't work, fate is a tide — but refusing to do it blind. If I ever cast you down, I'll do it knowing exactly what I'm destroying, and I'll carry it, and I won't pretend it was written instead of chosen. That's all I can promise. It's not much."

"It's more than anyone's promised me," I said. "Marisol promised me a future and it was a harness."

And then — I want to be able to say I thought it through, that I weighed it, that some strategic part of me calculated the risk. I can't. What happened was simpler and stupider than that. He was close, and warm, and honest, and I had spent my whole life being handled carefully by people who wanted something from me, and here was a man telling me the worst thing about himself with his hands open.

I kissed him.

Or he kissed me. The truth is the space between those sentences didn't exist for a moment — there was just the fire's heat on one side and his warmth on the other and the taste of smoke and hardtack, and his hand coming up to cradle the back of my head like I was something breakable, which nobody had ever done, which nobody had ever *wanted* to do, and for about four heartbeats the world was a bothy and a banked fire and nothing else.

Then I felt it.

The stone. Against my sternum, between us, it woke — not singing, not humming, but *pressing*, a slow pulse like a finger testing fruit, and beneath my mouth Felix made a small sound and went rigid.

I pulled back. His eyes opened. They were wrong for a second — filmed, distant, lit faintly violet from within, the way the sky outside was lit — and then they cleared, and what flooded into them was horror.

"Isandro—"

"Don't." I was already moving, rolling away, putting the cold end of the bothy between us, my heart slamming. "What happened. Just now. What did you see?"

"Nothing. Nothing, I—" He sat up, dragged a hand down his face. "For a second I was somewhere else. Standing in an archway. Violet light. And I was—" His voice failed and restarted. "I was turning away from you. Walking away while something behind you came apart. The dream. The exact dream, except this time I could feel my own feet doing it, feel myself *choosing* it—"

"Because you were kissing me," I said. My voice came out flat and cold and I hated it and couldn't stop it. "Because we — and the stone *felt* it, and it showed you. Or the prophecy did. Or they're the same thing, maybe, out here." I wrapped my arms around myself. The warmth of the kiss was still on my mouth and it felt like a brand, like evidence. "This is what I am, Felix. This is what Marisol built. I can't even — I can't touch someone without the covenant reaching through me and reminding everyone what the ending is."

"It wasn't the kiss that—"

"Wasn't it?" I laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Everything I feel, everything I *am*, gets read by that rock and played back as omen. I'm a tuning fork, remember? Second string on the lute? Well, congratulations, you just heard the note we're tuned to, and it's the verse about you casting me down."

He stood. Slowly, hands visible, the way you approach a spooked animal, and I realized with a fresh wave of misery that I was the spooked animal.

"Isandro. Look at me." I looked. "I don't know what the stone is. I don't know what the prophecy is. But I know what I felt just now, and it wasn't fate. Fate doesn't make your hands shake. Fate doesn't make you want—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Whatever that vision was, it was a warning, not a sentence. Warnings can be heeded. Sentences can't. I refuse to believe we've already run out of choices just because we ran out of caution for one minute by a fire."

"One minute," I repeated. "Yes. Let's log it accurately. One minute, and the universe filed a report."

Despite everything — despite the cold knot in my chest — the corner of his mouth moved. "Filed a report," he agreed. "Warden Ashgrave, incident report: subject experienced four seconds of happiness. Recommend immediate corrective vision."

And I laughed. It burst out of me, ragged and unwilling, and it broke something loose, and for a moment we just stood there in the dying firelight laughing at the absurdity of it — two fugitives in a shepherd's hut, punished by an ancient artifact for the crime of a single kiss, negotiating terms with destiny like it was a market trader.

Then the laughter drained away, because the light through the door-slit changed.

The hanging snow outside began to move. Downward. All of it at once, every suspended flake in the bell-jar sky releasing together, sifting down through the violet dark like ash from a burnt page — and beneath it, far off across the Waste, a sound reached us. Not wind. Not wolves. A long, thin tone, like a wet finger on a glass rim, rising.

Against my chest, the covenant-stone woke fully and sang back.

Felix was at the door slit in three strides, knife drawn. "That's a hunting call," he said quietly. "Queen's hunters use it to flush quarry. But it's coming from the north, and Vess's border shrines are south." He glanced back at me, and the ember-light caught the scar through his eyebrow, and his face had gone warden-flat again, all the tenderness packed away somewhere deep. "Someone else knows exactly where we are. And they're not trying to hide it."

The tone rose again, closer. Under my hand, the stone pulsed like a second heart — and this time, threaded through its song, clear as a voice through a keyhole, I heard something that turned my blood to meltwater.

A woman's voice. Dry, amused, familiar as my own heartbeat.

*"Well found, little string. Now run along and bring it home — or shall I come collect you both myself?"*

Marisol.
