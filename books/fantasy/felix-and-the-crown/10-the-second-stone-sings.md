## 10. The Second Stone Sings

### Isandro

The amphitheater had been built for games, back when the mirror-realm still had summers to celebrate. Now it held a truce feast instead: two armies seated in opposing crescents, a hundred fires guttering in the frozen air, and between them, on a dais of black ice, the second covenant-stone mounted on a spear like a trophy neither side would surrender.

"It's *singing*," Isandro whispered.

They lay flat on the ridge above the bowl of the arena, bellies pressed to snow that fell upward in slow, patient spirals. From this distance the stone looked like nothing — a gray lump, unremarkable as river rock. But she could feel it through her teeth, through the soles of her borrowed boots, a hum that climbed into her chest and set her heart beating slightly off its true rhythm.

Felix's breath fogged beside her ear. "It sings when both armies are near. That's why they parley here. Neither commander will move it, and neither will leave without it."

"So we steal it from under two thousand soldiers during a peace feast. Wonderful. Any other impossible errands on your list, or is this the finale?"

"This is the middle," Felix said grimly. "The third stone's worse."

She'd stopped asking how he knew things like that. He always did — the layout of fortresses he'd never entered, the names of wardens dead fifty years, the exact hour the gate-guards changed watch. She'd decided it was a warden thing, some crown-sworn training she didn't have. The answer was sitting in his blood where he refused to look at it, but she'd promised herself not to push. Not yet. Not when they were about to walk into an amphitheater full of killers.

"Again," she said. "Walk me through it."

"Truce feast starts at moonrise. Both armies eat together under oath-bond — anyone who draws steel inside the ring forfeits their claim to the stones. It's old law, older than Vess." His voice was flat, reciting. "Which means the only people allowed to approach the dais are the two commanders, the oath-keeper, and—"

"Court witches." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. "Because someone has to keep the feast-songs honest."

"The realm's witches sing the wards open at moonrise and closed at midnight. Two hours. You'll be one of them."

"And you?"

"I'll be dead," Felix said, with the ghost of a smile. "Warden Ashgrave died at the border shrine. Officially. There's a body report filed and everything — I wrote it myself. Tonight I'm Corvus, a hired sword in Queen Vess's auxiliary, which means I stand on her side of the crescent and try very hard not to make eye contact with anyone who knew my face."

"You're going to walk into your own queen's army wearing a dead man's name."

"I'm going to walk into my *enemy's* army," he corrected quietly. "That's rather the point of all this."

Isandro looked down at the fires again. Somewhere below, two thousand soldiers were sharpening their patience for war, kept apart by nothing but an oath and a singing rock. And somewhere in that crowd, if Marisol's vision had been true — and it had been, every cursed word of it — there were hunters who knew Isandro Zhou's face too.

"Fine," she said. "But if I die in a dress, I'm haunting you specifically."

"That seems fair."

* * *

The court witch whose robes Isandro wore was named Sera Ilven, and she had been paid three silver marks and a bottle of plum brandy to spend the night drunk in a supply tent. Her robes were dove-gray, trimmed in frost-blue thread, and they smelled of another woman's incense. The half-mask of woven silver hid everything above Isandro's mouth, which was apparently how court witches walked among the unmagicked — unseen, untouchable, a little frightening.

The disguise worked better than she'd expected. Soldiers stepped out of her path. An officer bowed. Nobody looked twice at a witch gliding toward the dais steps with her hands folded and her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to escape.

Up close, the stone was worse.

The hum became words. Not quite words — the way wind through a pass almost sounds like voices, the way a dream almost makes sense. But underneath the noise of the feast, under the roasting meat and the oath-drums and the thousand overlapping conversations, Isandro could hear it working at the edges of her mind, patient as water on stone.

*Found you,* it seemed to say. *Found you. Found—*

She cut the thought off and turned to the other court witches waiting at the base of the dais. Three of them, real ones, faces bored behind their masks. "I'm the replacement," she said, pitching her voice low and flat. "Sent from the citadel. Don't ask."

Court witches, it turned out, were exactly as territorial as hedge-witches, which was to say: not at all, because territory meant responsibility. One of them — a heavyset woman with ink-stained fingers — thrust a songbook at Isandro and said, "You take the western arc. Open the wards at first drum, close them at last. Don't improvise, don't harmonize, and for the love of the thawed world, don't touch the stone."

"Don't touch the stone," Isandro repeated solemnly.

The opening rite was simpler than she'd feared. The songbook's notation was archaic but readable — Marisol had made her learn four centuries of musical spellcraft, back when Isandro had complained that it was useless. Another entry on the long ledger of things her mistress had known and never explained.

At the first drumbeat, she sang.

The wards came up around the amphitheater like walls of glass — she felt them rise through her own voice, felt the oath-law settle over ten thousand throats, making violence impossible for two hours. It was beautiful work, honestly. Whoever had written this rite had understood that peace needed architecture.

And the moment the wards sealed, the stone began to sing in earnest.

Not to the armies. To *her.*

The sound threaded through the feast-noise straight into her bones, and this time it wasn't almost-words. It was a melody, old and aching, and her feet wanted to follow it the way rivers want the sea. She gripped the songbook until its spine cracked and forced herself to walk the western arc, singing the maintenance verses, smiling beneath her mask at officers who raised cups to her.

Two hours. Felix would take the dais at the changing of the honor guard, halfway through. She just had to keep singing and not listen to the thing that was calling her name.

Except it wasn't calling her name anymore.

Somewhere in the second hour, mid-verse, the melody shifted — dropped lower, darker, found a new key. And the words stopped being almost-words entirely.

*Ashgrave.*

Isandro's voice caught. She covered it with a cough, kept walking, kept singing, but her pulse had gone cold.

*Ashgrave. Ashgrave. Heir-of-ash. Little lost king. Come home. Come home.*

The stone knew him. The stone was calling for him across a crowded amphitheater, through wards and oaths and two thousand ears, and if any witch on that dais had half her sensitivity—

She finished the verse. Started the next. Kept her eyes forward and her voice level and prayed to whatever governed stolen names and broken covenants that Felix was already moving.

* * *

### Felix

The honor guard changed at the top of the hour, and Felix went over the dais wall in the gap between torchlight and shadow, the way he'd gone over a hundred walls in a decade of warden service. Old habits died harder than reputations.

Below him, the feast roared. Above him, the stone sat on its spear-mount six feet away, humming like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. Two sentries flanked the dais, both facing outward, both watching the crowds — because who would rob a truce feast from *above?*

A fool. A fool would. A fool in auxiliary grays with a dead man's name stitched inside his collar.

He reached the mount. The stone was arm's length away now, and up close the singing was unbearable — not loud, but *close*, the way a whisper against the ear is louder than shouting. He'd heard it once before, years ago, at his father's funeral, when the crown regalia had hummed as he passed. He'd told himself then it was grief playing tricks.

It hadn't been grief.

*Come home,* the stone sang. *Little lost king.*

His hand closed around it.

The world went white and silent. For one suspended heartbeat Felix stood outside the feast, outside the winter, outside his own skin — and saw, with terrible clarity, a hall of ice and banners, a throne he'd never wanted, and himself upon it, crowned, while Isandro knelt before him with her throat bared and her eyes full of forgiveness he hadn't earned.

Then the vision snapped, and he was falling, and the stone was in his arms, and the sentries were turning.

He dropped off the back of the dais into the service trench — a drainage channel from the old games, dry for a century, running under the eastern stands. He hit the ice at the bottom hard enough to see stars, rolled, came up running, the stone clutched to his chest like a child.

Behind him, a whistle blew. Then another.

*No,* he thought, sprinting into the dark of the tunnel. *Not yet. We have forty minutes before the wards fall. They can't draw steel, they can't—*

But the whistles kept coming, and boots thundered onto the trench behind him, and Felix understood with sick clarity that oath-law only forbade blades. It said nothing about hands, or ropes, or twenty men who wanted to be heroes.

He ran.

* * *

### Isandro

She felt the wards shudder before the whistles started — a wrongness rippling through the song she was holding like a held breath. Then the eastern stands erupted, torches scattering like kicked embers, and she knew.

*Go,* she told herself. *Don't be clever. Go.*

She abandoned the western arc mid-verse, shoved the songbook into the ink-stained witch's arms ("keep it going, don't ask"), and ran for the rendezvous — the old beast-pens beneath the stands, where the tunnel met the outer dark.

She got there seconds after Felix did, which was to say: seconds after six soldiers did.

They'd cornered him at the pen gates. Auxiliary grays, no drawn weapons — oath-law held even now — but they'd linked shields and were advancing in a line, and Felix stood with his back to the wall and the stone glowing faintly in his arms like a coal refusing to die.

"—just put it down, aux," the lead soldier was saying. "Whoever paid you, they'll pay more for it back. Nobody has to get hurt."

Felix said nothing. Isandro could see him calculating angles and finding none.

So she did the stupidest thing of her life, which was saying something, because her life had developed a pattern.

"*Wardens!*" Her voice cracked out of her like a whip, court-witch authority poured over smuggler's nerve. She swept out of the shadows with her mask on and her hand raised, and every soldier flinched. "By oath-law, this man is *mine.*"

The lead soldier blinked. "Begging pardon, mistress?"

"The stone was taken under witch-aegis. Witch business. Crown business." She stalked forward, praying her knees didn't show what this was costing. "Unless you'd like to explain to the oath-keeper why you laid hands on a court matter. In front of both armies. During a truce."

Six men looked at each other. Six men weighed a promotion against a tribunal. Isandro watched the arithmetic land behind their eyes, one face at a time.

The line broke. They saluted — actually saluted — and withdrew into the torchlight, already inventing excuses.

Felix stared at her. "That," he breathed, "was the most reckless—"

"Move. Argue later."

They ran. Through the beast-pens, through a rusted gate Felix tore open with his shoulder, out into the upward snow and the violet dark beyond the amphitheater's firelight. Behind them the feast-song faltered, recovered, faltered again — the ink-stained witch holding the wards together through sheer professional spite, buying them minutes.

They ran until the fires were a smudge and the whistles were memory, and then they walked, and then they stopped, both of them bent double, gasping, in the lee of a fallen obelisk crusted with ancient frost.

"We did it," Isandro managed. "We actually—"

The stone sang.

Not the almost-words. Not the distant hum. It sang *out loud*, clear as a chapel bell, a single rising note that lit the snow around them violet-white — and in the heart of the light, Felix went rigid.

"What—" Isandro reached for him. "What's happening? What's it doing?"

He couldn't answer. The light had crawled up his arms like frost climbing glass, and where it touched, his skin showed through — not wounded, not burned, but *revealed*, thin as parchment, and beneath the reveal:

A sigil. Over his heart. A crown of thorns and ash, royal mark of a line everyone swore had ended a century ago, blazing like a brand.

"Ashgrave," the stone sang, sweet and merciless, in a voice like his mother's might have been. "*Heir of ash. Last of the line. Blood of the breaking. Come home, little king. Come home and pay.*"

"Felix." Isandro grabbed his shoulders. "Felix, look at me. What is it saying? What does it—"

The light died. The song cut off. The stone sat dull and gray in his shaking hands, and Felix Ashgrave — Corvus, warden, liar, friend — stood in the upward-falling snow with his secret finally, irreversibly visible between them.

He didn't meet her eyes.

"Now you know," he said, "why I never let you carry it."
