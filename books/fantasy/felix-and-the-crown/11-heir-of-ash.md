## 11. Heir of Ash

The stone sang his name.

Not *Felix*. Not *Warden Ashgrave*, the flat rank he'd offered me on the road like a coin of small denomination. It sang the other thing — the true thing — in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and it kept singing it as we ran through the war-camp's burning periphery with two hundred soldiers' torchlight at our backs and the second covenant-stone wrapped in my scarf against my chest.

*Ashgrave of the line of Vaelor. Heir of ash. Blood of the broken crown.*

I heard it even after I'd muffled it in wool and witch-warding. I heard it in my teeth.

We didn't stop running until the parley camp was a smear of orange behind us and the pines closed overhead, black and dripping with old snow. Felix picked the trail — I'll give him that, he moved through this realm's winter like a man reading a book he'd written himself — until we dropped into the hollow beneath a lightning-split fir, and he turned to face me, chest heaving, and said, "We should be safe here until—"

"Say it."

He went still. Snow sifted down through the split crown of the tree, catching in his hair, and for one absurd moment all I could think was that he looked exactly like the boy in Marisol's woodcut illustrations — the one she'd never let me look at too long, the one always drawn standing just outside the frame of every prophecy diagram, holding a sword he never used.

"Isandro."

"The stone sang your name." My voice came out low and steady, which surprised me, because inside I was anything but. "The real one. The one you've been not-telling me since the drowned chapel. Since before the drowned chapel, probably. So say it, or I swear by every hedge-god Marisol told me were lies, I will take this stone out of my shirt and let it sing to the whole White Waste."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, something had gone out of them — the careful warden's steadiness, the performance of calm. What was left underneath looked younger. Looked, in fact, like someone who had been carrying a weight so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

"Ashgrave," he said. "That's real. That's the name I was born under. But it's not mine alone. It's the last name of the House of Vaelor."

The words landed in me one at a time, like stones dropped into a well, each one taking longer than the last to hit bottom.

Vaelor. I knew the word from the prophecy book itself, from the pages that glowed and named me. *When the covenant breaks, it breaks by royal blood; when it reforges, it reforges by royal blood.* And beneath that, in the cramped marginal hand of some long-dead scribe: *the line of Vaelor, kings of both Thornfells, whose oath held the winter back.*

"You're—" I started, and stopped, because there were about six sentences trying to come out of my mouth at once and none of them were finished. "You're the *king's* line. You're the blood the ritual needs."

"Not king," Felix said quietly. "Never crowned. The last Vaelor king died a hundred years ago — murdered, they say, by his own brother, though the histories get conveniently vague about who paid the brother. The house scattered. Some fled into exile across the waygate, into your realm. Some stayed and bent the knee to whatever power rose next." His mouth twisted. "And one branch stayed hidden in plain sight, took a gray name, and joined the very order sworn to guard a covenant their grandfather broke."

"That's why the wardens exiled you."

"That's why the wardens *found* me," he corrected. "Exiling implies I was ever allowed to belong. Queen Vess keeps the last Vaelor alive the way a jailer keeps a key. Useful. Dangerous. Never out of sight. I was eleven when she had my parents' farm burned and gave me a choice: the white cloak or the grave. I took the cloak. I told myself I'd wear it until I found a way to make it mean something." He laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "Ten years later a runaway hedge-witch falls through a dying gate with her own name in a century-old prophecy, and every plan I ever made catches fire at once."

The hollow was very quiet. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the split fir, and snow came down in slow spirals — downward, here, always downward in this realm, though everything else about this world felt inverted.

I sat down on a root because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

All the pieces rearranged themselves in my head with an awful, clicking finality. The way he'd flinched when the first stone resonated off my magic. The questions he wouldn't ask about the ritual. The night in the shepherd's bothy when I'd told him about Marisol planning to use me, and he'd held me like a man apologizing in advance, and I'd been too warm and too tired to wonder what he was sorry *for*.

"You knew," I said. "Before we ever left the Undermarket. Before the chapel. You knew what the reforging requires, and you knew what you are, and you let me walk toward it thinking we were gathering stones for some vague, survivable purpose."

"I was going to tell you."

"When? At the altar?" My voice cracked upward and I hated it. "When you stepped forward and bled out on the covenant-stones and I got to spend the rest of my life knowing I built the knife?"

"Isandro—"

"Don't." I was on my feet now, though I didn't remember standing. The stone against my sternum pulsed with my heartbeat, or maybe my heartbeat was following the stone; lately I couldn't tell the difference. "Don't say my name like that. Don't do the voice. You don't get the voice."

He took a step toward me and stopped himself, hands half-raised, the universal gesture of a man approaching something that might bolt. "What would you have done?" he asked, and there was no warden in his tone now, no heir, just a tired man in a frozen forest. "If I'd told you in the Undermarket — a stranger who'd known me three days — that I'm the last drop of royal blood in either realm, and that the only ritual that saves both worlds requires spilling it? Would you have walked away? Would you have let me go to Vess and trade myself for nothing?"

"Yes!" It tore out of me before I could dress it up. "Yes, I would have argued, I would have raged, I would have hated it — but I would have *chosen*, Felix. Do you understand? That's the whole point of me. That's the entire reason I ran from Marisol. Everyone in my life keeps deciding things *for* me. She decided I was a vessel. The prophecy decided I was a fulcrum. And you—" My breath hitched. "You decided you were a sacrifice, and you decided I didn't need to know, and you did it while kissing me under a roof full of sheep, which is possibly the cruelest thing anyone has ever done to me, because now I can't even hate you cleanly."

He flinched like I'd struck him. Good, some vicious corner of me thought. Then the rest of me saw his face in the dimness — saw the ten years of white cloak and gray name sitting on him like frost — and the vicious corner shut up.

"I wasn't using you," he said. Each word came out carefully, like a man walking on lake ice. "I need you to know that. Whatever else is true — whatever I hid — I wasn't using you. The nightmares started when I was six. A girl with storm-colored eyes standing in a gate of light, and a voice saying, *you will betray her, you will betray her, you will betray her.* I grew up knowing your face before I ever saw it. When they dragged you into that interrogation room, I recognized you the way you recognize a scar." He dragged a hand through his hair, dislodging snow. "I've spent my whole life waiting to become the villain of someone else's story, Isandro. Can you blame me for wanting a few weeks of being a man instead? Of being — near you — without the prophecy in the room?"

"Yes," I said. "Blame is exactly what I have available. Take it up with my inventory."

Something almost like a laugh escaped him, wrecked at the edges. "There she is."

"Don't 'there she is' me." But the fury was curdling now, going thick and strange in my chest, because that was the trap of Felix Ashgrave: he made you want to forgive him even while he was handing you reasons not to. I turned away, arms wrapped around myself, and stared at the dark wall of the pines. "Here's what I can't get past. Here's the thing chewing holes in me. The prophecy says the covenant reforges by willing sacrifice of royal blood. Fine. Terrible, but fine — prophecies are terrible, that's their whole business. But then it also says *the warden will betray the witch*. And everyone — Marisol, the stone, probably Queen Vess's court astrologers — everyone reads those two lines together and thinks: the betrayal is that he leads her to the altar believing she'll live, and dies instead. The betrayal is the lie of safety."

"Yes," Felix said softly. "That's how I read it too. Every night. For twenty years."

"But what if it isn't?" I turned back to face him. "What if the betrayal hasn't happened yet? What if the betrayal is still coming — something worse, something neither of us has imagined — and everything we've done since the gate has just been us walking the groove the prophecy already cut?" I pulled the stone out from my shirt. In the darkness it threw faint light, the color of deep ice, and its song had changed — lower now, satisfied, like a hound lying down beside a kill. "It sang your name, Felix. Not when we touched it. Not when I channeled through it. When we *ran*. Like it was checking you off a list."

He looked at the stone the way men look at open graves. "Then we stop following the list."

"How? How does anyone stop following a list that's been writing itself for a century? Marisol trained me for this before I could write my own name. Vess has been keeping you alive like a spare key. The stones sing to us. The armies march where the prophecy points. We're not people in this story, Felix, we're *load-bearing walls*." My voice shook and I let it. "And the worst part — the genuinely worst part, worse than the lies, worse than the bothy — is that I love you anyway. I love you and I don't trust you and those are both completely true at the same time and I don't know how to hold them in one pair of hands."

The silence after that was enormous.

Felix stood very still in the falling snow, and I watched him decide something — watched it happen behind his eyes like weather changing. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"Then don't hold them tonight. Put one down. Just for tonight." He reached out slowly — giving me every chance to step back — and touched the edge of my sleeve, no more. "I'm done deciding things for you. Both of us. From this hour, you know everything I know. The third stone — Vess has it, or knows where it sleeps; that's why she let the parley happen, why her hunters herd instead of kill. Marisol is somewhere in this realm with her own plans and her own version of the truth. And the altar where it all ends stands in the Vale of Oaths, four days north, straddling the seam between worlds." His thumb pressed once, lightly, against my wrist, over the pulse. "Four days. Whatever you choose when we get there — run, fight, feed me to the stones — you choose it knowing everything. That's the only amends I have to offer. It's not enough. It's what's true."

I looked at his hand on my wrist. At the man attached to it: disgraced, prophesied, royal in the worst possible way, and still — infuriatingly, unforgivably — the first person in either realm who had ever stood between me and danger without wanting anything out of me except my company.

Heartbreak and fury braided together in my chest, and I couldn't untangle them, and I was suddenly so tired of trying.

"We sleep in shifts," I said finally, my voice gone hoarse. "You first watch. And Felix — the next thing you hide from me, however small, however kind — I will walk into the White Waste alone and let the Winter have its bargain. I mean it. I'd rather be Winter's than managed."

"I know," he said. And the awful thing, the thing that made my throat close, was that he clearly did.

He settled against the split trunk with his sword across his knees, and I curled up opposite him with the stone humming its low, contented, list-keeping hum against my heart, and I did not sleep. I lay in the dark watching snow fall downward in a broken world, doing arithmetic I couldn't stop doing:

One heir. One altar. One ritual that wanted royal blood, willingly given.

And a prophecy with one line still unfulfilled — *the warden will betray the witch* — waiting, patient as winter, to find out what shape its ending would take.

Somewhere far to the north, past the frozen camps and the hunting wards, a bell began to ring in a citadel I couldn't see. Long, slow strokes, carried on wind that smelled suddenly of iron and thaw.

Felix's head came up. "That's Vess's war-bell," he breathed. "She's mustering. Everything she has."

Toward the Vale of Oaths.

Toward us.
