## 12. Split Roads

I left before dawn, which is the coward's hour — the hour when the light hasn't committed to anything and neither have you.

I'd told Isandro I was going out to check the snares. She'd looked at me with those hedge-witch eyes that saw through every lie I'd ever told, and she'd said, "Take your sword, then," and turned back to the fire. That was the worst part. Not an argument. Not a plea. Just *take your sword*, as if she already knew what I was carrying out into the snow wasn't a blade at all.

The bothy door groaned behind me. The White Waste opened ahead, a country of nothing, white on white under a sky the color of old bruises. Somewhere past that horizon sat Queen Vess's citadel, and somewhere inside it was the answer to the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing in my head.

One heir of Ash. One covenant demanding royal blood, freely given. Two worlds slowly freezing because my ancestors broke their word a hundred years ago.

It wasn't complicated math. It had never been complicated math. The only variable that made it hard was asleep by a dying fire back in the bothy, wearing my spare cloak, with frost-melt drying in her hair.

*Felix Ashgrave, last of the line that shattered the world.* Vess would want me alive. A claimant is worth more breathing than dead — she could parade me, execute me publicly, close the prophecy like a book slamming shut. And if the stones needed royal blood anyway, then better mine spilled on her terms than everyone else's spent on no terms at all.

I'd been walking for an hour when I heard her.

Not footsteps. Isandro moved quiet as a smuggler when she wanted to; I'd learned that in the Undermarket, watching her talk her way past men who'd have sold their mothers for less than she was worth. What I heard was the wind changing. It bent around something small and stubborn moving through it, the way water bends around a stone.

She came up over a drift twenty paces behind me, hood down, cheeks scoured red, and she didn't even look angry. That was somehow worse.

"You checked the snares," she said, "in the wrong direction."

"The snares are scattered."

"The snares are south. You're walking north." She stopped three paces away, close enough that I could see the ice in her lashes. "Toward the citadel. Toward Vess. Toward the exact place you told me last night you were never going."

"Last night I hadn't finished thinking."

"You'd finished thinking. You just hadn't finished lying." She pulled something from her coat — the second covenant-stone, wrapped in wool, humming faintly against her palm like a struck bell that wouldn't fade. "You were going to leave this with me. Leave me the stone and take yourself to her, and let the prophecy do the rest. Am I close?"

I said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Felix." My name in her mouth did things to my chest I refused to examine. "Walk me through it. Walk me through how handing yourself to the woman who murdered her way onto a frozen throne saves anyone."

"It's not her I'm bargaining with. It's the covenant." I kept my voice level, warden-flat, the voice I'd used on a hundred interrogations. "The rite needs willing royal blood. Mine is the only royal blood left that isn't hers. If I give it freely, at the altar, the reforging happens. You walk away. Both realms thaw. Everyone lives except—"

"Except you."

"—which is the entire point."

"No." She said it simply, without heat, the way you'd correct a child's sums. "That's not the point. That's the cost. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it, because you're a warden and wardens know the difference between justice and slaughter better than anyone."

The wind picked up between us, carrying grit and cold that bit through wool and leather alike. Above us the sky had gone the deep violet of the mirror-realm's endless dusk, and the snow was still falling upward off the drifts, thin white threads climbing toward a heaven that didn't want them.

"I watched my whole family die for this crown," I said. "My father. My brothers. Executed in the citadel square while Vess wore our colors. I am the last Ashgrave, Isandro. Everything my blood broke, my blood can mend. There's a rightness to it I can't argue with, and believe me, I've tried."

"And there it is." She stepped closer. "You're not offering your life because it's the only way. You're offering it because it feels like penance, and penance isn't love, Felix, it's just pride wearing a hair shirt."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It's not. Nothing about any of this is fair. But you don't get to make yourself a martyr to make yourself feel clean." She held up the stone. It pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat. "This thing sang your name in front of two armies. It knows what you are. Maybe it also knows what you're for, and I promise you it isn't Vess's chopping block."

I turned north again. It was petty and I knew it, but if I kept looking at her I was going to say something true, and true things, in my experience, got people killed.

"Go back to the bothy," I said. "There's food for four days. The truce roads hold until the new moon. Marisol has the third stone and she'll come looking for you, and when she does, you'll need—"

The blizzard hit us.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is all I have left of some things, and because later — much later — Isandro would ask me to describe it, and I'd need the words ready.

One moment the Waste was merely hostile: wind, cold, the ordinary cruelty of high winter. The next moment it was *attentive*. The snow stopped falling upward and began to fall sideways, then to fall *toward us*, streaming in from every direction at once like the whole white country had inhaled. The temperature dropped so fast my breath froze in my beard, a crackling mask. And in the heart of the storm, in the way the whiteness swirled and gathered and *leaned*, I felt something look at us.

Isandro grabbed my arm. "It's here. The winter itself — Felix, it found us."

"I see it."

"Do you? Because I don't think you've been seeing anything since you decided to die nobly!" But her grip on my arm was iron, and she hauled me sideways as a wall of white came down where we'd stood — not a drift, not a gust, a *wall*, standing vertical for one impossible second before collapsing into a claw of wind that scored my cheek like a knife.

We ran. There was nothing tactical about it; running was the only verb left. The Waste had gone featureless, horizon erased, sky and ground the same screaming white, and the storm herded us — I understood that within minutes, the way you understand a trap closing — herded us away from the bothy, away from the border shrine, away from every shelter marked on the maps I'd memorized as a boy, and out onto open ice where nothing could hide us.

A voice came through the wind. Not words exactly. More like the memory of words, spoken by someone very large and very patient, the way a glacier might speak if glaciers could hold grudges.

***Little king.***

Isandro heard it too. I saw her flinch. "It talks?"

"It's the dying covenant given teeth," I shouted over the gale. "The pact was supposed to keep the seasons balanced. When it broke, the winter didn't end — it just kept going, and going, and somewhere in all that going it woke up." Another white wall rose ahead of us. We split around it and it split with us, folding like a living thing. "Vess feeds it. I've always suspected. An eternal winter makes an eternal queen."

***Little king,*** the storm said again, and now the voice had shape, had *appetite*. ***Bring me the witch's bargain and I will open a road for you. Warm. Clear. Straight to the citadel gates.***

My blood went cold in a way the cold couldn't manage. "What bargain?"

Beside me, Isandro had gone rigid. "It's talking to me," she breathed. "Felix — it's not talking to you. It's talking to *me*."

***Witch,*** the winter crooned, and the snow around us formed shapes — brief, melting figures, a woman bent over a cauldron, a girl running through passes, images from a life I only half knew. ***Give me the little king's blood. Spill it here, on my ice, and the gate home opens. Your own world. Your own sun. No more prophecy, no more stones, no more dying realms. You may go home unharmed.***

"No," Isandro said.

***You hate it here. The cold hurts you. The magic frightens you. Your mistress lied to you, your ally lies to you, the prophecy uses you—***

"I said *no*." Her voice cracked across the wind like a whip, and for one staggering moment the storm actually recoiled, the walls of white shivering backward. Hedge-witch magic, Marisol's training, whatever ran in Isandro's blood — it answered her anger, and the winter, for all its size, took a step back from it.

Then it remembered what it was, and hit us like an avalanche with intent.

* * *

We lost the sledge first.

It went sideways off the ice shelf with everything on it — the rope, the tent, the dried fish, the lamp oil, the second blanket, the small iron pot Isandro had carried from her own world and guarded like a relic. One minute we were roped together crossing a pressure ridge, the next the ice groaned, tilted, and the sledge slid down a blue-black crevasse with a sound like a swallowed scream.

Isandro lunged after it. I caught her harness and hauled her back, and we ended up on our knees on the tilting ice, her fists knotted in my coat, both of us breathing hard enough to fog the air solid.

"The supplies," she gasped.

"Gone."

"The pot—"

"*Gone.*" I pulled her upright. "We can't carry grief and supplies both. Pick one."

She picked, visibly, deliberately, and let it go. I loved her a little for that, in the ugly practical way you love someone in a war.

The winter didn't let up. It stalked us across the ice for hours, testing, probing, sending knives of wind into every gap in our clothing, sculpting the snow into shapes meant to unsettle — faces, mostly, long-jawed and patient, the faces of everyone either of us had ever lost. I walked past my father's face twice. The third time I stopped and stared at it until the wind tore it apart, and I said, out loud, "You're not him. He died free. You're just weather wearing his ghost," and the face collapsed, and Isandro looked at me like I'd done something holy instead of something desperate.

By the time true dark fell — the violet dusk deepening to black — we were stumbling rather than walking, and I knew the arithmetic had changed. Without the sledge, without shelter, exposed to a storm that hated us personally, we had perhaps six hours. Maybe eight if we found a windbreak. The citadel was three days north. The bothy was a day and a half south, and the storm stood between us and it like a locked door.

Which meant, I realized with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that my plan had failed in the most complete possible way. I hadn't even managed to reach Vess. I was going to die stupidly, in the middle of nowhere, with the woman I was trying to save standing over me shaking her head.

"We need to dig in," I said. "Snow cave. It's the only chance."

"In this?" She gestured at the maelstrom. "It'll bury us before we finish."

"Then we dig fast." I drove my knife into a drift and started cutting blocks. "Talk to it while I work."

"What?"

"You argued with a glacier today and it flinched. Talk, Isandro. Loudly. About anything. About how much you intend to live."

She stared at me — snow-crusted, exhausted, furious, magnificent — and then she planted herself in the wind like a hedgerow and started talking. Not arguing. *Defying.* She told the winter she had run from one cage and she'd be damned if she froze in another. She told it about Marisol, about the prophecy book, about the date that wasn't hers, and how she'd decided — *decided*, do you hear me — that her life belonged to her and no one else, not a mentor, not a book, not a storm. She told it she was going to reforge the covenant or die trying, but she was going to do it *her way*, with *her people*, and its opinion was not requested.

And the wind, impossibly, eased. Just at the edges. Just enough.

I cut blocks. She talked. We built a burrow in the lee of the pressure ridge, small and mean and ours, and crawled into it with our backs pressed together for warmth, sharing one cloak and half a bar of travel bread I'd had in my inner pocket, forgotten until now, the single most valuable object in two realms.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Outside, the winter circled our little den, scratching at the walls like a dog denied entry, patient as only a century of patience can make a thing.

"You were going to leave me the stone," Isandro said finally. Her voice was quiet in the dark. "You were going to leave me the stone and a four-day head start and a note, weren't you. I know you, Felix. There was a note."

"There was a note."

"What did it say?"

I closed my eyes. Tiredness made honest, sometimes. "It said: *the prophecy says I betray you. I couldn't live with doing it this way instead. Forgive me. Live well. Spring will come for you even if it doesn't come for me.*"

Silence. Then, muffled, furious, wrecked: "*Spring will come for you.* You absolute — you were going to leave me a *poem* and die."

"It wasn't a poem."

"It rhymed in your head, Felix. I know it rhymed." She twisted around in the dark until she could press her forehead against the back of my shoulder. "Listen to me. Listen, because I'm only saying this once and the storm's probably listening too, and I don't care. I am not letting you buy the world with yourself. I am not letting you turn love into a transaction where you're the coin. If the altar wants blood, we'll bring it blood, but we will decide *together* what it costs, and if you try to sneak off again I will follow you across every frozen hell in both realms, and I will find you, and I will be *so angry*, and then I will save your life anyway, because that's apparently what I do."

The winter scratched at our door. The stone, wrapped in wool against her chest, hummed a low warm note, like a hearth-fire remembering itself.

"Isandro," I said. "If we get out of this—"

"When."

"*When* we get out of this. Vess has hunters. Marisol has the third stone. The winter has our scent. Any one of them—" I stopped. Because through the wall of the snow cave, threading down through the howl of the storm, I heard something that turned my blood to ice water.

Bells. Sledge bells, many teams of them, coming fast from the north.

Hunters. Vess's hunters — or worse, given what the storm had been whispering, something riding *with* the storm's permission, on a road the winter had opened.

Isandro had gone silent against my shoulder. Then, very softly, in the dark: "Felix. Tell me that's rescue."

I reached for my sword. "It's not rescue."
