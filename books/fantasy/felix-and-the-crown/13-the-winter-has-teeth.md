## 13. The Winter Has Teeth

**ISANDRO**

The blizzard found us an hour after Felix turned back toward the White Waste, and it did not behave like weather.

I'd been in storms before. Marisol had dragged me up the Thornfell passes twice a year to cut ice-herbs, and I knew how wind screamed and how snow blinded. This was different. This had *intent*. The wind came at us in long deliberate shoves, like something testing our weight, learning where we'd fall. The snow didn't drift — it *circled*, spiraling around us in slow white coils, the way a snake circles before it strikes.

"Keep moving," Felix shouted over the roar. He was ahead of me on the sledge runners, one hand on the rail, the other holding the rope that tethered us together. We'd argued about the rope. He'd won. "Don't stop. Stopping is dying."

"I'm not an idiot!"

"You're a hedge-witch with no cold-craft in the middle of a blizzard that's hunting us! Idiot is a generous word!"

I hated him. I loved him. Both of those things were getting easier to hold at once, which frightened me more than the storm did.

We'd been walking since dawn — or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of a bruise going bad. Felix had tried to leave alone in the night, to walk into Queen Vess's citadel and trade his royal blood for my freedom, and I had followed him across the frozen waste because I am stubborn and stupid and because the thought of him kneeling on some altar while Vess smiled had made me physically ill. So now we were both out here, two fools on a stolen sledge with three days of food and a blizzard that moved like a predator.

The first thing we lost was the dog.

Not a real dog — a fetch-spirit Felix had bound from snow and bone to pull the sledge. It had been loping ahead of us all morning, gray and silent, and then the storm simply *took* it. One moment it was there; the next, the snow closed around where it had been and when the coils unwound there was nothing left. Not even tracks.

Felix stopped so fast I walked into his back.

"No," he said quietly. It wasn't a question or a prayer. It was grief, compressed very small, and shoved down deep where he kept everything.

"It can't be dead," I said. "It was made of snow."

"That's what worries me." He turned, and his face was bare for half a second before the warden-mask came down — but in that half second I saw he understood exactly what I understood. Snow things don't die in snow. They get *called home*.

The storm was not weather. The storm was the Winter itself, the personified death of both realms, the slow suffocation of a broken covenant given teeth and hunger and a hundred years of patience. And it had noticed us.

"Isandro." Felix's voice went flat and careful, the way it did when he was about to say something tactical instead of true. "If it offers you anything—"

"If *what* offers me anything?"

The wind stopped.

Not died down. *Stopped*, all at once, the way a door shuts. The silence rang. And in the middle of that silence, the snow around us began to rise — not upward, not downward, just *up*, off the ground, hanging in the air in a vast slow spiral a hundred yards across, and inside the spiral the air thickened and took shape.

It looked like a woman made of frost and absence. Tall as a chapel spire. Her face was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful, which is to say it would kill you as soon as look at you, and her eyes were two points of absolute black, holes punched through the world into somewhere colder.

"Well," I said, because bravado is the only weapon I've ever been good with. "You must be the landlady."

"*Little witch.*" Her voice didn't come through the air. It came up through the ice under my boots, through the bones of my feet, resonating in my teeth. "*You carry what was broken.*"

"The stones? Yeah, they're heavy. You want to help carry?"

Felix's hand found my wrist. Squeezed once. A warning: *don't.*

The Winter ignored me, which stung more than I expected. Her black eyes fixed on Felix, and something moved across her face that might have been hunger, might have been recognition.

"*The blood of Ashgrave,*" she said. "*A century I have waited. A century the covenant has bled, and bled, and bled, and no one has paid.*"

"You'll wait longer," Felix said. His voice didn't shake. I don't know how it didn't shake.

Then she turned those hole-punched eyes on me, and the world got very small and very cold.

"*Little witch. I know what you are. I know what you fled, and what you carry, and what you have already given up to be here.*" The spiral of snow drifted closer, close enough that I could see each flake was shaped like a tiny six-pointed star, perfect and identical, millions of them, all turning. "*I offer you a bargain, and I offer it once.*"

My mouth was dry. My hands, inside their mittens, were shaking. But I'd spent four years learning to bargain in Marisol's kitchen, where every deal had a hook in it, and I knew the shape of this one before she spoke it.

"*Deliver me the blood of Ashgrave. Open his throat at the altar of your choosing, willingly given by your hand, and I will let you go. Through the gate. Home. Unharmed. Unhunted. Your mistress will never find you. Your name will be scrubbed from every prophecy ever written.*"

Home. The word hit me like a fist. I hadn't let myself think about it — the smuggler's cache, the passes, the sun, the smell of pine and woodsmoke, a life where nobody had ever heard of covenant-stones or exiled heirs or the exact date of my death. Marisol would hunt me forever otherwise. I'd known that. Running only worked if you could run far enough.

And here it was. Offered. All I had to do was kill the man whose hand was currently wrapped around my wrist.

I looked at Felix.

He was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — and then I realized I could read it perfectly, and that was the problem. He wasn't afraid I'd take the deal. He was *relieved*. Standing there in the teeth of the Winter, he looked at me like a man watching a rescue arrive for someone else. Like the bargain was a door opening and the only question was whether I'd be smart enough to walk through it.

"Oh," I said softly. "Oh, you absolute idiot."

"Isandro—"

"You were going to let her have you. That's why you turned back. Not to bargain — to *surrender*. You thought if you gave yourself up I'd stop following you."

"The math is simple—"

"The math is *wrong*." I pulled my wrist free of his grip and turned to face the Winter, and I felt something in my chest go very still and very clear, the way it did right before I did something irreversibly stupid. "Here's my counteroffer. No."

"*Little witch—*"

"No. You don't get him. You don't get either of us. You're not a god, you're a *symptom* — you're what happens when a promise breaks and nobody fixes it. And I'm going to fix it. Not with his blood. Not with anyone's blood spilled the way you want it. So you can take your bargain and freeze."

For a moment, nothing. The spiral hung there. The black eyes regarded me with something that in a human face I would have called surprise.

Then the Winter smiled, and every flake in the air sharpened into a needle.

"*As you wish.*"

**FELIX**

The storm hit us like an avalanche with a grudge.

I got Isandro down behind the sledge and threw my cloak over us both, and the world became white noise and white pain. The needles of snow weren't falling anymore; they were flying horizontally, driven by wind that screamed in a register I felt in my fillings, and they hit like gravel thrown by a slingshot. My cloak — warden-issue, spelled against cold, spelled against blades — began to fray. I could hear it fraying. Little tearing sounds, thread by thread, patient as moths.

"She's stripping our protections!" Isandro shouted into my ear. "She's picking us apart!"

"How long can you hold a shield?"

"With what magic? I'm running on fumes and spite!"

"Then spite harder!"

Something enormous struck the sledge. The whole frame jumped; the fetch-dog's harness traces snapped and whipped away into the white. I grabbed the cargo netting and held on, and Isandro grabbed me, and we slid ten feet across the ice as a wall of snow the size of a house shouldered past us — deliberately, slowly, the way a cat shoulders a cup off a table. Watching us. Deciding what to break next.

The supplies went first. Our food chest tore loose from the netting and simply *vanished*, swallowed whole, gone without a sound. Then the tent roll. Then the water skins, bursting one by one like popped blisters, their contents freezing mid-air into drifting crystals. The Winter wasn't trying to kill us quickly. She was taking everything we needed to survive, piece by piece, making the waste itself into a slower version of the same bargain: *give me the Ashgrave blood, or freeze on your knees.*

"Isandro!" I hauled her toward me by the front of her coat. Her lips were going blue. The rope between us had iced over into a rigid bar. "The stones! Are the stones—"

Her hand went to the bundle at her chest — the two covenant-stones, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart. The Winter's spiral paused. Recoiled, almost, from the bundle, the way a wolf recoils from fire. Then it redoubled its assault everywhere else, furious, and I understood two things at once.

One: the stones were the only thing keeping us alive. Whatever lived in them, whatever fragment of the old covenant still burned there, the Winter could not touch it directly.

Two: that meant the Winter couldn't afford to kill us outright, either. Not while we carried them. Which meant every ounce of this fury was theater — pressure, negotiation, punishment. She wanted us alive and desperate.

Fine. Two could play at desperate.

"Give me your hands!" I shouted.

"What?"

"Your *hands*!" I ripped off my mittens with my teeth. The cold hit my bare skin like boiling water, a flash-burn of pain, and I pressed my palms flat against the ice beneath us. Warden-craft. The oldest trick in the crown's book, taught to every recruit in their first winter: *the ice remembers being water.* You don't melt it — melting takes power you don't have. You *ask* it. You remind it of what it used to be, and for a moment, if it likes you, it softens.

"Isandro, put your hands next to mine. Hedge-magic runs on bargains, doesn't it? On *exchange*?"

"Yes—"

"Then offer the ice a trade. Anything. Warmth, memory, a secret, I don't care. Ask it to open."

She tore her mittens off and slammed her palms down beside mine, and I felt her magic reach out beside my craft — wilder than mine, messier, smelling of woodsmoke and cut herbs and Marisol's kitchen — and together we pushed our will into the ice and *asked*.

For three heartbeats, nothing.

Then the ice groaned, sighed, and opened beneath us like a mouth.

We dropped through into darkness and freezing water, and the last thing I saw above us was the Winter's face in the storm, watching us go down with those black hole-punched eyes — and I swear on every oath I ever swore to a crown that no longer wanted me, she looked *amused*.

**ISANDRO**

The water was a knife. The dark was a second knife.

I came up gasping in an air pocket under the ice shelf, lungs full of fire, and Felix surfaced beside me coughing. Above us the ice glowed faintly where the storm-light filtered through, a ceiling of dirty glass. Somewhere beyond it, the blizzard still raged. Down here, it was quiet. Down here, we were alive, and the Winter's needles couldn't reach us.

"We're going to die down here," I said. My voice echoed strangely off the ice.

"Probably." Felix trod water, breathing hard, hair plastered to his skull. In the dim glow his eyes found mine. "But not quickly. Air pocket's big enough to breathe. Water's cold enough to kill us in an hour, though."

"So we have an hour."

"To find a way out, yes."

"And if we don't?"

"Then I'm glad," he said, "that the last hour was spent telling you no."

I laughed. It came out cracked and half-hysterical and completely involuntary, and then I was treading water in a flooded hollow under a frozen lake laughing like a madwoman while the man I loved — and I wasn't pretending anymore, even silently, even to myself — treaded water beside me, and above us the thing that was killing two worlds scratched patiently at the ice like a cat at a door.

"There," Felix said suddenly. "Look."

Light. Faint, gold, wrong-for-this-world light, seeping through a crack in the ice maybe forty yards away. Not storm-light. Storm-light was violet and bruised. This was warm. This was *covenant*-light.

And I felt it before I understood it — a pull behind my sternum, gentle and insistent, the same pull I'd felt in the drowned chapel, the same pull that had named me in a book a century before I was born. The stones, wrapped against my chest, had begun to hum.

"They're calling something," I whispered.

"Not something." Felix was staring at the golden crack, and his face had gone strange. "Someone. There's a shrine down here. An old one. Pre-regicide." He paused, and I watched him decide to tell me the truth, watch the cost of it move across his face. "My family built shrines like this. Before. When we still—" He stopped. Started again. "When we were still allowed to be trusted with holy things."

The humming grew. The stones against my chest grew warm through the oilcloth, warm through my coat, warm as a sleeping animal, and the golden light brightened until it painted the whole underside of the ice, and I understood with sudden total certainty that the Winter had made a mistake.

She'd thrown us *down*. And down was where the old things slept. Down was where the covenant had been poured into the world like foundation-stones, and down was the last place her cold had never fully reached.

"Felix." I was swimming toward the light before I finished saying his name. "Felix, I think the lake wants to talk to us."

Behind us, far above, muffled through fathoms of ice, the blizzard screamed — and for the first time since it had found us, it sounded less like a hunter and more like something locked outside a door.

The golden crack widened as we approached. Warmth spilled through it, and with the warmth came a voice — old, layered, neither male nor female, speaking in the creaking grammar of a language older than either realm:

*A third stone sleeps below. The heir and the vessel wake it, or the waste keeps them both.*

Felix and I looked at each other across the glowing water.

"A third stone," he said slowly. "Marisol has the third stone."

"Marisol *had* the third stone." I kicked toward the light, toward the warmth, toward whatever waited below. "I think it's about to change hands."
