## 14. Marisol at the Gate

The waygate opened like a wound that had decided to heal.

Isandro felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air above the White Waste, a hum in her teeth, the old familiar itch of hedge-magic being *used* nearby, and used well. She stopped mid-stride, sledge runners hissing over packed snow, and Felix, ten paces ahead with the second stone wrapped against his chest, turned when the silence behind him changed shape.

"What is it?"

"Someone's opening a gate." She squinted north, where the blizzard had thinned to a grudging drizzle of sideways snow. "A *stable* one."

Felix went still in the way he did when bad news arrived wearing worse news's coat. "That's not possible. The gates have been dying for a century. The covenant—"

"The covenant is broken, not dead." Isandro's mouth had gone dry. She knew only one person alive who could hold a dying gate open long enough to walk through it carrying luggage. Only one person who'd taught her the trick of it, kneeling beside her in the herb shed, her hands steady on Isandro's shoulders: *You don't force a gate, child. You ask it nicely, and you mean it.*

"Marisol," she said.

Felix's hand went to his sword. Isandro caught his wrist.

"No. Not yet. Let me talk first."

"To your mistress? The one who trained you as a vessel?" His voice was gentle, which was how she knew he was frightened.

"She's still my mistress." The words came out older than Isandro felt, worn smooth by years of use. "She fed me when I was starving. She taught me everything I know. Whatever she planned — whatever I was *for* — that doesn't unmake eleven years of her roof over my head."

"It doesn't unmake it," Felix agreed quietly. "It just sits next to it. Both things true at once. That's what betrayal is."

Isandro wanted to hit him. That was usually a sign he'd said something correct.

The gate finished opening half a mile north, where the waste rose into a ridge of blue ice. It didn't blaze or thunder. It simply became — an oval of soft gold light standing upright in the snowfall like a door someone had left ajar in the middle of nowhere, its edges knitting themselves shut even as they watched, Marisol's magic spending itself to keep it whole.

And through it, small and straight-backed and utterly unhurried, came Hedge-witch Marisol Vane, leading a mule.

The mule carried saddlebags. One of them glowed.

Even from this distance, even through the wrap of oilcloth and warding charms, Isandro could feel the third covenant-stone the way you feel a struck bell through the floorboards — a deep, patient pulse that made the snow around the mule's hooves melt and refreeze in flower patterns.

"Oh," Isandro said. Her voice cracked. "Oh, she actually has it."

"You knew she was looking?"

"I knew she'd want it. There's a difference." Isandro watched the small figure pick her way down the ridge, and eleven years of muscle memory tried to make her run forward, make her kneel in the snow, make her say *Mistress, I'm sorry, I ran, I was frightened.* Eleven years of habit versus four months of running, and the habits were winning, and that terrified her more than the winter did.

"We should go around," Felix said. "Circle wide, lose her in the whiteout—"

"Mistress Marisol." Isandro stepped forward, out from behind him, into the open. "Don't bother circling. She'll find us anyway. She found me across two realms once already."

* * *

Marisol looked exactly as Isandro remembered her and completely different, both at once. Same iron-grey braid coiled at the nape. Same patched traveling cloak, same walking staff crowned with a hagstone that had been old when the covenant was young. But her face was thinner, and there was frost-burn on her cheekbones that no competent hedge-witch should ever have let happen to herself, and her eyes — her eyes had the look Isandro had seen once in a mirror after she'd read the prophecy book: the look of someone who has read their own ending and is trying to decide whether to fight it or arrange the furniture around it.

"You look terrible, mistress," Isandro said, because if she didn't start with something small and familiar she was going to start screaming.

"You look taller," Marisol said. "Running agrees with you. I hate that."

The mule snorted. Somewhere under the snow, the wind paused, listening.

"I've crossed a realm and half a waste to find you," Marisol said. "You could offer an old woman a seat."

"There's no seat. There's snow."

"Then we stand, as we always did in the herb shed when you were bleeding from a spell gone sideways." Marisol set her staff in the snow and leaned on it, and for a moment she just looked at Isandro, and the look was so full — of pride and grief and calculation all braided together — that Isandro's chest hurt. "You've grown, Isandro. Not in height. In *spine*. I gave you a shape to fill and you've grown past the mold. Do you know how rare that is? How rarely anything I plan survives contact with a living will?"

"Don't." Isandro's hands curled into fists. "Don't do that. Don't be proud of me like I'm a spell that worked. I'm not a working, mistress. I'm your apprentice. I was your *child*, near enough, and you knew — you knew about the book, you knew what I was for, and you never said a word."

"I said every word I was permitted," Marisol said, and there it was, the crack in the iron voice, quick and quickly sealed. "You think I trained you without loving you? You think I didn't lie awake for eleven years doing sums I couldn't balance — what the realms needed weighed against what I could bear to spend? You were never a tool to me, girl. You were the price. And I paid it every day you smiled at me and called me mistress and didn't know why my hands shook."

"Why?" Isandro's voice came out raw. "Why does it have to be anyone? Why you, why me, why—" she jerked her chin at Felix, who stood very still, very watchful, three paces back, "—why him?"

Marisol's gaze slid to Felix, and Isandro watched something cold and deliberate settle over her mentor's face, the way frost settles over a window: gradually, then all at once.

"Because the prophecy is not a suggestion," Marisol said. "I have spent four months in archives that predate the regicide, boy-warden. I have read the covenant's original terms in the hand of the king who wrote them. The stones are not keys. They are *witnesses*. They remember the blood that sealed them, and they will accept nothing else. Royal blood, freely given, at the altar of both realms. There is exactly one drop of royal blood left in either world, and it is currently standing behind my apprentice pretending to be a warden."

"You knew," Felix said quietly.

"I deduced. Your face is your grandfather's, Ashgrave. I served at court before you were born; I poured wine for your grandmother." Marisol turned back to Isandro, and her voice gentled into the register she'd used for teaching, for comfort, for lullabies over fevered nights, and Isandro hated how much of her still leaned toward that voice like a plant toward light. "Child. Listen to me. I am not your enemy. I never was. Here is what I propose, and it is the only arithmetic that closes:

"The boy dies. As written. At the altar, willingly — and he must be willing, the stones will taste reluctance and reject it, so he goes to it knowing, which is a mercy most kings' blood never got. The covenant reforges. The winter ends. Two realms live. And *you* keep your magic, your life-thread, your whole self — you walk out of this with everything, because the price was paid by the one person whose blood was minted for it. That is not cruelty. That is the kindest version of this story that exists. I have searched, Isandro. I have read every variant. This is the best one."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Felix spoke first. "And if I refuse?"

"Then the winter eats both realms within a generation, and everyone dies anyway, and your refusal buys nothing but time." Marisol didn't flinch. "But you won't refuse. I've read your file, warden. Disgraced for mercy. Struck from the rolls for refusing an order to burn a village. You've been trying to die usefully since before you met her. I am offering you the useful death you've been shopping for."

Isandro felt the world tilt. Because that was the horror of it — Marisol wasn't wrong about Felix. She'd seen it in the bothy, in the way he talked about his disgrace like a debt he was relieved to keep paying. He *would* say yes. He was already halfway to saying yes. She could see it in the set of his shoulders, the terrible calm coming over him, the man arranging himself around his own ending the way Marisol arranged furniture around prophecies.

"Felix," Isandro said. "Don't."

"It's a good bargain," he said, and his voice was light, and that lightness was the worst thing she'd ever heard.

"It's a *trap*. It's the same trap. Everyone keeps deciding what we're worth and telling us it's kindness—"

"Isandro." Marisol raised one hand, and power moved through the snowfield — subtle, vast, the authority of forty years of craft pressing down like weather. The third stone pulsed on the mule's back, and the pulse answered something in Isandro's own chest, in the second stone against Felix's ribs, and for one vertiginous moment Isandro understood that the stones were *listening*, that they had always been listening, that witnesses remember everything including this. "Child. Take my hands. Let me show you the archives. Let me show you what happens to realms without a covenant. Then choose. I will even let you choose freely — that much I can give you. But choose with your eyes open, not with a boy's mouth on yours and spring fever in your blood."

"That's not fair," Isandro whispered.

"No," Marisol agreed. "None of it is. Fairness left this story a century ago with the king's blood on the altar steps. We are only deciding now how the rest of it gets paid."

And Felix — Felix stepped forward.

"One condition," he said.

"*No*—" Isandro grabbed his sleeve.

"Name it," Marisol said.

"When it's done — when the covenant holds — you see to it she's looked after. Wardenship, position, whatever she wants. She loses nothing else. No one touches her for what we're about to do. Swear it on your craft."

"I swear it," Marisol said instantly, gravely, hand over heart, the oldest gesture of their order, binding as iron.

And that — that cheap, instant, *easy* oath, sworn while Isandro stood right there, sworn over her head like she was cargo — that was what broke the last thread.

Because Isandro suddenly saw it all laid out, clean as a ledger: Marisol, who loved her and had spent her; Felix, who loved her and was spending himself; the prophecy, the archives, the arithmetic, everyone so busy balancing accounts that nobody had asked the one question that mattered.

Nobody had asked *her*.

The rage that came up through her wasn't hot. It was cold and clear as the lake water in the drowned chapel, and it tasted like the moment she'd first read her name in a dead man's book.

*They keep deciding,* she thought. *Marisol decided. The prophecy decided. Even Felix decides — decides to die beautifully rather than stay and be difficult. Well. I can be difficult too.*

"Mistress," Isandro said sweetly, "may I see the stone?"

Both of them blinked. It was, she realized, the first obedient thing she'd offered, and obedience was the one coin Marisol had never learned to distrust from her.

"Of course, child." Marisol turned to the mule, fingers already working the knot of the warding cloth, already murmuring the release-phrase Isandro had known since she was nine years old — *softly, softly, the door remembers* —

Isandro moved.

Not toward the mule. Toward Marisol. She caught her mentor's wrists — those strong, steady, shaking hands — and pressed her forehead to them, one breath, one heartbeat, the old gesture of fealty, and felt Marisol go tender with surprise, felt the great weather-press of her craft ease for just one unguarded instant—

—and spoke the release-phrase herself, backward, the way Marisol had taught her for emergencies and never dreamed she'd remembered, and the warding cloth fell open, and Isandro's hand closed around the third covenant-stone.

It was warm. It was singing. It knew her — of course it knew her, they all knew her, she was beginning to suspect she'd been humming their song her whole life without knowing the words — and the moment her skin touched it, the snowfield *woke*. Light ran outward from her fist in veins through the ice. The wind screamed up from nowhere. Marisol's craft slammed down on her like an avalanche—

—and stopped.

Stopped, because the stone was in Isandro's hand, and the stones were witnesses, and the witnesses had apparently chosen sides.

"Isandro." Marisol's voice, for the first time in eleven years, sounded old. Actually old. Frightened. "Child, you don't understand what you're holding. What you're *unmaking*. Give it to me and we can still—"

"I understand perfectly." Isandro backed away, one step, two, the stone blazing against her chest, Felix already moving to her side with his sword drawn and his face white and *alive*, gloriously, stubbornly alive. "You want to write the ending where everyone's death is tidy and I get a pension. I've read that book, mistress. My name was in it. I'm done letting other people hold the pen."

"You'll kill us all."

"Maybe. Or maybe I'll do what you never once tried in eleven years." Isandro's eyes were burning, and she let them burn. "Maybe I'll try something *new*."

She turned and ran, and Felix ran with her, and behind them Marisol did not pursue — because the gate behind her was closing, and the stone was gone, and somewhere in the archives of a dying realm a hundred careful plans had just come apart in the hands of a girl who'd finally stopped asking permission.

The last thing Isandro heard, thin across the snow, was not a curse.

It was Marisol, saying, almost gently, in the voice she'd used over fevered childhood beds: *"Then run well, child. Winter knows where you're going."*

And ahead of them, at the far edge of the White Waste, the horizon began to glow — not gold, not dawn.

Violet. Bruised. Hungry.

The winter itself, rising to meet them.
