## 15. Queen Vess's Court

They took my boots first.

I didn't understand why until the third hour in the cell, when the cold came up through the stone floor and settled into my bones like a tenant who'd signed a lease. No boots, no coat, no belt, no knife. Just me and the grey dark of a citadel dungeon that I knew too well — because I'd helped design its locks, back when I was still Warden Ashgrave instead of a name scrawled on a kill-order.

The manacles were iron, but that wasn't what held me. What held me was arithmetic. Isandro had the second stone. Marisol had the third — or had had it; whether she still did after her apprentice robbed her, I couldn't know. Vess needed one more piece, and she had taken the only hostage worth taking.

Me.

So every hour they left me alive was an hour Isandro was free to run. Every hour was a gift I hadn't earned and couldn't refuse. I sat on the freezing floor with my back against the wall and counted them like coins.

* * *

She came at what I guessed was dawn, though down here there was no way to tell. The door opened without a sound — warded hinges, another thing I'd helped build — and Queen Vess walked in alone.

No guards. No crown. She wore a plain grey riding dress, the kind you'd wear to inspect a granary, and her hair was bound back with a leather cord like a soldier's. She looked younger than her portraits. That was the first wrong thing about her, and it took me longer than it should have to understand *why* she looked younger.

"Lord Ashgrave," she said. "Or should I say Highness? I've never been sure which title offends you more."

"Your Majesty." I stayed seated. Standing for her felt like conceding something. "You've read the kill-order, then. I'm told it's very flattering."

"I wrote it." She gestured, and a chair of black ice grew out of the floor beneath her — smooth, silent, wrong. Hedge-magic, but not hedge-magic. Something older wearing hedge-magic's clothes. She sat, folded her hands, and studied me the way a jeweler studies a flawed stone before deciding whether to recut it or discard it. "You've been busy. Two stones moved through your hands this season. A chapel drowned for three hundred years opened like a door. The war-camp amphitheater emptied mid-truce. My hunters are still finding frost-burned bodies along the White Waste road."

"Some of those were yours," I said. "Dain among them."

Something crossed her face. Not grief. Recognition, maybe — the look of a woman confirming an entry in a ledger. "Yes. Dain. He was loyal to the idea of you, which is the most dangerous kind of loyalty there is. I'm sorry he chose it."

"You're not sorry."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But I know the words, and their weight, and where to place them. That's a kind of courtesy, isn't it?"

I said nothing. In the old days — the real old days, the ones nobody alive remembered — I'd have called this a negotiation. Now I understood it was something else. She hadn't come to bargain. She'd come to show me the board.

"Do you know how long I've ruled?" she asked.

"A century," I said. "Give or take. The histories disagree on the coronation date, which everyone always assumed was poor record-keeping."

"The histories disagree," Vess said, "because there was no single coronation date. There were eleven of them." She let that sit in the cold air between us. "Eleven times I have taken this throne. Eleven times the covenant-stones have surfaced, someone has tried to reforge the pact, and the winter has been pushed back just far enough for people to start hoping. And eleven times I have ended it."

The cold in the cell got colder. Or maybe that was just me.

"That's not possible," I said, and heard how thin it sounded even as I said it.

"Isn't it?" She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse. "You've seen the waygates bend time. You watched a book pull a hedge-witch across two realms in a heartbeat. Time in Thornfell is not a river, Felix. It's a frozen lake — and I learned, a hundred years ago, how to walk on it. Backward. Sideways. To a night in particular."

"What night?"

"The regicide." Her voice didn't change at all. That was the horror of it — no relish, no confession-weight, nothing. Inventory. "Every account agrees: King Aldous Ashgrave was murdered by his own wardens at the covenant altar, his blood spilled on the stones, and the pact shattered. Traitors, the histories say. Ambition, the ballads say. But no one has ever explained *why*. Why would eleven sworn men break the oldest oath in the world? What could possibly pay them enough?"

"You're going to tell me," I said.

"I paid them," Vess said. "In advance. From the future. I went back and I bought the murder that made me queen, and then I lived forward through a century of winter to make sure no one ever unmade it. The regicide wasn't a crime, Lord Ashgrave. It was an investment. And I am the return."

* * *

I want to say I raged. I want to say I lunged at her, manacles and all, and had to be dragged off. That would be a better story than the truth.

The truth is I laughed. One short, ugly sound, there in the freezing dark, and Vess tilted her head like a bird hearing a note out of season.

"You find it funny."

"I find it *familiar*," I said. "A prophecy says I'll betray the woman traveling with me. Everyone — me included — spent months assuming fate was a wall we were walking into. And here you are, telling me you built your whole reign the same way. You saw an ending and you walked backward into it and called it destiny." I shook my head. "You're not a queen. You're a coward with a time-gate."

For the first time, the pleasantness cracked. Just a hairline. Just enough to see the thing underneath, and the thing underneath was very old and very tired and had done arithmetic for a hundred years without once checking its work.

"Cowardice kept these realms from tearing each other apart," she said. "Do you know what the covenant actually was? Not peace. *Stasis.* Two worlds stitched together so tightly neither could change. The old kings called it harmony. It was a knot. And knots strangle slowly, Felix — the winter is the knot tightening. I didn't break your family's precious pact out of malice. I broke it because stasis is death with good manners, and I preferred a slow freeze to a slow strangulation. Then I discovered the freeze was *useful*. Fear rules better than love. Cold keeps people close to their fires and far from their questions. So I stopped the thaw. All of them. Eleven chances at spring, and I killed every one."

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "You've won. You have my location, my face, my name in every garrison from here to the coast. Isandro will walk into whatever trap you've built around me, because she's stubborn and half-feral and loves like a house fire. So why waste breath explaining yourself to a dead man?"

Vess rose. The ice chair melted back into the floor behind her, and she stood over me in her plain grey dress looking, suddenly, exactly as young as she appeared — a woman who had made one enormous choice and then spent a century refusing to look at it directly.

"Because you're going to offer me a trade," she said. "And I want you to understand precisely what you're trading against."

* * *

They fed me after she left. Bread, hard cheese, water in a tin cup. Someone had decided a dead man deserved a last meal, or a turned man deserved a welcome, and either way I ate every crumb because hunger makes liars of us all and I refused to be lied to by my own body.

Then they brought me up.

Not to a throne room — to the map room, high in the citadel's eastern tower, where I'd once stood as a probationary warden and memorized border passes under a tutor who smelled of pipe smoke. The maps had changed. Whole ranges were gone under white. The southern coast was drawn in dotted lines now, the cartographer's admission that nobody had surveyed it in decades. Winter, rendered in ink.

Vess stood at the great table with her hands flat on the White Waste, and around her stood six wardens in crown-grey, and every one of them had trained beside me, bled beside me, drunk with me in the barracks at Harrow Ford. Captain Sera Iln. The Voss twins. Old Brennick, who'd taught me sword-forms and cheated at cards. None of them would meet my eyes except Brennick, who looked at me the way you look at a grave you dug yourself.

"There she is," Vess said, tapping the Waste. "Your hedge-witch. My watchers put her at the shepherd line two days ago, moving north with a stolen sledge and no supplies, in weather that kills grown men in an hour. She's following *you*, Felix. Which means she'll follow any trail I choose to leave." She slid a marker across the map — a small black stone, clicking against the wood. "Here. The old altar. Where your ancestor died. Where the covenant was knotted. She'll arrive within four days, because the prophecy wants her there and prophecies are just roads I haven't paved yet."

"And if I refuse to play?" I asked.

"Then she arrives anyway, with two stones, into a garrison of three hundred, and dies tired and frozen and certain you abandoned her." Vess's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "If you cooperate, she lives. That's the trade. Bring me the stones — all three — kneel, renounce your claim publicly, and I let her walk back through a stabilized gate to her own world with her magic intact. She goes home. She forgets. Realms heal over wounds like her; they always do."

"And you get eternal winter."

"I get *order*," she said. "Eternal winter is your word. Here is mine: a kingdom that cannot be broken again, because nothing in it can change. No regicide. No prophecy. No hedge-witches falling through gates with names in old books. The stones, knotted into a new pact with me at the center — not royal blood, *my* blood, blood that has already proven it can hold a century together. Your family's experiment failed, Felix. Mine hasn't. I'm simply asking you to sign the peace treaty."

I looked at the map. At the little black stone sitting on the altar site. At the dotted coastline where spring used to reach.

And the strange thing — the thing I'll remember longest about that room — is how clear everything became. Not hot clarity, not the burning righteous kind the ballads like. Cold clarity. The kind you get at the bottom of a frozen lake, looking up through the ice at a light you can't reach. I saw Vess exactly as she was: not a monster, not a tyrant out of a nurse's tale, but a woman who had found one answer a hundred years ago and had been too afraid to check whether it was still true. Eleven springs murdered, not out of evil, but out of *maintenance*. She was a steward who had fallen in love with the ruin she was keeping.

Isandro would have hated her. Isandro would have set her on fire with a word and felt bad about it later. But standing there, I understood something Isandro couldn't have understood from outside: Vess was what I would become if I ever let fear finish its arithmetic. If I decided the only safe world was a world where nothing could ever go wrong again.

"You're waiting for my answer," I said.

"I'm waiting for you to be sensible," Vess corrected. "Sensibility runs in your line. It's why I chose your ancestor's wardens, and it's why I'm choosing you. Kings die for glory. Wardens negotiate."

I thought about Dain, dying in the snow at the border shrine with my knife in him and my name in his mouth. I thought about Brennick's eyes, avoiding mine. I thought about a bothy with no snow falling outside it, and a girl who kissed me like an apology and a dare at the same time, and the way she said *I choose my own path* like she was carving it into a doorpost.

Then I thought about the one thing Vess didn't know she'd given me.

Because she'd told me the truth. All of it. The regicide, the time-walking, the eleven murdered springs — she'd laid her whole board out in front of a prisoner, because she was certain I had no moves left. And she was almost right. Almost.

But she'd confirmed it herself, in her own voice, in front of six witnesses: *prophecies are just roads I haven't paved yet.* She believed fate was terrain. She believed the future was a country you could annex.

And the prophecy — the real one, the one in a smuggler's cache that glowed with a runaway's name in it — had never once said the betrayal would be of Isandro. I had read those lines a hundred times in my nightmares. *The crown-sworn shall turn his cloak.* Turn his cloak. Against whom? Everyone had assumed. Vess had built a century on the assumption.

There was more than one crown in that room.

"I accept," I said.

Vess smiled, and it reached her eyes, and that was the saddest thing I'd seen all winter. "Kneel, then, Lord Ashgrave. Let's begin."

I knelt. On the map-room floor, in front of the woman who had murdered my family's future before my grandfather's grandfather was born, I bowed my head and gave her exactly what she wanted.

And began, quietly, to plan her undoing.
