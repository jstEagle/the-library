## 16. The Rescue That Proves the Prophecy

The citadel of Queen Vess rose out of the ice like a black tooth, and I hated it on sight.

Not because it was ugly — though it was, all buttresses and frost-scarred granite, a fortress built by people who expected to be besieged forever. I hated it because it was *confident*. Everything in this realm had been dying for a hundred years, the gates, the fields, the sky itself, and this place just sat there on its crag, warm-lit and smug, drinking the winter like it was wine.

Somewhere inside it, Felix was in chains. Or worse. Or being *asked questions*, which with Vess was probably worse than chains.

I crouched in the shadow of a granary wall at the citadel's edge, wearing a guard's cloak I'd won off a drunk sentry two miles back — won fairly, if you didn't count the sleeping draught in his ale, which I didn't, because hedge-witch ethics are flexible and mine have always been more flexible than most. The cloak smelled of wet wool and someone else's sweat. My fingers were numb through stolen gloves. The second covenant-stone hung against my sternum in its sling of braided thread, warm as a held bird, humming that low note it never stopped making since the amphitheater.

*Felix Ashgrave,* it had whispered, mid-heist, in front of both armies' worth of witnesses. *Heir of ash.* And then everything had gone sideways, and we'd run, and we'd fought, and he'd told me the truth with snow in his hair and shame in his voice: royal blood, exiled line, the whole rotten inheritance. And then he'd tried to leave me in the night like a coward, and I'd followed him across the White Waste, and Winter itself had tried to bargain with me — *give me his blood and go home* — and I'd told it no, and we'd nearly died, and Marisol had appeared at the gate with the third stone and a plan written in Felix's blood, and I'd stolen from my own mentor and run a second time in one lifetime.

And now here I was. Outside the last door. With two stones and no plan worth the name.

The plan, such as it was, had three parts. One: get in. Two: get Felix. Three: don't think about what comes after until we're running.

I've done a lot of jobs with plans like that. Most of them worked. The ones that didn't taught me that step three is where plans go to die.

* * *

Getting in was almost insultingly easy, which should have been my first warning.

There was a postern gate on the north face, half-buried in drift, guarded by exactly one man who was exactly as cold and bored as every sentry I'd ever been. I came up behind him with a whisper of hedge-craft — not a spell, nothing so grand, just the little trick Marisol called *borrowing attention*, where you pull a person's focus toward a sound you've placed ten feet from where you actually are. He turned toward a skittering noise in the snow. I put him gently to sleep against the wall. His keys were on his belt, because of course they were; this realm's army had been at war with winter so long it had forgotten there were other kinds of thieves.

Inside, the corridors were lit with oil lamps that burned a color I didn't like — a greenish gold, wrong for flame, right for something fed on more than oil. The stone under my boots drank sound. My own breathing felt loud.

The stone against my chest hummed louder as I went deeper. I took that as a compass. Covenant-stones want each other; the first one, the drowned chapel's, rode in my pack wrapped in wool and warding-threads, and between the two of them I could feel a faint pull, down and left, toward the heart of the fortress. Toward the dungeons, I assumed. Toward Felix.

A maid passed me in a corridor and nodded, because I was wearing a guard's cloak and walking like I belonged somewhere, and belonging is ninety percent posture. I nodded back. My heart hammered so hard I could hear it in my ears, and I thought, absurdly, of Marisol's voice: *You walk like a smuggler, Isandro. Walk like you're late for something boring.*

So I walked like I was late for something boring, down stairs that spiraled into cold, past cells with iron doors furred in frost, following the twin pulls of the stones and my own stupid, stubborn hope.

The dungeon was beneath everything, older than the rest of the citadel, its walls dressed in stone that predated Vess's dynasty — maybe predated dynasties altogether. And there, in the second-to-last cell, behind bars as thick as my wrist, was Felix.

He was sitting cross-legged on the floor, hands bare — they'd taken his gloves, his sword, everything crown-sworn about him — and when he looked up at me his face did three things in one second: disbelief, relief, and then a kind of horror, arriving last and staying longest.

"No," he said. "No, no — Isandro, you have to go."

"Hello to you too." I was already working the keys. "You're welcome."

"You don't understand." He stood, and the lamplight caught what they'd done to him — split lip, the bruise blooming along his jaw, wrists raw where the manacles had been. "She let them take me alive. Vess doesn't take prisoners she intends to keep. She takes bait."

"I know." The lock stuck; I swore at it in two languages. "That's why I'm getting you out before she springs the trap."

"That's not—" He gripped the bars, and up close I could see he hadn't slept, hadn't eaten, had spent whatever hours they'd given him doing exactly what I'd have done: turning the trap over in his mind, looking for the flaw, finding only himself. "Isandro. Listen to me. She doesn't need to spring anything. She needs me *here*, and she needs *you coming after me*, because the prophecy—"

"The prophecy says you betray me," I said. "I remember. I've been carrying it around since before I met you. It also said I'd die in the White Waste, and here I am, extremely not dead, so forgive me if I've stopped treating old books as gospel."

"It's not that part." His voice dropped, urgent, and the stone at my chest flared warm enough that I felt it through two layers of cloth. "It's the third verse. The one Marisol's book shares with the palace copy — Vess showed it to me during questioning, she wanted me to see it. *When the thief comes for the heir, the heir's chains will buy her the heart of the pact.*"

The lock gave. The door swung open. And for one long moment neither of us moved, because we both understood the shape of the sentence at the same time.

*The heart of the pact.* The second stone. The one hanging under my cloak, singing its head off, leading me straight down into the deepest cell of the citadel like a lantern in a dark I couldn't see the bottom of.

"She can't take it from me," I said. "It's warded. It's bound to me. I've been carrying it across a frozen waste, it's not going to jump into her hand because—"

"Because you'll give it to me," Felix said quietly.

The corridor went very still.

"To break me out," he went on, "you'll need to unbind it. You'll need your hands free and the stone's song quiet, because the wards on these cells answer to covenant-magic and the stones are covenant-magic, and the moment you loosen your hold on it — the moment you hand it over so I can fight, so we can run — it stops being yours. Stones don't stay loyal, Isandro. They stay *near*. And she has a vessel ready. She's had one waiting for a hundred years."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out cracked and ugly. "That's insane. That's the most paranoid, doom-soaked—"

"Isandro." He took my face in his cold hands, and his eyes were the gray of thaw-water, and they were terrified. "Everything I have ever feared about myself has come true in the exact shape I feared it. Please. Let me be wrong about something else instead."

* * *

Here is what I should have done. I should have knocked him out — I'd done it to a sentry an hour earlier, and Felix had a much more hittable jaw at the moment — stuffed him in the warded sling, and carried him out like contraband while keeping both stones bound tight to me and trusting my craft over his fear.

Here is what I actually did. I looked at his ruined wrists and his split lip and the way he flinched from his own prophecy, and I thought: *he's been in a cell for two days because of me. Because he killed a brother-in-arms for me. Because he burned his whole life down rather than hand me over.* And I thought: *one moment of trust. He's earned one moment of trust.*

"We run for the postern gate," I said. "North face, one guard, asleep. We don't fight anyone we don't have to. And you keep your hands where I can see them, Ashgrave, because if you're wrong about yourself I want to watch it happen."

His laugh was wet and brief. "Deal."

We made it forty feet.

The trouble wasn't an ambush. The trouble was the stairs — a flight of them, slick with frost-melt, and Felix's legs were two days weak, and he went down hard on the third step with a crack of knee on stone that echoed up the spiral like a gunshot. I reached for him. Instinct, pure and stupid: my hands full of helping, my attention all on him.

Which meant that for exactly four seconds, I was holding the second stone's binding with nothing but habit.

Four seconds. That's all it takes. That's all it has *ever* taken, I'd learn later; the whole architecture of Vess's century was built on four-second windows.

The stone sang. Not its low traveling hum — a rising note, a bell-note, a call. And something answered from above: a sound like a choir heard through a closed door, and a pressure in the air like the moment before lightning, and every lamp in the stairwell flared green-gold at once.

"Oh no," I said.

"Oh *no*," Felix agreed, already scrambling up, and we ran.

We ran through corridors that had been empty and were suddenly not empty, past guards who weren't reaching for weapons but for *us*, moving with a coordination that no garrison achieves by drill alone. Someone had sounded no alarm, because no alarm was needed. The trap hadn't sprung. The trap had simply finished closing, patiently, the way a hand closes around something small.

They funneled us — I saw it even as it happened, the smuggler in me screaming the geometry — down a broad stair into a hall I hadn't seen on the way in, a hall with a vaulted ceiling and a floor of black ice-glass, and standing at the far end of it, robed in white fur and absolute certainty, was Queen Vess.

She was smaller than I'd imagined. That was the worst part. All that winter, all those hunters, all that engineered history, and the woman at the center of it was compact and silver-haired and had eyes the pale blue of deep glacier, and she smiled at us the way you smile at children who've finally finished a long errand.

"Warden Ashgrave," she said. "And the witch. Right on schedule."

Felix stepped in front of me. Old instinct, crown-sworn to the bone: body between the threat and the charge. "Your Majesty."

"Don't." Her voice was mild, almost kind. "Don't perform loyalty for me. I read your file. I know what you did at the border shrine. I know what you are." She looked past him, at me, and her gaze snagged on my chest like a hook in cloth. "Give me the stone, child, and I'll let you both walk out of my winter. I keep bargains. It's the old kings who didn't."

"She lies," Felix said. "She always—"

"I know she lies," I said. "Everyone lies. It's the *shape* of the lie I'm checking."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: in that hall, for one moment, I genuinely believed I had a choice. Vess's guards ringed us but didn't close. Vess herself waited, patient as frost. And I stood there with two covenant-stones bound to my body by threads of my own craft, and I thought: *I can burn the bindings. I can spend the magic, dump both stones' power in one blast, blow a hole in the wall and run broken and empty but free.* It would cost me everything Marisol had poured into me, years of training, the whole hoard of my craft — but I'd kept my life-thread out of it. I'd walk away a person, not a husk.

I got as far as raising my hands.

And Felix — Felix, who had sworn on the waste, on the bothy, on everything sacred either of us still recognized — moved. Not toward Vess. Toward *me*. Fast, desperate, protective, exactly the man I'd chosen over the prophecy, reaching to shield me from whatever Vess was about to do.

And the guards, who had been waiting for precisely that movement, closed like a book.

Later — in the next hours, in the terrible arithmetic afterward — I would replay it a thousand ways. Had he been turned? Had they gotten to him in the cell? Was this the betrayal, the real one, the one the old book promised in its dry century-dead ink? But in the moment, in the hall, I saw his face, and his face was horror. He was reaching for me the way you reach for someone falling. He was doing the most loyal thing he knew how to do.

It didn't matter. That's the lesson, if the prophecy has a lesson, and I hate that it does: *it doesn't matter what you meant.* Fate reads actions, not hearts. A man who reaches for you at the wrong moment betrays you as thoroughly as a man who sells you, and the book doesn't record intentions. The book records what happened.

The guards took my wrists. The bindings I'd raised snapped like wet twine — not broken, *unpicked*, by wardens who knew covenant-work better than I ever would, who'd been trained their whole lives on exactly the locks I'd learned to make. And the second stone lifted from my chest on its own warmth, drifting, singing, across the black floor into the waiting hands of Queen Vess, who cupped it like an egg and closed her fingers and smiled her small, certain smile.

"There," she said softly. "Exactly as written."

Felix was on his knees, held by two men, straining against them, saying my name in a voice I'd never heard him use — not the warden's voice, not the heir's voice, the other one, the private one, the one from the bothy — and I couldn't look at him. I looked at the floor instead, at our two shadows stretched long and thin by the green-gold lamps, lying side by side on the ice-glass like figures cut from paper.

One page. Two shapes. One story, already written, and we'd walked into it holding hands.

"Take the witch to the tower," Vess said. "The heir stays where he is. We'll want them both at the altar." She turned to go, the stone glowing through her fingers, and paused, and glanced back with something that in a kinder face might have been pity.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you never had a chance, child. The book was finished before you were born. All you ever chose was the scenery."

They dragged Felix one way and me the other, and the last thing I saw of him was his hand, open and reaching, closing on empty air.

The tower room they put me in was warm. That was its cruelty. A fire, a bed, a window of thick glass showing the bruised-violet sky and the upward-drifting snow. They wanted me rested. They wanted me *whole*, whatever wholeness meant to people who thought a person was a component.

I sat on the bed with my back to the wall and my knees to my chest, and I did not cry, because crying was a luxury with a price and I'd stopped being able to afford luxuries somewhere on the White Waste.

Instead I did the thing I do, the only thing left, the thing Marisol beat into me across a decade of cold mornings: I counted what I had.

Two hands. One life-thread, still mine, unspent. No stones. No magic I could reach — they'd warded the room, dampened me like a snuffed candle. No Felix. No plan.

And one sentence, circling, circling, like a bird over open water:

*All you ever chose was the scenery.*

I pressed my forehead to my knees and let the words sit there, and underneath the despair — under the cold lead weight of it — something small and contrary stirred. The same thing that had made me steal a prophecy book off a smuggler's shelf. The same thing that had made me tell Winter *no* to its face. The same thing that had made me run from Marisol twice.

Because here's what Vess didn't know, reading her finished book: I'd read it too. Every word. And books, even prophetic ones, are written in language, and language lives by rules, and rules have edges.

*The heir's chains will buy her the heart of the pact.* Bought and paid for. Fine. But the book never said she'd *keep* it. And it never said a word — not one syllable in a hundred years of ink — about what happens when a hedge-witch with nothing left to lose decides that fate can have its letter, every letter, exactly as written —

and still loses.

Down in the hall, the stone had sung for Vess. Up in the tower, I put my palms flat against the warded wall, felt the dampening press back against my skin like a hand on my face, and started, very quietly, to work.

Somewhere below me, they were moving Felix. Somewhere below me, an altar was being prepared. And somewhere in a century-old book, a page was turning toward a chapter whose ending nobody — not Vess, not Marisol, not the dead fool who first set pen to parchment — had actually read to the end.

I had taken everything from me except the one thing I'd never been trained to use.

Desperation.

I flexed my fingers against the stone, and began.
