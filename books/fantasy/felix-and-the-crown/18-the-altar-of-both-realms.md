## 18. The Altar of Both Realms

### Isandro

The altar sat in both worlds at once.

Isandro felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in her teeth, a hum in the marrow of her arms, the same sensation as standing too close to a struck bell. The White Waste ended here at a shelf of black stone that jutted from the ice like the prow of a drowned ship, and when she climbed it, she was climbing two things at once: a glacier under a bruised-violet sky, and a hilltop meadow under a summer sun so ordinary it made her eyes sting.

She could see both Thornfells.

Below and behind her, the mirror-realm's winter stretched to the horizon, white on white, the frozen city a smudge of grey. Ahead — through, somehow, *through* — the other Thornfell rolled green and gold in late afternoon light, hedgerows and sheep-pastures and a river catching fire from the westering sun. The border between them was not a line but a seam, a place where the air itself had been stitched shut a century ago and had been slowly tearing ever since.

And at the center of it all, straddling the seam like a keystone, stood the altar.

It was older than the covenant. She knew that without being told, the way you know a thing in a dream. A slab of the same black stone as the shelf beneath it, ringed by nine pillars of which only four still stood whole, carved with script in no alphabet she'd ever seen — though her fingers, when she touched them, read it anyway. *We gave freely,* said the stone under her palm. *Freely given, freely held.*

"Isandro." Felix's voice, low, behind her. "The stones."

She turned. He stood at the base of the altar with the sledge they'd hauled across the Waste, and he looked terrible and beautiful and half-dead, frost in his black hair, the wound on his side seeping through its bandage, his eyes fixed on her with an expression she'd learned to dread over these long weeks: the look of a man memorizing something he expected to lose.

"The stones," she agreed, because there was nothing else to say yet.

Three of them. The first, from the drowned chapel, cool and blue-grey as lake water, humming its low drowned note. The second, stolen from the war-camp amphitheater mid-song, warm as a held breath, still whispering sometimes — though since it had whispered Felix's true name, Isandro kept it wrapped in wool and tried not to listen. And the third, wrenched from Marisol's hands in a stable loft three days ago while her mentor screamed her name after her down a frozen street.

*Isandro.* Not *child*, not *apprentice*. Just her name, over and over, like a hook thrown into dark water.

She unwrapped them now, all three, and set them on the altar's slab, and the moment the third one touched stone the humming changed. It became a chord. It became almost — almost — a voice.

*Freely given,* sang the pillars. *Freely held.*

"Now what?" Felix asked.

"Now we find out." She spread her hands over the stones. Her hedge-magic rose to meet them — what was left of it, what Marisol had spent twenty years cultivating in her like a gardener raising a rare flower for someone else's table — and the stones drank it greedily, and light began to move inside them like veins filling with blood.

The seam between the worlds shuddered.

Snow fell upward off the glacier and hung suspended, undecided. In the summer world ahead, birds went silent all at once. Isandro felt the covenant-stones reaching past her, reaching *through* her, feeling along the torn edges of a pact broken a hundred years ago, searching for the shape of what had been lost.

And then the stones showed her.

Not words. Not images, exactly. A *weight*. The weight of what the reforging required, settling onto the altar between them like a fourth stone, black and absolute.

Royal blood. Willingly given. All of it.

She already knew. She'd known since the vision in the chapel, since Marisol's face had swum up out of the first stone wearing an expression of terrible tenderness and said, in the dream-voice of memory: *It must be royal blood, child. Freely offered, or the winter only pauses. That is the price, and it has always been the price.*

But knowing it alone in a vision was different from knowing it here, with the wind pulling at her coat and the chord of the stones rising toward some final resolution, and Felix standing six feet away reading the truth in her face the way he read everything — quickly, completely, without flinching.

"No," she said.

"You haven't said anything."

"I'm saying it early." Her voice cracked on the second word. "No. Whatever you just understood — whatever the stones just told you — *no*."

Felix smiled. It was the worst smile she'd ever seen on him, because it was gentle. "You're crying."

"I'm not." She was. The cold made it worse; the tears froze at her jaw. "Felix. There are other ways. We've come this far, we've beaten every single thing the prophecy said would happen—"

"We've walked *beside* it," he corrected softly. "Every step. That's what I finally understand, Isandro. You don't beat a prophecy by running sideways from it. You beat it by choosing your part in it." He stepped closer to the altar. To the stones. To the weight hanging above them. "This is my part. It always was. Heir of Ash. The blood that broke it pays to mend it. There's a rightness in that I can't argue with, and believe me—" his mouth twisted, "—I have argued with everything my whole life."

"I won't let you."

"It isn't yours to let or not let." He reached out and took her hand, and his fingers were freezing and steady. "That's the whole point of *freely given*. Nobody can take it from me. Nobody can refuse it for me. Not even you." His thumb moved once across her knuckles. "Especially not you."

Above the altar, the chord of the stones climbed another interval, patient, inevitable, a door slowly opening.

And far away, at the southern edge of the glacier where the Waste met the passes, Isandro saw movement. Two movements. Two convergences.

An army column, small at this distance, banners snapping — the crowned snowflake of Queen Vess.

And behind it, moving faster, unarmored, wrapped in traveling grey with her silver hair whipping loose in the wind, a single figure walking like the weather itself bent around her.

Marisol.

* * *

### Felix

Felix had imagined his death a hundred times. As a boy, hiding in the ash-cellars below Ashgrave Hall while his father raged about prophecy and ruin, he'd imagined it as a mercy. As a warden, he'd imagined it as duty — a clean arithmetic, one life against the realm. On the road with Isandro, waking in shepherd's bothies with her breath warm against his collarbone, he had stopped imagining it at all, which had been its own kind of cowardice.

Now it stood in front of him, patient as the altar, and he found he felt none of the things he'd rehearsed.

No terror. No relief. Just a fierce, clarifying attention, as if his whole life had been a blurred page and someone had finally held it up to the light.

He watched Vess's column come. They had perhaps an hour, less. He watched Marisol come behind them, and watched Isandro watch her, and saw the war in her face — the girl who'd run from that woman twice, who'd chosen him over her, who was nonetheless still, somewhere underneath, the apprentice who had loved her teacher for ten years.

"Listen to me," he said, and took her shoulders, and made her look at him instead of the horizon. "When they get here — and they will get here — everything becomes noise. Vess will want the stones for a throne. Marisol will want them for her own reasons, and her reasons have never once been simple. The wardens will want orders. Don't listen to any of it. There's only one thing that matters on this altar today, and it's the price, and the price is mine."

"Felix—"

"*Mine.*" Gently. "Say you'll let me do this one thing right. My whole life I've done things wrong, Isandro. I ran from my name. I ran from the prophecy. I let a good man die at the border shrine and called it grief instead of murder. Let me do this one thing cleanly. Let me be, for five minutes at the end, the heir my house should have produced."

Her jaw worked. Frost had built up in her lashes. She looked, in that moment, so much like the figure from his childhood nightmares — the witch in the flames, the one he was fated to betray — that his chest ached with the old fear and the new love tangled together past any hope of separating them.

Because that was the joke of it, wasn't it? The prophecy had said he would betray her. Everyone had assumed betrayal meant a knife, a lie, a sold secret. But standing here, offering himself to the altar, Felix finally saw the truth with total clarity:

Handing her a corpse would be the deepest betrayal of all. And refusing the sacrifice to stay with her would betray everyone else. There was no path through this that didn't cut. There was only the choice of which wound to bleed from.

He chose. He had always, secretly, already chosen.

"They're coming," Isandro said hollowly.

"Yes."

"And you're really going to stand there and die."

"I'm going to stand here," Felix said, "and live more in the next hour than I did in thirty years. That's not nothing. That might even be enough."

The first arrows of sound reached them then — boots on ice, the creak of harness, the flat carrying voice of an officer. Vess's vanguard crested the southern ridge, maybe two hundred wardens in white-and-gold, and at their center, riding a pale horse whose breath steamed like a forge, came the Queen.

She did not look like a usurper. She looked like winter itself had decided to take human form and found a tall woman with iron-grey hair and eyes the color of glacial ice, and had been very pleased with the result.

Behind her, walking through and past the army as if it were furniture, came Marisol.

* * *

### Isandro

They converged on the altar from two directions, and Isandro discovered that hatred and love, at sufficient intensity, feel identical in the body: heat in the throat, ringing ears, hands that want to make fists and cannot decide whether to strike or hold.

Queen Vess dismounted ten paces from the altar and regarded the stones with an expression of proprietary satisfaction, as if they were furniture delivered late.

"Warden Ashgrave." Her voice carried without effort across the ice. "Or should I say — Highness? The realm has wondered where you buried yourself. What a convenient place to surface." Her gaze slid to Isandro, weighed her, dismissed her, returned to Felix. "Step away from the altar. You've done my work for me — gathered all three stones into one place, at the one spot in creation where they can be used. I confess I couldn't have managed it better myself. A century of planning, and the prophecy delivers."

"A century?" Felix's voice was arctic. "You mean the regicide. Your time-bent magic. I know, Vess. I've known since the citadel."

"Then you know I am the only one here with the patience to rule what comes next." Vess gestured, and two hundred wardens leveled crossbows. "The stones, witch. Or the warden dies first, and then you, and I take them from your cooling hands. The order of events is negotiable. The outcome is not."

"Don't," Isandro started—

"Let me guess," said a new voice, dry as old paper, and Marisol walked forward out of the ranks of soldiers as though they were reeds. Up close her mentor looked older than Isandro had ever seen her — not frail, never frail, but worn, like a tool used hard and well. Her eyes found Isandro's and held them, and there was no apology in them, and no cruelty either. Only a kind of exhausted honesty that was somehow worse than both.

"You're going to tell me I planned this," Marisol said. "That I raised you for the altar. That every lesson, every scar, every night I woke you to practice in the cold was preparation for this exact hour." She glanced at the stones, at the light moving in them, at the seam between worlds shimmering above the altar like heat-haze. "Good. Then I don't have to say it, and you don't have to pretend you didn't already know. We can skip to the part where you hate me and I accept it, because we're short on time, and the ritual doesn't care about our feelings."

"You trained me to die," Isandro said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You trained me to be the knife that kills him."

"I trained you to be the *witch who chooses*," Marisol snapped, and for one flash her composure cracked and Isandro saw the woman underneath — the one who had sat up all night with her through fevers, who had taught her to read the weather in bird-flight, who had never once said *I love you* and had said it in a thousand other ways. "The prophecy gives us a sacrifice, child. It does not give us a *volunteer*. Someone had to make sure that when the hour came, the person at this altar was strong enough to act rather than be acted upon. Yes, I knew. Yes, I used you. And yes—" her voice dropped, roughened, "—I have hated myself for twenty years, and I would do every minute of it again, because the alternative was a hundred centuries of winter and both our worlds dead under the ice. Love doesn't excuse it. But it explains why I could pay the cost."

"The cost isn't yours to pay!" Isandro's magic surged up unbidden, the last dregs of it, crackling along her arms. "None of you — not you, not Vess, not the stones, not the stupid prophecy — gets to spend him like coin! That's the whole rotten architecture of this world, isn't it? Kings and queens and hedge-witches' masters, all deciding whose life is currency!"

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Vess laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Spare us the sermon, hedge-child. Warden — Highness — whoever you are this week. The stones. Now. Or my crossbows settle the theology."

Felix stepped forward.

He stepped forward the way water flows downhill, the way a door closes, the way certain things simply complete themselves — without hesitation, without ceremony, without looking back at her. That was the part that broke Isandro's heart most cleanly: he didn't look back. Looking back would have been doubt, and he had burned his doubt at the border shrine along with his brother-in-arms and his last bridge to the crown.

"I am Felix Ashgrave," he said, and his voice rang off the nine pillars, off the stones, off the seam between the worlds, "last heir of the royal line that sealed this covenant and shattered it. The blood that broke it stands here to mend it. Freely. Willingly. Ask the stones if you doubt me."

The stones *sang*. All three at once, a chord that was nearly a word, and the light in them flared white, and the seam above the altar tore open a hand's width, and through it Isandro heard — impossibly, unmistakably — birdsong from the summer world, and smelled cut grass, and understood that the covenant was listening. Was waiting. Was *hungry*.

"Ask them," Felix repeated quietly, to Vess, to Marisol, to Isandro, to the winter itself. "They'll tell you. This is the price. This is the payment. And no queen's crossbows, no mentor's schemes, no prophecy's letter can change what is freely given."

He turned to Isandro last of all.

He smiled — the real one this time, the crooked one, the one from the bothy, from the night without snow. "Hey," he said, soft, just for her. "Walk out of winter with me. Both of us. That was always the plan. I'm just going first to hold the door."

And he laid his bare wrist against the edge of the altar's black stone, and the stones' song rose toward its final, resolving note, and the blade-light of the covenant began to gather above his arm like a falling star —

— and Isandro moved.

Later she would not remember deciding. There was no decision. There was only the certainty that had been building in her since the chapel, since the vision, since the first moment she'd read her own name in a dead man's book and felt the trap close: *the letter of the prophecy is not the spirit of it, and the spirit of it is choice.*

She seized Felix's wrist with one hand — hauling it back from the stone, hauling *him* back, both of them stumbling — and with the other she plunged her own hand into the gathering blade-light and spoke the oldest words Marisol had ever taught her, the first spell, the hedge-witch's primer, the one every village cunning-folk learns before any other:

*"What is freely given may be freely shared."*

The light screamed.

So did she.
