## 19. Half a Life Given

The knife came down and I moved before I knew I was moving.

Later I would try to remember the order of things — whether I shouted or simply ran, whether the stones were already singing or only began to sing when my blood hit the altar — but in the moment there was no order at all. There was Felix on the covenant-stone, wrists bared, chin lifted, wearing that terrible calm he'd worn since the White Waste, since he'd decided his death was a debt already owed. There was Marisol with her hands raised, feeding power into the rite like a woman pouring wine into a cup that could never fill. And there was Queen Vess at the edge of the altar terrace, frost crawling up her throat, her eyes fixed on the blood with the hunger of a thing that had waited a hundred years to drink.

And there was me, crossing the sacred ground that every warden in Thornfell would have died to keep clear, screaming a word I didn't know I knew.

The knife stopped.

Not because Felix caught it. Not because Marisol turned it aside. It stopped because the first stone — the drowned chapel stone, the one that had hummed against my palm in the frozen lake like a heart remembering how to beat — rose off the altar and hung in the air between the blade and Felix's wrist.

The whole terrace went silent. Snow hung motionless around us, neither falling nor rising, as if the world itself were holding its breath.

"Isandro." Marisol's voice was gentle, the way it had been gentle when she taught me my first cantrip, when she bandaged my burned fingers, when she told me — lied to me — that I was special. "Child, step back. This is not your sacrifice to make."

"You're right," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "It's mine."

Felix's head came up. For the first time since I'd known him — through the interrogation cell, the Undermarket, the ice-wraiths, the blizzard that had teeth — I saw fear on his face. Real fear. Not of dying. Of me.

"Isandro, no." He strained against the binding-threads of light that held him to the stone. "The covenant demands royal blood. Mine. That's the whole of it, that's what the prophecy—"

"The prophecy says a willing sacrifice of royal blood." I walked closer, one step, two, my boots crunching on snow that hadn't dared to move. "It doesn't say whose. And it doesn't say all of it."

Marisol's eyes narrowed. "You don't know what you're doing."

"I know exactly what I'm doing." And that was the strange part — I did. The three stones circled me now, slow and luminous, and their song was inside my skull, inside my blood, and it was showing me things. Showing me the shape of the covenant the way a smith sees the shape waiting inside raw iron. A pact is just a promise made permanent. A promise is just a thread. And threads can be spliced.

I was a hedge-witch. Mending was what we did. Knots and bindings, patchwork charms, the small desperate magic of people who couldn't afford clean power. Nobody had ever asked whether hedge-magic could reforge a covenant between worlds.

Nobody had ever been stupid enough to try.

"Vess!" I called, without turning. "You want eternal winter? Look closely at what eternal winter costs."

The Queen's voice came out smooth as lake ice. "Girl, when this is done, I will take those stones from your cooling hands."

"Maybe." I knelt beside the altar, across from Felix, and took his wrist in both my hands. His pulse hammered under my fingers, fast and human and alive. "But you'll have to watch first."

"Isandro." His voice cracked. "Please. I have spent my whole life knowing I was meant for this. Let it mean something."

"It will mean something," I said. "Just not what anyone wrote down."

I looked up at Marisol. She stood very still, and behind the mask of her face I could see her calculating — running probabilities, weighing outcomes, deciding whether I was a variable to be corrected or an instrument to be used. It was the same look she'd given me over the prophecy book, I realized now. Over every lesson. *A vessel*, the stone had shown me. *Trained deliberately.* Well. Vessels could choose what they carried.

"Master," I said, using the old address, the one that used to feel like safety. "Watch me do the one thing you never taught me."

Then I reached into myself and found the thread.

Every witch has one. Marisol called it the life-line, the silver cord that runs from the soul down into the deep places where magic comes from. Cut it and you die. Fray it and you go hollow. Every spell I'd ever cast had been a tug on that cord, a little borrowed light. And every witch knows, deep down, that the cord has a length. You spend it or you keep it, but you never, ever give it away.

I gave it away.

Half of it.

The cutting didn't hurt the way I expected. It hurt the way drowning hurts — vast and slow and intimate, cold filling up all the spaces where warmth used to be. I felt the shears of the rite close around my life-thread and I guided them, *guided* them, because the difference between sacrifice and butchery is consent, and I was consenting, and the stones sang louder, sang *hungrier*, and half of everything I was poured out of me and onto the altar—

—and I caught Felix's thread with my other hand.

His life-thread was gold. Of course it was. Royal blood, heir of ash, the line that broke the world. It burned to touch, bright and proud and heavy with a century of inherited guilt. I took the severed half of mine — gray-silver, hedge-born, patched and mended a thousand times, tough as smuggler's rope — and I spliced them together.

Knot work. Just knot work. The first thing Marisol ever taught me.

*When the thread frays, child, don't fight the fray. Weave it in.*

I wove his gold into my gray and my gray into his gold, half and half, until neither thread knew which life it belonged to. Until the covenant, reaching down the rite for a king's blood to drown itself in, found instead something it had no name for: a shared cost. Two halves of two lives, willingly given, neither whole nor wholly spent.

The silence broke all at once.

Light went up out of the altar like a geyser, white and gold and gray braided together, and the three stones slammed home into the ancient sockets with a sound I felt in my teeth — a sound like a door closing gently after a hundred years of standing open in the cold. The runes along the terrace blazed. The frozen lake below us groaned and shifted. And through the bruised-violet sky above Thornfell's mirror-realm, I watched the color come back — thin at first, then spreading, dawn-colors bleeding into a sky that had forgotten them.

The winter didn't end dramatically. It ended the way a fever breaks. One moment the cold had teeth; the next, the teeth were gone, and somewhere far below us I heard water moving under ice that was learning how to be thin again.

The binding-threads holding Felix dissolved. He sat up slowly, staring at his own uncut wrists, and then at me, and his face was doing several things at once and none of them well.

"What did you do?" he whispered.

"Knot work," I said.

And then the ground tilted, and the light went soft at the edges, and the last thing I saw before the dark took me was Felix lunging to catch me, and Queen Vess screaming — actually screaming, fury and terror in equal measure — as the frost on her skin began, very slowly, to melt.

* * *

I woke to birdsong.

That was wrong. I knew it was wrong before I opened my eyes, because there had been no birdsong in either Thornfell for a hundred years. But there it was, insistent and ordinary, some small brown thing declaring ownership of a thawing branch somewhere above me.

I was lying under a cloak in the shelter of the altar terrace, firelight flickering against my eyelids. When I opened them, Felix was sitting beside me, still in his ruined warden's coat, watching me with an expression I couldn't read.

"You've been out for two days," he said.

"Have I." My voice was a rasp. I tried to sit up and the world politely declined to participate. Felix's hand steadied my shoulder — warm. Everything was warmer. "The stones?"

"Set. Sealed. The waygates are... waking up. Wardens from both realms are standing at them staring at each other, trying to decide if this is peace or a trick." He paused. "Vess is gone."

"Gone?"

"Dissolved. In front of forty witnesses. The new covenant—" he stopped, started again. "The new covenant doesn't recognize her claim. Whatever she was, whatever kept her alive a hundred years, it was built on the old terms. The old terms are void." He looked at his hands. "She melted. Like dirty snow."

Good, I thought, and then, immediately, horribly: *and I felt nothing about it.* No triumph. No horror. Just a tired acknowledgment, the way you note weather.

"Felix." I reached for my magic.

Nothing.

I reached again, harder, the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. Where the silver cord had lived — that bright humming presence I'd had since childhood, the warmth in my chest, the sense of the world's seams — there was smooth, quiet nothing. A healed place. A scar.

My breath went out of me in a long, shaking rush.

"Isandro—"

"Half," I said. "I gave half. I thought—" My hands were trembling. I pressed them flat against the stone. "I thought half would leave half. Something. An ember. But it's all quiet in there. All of it."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, carefully, like a man defusing something: "What does it feel like?"

"Like being ordinary." The word came out bitter. "Like being everyone else."

"Isandro." He said my name the way he'd said it in the bothy, the night without snow, before either of us ruined it by wanting more than we could afford. "You reforged a covenant that killed kings. With knot work. If that's ordinary, then the word means nothing."

I wanted to believe him. I lay there under the cloak, listening to that ridiculous bird, and I wanted it so badly my chest ached worse than the empty place where my magic used to be.

But underneath the wanting was a colder question, the one that had followed me out of the dark and was waiting patiently for me to be strong enough to ask it:

*Does he love me, or does he love the sacrifice?*

Because I had seen his face when I cut the thread. Because I had heard him beg me to stop. Because a man who watched you give up half your life to save him carries that the way a soldier carries shrapnel — gratefully, and forever, and it changes the shape of every look that passes between you.

"Felix," I said quietly. "When you look at me — what do you see?"

He went very still.

Above us, the bird sang on, indifferent to covenants and queens and the price of reforged worlds. Somewhere beyond the terrace, I could hear voices — wardens, heralds, the machinery of two realms waking up and needing to be governed. The world was going to need so much from both of us, starting very soon.

Felix opened his mouth to answer.

And that was when the horn sounded from the valley below — three notes, urgent, the old signal for *a gate is opening that no one ordered* — and we both turned toward the sound, and the question hung there between us, unanswered, as the light on the far ridge began to bend and shimmer.
