## 20. The Long Winter Ends

The light that came off the reforged covenant did not behave like light.

It moved like water finding its level — slow, patient, filling every crack in the altar terrace, every seam in the shattered stones, every hollow place in Felix's chest that had been empty since he was nine years old and learned what his name cost. He stood at the edge of the altar with Isandro's hand still wrapped in his, her knuckles white, her breathing ragged, half her life-thread spent and stitched into the bones of two worlds.

She was laughing. Or crying. Or both. With Isandro it had never been possible to tell the difference, and Felix had stopped trying somewhere on the White Waste.

"Is it done?" she asked. Her voice came out scraped raw. "Is it actually done?"

He looked up.

Above them, the sky over the altar of both realms — the impossible straddle-point where Thornfell's winter realm bled into the green hills of home — was changing color. The bruised violet that had hung over this world for a century was thinning like smoke in wind. Through it, pale and uncertain, came something neither realm had seen in a hundred years: ordinary daylight.

"It's done," Felix said, and the words felt like setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

Then Vess screamed.

* * *

It was not a sound a queen should make. It was not even entirely a sound a person should make. Queen Vess of the Frozen Throne — architect of a regicide bent backward through time, murderer of kings, keeper of an eternal winter she had called mercy — stood at the far end of the altar terrace where the new covenant's terms had caught her, and she was coming apart.

Not burning. Not bleeding. *Thawing.*

Felix watched, unable to look away, as frost bloomed across her skin from the inside out — no, not frost. Water. She was turning to thaw-water from within, the way ice does when spring finally wins its argument. Her crown of black iron slid from a brow that was losing its edges, softening, running. Her eyes found Felix across the terrace, and there was fury in them still, and disbelief, and beneath both — worst of all — a flicker of something that looked almost like relief.

"You," she said. The word slurred, liquid. "You were supposed to be *mine*. Ashgrave blood. Ashgrave obedience."

"My family stopped being yours a century ago," Felix said. "When your hunters put my grandmother's head on a spike. You just hadn't noticed yet."

"I made the winter to *save*—"

"You made the winter to save yourself." His voice didn't shake. He'd expected it to. A hundred years of his line's grief, and his voice came out steady as a warden reading charges. "I've read the old wardens' logs, Vess. I know what happened at the regicide. I know you bent time to do it and bent the covenant to hide it. You didn't break the pact to free anyone. You broke it because a living covenant needs a living king, and you couldn't rule while one breathed."

Vess's mouth opened. What came out was water.

Her shoulders went first, then the elegant line of her spine, then the hands that had signed death warrants for three generations of Felix's family — dissolving into a sheet of meltwater that spread across the altar stones, thinning, spreading, catching the new daylight and throwing it back in broken pieces. The black iron crown rang once against stone and rolled, wobbling, until it tipped over and lay still.

Where the Queen of the Long Winter had stood, there was only a puddle going shallow in the sun, and steam beginning, very faintly, to rise.

Isandro's grip tightened on his hand. "Felix."

"I saw."

"Are you — I mean—" She gestured vaguely at the puddle with her free hand. "Do you feel *bad*? Because I can't tell if I feel bad. I think I'm too tired to feel bad. I think I might be feeling bad on a delay."

Felix considered the question honestly. He had hated Vess since childhood, in the abstract way you hate a story's villain before you learn the villain has a face. Now the face was a puddle, and the hatred had nowhere to go, and what remained underneath it was stranger: a kind of enormous, exhausted emptiness, like a room after the furniture's been sold.

"I feel," he said slowly, "like I've been holding my breath since I was nine years old, and I just remembered how lungs work."

"Yeah," Isandro said. "That."

They stood there a while longer, watching the water evaporate. Somewhere below the terrace, in the citadel grounds, the civil war that had ignited when Felix renounced his claim was ending — not with a battle but with a sound Felix had never heard in this realm: cheering. Ragged, disbelieving, snow-crusted wardens cheering. Half of them had fought for Vess an hour ago. The covenant's new terms were written into the air itself now; everyone in both realms could feel them, the way you feel a fever break.

"The wardens will want a story," Isandro said. "About what happened here. They'll want it clean."

"They can have the true one. It's clean enough." He looked down at her — at this runaway hedge-witch who had walked out of a smuggler's cache and through a dying gate and into the middle of a prophecy and torn the whole rotten thing open with her bare hands. "Mostly clean."

"Define mostly."

"You gave away half your magic to spite a hundred-year-old book."

"Spite is a legitimate magical tradition," Isandro said, with such dignity that Felix laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than he could measure, the sound startling him out of his own mouth.

* * *

Marisol was waiting for them at the foot of the terrace stairs.

Felix had seen the hedge-witch arrive through the stabilized waygate with the third stone, had seen Isandro steal it and run — from her own mentor, a second time, the first having been the whole beginning of everything. He had expected rage. He had expected Marisol to come for the girl she had trained for twenty years as a vessel, the way a storm comes for a coastline.

Instead the old woman simply stood at the bottom of the stairs, staff planted, silver hair loose in the new warm wind, watching them descend with an expression Felix could not read and did not much want to.

Isandro stopped three steps up. Close enough to speak. Far enough to run.

"You knew," Isandro said. Not a question. Felix had heard the vision-story twice now, once in a shepherd's bothy and once shouted across a blizzard, and each retelling had stripped more tenderness out of the words. "You knew the prophecy. You knew what the covenant would demand. You raised me to be the knife or the hand or whatever the book needed me to be, and you never once told me."

"Yes," Marisol said.

That was all. No defense. No apology. Felix watched Isandro's jaw tighten, watched her hand flex at her side where the witch-light used to gather and no longer did.

"Why?" The word cracked down the middle. "Twenty years, Marisol. Twenty years of porridge and lessons and 'mind the third knot, girl' — and the whole time I was livestock?"

"You were a child who read everything in the house and asked questions no child should survive asking," Marisol said. Her voice was flat as a frozen pond. "You were going to find the prophecy whether I showed it to you or buried it. I chose to prepare you. Preparation looks like use, from the inside. I accepted that price when you were six years old."

"And I never got to accept anything."

"No," Marisol agreed. "You didn't. That was the cost of my choice, and it was a greater cost than you know, and I have paid it every day since, and I am not going to stand here and dress it up as love so you can forgive me faster." She lifted her chin. "You broke the prophecy's letter. Do you understand what you did? A thousand years of fate, and you rewrote its grammar with half a life-thread. There are perhaps four people alive in either realm with the craft to comprehend what that cost you. I am one of them. And I am telling you, Isandro Zhou: I do not forgive myself, and I do not expect you to forgive me, and I am proud of you past the reach of any word I trust myself to say."

The silence stretched. Wind moved through it — real wind, soft, carrying the smell of wet stone and, impossibly faintly, of growing things.

Isandro came down two more steps. Stopped again.

"When I was small," she said, "you told me magic wasn't power. You said it was attention. You said a witch is someone who pays attention until the world pays it back." Her voice shook, steadied. "You paid attention to me for twenty years. Some of it was even true. I'm going to take the true parts and leave the rest, and someday — maybe — I'll send word about which parts those were."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Then she inclined her head — a small motion, grudging, precise, the nod of one professional acknowledging another's work rather than a mother blessing a daughter.

"That," she said, "will be sufficient."

She turned, took up her staff, and walked toward the waygate without looking back. At the threshold she paused — one heartbeat, maybe two — and then stepped through into the other realm, and the gate's light closed behind her like water over a dropped coin.

Isandro let out a breath that seemed to empty her entirely. Felix put a hand on her shoulder and she leaned into it, just briefly, just enough.

"Well," she said. "That went better than the last time."

* * *

They slept — actually slept, ten hours in a warden barracks bed built for one and generously shared by two — and woke to shouting that was, for once, the good kind.

Felix dressed in a borrowed uniform that fit badly and went out into a citadel transformed. The Long Winter had not merely ended; it was *retreating*, visibly, hour by hour, like a tide going out. Ice that had sheathed the battlements for a century groaned and slid. In the courtyard, wardens who had been trying to kill each other yesterday were working side by side to clear snowdrifts that were collapsing into gray, honest slush. Someone had opened the granaries. Someone else was playing a fiddle very badly and no one minded.

A young warden — Ferren, one of Vess's former hunters, still wearing the bruise of his own surrender — fell into step beside Felix.

"The council's assembled," Ferren said. "Both realms sent people through the gates this morning. First joint session in a hundred years. They're asking for you."

"For me?"

"For the heir of Ash." Ferren's mouth twitched. "Or for the man who broke a queen into a puddle. Depends which faction you ask. Also—" he hesitated, then grinned, boyish and exhausted, "—there's a woman at the north wall who says if you don't come see it in the next five minutes, she's coming to get you, and she doesn't have any magic left but she says she still remembers where wardens keep their soft parts."

"That sounds like my witch," Felix said.

The north wall faced the White Waste, and the White Waste was where the winter had lived hardest — a hundred miles of ice so deep and old it had its own weather, its own teeth, its own appetite. Felix climbed the stairs two at a time and came out onto the parapet into a crowd of wardens and underfolk and freed prisoners, all pressed to the stone, all silent, all looking north.

Isandro stood at the front, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, her dark hair whipping in a wind that had lost its knives. She didn't turn when he reached her. She just reached back and found his hand without looking, the way you find a doorframe in the dark.

"Watch," she whispered.

And Felix watched.

Beyond the wall, the White Waste lay under a sky scrubbed clean and blue for the first time in living memory. Clouds were gathering over the far ridgeline — fat, gray-white, ordinary clouds, the kind that meant nothing worse than weather. And from those clouds, falling in long slow curtains across the waste, came snow.

Downward.

All of it downward. Every flake drifting the way flakes were meant to drift, unhurried, gentle, obeying the oldest law in either world. A hundred years of upward-falling snow, of winter running backward, of a covenant dying so slowly no one alive remembered the correct direction of the sky — and now it was simply, quietly, over.

Around Felix, hardened soldiers wept openly and pretended they weren't. An old quartermaster dropped to his knees. Somewhere behind them the fiddle struck up again, and this time it was less bad.

"Falling," Isandro said softly. "It's actually falling. We did that. You and your half-life-thread and your terrible plan to die alone in a blizzard — we made snow fall the right way."

"We made a great deal of trouble," Felix said. "The snow was incidental."

"Don't be modest, Warden Ashgrave. It's unbecoming in an exiled king."

"Not a king." He said it and felt the truth of it settle into place like a bone set right. "The council can argue about what I am. Renounced is renounced. I burned that bridge with witnesses." He turned to look at her — really look, taking in the shadows under her eyes, the hollows the spent life-thread had left in her cheeks, the stubborn set of her mouth that had carried her through a prophecy and out the other side. "Which leaves me with a problem."

"Oh?"

"A hundred years of history ended yesterday. Two realms need rebuilding. The gates need wardens who've seen both sides of them. The covenant needs keepers who understand that it runs on shared cost, not royal blood." He paused. "And I find I don't want to do any of it without the person who taught me that fate is negotiable."

Isandro looked up at him. For a moment the exhaustion in her face parted, and underneath it was something bright and terrified and hopeful, the same expression she'd worn in a smuggler's cache a lifetime ago, reading her own name in a dead prophet's book.

"Felix Ashgrave," she said, "are you offering me a job?"

"I'm offering you a map," he said. "Two roads. One of them goes anywhere you want. The other one goes with me."

Above the White Waste, the snow kept falling — downward, gently, endlessly — and for the first time in a century, both realms held their breath not in fear, but in the stunned, disbelieving hush of a world remembering how to begin.
