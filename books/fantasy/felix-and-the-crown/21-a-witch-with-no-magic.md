## 21. A Witch With No Magic

The morning after the world was saved, I woke up and reached for my magic the way you reach for a blanket that's fallen off the bed in the night.

It wasn't there.

I lay still under the furs in the shepherd's bothy — the same one where Felix had told me about the prophecy, where we'd kissed and then spent three days pretending we hadn't — and I did the thing every hedge-witch does first thing in the morning. I felt for the thread. The warm little pulse behind the sternum, the hum in the fingertips, the sense of the world's edges being soft and negotiable.

Nothing. Just me. Just a body, breathing, ordinary as bread.

I'd known it would be like this. I'd chosen it, half a life-thread spliced into the covenant, my craft traded so that Felix could keep his pulse and his crown-claim and his stubborn, infuriating face. In the moment — at the altar, with Vess converging from the north and Marisol from the south and the stones singing like struck bells — it had felt clean. Simple, even. A price I could pay with my eyes open.

Nobody tells you that the bill arrives later, in installments, on ordinary mornings.

I got up quietly because Felix was still asleep by the hearth, sprawled and unguarded in a way he never let himself be awake, and I went outside into the cold to grieve where no one could see me do it.

* * *

Thornfell's other realm was thawing. That was the miracle everyone kept talking about — the wardens in the valley below, the ice groaning off the lake, the first green shoots pushing up through mud like they couldn't believe their luck. Snow fell downward now. People kept stopping in the streets of the recovering villages just to watch it fall, pointing up at the grey sky like children.

I stood on the bothy's step and watched it too, and tried to feel what they felt.

All I could feel was the absence. It had a shape. That was the cruelest part — the loss wasn't a blank, it was a silhouette, the exact outline of everything I'd been since Marisol found me scrying with puddle-water at seven years old. When I flexed my fingers, they remembered how to pull warmth out of a fire and push it into a freezing traveler's hands. When I looked at the treeline, my eyes still went to the places where a gate *used* to sit, thin spots in the world I could once have slipped through like a door left unlatched. My whole body was a map of a country that no longer existed.

"Morning."

Felix came out wrapped in his warden's cloak, hair sleep-mussed, and I watched him do the thing he'd been doing for two days: look at me first, always, before anything else. Checking I was real. Checking I was still there.

"Morning," I said.

"You slept?"

"A bit." Lie. He knew it was a lie. He didn't call it.

He leaned on the rail beside me, close but not touching, and we watched the downward snow together in silence for a while. That was another new thing between us — silence that wasn't a wall anymore. Or maybe it was still a wall, but we were both leaning against the same side of it.

"There's a warden column coming up from the valley today," he said eventually. "Reyes wants us at the lake chapel by midday. They're drawing up the joint wardenship charter. Both realms, one order." He paused. "They want the two of us there. Specifically. The woman who reforged the covenant and the heir who renounced his claim."

"I noticed you said 'the two of us' before you said 'they want us,'" I said. "That's either very smooth or very clumsy."

"Clumsy," he admitted. "I've been practicing sentences all morning. Most of them came out worse."

I almost laughed. It caught somewhere in my chest and turned into something else, something tighter, and I stared hard at the snow until my eyes stopped stinging.

"Isandro." His voice dropped into the register he used for hard truths on bad roads. "Talk to me. You've been polite for two days. You've never once been polite to me in your life. It's terrifying."

* * *

Here is what I could not say, standing there in the cold with him waiting:

I didn't know if I was grieving my magic or grieving the version of myself he might have loved.

Because here was the arithmetic I kept doing, alone, at three in the morning, staring at the rafters: Felix Ashgrave had been raised on a prophecy that named him a betrayer. He'd spent his whole life flinching from it, then walking toward it, then finally breaking its letter in front of an entire citadel. And then a witch had stepped into a ritual meant to kill him and given away half of herself so that he wouldn't die. He had watched me do it. He had screamed my name while I did it — I remembered that much through the white fire, his voice tearing across the altar.

So when he looked at me now with that careful, wondering tenderness — when he checked that I was real, when he practiced clumsy sentences — some small, mean, frightened voice in me whispered: *he loves the sacrifice. He loves what you gave up. He loves the story of you. And the girl who actually exists, the one who can't light a candle with a word anymore, the one who is just tired and sharp-tongued and empty-handed — what happens when she stops being miraculous?*

Marisol had loved me as a vessel. The prophecy had needed me as a key. Queen Vess had wanted me as a tool. Everyone, my whole life, had loved some function I served.

And Felix was kind, and Felix was different, I believed he was different —

But I'd believed Marisol was different too, once.

"Last night," I said, before I could stop myself, "when you thought I was asleep. You said something."

His jaw tightened. "You were awake."

"I know. I'm sorry. I should have—"

"You said 'the thread's gone quiet.'" He said it flatly, like reciting a report, which was how he handled things that hurt. "And I said—" He stopped. Started again. "I said, 'Good.'"

The wind moved through the thawing pines. Somewhere down the slope, meltwater ticked off the rocks.

"'Good,'" I repeated.

"It sounded wrong the second it left my mouth." He turned to face me fully, and I made myself meet his eyes, because running from this conversation would have been easier than breathing and I was so tired of easy. "What I meant — what I was trying to say, badly, at midnight, to a woman I thought was asleep — was that you're not a vessel anymore. Nobody's conduit. Not the prophecy's, not the covenant's, not Marisol's. The thread going quiet means the war's over for you. It means you get to be—" He gestured helplessly at all of me. "*This.* Whatever this turns out to be. And I was glad. That's what 'good' meant. I swear it on the crown I don't want."

"And if I'd stayed a hedge-witch?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "If the covenant had taken your half instead, and I still had my craft — would you have been glad then?"

"Yes." No hesitation. Then, honestly: "And I'd have been dead, so take my enthusiasm with appropriate skepticism."

Despite everything, something cracked open in my chest that was adjacent to laughter.

"Felix." I hugged my arms around myself. "I can't do anything. You don't understand what that's like. I have been *useful* since I was seven years old. I was the girl who could find water in a drought, who could charm a fever down, who could open doors nobody else could even see. Marisol built her whole practice around me. The prophecy built itself around me. And now I'm standing in a country I saved and I can't so much as warm my own hands, and every time you look at me I'm waiting for the day you realize you got the short end of the bargain — that you gave up a throne for a woman who can't even—"

"Stop." He said it gently, but he said it. "Can I tell you something? Something true and slightly insulting?"

"That's usually how you talk."

"The night we met — properly met, in the Undermarket, over that terrible eel stew — do you know what I noticed about you? Not the magic. Wardens are trained to sense craft; yours was masked anyway, you were smuggling yourself past our sensors like the professional little criminal you are. What I noticed was that you'd talked the ferryman down to half price *and* gotten him to throw in the stew, and you were so pleased with yourself about it. This girl with no army and no title, in a realm that wanted her arrested, negotiating like her life depended on it and enjoying herself." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't follow you because of the prophecy, Isandro. The prophecy told me to betray you. I followed you because you were the most alive person I'd ever seen, and I wanted to know how you worked."

The snow came down, soft and downward, and I let his words sit in me like heat in cold hands.

"That's not nothing," I managed. "What you just said."

"No. It isn't."

"But it doesn't fill the hole."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It doesn't. Grief doesn't work like that. Ask me how I know." He meant his family, the line of Ash, the throne he'd burned in front of the wardens. He meant the century of guilt he carried for ancestors he'd never met. "You don't get to skip it. You gave up half your life. You're allowed to mourn it. I'll stand here while you do, if you want. I'm apparently good at standing in cold places watching things I love suffer." He winced. "That came out wrong."

"Everything comes out wrong with you."

"It's a gift."

And then — because he was Felix, because he was clumsy and honest and mine in whatever unfinished way we were each other's — he reached out and took my hand. Not the dramatic hand. The left one, scarred from the ice-wraiths at the drowned chapel, ordinary, human, cold.

"Warm it yourself," he said. "You used to complain you could never feel your own fires properly. Now you can. There's your silver lining. Terrible silver lining. I'm bad at this."

"You're terrible at this."

"Word by word," he said. "That's how we'll do it. One terrible sentence at a time."

* * *

We walked down to the lake chapel together, because Reyes's column was waiting and history does not pause for grief, even earned grief.

Halfway there, I stopped.

It was the chapel path itself that stopped me — the place where the ground sloped toward the frozen shore, where three weeks ago I had waded into killing water with ice-wraiths closing around me and pulled the first stone from its altar with magic singing up my arm like a struck bell. I stood on the ridge and looked down at the black water, unfreezing at the edges, and my body did the reaching again. Automatic. Hopeful. Empty.

Felix waited. He'd learned that about me already — that I hated being hurried, that I'd come along in my own time or not at all.

"I keep expecting it back," I said. "Like it's a borrowed coat someone will return."

"Would you take it back?" he asked. "If it were offered?"

I thought about it seriously, which surprised me. I expected the answer to be yes — instant, hungry, obvious. But standing there, I found the question had teeth.

"If taking it back meant undoing the covenant," I said slowly, "no. Obviously not. Two worlds over one craft — that's not even a bargain, that's a joke." I kicked at a stone on the path. "But if I could have it back *clean*, without cost to anyone... I think I'd say yes. And I hate that. I hate that some small ugly part of me would trade the peace we bought for the warm little pulse behind my sternum."

"That's not ugly," Felix said. "That's just you. You've been that person since the ferryman and the eel stew. You love the work. Losing the work doesn't make you shallow. It makes you someone in mourning who shouldn't be ashamed of it."

"When did you get wise?"

"About a week after I stopped believing prophecies. It freed up a lot of time."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the altar — and the sound startled a bird out of the pines, some brown ordinary bird that had no business surviving a hundred-year winter and yet here it was, exactly like the rest of us.

We walked down to the chapel together, and I did not reach for the magic again. Not because the wanting was gone. But because I was starting to suspect that a person could carry a hole inside her and still walk upright, still argue about rations, still hold a scarred hand in the cold — that the hole might even be part of how I held on. Hands close around absences. That's what hands are for.

* * *

The chapel was crowded when we arrived — wardens of both realms, Reyes at the center with her maps, the air thick with woodsmoke and the smell of a world remembering spring. Someone had set the three covenant-stones on the old altar, dull and quiet now, their work done. I looked at them and felt nothing from them at all, and the nothing was so complete it was almost peaceful.

Almost.

Because as I passed the altar, the middle stone — the second stone, the one we'd stolen from the amphitheater, the one that had whispered Felix's true name to me in the dark — twitched.

Not glowed. Not sang. The covenant was sealed; the stones were spent. But for half a heartbeat, no longer than a blink, the surface of the stone shivered like water disturbed by a falling drop, and against my silent, powerless, emptied mind, clear as a bell rung once in an empty church, came a single word.

*Not finished.*

I stopped dead. Felix, a step ahead, turned back. "Isandro?"

The stone sat inert on the altar. Grey. Quiet. Ordinary as river rock.

"Nothing," I said. "Thought I saw something."

He studied my face with those warden's eyes that missed nothing, and I could see him deciding — deciding whether to press, whether to trust, whether the woman who had rewritten fate deserved the courtesy of being believed about her own perceptions.

"Okay," he said finally. "But if the stones start whispering again, you tell me first. Before the wardens. Before anyone. Word by word, remember? Starting with the words that matter."

Behind us, unnoticed by anyone else in the crowded chapel, the middle stone shivered once more — and this time, faint as breath on winter glass, I heard it laugh.
