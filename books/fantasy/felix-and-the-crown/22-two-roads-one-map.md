## 22. Two Roads, One Map

**FELIX**

The waygate at Thornfell Pass had been a wound for a hundred years. Felix remembered it from the old wardens' maps — a black seam in the air where the mountain should have shown through, weeping cold that never melted anything because everything was already frozen.

Now it was just a door.

He stood before it in the first true morning of spring, watching green creep up the far slope like something spilled and spreading. The gate's arch had regrown itself out of living granite, veined with pale gold light — covenant-light, Isandro called it, though she'd lost the magic to see it clearly anymore. She'd described it to him anyway, twice, with her hands moving like she could still feel it.

Behind him, the joint wardenship's first cohort was assembling on the training field: twelve wardens of the crown, twelve hedge-witches of the scattered covens, all of them awkward as foals, all of them armed with the same oath he'd written himself at three in the morning on the back of a supply manifest.

*We keep both roads open.*

It wasn't poetry. It was policy. But half the recruits had already started calling it the Two Roads Oath, and Warden-Captain Sera Vane — his former sister-in-arms, the one who hadn't tried to kill him at the border shrine — wore the phrase stamped into a new badge she'd commissioned without asking anyone.

"You're staring at the gate again," Sera said, coming up beside him.

"I'm inspecting it."

"You're staring."

He didn't argue. There was no point. He'd been staring at gates, and at the woman who walked through them, since the night the winter ended.

The thaw had taken four months. Rivers that had been solid since before his grandfather's grandfather came back with a sound like applause. The White Waste was retreating north a mile a week, and the mapmakers couldn't redraw fast enough — every survey came back with new valleys, new forests, new villages of people who had spent generations huddled against a cold they thought was simply the shape of the world.

Felix knew the shape of the world now. It was a circle, and it had been broken, and a runaway hedge-witch with no magic left had held the pieces together with half her own life.

"Vess's holdouts surrendered at Duskmoor," Sera said. "Last of them. The citadel garrison signed the accord this morning." She paused. "There's a name on the surrender list you'll want to see."

He took the paper. Scanned it. Stopped.

*Marisol Vey, hedge-witch, third stone-bearer — remanded to joint custody.*

"She turned herself in," Sera said. "Walked into Duskmoor with the third stone wrapped in a blanket like a baby and asked for you by name."

Felix folded the paper slowly. Of course Marisol had turned herself in. She would want to be present for whatever came next. She had spent twenty years arranging the future; she wasn't going to miss the part where it arrived without her permission.

"Where is Isandro?" he asked.

"The chapel. Where else."

***

**ISANDRO**

The drowned chapel was no longer drowned.

Isandro sat on the dry stone steps where the lake had been, boots dangling over what was now a meadow of white flowers nobody could identify, and turned the first covenant-stone over in her hands. It was warm. It had been warm since the reforging — all three of them were, humming faintly, like cats that had decided to forgive you but weren't going to make it easy.

Her fingers found no answering hum. That was the arrangement now. The stones sang; she listened. A witch with no magic, holding the most magical objects in two worlds, and somehow it felt less like a punishment than a job description.

She heard his footsteps before she saw him. She always did. Some things the loss of power hadn't touched — she'd spent a year learning Felix Ashgrave's tread on ice, on stone, on snow, on the floorboards of shepherd's bothies at midnight when neither of them wanted to admit they were awake.

"You're supposed to be drilling recruits," she said without turning around.

"Sera has them. She enjoys shouting more than I do." He sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Marisol surrendered at Duskmoor."

Isandro went still. The stone hummed against her palm, sympathetic or mocking, hard to say.

"When?"

"This morning. She asked for us both."

"Of course she did." Isandro laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twenty years I was her apprentice. Twenty years she was building me toward an altar, and I never once heard her lie to me. Not once. That's the worst part, Felix. Everything she told me was true. She just never told me *why* I needed to know it."

"Do you want to see her?"

"No." Then, honestly: "Yes. Eventually. Not today." She looked down at the stone. "Today I want to finish the survey of the southern passes and eat something that isn't garrison stew and sleep in a bed that doesn't have a warden's cot bolted to the wall."

"That can be arranged."

"Felix."

"Hm?"

"Stop arranging things and ask me."

He was quiet for a moment. When she finally turned, he was looking at the meadow where the chapel used to drown, at the light coming through the rebuilt gate behind them, at everything except her — which was how she knew this mattered to him.

"There's a posting," he said. "Joint wardenship. Rebuilding the covenant order from nothing — new oaths, new routes, new gates. It needs someone who understands both realms' magic, someone the covens trust, someone who—" he exhaled, "—someone who broke a prophecy rather than follow it. Because the whole point of the new order is that no one follows prophecies anymore. We choose."

"And you want me to—"

"I want you beside me. Not as prophecy. Not as sacrifice. Not as the girl in a century-old book who was always going to save the world." He turned to face her at last, and his eyes were doing the thing they did when he was terrified and refusing to show it, which was nothing at all, absolutely nothing, a perfectly flat gray calm. "As Isandro Zhou, who is insubordinate, reckless, terrible at following orders, and the only person I have ever wanted to walk next to."

"That was almost romantic until 'terrible at following orders.'"

"It's in the official posting description. I fought the committee on it. They wouldn't budge."

She laughed properly this time, and the stone in her hands flared gold, and somewhere across the valley a bell that hadn't rung in a hundred years rang back.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes. You absolute coward — you made it a *posting*. You could have just asked."

"I'm crown-sworn. Everything is paperwork."

"Everything was paperwork even before you were crown-sworn again."

"That's fair."

***

They walked back through Thornfell together that evening, because there was no reason anymore not to.

The pass road had changed. Isandro kept cataloguing the changes the way she used to catalogue exits and hiding places — smuggler's habits dying hard — except now the list ran: *snowdrops where the drifts were. Water running loud under the bridge. A shepherd's bothy with smoke rising from its chimney.* Their bothy. Someone lived there now, some family who would never know what had been confessed and un-confessed inside those walls during the worst blizzard of the long winter.

She stopped at the door anyway. Just for a moment.

Felix stopped with her. He didn't ask. He'd gotten good at that — knowing when her silences were doors she wanted opened and when they were doors she wanted left shut.

"The smoke smells like bread," she said.

"Then someone in there is having a better evening than the entire joint wardenship."

"We could knock. Tell them what happened here."

"And explain to a shepherd's children that the woman who saved two worlds is standing on their doorstep crying about bread smell?" He bumped her shoulder with his. "Some histories are better left unwritten. We'll write ours instead."

"Ours," she repeated. Testing the word.

"If the posting accepts."

"The posting accepted hours ago, Felix. Keep up."

***

**FELIX**

The gate between realms stood open at the top of the pass, and for the first time in a century, people were using it.

Not armies. Not hunters. A wool merchant with a handcart, arguing cheerfully with a hedge-witch about tariffs. Two children chasing each other through the arch, shrieking, one realm to the other and back, as if the boundary between worlds were a garden wall and not the scar of a broken covenant. Felix watched them go and felt something in his chest that had no warden's protocol for it.

Sera caught up with him at the arch, breathing hard from the climb, carrying a dispatch case and an expression of deep suspicion.

"The Duskmoor escort wants instructions for the prisoner," she said. "Marisol Vey. They're asking whether to transport her to the citadel cells or—" she glanced at Isandro, fifty yards down the path, crouched to show the merchant's children how the gate-light bent around a copper coin, "—whether to wait."

"Wait," Felix said.

"For how long?"

He watched Isandro stand, brush off her knees, and start up the path toward them with the stone tucked under her arm like a loaf of bread. He watched her face do several things in quick succession — none of them flat, none of them calm, all of them honest.

"As long as it takes," he said. "She'll know when she's ready. And when she isn't ready, we'll wait longer. That's the whole new order, Sera. Nobody gets marched anywhere they haven't chosen to go."

Sera snorted. "You've gone soft."

"I've gone *correct*. There's a difference. Write it down."

Isandro reached them slightly breathless, cheeks pink from the climb, and held up the copper coin the child had given her. "Payment," she said. "I'm apparently a street performer now. My magic's gone but my sleight of hand survives."

"Put it in the warden's fund."

"I'm keeping it. First wage of my new life." She looked past him, through the arch, at the other side — at her own world, the passes above Hedge-Marisol's territory, the smugglers' caches, the whole tangled country she'd fled through on the night a book pulled her name off its page. "I haven't been back," she said quietly. "Since the gate fell. Not really. Not to stay."

"I know."

"My mistress is in custody on your side of the mountains. My coven thinks I died in the White Waste. There's a boy in Lowmark who owes me eleven silver crowns and a very good knife." She turned the coin over in her fingers. "There's a whole life over there I ran away from. And a whole one over here I stumbled into. And everyone keeps asking me which one is home."

"What do you tell them?" Felix asked.

"Nothing yet. I wanted to walk through first." She stepped up beside him, shoulder to shoulder, facing the open gate with its veins of patient gold light. "Both sides are asking. Both sides have claims on me. Prophecies, debts, mentors, queens, dead kings, live ones." She slipped her hand into his. "And I keep thinking — home was never either realm, was it? Home was a bothy in a blizzard. Home was a man who let a prisoner escape because he was more afraid of fate than of duty. Home is wherever you're standing being stubborn at me."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said and it contains the word 'stubborn.'"

"Get used to it. It's in my contract."

They walked through together.

The crossing itself was nothing — a step, a shiver, the taste of ozone and rain — but Isandro stopped halfway, in the exact center of the arch, where the light ran thickest, and stood there with her eyes closed and her face turned up like someone standing in the first sun after a long illness.

"I can almost feel it," she murmured. "The covenant. Right here. Like a heartbeat under the floor."

"It beats because of you."

"It beats because of *us*. Half and half. That was the whole point." She opened her eyes. "Nobody dies whole for anyone ever again. That's the law now. I'm making it a law."

"I'll draft the paperwork."

"You do that."

On the far side, her world waited — greener than it had any right to be, the thaw racing ahead of the season, the high passes running with meltwater. Somewhere down there, a coven was mourning a witch who wasn't dead. Somewhere down there, a cell of Marisol's old allies still believed the prophecy could be salvaged, that royal blood could still be found, that the story wasn't finished just because two people refused to die in it.

Somewhere down there, in fact — though neither of them knew it yet, though the dispatches hadn't come, though the morning was too bright and too new for shadows — a page in a certain century-old book had begun, very faintly, to glow again.

But that was tomorrow's problem.

Tonight there was a road, and it ran in two directions at once, and for the first time in a hundred years, both of them were open.

Isandro Zhou and Felix Ashgrave walked down into the spring together, arguing happily about whose turn it was to cook, and the gate stayed open behind them, and the light held.
