## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The roof of Tam's cottage had been leaking for three days before anyone thought to tell Mirabel about it, and by the time he climbed the ladder with a bundle of cedar shakes under his arm, the water stain on the ceiling had spread into the shape of a river delta, branching and patient. He crouched at the ridge and studied it the way he studied everything: slowly, without hurry, as though the world had all the time in the world to be understood.

It did, more or less. That was the whole point of Ember Valley.

Below him, the valley unrolled in its usual greens — terraced barley going gold at the tips, orchards heavy with early plums, the millpond flat and bright as a coin someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, unhurried columns. Somewhere downhill, Wrenna was arguing with her goats again, her voice carrying up the slope in fragments that were mostly curses and occasionally endearments, and there was no telling which was which.

Mirabel pulled off his shirt, tied his hair back with the leather cord he kept for the purpose, and got to work.

Roofing was good work. It demanded exactly enough of him. His hands knew the rhythm of it — pry out the rotten shake, check the lath beneath, seat the new one, nail it home with two strikes that rang out over the valley like small bells. The sun came down warm on his shoulders. Sweat ran down his spine. By midmorning he had forgotten, in the way he tried to forget most things, that he was doing anything other than what he was doing.

That was the trick of it. Deliberate smallness. He'd learned it young, though he couldn't have said from whom — some instinct, maybe, or something his mother had whispered into him before she went wherever mothers go when they die in valleys that keep their secrets. Be small. Be useful. Be the man who fixes roofs and hauls barrels and never once looks over the border wall like he's wondering what's on the other side.

He was twenty-six years old and he had never left the valley. Not once. Not to the ridge line, not to the pass, not even to the ward-stones at the eastern edge where the land went strange and the air tasted faintly of iron. Other children had dared each other to touch the stones when they were young. Mirabel had never needed to dare himself. Something in him had always known, the way a dog knows a fence line, that the boundary was not decoration.

He set the last shake, tested it with his palm, and sat back on his heels to admire the work. From up here the whole valley fit inside a single glance, and the glance felt almost like holding it — this bowl of green and gold ringed by hills that rose on every side like cupped hands.

"About time," Tam called from below, where he sat whittling a stick into something that would never be anything. "I was starting to grow gills."

"You could've asked me three days ago."

"I did ask you three days ago."

"And I said I'd come when the barley was turned." Mirabel swung down the ladder and landed in the yard, rolling his shoulders until they cracked. "Barley's turned."

Tam squinted up at the roof, then at him, then offered the stick he was whittling, which Mirabel declined on principle. "You want lunch? Marta made more than we can eat. She always makes more than we can eat. I think she's trying to kill us with kindness."

"Kindness is a slow poison," Mirabel agreed gravely, and followed him inside.

* * *

The storm came up the western notch an hour past noon.

Mirabel felt it before he saw it — that was the thing nobody else in the valley could do, the thing that made him what he was, the thing that had gotten his mother exiled here before he was born and kept him here after. It started as a pressure behind his eyes, a faint metallic taste on the back of his tongue, like biting a coin. Then the light changed, going green-gray and bruised, and the birds went quiet all at once, and the wind swung around to come out of the west, smelling of rain and far-off dust and something older than either.

He stepped out of Tam's kitchen with his bread still half-eaten and stood in the yard with his face tipped up.

"Storm?" Tam said, following him out. He shaded his eyes. "Doesn't look bad."

"It will be."

"How do you—" Tam stopped. Everyone in the valley stopped, eventually, at the same question, and everyone arrived at the same answer, and everyone had long ago agreed not to say the answer out loud because saying it made it real in a way that none of them liked. Mirabel Okafor could feel weather coming the way other men felt hunger. And Mirabel Okafor could do things about weather that no one else could do anywhere.

Because storm-calling magic died at the borders of Ember Valley. Everyone knew that. It was the oldest fact in the valley, older than the mill, older than the church with its leaning bell tower, older than any story anyone told. Magic couldn't cross the ward-stones. Whatever Mirabel was, whatever gift ran in his blood like lightning waiting for ground, it lived and worked only here, inside the cupped hands of the hills. Outside, he'd be nothing. Ordinary. Safe.

He walked out past the orchard fence, alone, the way he always did. Nobody followed. Nobody ever followed; that was part of the arrangement too, unwritten like everything else. When the sky went wrong, Mirabel went out, and the valley looked away, and afterward the sky was right again and nobody asked how.

The clouds were stacking now, great blue-black towers shouldering up over the western notch, and the first flicker of lightning moved inside them like a thought behind a closed eyelid. Thunder rolled down the slopes a few heartbeats later, soft and distant, a warning rather than a threat.

But it would build. It always built. Storms that entered the valley gathered strength off the high country and then had nowhere to go — the hills held them like a bowl holds water, swirling them around and around until they spilled their violence on the fields, flattening barley, splitting orchard trees, drowning lambs. It had been that way for as long as the valley had records, which was to say: for as long as Mirabel had been alive, the storms had stopped being violent.

He raised his hands.

There was no incantation. There was nothing so theatrical. What there was, was attention — the whole of him turned upward and outward, his skin opening like a window, his breath slowing until it matched the slow pulse of the storm. He could feel it all: the charge stacked in the cloud bases, the cold air sliding over the notch, the hot updrafts rising off the sun-warmed fields, every current and counter-current of the great breathing machine of the sky. Most people heard thunder. Mirabel heard music, if music could be made of pressure and potential, vast and indifferent and beautiful in the way that only enormous indifferent things are beautiful.

And he could touch it.

He reached — not with his hands, though his hands rose as if to hold the weight of the clouds, but with whatever he was underneath the skin — and he found the first charge building toward discharge, the spark looking for its path to ground, and he smoothed it. Gently. The way you'd smooth a wrinkle out of a bedsheet. The energy didn't vanish; you couldn't destroy a storm any more than you could destroy a river. You could only persuade it. He drew the charge apart, spread it thin across miles of cloud, bled it off in increments so small that each one was only a breath of static, a tickle in the wool of the world.

Lightning still flashed — but high, and wide, and harmless, sheet-lightning blooming silently inside the cloud like lanterns behind paper. The thunder softened to a grumble, then a mutter, then the sound of a large animal settling itself to sleep. The rain came down steady and kind, straight and gentle, soaking the barley that had been begging for water all week.

Mirabel lowered his hands. His arms trembled, the way they always did, fine muscles deep in his forearms quivering with spent effort, and sweat stood on his brow though the day had gone cool. He stood in the soft rain with his face upturned and let it wash him, and for a moment — just a moment, the way it always was, just a moment — he let himself feel the whole size of what he was.

Then he folded it away, and was small again.

* * *

Wrenna found him at dusk, sitting on the low stone wall above the millpond, watching the water go copper and then violet as the light failed.

She was old the way the valley was old — bent, weathered, seemingly held together more by habit than by bone. She carried a basket of late plums and set it beside him without asking, and sat down next to him with the groaning deliberation of a woman who had earned every joint complaint she owned.

"Tam says you sang the storm to sleep again," she said.

"I don't sing."

"Tam says what Tam says. In this valley we let people say what they say, and then we say something else, and that's how we get along." She took a plum, polished it on her sleeve, bit into it. Juice ran down her chin and she wiped it with the back of her hand, entirely without shame. "Big one coming?"

"Passed already. Gentle rain. Good for the barley."

"Mm." She chewed. Swallowed. Watched the pond. A fish rose somewhere out in the copper light, leaving rings that widened and faded. "You know what today is?"

"Equinox soon. Few days."

"Equinox tonight," Wrenna corrected. "Sun crosses the line at moonrise. Old reckoning." She said it lightly, but Mirabel had known her his whole life, and he heard the weight under the lightness the way he heard weather under clear skies. "Stones'll hum tonight. They always do, on the equinox. You can put your hand on one and feel it, like a hive full of bees. Like the whole valley's purring."

"I've touched the stones," Mirabel said. "They're just rocks."

"Just rocks that hum twice a year." She smiled at the pond, not at him. "Your grandmother used to say—"

"She wasn't my grandmother."

"No," Wrenna agreed, after a pause that lasted one heartbeat too long. "No, she wasn't. Your grandmother, whoever she was, is none of my business. But old women get confused, boy, and old women say *your grandmother* when they mean *that woman who raised you half the time and taught you your letters and fed you plum cake until you were sick*, and you'll forgive us our confusion because we're old and it's getting dark." She ate another plum. "Anyway. She used to say the stones weren't walls. They were stitches. Somebody sewed this valley shut, she said, and the question you ought to be asking isn't *what's keeping me in*. It's *what was the sewing keeping out*."

Mirabel turned that over. He was used to Wrenna saying things that turned over slowly, like stones in a stream, showing different faces each time they rolled.

"And what did you say?"

"I said, *woman, you talk too much, and the plums won't pick themselves.*" She laughed, a dry rattle. Then the laugh faded, and she looked at him sideways, and her eyes in the failing light were sharp as a kestrel's. "Twenty years, Mirabel. Twenty years I've watched you be small. You do it very well. Better than she did, and she was the best I ever saw at it. You fix roofs. You turn barley. You sing storms to sleep and call it nothing. And every night you sit on this wall and look east."

"I look at the pond."

"You look east, past the pond, past the hills, at the place where the stars come down to the ridgeline. Every night. Don't think I haven't seen it. Don't think she didn't see it too, before—" Wrenna stopped herself. Closed her mouth. Opened it again. "Boy. Do you know why you've never seen the stars leave the sky?"

Mirabel frowned. "What?"

"The stars. Have you ever seen one move? Ever seen one set, the way the sun sets? Ever seen the sky change its mind?" She gestured upward, at the deepening violet overhead, where the first stars were pricking through — fixed, motionless, arranged in patterns that Mirabel had known since childhood and that had never once, in twenty years, shifted by a single finger's width. "Travelers used to say the sky turned. That the stars wheeled around a point in the north, all night, every night, slow as a millwheel. That constellations sank into the sea and new ones rose. Songs about it. Poems. Sailors steered by the turning."

"That's—" Mirabel started, and found he had no ending for the sentence. He looked up. The stars hung nailed in place, the same stars, the same places, the Hunter and the Sisters and the long pale river of the Veil, exactly where they had been on the night he was born. Exactly where they had been every night of his life. "That's just stories. The sky doesn't turn. Everyone knows the sky doesn't turn."

"Everyone *here* knows," Wrenna said softly. "Everyone here has always known. Because the woman who sewed these wards shut paid for them with her whole self, and she bought you a sky that stands still, and a valley that keeps you, and twenty years of roofs and barley and plum cakes." She stood, groaning, and picked up her basket, and for a moment her shadow fell long and dark across him in the last of the light. "Equinox tonight. Stones humming. Go put your hand on one, if you're brave enough. And when you hear what's in it — and you will hear something, boy, because it's been waiting for you — you remember that some of us loved her enough to lie to you for twenty years, and we'd do it again."

She walked away down the path, small and bent against the darkening sky, leaving the basket of plums on the wall between them like a debt or a gift, and Mirabel sat alone under the unmoving stars and felt, for the first time in his careful, deliberate, small life, the ground shift beneath him like a thing waking up.
