## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The storm came in from the western ridge an hour past sundown, and Mirabel felt it arrive the way other men feel a hand on their shoulder.

He was in his kitchen, mending a kettle handle with wire, when the pressure changed. The air went thick and sweet, the way it did before lightning, and every hair on his arms lifted as though greeting someone. He set down the kettle. Outside, the wind had begun to move through the barley in long, low waves, and beneath it he could hear the sound that never failed to raise the small hairs at the back of his neck: the hum of the ward-stones.

It was not equinox. It was not any night the stones had reason to sing. But they were singing now—a low, urgent note, like a struck bell that would not finish dying—and Mirabel stood in his doorway with the door half-open and listened to it climb.

"Wrong," he said aloud, to no one. "This is wrong."

The first stars were out. He knew them the way he knew his own hands: the Shepherd's Yoke hanging low over the western ridge, the pale scatter of the Loom above it, the one red star—Wrenna called it the Coal—that burned just off the Yoke's left shoulder like a spark caught in cloth. He had spent twenty years under these stars. He had fallen asleep counting them as a boy. He had never once seen them leave the sky.

They left the sky all at once.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Gone*—snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, as if a great hand had closed over the world and pinched out its lights. One moment the Loom was weaving itself above him; the next there was nothing where it had been, nothing but a black so total it seemed less like absence than like something *placed* there, a lid set down over the valley.

Mirabel's breath went out of him in a thin white stream.

The hum of the ward-stones climbed to a shriek.

He ran. He didn't decide to run; his body simply went, boots hammering the packed earth of the lane, past Wrenna's dark cottage, past the well, up the sheep track toward the border stones on the northern rise. Behind him a door opened and someone shouted his name—Tomas, probably, or old Bram—but the wind took the words apart before they reached him, and anyway he could not have stopped. Something was pulling at him from inside his own chest, something that had slept for twenty years and was now awake and *clawing*.

The stones came into view against the black sky, seven of them in their ancient arc, each taller than a man, each worn smooth by weather no one remembered. They had always glowed faintly at night—a deep ember-light, warm as a hearth seen through a window. His mother's color, though he hadn't known that until recently. Until Wrenna had begun telling him things.

Tonight they blazed white.

And tonight they were breaking.

The nearest stone split down its center with a crack like the sky snapping in half. Not crumbling—*shattering*, the way porcelain shatters when dropped on flagstone, all at once and everywhere, fragments bursting outward in a slow bright bloom. Then the second stone. Then the third. Mirabel felt each break somewhere behind his sternum, a physical grief, a tearing of something he hadn't known he was holding. He fell to his knees in the wet grass twenty feet from the arc and watched twenty years of safety come apart in silence and light.

Because it was silent. That was the terrible thing. Stones that size should have roared when they died, should have sent shards singing into the dark. Instead the pieces drifted, turning slowly, glowing white-hot and then fading to gray as they fell, settling into the grass around him like snow. Like ash. He picked one up without meaning to. It was cool in his palm by the time it touched skin, a sliver of gray nothing, and when he squeezed it, it crumbled to powder and blew away.

"No," he said. "No, no—"

The clawing thing in his chest tore free.

Later he would have no clean memory of what happened next—only sensations, arriving out of order, too large for the vessel of him. Light. Pressure. The taste of copper and rain. He was on his feet without standing, arms thrown wide, and the storm that had been circling the valley all evening turned and *came to him*, streaming down out of the black starless sky in ropes of wind, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was what had been taken from him. This was what the valley had been holding back. Not protecting him from—the thought arrived whole and unwelcome—not protecting him from anything. *Containing* him. And now the container was broken and everything his mother had dammed up was coming home through the ruin of her own spell, and it did not care that he was twenty years out of practice, that he had spent two decades being small on purpose, dousing chimney fires and quieting hail, using a king's weapon to do a tinker's work.

The lightning loved him. That was the horror of it. It came down the wind to his raised hands like dogs to a whistle, and he could not hold it and he could not refuse it, and when it broke over him he screamed—not in pain, exactly, though there was pain, his bones ringing like the ward-stones had rung—but in something closer to recognition. *Oh,* some buried part of him sang, even as the rest of him drowned. *Oh, there you are.*

The storm stood over the valley for the length of nine heartbeats. Lightning walked the ridgelines on legs of white fire. The rain came down sideways. Somewhere below, cattle were panicking, and a roof tile shattered, and a child was crying, and all of it happened very far away, at the bottom of the bright drowning place where Mirabel knelt with his fists in the mud and the sky pouring through him like he was a broken window.

Then, as suddenly as it had seized him, it let go.

The wind died mid-gust. The rain thinned to a drizzle, then to mist. The clouds tore open overhead—and showed him nothing. No stars. No moon. Only the lid, the vast placed darkness, pressing down.

Mirabel lay in the ruined grass, smoking faintly, laughing in a cracked and unsteady way that he did not recognize as laughter until it was already happening. Terror and joy had gotten tangled together somewhere in the flood and he could no longer tell which end of the feeling was which. His hands shook. He held them up against the empty sky and watched them tremble, and they were *his* hands, and they had just done something impossible, and he wanted to weep and he wanted to do it again.

"That," he rasped to the starless dark, "is going to be a problem."

That was when he saw the rider.

* * *

The horse came down the northern track at a dead gallop, or tried to. It was lathered and staggering, and the man on its back rode badly, hunched forward, one hand fisted in the mane. Mirabel scrambled up the rise toward the border arc, grass slick under his boots, and squinted through the mist.

Royal livery. Even at a distance, even in the dark, he knew it—the deep blue coat, the silver trim, the crest stitched on the breast: a crown above an open eye. He had seen it once, as a child, on a poster nailed to the market post in the low town, before Wrenna had torn it down and burned it and told him never to speak of it. Twenty years, and his heart still did what it did now: dropped, lurched, tried to hide behind his ribs.

The rider crossed where the ward-line had been.

Nothing stopped him. Nothing flared, nothing turned him aside, because there was nothing left to do the stopping. He rode straight through the gap in the shattered arc as though through an open gate, and Mirabel understood, watching him come, that the valley had just been unlocked for the first time in twenty years, and the first thing through the door was the crown.

The horse foundered at the top of the rise, ten feet from Mirabel, and went down hard. The man slid from the saddle and landed wrong and did not get up.

Mirabel approached the way one approaches a snake—one slow step, another, hands out and empty. Up close the livery was finer than the poster had suggested, silver thread catching what little light the mist allowed, and the man was young—younger than Mirabel—with a courier's satchel strapped across his chest and mud to his hips. His eyes were open. His chest was not moving.

There was no wound. That was the detail that lodged in Mirabel like a splinter. No blood, no arrow, no mark of any kind. The man simply looked *emptied*, the way a house looks when the family has gone—everything still in its place, nothing left living in it. His face was slack in a way faces are not supposed to be, all the expression drained out and leaving only the shape of a face behind.

Mirabel crouched beside him. "Hey," he said uselessly. "Hey."

The dead man's hand was clenched around a letter.

Not loosely, not incidentally—clenched, knuckles bloodless, fingers locked so tight around the sealed paper that Mirabel had to work them open one at a time, and they resisted even in death, and he murmured an apology to the corpse the whole while because it seemed important to apologize. The letter was heavy stock, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, stamped with the same crown-and-eye that rode the man's breast.

His name was stitched inside the collar. *Corwin.* Someone had taken the trouble to embroider it. Someone had expected him to come home.

"I'm sorry, Corwin," Mirabel said, and broke the seal.

The paper inside was covered in a tight, precise hand, the writing of someone who had never once written quickly. It was addressed not to Corwin but upward, through him, to whoever held authority over the finding it reported. Mirabel read it once. Then he sat down in the wet grass, because his legs declined to hold him, and read it again.

*To the Office of the Regent's Eye, Palace of Aldous.*

*Report of the Border Survey, Ember Valley, autumn equinox.*

*The wards have failed. Cause unknown; the stones broke without strike or siege, all at once, as if released. The survey party observed the phenomenon from the ridge at dusk.*

*Of greater urgency: the subject is located. After twenty years of search, the Okafor heir is confirmed within the valley. Description matches. The Regent's patience is ended. The heir is to be taken alive. The Eye comes north within the fortnight.*

*Do not read this letter. Do not remember this letter. Deliver it and forget your errand, per standard instruction.*

*Corwin has forgotten his errand. Corwin is riding home.*

*Praise to the Regent, who keeps us all.*

Mirabel read it a third time. The words did not change. The mist beaded on the page and blurred nothing, because the ink was good ink, palace ink, and it did not run.

*The Okafor heir is located.*

He looked at the dead courier. *Do not remember this letter. Deliver it and forget your errand.* And Corwin had delivered it—to the valley, to the ruins of the wards, to the one place the letter could go no further—and then he had forgotten, and ridden for home, and died at the border of a valley that no longer existed. Or perhaps he had died of the forgetting itself. Mirabel suspected he would never know which, and suspected, too, that the distinction mattered enormously, somewhere far away, in a palace where a crown sat on a head that ate memories the way his mother's spell had eaten storms.

*The Regent's patience is ended.*

He knew the name the way every child in the kingdom knew it, from festival songs and market gossip: the Hollow Regent, the king's shadow, the power behind the throne. In the valley he had heard almost nothing of it. The valley kept the world out, and Mirabel had let it. He had been *grateful*. He had built a life in the space the wards made and called the building of it freedom, and all the while—

All the while the wards had not been keeping the world away from him.

They had been keeping him away from the world. From the Eye. From whatever patient, institutional hunger had spent twenty years looking for a boy whose magic died at the border, and had finally, tonight, run out of patience on the exact night the borders ran out of strength.

His mother's voice came back to him unbidden—Wrenna's account of her, the woman who paid with her whole self—and for the first time he heard it correctly. She had not hidden him here. She had *spent herself* to hide him here, had bought twenty years of kettles and roofs and barley and smallness with everything she was, and tonight the bill had come due, and the purchase had expired, and her son sat in the ruins of her last spell holding a letter that ended his childhood in eleven precise lines of palace ink.

Mirabel laughed again, that cracked unfamiliar sound, and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

Below him, lights were kindling in the cottages. Voices carried up the slope—Bram shouting for him, Wrenna's window going yellow, someone ringing the well-bell that meant *come, all of you, come.* They had seen the stones die. They had seen the storm stand up and walk. They would want to know what it meant, and he would have to tell them, or some of it, and Wrenna—who had known, who had always known—would have to tell him the rest.

But first he sat a moment longer in the wet grass with the dead courier and the broken seal, and let himself feel both things at once, because they were both true and neither would wait its turn.

He was terrified. The Eye was coming north within the fortnight, and his magic was a wild unbroken thing that had nearly drowned him on its first night loose, and the sky over his whole life had gone dark as a closed eye, and a young man named Corwin lay dead at his feet for the crime of carrying paper.

And underneath the terror, shameless and singing, some buried son of a storm-caller was *exulting*. Because the cage was open. Because the lightning had come to his hands like a dog hearing its name after twenty years. Because whatever he had been before tonight—small, safe, deliberately dim—he was not that anymore, and could never be that again, and the thing in his blood that had waited two decades in the dark was awake now and *hungry for the sky*.

Mirabel folded the letter along its creases and put it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay cold as a ward-stone shard.

Then he stood, and took the dead man's satchel strap gently off his shoulder—"borrowing," he told the body, "I'm borrowing"—and turned down the slope toward the lights and the voices and the last night of the only life he had ever known.

Behind him, high above the shattered arc, the starless dark seemed to lean a little closer to the earth.

As if something, far away, had just turned its attention north.

And begun, patiently, to look.
