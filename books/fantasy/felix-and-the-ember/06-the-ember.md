## 6. The Ember

Wrenna did not come to the door. She came to the window.

Mirabel looked up from the table where he'd been mending the handle of a kettle with wire and patience, and there she was in the glass—small, bent, her white braid coming loose from its pin, holding a lantern that wasn't lit. She stood in the blue hour after the storm he had doused two days ago, and she did not knock. She only watched him through the pane the way she used to watch him as a boy, when she thought he couldn't see: with an expression he had never learned to name, because it was grief wearing a housekeeper's apron.

He opened the door before she could decide to leave.

"You've been avoiding me," he said.

"I've been letting you be angry," Wrenna said. "There's a difference. Anger needs a room of its own for a while, or it gets into everything." She lifted the unlit lantern slightly, as if presenting it. "Are you done being angry?"

"No."

"Good. Then you'll listen properly. Angry people listen properly. It's the comfortable ones who don't." She stepped past him into the cottage without waiting to be asked, which was her right and always had been; she had helped raise him in this room, and a house remembers its hands. She set the lantern on the table, sat down across from the mended kettle, and folded her knotted fingers together like a woman preparing to hold something down in high wind.

Mirabel closed the door. The fire in the hearth had burned down to a red thought. He fed it a split log and waited, because he knew by now that Wrenna spoke when she was ready and not one breath sooner, and rushing her was like rushing a river—you got wet and it went where it liked regardless.

"I lied to you," she said at last.

"You told me my mother was a stranger who passed through."

"I told you she was a stranger who passed through," Wrenna corrected gently. "I never said I was lying about anything else. A lie is a whole cloth, Miri. What I gave you was a patchwork. There's a difference, and the difference is what's kept me up at night for twenty years." She reached into the collar of her dress and drew out a cord—leather, old, darkened with wear—and on the cord hung a pendant of glass the color of a coal's heart.

The room changed.

It was not light, exactly. The ember-glass did not shine so much as *insist*, the way a name insists on being remembered. The shadows leaned toward it. The dying fire straightened its back. And Mirabel felt something in his chest turn over—not his heart, but something near it, some organ he hadn't known he owned until this moment, waking up and straining toward the little stone like a plant toward a window.

"What is that," he said. His voice came out wrong. Younger.

"This is the last of her," Wrenna said. "What she didn't spend."

* * *

She told it plainly, because Wrenna had never in her life dressed a hard thing in soft words—it was one of the reasons Mirabel loved her and one of the reasons he had spent three days not speaking to her.

"She came up the valley road in the rain," Wrenna said, "the autumn you were four. Not walking like a traveler walks. Walking like a woman who has decided exactly how far she can go and is spending every step getting there. I met her at the border stones because I was young then and foolish and I used to go touch them on the equinox to feel them hum, the way children put their hand on a beehive to prove they're brave. She was kneeling between two of them with her palms flat against the rock, and there was lightning standing in her hair like a crown that hated her.

"She said, 'I need a valley that forgets.' And I said, 'This one forgets everything but its grudges,' and she laughed—laughed, Miri, in the middle of whatever was chasing her—and said, 'Then we'll get along.'

"She paid me in stories. That was all she had. Stories of the capital, of the court, of a king who wept in her kitchen. I'll tell you those another night, or I won't, depending on how brave you turn out to be. Tonight is only about the stones.

"The wards aren't mine and they aren't the valley's. Old Wrenna's tale, the one I told you at the equinox—a woman who paid with her whole self—that was true, and it was also a disguise, because the truth was worse and better than a ghost story. The wards are hers. Your mother's. She called a storm down out of a sky that had no storm in it, and she poured it into the border stones, and she bound the valley shut against scrying, against finding, against the eyes of the crown. Nothing inside could be seen from outside. No watcher's glass, no king's magic, no—" Wrenna paused, and her thumb moved over the ember-glass without her seeming to notice she was doing it. "No *thing* that wears a crown and looks through it.

"But a working that size wants fuel. Storms want heat, and memory is the hottest thing there is. So she fed it herself. Not her life—her life she kept, barely. Her *past*. Every year of it. She walked those stones until she'd poured twenty-nine years into the ground, and when she stood up at the end she turned around and looked at me and said, 'Who are you? Why am I crying?'"

Mirabel found that his hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the cottage might lift off otherwise.

"She remembered forward," Wrenna said. "That's how she described it. She remembered *you*. Everything behind her was ash, but ahead of her there was a boy, and so she built a life backward from you, plank by plank, the way you build a bridge from the far side. She learned to bake again because you would need bread. She learned our names again because you would need neighbors. She chose to love you first and let everything else grow back around that, like grass around a stone." Wrenna's voice did not break. It simply got very level, the way water goes still before it goes over the edge. "And when the fever took her—you remember the fever winter, you were nine—she had one thing left she hadn't spent. One memory she'd kept out of the fire, saved up like a coin under a floorboard. She gave it to me and said, 'When he asks—and he will ask, he's my son, he'll worry a thing to death—give him this. But not before the stones break. If the stones break, the hiding is over, and he'll need to know why anyone bothered.'"

The fire popped. Outside, the valley lay quiet under its starless sky—the sky had stayed empty since that terrible night, and Mirabel had stopped looking up, because looking up felt like staring into a wound.

"And now they're broken," he said.

"Now they're broken," Wrenna agreed. "So." She held the cord out across the table. "Come and take your inheritance."

* * *

He didn't reach for it at once.

That surprised him. He had spent three days angry at Wrenna, three days pacing the borders of a life he now understood had been drawn for him by someone else, three days wanting answers with an appetite that scared him. And here were the answers, hanging off a leather cord, small enough to close his fist around—and his hand wouldn't move.

"It's not property," Wrenna said quietly, watching him not reach. "I know that look. You're thinking it's yours, blood and bone, your mother's last coin come home to her son. And it is, in the way a seed is yours. But listen to me, Mirabel Okafor, because I'm old and I get to say things bluntly: what she left you isn't a keepsake. Keepsakes sit on shelves. This is a *debt with a shape*. She kept one memory alive for twenty years so that it could be handed to someone at the right moment. Moments don't wait for comfort. Take it."

"Why do you keep saying debt?"

"Because I held it," Wrenna said, "and holding a thing like this changes what it is. For twenty years I've carried a dead woman's last thought in my pocket, and I'll tell you what it taught me: a preserved memory isn't the past. It's a *task*. It's her voice saying *do this*, frozen mid-word, waiting for ears." Her eyes were bright and fierce and old. "Take it, boy. And then forgive me or don't, but take it first."

He took it.

The cord was warm. That was the first impossible thing—that leather worn by two women should still hold heat after twenty years—and the second impossible thing arrived before he'd finished noticing the first. The moment the pendant touched his palm, the ember-glass flared, and the world went away.

* * *

Not darkness. Rain.

He was standing in rain—not feeling it on his skin but *being* it, being the falling of it, being the enormous slow breathing of a storm system rolling over mountains, and he understood suddenly, wholly, in the wordless way you understand your own heartbeat, what his mother had been. Not a woman who could call storms. A woman who was *fluent* in them. The lightning was language. The pressure drop was grammar. The thunder was not sound but *opinion*—vast, rolling, ancient opinion—and she could read it the way Wrenna read weather and Mirabel read the grain of wood, instantly, natively, without effort.

And underneath the fluency, warm as a hearthstone under a hand: her. Not her face—he never saw her face, the memory was too deep in her for faces—but her *weight*. The particular stubbornness of her. The way she held fear the way other people held tools, gripped firmly and put to work. A tiredness so total it had gone clean, the way pain goes clean when it stops being bearable and starts being simple.

Then the image.

One image, the single coin she had saved from the fire, and it unfolded in him like a letter opening:

A hall. High windows, gray with evening. A man standing in it who wore a crown, and the crown sat on him wrong—not crooked, but *hollow*, the way a shell sits on a shore—and the man was weeping. Not elegantly. Ugly, doubled-over weeping, the kind kings are never permitted, both fists pressed against his mouth to hold the noise in, his shoulders shaking under cloth worth more than the valley earned in a year. And his eyes above his fists were the worst part, because they weren't grieving eyes. They were *frightened* eyes. Eyes looking out from inside a house that was being eaten from within, watching the walls go thin.

His mother stood before him. He saw her only as a shape at the edge of the image, rain still dripping off her, a woman who had walked a long way to be spoken to like this.

And the king said—voice wrecked, muffled through his own fists, words falling out between his fingers like coins from a torn purse:

*"She eats from the edges inward. Do you understand? I woke up Tuesday and I could not remember my father's voice, and no one noticed, no one noticed, Mirabel's mother, no one in the entire palace noticed, because I covered it, because that is what I am *for*—"* His fists pressed harder. *"I felt her feed. God help me, I felt it, like a hook drawing line out of me. There will be less of me every year and more of it wearing my face, and when there is nothing left, it will sit on this throne and smile with my mouth and no one—no one will ever know there was ever anyone else in here to save."*

He lowered his fists. His face was ruined and royal at once.

*"You have a son."*

Not a question. His mother's shape went very still.

*"I have watched you carry him on your hip through these halls like a lantern. I have nothing left worth stealing soon—I am making sure of it, I am *emptying myself*, do you see, if there is nothing in me then there is nothing for it to eat, I have already given away my childhood, I gave my mother's lullaby to a fountain, I am becoming a fortress with nothing inside it—and if I succeed, if I hollow myself faster than it can drink, then the only thing left in this kingdom that it cannot bear to lose, the only treasure it hasn't found—"*

His voice failed. He knelt. The King of the realm knelt on wet stone in front of a storm-caller with rain in her hair, and pressed his forehead nearly to the ground.

*"Hide him. Hide him where even I cannot find him, because I cannot trust what I'll become to go looking. Hide him so well that someday, when this is over—if it is ever over—he can hate me for it safely, in peace, with stars overhead. Please. You are the last person in this palace who is not already eaten. Please."*

And his mother's voice, from outside the image, from everywhere, tired and clean and absolutely certain:

*"I don't hide things, Your Majesty. I weather them. But yes. I'll take him somewhere the storm can't be seen from shore."*

The image folded. The rain-language drained out of him. The hall, the king, the weeping—all of it collapsed back into a bead of glass the size of a plum stone, sitting warm in Mirabel's open hand.

* * *

He was on the floor. He didn't remember sitting down. Wrenna was beside him, one small hard hand gripping his wrist, saying his name in the tone she used for calling people back from faints and fevers, and the fire had been fed at some point, though he didn't know by whom, and the cottage was just a cottage again, small and safe and—no. Not safe. Never safe. Only *hidden*, and the hiding was over.

"Wrenna." His voice was gravel. "The king. In the memory. Was that—"

"Aldous," she said. "Before. When there was still a before."

"And the thing he was afraid of. The thing eating him." Mirabel looked down at the pendant. It had gone dim, ordinary, a pretty stone on a cord—and yet he could still feel the shape of the task inside it, the way you feel a footprint in snow after the foot has gone. "It's on the throne now. Isn't it. It's wearing him."

"That's what the rider's letter said," Wrenna said. "'The Regent's patience is ended.' Patience, Miri. Things that eat slowly call it patience." She squeezed his wrist. "Your mother heard that same hunger forty years ago and ran to the ends of the earth to put you beyond its sight. She spent her whole self on it. And now the stones are broken and the stars are gone and the letter found you anyway, and I am an old woman who lied to a child for twenty years because his mother asked me to, and I would do it again, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry."

Mirabel closed his hand around the ember.

It was warm. It had been warm for twenty years in Wrenna's pocket, waiting, and now it was warm in his, and he understood at last what Wrenna had meant about debts with shapes. This was not a locket. This was not grief made portable. This was his mother's final instruction, preserved mid-word: *hide him*—and the hiding had failed, and the instruction remained, and instructions outlive their circumstances and become something else.

Somewhere north, past the starless hills, a hollowed king sat on a throne wearing a face that had once wept for a stranger's child. And the last spell of the woman who had hidden that child was cooling in broken stones at the valley's edge, and the last memory of her was beating in his fist like a second heart.

"He asked her to hide me so I could hate him safely," Mirabel said slowly. "With stars overhead." He looked up at the ceiling, as if the sky were visible through it—as if the missing stars might be visible through roof-thatch and distance and forty years. "There are no stars, Wrenna. Whatever he planned, it's worse than he planned. And nobody's coming to fix it, because everyone who could remember the problem has been eaten."

Wrenna said nothing. She had the particular silence of a woman watching a decision assemble itself in someone else's face.

Mirabel stood. His legs held. The pendant swung from his fist, catching the firelight, and inside it—dim, patient, banked like a coal waiting for morning—something of his mother went on burning.

"Pack me bread," he said. "And tell me everything you remember about the road north."
