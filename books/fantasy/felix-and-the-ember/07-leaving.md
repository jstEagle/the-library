## 7. Leaving

The ember-glass pendant sat against Mirabel's sternum like a second heartbeat.

He had tried to leave three times that morning and failed each time without moving a single step. The first failure was practical: he'd packed his satchel with everything he owned and discovered he owned almost nothing worth carrying—a spare shirt, a whetstone, a coil of copper wire he'd been meaning to turn into a lightning rod for Wrenna's chimney, half a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in cloth. Twenty years of life in the valley, and it fit in a bag meant for a day's errand. He stood looking at it and felt something in him tilt, like a table with one short leg.

The second failure was Wrenna. She stood in her doorway with her arms folded, watching him fuss with the strap of the satchel, and said nothing at all, which from Wrenna was louder than any argument she could have shouted. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You'll want to say something," Mirabel told her. "So say it."

"I've said everything I'm going to say." Her voice was gravel over ice. "Twenty years ago your mother put you on my step and asked me for one thing, and I did it badly and I did it well, both at once, and now you're going to walk out of here because a dead man's letter told you to."

"The letter didn't tell me to." He cinched the strap. "It just made it impossible to stay."

"That's what telling is, boy. It doesn't have to use words."

* * *

The third failure was the valley itself.

He got as far as the orchard before he stopped, unbidden, and simply looked. It was late autumn, the season when the valley did its best work: the apple trees gone copper and gold, the terraced fields cut low and stubbled, smoke rising straight up from a dozen chimneys into air so still it seemed afraid to move. Somewhere downhill, Tam Broadbelt was arguing with his son about fence posts, their voices carrying in the clear cold the way voices do in small places where everyone knows everyone's arguments by heart. The mill turned. A dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Mirabel had spent twenty years learning this place the way you learn a song—without effort, without noticing, until you couldn't hear silence anymore without the melody filling it. Every stone on every wall had been placed or repaired or cursed by hands he knew. He had helped lay the western footbridge when he was eleven. He had buried old Hollis under the rowan tree. He knew which field flooded first in spring and which hearth smoked and which widow left her shutters open all winter because she liked the cold on her face while she slept.

And none of them would remember him correctly. That was the thing he kept circling and refusing to land on. Wrenna would grieve, but the others—the neighbors whose roofs he'd mended, the children he'd taught to whistle thunder—they would remember a quiet man who fixed things, and within a year even that would soften into nothing, the way a path softens back to grass when no one walks it. The valley forgot gently. It was built to.

"Then let it," he said aloud, to no one, and walked.

* * *

The border stones were still broken.

They stood along the eastern ridge in a line as old as anyone could remember—seven standing stones, each taller than a man, each humming on the equinox with a sound you felt in your teeth rather than heard. Now they were rubble. When the stars had gone out, they had shattered like dropped porcelain, and the pieces still lay where they'd fallen, fanned outward as if the stones had burst from the inside. Mirabel crouched among the shards and touched one, carefully, the way you'd touch a burned pot.

Nothing. No hum, no voice, no fragment of his mother. Whatever had lived in these stones had died the same night the sky went empty—and whatever had lived in them had been her. His mother's final spell. His mother's whole self, poured out into seven rocks so that her son could grow up unseen.

He picked up a shard the size of his palm. It was warm. Or he imagined it was warm. He slipped it into his pocket anyway, next to the cheese, because grief makes you superstitious and he was not going to pretend otherwise.

Beyond the stones, the land fell away eastward in long gray folds toward the lowlands, toward the river road, toward the kingdom he had never once set foot in. The sky above it was wrong. He had known it would be wrong—he'd seen the starless dark from the ridge every night since it happened—but knowing and seeing were different animals. From the valley the starless sky had been an absence, a hole. Out here, past the broken wards, it was a presence. It pressed down. There were clouds somewhere up there, he supposed, but no light came through them from anywhere; the whole dome of the world was the flat, sourceless gray of a closed eye, and the land beneath it seemed lit by memory rather than sun. Colors existed but reluctantly. The grass was gray-green. The distant hills were gray-brown. Even his own hands, held up in front of him, looked like hands remembered by someone else.

"So that's what it costs," he murmured. "Everything outside costs this."

Inside the valley, the sky had been blue yesterday. Blue, with clouds, with weather—with a sun that moved. He had never appreciated it. You don't appreciate water when you're a fish. Now he stood at the edge of the water, so to speak, one foot on the last stone of home, and understood that the entire world beyond had been living under this lidless gray for who knew how long, and most of them had stopped noticing that too.

He stepped across.

* * *

Nothing happened.

That was the strange part. He had braced for something—a sound, a shudder, the sensation of passing through a curtain. Instead there was only grass, then more grass, and the particular vertigo of discovering that a threshold you feared was made of the same air as everything else. He walked ten paces. Twenty. Fifty. He turned around and saw the seven ruined stones behind him, small already, and the valley beyond them folded into its hills like a sleeping animal, and he felt—

He felt like falling.

Not danger-falling. Something worse. The ground was solid under his boots and yet every step had the quality of a step taken off a ledge in a dream, that lurching moment when the dream remembers gravity. Because for twenty years the valley had held him in ways he'd never itemized: the wards had hidden him, yes, but also the routine, the roofs, the fences, the known faces, the storm-magic that answered his hands like a well-trained dog. All of it had been structure. And structures, he realized now, standing in gray grass with the wind coming off the lowlands smelling of ash and river mud, are invisible until they're gone. He hadn't been free in the valley and he wasn't free out here either; he was simply unsupported, a man walking on nothing, calling it forward motion.

His magic confirmed it. He reached, idly, the way you reach for a handrail—and found the air empty. Not dead, exactly. But far away. Inside the valley, storm-calling had been immediate, intimate, a language spoken directly into his skin. Here it was a letter sent to a stranger. He could feel weather happening miles off—a pressure system crawling down from the northern passes, fat with rain—and reaching toward it was like shouting across a canyon. Possible. Exhausting. Wrong.

"So that's why they exiled me here," he said. "Not to hide me. To mute me."

The thought arrived cold and stayed cold. His mother hadn't just chosen a hiding place. She'd chosen the one place in the world where his magic would be a whisper instead of a shout—where the crown's watchers, if they ever came looking for a storm-caller, would find a man who doused roof fires and calmed hail, nothing more. The valley hadn't been a home that happened to be safe. It had been a muzzle shaped like a home.

He kept walking. What else was there to do? Falling, after all, is just flying with the story removed.

* * *

He reached the first village at dusk, though dusk was a formality under that sky. It sat where two roads met—a cluster of maybe thirty houses around a well, a shrine, a tavern with a sign depicting a boar that might once have been painted red. Smoke rose from chimneys. Lamplight glowed yellow in windows, and the yellow looked almost violent against all that gray, like a shout in a library.

Mirabel was hungry, tired, and down to his last coin, so he did what travelers do: he went to the tavern.

The Boar's Rest was warm inside, low-ceilinged, half full of farmers drinking with the grim steadiness of people for whom drink is agriculture. Conversation hummed. A fire cracked. For one merciful moment it felt almost like the valley—like any small place anywhere—and Mirabel felt his shoulders drop half an inch.

Then he got to the bar, ordered bread and ale with his single coin, and made the mistake of being friendly.

"Fine evening," he said to the man beside him, a broad fellow with flour dusted up both forearms. "Cold out."

"Aye." The man drank. "Cold as it's been."

"How long has it been like this?" Mirabel nodded vaguely upward, toward the ceiling, toward the sky. "The gray. I've been—" He caught himself. "I've been traveling. From the mountains. We didn't get much news."

The miller—flour, so probably a miller—looked at him with mild, untroubled puzzlement. "Like what?"

"The sky. The—" Mirabel gestured again, helplessly. "No stars. No sun. This."

The miller considered the question the way you consider whether you left the gate open. "Been gray a while," he allowed. "Since... hm." He frowned faintly, a man reaching for a tool and finding the hook empty. "Since before harvest. Before last harvest." Another frown. "Or the one before. Huh." He laughed, short and embarrassed, and clapped Mirabel on the shoulder with a floured hand. "You know how it is. Winters run together. Ask my wife, she keeps the almanac. Or she did. Can't recall if we ever had one."

Something behind Mirabel's ribs went very quiet and very cold.

"It's been years," he said carefully. "The gray. Years?"

"Has it?" The miller sounded genuinely interested, as if Mirabel had told him his own hair color. "Could be. Time does that." He raised his tankard to the room at large. "Time does that!" he announced cheerfully, and several drinkers raised theirs back in agreement, and Mirabel understood that he was watching a whole village shrug.

He took his bread to a corner table and ate it slowly and watched them. The tavern keeper counted his till twice and got different numbers both times and didn't seem bothered. Two women argued amiably about the name of a third woman—one said Marta, one said Maren—and resolved it by agreeing the woman in question probably went by both. An old man by the fire told a long story about a wedding, and midway through the story he stopped, blinked, and started a different story about a funeral, and nobody remarked on it, because nobody remembered the first story had begun.

Last winter. The phrase from the plan he'd been carrying in his head since the letter—*people who don't remember last winter*—had been too generous. They didn't remember last spring either. Or the year before. Memory in this room was a candle burning down in a draft; it guttered, it shrank, and no one noticed the dark closing in because the dark closed evenly, everywhere at once, like the gray sky itself.

A tithe-collector had come through here. Mirabel was sure of it without knowing how he was sure. Sef Vane's name surfaced in his mind—*the archivist in the well*, a stranger he hadn't met yet, whom Wrenna had described in fragments: *if you must go east, find the one they threw down the well, the one who kept lists.* Lists. Someone had to keep lists, or the forgetting won by default.

At the corner table nearest the door sat a young woman, maybe nineteen, nursing a cup of cider she hadn't touched. She was staring at her own hands, turning them over, as though checking them for damage. As Mirabel watched, she mouthed something—a name, maybe—and lost it, and mouthed it again, slower, holding it the way you hold water in cupped palms.

He shouldn't. He knew he shouldn't. He was a stranger with no standing, no plan, and a face that would be forgotten by morning. But he crossed the room anyway, because twenty years of fixing neighbors' roofs builds a reflex deeper than sense.

"You're trying to keep something," he said quietly.

She looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, past crying. "My sister's birthday," she said. "I had it this morning. I *had* it." Her voice climbed. "It was the fourth of something. I know it was the fourth of something. And there was a cake, there was definitely a cake, she hated the icing, she always scraped the icing—" Her hands shook. "Why can't I—everyone else seems fine. Why am I the only one who notices?"

"Because you're fighting it," Mirabel said. "They're not fighting. Fighting hurts, so they stopped." He sat down across from her, uninvited, and pulled the shard of ward-stone from his pocket. It lay on the scarred wood between them, dull and ordinary. "Hold this. Don't ask why."

She held it. Nothing happened, visibly. But her breathing slowed, and after a minute she said, wonderingly, "Fourth of Embermonth. It's the fourth of Embermonth. She was born the fourth of Embermonth and she hates—" Tears came now, finally, uselessly, productively. "She hates the icing."

"Say it out loud. Ten times. Then write it on your arm. Then carve it somewhere they can't tax." He stood, because staying was a kindness he couldn't afford and she'd forget him by morning regardless. "There are others like you. Find them. People who notice. Count them. Keep lists."

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Nobody," said Mirabel Okafor, heir of the hidden bloodline, and meant it more than he wanted to. "Nobody from nowhere. But somebody has to be the one who notices."

* * *

He slept that night in the tavern's stable loft, on hay that smelled of other travelers, and lay awake under the gray-dark listening to the building settle. The ember pendant pulsed faintly against his chest—not warmth exactly, more like attention. Like something patient waiting for him to be ready.

He thought about the miller raising his tankard. *Time does that!* And the room agreeing. That was the Regent's kingdom, then: not chains, not fire, just a slow, comfortable erosion, a tyranny you had to be hurting to see. His mother had seen it. King Aldous had seen it—that was the whole point of scattering his own memories, Wrenna had said, or rather the ember had shown him: a king, weeping, asking her to hide her son. A king who had felt the feeding begin and chosen amputation over consumption.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or however long the gray road took, Mirabel would walk toward that king with an ember of his mother's last memory and no plan worth the name.

"Falling," he said to the rafters. "Just call it flying."

In the morning he bought nothing, learned the road east ran along the river toward the city of Corvale, and set out while the village still slept. At the edge of town he passed the shrine—a little stone alcove with a faded figure of some saint or other—and noticed, fresh-carved into the base, small and crooked, in a hand that had pressed hard enough to crack the chisel mark:

*EMBERMONTH 4. SHE HATES THE ICING.*

He touched it once, the way he'd touched the ward-stone shard, and walked on east, into the gray, toward the well and the archivist and the war he didn't yet know how to fight.
