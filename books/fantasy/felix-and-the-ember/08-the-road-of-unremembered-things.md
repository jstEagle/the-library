## 8. The Road of Unremembered Things

The valley's border was a line in the grass that no one could see and Mirabel could not stop feeling.

He had crossed it at dawn, walking backward for the first hundred paces so he could watch the place where he'd lived his whole life shrink into the morning haze—the crooked roofs, Wrenna's chimney smoke standing straight in the still air, the ward-stones' shattered stumps like broken teeth along the ridge. He kept waiting for something to happen to him. For the world to lurch, or his blood to catch fire, or some great bell to toll inside his chest announcing that Mirabel Okafor had finally left.

Nothing happened. The road just went on, gray under a gray sky, and his boots made the same stupid crunching sound they always made, and the storm-magic sat in him like swallowed lightning—there, enormous, and entirely without instruction.

By midday he had learned three things about the world outside the valley.

First: it was cold in a way the valley never was. Not the honest cold of altitude or season, but a thin, pervasive chill, like standing in the shadow of something vast. He found himself walking with his arms crossed over the ember pendant, as if it might warm him if he pressed it close enough. It didn't. It only pulsed faintly against his palm, patient as a sleeping animal.

Second: there were no stars. He had known this—he had seen the sky go black two nights ago, had felt the wards die in the same breath—but knowing and walking beneath it were different animals. The sky wasn't cloudy. Clouds would have been better. It was simply *empty*, a ceiling of nothing stretched from horizon to horizon, and twice he caught himself stumbling because he'd looked up expecting to navigate by starlight out of some habit he didn't know he had.

Third: people here forgot things. Not everything. Not all at once. But forget, steadily, the way a roof forgets its shingles—one at a time, in the night, until someone looks up one day and wonders when the holes got there.

He learned the third thing in the first village.

* * *

It didn't have a name on any signpost, or if it did, the sign had been taken down. It was a straggle of houses around a well and a shrine with an empty alcove where a statue used to stand—Mirabel could tell by the clean rectangle of unweathered stone behind the pedestal, the way a picture leaves a brighter square on a wall after years of hanging. Someone had removed whatever stood there recently enough that the wall hadn't finished forgetting it either.

The villagers were polite. That was the worst part. A man mending a net nodded to him. Children stared and then didn't, their attention sliding off him like rain off oilcloth. An old woman sweeping her step asked if he was hungry, and when he said yes she brought him bread and hard cheese and ate nothing herself, watching him with an expression he couldn't read until he realized what was wrong with it: there was nothing behind it to read. She was kind the way a chair is comfortable—without any memory of why.

"Where is everyone going?" he asked, nodding at a knot of people moving down the lane toward the village edge, carrying bundles.

"The tithe," she said, as though he were simple. "Collection day. You'll want to be indoors, love, unless you've got something they want." She smiled. "Do you have anything they want?"

"I don't know," Mirabel said honestly.

"Then you're lucky." She said it without bitterness, without irony, without any awareness that luck should not consist of having nothing worth taking. She went back to her sweeping. Three strokes later she stopped, frowned at the ground as if she'd lost her place in a book, and began again.

Mirabel followed the crowd instead of hiding from it. He told himself it was caution—knowing where the danger was, learning the shape of it—but that was only half true. The other half was that the woman's smile had frightened him more than the shattered ward-stones had, and he needed to see the thing that made smiles like that.

They gathered in a field beyond the last house, maybe sixty people, standing in a loose line with the terrible orderliness of cattle. At the field's far end stood a wagon. It was black lacquer and brass, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, drawn by two horses whose eyes were covered with embroidered hoods. Beside the wagon stood a single figure in a gray coat with silver buttons, holding a ledger.

That was all. One collector for sixty people. No soldiers. No chains.

The collector didn't speak loudly, but every word carried across the field as if the air itself were eager to deliver them. "Good afternoon. The kingdom thanks you for your continued peace. This quarter's tithe is one memory per household, freely given, of moderate weight. As ever, the crown does not take what is not offered."

Freely given. Mirabel watched a farmer step forward, hat in hand, and offer up—of his own accord, bowing slightly—the memory of his wedding day. The collector opened a small glass vial, touched it to the man's temple, and the man shuddered once, like a dog shaking off water, and stepped back with a pleasant, vague smile. His wife took his arm. She was crying, but she seemed not to know why, and neither did he, and they walked back to the line together comforting each other over a grief that had been amputated so cleanly neither of them could find the wound.

One by one. A girl gave up her grandmother's voice. A boy gave up the smell of his mother's kitchen. A fisherman gave up the taste of the sea, and afterward stood looking at his own nets with mild distaste, as if they belonged to a stranger.

Nobody wept except the ones who couldn't help it. Nobody ran. And Mirabel understood, standing at the edge of the field with his heart hammering against the ember pendant, that this was what the Regent had built—not terror, but *tidiness*. A kingdom where the tax was painless because the pain left with the memory. Where the people loved their king because they had forgotten anything else to feel about him. Sixty people in a field, selling themselves by the spoonful, and calling it peace.

His hands were shaking. Under his skin, the storm-magic stirred, tasting the air, wanting out. He thought of the rider in royal livery dead at the valley border, the letter crushed in his fist, and he pulled his hood lower and walked away before the lightning in his blood could answer the lightning in his chest.

He was half a mile gone before he realized he was crying, and another mile before he understood why: the old woman at the shrine had asked if he had anything they wanted, and he had said *I don't know*, and it had been true. Somewhere in his head was a list of things he owned—a mother's face, a valley's name, twenty years of small deliberate days—and he had no way of knowing which of them the collectors would have priced.

* * *

He walked for two days.

The road wound through country that should have been beautiful—rolling hills, stone walls, orchards heavy with late fruit—and was instead beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: composed, correct, and empty of anyone home. Villages passed like beads on a string. In each one, the same signs, once he knew how to look: the bright rectangle on the shrine wall; the innkeeper who greeted him warmly twice in the same hour; the children playing a game whose rules changed every round because none of them could remember how it had started. In each one, the tithe-wagon's wheel ruts cut fresh and deep in the road mud, going out full and coming back—Mirabel checked once, crouching in the rain-slick track—going back light.

On the evening of the second day he came to a larger town built along a river, and here he saw the other side of the trade.

There was a market near the bridge, and at the market's edge, laid out on a cloth like a peddler's wares, a man sold memories. Little vials of them, glinting amber and green and one impossible shade of violet, each labeled in a careful hand: *First snowfall, 812.* *Your father laughing.* *The taste of plums in summer.* People crowded around the cloth with the desperate politeness of the starving, holding vials to their temples, gasping, weeping, buying back fragments of themselves at prices that made Mirabel's stomach turn—a coat, a wedding ring, a lifetime of labor promised to the crown's granaries.

"That one's forged," said a voice beside him.

Mirabel turned. A boy of perhaps ten, sharp-faced, missing a tooth, jerking his chin at a vial labeled *The Sea, from a boat.*

"How can you tell?"

"It's too blue." The boy shrugged. "Real sea-memories are mostly gray. Everyone knows that." Then he squinted up at Mirabel, and his face did something complicated—hunger and fear fighting for the same ground. "You're not from here. Here, everybody's got the look. Like a room after the furniture's been moved out." He paused. "You've got the opposite look. Too much furniture."

"Is that bad?"

"Dunno. Never seen it before." The boy glanced over his shoulder, quick and practiced, then leaned closer. "You want advice? Don't sleep at the Drowned Lamb. Collectors lodge there. They dream loud, and the dreams leak, and if you sleep next door to a leaking dream you wake up missing something small. A color. A name. My sister woke up missing Tuesdays." He said it flatly, a fact among facts, and Mirabel felt the horror of it settle into his bones: a child explaining memory-theft the way valley children explained weather.

"What happened to your sister?"

"She's fine. Hates Tuesdays now, though. Thinks they're unlucky." The boy almost smiled. "She doesn't remember why she thinks that. That's the trick of it, see? They don't even leave you the hole. They fill it in smooth."

He melted into the market crowd before Mirabel could ask his name, and Mirabel stood a long moment among the stalls, listening to the river move under the bridge, thinking about holes filled in smooth. About a valley that had hummed at its borders for twenty years while he repaired roofs and doused storms and called his cage a home. Wrenna had lied to him with love. The Regent lied to a whole kingdom with ledgers. He was beginning to suspect there was very little difference between the two arts, except in who paid.

* * *

The woman was waiting for him at the edge of town the next morning, and he knew before she spoke that she had been waiting a long time.

She stood where the road narrowed between two hedgerows, a small figure in a brown cloak, and when she saw him coming she didn't wave or call out. She simply stepped into his path and held up a loaf of bread, wrapped in linen, still warm.

"You're the one with too much furniture," she said.

The boy's words. Mirabel stopped. Up close she was perhaps forty, with the fine, exhausted bones of someone who had been pretty once and had stopped keeping track. Her eyes were the problem. They were wet without being sad, fixed on him with a ferocity that had nowhere left to live.

"I don't—" he began.

"They say you came out of the valley." Her voice was low, urgent, pitched under the wind. "The Ember Valley. They say nobody comes out of there. They say the wards—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together. "Doesn't matter what they say. What matters is you're new. New people still have everything. Do you understand me? Everything."

"Madam—"

"My daughter." The word came out of her like a tooth being pulled. "Her name is Liss. She has—she *had*—a gap between her front teeth, and she laughed with her whole body, tipped right back like she was showing the sky to the whole street, and she had a scar on her left eyebrow from falling off the woodshed when she was six, and I—" Her breath hitched. "I gave her up. Quarter-tithe, four years ago. One memory, they said. Moderate weight. I thought—I thought I'd give the day she was born, because surely I could keep the rest, surely the rest of her would be enough. But the rest of her was *made of* that day. Everything else leaned on it. Take out the keystone and—" She made a shape in the air with her hands, a shape of collapse.

Mirabel said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be cruel.

"I have her things," the woman whispered. "Her shoes. A drawing she made. I can tell you facts all day—her birthday, her height, the scar, the teeth. Facts are cheap. Facts are *nothing*. But I can't hold her face anymore." She lifted the bread toward him like an offering at an altar. "You're a stranger. Strangers carry things. I'm asking you to hold my daughter's face for me. Just—carry it. Walk with it. So that somewhere in the world, someone is walking around with Liss's face in their head, and she isn't entirely gone."

And Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what she was actually asking. Not for him to remember a face he'd never seen. She was asking him to be a vault. To be, for one small family, what the loyalists had been for the king: a hidden vessel, carrying what the Regent would otherwise consume entire.

"I can't take a memory I never had," he said gently. "I can't hold what was never given to me."

"I know." Her hands shook around the loaf. "But you can listen. Listening is almost the same. Please. Let me spend an hour telling you about my daughter, and take the bread, and walk, and let her exist in one more head than she exists in now. That's all. That's all I'm asking. It isn't much. It's everything I have."

The wind moved through the hedgerow. Somewhere behind them, in the town, a bell rang the hour, and Mirabel thought of the collector's ledger, of vials labeled in a careful hand, of a kingdom filing its children away like receipts.

He took the bread.

They sat on a fallen tree at the roadside, and the woman talked for a long time. She talked about Liss's laugh, and demonstrated it, tipping her own head back, and for a moment—only a moment—her face was young. She talked about the woodshed fall, and the fever when Liss was nine, and the way the girl whistled through the gap in her teeth so that every tune came out with a bird in it. She talked until her voice wore smooth, and then she talked some more, and Mirabel listened with his whole self, holding each detail the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing most of it would seep away and refusing to let that stop him from trying.

When she finished, the sun had climbed high, and she looked lighter. Not healed. Lighter, the way a pack is lighter after you've set it down to rest, knowing you must pick it up again.

"Thank you," she said. "Now there are two of us."

"There are two of us," Mirabel agreed, and meant it as a vow.

She stood, brushed off her skirt, and became practical with the swiftness of someone who had learned to survive by not lingering. "The road forks ahead. Left goes to the salt flats and the drowned cathedral country—pilgrims used to go that way, before. Right goes to the capital." She hesitated, and something flickered across her face, some instinct reaching up through four years of smoothed-over holes. "If you're going anywhere, stranger—and you have the look of a man going somewhere dangerous—go carefully. The collectors are only the fingers. The hand is in the palace, and lately the hand has been *reaching*." She pulled her cloak tight. "There are men asking after travelers from the valley. Gray coats, silver buttons. Asking politely. They're always polite."

Mirabel's blood went quiet and cold.

"When?" he said. "How many?"

"Three days ago. Heading east." She met his eyes, and in them he saw the last uncollected thing she owned: fear, sharp and clear and hers. "Whatever you're carrying, stranger—whatever they sent you out of that valley to do—walk faster."

She left him the loaf and the warning and went back through the hedgerow toward a town that would forget her errand by nightfall, and Mirabel stood alone at the fork in the road with warm bread in his hands and the ember pulsing against his chest like a second heartbeat.

Left: the salt flats, the drowned cathedral, the vault Sef Vane had died cataloguing the way to—if the archivist in the well was real, if Wrenna's dying scraps of rumor were true, if any of it waited for him.

Right: the capital, where gray coats with silver buttons were already asking after travelers from the valley. Already hunting the last bloodline the Regent could not digest.

Mirabel looked down the left-hand road, where the land fell away toward a glitter of distant white, and felt the storm-magic turn over in him like a sleeper hearing its name.

He took the left fork, and walked faster.
