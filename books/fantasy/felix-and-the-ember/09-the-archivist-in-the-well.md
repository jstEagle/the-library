## 9. The Archivist in the Well

The village of Hollowmere had no name worth keeping. That was the first thing Mirabel noticed about it—the signpost at the crossroads had been scraped bare, the paint of its letters flaked away or deliberately sanded off, so that travelers arrived without knowing where they'd come to and left without being able to say they'd been anywhere at all. The second thing he noticed was the well.

It sat in the center of the square like a wound that had been dressed too neatly. A stone lip, a wooden roof, a rope and bucket all in good repair—too good, for a village where half the houses stood empty and the other half kept their shutters closed against a gray noon sky. Someone had tended this well carefully. Someone wanted it usable.

Mirabel had been three days out of the valley, and his body still hadn't decided how to feel about the world beyond the border. His storm-magic sat under his skin like water behind a dam—not gone, as it had been for twenty years, but sluggish, thick, responding to him the way a half-asleep animal responds to its name. He could feel rain coming hours before it came. He could feel the pressure dropping over the eastern hills like a hand pressing down on the world. But when he reached for the lightning the way he'd once doused storms with his bare hands, it came slow, and grudging, and small.

He was learning to live inside a body that had been given back to him broken in.

* * *

Sef Vane had been in the well for two days.

She had counted them by drips. The water rose and fell with some rhythm she hadn't bothered to work out—a spring beneath, probably, breathing—and between the swells there were long silences punctuated by the tick of water finding stone. Two days, give or take whatever sleep she'd stolen sitting upright in six inches of freezing water with her back against the wet wall and her arms wrapped around her satchel like a child around a doll.

They had taken her boots. That was the part that offended her most, later, when she had leisure to be offended. Not the beating—she'd been beaten before, in the archive's back rooms, in the early years when the Regent's people still pretended to ask questions rather than simply remove the people who asked them. Not the fall; the fall had been quick, and the water had broken most of it. The boots. They had taken her boots because one of the tithe-collectors had liked them, and now her feet were two pale prunes and every stone in the wall felt like a knife, and somewhere above her a man was walking around in her boots thinking himself improved.

She had shouted for the first day. She wasn't proud of it. She had shouted until her voice cracked into something that didn't carry, and then she had done the arithmetic: a village like this, hollowed by the tithe, would not pull a stranger from a well out of kindness. Kindness required memory—required knowing what it was to be helped, what it was to owe. The Regent had eaten Hollowmere's kindness along with everything else. The villagers who had watched her dragged here had watched with the bright, vacant eyes of people watching weather.

So she stopped shouting and started counting drips, and cataloguing.

That was the thing about Sef Vane that her enemies had never understood, and the reason they should have killed her instead of dropping her down a hole. You could break her body. You could not stop her mind from filing. She lay in the dark and catalogued: the well's construction, pre-Regent, likely two centuries old, mortar style consistent with the old capital's public works. The water table, high, suggesting an aquifer feeding the valley eastward—the valley eastward, which meant the Ember Valley, which meant the Okafor heir was close, if the letter's intelligence held. Her own injuries: two cracked ribs, left wrist sprained not broken, scalp laceration already clotting. Her assets: one satchel, waterproofed, containing three vials of forged memory, a cipher key sewn into the strap, and forty pages of the only complete catalogue of the Regent's thefts in existence, written in her own blood-and-ink shorthand across the backs of tax ledgers no one had thought to burn.

Her liabilities: no boots.

On the second night—or what she judged was the second night, though the sky overhead was a lid of cloudless gray with nothing in it to mark the hours—she heard footsteps on the stone lip.

She did not shout. She pressed herself flat against the wall below the rope's shadow and waited, because in the starless kingdom, footsteps meant collectors, and collectors meant the well got a second body in it.

"Hello?" said a voice. A young man's voice, uncertain, carrying the particular careful politeness of someone who has spent his life trying not to be noticed. "Is someone down there? I saw—there are boot prints. Someone's boots, going away from here, and the bucket rope is frayed like it was used hard, and—" A pause. She could hear him thinking. It was an odd sound, thought made audible, like wind through a house's empty rooms. "I'm going to lower the bucket. If you're hurt, hold on to it."

Sef considered her options, which numbered exactly one.

The bucket came down bumping against the stones, and she hauled herself into it with arms that shook, and the rope went taut, and for a long terrible moment nothing happened except the creak of wood, and then the rope began to rise in slow, straining jerks—someone pulling her weight hand over hand, someone strong but inexperienced, someone who paused twice to shift his grip and once swore softly under his breath in a way that suggested swearing was not native to him.

Her head cleared the lip of the well and she saw him: a young man, mid-twenties perhaps, brown skin gone gray with road dust, dark hair cut short and practical, wearing a traveler's coat that fit him badly and an expression like a man standing at the edge of a height. Behind him the gray sky went on forever. Above the gray sky, where stars should have been, nothing.

He looked at her the way no one had looked at her in ten years. He looked at her like she was real.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"I'm Sef Vane," she said, because names were currency and she was nearly broke. "And you're either the stupidest man in the kingdom or the one I've been walking toward for three hundred miles. Help me out of this hole and I'll tell you which."

* * *

His name was Mirabel Okafor, and he did not know what to do with her.

He'd built a fire in the ruin of a barn at the village edge—Hollowmere's outskirts were all ruins, houses with their doors open onto emptiness, furniture still arranged around hearths as if the families had stepped out to fetch water and been unwritten—and she sat wrapped in his blanket with her feet steaming near the flames, drinking broth he'd made from the last of his travel ration, and she talked the way a landslide talks.

"The Regent doesn't keep what it steals," she said. "That's the mistake everyone makes, thinking it's a dragon on a hoard. It eats. Memory is food. But it can't digest everything—it chokes on certain kinds. Deep-structured memory, memory bound to place, memory bound to love. So for forty years it's been hiding the pieces it can't swallow, sealing them away where its own hunger can't reach them, and the locations exist in exactly three documents, two of which I wrote and one of which is burned." She drank the broth like it was an enemy. "You're the Okafor heir."

"I'm a roofer," Mirabel said.

"You're the son of Adaeze Okafor, storm-caller, who died buying you twenty years of darkness, and three days ago the wards fell and every star in the sky went out with them, and you walked out of the valley because you had nowhere else to go and no idea why your hands itch when it rains." She lowered the bowl. Her eyes were gray and hooded and absolutely without mercy. "Am I wrong?"

He was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the ruined village a shutter banged in a wind he couldn't yet feel coming, and his skin prickled with the promise of it.

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I was the Royal Archivist," Sef said, "before I was a body in a well. Because I have spent eleven years cataloguing what that thing in the crown has taken, one theft at a time, and eight months ago a loyalist courier reached me with word that the last Okafor was alive in the Ember Valley, and that when the wards fell, the race would be on. I came to find you." She gestured at the well with the bowl. "The village found me first. The Regent's collectors have a description of me in every garrison from here to the capital. Thrown down the well as an example, they said. To whom, I'm not sure. There's hardly anyone left in this village to make an example to." Something moved behind her eyes then, brief and quickly buried—grief, maybe, or rage, or the place where those two things had worn smooth into each other. "They took my boots."

"I'll get your boots back," Mirabel said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded absurd, a roofer promising to fight tithe-collectors over footwear.

But Sef looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—the first crack in the armor, hairline, quickly mortared over. "No," she said. "You won't. You'll get me new boots, because you're going to walk into the next town and buy them with whatever money you have, and you're going to do it because you need me, and I want you to understand that transaction clearly from the start. I'm not your friend. I'm not your guide. I am the only person alive who knows where King Aldous's mind is buried in pieces, and the thirteenth moon turns in—" she counted silently, lips moving, "—eighty-one days. After that, whatever is left of Aldous dissolves, the Regent becomes permanent, and every person in this kingdom forgets there was ever anything to mourn. Including you. Especially you. You're the last bloodline it can't digest, Okafor. When it finishes the king, it will come for you, and it will have eighty years of practice."

The wind arrived then, moving through the ruined village, and Mirabel felt it the way he'd never felt weather outside the valley—as a thing with texture, with intention almost, pressure gathering in the east where the clouds were beginning for the first time to stack themselves into something with a temper. His hands ached. He looked down at them, at the faint blue flicker dancing between his fingers like static off wool.

Sef watched the flicker too. She didn't flinch. That surprised him. Everyone else he'd met since the border had flinched at something.

"Storm-caller," she said quietly. "I've read the accounts. They called your mother's line the crown's oldest weapon, back when crowns were honest. The Regent fears you. Do you understand that? It hid you behind wards for twenty years not out of mercy but because you are the one thing in this kingdom it cannot eat, and things that cannot be eaten must be destroyed, and it hasn't found a way yet." She set down the bowl. "So. Here is my offer, and I'll only make it once. You get me out of collector territory, you follow my route, and I take you to the places where the king's memories are hidden. In exchange, when we reach the capital—if we reach the capital—you put that lightning of yours between me and whatever comes. Not friendship. Contract."

Mirabel turned his hands over. The blue light guttered and steadied. Outside, the first drops began to hit the barn's broken roof, tapping through the gaps, and he felt each one land somewhere under his skin, felt the whole storm hanging over the hills like a held breath, and understood suddenly that he would never again be a man who merely watched weather.

"A contract," he said.

"Witnessed by fire and bad broth," Sef said. "The most binding kind."

* * *

Later, when she slept—curled by the fire with her satchel under her head and her bandaged feet twitching through dreams she would deny having—Mirabel sat up and watched the rain and tried to count what he had lost and gained in four days.

Lost: the valley. Wrenna's kitchen. The smell of the forge smoke at dusk. Twenty years of being small and safe and no one.

Gained: a woman made of knives and catalogues, sleeping in his blanket. A deadline. Eighty-one days.

He thought about the ember pendant against his chest, warm even now, holding his mother's single preserved image—a king, weeping, asking her to hide her son. He had thought, leaving the valley, that he was walking toward answers. Sitting in a ruined barn with a stranger who knew more about his life than he did, he understood it was worse than that. He was walking toward questions he might not survive asking.

In the east, past the hills, lightning stitched the horizon—silent, distant, patient. His fingers answered it without his permission, blue light rising off his knuckles like heat shimmer.

Sef opened one eye. "Save it," she murmured. "You'll need every spark."

"For what?"

"For the salt cathedral," she said, and closed her eye again. "Ask me in the morning."

He asked her in the morning. What she told him made him glad, for the first time since the border, that the storm had followed him out of the valley—and afraid, for the first time, of what it might cost to use it.
