## 10. The Hollow Regent

Sef Vane had been thrown down a well for writing the truth, so she supposed she could be forgiven for keeping a second copy.

She wrote it by firelight in the ruined mill where Mirabel had carried her after hauling her out of the water—her ribs wrapped in linen torn from his own shirt, her hands still shaking too badly to hold a pen steady. The mill had been abandoned long enough that the roof had opinions about weather, but it was dry enough, and hidden enough, and far enough from any road that the tithe-collectors' lanterns passed it by like a stone they'd learned not to look at.

The chronicle itself was small. A slim book bound in gray leather, unmarked on the spine, no title anywhere on its face. That had been deliberate. In the royal archive of Corvenhall, Sef had learned early that the most dangerous books were never the ones that looked dangerous. A book that announced itself got burned. A book that looked like an inventory of candle-wax requisitions survived forty years, because no one wanted to read about candle wax.

This one had begun as exactly that: an inventory of candle-wax requisitions, in Sef's neat archivist's hand, page after page of tallow counts and wick lengths and delivery schedules for the palace's west wing. And then, beginning on the eleventh page, in the same hand, in the same ink, threaded between the entries like a smuggler's cargo sewn into the lining of a coat—

*Year 41 of Aldous's reign. The king did not attend the autumn assizes. Third such absence this year. His secretaries read his addresses aloud. Their voices were very good.*

*The wax requisitions for the west wing have doubled.*

*The king's private secretary, Lord Emmerich, has stopped signing documents in the king's name and begun signing them in his own. No one has remarked upon this. I have checked. I asked three clerks, casually, over wine. None of them found it strange. One of them could not remember who the king was.*

That last line Sef had written with a hand that would not stop trembling, and even now, reading it back by the mill-fire, she felt the cold of it climb her arms again. She had spent eleven years in the archive learning to trust documents more than people, because documents at least admitted when they contradicted themselves. People lied beautifully. Documents lied only through omission, and omission could be found, if you dug deep enough, if you cross-referenced enough ledgers, if you were patient as mold growing through a wall.

She had been patient. She had grown through the wall. And what she had found on the other side of it was the reason a man in palace livery had held her over the well-mouth by the hair and said, almost gently, *the archive is closed, mistress Vane, it has been closed for years, why do you keep opening it* — before letting go.

* * *

"Read it to me," Mirabel said.

He sat across the fire with his back against the mill wall, the dead rider's letter folded into his breast pocket like something he couldn't decide whether to keep or bury. He had barely spoken since they'd left the well. Sef understood. She had seen the letter. *The Okafor heir is located; the Regent's patience is ended.* There are sentences that change the shape of the air around them, and after that you breathe differently whether you want to or not.

"You should rest," she said. "You pulled me out of a well. Your hands are—"

"I'll rest when someone tells me what's wearing the king's crown." His voice was level, but she heard the storm under it—not the magic, though she'd already guessed there was magic in him, something that made the air taste like iron before rain. Just grief, and fear, and the particular anger of a man who had learned his whole life had been arranged without his consent. "You know something. You've known it longer than I've been alive, maybe. So read."

Sef looked at him across the fire. Twenty years old, or near it. Broad-shouldered from farm work, careful-handed from roof work, with the wary stillness of someone who had spent two decades being deliberately unremarkable. He didn't look like the last heir of a bloodline the crown couldn't digest. He looked like a young man with tired eyes sitting in a ruined building, asking her to tell him the truth because no one ever had.

She opened the gray book to the eleventh page.

"The candle wax first," she said. "Because that's how it started. Not with armies. Not with monsters. With requisition forms." She cleared her throat, and began.

* * *

The thing that ruled Corvenhall had entered the palace forty years ago, in the reign's ninth year, and no record of its arrival existed—which was itself a record, once you knew how to read absence.

"It came in through the court," Sef said. "Not through a gate. Through a door that the court opened for it. I'll come back to that—it matters, and I hate it, and I need to say the parts I understand before the parts I don't."

Mirabel nodded once. Waiting. Patient the way valley people were patient, she thought—the patience of men who fixed roofs and waited out storms rather than fighting them.

"The earliest anomaly is small. Year nine, winter. The king's food-taster, a woman named Brey, requests reassignment. Her stated reason is a fear of poisoning. Her unstated reason—" Sef turned a page. "Her unstated reason appears nowhere, because she was reassigned to a coastal garrison and died within the year of a fever, and her personal correspondence was destroyed in a records purge that also destroyed four years of kitchen accounts. But her request came eleven days after the king began sleeping badly. We know he slept badly because his physician's journal survives—buried in a mislabeled crate of veterinary records, which is the only reason it survived. Physicians write everything down. It's their one virtue."

"And the sleeplessness?" Mirabel asked.

"That's the question, isn't it." Sef fed a stick to the fire. "Here is what I believe, and I believed it for six years while telling no one, and the telling-no-one is how I ended up in a well, so I'm done with it. I believe something entered the king's household in year nine. Something small. Something that lived the way a tapeworm lives—not violently, not dramatically. Patiently. It attached itself to the king's mind, and it began to eat."

"Eat what?"

"Memories." She said it plainly, because plainness was all the armor she had left. "It ate them one at a time. Slowly. Over years. Do you understand what that means? Not a theft anyone could point to. The king forgot a servant's name, and everyone smiled and said kings forget things. He forgot a treaty negotiation, and his secretaries helpfully summarized it for him—and the summary became the memory, and the summary was written by men who served the crown, and so the past quietly became whatever the present needed it to be. He forgot his mother's face. He forgot the smell of the orchards at Highsummer. He forgot, year by year, decade by decade, every particular thing that made Aldous into Aldous—and each forgetting was so small, so reasonable, so deniable, that no alarm was ever raised. The parasite didn't hide its feeding. It made the feeding look like ordinary time passing."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the dead fields with a sound like pages turning.

"How do you know it wasn't just—" Mirabel hesitated. "Old age? Grief? Kings go strange."

"Because of the wax," Sef said, and almost smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "The west wing's candle consumption doubled between years nine and fourteen. Doubled again by twenty. The king's wing burned candles day and night, every night, in rooms no one entered. Why? Because the thing wearing him could not abide the dark. Or—" and here her voice dropped, because this was the part she had reconstructed at three in the morning, alone, with the certainty of a woman watching a trap assemble itself out of ledger lines, "—because it was doing its deepest work at night, in the dark, where no witness could see the king's face while it happened. Light or dark. I don't know which. But something in that wing needed forty years of uninterrupted night-work, and it ordered tallow by the hundredweight to get it."

* * *

She turned pages slowly, giving him the chronology the way she'd built it: not as a story, because stories invited belief, but as a wall of evidence, brick by brick.

Year twelve: the queen, Lysandra, stops appearing at court functions. Officially, ill health. Unofficially—her letters to her sister survive, thirty-one of them, seized in another purge but misfiled under a dead duke's estate accounts, and Sef had read every one. The last was dated midwinter of year thirteen. *He looked at me today and asked who I was. Not cruelly. Politely. As one asks a stranger's name at a wedding. I laughed, thinking it a joke, and he waited for my answer, and his eyes were so patient, and I stopped laughing.* After that, nothing. The queen's apartments were closed. She died in year nineteen; the cause listed was wasting sickness. Sef believed the wasting had a shape, and the shape was a mouth.

Year sixteen: the king's oldest friend, General Cavendish, is retired with honors and sent to a country estate. His farewell speech at court is remembered by witnesses as moving. What none of the witnesses remember—what Sef found only in the general's own diary, smuggled out by his daughter—is that the speech was written for him by the king's secretaries, and that Cavendish had requested, in writing, one final private audience with Aldous, and been refused, and had wept in the antechamber, and that his diary's final entry reads: *That is not entirely my friend in there. I cannot say what is missing. I can only say that when he touched my shoulder I felt thanked by something that was performing gratitude.* The general died in year twenty-four. Comfortable. Ignored. Dismissed in every history as a sentimental old man.

"Do you see the pattern?" Sef said. "Everyone who noticed was removed—but gently. Honors. Estates. Reassignments. Deaths by fever. Nothing that would make a story. Nothing anyone could point at and cry tyranny. The court was not conquered. It was *tidied*. One inconvenient perception at a time, until the only people left close to the throne were people who saw nothing wrong, because seeing nothing wrong was the qualification for staying close to the throne."

"And the ones who saw?" Mirabel asked quietly.

"Some fled. Some died. Some—" She stopped. Her mentor's name rose in her throat like a swallowed stone, and she pushed it down. Later. That account would come due later. "Some were thrown down wells. But mostly, mostly, they simply grew old believing they had imagined it, because that was the genius of the thing. It never gave anyone proof. It gave them doubt. Doubt is self-renewing. Proof has to be guarded forever; doubt guards itself."

* * *

By year twenty-five, the king did not leave the west wing. By year thirty, he did not speak in public at all; his addresses were read by secretaries whose voices were, as Sef had noted with a clerk's bleak humor, very good. By year thirty-eight, the Hollow Regent—as the loyalists in hiding had come to call it, though never in writing, never where a tithe-collector might hear—ruled openly, in the king's body, behind the king's face.

And this was the part Sef hated most to explain, because Mirabel kept looking at her as if the horror must be some distant, fanged thing, and it wasn't. It wore ermine. It held audiences. It was beloved.

"People have seen it," she said. "Ambassadors. Peasants petitioning for justice. Whole crowds at coronation-day processions. They see a tall man with the king's gray eyes and the king's famous patience, and he listens to them, and he is courteous, and he remembers—oh, he remembers beautifully—the names of their villages, the details of their petitions, the grievances of their grandfathers. Because remembering is what it does. It has eaten forty years of a king and it wears the memories like a coat, and it is magnificent at it. The kingdom loves its king. The kingdom has never been better governed. Taxes are fair. Roads are repaired. Justice is swift." She closed the book briefly. "It eats minds, Mirabel. And it runs the cleanest state in living memory. Those are not contradictions. The tidiness is the hunger. A kingdom of people who remember less argues less, grieves less, rebels less. The memory-tithe isn't greed. It's agriculture."

Mirabel had gone very still. "The tithe. On the road—I saw—"

"You saw a woman who sold her daughter's face for bread. Yes." Sef's voice went flat. "I've catalogued the tithe-ledgers. Eleven provinces. Nineteen years of collections. Do you know what happens to the memories after collection? The useful ones go to the Regent—skills, loyalties, faces of popular heroes, anything it can wear or use. The rest are discarded. Poured out." She thought of the moors east of here, where travelers swore the fog had shapes in it, where shepherds wouldn't graze their flocks after dusk. "They walk, sometimes. The discarded ones. Like clothes no one is wearing. But that's not tonight's lesson. Tonight's lesson is simpler: the thing in the palace is not a monster that seized a kingdom. It is a parasite that inherited one, legally, patiently, through the machinery of the kingdom itself. Every clerk who filed a false record, every guard who looked away, every minister who found the new king easier to serve than the old one—they built it a throne and called it continuity."

* * *

"Why hasn't it finished?" Mirabel asked.

Sef looked up. It was the sharpest question he'd asked all night, and she marked it—this exile boy with storm in his blood was quicker than his quietness suggested.

"What?"

"The king. If it's eaten him for forty years, why does Aldous still exist? Why keep the husk alive? Why not wear a younger body, a stronger one? Why the thirteenth-moon business, the patience, the—" he touched the letter in his pocket, unconsciously, the way a man touches a wound. "Why am I in that letter at all?"

And there it was. The question she had hoped to defer until morning, or until never.

Sef banked the fire, buying herself ten seconds, and decided—because she had been thrown down a well for hoarding truth, and once was instructive—to give it to him straight.

"Because it can't finish," she said. "That's the center of the whole black design, and I only pieced it together in my last month at the archive, and it's why they drowned me in a well instead of simply killing me, which they could have done anywhere. Listen. The Regent doesn't just eat memories. It *becomes* them. It has no self of its own—or none that survives contact with a mind. It is a hollow shaped by whatever it consumes. For forty years it has worn Aldous, and so it has needed Aldous alive, the way a glove needs a hand. But a glove wears thin. The king's original mind is nearly gone—a rind, a residue, the last few rings of the tree. And here is the parasite's problem: those last memories are the load-bearing ones. The oldest ones. The ones laid down before it ever arrived. Childhood. First griefs. The deep strata. It can't digest them cleanly—it chokes on them. Every attempt leaves cracks in the mask. Witnesses at court speak of the king's 'strange days,' moments of vacancy, of staring, of a face that forgets, briefly, how to be a face."

"So it needs—" Mirabel started.

"It needs a bridge," Sef said. "A mind that can anchor the last of Aldous while the Regent digests it. Fresh blood. Old bloodline. Someone whose own memory is strong enough, rooted enough, to hold the king's remaining self in place during the final feeding—like a trellis for a vine, like a—" she gestured vaguely, hating the poetry her mind offered, "—like a mother's hand holding a dying man's. The loyalists' oldest prophecies spoke of a bloodline the thing could not eat. Okafor blood. Storm-blood. Your family, Mirabel. Your mother didn't just hide you from the Regent. She hid you *from being useful to it*. And the wards fell, and the stars went out, and within a single night its riders found you, because your usefulness just came due."

The fire had burned low. In its red light, Mirabel Okafor looked older than twenty years, and younger, both at once—a man hearing the size of the thing that had been reaching for him since before he could walk.

"They're not coming to kill me," he said slowly.

"No."

"They're coming to *use* me. To hold the king's ghost steady while it eats the last of him. And then—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Sef had read enough requisitions to know how inventories ended.

"And then," she agreed quietly. "Which means, if you follow the logic to its ugly end, that the Regent's plan and the loyalists' hope use the same door. You. Your blood. Your memory. The difference is only in what happens on the other side of it—one side feeds the parasite; the other, if the old prophecies are true, starves it. Which is why I crawled out of a well with cracked ribs instead of staying drowned like a sensible woman. Because there are three vaults of the king's stolen memories still hidden in this kingdom, and the thirteenth moon is turning, and I cannot carry them back to him alone."

Outside, the wind changed. Sef felt it before she heard it—that faint iron-and-rain taste in the air, the one she'd noticed around Mirabel since the border, the one that belonged to no weather she knew. The young man by the fire was staring into the coals, and the embers reflected in his eyes were bending, slightly, as if seen through heat that had no heat in it.

"Three vaults," Mirabel said at last. "Where?"

Sef opened the gray chronicle to the page she had never shown a living soul, and prayed—for the first time in eleven years, actually prayed—that the dead archivist who had taught her everything had not taught her a lie.
