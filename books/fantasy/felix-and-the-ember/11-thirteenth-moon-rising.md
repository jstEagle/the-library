## 11. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The moon was wrong. Mirabel had known it since the night the stars went out, but he hadn't had a name for *how* it was wrong until Sef said it aloud, three days out from the well, in the blue hour before dawn when even the ghosts on the moors went quiet.

"Count the phases," she said.

They were walking again, because walking was what they did now—Sef with her patched archivist's coat and her ink-stained fingers, Mirabel with his mother's ember pendant warm against his sternum like a second heartbeat. The road ahead unspooled gray under a sky the color of old pewter. No stars. There would never be stars again until—well. Until whatever Sef kept not saying.

"I don't understand," Mirabel said. "It's a moon. It goes around."

"It goes around once every twenty-eight days. That's one turn." Sef held up her hand and ticked off fingers as they walked. "New moon to new moon. Every kingdom keeps its calendars by it. Harvest tithes, tax seasons, coronation anniversaries—all of it hung on that wheel." She folded down four fingers, then five, then six. "But the moon doesn't only turn. It *accumulates*. Every turn leaves a residue—a thirteenth phase, hidden, that no calendar marks because no one alive remembers it exists."

Mirabel stopped walking. "That's not possible. I've watched the sky my whole life. In the valley we could see the moon even when we couldn't see anything else."

"You saw a moon," Sef corrected gently, and there was something almost kind in how she did it, which from Sef was more alarming than cruelty. "You saw what the wards let through. Your mother's spell didn't just hide you, Mirabel. It hid the truth of the whole sky from you, because the truth of the sky is the Regent's clock, and your mother wanted you to grow up without hearing it tick."

They stood at the edge of a fallow field, frost silvering the dead stalks, and above them the moon hung fat and gibbous and faintly, horribly *off*—like a coin worn past recognition, like a face seen through fogged glass.

"How many turns?" Mirabel asked.

Sef looked at him for a long moment. Then up at the moon. "Since the night the stars went out? Since the wards fell and the Regent felt the last bloodline it couldn't digest slip loose into the world?" She exhaled slowly. "Twelve. We are in the twelfth turn. Which means somewhere in the next handful of weeks, the moon will complete its thirteenth phase—the hidden one—and everything the Regent has been patient about for forty years stops being patient."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a tithe-station, one of the squat stone buildings the crown had raised along the roads in the Regent's early years—back when the thefts still pretended to be law. Someone had burned it decades ago; the rafters were charcoal lace against the starless dark, and the hearth still drew well enough for a small fire. Sef insisted on the fire. "Cold makes people stupid," she said, "and we cannot afford stupid."

Mirabel fed the flames while Sef unpacked her satchel with the ceremony of a priest at altar. Out came oilcloth bundles, and inside those, notebooks—dozens of them, water-swollen and fire-scoured and annotated in three colors of ink. Her forbidden chronicle. He'd seen her consult it before, always briefly, always with the wariness of a woman handling something that might bite.

Tonight she opened it flat across both their knees.

"Before I explain the deadline," she said, "you need to understand what the deadline is *for*. Not the politics. The mechanics."

"The mechanics," Mirabel repeated.

"Memory is not a thought. It's not a picture in your head. It's a *substance*—the oldest substance in a person, older than bone. When the Regent eats a memory, it doesn't erase it. Nothing is ever erased. It *dissolves* the memory into itself, the way salt dissolves in water. The memory still exists. But it can't be separated back out—not by the person who lost it, not by anyone." She tapped the page. "Except."

"Except?"

"Except that dissolution takes time. Forty years of eating King Aldous, memory by memory, choice by choice, and the king is still—technically—in there. A residue. The last insoluble grain at the bottom of the cup." Her finger traced a diagram: a circle, mostly filled in with dense crosshatching, one small white space remaining at its center. "That grain is why the Regent hasn't crowned itself openly. Why it rules through Aldous's face instead of its own. A wholly consumed mind is a throne with nothing sitting on it. The Regent needs the king's last self intact long enough to finish digesting it, and when the digestion completes—" she drew a single line through the white space, "—it becomes permanent. Unremovable. The kingdom doesn't get a new tyrant. It gets a *fact*."

"And the thirteenth turn?"

"Is when the digestion finishes. The moon's hidden phase is the Regent's own working—it bound its feeding to the lunar wheel forty years ago so the process would be slow enough to hide and steady enough to trust. Twelve turns of stolen memories, poured in turn by turn. On the completion of the thirteenth, the vessel seals." She closed the notebook. "After that, there is no king to save. There is only the thing wearing him, forever, and every memory it has ever eaten becomes a room in a house with no doors."

Mirabel stared into the fire. He thought of the hollow politeness he'd heard described in villages along the road—the king's proclamations read aloud by criers, gentle and empty as weather reports. *The kingdom is at peace. The harvest was sufficient. All is remembered.*

"All is remembered," he said aloud. "That's what the criers say."

"The Regent's motto." Sef's mouth twisted. "It genuinely believes it. In its way, it's the most honest ruler this kingdom has ever had. It remembers *everything*. It simply remembers it instead of us."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the moor, faint and directionless, a ghost was repeating something—a snatch of a child's rhyme, worn smooth as river stone. Mirabel had learned not to flinch at them anymore. That frightened him more than the ghosts did: how quickly he was learning to live in a country where the dead walked because their memories had been spent.

"So we find the king's memories before the moon turns," he said. "The ones the loyalists hid. You said you knew where they were kept."

"I know where *some* of them were kept." Sef pulled a second notebook from the satchel—smaller, its cover scorched, its pages soft as cloth from handling. "This is a partial index. Loyalist vaults, recorded before the archive purges began. Three confirmed sites. Possibly a fourth that was never written down, because the archivist who knew it died before recording it—or recorded it somewhere I haven't found yet." She met his eyes. "Three vaults, Mirabel. Across half the kingdom. In five weeks."

"Then we start tomorrow."

"We started yesterday. We started the day I climbed out of that well." But there was no bite in it. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking—tired somewhere structural, load-bearing. "Do you understand what you're agreeing to? Not the danger. The *arithmetic*. Even if we reach all three vaults—even if every vial is intact, every ward unbroken—we may be carrying home a third of a man. The rest dissolved forty years ago, in the dark, while everyone sang the king's praises."

"Then we carry home a third of a man," Mirabel said, "and it will be a third more than the Regent wants him to have. That's how storms work, Sef. You don't need to beat the wind. You just need to be the one thing it can't move."

Sef looked at him for a moment too long, then snorted and turned back to her notes. "Valley poetry. Fine. Sleep. We make the drowned cathedral in two days if the roads hold, and I want you rested enough to do whatever it is you do to water."

He lay back against his pack and watched the fire, and the ember pendant sat warm against his chest, and he thought about his mother paying with her whole self to buy twenty quiet years, and about a king who had spent forty years being eaten in polite silence, and about the arithmetic of what remained.

Above the broken roof, the moon slid westward, fat and wrong, keeping its secret count.

Twelve turns done. One left.

* * *

Two days later, the land began to sink toward the sea, and Sef grew quieter with every mile—which Mirabel had learned meant she was more afraid, not less. They crested a ridge at dusk and she stopped so abruptly he nearly walked into her back.

Below, in a bowl of drowned hills, lay the cathedral.

It had been built on high ground once—you could see the old processional road marching straight into the floodwater and continuing beneath it, a dark spine of stone. Now the water stood chest-deep in the lower streets of the ruined town around it, and higher still within the cathedral itself, which rose from the flood like the ribcage of something enormous and patient. Its spire had fallen centuries ago. Its windows were either boarded or gone. The water around it was the color of tarnished silver, utterly still, holding the starless sky on its surface like a lid.

"That's the first vault?" Mirabel asked.

"Under it. The loyalists flooded the crypt deliberately—drowned the approach tunnels, sealed the reliquary below the waterline. Water was the ward." Sef's voice had gone flat and recitative, the tone she used when reciting facts she didn't trust herself to feel. "No collector would think to look for memories underwater. Memory fears drowning—it's the one substance that knows it can dissolve."

Mirabel touched the ember pendant. It pulsed against his palm, warm, insistent—his mother's preserved magic leaning toward the flood like a dog toward an open door.

"And I'm supposed to get us through it."

"You're supposed to do," Sef said, watching him with those sharp, cataloguing eyes, "whatever it is your hands have been doing since the wards fell. Because I've read every account of storm-calling in the archive, Mirabel, and none of them describe magic like yours. Magic that *calms*. Every other caller in history summoned the storm. Yours listens to it and asks it to sit down." She shouldered her pack. "The crypt is flooded. So ask."

They picked their way down through the drowned town as the light failed—wading streets where doorframes stood to their shoulders in black water, past a chapel bell hanging silent in its rotted yoke. The cold came up through Mirabel's boots and settled into his bones, and still the water never rippled. It lay everywhere like held breath.

At the cathedral's western door, the flood stood waist-deep in the entryway, and beyond it the nave descended in darkness toward the drowned crypt stairs.

Sef lit a lamp. Its light barely dented the dark; the water took it and gave back nothing.

"I'll say this once," she said quietly. "If the vault's wards recognize your bloodline—if your mother co-wove these the way she wove the valley's—then the water may part for you. If it doesn't, we drown in a basement full of dead men's treasures, and the Regent wins by default. So." She lifted the lamp. "Ask."

Mirabel stepped down into the flood.

The cold hit him like a wall—but beneath the cold, something else. A hum. Faint, deep, familiar as his own name. The same note the ward-stones had sung on the equinox. His mother's note.

His mother had been here. Her magic was in this water, woven into the loyalists' warding forty years ago, waiting.

Mirabel closed his eyes. He did not command. He had never commanded—he *listened*, the way he listened to storms over the valley, feeling for the shape of the thing and asking it, wordlessly, to be otherwise. He felt the flood the way he felt thunderheads: vast, patient, obedient to a logic older than kings. And threaded through it, unmistakable now, a woman's voice in the magic itself, worn thin but unbroken:

*Hold. Hold until my son comes. Hold.*

His breath caught. The water shivered.

Then, with a sound like a held sigh released, the flood *parted*—not violently, not with spray and roar, but the way a crowd parts for a funeral: smoothly, mournfully, walls of black water rising on either side of the nave to a height that made Sef swear softly in three languages, leaving a corridor of wet stone descending into the dark, lamplit and open, all the way down to the crypt stairs.

The ember pendant blazed against Mirabel's chest like a coal.

"Hold," he whispered, echoing her. "I'm here. I came."

Behind him, Sef's voice, hushed and shaking slightly: "The accounts were wrong. They didn't describe magic like yours because there's never been magic like yours." A pause. "Come on. Vials first. Feelings later."

They descended together into the dark, between standing walls of sea, while overhead the wrong moon rose and began, silently, to count.
