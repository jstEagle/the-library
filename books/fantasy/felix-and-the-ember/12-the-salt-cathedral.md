## 12. The Salt Cathedral

The sea had taken the cathedral a hundred years before the Regent ever wore the crown, and in taking it, had done what no loyalist could: it had hidden the thing so thoroughly that even the tithe-collectors' maps forgot it existed.

Sef told him this on the second morning of their walk to the coast, her voice pitched low as if the wind itself might be taking notes. "Saint Vessa's. Dedicated to the tide, of all things. The old faiths built their holiest places where they were most likely to drown them." She walked with her collar up and her hands jammed in her sleeves, a thin woman made thinner by three days of hard road. "When the loyalists needed somewhere to put the first thefts—before anyone understood what was being stolen—they remembered a church that was already halfway to God's other pocket."

"And now it's all the way there," Mirabel said.

"All the way." She glanced at him sideways. "You're not afraid."

"I'm afraid of plenty of things. Drowning is one of them." He said it lightly, but it wasn't entirely a lie. The sea frightened him in a way storms never had. A storm had rules; he'd grown up inside its rules like a child raised in a house. The sea was just hunger with a horizon.

"You should be more afraid than that," Sef said. "But less afraid than you are. That's the trouble with valley-boys—you learned your magic in a place where nothing could punish you for it."

"Something punished me for it," Mirabel said. "The whole world did. That's why I lived in the valley."

She didn't answer that, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They came down to the coast at dusk, and Mirabel stopped walking because the sky was wrong.

He had known, intellectually, that the stars were gone everywhere—that the night the wards fell had emptied every sky from the valley to the capital. But knowing and seeing were different things. Here, where the land flattened out and gave the heavens room, the absence was enormous. The darkness above them wasn't empty the way a room is empty; it was empty the way a mouth is empty. It felt like something waiting to be fed.

"The fishermen call it the Blind Dark," Sef said, following his gaze. "They say the stars didn't die. They say something swallowed them and hasn't finished digesting." She snorted. "Superstition. But superstition is usually memory wearing rags, and memory has been currency here for forty years, so who can say."

Below them, the village of Greyharbor clung to the cliffs like barnacles—a scatter of lamps, the smell of fish and tar and woodsmoke, the sound of waves working patiently at the base of the world. And beyond it, past the last house, past the last path, the sea.

Mirabel's hands ached. He looked down at them, flexed his fingers, and found them prickling with the same pressure he used to feel before equinox storms in the valley—the feeling of weather gathering somewhere behind his ribs, looking for a door.

"It does that now," he said. "All the time. Since the stones broke."

"Good," said Sef. "You're going to need it. Come on. We hire a boat tonight; we dive at slack water tomorrow."

"Dive?"

Her smile was thin and entirely without comfort. "I told you. The cathedral drowned. We're going to have to go in after it."

* * *

The boatman's name was Pell, and he took their coin without asking questions, which Sef said was the most valuable quality a person could have these days. He rowed them out along the cliff-shadow while the Blind Dark pressed down on the water like a lid, and when he spoke at all, it was in the flat voice of a man narrating a route he could take blind.

"Cathedral spire shows at low spring tides," he said. "Not tonight. Tonight you'll want the bell tower gap, east face. Water's thirty feet over the roof. There's an air pocket in the crossing—old divers' tale—and if you believe tales, that's where you'll come up or you won't come up at all." He shipped his oars. "I'll be back at dawn. If you're not on this rock by dawn, I'll tell your families you died brave, and I'll keep the boat."

"We don't have families," Sef said.

"Then I'll keep the boat twice as hard."

They went over the side into water so cold it rang against Mirabel's teeth like a struck bell.

* * *

Down was a color he had no name for—not black, because black implied a surface, and this was depth without end. His little lamp threw a coin of light that shrank as he sank, and the pressure closed around his chest like a hand remembering how to squeeze. Above him, Sef's shape descended beside his own, her rope trailing up toward the faint gray ghost of the boat.

And below him—

The cathedral rose out of the dark.

Even drowned, even furred with weed and crusted white with salt, it was vast. Buttresses like the ribs of some god-sized whale. Arched windows whose glass had long since gone, so that the lamplight passed through their empty frames and fell in pale ladders across the seafloor. Gargoyles stared down at him with mouths open, still screaming after a century underwater. The whole structure leaned slightly eastward, as if bowing—or flinching.

Mirabel had never seen anything so beautiful. He also had never felt magic wake in him the way it woke now.

It started in his palms. A heat, then a hum, then a pull—not toward the cathedral but through it, as though the flooded nave were a chord waiting for a hand to strike it. Storm-magic, Wrenna had called it once, in one of her careful half-truths. The calling-down of sky. But there was no sky here. There was only water, and water, he realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with drowning, was only a storm that had already fallen.

The salt stung his eyes. His lungs were beginning to burn. Thirty feet was nothing—he'd held his breath longer in the millpond as a boy—but the cold was eating his strength, and the dark below the lamp's reach seemed to breathe.

Then the current caught him.

It came around the cathedral's seaward flank without warning, a river inside the sea, and it took him like a hand takes a dropped scarf. He spun. His lamp tore from his grip and tumbled away, its light wheeling crazily across the drowned stone, and for one horrible moment Mirabel Okafor hung in the black water of Saint Vessa's churchyard with nothing to show him which way was up.

His mother's voice, or the ember's memory of it, moved through him: *Storm doesn't ask the wind's permission.*

He stopped fighting.

He opened his hands.

What happened next, he would spend years trying to describe and never quite manage. The water around him *listened*. Not stilled—stilling the sea would have been like stilling a landslide—but listened, the way a crowd listens when someone they trust steps up to speak. The current's grip loosened into something almost curious. And in the dark around him, far off, he heard it: a low rolling groan, deep under the water, deep under the seafloor, the sound of thunder deciding to happen somewhere it had never been invited.

Light flickered. Not his lost lamp—something bluer, colder, crawling along the cathedral's buttresses like veins of lightning trapped in glass. Salt-water lightning. He hadn't known such a thing could exist, and apparently neither had the sea, because the current let go of him entirely and drew back, coiling, the way a horse draws back from a fence it has just discovered is electrified.

He kicked for the bell tower gap. Sef was there—her hand shot out of the dark and seized his sleeve, and her grip was iron, and her eyes above her nose-clip were wide with something she would later deny was awe.

*Later,* she mouthed, and pointed down.

They swam in through the broken rose window together, two small shadows entering the throat of a drowned giant, and the blue light followed them like a dog that had decided, provisionally, to be theirs.

* * *

The air pocket in the crossing was real.

They broke the surface gasping, into a hollow dome of trapped breath maybe twenty feet across, ringed by the cathedral's flooded arches. Their splashing echoed strangely—up, and around, and back—as if the building were repeating everything they said in a language made only of their own voices. When Mirabel got his breathing under control and lifted his head, he saw that the dome of air was lit from below.

The blue light had followed them in. It lay along the flooded floor of the crossing like a lake of captured sky, and beneath it, visible through yards of clear, unnaturally still water, was the cathedral floor—and on the cathedral floor, arranged in a spiral that must once have been a mosaic of stars, were vials.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Glass glinting in ordered rows, each one sealed with wax and lead, each one cradled in a niche carved into the stone as carefully as an egg in a nest. Some were clouded. Some were clear as morning. All of them, Sef had told him, and now he saw it with his own eyes, held a human memory—one stolen hour, one stolen face, one stolen grief, smuggled out of the palace in fishing boats and pilgrim carts by people who had mostly died doing it.

"Oh," Mirabel said. The word came out very small in the huge dark. "Oh, it's *true.*"

"I've read about this place my entire career," Sef said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "I catalogued the smuggling routes. I interviewed the descendants of the couriers. I wrote four hundred pages proving the Salt Cathedral was a legend invented by drunk sailors." She laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "Four hundred pages, and the truth was thirty feet under the sea the whole time, waiting for a storm-caller to knock on the door."

"Why do you need me to knock? You knew where it was."

"Because of *that.*" She pointed.

He followed her finger and saw what he had taken for shadow along the far arches: chains. Great rusted chains, anchored into the pillars, running down into the flooded nave—and hanging from them, half-dissolved by a century of salt, shapes. Bells. The cathedral's bells, sunk from the tower, each one bound with iron bands etched in script he couldn't read.

"Ward-bells," Sef said. "Loyalist work. They were tuned to the Regent's hunger—if it ever came looking for the vault, the bells would ring, and the ringing would scramble every memory in the building into noise. Nothing edible. A scorched-earth spell, cast on glass." Her jaw tightened. "They've been silent for a hundred years. But the iron's failing—I can see it failing—and when the last band goes, the bells fall, and everything down there—" she nodded at the spiral of vials beneath the water "—goes with them. We're not early, Mirabel. We're *last.*"

The blue light pulsed around them, slow as a heartbeat.

"So the magic," Mirabel said slowly, working it through, "the thing I did outside—it quieted the water. Can it quiet the bells?"

"Can it?" Sef turned to look at him fully, and in the blue glow her face was stripped of all its bitterness, all its armor, and what was underneath was simply a scholar standing in front of the one book she'd been told didn't exist. "I don't know. Nobody knows. Nobody has stood in this room in a hundred years, and nobody who stood in it before that could call thunder out of the sea." She pushed wet hair from her face. "Your mother hid you from the Regent for twenty years with a dying spell. Whatever runs in your blood, it has never once been tested at its full length. This—" she gestured at the drowned church, the chained bells, the thousand sleeping memories below "—this is the testing."

Mirabel looked down through the still water at the spiral of vials. Somewhere in those rows, he knew, were hours of King Aldous's life—the young man dancing in rain, the father, the friend, the self being eaten bite by bite in a palace two hundred miles north. Somewhere in those rows was the beginning of everything Sef had given her career to understand.

And his hands were burning. Not painfully—burning the way a hearth burns, the way a home burns, warm and certain and *ready*, as if the magic had been waiting twenty years in exile for exactly this room, exactly this water, exactly this hour.

"Tell me what to hold," he said.

Sef blinked. "What?"

"The bells. The iron bands. Tell me what to hold still, and I'll hold it." He was already pulling himself toward the edge of the air pocket, already filling his lungs, and the blue light rose to meet him like a tide rising to meet the moon. "Because I spent twenty years learning to make my magic small enough to hide. I am extremely tired of hiding it."

For the first time since the well, Sef Vane smiled like something other than a woman surviving.

"Then go down," she said, "and introduce yourself."

* * *

He dove.

The blue light came with him, streaming from his fingertips now, wrapping his arms in slow coils of cold fire, and as he sank toward the mosaic floor the water around him went utterly, impossibly calm—a column of glass in a drowned church, still enough that he could see his own reflection in it, lit blue, arms spread, looking for all the world like something the old faith would have carved on the lintel and called a saint.

The nearest ward-bell hung ten feet above the floor, its iron bands eaten to lace. As he reached toward it, the metal groaned—a sound that traveled through the water and through his bones and through the whole patient body of the cathedral—and a crack ran across the topmost band like ice giving way on a spring pond.

Mirabel pressed both palms flat against the iron.

And called the storm *down* instead of *up*.

Thunder rolled through the sea. Every vial in the spiral shimmered. The failing band knitted itself whole under his hands—not healed, exactly, but *held*, frozen at the edge of breaking, suspended the way a note is suspended when the singer refuses to breathe. One bell. Then the next. Then the next, his light leaping from chain to chain, his magic running down the loyalists' old iron like warmth returning to a cold house, and overhead, dimly, he heard Sef shouting something joyful and profane into the air pocket, and the cathedral—drowned, forgotten, waiting a hundred years—seemed to exhale.

He hung there in the heart of it, holding a hundred years of silence in his two hands, and looked down at the nearest vial in the spiral. Clear glass. Old wax. And inside, swirling faintly, like weather seen from very high up:

Rain.

A memory of rain, moving softly in its little prison, waiting for someone to open the sky.

Mirabel reached for it.
