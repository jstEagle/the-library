## 14. The Tithe-Collector

The trouble began, as most trouble did in the starless kingdom, with a ledger.

Mirabel saw it first: a fat book bound in gray leather, held open on a folding table at the crossroads of Hallowmere, where the road bent north toward the salt flats. Behind the table sat a man in the Regent's livery— dove-gray coat, silver thread at the collar shaped like a closed eye—and beside him stood two others with the same stillness that Mirabel had learned to distrust on the road. They didn't shift their weight. They didn't glance around. They simply *waited*, the way a trap waits, and the villagers of Hallowmere queued before them like rain queuing behind a cloud.

"Name," said the collector to an old farmer at the front of the line. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a man asking after someone's health.

"Tam Broadfield."

"Tam Broadfield." The collector's pen scratched. "Recite for me the name of your wife's mother."

"Marigold. Marigold Broadfield, born Hesse."

"And what did she smell of?"

The farmer blinked. "Of—of bread, sir. And lavender water."

"Lavender water," the collector repeated, writing. "Lovely. And your daughter's birthday?"

"First thaw. Every year we—"

"Thank you, Tam Broadfield. Your tithe is current. Next."

Mirabel drifted past the table with his hood up and his eyes down, the way he'd learned to walk through every village since leaving the valley. Sef was three paces behind him, disguised in a borrowed cloak that made her look like a traveling tinker, her satchel of forbidden chronicles padded out with pots and pans so it clanked convincingly. It was a good plan. It had been a good plan right up until the collector's pen stopped scratching.

"Next," the collector said again—but his eyes had lifted from the ledger, and they were not on the old woman shuffling forward. They were on Sef.

On the way Sef's hands moved. On the small, precise gesture she made with two fingers, half-hidden by her cloak—the gesture of someone who had spent thirty years in the royal archive and knew exactly how archivists were taught to hold a pen.

"Your tithe, tinker," the collector called out. "You've been through Hallowmere before. I remember your gait."

* * *

Sef Vane had been cornered by better men than this one and had talked her way past all of them, but never with her heart hammering quite this hard, and never with Mirabel Okafor standing close enough to hear it.

She turned, slow and easy, letting her shoulders round into the tinker's stoop she'd practiced. "Beg pardon?" she said, and pitched her voice lower, rougher, a market voice. "I don't recall owing anything."

"You wouldn't." The collector smiled. He was younger than Sef had expected—thirty perhaps, with soft cheeks and ink-stained fingers, a clerk's hands on a butcher's work. "Memory's a funny thing. Slips away from people. That's rather the point of me." He closed the ledger with a sound like a door shutting. "Walk with me, tinker. Just you. My associates will keep your large friend company."

The two silent ones stepped out from behind the table. Up close they were worse than still—they were *smooth*, their faces like wax left too near a fire, features present but uncommitted. Sef had read about them in the marginalia of her mentor's notes and had always assumed the descriptions were exaggerated. Hollowed ones. People the Regent had drunk down to the dregs and then set walking, because empty vessels made excellent tools.

Mirabel's hand found her elbow. Not gripping. Just there. A question.

"It's fine," Sef said, loud enough for the collector to hear. "Man wants to talk trade. Tinkers talk trade." She squeezed Mirabel's wrist once—a code they'd agreed on weeks ago, back when codes had felt like a game: *one squeeze means trust me, I have a plan.*

She did not, strictly speaking, have a plan. She had a theory, and theories had gotten her thrown down a well.

The collector led her off the crossroads, past the well (she looked at it sideways and felt her stomach clench), to a lean-to behind the tithe-house where a brazier burned and a second table stood, this one bare except for a single object under a glass dome: a vial, no longer than her thumb, filled with something that shimmered like oil poured over moonlight.

Except there was no moonlight anymore. There hadn't been for months.

"Do you know what this is?" the collector asked.

"A pretty trinket," Sef said.

"It's a memory. A good one. A farmer's wedding day, if you must know—harvest festival, dancing until dawn, his bride with straw in her hair. He sold it willingly. Most of them do, at first. They think they're selling a *day*." He tapped the glass. "They don't understand that a day is a load-bearing wall. Pull it out and the whole house leans." He looked up at her, and his pleasantness fell away all at once, like a mask slipping its string. "Now. You're going to tell me where you got those calluses, tinker. Because tinker's hands are scarred across the palm. Yours are scarred along the middle finger and thumb. Pen calluses. Archivist's calluses. I catalogued enough confiscated archivists in my training to know them anywhere."

The brazier crackled. Somewhere beyond the lean-to, Mirabel was standing between two hollowed men, and Sef could not see him, and that fact occupied a disproportionate amount of her attention.

"Sef Vane," the collector said softly. "Royal Archivist, third chair. Convicted of sedition eight months ago. Escaped custody. There's a price on your head that would buy this whole village twice over." He leaned back. "Here is what I don't understand. You're a clever woman. Cleverer than me, probably. So why come *back*? Why walk toward the tithe-roads, toward the collectors, toward the very machinery you fled? What could possibly be worth it?"

Sef looked at the vial under the glass. At the shimmering nothing inside it. At a stranger's wedding day, bottled and waiting to be poured down a parasite's throat.

And the theory arrived fully formed, the way the best and worst ideas always did.

"Trade," she said.

The collector's eyebrows rose.

"You want Sef Vane. I'm offering you a deal instead. One memory, freely given, in exchange for walking away and forgetting my face." She held up a finger before he could speak. "And before you say the Regent doesn't bargain—it does. It bargained with forty years of courtiers. It bargained with the king himself, if half of what I've read is true. It's patient. Patience is just bargaining with extra steps."

"What memory," the collector said, "could a disgraced archivist possibly hold that interests the crown?"

"The archive fire," Sef said. "Eleven years ago. The west wing. Everyone believes it was an accident—a lamp, a dry summer, bad luck. It wasn't. I know what started it. I know *who*. I was there. I've kept it locked up tight for eleven years because saying it aloud would have killed me." She let a beat pass. "It implicates someone very high. Someone whose name the Regent has been trying to dig out of me for a decade. That's why I ran, when I was arrested. Not the sedition charges. The fear that they'd finally take it."

It was, of course, almost entirely false. There had been a fire. There had even been a lamp. But the rest—the conspiracy, the secret, the high name—was mortar and bricks assembled in the last four seconds from materials Sef had spent her life handling: rumor, inference, the shape of a lie that fit the hole people wanted filled.

The collector was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached out and lifted the glass dome off the vial, and Sef understood with a cold drop of clarity that the wedding-day memory had never been the merchandise. It was the demonstration.

"We'll need a vessel," he said. "And your cooperation. Memories taken unwillingly curdle. The Regent prefers them offered." He produced, from a drawer beneath the table, an empty vial and a thin silver needle with a pearl-white grip. "Hold this. Think of your secret. The vial does the rest."

*Sef,* she thought, *you are about to hand a servant of the Hollow Regent a forged document, and the forgery is your own mind, and if he checks it against the original—which does not exist—you are dead in a village you can't find on a map.*

She took the needle.

* * *

Mirabel counted his heartbeats to keep from doing something stupid.

He'd learned that trick in the valley, back when storms used to gather in his chest before he understood what they were. Sixty beats, in and out, and the lightning settles. He was at forty-one when the collector's associate drifted closer to him—not walking so much as *gliding*, that terrible smoothness—and said, in a voice like wind through an empty house:

"She lies beautifully. Doesn't she."

Mirabel went very still. "I wouldn't know. We met on the road last week."

"Yes," the hollowed one said. "That's what she told you to remember."

Forty-two. Forty-three. Mirabel kept his face blank and his breathing even and thought about the ember pendant warm against his sternum, his mother's last preserved memory resting over his own heart, and how many steps it was to the tree line. Two hundred, maybe. Could he outrun them? Could he fight them? His storm-magic came when it came now, wild and half-trained, more likely to level the village than save them—

But the hollowed one wasn't attacking. It was just standing there, smiling faintly at nothing, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that it wasn't threatening him at all. It was *lonely*. Whatever the Regent had left inside this person, it remembered conversations. It remembered wanting them.

"Your friend," the hollowed one said, "is going to give away something she can't get back. They always think they're giving away less than they are. The woman who owned me thought that too. She sold a Tuesday. Just one Tuesday. But the Tuesday had her son's first steps in it, and once it was gone she couldn't remember why the boy mattered, and then she couldn't remember the boy, and then—" It paused, head tilting, as if listening to something far away. "Then there was room. That's what the Regent leaves. Room."

Behind the lean-to, Sef laughed at something the collector said. It was a good laugh. Warm, easy, exactly the laugh of a tinker telling a story over drinks. Mirabel had heard Sef laugh maybe four times since the well, and none of them sounded like that, and the difference frightened him more than the needle surely frightened her.

Forty-four. Forty-five.

He thought about the letter in the dead rider's hands. *The Okafor heir is located; the Regent's patience is ended.* He thought about Wrenna's face when she'd told him the truth about the wards—that his mother had paid with her whole self, that every safe day of his childhood had been bought with pieces of a woman he couldn't remember clearly anymore. He had spent twenty years believing the valley was home. It had been a cage built of love, and he had only learned the difference when the bars broke.

And now here was Sef, offering to sell a lie to a monster, and Mirabel understood suddenly—viscerally, in his teeth—that he could not know whether it was a lie. That was the thing no one warned you about trust. It wasn't believing someone was good. It was accepting that you had no way to check, and choosing them anyway.

The hollowed one watched him with its borrowed eyes. "You're deciding something," it said. "I can always tell. The face goes quiet."

"I'm deciding," Mirabel said, "whether to believe my friend."

"Belief is expensive," the hollowed one said. "I'd know. I paid everything I had for mine."

* * *

In the lean-to, Sef pressed the needle to her forearm and thought about a fire.

Not the real fire—the forged one. She built it carefully, the way she'd been trained to build arguments: foundation first, then walls, then a roof convincing enough to live under. The west wing, midnight, dry summer. Herself at twenty-six, unable to sleep, walking the stacks. And then the figure with the lamp—tall, robed, moving with purpose, setting flame to the shelf of accession records with a deliberateness that no accident could explain. And the name. She gave the fire a name, a real name, a name that would burn hot in the Regent's mouth: Lord Chancellor Aldous's private secretary, a man long dead, conveniently beyond verification.

*Why,* the memory asked itself, *would a secretary burn his own master's records?*

Because, Sef answered inside the forgery, he'd been ordered to destroy evidence of the Regent's arrival—and had kept copies, and the copies were hidden, and the hiding place was—

No. She caught herself. No hiding place. Never give them a location. Give them a hunger with no meal attached. Let the Regent chew on the idea that a copy exists somewhere, forever unrecoverable, forever just out of reach. A secret that pointed nowhere was a leash; a secret that pointed somewhere was a map, and maps got people killed.

The vial drank. Sef felt it happen—a faint tug behind her eyes, like a hook easing through silk—and then the collector held the little glass tube up to the light, and inside it swirled a miniature midnight: flames, a robed figure, a young woman frozen mid-horror in the stacks.

The collector watched it turn. And turned it. And turned it.

"Well?" Sef said, when the silence had gone on too long to be comfortable.

"It's clean," the collector admitted. "Beautifully constructed. Almost a shame." He set the vial down and looked at her, and for a moment something flickered across his soft young face that was not the Regent's pleasantness at all—it was tiredness, human and ordinary. "Do you know what I was, before the livery? A schoolteacher. In Duncarrow. I collected my first tithe at nineteen because my mother was sick and the tithe-waiver came free with the uniform." He shrugged. "The Regent doesn't make monsters. It makes arrangements. Everyone in this kingdom is an arrangement now."

"Is that a confession?" Sef asked. "Should I be flattered?"

"It's a warning." He slid the vial into a lead-lined case, and Sef's stomach dropped through the floor, because lead-lined cases meant transport to the capital, meant the Regent itself would examine the memory, meant the forgery would be inspected by the one entity in the world qualified to catch it. "This goes to the palace. If what's in it holds—if the Regent finds your fire as solid as I do—you're forgotten, and the price on your head quietly dies in a drawer. If it doesn't hold..." He smiled the pleasant smile again. "Well. The Regent collects debts in person."

"That sounds like a risk for both of us," Sef said.

"It does." He closed the case. "Go. Take your large friend. And Archivist Vane—one piece of professional courtesy, from a schoolteacher who remembers being a teacher." He leaned in, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Whatever you're really hunting—and you are hunting something, nobody walks into the tithe-roads for fun—hunt it faster. The thirteenth moon turns in five weeks. After that, there's nothing left in the palace worth stealing. Nothing left at all."

* * *

They walked out of Hallowmere without looking back, which required enormous discipline, and did not speak until the village was a smudge of smoke behind them and the road had bent twice.

Then Mirabel stopped, turned, and said, "What did you give them?"

"A lie," Sef said.

"What did you *give* them, Sef. The needle takes something. I saw your face. Something went out of you."

She was quiet for a moment. The gray sky pressed down on the moor around them, starless even at noon, and the wind smelled of salt and coming weather.

"Three hours," she said finally. "A market day in the capital, when I was a girl. My father bought me honey-bread and lied to me about where my mother had gone. It was already a sad memory. I figured—" Her voice did something complicated and settled on flat. "I figured it was the cheapest thing I owned that felt heavy enough to be believable. Real memories have weight. Forgeries made of nothing feel like nothing. So I wrapped the lie around something true, and gave them the bundle."

Mirabel stared at her. "You gave the Regent a piece of your childhood."

"I gave the Regent a *document*," Sef corrected. "One that says the evidence it's been looking for was destroyed by a dead man. Which buys us—" she ticked it off on her fingers— "a dead price on my head, a five-week head start, and a collector who thinks I'm paid in full. Do you know what I'd have given for a forgery that clean back when I was running the archive? Half the kingdom's problems are forgeries that weren't careful enough."

"That's not—" Mirabel stopped. Started again. "You didn't hesitate. Back there. You squeezed my wrist and walked behind that lean-to and I stood there counting my heartbeats, terrified, and you sat down at that table and *invented* a memory like it was nothing. Like memory was paper. Like it was yours to write on."

"It *is* paper," Sef said. "That's the whole horror of this kingdom, Mirabel. That's what the tithe-collectors prove every single day. Memory is paper. It can be taxed, traded, torn out, rewritten. I've known it since I was a junior archivist watching the accession ledgers get thinner every year. The only question—the only one that matters—is who holds the pen." She met his eyes. "Today I held it. Today it saved our lives. Tomorrow the Regent holds it, and it's rewriting a king. I'm not going to pretend there's a moral difference in the *act*. Only in the aim."

Mirabel looked at her for a long time. The wind pulled at their cloaks. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered—not his, just weather, just the ordinary sky doing ordinary things, and he missed the valley so sharply it was like a knife between ribs.

"When you squeezed my wrist," he said at last, "one squeeze meant *trust me, I have a plan.*"

"It did."

"You didn't have a plan. You had a gamble."

"I had a plan," Sef said. "The plan was: be the best liar in the kingdom for four minutes straight. It's the only plan I've ever been certain of." She shouldered her clanking satchel and started walking. "Come on. Five weeks. The salt cathedral is six days north, and if the tide charts in my head are still accurate—and memory being paper, they might not be—we want to reach it at low water or not at all."

Mirabel followed. But as the moor swallowed the road behind them, he found his hand had drifted to the pendant at his chest—to the ember, warm through his shirt, holding the one true memory he had left of his mother. A king, weeping, asking her to hide her son.

Paper, Sef said. Memory was paper.

Then he would spend the rest of this journey learning everything he could about how paper burned, and how it was kept safe, and who could be trusted to hold the pen.

Behind them, unnoticed, the collector opened his lead-lined case one last time and looked at the swirling little fire—and frowned, very slightly, at the edge of the vision, where a young woman stood frozen in the archive stacks.

Where her face should have been, the memory showed a blur.

And the blur, if he had been the sort of man who still noticed things, looked almost like it was smiling.
