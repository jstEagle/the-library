## 15. Ghosts of the Borrowed

The moors began three days out from the well, and Sef knew it before the land did.

"You'll want to walk faster here," she said, not slowing. "And don't look at anything that calls your name."

Mirabel had learned in three days that Sef's warnings came in two varieties: the kind that explained themselves, and the kind that didn't. This was the second kind. He'd stopped asking after the first night, when he'd asked what lived on the moors and she had looked at him with an expression he was beginning to understand meant *you are not ready for the answer, and I am not ready to give it.*

So he walked faster.

The land itself was wrong in a way he couldn't name at first. The valley had been green and loud—grass that grew too fast, storms that argued all night, a world so full of itself it barely noticed him. The road since had been gray and starless, but it had still been *alive*: crows, hedgerows, the smell of woodsmoke from villages where people squinted at him like he was a word they'd almost remembered.

The moors were not alive. They were *remembered*. That was the difference, and once Mirabel felt it he could not unfeel it. The heather grew in patterns too regular, repeating every forty paces like a stitch gone round and round a hem. A stream crossed their path twice—the same stream, same stones, same bent alder—and when Sef stepped over it the second time without comment, Mirabel understood that the water wasn't flowing anywhere. It was performing flowing. Somewhere, something had taken the memory of this place and worn it thin, the way a favorite coat wears at the elbows, and now the moors were nothing but elbows.

"What happened here?" he asked, because some questions you can't not ask.

"The Regent eats," Sef said. "It doesn't digest everything. Some memories it chews and spits out—too old, too tangled, too sad to be useful. And what it discards doesn't die." She walked with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, her archivist's satchel riding her hip like a weapon. "Memory is stubborn, Mirabel. It's the most stubborn thing there is. You can pull it out of a person, but it remembers being loved. It remembers having somewhere to live. So it goes looking."

"Looking for what?"

"A body." She said it flatly, the way you say *it rains in spring.* "It can't make one. But it can borrow one. There are bodies on these moors that died with their memories already eaten—hollow folk, we called them, back when anyone was allowed to call them anything. The discarded memories find the hollows and move in. Not all the way. Just enough to walk. Enough to repeat whatever fragment survived the chewing."

"And then?"

Sef finally looked at him. Her eyes were pale as dishwater and twice as tired.

"And then they walk until someone puts them down. Or until they wear out. Whichever comes first." She turned away. "Keep your hood up. The ghosts aren't dangerous, mostly. They're worse than dangerous. They're *persuasive*."

* * *

He saw the first one at dusk.

It stood in the heather maybe fifty yards off, a figure shaped like a man but standing the way furniture stands—upright, patient, purposeless. As the light failed, Mirabel saw that it wasn't quite solid. It flickered at the edges, like a candle flame seen through dirty glass, and where its face should have been there was a smudge, a blur, a thing his eyes kept sliding off of.

But its hands were clear. Its hands were terribly clear: a farmer's hands, cracked and weathered, and they moved constantly, folding and unfolding, the way hands do when they're remembering work they no longer have to do.

"Harrowing," the ghost said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, soft as ash falling. "Harrowing. Harrowing. The east field first, then the—harrowing. Harrowing."

"It's a planting," Sef said quietly. "A whole spring, probably. Someone's best year. The Regent took it, chewed through the middle, spat out the edges." She didn't slow her pace, and Mirabel had to hurry to keep up. "Don't answer it. Don't finish its sentences. If you complete a ghost's pattern, it latches onto you. It thinks you're the rest of it. And then it follows you for miles, harrowing at your heels, wanting you to be its field, its oxen, its hands."

"That's—" Mirabel started.

"Cruel. Yes. I know. I catalogued four hundred of them before they threw me down a well." Her voice didn't waver, but her jaw did, briefly. "The crown's records used to call them *reclamation events.* I called them what they were. That was my crime, if you're wondering. Not the thefts. The naming."

They walked on. Behind them, fainter and fainter, the ghost kept saying *harrowing, harrowing,* and Mirabel found that his chest hurt in a way he couldn't explain, and could not stop hearing it, and knew he would hear it for the rest of his life.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen boundary wall, and Mirabel lay awake listening to the moor.

Because the moor was talking.

Not loudly. Not all at once. But everywhere, in fragments, the way a dream talks when you're half out of it: a woman humming three notes of a song over and over; a child counting to seven and starting again; an old man saying *the door is blue, the door is blue, mind the step, the door is blue.* Dozens of voices. Hundreds. Scattered across the dark like embers raked out of a fire, each one glowing with the last piece of somebody's life that the Regent hadn't wanted.

"They come closer at night," Sef said. She was sitting up, feeding twigs to a fire no bigger than a cupped hand. "Warmth draws them. Not heat—warmth. Living warmth. They can't feel it anymore, so they drift toward it like moths, except moths know why they're flying." She poked the fire. "Sleep in shifts. If one gets within arm's reach, stand up slowly and walk away. Don't run. Running reads as *chasing*, to them. Like you're playing their game."

"Whose game? What game?"

"Whatever game they were in the middle of when they were eaten." Sef stared into the flames. "That's the part the chronicles never put in words, Mirabel. These aren't monsters. They're *mid-sentence*. Every one of them is somebody's interrupted life, still trying to finish the sentence. And nobody—not the crown, not the loyalists, not the gods if they're listening—is going to let them finish."

Mirabel watched a shape drift past the far edge of the firelight. It paused. Turned. For one horrible moment he thought it would come toward the warmth—but then it drifted on, murmuring *seven, eight, nine, seven, eight, nine,* and the darkness took it.

"I'm going to kill it," he heard himself say. "The Regent. Whatever it is. I'm going to end it."

Sef looked at him for a long moment.

"Good," she said. "Hold onto that. Because in about ten minutes you're going to want to stop feeling anything at all, and you'll need something to hold."

"Why? What happens in ten minutes?"

She didn't answer. She just fed another twig to the fire, and looked north, toward a low rise in the moor, and Mirabel followed her gaze and saw nothing there yet.

Nothing there yet.

* * *

It came an hour past midnight, when even Sef had finally slept.

Mirabel woke to singing.

It was a lullaby. He knew it instantly, the way you know your own name—instantly, completely, and with no idea how. Four notes rising, two falling, a pause like a held breath, then four notes rising again. His mother had sung it. Of course she had sung it. Every mother sang it; it was older than the kingdom; it was the sound every child fell asleep to before they knew there was a world outside the sound.

Except that wasn't true, and he knew it wasn't true, because Wrenna had told him once—drunk on plum wine at midwinter—that his mother had made it up herself, just for him, changing the old tune note by note until it was theirs alone. *No other child in the world has your song,* Wrenna had said. *Remember that.*

Four notes rising. Two falling. The pause. Again.

He sat up.

The ghost stood on the low rise, silhouetted against a sky with no stars in it, and it was wearing his mother's face.

Not all of it. That was the horror of it, the part that would live under his skin forever: it was wearing *pieces* of her face, the way the farmer-ghost wore pieces of a harvest. The line of her jaw. The set of her mouth. The small scar above her left eyebrow that she'd gotten falling out of the ward-stone oak at nineteen, the story Wrenna told every year at the equinox. The rest was smudge, was blur, was a thing his eyes slid off of—but those pieces were hers, exactly hers, arranged in the shape of a woman singing.

"No," Mirabel said. He didn't mean to say it aloud.

The ghost's head tilted. The pause between verses stretched.

Then it started down the slope toward him, singing, and its feet didn't bend the heather, and its shadow fell the wrong way, away from the fire, and Mirabel stood up because standing up was the rule, stand up slowly and walk away, except his legs wouldn't take the instruction because that was his mother's mouth making the sound, his mother's scar catching the firelight, and—

"*Mirabel.*"

Sef was awake. She had a knife in her hand, though he couldn't imagine what she thought it would do, and her other hand was clamped around his wrist hard enough to bruise.

"Don't finish it," she breathed. "Whatever it sings next—don't hum it, don't think it, don't *complete* it. If you complete her song, she'll think you're the child. She'll follow you until she wears out. Is that what you want? To be followed across a starless kingdom by a scrap of your mother that thinks you're a baby who needs sleeping?"

"She's not—she isn't—"

"She's a fragment. A chewed-up leftover the Regent spat out because it wasn't useful enough to keep." Sef's grip tightened. "I'm sorry. I am so sorry. But you need to hear it: that is not your mother. Your mother is dead, and what's walking down that hill is what the Regent did with one piece of her."

The ghost reached the bottom of the rise.

Up close it was worse and better—worse because the smudge resolved into details he recognized, the way she tucked her chin when she sang, the way her shoulders rocked side to side; better because up close he could see the seams, the places where the borrowed body didn't fit the memory, the places where his mother ended and the hollow woman underneath showed through like bone through a torn glove.

Four notes rising. Two falling. The pause.

And in the pause, the ghost looked directly at him, and its mouth—the mouth that was and was not his mother's—shaped a word.

His name.

*Mirabel.*

The lullaby resumed. But now, threaded through it, over and over, woven into the melody like a thread pulled loose from a hem:

*Mirabel. Mirabel. Four notes rising. Mirabel. Two falling. Mirabel.*

"She knows me," he whispered. "Sef. Sef, she knows my name."

"Ghosts repeat what they last held," Sef said, and her voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of a woman handling something explosive. "If it's holding your name—if it's holding *you*—then this isn't a random fragment. This is a memory *of you*. Something the Regent took from someone who knew you. Something it discarded because—" She stopped.

"Because what?"

"Because it was too much." Her pale eyes were wide. "Memories of love are the hardest to digest, Mirabel. The Regent keeps the useful ones—secrets, locations, fears. Love has no leverage. So it eats around the love and throws the love away. And a mother's memory of her son is mostly love. It would spit that out whole. It would spit it out *fast*."

The ghost was close enough now that he could see the fire reflected in eyes that weren't quite eyes. It swayed, rocking side to side, singing his name into the lullaby, and Mirabel understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that he was looking at the last living echo of how his mother had felt about him. Not her face. Not her voice. Her *love*, walking around in a stolen body on a cold moor, looking for the child it had been sung to.

Looking for him.

"Can I—" His throat closed. He tried again. "Can I hold it? Just for a moment. Can I—"

"If you touch it, you'll feel everything it feels," Sef said. "All of it. Unfiltered. A mother's love for her child, with none of the person left to soften it. People have drowned in less. I've read the reports. I've *written* the reports."

"Good," Mirabel said.

"Mirabel—"

"Good," he said again, and stepped forward, and took the ghost's hands in his.

* * *

There was no storm inside her. That was the first thing. Twenty years of wondering what his mother had been, what her magic had felt like, whether the storm-calling lived in her blood the way it lived in his—and there was no storm. There was only warmth. Enormous, specific, undiluted warmth, aimed at one point in the world like sunlight through a lens, and the point was a small boy with dark hair standing in a kitchen doorway, and the boy was laughing at something, and the warmth had no edges and no conditions and no fear in it anywhere, and it was *his name*, his name was the center of it, his name was the word she'd chosen out of all possible words to be the sound of her whole heart—

He came back to himself on his knees in the heather, and the ghost was kneeling too, its borrowed face level with his, and the lullaby had stopped.

In the silence, the smudge of its features shifted. Settled. And for one moment—one moment that lasted either a heartbeat or a year—it looked at him with his mother's full attention, and the warmth poured off it like heat off a stone, and its mouth moved, and this time it was not singing.

*"Grow,"* it said. *"Grow, and be warm, and forget the cold."*

Then the light went out of it.

Not gradually. All at once, like a snuffed candle, like a held breath released. The borrowed body folded gently into the heather, and the fragments of his mother's face dimmed and blurred and came apart, and what remained was a hollow woman, empty, finished, the last scrap of a chewed memory finally spent.

Mirabel knelt there. The moor was silent. Even the distant ghosts had gone quiet, as if they'd heard.

Sef crouched beside him. She didn't touch him. She let him kneel.

"It gave the rest to you," she said softly. "What was left. It spent itself finishing the sentence." Her voice caught, just slightly. "Twenty years an archivist, and I have never once recorded a ghost doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Choosing." She exhaled. "It chose to give you the last of it instead of keeping it. Memory isn't supposed to choose, Mirabel. That's what makes it memory." She shook her head slowly. "Your mother's love just did something impossible, and I watched it happen, and the first thing I thought was: *the Regent should never have let this exist.*"

Mirabel looked down at his own hands. They were shaking. Inside them, fading fast, the warmth was already going—the way a dream goes, the way you can hold the shape of it but not the substance. He tried to press it into memory, all of it, the kitchen doorway, the laughing boy, the name at the center of the warmth.

Most of it slipped away even as he grasped. But not all. One piece stayed, lodged under his ribs like a splinter of light:

*Grow, and be warm, and forget the cold.*

"Come on," Sef said, after a long time. She rose, and offered him her hand, and he took it. "We have miles yet. And the third vault won't wait, and neither will the moon."

He stood. His legs held. They gathered the camp in silence and walked north into the starless dark, and behind them the moor stayed quiet, all its interrupted sentences holding their breath.

It was nearly dawn when Sef stopped so suddenly that Mirabel walked into her.

Ahead, where the moor dropped toward a valley of chalk and scree, fires burned in the earth itself—dozens of them, ringed and deliberate, marking the mouth of a mine no map should have shown them.

Sef had told him about the loyalist cell. Nine people, she'd said, guarding three vaults, dying of old age in the dark.

Nine fires, though. Mirabel counted them twice.

Ten.

"Somebody new got here first," Sef said quietly. "Or somebody's waiting for us."

Her hand went to the knife at her belt, and together they started down toward the fires.
