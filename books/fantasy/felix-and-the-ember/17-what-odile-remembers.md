## 17. What Odile Remembers

The chalk mine smelled the way old promises smell—dust and cold stone and something faintly sweet beneath it, like honey left too long in a shut jar. Odile had lived with that smell for thirty-one years. She had stopped noticing it around year ten, and then Mirabel Okafor had walked into her mine with rain still clinging to his coat, and she had noticed everything again all at once.

He sat across the fire from her now, on an upturned crate that had once held lamp oil, his hands wrapped around a tin cup of barley tea he was too polite to say tasted like chalk. The ember-glass pendant at his throat caught the firelight and held it, a small captive sun. Sef Vane sat beside him, sharp-elbowed and suspicious, cataloguing the mine's defenses with her eyes the way she probably catalogued everything—shelves, exits, weaknesses.

Nine of them left. Odile counted them the way she counted everything now: Maren at the tunnel mouth, half-asleep on watch; the twins, Pell and Anselm, arguing over a map by candlelight; old Hask dozing upright against the wall with his sword across his knees because he no longer trusted his hands to find it quickly if he laid it down. Nine. When she'd sworn her oath there had been two hundred and forty. She did the arithmetic anyway, some nights, lying awake. It always came out to grief.

"You want to know where the third vault is," she said. "That's what you came for."

"We want to know *everything*," Sef said. "But yes. Start there."

Odile looked at Mirabel instead. He had his mother's patience—or what she remembered of it—and his mother's eyes, dark and level, that made you feel like your words were being weighed rather than merely heard. It was unsettling. It was also, she suspected, why she was going to tell him more than she'd told anyone in three decades.

"Before I tell you where," she said, "you need to understand *why*. Because everyone who comes looking for the king's memories comes looking for a treasure. A hoard. Something that was taken." She set down her own cup. "It wasn't taken. Not the way you think."

Sef leaned forward. "The Regent stole them. I've read the ledgers. I've spent eleven years documenting—"

"I know what you've documented, archivist. I carried half those vials myself." Odile let that land. Saw it land—the flicker in Sef's face, the recalculation. Good. Let them both sit with it. "I was in the palace guard when it began. I stood at the king's door for six years before any of us knew there was anything standing behind him worse than assassins."

*

She had been twenty-three, and proud of her post. Third Door of the private apartments, night rotation, which meant she saw the king more often than most of the court saw their own husbands. King Aldous the Second—young then, only nine years crowned, with a laugh that carried and a habit of learning the names of every servant's children. He used to stop on his way to the observatory and ask Odile whether her mother's hip had eased that winter. No king should have known her mother had a hip. He knew anyway. That was the kind of man he was, back when he was a man and not a shape wearing one.

The first thing she noticed was small, the way rot is always small at the start. The king stopped humming.

He had hummed constantly—a little walking tune, nameless, tuneless, the kind of thing a person makes up without knowing they're doing it. Then one week he didn't. Odile mentioned it to the chamberlain, who mentioned it to no one. The second thing was that he began asking questions he'd already asked. Not the vague questions of a busy man—precise ones. *What was my father's voice like? Did I enjoy the harvest festival? Why did I weep at the opera in Vell?* And each time someone answered, he would listen with terrible attention, like a man drinking water in a desert, and an hour later the well would be dry again.

"The physicians called it melancholy," Odile said. "Then they called it a wasting of the spirits. Then they stopped being consulted, because the king dismissed them, one after another, and the ones who talked too freely found their careers ended." She paused. "Or found themselves forgetting things. Small things. Their mothers' faces. The layout of their own houses."

"That's how it fed," Sef said quietly. "On the household. Anyone close enough."

"It started with him. The rest was spillage." Odile's hands had gone cold; she rubbed them. "We didn't have a word for it then. We barely have one now. But there was a woman in the archive—"

"Ysolde Vane," Sef said. Her voice had gone carefully flat.

"Your mentor. Yes." Odile studied her. "You knew her?"

"I inherited her life's work. I'm beginning to suspect I inherited her mistakes as well." Sef's jaw tightened. "Go on."

"Ysolde was the first to see the pattern, because Ysolde kept records the way other people keep breathing. She cross-referenced the king's lost years against the palace's lost people and found the same shape in both: a hunger, moving inward, patient as water. And she found something else. She found that the king had figured it out before any of us. Months before. Maybe a year."

Mirabel spoke for the first time. "How?"

"Because he told me." Odie laughed, short and bitter. "Not me specifically. He would walk the corridors at night when he couldn't sleep—which was always—and sometimes he'd stop at my door and talk. Kings aren't supposed to do that. He did it anyway. One night in the autumn of his fifteenth year, he said, *'Odile, if you woke tomorrow and couldn't remember anything bad that ever happened to you, would you be happier?'* And I said something stupid and loyal, that our sorrows make us who we are, or some such nursery wisdom. And he smiled at me—" her throat closed briefly around the memory— "he smiled like a man watching the last ship leave, and he said, *'Yes. That's exactly the problem.'*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere deeper in the mine, water dripped its slow count.

"He knew," Mirabel said. "He knew what it was doing to him."

"He knew what it *was*, near enough. He could feel it feeding—he described it to Ysolde once as a warmth behind the eyes that arrived whenever he tried to hold on to something precious. The harder the memory glowed, the warmer it got. And the warmer it got, the faster it went out." Odile spread her hands. "Understand what that means. Every joy he had became bait. His wedding day. His daughter's first steps. The taste of the wine he loved. Each one a lit candle in a dark room, and the thing in the crown came to every flame and drank it down. So he did the only thing left to a man being eaten alive from inside his own happiness."

"He scattered them," Mirabel said.

"He scattered them." She nodded slowly. "Ysolde built the vaults—I'll grant her that much, whatever else she was. Salt Cathedral, the deep cisterns, the places the loyalists could reach without passing the throne. And the king chose what went into them. Sat up whole nights choosing, with Ysolde writing down his instructions in cipher. He gave away his childhood. His coronation. His wife—not her death, mind you, but her *life*, every morning of her, every argument, every kindness. He said if the thing wanted him empty, it would have to work for its meal. He said—" Odile had to stop, press the heel of her hand against her eye. "He said, *'Let it starve politely at a full table.'*"

"And it worked?" Sef asked. "Partially?"

"Partially. It slowed the feeding to almost nothing for six years. The Regent—that's what Ysolde named it, though none of us used the word aloud—grew careful. Hungry things get careful when the food starts hiding. It turned to the kingdom instead. The tithe. The quiet erasures. Forty years of a nation forgetting itself one winter at a time, so no one would notice the man on the throne had gone hollow." Odile looked at Mirabel directly. "And here is the part that will not sit comfortably in your chest, boy, so take it sitting down. Your mother came to the palace because the king sent for her. Storm-caller, ward-wright, the last of a line the crown had exiled a generation before. He asked her to build a hiding place no hunger could find. And she asked him why he didn't simply fight the thing, destroy it, and he told her the truth."

"Which was?"

"Which was that he was afraid he'd already lost too much to win. That a man who can't remember why the kingdom matters won't fight hard enough to save it. That the safest place for what remained of Aldous was anywhere he wasn't." Odile's voice dropped. "He co-authored his own erasure, child. Every vial we carried out into the dark, he handed to us himself. We weren't thieves. We were undertakers, burying a living man piece by piece at his own instruction, so that something of him might survive the grave."

Silence took the mine. Pell and Anselm had stopped arguing over their map. Even the drip seemed to be listening.

Mirabel stared into the fire. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "So when we return his memories—we're returning things he *chose* to lose."

"Yes."

"What if he doesn't want them back?"

"There it is." Odile nodded, unsurprised. "That's the question nobody asks until it's too late to ask it politely. I've had thirty-one years to turn it over, and I'll give you my answer: it doesn't matter what he wanted then. He was drowning and cutting off his own arm to float. What he wanted was to survive *as himself*. Not as a pleasant hollow thing the court could steer. If you ask the ruin in the palace whether it wants to remember, it will say no, because the ruin wants nothing except to continue. You'd be asking the wound for permission to heal." She leaned forward, firelight carving the deep lines of her face. "But hear the other side too, because I owe you honesty and not just comfort. Some nights I wonder if we did him a mercy or a violence. He lives in a fog of almost-contentment now. The Regent keeps him comfortable the way a farmer keeps a beast comfortable. Is that a life? Would he thank us? Or would he look at us with those empty kind eyes and say, *who are you, and why have you brought me all this pain?*" She shook her head. "I don't know. I've never known. I only know which choice lets him make the answer himself. And that's the only one I can live with."

Sef had gone very still, her fingers pressed together like a closing book. "Ysolde never wrote any of this. The chronicles say theft. Abduction of the royal memory. Resistance cell, loyalist network—"

"Ysolde wrote the truth in cipher, and burned the plain version, and spent the rest of her life making sure the lie was the one history kept. Because the truth was dangerous." Odile fixed her with a look. "Think, archivist. If the kingdom learns its king emptied himself willingly—if the court learns the Regent's reign began as the king's own strategy—what happens to the story of rescue? What happens to the legitimacy of anyone who restores him? Half the court would call it madness. The other half would call it treason. The Regent itself would use it: *see, even your true king chose to forget you.*" Her mouth twisted. "A weaponized truth is worse than a clean lie. Ysolde understood that. Perhaps too well."

Something moved behind Sef's eyes—some ledger being balanced, some entry amended. She said nothing. Odile noted the silence and filed it away, the way she filed everything now. Thirty-one years of filing.

*

Later, when Sef had been drawn off by Anselm to argue about vault access routes, Odile walked Mirabel down the eastern gallery, past the sleeping bunks and the shrine—just a shelf, really, holding a dried sprig of palace lavender and a tin badge of the royal guard polished bright despite everything.

"You have her ember," Odile said, nodding at the pendant. "Wrenna's keeping, I'd wager. She always was the sentimental one."

"You knew my mother."

"I trained her in the tunnels. She was hopeless with a sword and magnificent with a storm." Odile smiled, and it cost her. "The night she finished the valley wards, she came back gray as ash. Gave too much. I asked her if it was worth it—one child, hidden, against everything she'd surrendered. And she said something I've thought about every day since." Odile stopped walking and turned to face him fully. In the lamplight her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "She said, *'It isn't a hiding place, Odile. It's a seed vault. Someday the ground outside will be ready again, and something has to be growing.'*"

Mirabel's hand had risen unconsciously to the pendant. "I don't understand what I am," he said quietly. "Storm-caller. Heir. Key. Everyone keeps telling me what I'm for."

"Good," Odile said. "Hold on to not understanding. The ones who think they understand themselves completely are the ones the Regent eats first—it finds the shape of a person and pours itself in. Stay unfinished." She resumed walking, boots ringing on chalk. "Now. The third vault. You'll want to know why it's the hardest of the three, and I'll tell you: because of what's in it. The first vault holds his joys. This one holds his knowledge. Everything Aldous learned about the Regent in those early years—its origin, its nature, its weakness, if it has one. And above all, one memory he guarded more jealously than any other. The night it arrived. Who opened the palace doors to it. He made Ysolde seal that vial separately, triple-warded, and he said—" she pushed open the iron door at the gallery's end, onto cold air and the sound of the mountain breathing— "*when they come for this one, they'll wish they hadn't.*"

Beyond the door, the mine opened into a natural cavern where the loyalists kept their maps, and pinned among them, older than the rest, yellowed and creased, a diagram of the capital with one building circled in faded ink.

Mirabel stepped closer. Read the label. Went pale.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"The Royal Archive," he said. "The circle is around the royal archive. And underneath it, someone wrote—" he looked up at her— "*the betrayer works here.*"

Behind them, unnoticed, Sef Vane had stopped in the doorway, and her face had gone the color of the chalk walls.
