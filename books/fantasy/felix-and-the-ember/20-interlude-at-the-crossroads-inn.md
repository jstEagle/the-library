## 20. Interlude at the Crossroads Inn

The inn stood where three roads met and none of them went anywhere important anymore—a squat stone building with a sagging porch and a sign that had been repainted so many times the original name was a ghost under layers of blue. *The Thirteenth Moon*, someone had once painted there, in a world that still counted its moons honestly. Now it just said THE INN, as if naming it anything more specific felt like a risk.

Mirabel smelled woodsmoke and stew before they crested the last hill, and something in his chest unclenched for the first time since the chalk mine.

"We can't stop," Sef said, though she was already looking at the light in the windows the way a starving woman looks at bread.

"Odile's people need rest. Tam needs his shoulder bound properly, not with your archive ribbon." He nodded toward the loyalists strung out behind them on the road—nine had become seven, and seven moved like people carrying invisible weights. "One night. We're nobody here. That's the whole trick of crossroads inns; nobody remembers who stayed."

"You would know," Sef said, but without venom. She'd been making that joke for two weeks now, and it had softened into something almost like affection. "Fine. One night. But we sleep in shifts, and I take first watch because I don't trust any of you to notice the sky doing something wrong."

"The sky already did everything wrong," Mirabel said. "It ran out of stars."

They took the common room. The innkeeper was a broad, gray-haired man named Pell who asked no questions beyond *stew or bread?* and *how many nights?*—the practiced incuriosity of a man whose roads had gone quiet and whose business depended on not wondering why. His wife brought ale without being asked. A fire cracked in a hearth big enough to stand in, and above it hung a row of iron hooks from which hams swung gently whenever the door opened, like slow pendulums keeping time for a room that had forgotten what it was counting down to.

Tam slept almost immediately, his bandaged shoulder propped against the wall, his face slack with the particular exhaustion of men who have buried a leader. Odile sat by the fire sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, her eyes half-closed, her hands keeping their old soldier's rhythm. The other loyalists drifted up the stairs in pairs.

And in the corner, small and solemn and utterly failing to look like she wasn't staring, sat a girl of perhaps six, watching Mirabel with the naked fascination only children and cats permit themselves.

She was the innkeeper's daughter. She had a smudge of ash on one cheek and a wooden horse clutched in one fist, and every time Mirabel glanced over she looked away too fast, then looked back, then away again, like a bird deciding whether the scarecrow was real.

"Go on," Sef said dryly. "You're going to be useless until you deal with that."

"Deal with what? She's just looking."

"She's been looking for an hour. Either go talk to her or let me do it, and I promise you my version involves footnotes."

Mirabel huffed a laugh, got up, and crossed the room. He crouched down near the girl's table—not too close; he remembered being small and suspicious of strangers—and set his hands on his knees.

"Hello," he said. "I'm Mirabel."

"I know," the girl said. "You came in from the star road."

"The star road?"

"That's what Papa calls it. The north road. Because you can see all the stars from it." She said this with the flat certainty of a child reporting a fact that adults had somehow gotten wrong. "But there aren't any stars now. Papa says they're just behind clouds. But clouds move, and the stars didn't come back. So Papa's wrong."

Mirabel felt that little sentence land somewhere beneath his ribs. *Clouds move, and the stars didn't come back.* Out of the mouths of babes, as the old archivists used to say before the archives started burning.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Pell's daughter is called Nettie," she said, in the tone of someone reciting how she'd been introduced her whole life. Then, quieter, with the actual self showing through: "Nettie."

"Well met, Nettie." He glanced at the wooden horse in her fist. Its paint was worn to bare wood along its back, loved nearly to death. "Does he have a name?"

"He's called Thunder. Because Papa says when I gallop him across the floor fast enough, it sounds like thunder." She demonstrated on the tabletop, a soft clattering drumroll. It sounded nothing like thunder. It sounded wonderful.

"It does," Mirabel said gravely. "Though I'll tell you a secret. Real thunder has a whistle in it. Right at the start, before the boom. Most people never hear it because they flinch. But if you knew how to listen—" he tilted his head, "—or how to make it yourself, you could call a storm right down out of the sky."

Nettie's eyes went very wide and very bright. "That's a lie."

"It's absolutely true."

"Papa says magic isn't real. He says the stories are just stories."

"Papa," Mirabel said, leaning in conspiratorially, "is trying to keep you safe from disappointment. Which is kind. But it's also wrong." He looked around the room—Sef pretending not to watch while watching intensely, Odile's knife paused mid-stroke—and lowered his voice. "Now, I can't do real storm-whistling here. Not properly. There's a reason, and it's long and boring. But I can teach you the shape of it, and someday, if the sky ever gets fixed, you try it on a hilltop and see what happens."

"Teach me," Nettie said instantly, setting Thunder down with the care of a general laying down arms.

"First: lips. Small hole, like you're going to kiss the wind goodbye." He demonstrated. "Second: tongue goes back, like a little wave curling. Third—and this is the part everyone gets wrong—you don't blow *hard*. You blow *low*. Thunder isn't loud because it's pushing. It's loud because it's *big*. You have to feel big inside even when you're small outside."

"That doesn't make sense."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "Most true things don't, at first."

He whistled then—the low, rolling note his mother had taught him on a porch in a valley that no longer existed anywhere except in his own skull. It wasn't much, indoors, without wind to carry it. But the hearth caught it and threw it around the chimney, and for a moment the fire itself seemed to rumble, a deep harmonic groan in the flue that made the hams swing on their hooks.

Nettie shrieked with delight. From the stairs, one of the sleeping loyalists shouted something indistinct and alarmed. Pell appeared in the kitchen doorway with a ladle raised like a weapon, saw his daughter laughing, saw the stranger crouched beside her, and slowly lowered the ladle with the expression of a man deciding some mysteries were safer unsolved.

"Do it again," Nettie demanded.

So he did. And again. And by the fifth time she had the shape of it, producing a sound like a dying goose, which she declared triumphantly was *exactly* thunder, and who was Mirabel to argue? He taught her the tongue-wave again, slower. He put her small hand flat against her own chest and told her to feel for the bigness. Outside, the starless night pressed against the windows, and inside a child whistled at the dark like it owed her money.

Sef watched all of this from the fireside with an expression Mirabel couldn't parse—something between tenderness and grief, aimed not at the girl but at him.

* * *

Later, when Nettie had been carried upstairs asleep with Thunder clutched to her chest, and the common room had emptied to embers and the two of them, Sef finally spoke.

"You shouldn't do that."

"Teach children to whistle?"

"Teach children to hope." She turned her cup in her hands. It was her fourth; she drank like a scholar reads, steadily and with annotation. "Do you know what happens to children in this kingdom, Mirabel? They learn early. They learn that asking questions gets their parents visited. They learn that remembering things is dangerous, so they practice forgetting, the way other children practice scales. And then a stranger comes through and teaches them to call storms, and tells them the sky can be fixed, and then the stranger leaves—or dies, because our odds are not good, I've done the arithmetic—and she spends the rest of her childhood waiting for a thunderclap that answers her."

"You think I gave her a false hope."

"I think hope is a debt," Sef said. "And children always end up paying their parents' debts, and now she owes one to a stranger."

Mirabel turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left. "My mother taught me to whistle thunder when I was five," he said. "On the porch of our house in the valley. She put my hand on my chest exactly like I did with Nettie, and she said *feel big inside even when you're small outside*, and I thought it was a game." He looked up. "Two years later she was dead, and the valley closed around me like a hand, and I spent twenty years believing the whole thing—the porch, the lesson, the lightning in my palms—was just a game I'd invented to miss her less. So yes. Maybe hope is a debt. But I'd rather owe it than never have been taught."

Sef was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled.

"That's not the argument I actually want to have," she said at last.

"No," Mirabel said. "I didn't think it was."

She set down her cup. When Sef Vane prepared to argue, she did it the way she did everything—precisely, with sources.

"Let's say we succeed," she began. "Let's say we find the last of Aldous's memories, and we get them back to him, and the Regent starves out of his skull like smoke from a doused fire. You've met him. You stood in that throne room."

"I stood in a throne room with a hollow thing wearing him."

"You stood in a throne room with *him*. The hollow is what forty years of eating made, but the shape underneath is still Aldous. And here's what your shape-underneath did, Mirabel, according to records I catalogued myself, back when cataloguing was allowed." She began counting on her fingers, an archivist's habit, terrible and precise. "He signed the tithe edicts—all of them, the early ones, before the Regent needed to forge his hand. He approved the first memory levies to pay for a war nobody remembers fighting. He let his own court invite the thing in—I've read the arrival records, Mirabel, whatever invited it, it was welcomed with a feast. And when his friend Okafor—your mother—came to him terrified, begging him to see what was happening to his own mind, do you know what the great king Aldous did?"

Mirabel said nothing.

"He had her exiled," Sef said softly. "Officially for unlicensed storm-calling. Unofficially because she was saying things about the crown that the crown could not afford to have said. Your valley—that beautiful prison that kept you safe—was *his sentence*, carried out by *his guards*, under *his seal*. He didn't save you, Mirabel. He threw you away, and your mother caught you, and it cost her everything she was."

The fire crackled. On its hooks, the hams swayed.

"So I'll ask it plainly," Sef said. "Why him? Why does this man deserve to be reassembled? We have seven loyalists left, average age sixty-one. We have a mother's ghost walking the moors repeating a lullaby. We have a kingdom full of Netties learning to forget. Every hour we spend chasing Aldous's scattered self is an hour we don't spend on any of them. So convince me. Convince me the king is worth a kingdom's last hope."

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. Not because he didn't have an answer—he'd been turning the question over since the throne room, since the flicker of focus in those empty eyes when the ember glowed—but because he wanted to give it honestly, not cleverly.

"When I touched the vial in the salt cathedral," he said finally, "I saw him dancing in the rain. Young. Alive. Laughing at the thunder like it was a rival and he intended to win." He smiled faintly at the memory that wasn't his. "And I thought: this is who we're saving. This bright boy. And then in the throne room I saw what's left, and I thought: we're not saving the boy at all, are we. The boy's been eaten. We're saving... the ruin. The polite, empty ruin."

"Yes," Sef said. "Thank you. Exactly."

"But here's what I keep coming back to." He leaned forward, elbows on the table. "In the throne room, when the ember glowed—for one second, maybe less—one flicker. And do you know what was in his face? Not confusion. Not hunger. *Shame.* He looked at me and he was ashamed. Something in him, down under forty years of being eaten alive, recognized what had been done to my family and was *ashamed of it*." He tapped the table. "A monster doesn't feel shame, Sef. A parasite doesn't. Whatever's left of him in there knows what he did—to my mother, to the kingdom, to himself. He's been awake inside his own erasure for four decades. Can you imagine anything worse? Being forced to watch, from the inside, while something wears your face and unmakes everything you loved?"

"I can imagine seven loyalists who might disagree about what's worst."

"I'm sure they could. And if Odile stood here and told me I was wrong, I'd listen, because she's earned the right to say it and I haven't." He held Sef's gaze. "But here's the thing. You asked why *him*. And I think that's the wrong question. I think the question is: what does it say about us if we decide anyone is too far gone to save? The Regent's whole method is that. It decides what memories are worth keeping and burns the rest. It looks at a kingdom and says *this part deserves to exist, this part doesn't*. If we walk into that palace and choose to leave Aldous hollow—if we say the ruin isn't worth the rain—we're doing his work for him. Just with better reasons."

Sef stared at him. The firelight moved in her eyes.

"That's a dangerously principled position for a man with no army," she said at last.

"I grew up in a valley built on a dangerous principle. One woman decided one child deserved twenty years of safety, and paid her entire self for it. Nobody sane would have made that trade." He shrugged. "I come from unreasonable love, Sef. It's the only inheritance I've got."

Sef looked away first. She picked up her cup, found it empty, and set it down again with slightly too much force.

"My mentor used to say the archive exists because memory is a debt the living owe the dead," she said quietly. "I thought it was a motto. A nice line for the title page." Her jaw tightened. "I'm starting to think it was a warning. And that he wrote it knowing what he'd done."

"Sef—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Not tonight. Tonight I want to drink bad ale in a crossroads inn and pretend we're two travelers arguing about kings, because tomorrow we go to the third vault, and the third vault is where Odile says the Regent's attention will be waiting, because the Regent is not stupid, and neither am I, and I know a trap's shape when I'm standing in it."

"Then we won't stand in it."

"We'll stand in it," Sef said, with the flat certainty of a woman reading a prophecy she'd written herself. "Because the king's memories are in it, and you've just finished explaining to me, very movingly, why we don't get to leave anyone hollow."

Mirabel laughed despite himself—a short, tired sound. "You could have argued harder."

"I could have. But I watched you teach a six-year-old to whistle thunder tonight, and I realized something that ruined my argument." She rose, gathering her notes and her empty cups. "You weren't teaching her to call storms. You were teaching her that the sky can be answered. That's either the stupidest thing I've ever seen or the whole reason we're going to win, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," Mirabel said. "It's usually both."

She snorted and headed for the stairs, then paused on the bottom step, silhouetted against the hall's darkness.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "your mother would be proud of you. I only met her once, at a distance, in the palace gardens, the year before they sent her away. She was arguing with three court mages simultaneously and winning. Everyone said the Okafor storm-callers were arrogant." A pause. "She didn't look arrogant. She looked like someone who knew exactly what the sky owed her and intended to collect."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and Mirabel sat alone with the embers.

* * *

He woke past midnight to a sound he hadn't heard in twenty years and had never once stopped listening for.

Thunder.

Real thunder—distant, low, rolling along the underside of the horizon like a barrel dragged across a cellar floor. He was out of bed and at the window before his mind fully surfaced, heart hammering, staring at the starless dark over the eastern hills.

There was no storm. No lightning flickered behind the hills. The sound came again, softer, and he understood—it wasn't weather at all. It was coming from below, from the ground itself, a deep tectonic grumble, rhythmic, almost patient. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the earth. Or like something enormous beginning to wake.

Down the hall, a door opened. Sef stood in the corridor with a candle, and her face in the candlelight told him she'd heard it too, and worse—that she knew what it was.

"That's not thunder," Mirabel said.

"No," Sef said. "That's the observatory mountain. Old texts called it the King's Drum—they said the founders built the vaults to resonate with it." Her knuckles whitened around the candlestick. "Mirabel, that sound means the third vault's outer ward just broke. From the *inside*."

Below them, in the room where she slept, Nettie stirred and murmured—and in her sleep, small and clear, she whistled.
