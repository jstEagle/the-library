## 21. The Regent's Eye

The third vault was supposed to be a day's walk from the chalk mine.

It took them four.

Odile's map was drawn in a hand that had stopped trusting ink decades ago—faint pencil lines over older fainter lines, annotations in three different scripts, one of which Sef identified as a dead cipher used by palace cartographers before the Regent made maps illegal. *Maps are arguments about what exists,* she'd said, tracing the route with a cracked fingernail. *He doesn't want anyone arguing.* The vault itself sat beneath a ruined watchtower on the moor's high shoulder, a place called Cael's Rest that had been neither restful nor, apparently, named after anyone named Cael in living memory. Nobody remembered who Cael was. That was the whole kingdom now: a country of orphaned names.

They walked through weather that had forgotten how to be anything but gray. No rain—the sky hadn't managed rain since the stars went out, as if even the clouds were holding their breath—but a low pressure that sat on Mirabel's shoulders like a hand. His storm-magic stirred under it constantly now, restless, a dog pacing at a door. Inside the valley it had been a thing he cupped gently, doused storms with his bare palms, kept small on purpose. Out here it was a thing with teeth, and every mile from home it grew another one.

"You're doing the thing again," Sef said, without looking up from her notes.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you pretend you're not listening to the sky."

"I'm always listening to the sky. It's very loud lately."

"It's been loud for two hundred miles." She snapped her notebook shut—a real book, leather-bound, salvaged from her archivist days and stuffed with everything Odile's cell knew about the vaults. "You keep looking up like you're waiting for an apology."

"I'm waiting for stars," Mirabel said. "Two hundred miles of nothing. You'd think somewhere there'd be one."

"There won't be." Sef said it flatly, the way she said most true things, as if truth were a tax she'd long ago stopped resenting paying. "The Regent eats light out of the sky the way it eats memory out of heads. Same hunger. Stars are just memories the universe keeps of its own fires. He can't abide them."

"That's not how astronomy works."

"No," Sef agreed. "That's how *he* works. Learn the difference or die of confusion."

Mirabel almost smiled. Two weeks ago he'd have bristled. Now her bitterness felt like ballast—heavy, ugly, and the only reason they didn't tip over. Behind them, Odile's remaining people strung out along the trail: Tam, the youngest, nineteen and already gray at the temples; the twins, Hesk and Brann, who spoke so rarely they'd begun to seem like one person split across two bodies; old Marette, who carried the vial-cases wrapped against her chest like a sleeping infant. Nine loyalists when they'd left the mine. Nine still. Mirabel kept counting them the way you count coins you're afraid of losing.

* * *

They reached Cael's Rest at dusk, if dusk could be said to exist in a sky the color of dirty wool. The watchtower rose out of the moor like a broken tooth, half-swallowed by turf, and around its base the heather grew in a perfect ring of silver-white—bleached, Sef explained, by wards that had been quietly drinking the color out of the ground for forty years rather than let anything notice them.

"Your mother's work?" Mirabel asked Odile.

"Her co-spell. With the king himself." Odile knelt at the ring's edge and pressed her palm flat to the bleached earth. For a moment nothing happened; then the white spread outward from her hand like frost blooming across a window, racing along the circle until the entire ring glowed faintly, and beneath their feet something vast turned over in its sleep. "Third vault. King Aldous's own hiding place. The first two were loyalist caches—things we smuggled out. This one he built himself, before the hollowing went too far. Before he couldn't trust his own hands."

"What's inside?"

Odile looked up at him, and her face did something complicated—grief and pride and fear braided together so tightly Mirabel couldn't tell which strand was which. "Him," she said. "The best parts of him. The parts he loved enough to bury."

The entrance was a hatch beneath the tower's fallen eastern wall, sealed with a lock that wasn't a lock—it was a question, Sef realized, after twenty minutes of study. A mechanism of interlocking rings engraved with words in Old Court script, each ring needing to be turned to answer a riddle only the true king would know.

"*What did I lose first?*" Sef read aloud, translating. Her voice caught on the last word. She cleared her throat and tried again. "*Answer me, and enter.*"

"Do we know?"

"We know three possible answers. Odile's testimony gives us one. The confession gives us another. And—" Sef hesitated, and Mirabel saw her weigh something, some small shame, before setting it down between them like a stone. "And I know a fourth. From my mentor's private papers. Things Aldous told her in confidence, back when she was still loyal. Back before."

"Back before she opened the door," Mirabel said quietly.

Sef's jaw tightened. "Yes. That."

They worked the rings as the gray light failed. It was slow, careful work—each answer tested against the mechanism's resistance, wrong answers producing a soft, apologetic click and a fresh engraving surfacing, as if the lock were keeping score. Mirabel held the lantern. Odile fed Sef fragments of memory from her guard-days. Tam stood watch on the tower's crown because someone had to, though watching for what, out here, none of them could have said.

The lock answered on the eleventh try. *A name,* the mechanism wanted, *the first name I chose to forget*—and Sef whispered a word into the central ring that Mirabel didn't catch and she clearly hoped he wouldn't ask about, and the hatch exhaled a breath of cold, dry air that smelled of paper and cedar and time.

Then the world went wrong.

* * *

Mirabel felt it before he saw it, because of course he did—his magic had been screaming for an hour and he'd been calling it nerves. Every hair on his arms lifted. The pressure that had ridden his shoulders all day suddenly *deepened*, became directional, became a weight leaning toward the northeast horizon. The storm-sense in him, the thing that had once let him feel a thunderhead building fifty miles off inside the valley's sheltered air, turned like a compass needle and pointed at a patch of darkness that should not have been there.

There was no night yet. But there was a piece of night anyway.

It hung above the moor a mile distant, maybe less—a patch of absolute black, starless even by the standards of this starless sky, a hole cut in the gray with scissors too sharp for the material. It was the size of a barn. Then it was the size of a house. It moved without moving, the way a shadow moves when the candle gutters—not traveling so much as *arriving*, again and again, closer each time.

"Tam," Mirabel said. His voice came out very calm, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Tam, get down here."

Tam was already coming, sliding down the turf-slope in a clatter of loose stone. "I saw it," he gasped. "On the horizon. I thought—I thought it was cloud—"

"It's not cloud." Odile had gone rigid beside the open hatch. In thirty years of hiding, Mirabel realized, she had never actually seen the Regent's attention arrive. She'd only ever cleaned up after it. "Everyone away from the entrance. *Now.* If it reaches the vault while it's open—"

"It drinks it," Sef finished. All the blood had left her face. "Like chapter twelve of my chronicle. The purge at Vellamoor. It drank a loyalist cache through a keyhole. Through *solid oak*. An open hatch is an open mouth."

The darkness crossed half a mile of moor in the time it took Mirabel to draw breath.

Up close it resolved into something worse than absence. It had texture—slow, churning interior movement, like smoke underwater, like ink dropped in milk and refusing to mix. It had no eyes. It needed none. Mirabel understood, with the sick clarity of a man standing in an open field, that he was being *looked at*, comprehensively, from every angle at once, by something that found him interesting the way a man finds an unopened letter interesting.

It stopped thirty feet away.

The heather died in a widening circle around it. Not burned—*emptied*. Color drained out of the plants and streamed inward in thin bright threads, silver and green and the dusty purple of moor-flowers, absorbed into the dark like wine into blotting paper. Where the threads passed through the air, they made a sound. Mirabel had heard that sound once before and never told anyone: the sound the ward-stones had made, the night they shattered. A hum just below hearing. A hum like a voice deciding whether to speak.

"Marette," Odile breathed. "Get the cases behind the tower. Quietly. Everyone else—"

The darkness pulsed.

And a voice came out of it.

Not a voice, really. Mirabel would spend years failing to describe what it actually was—an impression of speech, the way a footprint is an impression of a foot, left in the air by something that had eaten so many voices it no longer needed its own. It borrowed. It reached into the space between them and pulled out cadences they recognized: a hint of Odile's parade-ground bark, a thread of Sef's dry precision, and—Mirabel's stomach dropped—something warmer, older, a woman's tone worn smooth by lullabies.

His mother's cadence. Or the memory of it, stolen off some ghost on the moors, worn like a coat.

*"You have been busy,"* the Regent's eye said.

Nobody answered. The silence stretched. Somewhere behind Mirabel, Marette was moving, slow as syrup, cases hugged to her chest, and every grain of sand in the world seemed to pass through the neck of the hourglass between her steps.

*"The salt cathedral,"* the voice continued, mild, almost conversational, a neighbor remarking on weather. *"Emptied. The mine-vault. Emptied. My collectors report a storm where no storm should be. A boy who calls lightning with his hands."* A pause, delicate as a knife laid down on silk. *"The Okafor heir. Twenty years I wondered where she hid you. Twenty years, little stormcaller, and all along you were growing in the one garden I couldn't weed."*

"Don't talk about her," Mirabel said.

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out of him the way lightning comes out of a cloud—not chosen, merely discharged. And the darkness *flinched*. It was a tiny thing, a ripple across its surface like wind over black water, but Mirabel saw it, and understood it, and something in his chest that had been cold with fear all his life suddenly caught fire.

It was afraid of him. Of the magic. Of the one inheritance the valley had watered for twenty years while the rest of the kingdom forgot how to fight.

*"Ah,"* the Regent's eye said softly. *"There it is. Do you know, I ate your mother's memory of you? One of the last things she ever spent. I remember you as she remembered you—newborn, furious, gripping her finger hard enough to bruise. Would you like to hear how she described you? She thought you'd be a healer, of all things. She was so certain the storms would gentl—"*



Mirabel raised his hand.

He didn't decide to. Later, trying to reconstruct it, he would find the decision missing entirely, a gap in his own record exactly the shape of instinct. The pressure that had leaned on his shoulders for four days came down through his arm and out through his palm, and the air above the moor *tore*. Not lightning—there was no storm to steal from, no charge to call—and so his magic did the impossible thing instead, the thing that only happened outside the valley when he stopped being careful: it made weather from nothing. Cloud boiled out of clear gray air. Wind stood up from dead grass. Thunder cracked across a sky that had forgotten the word, and the sound hit the Regent's eye like a physical blow, scattering it, shredding its edges into frayed streamers of dark.

For three heartbeats, the moor was lit by stuttering violet light, and everyone was shouting, and the darkness recoiled up and back like a struck animal—

—and then it gathered itself, and Mirabel felt its attention settle on him again, and this time it was not curious.

This time it was hungry.

*"Keep it,"* the voice said, and now there was nothing borrowed in it at all, nothing warm, only patience stretched thin as wire. *"Spend it freely, little stormcaller. Every spark you burn is a spark I will eventually collect. I have waited forty years in a crown. I can wait out one angry boy."*

It began to move—not toward them, Mirabel realized with a fresh surge of horror, but *around* them, a wide unhurried arc, circling the watchtower the way a wolf circles a fire it has learned will not burn forever. Toward the open hatch. Toward the vault that stood unsealed behind them, full of the best parts of a king, breathing its cold cedar breath into the dark.

"Close it," Sef shouted. "We can't hold it open and fight—we *close it*, we come back with the loyalists and proper wards—"

"We may not get a chance to come back!" Odile had her sword out, absurdly, a forty-year-old blade against a hole in the world.

"Then we lose everything *now* instead!"

Marette was ten feet from the hatch. The cases were heavy. The darkness was arcing wider, patient, its edge beginning to reach toward the tower wall where the vault's open throat waited, and Mirabel stood in the center of his own small storm with rain starting—actual rain, the first rain in months, falling soft and impossible on the dead heather—and understood that whatever he did in the next ten seconds, he would spend the rest of his life doing it.

The Regent's eye paused at the edge of the circle of light.

And began, deliberately, to pour itself toward the open vault.
