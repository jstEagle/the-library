## 24. The Starless City

The road to Corvale ran straight as a ruled line, and that was the first wrong thing about it.

Mirabel noticed it from the crest of the last hill, where the highway dropped away toward the plain and the capital sat on it like a stone set in gray velvet. Twenty years of valley roads had taught him what roads should look like—crooked, argumentative, worn into ruts by generations of carts that each chose their own path. This road had no ruts. It had been scraped, graded, widened, and its edges were trimmed so neatly that wildflowers grew in a border exactly one hand-span wide, as if even the weeds had been given instructions.

"It used to be called the Pilgrim's Way," Sef said beside him. She hadn't slept well; her voice had a sanded-down quality, like wood rubbed too long. "Pilgrims walked it for six hundred years. There were shrines every mile. Way-stations. Carvings in the stones where travelers marked their passing." She gestured at the immaculate gravel with something close to contempt. "You could dig anywhere along this route and find a saint's fingerbone or a wedding ring. The Regent had it all paved over eleven years ago. Called it *modernization*."

"Eleven years," Mirabel said.

"The tithe began quietly. A memory here, a memory there, taken from criminals first—the condemned, the mad, people nobody would miss or believe. Then debtors. Then, when there was protest, the protesters' names went onto lists, and their neighbors couldn't remember why they'd liked them." She pulled her cloak tighter. "By the time anyone understood what was happening, no one could remember enough to object."

Odile's remaining loyalists had split at the crossroads—four going north to spread word among the old cells, three coming south with Mirabel and Sef. One was a stooped former gatekeeper named Bram who remembered the capital as it had been, and he walked now with his jaw set like a man approaching a grave. The other two, twins named Hesta and Rill, had never seen the city at all and kept pointing out things they thought were beautiful: the trimmed flower borders, the white-washed milestone posts, the way the distant walls caught the lightless sky and held it.

"They're not wrong," Mirabel said quietly. "It *is* beautiful."

"That's the horror of it," Sef said. "Wait until you're inside."

* * *

They passed through the Southgate at midmorning, though morning made little difference under a sky that had forgotten how to be anything but overcast. The gate itself was tall and clean, its arch carved with the royal crest—and beneath the crest, freshly chiseled, a motto in letters that still showed pale against the older stone:

**REMEMBRANCE IS ORDER. ORDER IS PEACE.**

Bram stopped dead beneath it. His lips moved. Whatever he was reading wasn't the motto; it was everything that had been cut away to make room for it.

"Move along," said a gate-guard, bored, waving them through. He was young, healthy, clear-eyed. He looked directly at each of them with bright, untroubled attention, and Mirabel felt his skin crawl before he understood why. The guard's eyes were the eyes of a man who had never once wondered about anything.

Inside, the city opened up around them, and Mirabel understood what Sef had meant.

Corvale was clean. Not tidy—*clean*, in the way a body is clean after washing, after the dirt of living has been scrubbed from every crease. The streets were swept. The gutters ran clear. Shopfronts gleamed with fresh paint in a limited palette of whites and blues, and the signs above them were uniform: same height, same font, same distance apart, hanging in rows like soldiers at attention. Where older buildings stood, their facades had been restored to some imagined perfection, every crack filled, every stain bleached out, every century of weathering erased.

And there were no old buildings. That was the thing that took him longest to see. A city twenty centuries old should have shown its age somewhere—in a leaning wall, a sagging eave, a street that wandered because it had once been a footpath. But Corvale stood straight-backed and identical, district after district, as if the whole city had been built in a single afternoon by someone who had read about cities but never lived in one.

"Where are the tenements?" Bram asked hoarsely. "The tanneries were here—right here, you could smell them from the gate. And the Drowned Quarter, the poor streets down by the river—" He turned in a slow circle. "Gone. All of it gone. They built *gardens*."

He was right. Where the map in Sef's head insisted a warren of poverty had stood, there was now a public park, manicured, dotted with benches and fountains, populated by citizens strolling in clean clothes. Children chased a ball across grass that had probably never known a mud puddle. Their laughter rang out, bright and ordinary, and it was the laughter that finally frightened Mirabel—not because it was false, but because it wasn't. These children were happy. They simply didn't know there had ever been anything else to be.

A woman sold roasted chestnuts from a cart near the fountain. Her stall was spotless, her apron starched, her smile ready. When Hesta bought a paper cone of them, the woman counted change with quick competence and said, "Lovely day, isn't it?" with genuine warmth.

"What day is it?" Mirabel asked her, testing.

"Thursday," she said promptly. Then she paused, and behind her bright eyes something flickered—a hesitation, a small dark room where a thought should have been. "It's... Thursday," she repeated, less certainly. "Every day is Thursday." She laughed, a little too loudly. "That's a joke. I don't know why I said that. Lovely day!"

She turned to her next customer with the same ready smile, and Mirabel walked away with the chestnuts burning in his pocket like coals.

* * *

They lodged that night in an inn Sef knew from her archive days—the Gilded Anchor, which survived, she guessed, because it was too ordinary to be worth improving. Its sign was crooked. Its floors creaked. Inside, it smelled of tallow and old beer, and Mirabel could have wept with relief at the mess of it.

The common room was full, and the talk was the strangest part. Because the people talked. They laughed, argued over dice, complained about prices, gossiped about a baker's new wife. It was almost normal—until you listened closely and heard what was missing. No one spoke of the past. Not the king's jubilee, not the flood two winters back, not their grandparents, not their childhoods except in the flattest terms. When an old man tried to tell a story about his army days, his companions smiled politely and let the words wash past them like rain off glass, and eventually he trailed off, looking confused, as if he'd forgotten mid-sentence why he'd started.

"Watch," Sef murmured. A city crier had mounted the steps of the town hall across the square. He unrolled a scroll and rang his bell.

"Hear ye! By order of the Crown: this week's Schedule of Remembrance has been posted. Citizens whose names appear will present themselves at the Hall of Records within three days. Attendance is mandatory. Remembrance is Order. Order is Peace."

No one in the square so much as looked up. Dice kept rolling. Beer kept pouring. A woman shushed her child without glancing at the crier, the way you'd ignore a church bell you'd heard your whole life.

"They think it's normal," Rill whispered. "They think everyone does this."

"They don't think anything," Sef said. "That's the point. Thinking requires a past to think *with*. Take a person's memories and leave them their skills, their manners, their cheer—you get exactly this. Functional. Pleasant. Empty." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "The Regent doesn't want corpses. Corpses can't pay taxes. It wants a kingdom of the well-kept."

Mirabel looked out the window at the lamplit square, at the citizens strolling home through streets their great-grandparents would not recognize, humming songs whose origins no one knew. Somewhere in that palace on the hill, a thing wearing a king's face was eating a nation one evening at a time, and the nation thanked it for the meal.

His hand found the ember pendant at his throat. It was warm. It had been warmer lately, he realized—as if something inside it stirred when he came closer to the palace, the way a sleeper stirs before waking.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The archive."

Sef followed his gaze to the hill, where the royal palace rose above the city, vast and pale and lit from within by a light that cast no shadows.

"Yes," she said. "Tomorrow we burn our way into history."

But neither of them saw the figure across the square, standing motionless beneath the town hall clock—a collector in gray livery, its face smooth and patient, watching the inn's windows with the fixed attention of something that had already counted who was inside.

* * *

Morning came gray and immediate, the way it did now everywhere. They dressed, ate, and joined the river of citizens moving toward the city center, and no one gave them a second glance—except, Mirabel noticed, everyone gave them exactly one glance. Quick, bright, assessing, then away. As if some quiet instruction lived in every skull: *notice strangers, report nothing, forget noticing.*

The Hall of Records squatted at the base of the palace hill like a fat tick. Sef had explained the plan twice on the walk: her credentials were real, her name still in the rolls—disgraced archivists weren't struck from the registry, merely flagged—and the weekly record-burning was due today. She would walk in as staff. Mirabel would come as her hired porter, hood up, hauling the document chest they'd bought yesterday. Bram and the twins would wait outside and cause a distraction if needed.

"You understand," Sef said at the Hall's steps, low and fast, "that once I show my seal, there's no walking it back. If the Regent's people check the flag on my file, we're finished. And if we're finished, the king stays hollow, and the thirteenth moon turns, and everything your mother spent herself to protect ends in a tidy little room with a motto over the door."

"I understand," Mirabel said.

"Do you?" Her eyes searched his face with an intensity that had become familiar and that he pretended not to notice. "Because I've been thinking about what you did at the third vault. What you're willing to do. And I need to know that when it comes time to run, you'll actually run."

"I ran from one place for twenty years," Mirabel said. "I'm not good at it."

Something crossed her face—too fast to name—and then she squared her shoulders, touched her hidden seal, and led him up the steps into the Hall of Records.

Inside smelled of ash.

Not smoke—ash, old and ground-in, worked into the very grain of the floors, the shelves, the air itself, a smell so constant the clerks presumably no longer noticed it. Long tables stretched away under gray light. Clerks bent over them, sorting documents into two piles with brisk efficiency: keep and burn. The keep-piles were small. The burn-piles were mountains.

At the far end, a great iron stove glowed behind a grate, and every few minutes a clerk rose, gathered an armload of history, and fed it in. Deeds. Letters. Ledgers. Birth records. Mirabel watched a woman feed in a bundle of children's drawings, yellowed and curling, and return to her desk without a flicker of expression.

"Porters to the left," said a supervisor without looking up. "Manifests on the table. Chests weighed on exit."

Sef produced her seal with steady hands. The supervisor took it, pressed it into a wax pad, and consulted a ledger—Mirabel's heart stopped while the man's finger slid down columns of names—and then stamped the wax and handed it back.

"Archivist Vane. Flagged, not struck." He looked at her for the first time, and his eyes were bright and empty and faintly puzzled, like a man trying to remember a dream. "You used to work here."

"I did."

"What was it like?"

Sef's voice didn't waver. "Dustier," she said. "This way, porter."

They moved deep into the stacks, past the working tables, into the older sections where the shelving rose into shadow and the ash-smell thickened. Here the burn schedule hadn't reached yet—or had been interrupted, or forgotten, because the Regent's tidiness apparently had limits even it couldn't see past. Dust lay undisturbed in gray felt. Sef counted aisles under her breath, turned left, right, left again, and stopped before a shelf of ledger boxes labeled in faded ink: **CROWN CORRESPONDENCE — RESTRICTED — YEAR OF THE LONG RAIN**.

"This is it," she breathed. "The year the Regent arrived. If any record of the true succession survived the purges, it'll be here—or it'll tell us where it was moved before the burning started."

She lifted down the first box. Mirabel set down the document chest and laid his hands flat against the shelf, listening—not with his ears but with the thing in his blood, the storm-sense that had flooded back into him the night the wards fell. The palace hill above them was threaded with power, old and deep, and underneath it, far below, something else: a slow, rhythmic pulse, patient as digestion.

The ember pendant flared hot against his sternum.

"Mira—" Sef started to say his assumed name, and stopped, because the lamps in the aisle had just dimmed, all together, as if the building had drawn breath.

Down the length of the stacks, between the shelves, the gray light changed texture. It didn't darken so much as *empty*—the way the sky had emptied of stars. And into that emptiness walked a figure in gray livery, smooth-faced, unhurried, carrying a ledger of its own.

The supervisor's voice floated distantly from the front hall, pleasant and bored: "Collector! So soon? We posted the schedule only Monday—"

The collector did not answer. It turned its head with the slow precision of a compass needle and looked directly down the aisle at Mirabel Okafor.

In its smooth hands, the ledger opened itself. And Mirabel felt, rather than heard, a voice like dry paper turning:

***Found you.***

The ember burned. The storm in his blood rose, howling, against the inside of his skin—and Mirabel reached for the shelf, for the box, for forty years of buried truth, as the lights went out.
