## 25. The Palace of Forgetting

The capital did not smell like a city. It smelled like ash.

Mirabel noticed it before anything else—the thin, sweet char on the wind as they came down off the ridge with the morning fog, riding in a hay cart Odile's people had procured from a farmer who no longer remembered owning a farm. Beneath the ash was something else: bread baking, horse dung, river water, all the ordinary smells of ten thousand lives. But the ash sat over everything like a lid.

"It burns every seventh day," Sef said beside him, not looking at him, looking instead at the skyline where the palace rose out of the haze like a jaw of white teeth. "The records. I told you that."

"You said it," Mirabel agreed. "I don't think I understood it until now."

The city itself was clean. That was the horror of it, and it took Mirabel another mile of walking to name what was wrong: there were no old buildings. Or rather, there were old buildings, but they had been scrubbed—facades renewed, plaques replaced with blank bronze, statues whose inscriptions had been chiseled away leaving only smooth rectangles of pale stone where words used to live. The streets were wide and swept. The gutters ran clear. Children played a game with chalk lines, and when Mirabel slowed to watch, he saw that the hopscotch squares had numbers but the counting rhyme they sang had no names in it—no kings, no battles, no grandmothers, nothing older than last spring.

"They're bright-eyed," he murmured, repeating what he'd written in his own head for days. "Vacant." He'd thought he was prepared. He hadn't been.

"Don't stare," Sef said. "Staring gets you reported. And don't look sad. Sadness is also reportable."

"What feeling is allowed?"

Sef considered this with genuine scholarly care, which was somehow worse than if she'd joked. "Contentment," she said finally. "Mild contentment. The Regent likes its subjects comfortable. Fear sours the memory. Grief makes people hold on to things. Contentment lets go easily."

They walked. The palace grew. And Mirabel kept his face arranged into mild contentment while everything in him screamed.

* * *

Sef Vane had not been inside the Royal Archive in six years, and she had expected to feel grief. What she felt instead was vertigo—the specific vertigo of returning to a place that has been rearranged by someone who hated you.

The credentials still worked. That was the first shock. She had rehearsed eleven different ways to talk her way past the gate, and none of them proved necessary; she pressed her copper seal into the reader's plate at the Archive's side door, and the plate glowed amber, and the door unbarred itself with a sound like a sigh.

"Junior Archivist, Third Rank, Vane, Sef," intoned the door, in the flat ceremonial voice the founders had given it a century ago. "Welcome back. Your last recorded entry was—" a pause, a whir of something mechanical behind the wall, "—six years, two months, eleven days ago."

"Hello, Door," Sef said, because the archivists had always talked to the Door; it was tradition, and traditions were the only things left worth defending.

"The schedule has changed since your last visit," the Door said. "Please review the posted notices. Unauthorized retention of documents is punishable under Statute Nine. Have a productive day."

"Notices," Sef repeated softly, once they were through and the door had sealed behind them. "It never used to have notices."

The corridor beyond was familiar and wrong. The Archive had been built downward—a tower inverted, floors descending into the bedrock, because paper feared fire and stone did not. Sef had spent her twenties in those descending halls, cataloguing under lamps that burned cold blue witchfire, surrounded by four hundred years of the kingdom's memory: tax rolls and love letters, treaties and recipes, coronation oaths and a fisherman's complaint about the price of eels in the year the old king died. Everything mattered. That had been the doctrine. *Everything matters, because you do not know yet what will.*

Now the doctrine hung on the wall of the entry hall, repainted in cheerful gold letters:

**THE PAST IS A BURDEN GRACIOUSLY SET DOWN.**

Below it, the posted notice. Sef read it twice so she could recite it later, verbatim, to whoever would listen:

*By order of the Crown: weekly combustion of redundant records occurs each Seventhday in the Great Yard. Categories scheduled for combustion are posted in advance. Citizens are reminded that memory is a gift the Crown holds in trust. Hoarding the past is hoarding against your neighbors. A light heart needs no library.*

"A light heart needs no library," Mirabel read over her shoulder. His voice was very quiet. "Who wrote that?"

"Somebody who got paid," Sef said. "That's always the answer to bad writing."

She started down the first stair, and stopped, because the stairwell was lined with alcoves, and the alcoves were empty.

Not mostly empty. Not even half empty. Empty the way a mouth is empty after teeth have been pulled—all those dark sockets where the shelves had been, and pale rectangles on the stone where shelf-labels had been pried off. She counted as she descended. Forty alcoves on the first flight. Thirty-one empty. On the second flight, thirty-eight of forty. By the fourth flight down, the shelves held nothing but dust arranged in neat, deliberate drifts, swept into rows by someone conscientious.

"They didn't even burn them here," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "There's no scorching. They carried them up. Four hundred years of records, carried up these stairs by hand, and burned in the yard, and then they swept."

"Maybe it was kinder this way," Mirabel said. "Faster."

"Nothing about this is kind." She heard the edge in her own voice and couldn't blunt it. "You don't understand what was down here. There was a room—Floor Nine, West—that held nothing but the correspondence of a woman named Hetta Brandt, who ran a bakery in the low city sixty years ago and wrote letters to her sister every week for forty years. Nothing happened to her. No scandal, no war, no plague. She just... lived. Made bread. Argued with her sister about whether their mother had been fair. And we kept every letter, because the doctrine says everything matters, and some future historian might need to know what bread cost, or how sisters loved each other, or—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "Nine floors, Mirabel. I catalogued Floor Nine myself. Shelf by shelf. It took me two years. I knew her handwriting better than my own by the end. And now it's smoke, and the smoke is probably over the river by now, and nobody will ever know whether the sisters made peace."

Mirabel put a hand on her shoulder. She almost shook it off. She didn't.

"And the worst part," she said, quieter now, "is that I understand the logic. That's what I can't forgive. If you want to rule forever, history is your enemy. Every record is a witness. Every witness is a threat. So you burn the witnesses—and you call it mercy, you call it setting the burden down, and the people thank you, because remembering hurts, and being told you don't have to remember feels like being loved." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like closing a ledger. "My mentor taught me the doctrine. *Everything matters.* And then he taught me the other thing too, the unwritten one: an archive is an act of faith that there will be a future to read it. Burn the archive and you're saying there won't be. You're saying the present is permanent. That's the real crime. Not the loss. The arrogance."

They stood in the empty stairwell, two small figures in a throat of stone, and somewhere far above them, faintly, through feet of rock, they could hear the city going about its contented day.

"We're here for a reason," Mirabel said gently. "Tell me the reason before I start breaking things."

* * *

The reason was a room that should not have existed.

Sef led him down to Floor Eleven, past the empty alcoves, to a plain iron door with no label. She pressed her seal to it. Amber light. The lock turned.

"This was the Restricted Stacks," she whispered. "Access required Second Rank or above. I was Third. I got in exactly once, as an apprentice, carrying boxes for my mentor. I memorized the layout in ninety seconds because I knew I'd never be allowed back." She pushed the door open. "And this is why the credentials matter. The Regent burned the public records. But it never emptied this room. Do you know why?"

Inside, lamplight revealed shelves upon shelves—not empty, not stripped, but crowded, chaotic, overflowing with documents stacked sideways and double-deep, scrolls in bins, folios in towers. Dust lay thick as snowfall. Spiderwebs bridged the aisles like rigging.

"No," Mirabel said. "Why?"

"Because the Regent doesn't read." Sef stepped inside, and her voice took on the cadence of lecture, which Mirabel had learned was how she handled fear. "It eats. Memory is its food, and written records are only useful to it as bait—as traps for people who come looking. It left the Restricted Stacks intact the way a hunter leaves a snare intact. Anyone loyal enough to sneak down here, anyone who still cared about the truth, would eventually find this room. And then the watchers would find *them*." She pulled a lamp from its bracket and lit it with a taper. "Odile's cell lost three people to this room over the decades. Two collectors' lists, one informant. The Regent doesn't guard the truth. It gardens it. Lets it grow, and harvests the gardeners."

"So why are we here?"

"Because I am not a gardener." Sef set the lamp on a table and began pulling documents with swift, practiced hands. "I'm a rat. Rats don't get harvested; they're beneath notice. And because I know how my mentor filed things—which means I know how the *old* archive was organized, before the Regent reorganized everything above us into nonsense. The king's memories, the ones the loyalists smuggled out forty years ago—they went through this room. Records of the transfers would have been made. Transfer manifests. Vault assignments. My mentor signed them; I've seen his signature ten thousand times. If any copy survived the purges, it's here, misfiled, because the Regent's clerks couldn't read the old system." She looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "We lost the third vault. We lost Odile. We cannot afford to walk into the palace blind. Somewhere in this room is a map of where the rest of the king is buried. And I am going to find it if it takes all night."

"All night," Mirabel agreed, taking the opposite side of the table. "Then let's work."

* * *

They worked.

It was, Mirabel would think later, the strangest night of his strange year: crouched in a buried room full of dead paper, while above them the kingdom slept its starless sleep, and somewhere in the white palace overhead the thing wearing the crown dreamed—or whatever passed for dreaming in a creature made entirely of appetite.

Sef taught him the old filing system as they went—the mnemonic lattice, the color-coded spines, the way dates folded into subject headings like rivers into deltas. He was slower than her, clumsier, but he had a storm-caller's patience for pattern: lightning finds the path of least resistance, and so does information. By the second hour he could triage a stack in seconds. By the fourth, he'd found three documents in his mentor-to-be's hand—Sef's mentor, though Mirabel still hadn't said the name aloud, sensing the wound under it—and set them aside without comment.

What he found instead, in the margins of unrelated files, was the texture of the resistance. Little marks. A pressed flower between treaty pages, forty years browned. A child's drawing of a lighthouse tucked into a customs ledger. Notes in a dozen hands: *moved to salt site, all well.* *Third bell, west door, tell no one.* People had fought for this room. People had hidden things in it the way animals hide food against winter, and most of them had been caught, and the things they hid had waited in the dark ever since, patient as seeds.

Around midnight by Sef's water-clock, Mirabel opened a folio and went still.

"Sef."

"Mm." She didn't look up.

"Sef. Look at this."

She looked. The folio was a shipping manifest, mundane, decades old—crates of candle wax, inbound from the coast. But across the bottom, in a hand Mirabel recognized now from three other documents, someone had written a single line in cipher. Sef read it, and her breath caught.

"That's his cipher. The mentor's personal cipher—I've seen fragments, never the key." Her finger traced the line without touching it. "But I know enough of it to make out the shape. It's not cargo. It's a count." She went pale in the lamplight. "Mirabel. This is a list of vaults. Not three. Not four. It says—"

She stopped. Read it again. Her lips moved.

"How many?" Mirabel asked.

"Seven," she whispered. "It says seven. Seven vaults. We've found two. The Regent drank the third. Which means there are four more caches of the king's memory scattered across the kingdom, and no one alive knows where they are except—" she tapped the cipher, "—a dead man, in a code nobody has broken."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

Then, from far above—through eleven floors of stone, through the sleeping palace, faint as a heartbeat—came a sound neither of them could mistake.

Bells. One bell, struck once, deep and slow.

Sef's head came up like a deer's. "That's not the hour bell," she breathed. "That's the summons bell. It hasn't rung in six years. It rings for—" She swallowed. "It rings for royal audiences. For people summoned into the king's presence."

"But nobody knows we're here."

"Nobody knows we're *anywhere*," Sef whispered. "Which means it isn't for us. Unless—"

The lamp guttered again, and this time it did not recover, and in the sudden dark of the buried room, Mirabel felt the ember pendant against his chest grow warm—warm as a living hand—and heard, very faintly, his mother's voice say a word he did not know, in a language he had never learned.

Above them, the bell rang a second time.

And a third time rang for them.
