## 26. The Hollow King

They came for him at dawn, though there was no dawn to speak of—only the hour when the starless sky lightened from ash to bone.

Mirabel heard them on the stairs of the safehouse first: boots, measured and unhurried, the cadence of men who had never once needed to run. Sef was already awake. She had been awake all night, he suspected, her back against the wall with her mentor's confession hidden in the lining of her coat, her eyes fixed on the door as if she could hold it shut by staring.

"Four sets of feet," she said quietly. "No armor clink. They're not soldiers."

"Then what are they?"

"Worse. Bureaucrats."

The knock, when it came, was almost gentle. Three soft raps, like a neighbor asking to borrow sugar. Odile's remaining loyalists had melted into the city two days ago—one had gone north with the empty vials wrapped in oilcloth, one had simply vanished between one street and the next—and that left Mirabel and Sef alone in a rented room above a dye-shop that smelled of vinegar and old mordant, with nowhere left to be inconspicuous. The Regent's Eye had found them at the third vault's threshold. After that, Mirabel understood, hiding had been a courtesy extended to them, nothing more. A game of blindfold played by something that could see through cloth.

Sef put her hand on his arm as he moved toward the door. "You don't have to open it."

"If they wanted me dead, I'd already be dead." He tried for a smile and felt it come out crooked. "And if they want me alive, then whatever's behind this door wants something. And things that want something can be talked to."

"Things that want something," Sef said, "can also lie."

The door opened onto four figures in dove-gray livery, unadorned except for a small stitched sigil at the collar: a crown drawn as an outline only, hollow at its center. Their faces were pleasant. That was the worst part. In the valley, Mirabel had imagined the Regent's servants as gaunt and gray-eyed, hollowed like their master. But these were ordinary people—middle-aged, well-fed, one of them with laugh lines around her mouth. They looked like people who remembered their children's names.

"The Okafor heir," said the woman, reading from a card she did not need to read. "His Majesty requests your presence at court."

"Requests," Mirabel repeated.

"You will be treated with every courtesy," she said, and her voice had the smooth, worn quality of a sentence said many times. "You will not be harmed. You will be returned to this address afterward, should you wish it." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "Should you survive the audience."

"And if I refuse?"

The woman looked at him with genuine, mild puzzlement—the look of someone asked whether water might decline to be wet. "He is the king," she said.

*He is the king.* Four words, and under them, faint as a watermark, Mirabel heard what they really meant: *there has never been anything else; there never will be.* He thought of the tithe-collectors on the moors, of the woman who had sold the memory of her daughter's face for bread, of villages where no one could recall last winter. It wasn't fear that held the kingdom. It was this—politeness, procedure, the terrible tidiness of a thing that had eaten doubt itself.

"I'll come," he said.

Behind him, Sef pulled on her coat. The woman in gray regarded her without hostility. "The archivist Vane is expected as well," she said. "His Majesty remembers—" and here she stopped, and something flickered across her pleasant face, some small static of confusion, gone as fast as it came. "His Majesty's records note your service."

*His Majesty's records.* As if the king were a building one kept accounts for.

They walked out into the starless morning, escorted by four kind-faced strangers, toward the palace at the heart of the city that had forgotten how to want anything.

* * *

The Starless City was worse in daylight. At night it had merely been dark; now, under a sky the color of unwashed wool, Mirabel could see the details he'd missed—the way the streets were swept too clean, no litter, no leaves, as if even autumn had been audited. The way shopkeepers smiled at passersby with smiles of identical width. The way children played games with rules that seemed to shift mid-game, because none of them could remember which rules they'd agreed to, and none of them minded.

"They're not unhappy," Sef murmured beside him. She had gone pale. "That's what I couldn't make the loyalists understand. They're not suffering. They're *content*. He doesn't take joy—he takes depth. Everything still tastes like something. Nothing tastes like very much."

"Is that better or worse?"

"Worse," Sef said. "Pain makes people ask questions."

The palace rose ahead of them, white stone gone slightly yellow, its spires cut off at strange angles—as if the architects had begun towers and forgotten why. Guards flanked the gates, but they did not check papers. They did not check anything. One of them looked at Mirabel as he passed, and for a moment Mirabel saw in the man's eyes a vast, patient vacancy, like a room after the furniture has been sold, and he understood that these guards were not guarding the king from threats.

They were guarding the king from himself. Whatever was left of him.

Inside, the corridors were warm and quiet and smelled of beeswax. Servants glided past with trays. Portraits lined the walls—generations of monarchs, and here and there a gap: a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where a frame had been removed. Sef stopped before one gap and stared at it, and her hand rose halfway to her mouth.

"That was Aldous's coronation portrait," she whispered. "I catalogued it. It hung there for thirty years."

"Not anymore," said their escort pleasantly, and walked on.

* * *

The throne room doors were oak banded in bronze, tall enough to admit a man on horseback, and they opened without a sound. Beyond lay a hall built for a thousand courtiers and holding perhaps forty: gray-livered officials standing in loose clusters, murmuring to one another in voices pitched too low to carry, like wind through wheat. At the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of faded velvet, stood the throne.

King Aldous sat upon it.

Mirabel had steeled himself for a monster. He had spent weeks imagining the Hollow Regent—a coiled parasite behind the royal face, something he would see if he looked closely, some wrongness in the pupils, some suggestion of teeth. What he had not prepared for was how *ordinary* the king looked. An old man, tall once but settling now into his chair like sand into an hourglass, dressed in robes of state that fit him loosely, as though tailored for a larger version of himself. His hair was white and fine. His hands rested on the arms of the throne, and they were beautiful hands—long-fingered, ink-stained at the nails, a scholar's hands.

His face was the face from the coins and the proclamations and the portraits that hadn't been taken down yet. Kind eyes. A patient mouth. The face of a grandfather.

It was the emptiness behind it that made Mirabel's skin crawl. Not vacancy—vacancy he could have borne. This was worse. The king's gaze moved across the hall with perfect attention, resting briefly on each official, each guard, each servant, and Mirabel realized the old man was doing what Mirabel did when he entered a new room: taking inventory. Counting exits. Reading faces.

Except the king wasn't reading anything. He was performing reading. The attention moved like water over glass—touching everything, holding nothing.

"He's in there," Sef breathed. "Gods help us, he's *in there*, watching himself do it."

A chamberlain struck the floor with a staff. "The Okafor heir," he announced, "and the archivist Vane, presented to His Majesty's justice and mercy."

The hall went silent. Forty pairs of pleasant eyes turned.

Mirabel walked the length of the floor alone—Sef had been directed, with another of those courteous impossibilities, to stand among the clerks along the wall—and stopped at the foot of the dais, and bowed, because his legs decided to before his pride could object.

"Your Majesty."

"Okafor." The voice was mild, unhurried, a voice made for reading aloud to grandchildren. "You've come a long way. Ember Valley, my letters say. Do you know, I have never seen Ember Valley? I am told it rains beautifully there." A small smile. "I am told many things. I find I believe most of them. It saves time."

Something in Mirabel's chest twisted. He had rehearsed fury on the walk over. He had composed speeches about stolen memories and murdered stars and a kingdom sleepwalking into permanent noon. And now he stood in front of the ruin of it all and found he could not shout at a grandfather.

"Why did you summon me?" he asked. "You know what I am. Your collectors named me in their letters. The Okafor heir. The last bloodline you can't digest. Why bring me here instead of sending the Eye?"

The king considered this with the air of a man considering a chess move he found mildly charming. "Because I wished to look at you," he said. "Do you know, you are the only thing in this kingdom I cannot taste?" He leaned forward slightly, and the movement had a hunger in it so controlled, so exquisitely leashed, that Mirabel's storm-magic woke in his chest like a startled bird. "Everything else is broth. Warm, nourishing broth. But you—" the king's nostrils flared, delicately, "you are a closed book written in a language I burned out of myself trying to learn. Storm-calling. Your mother's line. Memory given flesh instead of kept in the head, where honest parasites can reach it." He settled back. "I wanted to see what indigestion looks like."

"You're going to eat him anyway," Mirabel said. "When the thirteenth moon turns. When there's nothing left of Aldous to finish."

The king laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and real-sounding, and that was the most horrifying thing yet—that the parasite had learned laughter so thoroughly it could spend it like coin. "Eat *him*?" The king spread his beautiful hands. "My dear boy. There is no *him*. There is a crown, and there is a shape that holds it, and there are forty years of meals. When the last of the shape dissolves, I will simply be the crown, and the crown will be the kingdom, and the kingdom will be at peace forever. No more winters of discontent. No more wars of succession. No more grief." His voice softened into something almost tender. "You cannot grieve what you cannot remember. I have done this kingdom the greatest kindness any ruler has ever done it. I have ended its history. History is where all the pain lives."

"History is where all the *people* live," Mirabel said.

For a moment—just a moment—the smile on the king's face did not change, but everything underneath it shifted, the way a pond shifts when something large turns over far below. The mild eyes fixed on Mirabel with an attention that had weight, that pressed on him physically, and Mirabel felt, for one vertiginous second, that he was being read page by page by something older than the dynasty, older than the city, patient as erosion.

Then it was gone. The grandfather returned, apologetic, adjusting his robes.

"You'll forgive me," the king said. "Conversations excite me. Everything excites me, briefly. Nothing stays exciting." He sighed, and the sigh was so perfectly human that Mirabel hated him for it. "I summoned you for a reason beyond curiosity, of course. I am told you have been collecting things. Little glass vials. Memories of mine, scattered by sentimental traitors across my own kingdom like a drunkard scattering coins." He tilted his head. "I would like them back."

"They're his," Mirabel said. "Whatever's left of him. They're not yours to want."

"But I do want them," the king said, with the simple, devastating honesty of a child. "Wanting is nearly all I have left. Won't you give them to me?"

"No."

The word fell into the silence of the hall like a stone into a well. Along the walls, the gray officials had stopped murmuring. Every pleasant face was turned toward the dais, watching, and Mirabel understood suddenly that he was not the first person to stand here and refuse. He was simply the first whose refusal had ever mattered.

The king studied him for a long moment. Then, gently, sadly, he said: "That was the wrong answer. Or possibly the right one. I genuinely no longer remember which answers please me." He raised one ink-stained hand, and the chamberlain stepped forward. "Escort our guest to the—"

And the ember pendant flared.

It happened without warning. The pendant had slept against Mirabel's chest since the salt cathedral, warm as a held hand, silent since Wrenna placed it over his head. Now it blazed through his shirt like a coal brought to bellows, and light spilled gold across the marble floor, and the whole throne room flinched—every gray liveried figure recoiling half a step, the chamberlain raising a forearm against the glow, the guards' hands flying to sword hilts.

But Mirabel saw none of it. Because the king had risen from the throne.

King Aldous stood swaying, gripping the arm of the throne, and his face had *changed*. The pleasant blankness cracked down the middle like river ice in spring, and behind the crack something moved—something drowning. His eyes, which had touched everything and held nothing, locked onto the ember with an intensity that hurt to witness, and his mouth opened, and what came out was not the mild voice of the crown.

"*Rain,*" the king whispered. "There was—I remember—it was raining, and she was dancing, no, *I* was dancing, in the courtyard, and the thunder didn't frighten anyone because—" His voice broke, splintered, became two voices arguing inside one throat. "Where is it. Where is the rest of it. Give it back, give it *back*, I had a daughter who—no. No, that's not mine, that's not—" His hands tore at his own sleeves. "Who is standing there? Who is—your pendant. Boy. *Boy.* Where did you get that light?"

"Aldous," Mirabel said, and took one step up the dais.

The king's eyes found his, and for three heartbeats—three heartbeats in which the entire hall did not breathe—Mirabel looked into the face of a man, a real man, terrified and lucid and drowning in a body that had been eaten around him for forty years. The man's lips shaped words without sound. Mirabel leaned close enough to feel the heat of the ember-light on his own cheeks, and read them:

*Don't let it finish me.*

Then the light guttered. The ember dimmed to its usual banked warmth, and the crack sealed over, and the grandfather settled back into the throne like a man resuming a nap, smoothing his robes, arranging his kind face.

"How curious," the Hollow Regent said pleasantly. "Static. Old recordings have static." Its gaze lifted to Mirabel, and beneath the courtesy, for the first time, Mirabel saw the thing plainly: appetite wearing patience like a mask worn so long it had grown into the skin. "You will leave the vials somewhere I can find them, Okafor heir. Or you will keep them, and I will come to collect. Either way, we will conclude our business before the moon does." The smile widened by exactly the width of politeness. "In fact—since you interest me so much—let us discuss terms. Privately. Now."

The chamberlain gestured, and the great doors began to swing shut, and Sef's face across the hall had gone the color of paper.

The Regent folded its stolen hands and said, in the king's warm voice:

"There is something you want, too. Something buried. Shall we talk about your mother?"
