## 27. The Regent's Offer

I have worn this crown for forty years, and in all that time I have learned one truth above all others: every mind has a door.

Some doors are iron—soldiers who have burned their own childhoods to survive a war, widows who have nailed their grief shut from the inside. Some doors are paper dressed as iron, and those are my favorites, because paper burns so beautifully. But the finest doors, the ones I savor the way a glutton savors a long table, are the doors that do not know they are doors. The minds that believe themselves whole. The men and women who walk through their days certain that everything they are was always theirs.

I open those slowly. From the inside, it feels like nothing at all. A winter here. A face there. A name that stops coming when called. By the time they notice the rooms going dark, they have already forgotten what light was for.

The boy standing before the throne does not have a door.

He has a wall.

I felt him the moment he entered my city—a wrongness in the taste of the air, like lightning bottled and left to steep for twenty years. Storm-calling. The last bloodline that resists me, the line I swallowed once and could not keep down. His mother's magic curdled on my tongue like milk gone to thunder, and I spat her son out beyond the valley's wards and told myself patience. Patience is the oldest thing about me. Older than the crown. Older than the name Aldous, which I wear now the way a hermit crab wears a shell it did not build and cannot leave.

And now the boy stands in my throne room with his mother's ember burning against his chest, and the hollow king beside me—the poor, scraped-clean remnant I have fed on for four decades—flickers. For one heartbeat his eyes find their focus, and something ancient and terrified moves behind them, and I feel it like a splinter working loose: *he remembers something.*

The ember. Of course. Her last preserved memory, sealed in glass, humming with all the power she spent to hide him. I can smell it from here. It smells like rain on hot stone. It smells like the only meal I ever failed to finish.

*You will not have it,* says the remnant, faintly, from somewhere deep inside the face we share. He says very little these days. Most of him is in vials scattered across my kingdom like seed grain, waiting for a spring I intend never to come. But tonight he says it, and tonight I let him, because I am busy.

I am looking at the boy.

Mirabel Okafor. Twenty years old, give or take the years the valley stole from the counting. Tall the way reeds are tall—bent by weather but not broken by it. His hands hang at his sides and even from here I can see the calluses of honest work, roof-mending work, small deliberate work, the work of a man who taught himself to be little because no one told him he was allowed to be large. His eyes are his mother's exactly. Dark, quick, always measuring the exits. She measured exits too, at the end. There were none, so she built one out of herself.

I remember eating her. That is not quite true—I remember *trying*. She came to me across the palace floor with her storm in her fists and her dying spell already half-spoken, and when I reached into her for the memory of her son's name, I found a wall where a door should have been. She had paid with her whole self, the old woman in the valley said once, and she was more right than she knew. Wrenna. I will deal with Wrenna later. First, the boy.

Because here is what I know, and what none of them in this room fully understands—not the guards, not the trembling archivist's ghost of a reputation lingering on the boy's companion, not even the remnant muttering inside my borrowed skull:

I know exactly what he wants.

Not victory. Not justice. Boys like this do not come south for abstractions; they come for faces. And I hold more faces than any treasury in the world holds coin.

* * *

"Leave us," I say, and my voice comes out in Aldous's timbre, warm as an old coat, worn smooth by decades of use. The courtiers go. They always go quickly; I have trained them the way one trains dogs, not with cruelty but with consistency. The guards go last, and the great doors close, and then it is only the three of us—the boy, the shell, and the thing inside the shell wearing the shell like a crown inside a crown.

"You came far," I say. "Ember Valley. Four hundred miles of starless road." I rise from the throne, slow, an old man's care, though nothing about me is old except my appetite. "That is a long way to walk for a man who claims he wants nothing."

"I want the king back," the boy says. His voice does not shake. Good. Shaking would have disappointed me. "All of him."

"The king." I smile, and I feel Aldous's mouth make the shape, and I feel the remnant flinch at the sight of his own smile on his own face. That is a small cruelty I permit myself. One must have hobbies. "Which king? The one who danced in storms? The one who signed the tithe edicts? The one who forgot his mother's face on a Tuesday eleven years ago and wept without knowing why?" I descend the dais step by step. "A king is not a man, Mirabel Okafor. A king is a pile of memories wearing a title. And I have been eating that pile for forty years, one spoonful at a time, and do you know what I have learned?"

I stop three paces from him. Close enough to smell the rain on him. Close enough that the ember at his throat brightens, just slightly, like a dog raising its hackles.

"I have learned that no one misses the first hundred things you take. Grief, yes—they miss grief, oddly, they come asking for it back, as if sorrow were a heirloom. But joy? Joy goes quietly. A first snowfall. A wedding morning. The taste of a particular bread from a particular street. People surrender their joys without a fight and mourn their miseries for generations." I tilt my head, letting Aldous's gray brows fold into an expression of gentle, grandfatherly sorrow. It fits the face well. It fit the man well, once, before I wore it smooth. "Your mother understood this. It is why she hid you in a valley with no stars. Not to keep you safe from me. To keep you *small*. Small lives have fewer doors."

"That's a lie," he says. But his voice has changed key. There it is. The crack in the wall.

"It is not," I say softly, "but it hardly matters whether it is. What matters is this."

I extend my hand, palm up, and into it I pour a memory.

This is a thing I can do that frightens even me, some nights, when I sit alone in the dark of the king's skull and wonder what I am becoming: I can give back. Every stolen thing is still mine—flavor, texture, weight—and I can pour them out like wine from a jug. I choose carefully. I have been choosing for weeks, since the ward-stones shattered and I tasted the valley's magic flooding back into its thief of a son. I chose these the way a jeweler chooses stones for a ransom.

The memory takes shape between us as mist, and in the mist: a kitchen. Low ceiling, copper pots, rain hammering a window. A woman laughing—her head thrown back, flour on her dark hands, her braid coming loose—and a child of perhaps five shrieking with delight as she makes the rain on the glass dance in patterns, little galloping horses, little running men. The child's laughter. The woman's voice saying, clear as the day it was spoken: *"Again! Again, Mama!"*

I watch the boy's face as the memory plays.

I have watched ten thousand faces receive their stolen pasts, and I will tell you what I have learned about human beings, which is more than any philosopher ever learned, because philosophers only ever studied minds that were still whole: a person receiving a lost memory does not look happy. They look *wounded*. As if the memory were a limb grown back in a moment, with all the pain of regrowth compressed into a single instant. The boy's knees nearly go. He catches himself on the arm of a courtier's abandoned chair, and his breath comes ragged, and the ember at his throat flares white-hot, and somewhere inside the ruin of the man whose face I wear, the remnant whispers: *you monster,* with feeling.

Yes, I think. And your point?

"Do you know how many of those I have?" I ask him. I let the mist dissolve, deliberately, cruelly, and watch him reach for it with his whole body before it goes. "Her. Your mother. I took forty-one memories from Sefina Okafor before she walled herself away from me. Forty-one. Her wedding day. The first time she held you. The sound of your father's voice—he died when you were two; you barely remember him, and what you remember is wrong, his voice was deeper. The lullaby she sang you—you've heard its ghost on the moors, haven't you? A fragment, walking? I have the whole song. Every verse. I have the way she said your name when you were sleeping and she thought no one heard: *my little storm.*"

"Stop." His voice cracks now. Good. Better than good.

"She bargained with me, at the end," I continue, and here I lower my voice, making it intimate, conspiratorial, the voice of a man sharing a shameful secret in a confessional. "Did your precious Wrenna tell you that? No, of course not. She bargained. Her life for yours, her memories for your hiding. And I agreed, because I always honor a bargain—it is the one law I keep, the only law worth keeping—and because her memories were *magnificent*. Forty years of loving people, distilled. I have been savoring them slowly. Rationing myself. Do you understand what I am telling you, Mirabel?"

I lean close. Close enough that he can see, if he looks—and he looks, they always look—that Aldous's eyes are not empty at all. They are full. Terribly, awfully full. Full of everyone I have ever eaten.

"I still have her," I whisper. "All of her. Every memory I took is intact. Untasted, some of them. The last twenty years of her life I never got to touch—she died before I could follow her north—but the forty-one I hold are whole, and they are *hers*, and they can be *yours*."

* * *

The throne room is very quiet. Somewhere far off, a clock counts the hour, and I note with satisfaction that we are closer to the thirteenth turn of the moon than the boy knows. Time is on my side. Time has always been on my side; it is the one accomplice who has never asked for a share.

The boy stares at the space where the memory-mist stood. His hand has risen without his permission—I can see he doesn't know he's done it—to the ember at his throat. His fingers rest on the warm glass the way a drowning man rests his hand on a plank.

There it is, I think. There is the door after all. Not in his mind—in his *heart*, which is only a door made of softer wood, and burns faster.

"Here is my offer," I say, straightening, resuming the king's measured cadence, because the moment requires ceremony and ceremony is a lever like any other. "Give me the ember. Walk out of my city, out of my kingdom, back to your valley—which I will re-ward myself, with my own art, stronger than your mother's, so that no watcher, no collector, no tithe-man ever crosses its border again. You will live as you lived. Roofs. Rain. Smallness. Safety." I pause, and let the knife slide in gently, the way it must. "And once a year, on the anniversary of her bargain, I will send you one memory. One. Wrapped and sealed, delivered by no hand you need fear. In forty-one years you will have all of her. You will grow old in the valley knowing your mother's voice a little better every spring. You will teach her lullaby to children of your own, perhaps, singing words you'll finally understand."

I spread my hands—an old man's open hands, a king's generous hands.

"In exchange I ask only this: the ember, and your departure. No oath. No service. No silence even, if silence chafes you. Go and tell the world what sits on the throne of Aldous, if it pleases you—no one will believe you, and I will not chase you, and you will die warm and remembered and *whole*, which is more than any man in this starless kingdom can promise himself."

The remnant stirs inside me, horrified. *You lie.* I do not bother answering him. It is not a lie, precisely. It is simply a truth with a trapdoor under it, and all truths are, if you dig deep enough. The valley I would ward, yes. The memories I would send—one per year, yes, each one genuine, each one a treasure. And every single one delivered carrying a thread of my own substance, thin as spider silk, so that in forty-one years the boy who opened forty-one letters would be a web of silk wearing a man's shape, and the last free bloodline would end not in fire but in *correspondence*. I am patient. I told you. Patience is the oldest thing about me.

But he does not need to know that. None of them ever need to know that. That is the entire art.

"Think," I say gently, while his hand trembles on the ember. "You have spent your whole life with half a mother. A grave you can't visit. A lullaby with missing verses. An ember that shows you one image, over and over, like a beggar's coin." I let Aldous's face soften into something almost tender. Almost. I have had forty years of practice at almost. "I am offering you *her*. All of her. And all I ask is that you stop trying to unmake the only peace this kingdom has ever known."

His eyes lift to mine. Dark, quick, measuring the exits—and finding, for the first time in his life, that the exit is the offer itself. That is the cruelest architecture I know: to build the cage with the door left open, so that staying becomes a choice and choices become chains.

Inside the shared skull, the remnant of Aldous is screaming. Not words anymore. Just a sound, old and raw, the sound of a man watching a stranger dangle his friend's ghost before her son. I press him down. There will be time for him later. There is always time; that is my gift and my curse and my kingdom entire.

The boy's fingers tighten on the ember.

And the ember *answers*—flares against his palm, bright enough to throw our shadows huge up the throne room walls, and in the flare I feel it again, that taste of rain on hot stone, that curdled-thunder resistance, her magic recognizing her son's hand and *gripping*. For one instant the temperature of the room drops and the torches gutter and somewhere overhead, beyond the vaulted ceiling, beyond the starless sky, I would swear I hear the first low grumble of a storm that has no business existing in my tidy, scrubbed, forgetting kingdom.

The boy looks down at the light in his hand.

Then up at me.

And I see—with the peculiar double vision of a parasite who has spent forty years learning to read the faces it wears—that he is not looking at my offer at all. He is looking through it. Through me. At something behind my eyes, in the deep of the ruined king, where a flicker of focus still burns like a lamp in a drowned house.

"No," Mirabel Okafor says.

It is not loud. It is not heroic. It is quiet and furious and absolutely final, like the first drop before the sky splits open.

And outside the palace windows, over a city that has forgotten what weather is, the darkness begins—very softly, very impossibly—to *rain*.
