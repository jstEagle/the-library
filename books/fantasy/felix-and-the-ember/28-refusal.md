## 28. Refusal

The Regent's voice came from the king's mouth, and that was the worst of it.

Mirabel had expected something else—something wrong. A voice like ash, like static, like the starless patch of dark he'd seen crawling across the moors. But the thing wearing the crown spoke with King Aldous's own throat, warm and measured, the voice of a man who had spent forty years learning to be gracious in a body that was no longer entirely his.

"Sit," it said again, gently. "You've come a long way to stand."

The throne room stretched around them, vast and pale as the inside of a shell. No banners. No portraits. The walls had been scrubbed of everything—the regnal paintings, the lineages, the stained glass kings and queens. Even the history had been eaten here, or perhaps simply tidied away, and what remained was a room designed for one purpose: to make whoever stood in it feel small before an empty chair.

Mirabel did not sit. Sef stood at his shoulder, very still, her hands hidden in the folds of her coat where they could reach the vials. Behind them, at the far doors, two guards in royal livery waited with the patience of furniture. Odile's people were not coming. Odile was dead. The thought arrived and Mirabel set it down carefully, the way you set down something breakable when your hands are shaking.

"You know why I'm here," he said.

"I know why you *think* you're here." The Regent tilted Aldous's head, and the crown caught the gray light. "You think you've come to steal back what was taken. But I've watched you cross my kingdom, Mirabel Okafor. I've watched you open vaults and carry ghosts and grieve for a woman whose face you only know from glass." It smiled with the king's mouth, and it was a kind smile, and that was unbearable too. "I am not your enemy. I am the only creature in this palace that remembers what your mother looked like."

The words went into him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

Because it was true. That was the obscenity of it. Wrenna had told him stories, but stories wear smooth with telling; the ember held one image, one moment, one weeping king. He did not know the sound of her laugh. He did not know whether she'd been tall. Twenty years, and the parasite on the throne possessed more of her than her own son did.

"You're lying," he said, and heard how young his voice sounded.

"Am I?" The Regent rose. Aldous's body moved slowly, stiffly, an old man's body worn by decades of tenancy, and yet there was nothing frail in how it descended the dais steps. "Ask me anything. Ask me the color of her eyes. Ask me what she called the storm she died making. Ask me what she said when they carried her to the valley, half-empty already, holding you." It stopped three steps above him. Close enough that Mirabel could see the king's eyes—pale, washed-out blue, with something behind them that was not looking out. "I ate those memories forty years ago, boy. They're mine now. And I am *offering them back*."

The throne room was silent. Somewhere far off, beyond the walls, the city murmured its vacant murmur, bright-eyed and tidy.

"The ember," the Regent said. "And your departure. Walk out of my kingdom and never look back, and every memory I took from Liora Okafor is yours. All of them. Her childhood. Her first storm. The day she learned your name before you had one." Its voice softened, and the softness was the cruelest instrument Mirabel had ever heard. "You could know her, Mirabel. Not the shape of her. *Her.* You've spent twenty years loving a woman made of other people's accounts. I can give you the account that matters."

His hand had gone to the pendant without his deciding to. It hung against his chest, small and warm, and through the ember-glass he could feel it: the faint pressure of his mother's preserved memory, the storm-calling coiled inside it like a sleeping bird. His whole life he had carried it. His whole life it had been enough and never enough.

He wanted it. Gods, he wanted it. Wanting rose in him like floodwater, and for one long moment he let himself drown—let himself see it, the whole of her, restored, handed over by the thing that had stolen her in the first place. He would walk out of the starless city carrying his mother entire. He would sit down somewhere quiet and learn her the way you learn a language. He would finally stop being an orphan who owned an object and become a son who owned a past.

It would cost him nothing. That was the trap's genius. It would cost him nothing except everything everyone else had paid.

"No," Mirabel said.

The Regent waited. It was patient; it had been patient for forty years; it could wait out a boy's trembling.

"No," he said again, louder, and the word cracked something open in him, and what came out was not courage. It was fury. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to *offer* her back to me like—" His voice broke and he hated it and kept going. "Like she's yours to give. She isn't. She was never yours. You *ate* her. You ate her while she was still alive, didn't you? Bit by bit, year by year, while she sat in that valley paying for me with pieces of herself—and then you come down here wearing a dead man's face and you have the gall, the *unbelievable gall*, to offer me the leftovers as a gift?"

"Mirabel," Sef said quietly. A warning.

He ignored her. The storm was rising in him—he could feel it, the pressure behind his sternum, the old violence that had flooded back the night the wards fell, unpracticed and enormous. Inside the valley it had been a gentle thing, a dousing thing, rain-callused palms and quiet skies. Out here it was a weapon with no safety on it.

"My mother didn't spend herself to hide me so I could be *bought*," he said. "Do you understand that? Do you understand anything? She gave up everything—her memories, her self, whatever a person is when you take all that away—she gave it so that I could grow up *free*. Not safe. Free. There's a difference, and you wouldn't know it, because freedom is the one thing you can't digest." He was shaking now, and the air was shaking with him, a low hum running through the pale stone. "She paid with her whole self so I wouldn't have to pay at all. And you think I'll trade her sacrifice for a refund?"

The Regent regarded him. Something shifted behind the king's eyes—not anger. Interest. The look of a scholar encountering an argument it hadn't catalogued.

"You misunderstand the transaction," it said. "I am not buying you. I am returning stolen property. There is a difference, and you, of all people, should appreciate—"

"*There is no difference!*" The thunder came out with the words. Actual thunder, indoors, rolling through the rafters of the scrubbed-clean throne room, and both guards flinched, and somewhere high above a pane of glass starred and cracked. Mirabel felt the storm surge up his arms and fought it, wrestled it, the way you fight a horse that has smelled fire. "Everything you touch becomes a transaction. Memories are currency. Grief is leverage. Love is a price tag. That's all you *are*—you're the thing that turns people into ledgers!" He pointed at the crown, at the hollow face beneath it, and his hand was sparking, literally sparking, little blue-white threads of lightning crawling between his fingers. "My mother is not in your ledger. My mother is *spent*. She spent herself on me, and the spending was the point, and no amount of returned goods will ever make it un-spent. You can't sell me back what she gave away, because what she gave away wasn't memories. It was *me having to need them*."

Silence. The hum of the storm filled it, pressing against the walls.

And then the Regent did the last thing Mirabel expected.

It laughed. Aldous's laugh—deep, rueful, human-sounding, a laugh that must once have charmed ambassadors. "Oh," it said, wiping at the corner of the king's eye with one knuckle, a gesture so perfectly, horribly human that Mirabel's stomach turned. "Oh, *there* he is. Liora's son. She said almost exactly that, you know. Forty years ago, standing in this room, in nearly this spot." The pale eyes fixed on him. "She told me I couldn't buy what had been given freely. She was the only person who ever said it and meant it. Everyone else bargained. Everyone else always bargains." It stepped closer, and Mirabel forced himself not to step back. "So understand me clearly, boy, since you insist on being difficult. The offer expires when the moon completes its turn. After that, I take the ember, I take the king's scattered leavings, and I take *you*—and I will spend the next century learning whether your mother's stubbornness is hereditary. This is not cruelty. It is appetite. Try to tell the difference."

"I don't need to," Mirabel said. "I've met collectors before."

Behind him, barely audible, Sef whispered: "*Now*, Mirabel."

He didn't need telling. The storm had been building in him since the Regent spoke her name, and holding it any longer was like holding a river with his hands. He let go.

Not all the way—he tried to steer it, tried to keep it aimed—but control was a fiction he'd maintained for about four seconds. Lightning left him in a white sheet, not a bolt, a *wall* of it, cracking across the throne room floor and up the dais, blowing the guards off their feet and bursting every window along the eastern wall in a single cascading roar. Rain followed, impossibly, falling from a ceiling that had no clouds above it, hammering down in the ruined light. The great doors blew inward. The tapestry-less walls ran with water.

Through it all, the Regent stood on the dais and did not move. Water streamed off the crown. The king's face turned to follow Mirabel through the chaos, calm as a portrait, and the last thing Mirabel saw before he turned and ran was that calm face beginning, very slightly, to smile.

Then Sef had his arm and they were running—through the blown doors, into the corridor, into the archive wing where the fires burned on their weekly schedule and the smoke lay along the ceilings like a low gray sky. Behind them, unhurried, unhurt, the sound of the palace waking up. Bells. Boots. A voice—not the Regent's, a steward's, a herald's—crying orders through the flood.

"He let us go," Sef gasped, hauling him left at a junction. "You understand that, don't you? He could have stopped us. He *wanted* you angry—he wanted to see—"

"I know." Mirabel's hands were still sparking. He couldn't make them stop. Every heartbeat sent another thread of lightning skittering across the flagstones, and the storm in his chest showed no sign of ending, because it hadn't been summoned—it had been *released*, and released things don't go back in bottles. "I know, Sef. I know it was a test. I passed it anyway."

They plunged into the burning archive, into the smoke and the falling ash of forty years of records, and behind them the bells kept ringing, and ahead of them—somewhere beneath the old observatory, under the one roof in the kingdom built to watch the murdered stars—a fourth vault waited, warded by a dead woman's final spell, holding the last of a king.

Mirabel ran toward it with his mother's empty ember burning cold against his chest, and the storm he could not put down crackling off his skin like a warning:

*Come and see what her stubbornness built.*
