## 29. Hunted Through the Archive

The archive was burning on schedule.

That was the thing Sef could not stop thinking about as they crouched in the shadow of the west stairwell, ash sifting down like gray snow: the fire had a schedule. Every seventh day, at the third bell of the afternoon, the Regent's clerks carried the week's condemned records into the great hearth-hall and fed them to the flames with the same unhurried devotion other kingdoms gave to prayer. She had watched it happen for years from behind her cataloguing desk. She had even, once, helped carry a cart. She had told herself it was arithmetic—burn one shelf so they let you keep ten—and she had believed herself right up until the day they threw her down a well.

Now she stood inside the arithmetic, and it smelled like burnt vellum and tallow, and it was beautiful, and she hated that it was beautiful.

"Which way?" Mirabel whispered. He had a smear of soot across his jaw from the servant's passage, and his hands kept flexing open and closed at his sides, the way they did when the storm under his skin wanted out and he wouldn't let it. The escape from the throne room had cost him; she could see it in the tremor of his fingers, in the too-bright edge of his eyes. Storm-magic unpracticed was a river without banks. He had flooded half a corridor with lightning to get them out, and now every guard in the palace knew exactly where the Okafor heir was.

"East gallery," Sef said. "The historical collections. If my mentor's confession still exists anywhere, it's there—in the section they burn last."

"Why last?"

"Because no one remembers why it matters." She allowed herself one bitter breath of a laugh. "That's the trick of it. You don't have to destroy a truth if you can make it boring enough that nobody checks."

* * *

They moved through the smoke in the gaps between the clerks' rounds. Sef knew this building the way other people knew their own heartbeat: the east gallery through the map room, past the cases of treaty seals, along the aisle where the dynasty's founding charters had rotted quietly for two hundred years because no clerk had been assigned to care for them. Her feet remembered everything. That was the cruelty of memory—you couldn't tithe what lived in the body.

The east gallery was already half-eaten. Someone had started early today. A whole run of shelving had gone up in the night hours, and the heat came off the char in slow waves, and the light through the high windows was the color of old honey gone bad.

Sef stopped at the mouth of the aisle and felt something in her chest crack along an old seam.

She had spent eleven years here. Eleven years of cataloguing, cross-referencing, indexing the kingdom's memory into order while the kingdom itself forgot how to remember. She had thought she was keeping the flame. She had thought the work was resistance. And all the while the man who taught her—the man whose handwriting was on half the marginalia in this room, whose voice she could still hear saying *a record is a promise made to strangers*—had been the one who opened the palace doors to the thing that wore the crown.

"Vane." Mirabel's hand on her arm. "We can't stand in the doorway. In or out."

"In," she said, before she could decide not to. "Third case from the far wall. Bottom shelf. There's a false back."

"How do you know?"

"Because I built it." She was already moving. "When I was twenty-two. Master Corvane caught me hiding a banned genealogy and instead of reporting me he showed me his own cache and said—" her throat closed around it— "he said, *one day you'll need somewhere to put the truth, girl, and it should be somewhere the fire can't find.*"

"And then he burned the kingdom anyway."

"Yes," Sef said. "And then he burned the kingdom anyway."

* * *

The false panel gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Behind it, wedged into a space barely deeper than a hand, were the things Corvane had judged worth saving from seven years of scheduled erasure: three slim volumes of the true regnal record, a bundle of letters tied with palace cord, and a single black ledger with no title on its spine.

Sef took the ledger. Her hands were shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

Above them, the fire found a new voice. The old dry law-codes went up all at once with a roar like applause, and the gallery's temperature climbed ten degrees in a breath, and somewhere below a bell began ringing—not the third bell, not the schedule, but the alarm bell, which meant the guards had traced the lightning-scorched corridor to its end and were working their way backward toward the only two intruders the palace had seen in forty years.

"We read it here or we don't read it at all," Mirabel said. He'd dragged a fallen oak table upright against the far wall, beneath the window, and he was watching the door with the coiled readiness of a boy who had once doused storms with his bare hands and was only now learning what his hands could do the other way. "Smoke's going to kill us before the guards do. Read fast."

"It's coded." She opened the ledger. Corvane's cipher stared back at her, the one he'd taught her when she was nineteen, over cups of terrible wine, laughing: *learn this and you'll be able to read anything, including me, and you'll regret both.*

"I know it," she said. "He made sure I knew it. That's the joke of him—he hid everything in plain sight of the one person he trusted, and then never told her."

She read.

* * *

The first pages were dates. Of course they were. Corvane had been an archivist to the marrow; even his treason was organized chronologically. Sef translated in a low murmur, Mirabel leaning close, the fire climbing the shelves behind them like a patient animal.

*Year thirty-nine of Aldous. The court physician brought it to me in a jar. Said it had come out of the king's ear after the fever, said His Majesty asked that it be studied. It moved. Gods forgive me, it moved, and I looked at it, and it looked back, and it was hungry in a way I recognized because I was hungry too—for standing, for access, for the inner rooms. It offered. I did not refuse.*

Sef stopped reading aloud.

Mirabel waited. Then, gently, in the voice he used on frightened innkeepers and dying loyalists: "Keep going, Sef."

"He didn't invite it for power." Her voice came out flat and strange. "Everyone says it. Odile says it. The loyalists built forty years of hatred on the idea that Corvane sold the king for a seat closer to the throne. But look—" she turned the page so hard it nearly tore— "*it offered me my wife. She had been dead nine months. It held her whole, every day of her, and it said I could keep her if I opened the observatory door. I am writing this so that whoever finds it knows I knew the price. I was not deceived. I was grieving, and grief is a door, and I opened mine.*"

The words blurred. Sef realized she was crying, which was absurd, which was unforgivable, there was no time—

Mirabel said nothing. He just stood beside her in the smoke and let her cry for the length of four heartbeats, five, six, and then he touched the page. "What else?"

"The coercion." She scrubbed her face with her sleeve, archivist-quick, grief filed for later. "Listen. *It does not simply take memories. It takes the ones that hold you together first—a person's love, their name-day, their mother's face—so that what remains has no reason to resist. The king fought it for six years. Six years, do you understand? By the end he was giving his memories away himself, ordering them scattered, because he said a hollow king is better than a full plate. He chose the shape of his own ruin so the thing would starve slowly. I helped him hide the hoards. Three we sealed with the storm-witch's aid before she fled north. But there was a fourth.*"

Sef's finger stopped on the line.

"*There was a fourth,*" she read again, aloud, and her voice cracked clean through. "There was a fourth vault."

"Where?" Mirabel had gone very still. "Where does he say?"

"He doesn't. Not here. He—" She turned pages, faster, the cipher swimming. "Wait. Wait. The last entry. This is dated the year he died. Listen:

*They suspect me now. The Regent reads my ledgers while I sleep, wearing the king's patience like a coat. I cannot write plainly. So I will say it the only way left: the place where we watched the sky is the place where the sky is kept. I have paid the ferryman twice. Let the girl finish the sentence I was too afraid to speak. Forgive me, Mira. Forgive me, everyone. I was weak, and then, at the end, I tried to be otherwise, and perhaps that counts for something in whatever ledger God keeps.*

"Mira," Sef repeated. "Mira. That's—"

"That's my mother's name." Mirabel's whisper had a storm in it, literally, a faint static crackle lifting the hair on his arms. "Mirabel. She signed her letters Mira. Wrenna called her—" He stopped. Started again. "Your mentor knew my mother. He worked with her. He's telling us—"

"The observatory." Sef was already on her feet, the ledger crushed against her chest. "The place where we watched the sky. The Royal Observatory—it's been closed since the stars went out, they say it's structurally unsound, they say—" and then she laughed, one sharp disbelieving note, because of course, of course. Forty years of *structurally unsound*, of locked gates and no funding and clerks forbidden to enter, and no one had ever asked why, because the Regent made forgetting boring, boring was its favorite disguise. "The fourth vault is under the observatory. It's been under our feet the entire time. Under *my* desk, Mirabel, I walked past it every day for eleven years."

* * *

The door at the end of the gallery banged open.

Not guards—one guard, and behind him the smoke rolled thick and orange, and the man had a crossbow leveled and the vacant bright-eyed calm of the well-tithed, and he said in a pleasant, empty voice, "You're the storm-boy. The Regent would prefer you alive."

Mirabel moved.

Later, Sef would try to reconstruct what happened and fail. There was a sound like the sky clearing its throat indoors. There was light. The crossbow bolt never finished leaving the string—or it did, and the air simply declined to carry it, and it dropped smoking at Mirabel's feet. The guard's eyes rolled white and he folded down among the burning shelves like a coat slipping off a hook, breathing, alive, done.

Mirabel stood in the middle of it shaking, both hands out, sparks crawling up his forearms, and his face was terrified and lit with wonder in equal measure. "I didn't—I've never—that much, outside the valley, I've never—"

"You just did." Sef hauled the guard's cloak off him and wrapped it around the ledger. "Congratulations. Now run."

They ran. Through the map room where the burning atlas of the northern coast curled and blackened, through the treaty hall where two centuries of signatures went up like festival lanterns, down the servants' stair with the heat chasing them and the alarm bell hammering and Sef clutching a dead man's confession to her ribs like a second heartbeat.

At the bottom, in the cold stone dark of the undercroft, she finally stopped and leaned against the wall and laughed until it turned into something else.

"Sef." Mirabel hovered, awkward, ash-streaked, seventeen kinds of brave and none of them comfortable. "Are you—"

"He tried to tell me." She wiped her face. "Before he died, he tried to tell me. *Let the girl finish the sentence.* He hid the answer in a cipher he made sure I could read, and he wrote *forgive me, Mira*—he meant your mother, he loved her like a daughter or a sister or something, and I spent eleven years despising him, and he was—" Her voice failed and rebooted. "He was coerced. Not innocent. Never innocent. But coerced. Grieving. Human. And at the end he tried to fix it, and the fixing got him killed, and I—" 

She pressed the wrapped ledger flat against her sternum.

"I forgive him," she said, and discovered it was true. "God help me, I forgive him. Now come on. We need Odile's people, we need the observatory keys, and we need to get under that dome before the Regent works out what its oldest prisoner has been sitting on for forty years."

Mirabel fell into step beside her. At the foot of the stairs he paused, looking back up toward the roar of the burning archive, and said quietly, "The stars are kept under the place where they watched the sky."

"Yes."

"Then maybe," he said, and there was something in his face she'd learn to know very well in the years after, though she couldn't have named it yet—something that belonged to a future neither of them was promised—"maybe when we open it, some of them come back."

Behind them, above them, the archive burned on, feeding the Regent's tidy hunger one century at a time. Ahead, under the dead dome of the observatory, something older than the Regent's patience was waiting in the dark, warded by a storm-witch's final spell, holding the largest piece of a king that anyone had bothered to save.

Sef tightened her grip on the ledger and started running again.
