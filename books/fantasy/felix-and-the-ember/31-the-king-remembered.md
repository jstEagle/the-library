## 31. The King, Remembered

The first memory came back to him as the smell of wet stone.

Aldous had been a king for forty years and had forgotten, in that time, what it was to be a boy—but the smell arrived before any understanding did, rising through the fog of his mind like water through chalk. Wet stone. Rain on the steps of some stair he had climbed a thousand times in another life. His hands were bound loosely at his sides—not with rope but with ribbon, ceremonial, the kind used on state occasions—and he was kneeling on the cold floor of the old observatory while strangers moved around him with vials of glass no larger than a thumb.

He knew one of them.

Not by name—the names had been eaten long ago, scooped out of him like the flesh of a fruit—but by *weight*. The archivist woman with the bitter mouth and ink-stained fingers had stood in rooms with him before. Had argued with him. Had once, he thought, thrown something at him—a book?—and he had deserved it. The knowledge arrived without context, the way grief arrives, the way love arrives: whole, unbidden, unexplained.

"You're awake," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word like ice on a pond.

"I am always awake," Aldous said. His own voice startled him. It was thin. It was polite. It was the voice of a man who has spent forty years agreeing with everything said to him, because agreement required nothing, and nothing was all he had left. "That is the trouble. I am never anything else."

The young man knelt across from him. Dark-skinned, valley-born—you could see it in the way he held his shoulders, low and easy, a man accustomed to open sky. Around his neck hung a pendant of ember-glass, and inside it something glowed faintly, pulsing like a heartbeat slowed almost to stillness.

"You're King Aldous," the young man said. Not a question. But not quite a statement either—he was checking, the way one checks whether a lamp still holds flame.

"Am I?" Aldous looked down at his hands. They were an old man's hands now, though he could not remember becoming old. There was a scar on the left thumb he had no story for. "I have worn the crown for forty years. I have signed what they put before me. I have smiled at ambassadors whose names dissolve even as I hear them. If there is a king in here—" he touched his own breastbone, gently, as if it belonged to someone else— "he has been very quiet for a very long time."

The archivist made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob. "That's him," she said to the young man. "That's exactly how he talked. Before."

"Before," Aldous repeated. The word opened a door onto darkness. "And what was I, before?"

"Alive," said the young man simply. He uncapped the first vial. Inside it swam a light that was not light—thicker than light, slower, the color of late afternoon in autumn. "This is yours. We found them. All we could find. And we're going to give them back."

* * *

They had explained it to him beforehand—or tried to. He understood the shape of it if not the substance: forty years ago something had come into the palace wearing the skin of a counselor, and it had fed on him, meal by meal, year by year, until the man who remained was a suit of clothes with a crown on top. They had scattered his memories to keep them from the thing's mouth. Now they meant to pour them home.

No one had told him it would hurt.

The first vial touched his lips and the memory poured in like a swallowed river, and Aldous was suddenly seven years old, standing in a courtyard in the rain, laughing, soaked to the skin, while a woman with his mother's eyes scolded him from under an awning—and then the memory *tore*, and he was back on the cold floor, gasping, blood or something like blood running warm from his nose.

"It's rejecting them," the archivist said. Her name surfaced briefly—Sef, Sef Vane—and sank again. "The roots. Forty years of roots. The Regent hasn't just taken the rooms; it's grown into the walls."

"Then pull the roots," the young man said.

"If we pull the roots, we pull *him*. What's left of him. The parasite has interwoven itself with everything he is. Every memory we return has to fight its way past tendrils wrapped around his mind like ivy around a tree." She was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, an archivist's cage. "We can force them in. We've done four already. But look at him."

Aldous looked at himself, as instructed. His hands were shaking. The rain-courtyard memory was still in him—he could feel it, small and golden, like a coal wrapped in cloth—but around it the darkness pressed, patient, hungry, *familiar*. That was the worst part. The hunger felt like his own. After forty years he could no longer tell where the parasite ended and he began, and he suspected the answer was: nowhere. They had grown together like two trees planted too close, their roots indistinguishable in the dark soil.

"There are thirty-one vials left," Sef said quietly. "If we force them all, we will either kill him or finish hollowing him. Possibly both."

* * *

They gave him an hour. Kind of them. He spent it lucid—which was itself a gift, a strange side effect of the returned memories crowding the parasite's feeding paths, damming up its hunger for a little while—and he spent it looking at the observatory.

He remembered this place. That was the miracle of it; the rain-memory had brought its cousins. He remembered being carried up these stairs on his father's shoulders. He remembered the dome opening, the brass armatures groaning, the whole ceiling peeling back like the lid of an eye to show him the stars. He had learned their names here. He had made promises here. He had—

He had sat in this exact spot, he realized, twenty years ago, with a woman whose hair was going gray at the temples, and she had been crying, and he had been ashamed because he could not remember why.

"The storm-caller," he said aloud.

The young man turned sharply. "What did you say?"

"The woman. Twenty years ago. She came to me at night, when the palace slept, and she wept, and asked me to hide her son somewhere the crown's watchers would never look. Somewhere magic died at the borders." Aldous pressed his palm against the cold floor, as if he could press the memory deeper, keep it from dissolving. "I told her about a valley. I don't remember which valley. I don't remember her face. But I remember that I gave her my seal, and I remember that I was afraid—for her, for the boy—and I remember thinking: this is the only kingly thing I have ever done, and no one will ever know it, not even me."

The young man had gone very still. The ember-glass at his throat pulsed faster, brighter, like a heart remembering fear.

"That was my mother," he said.

"Yes," Aldous said. "I thought it might be. You have her way of standing in doorways as if the doorway owes you money."

Something that was almost a laugh broke out of the young man—wet, startled. "Mirabel," he said. "My name is Mirabel."

"Mirabel." He tasted the name. "She didn't say it. Or she did, and it's gone. But I'm glad to have it back. It's a good name. It means—"

"Wonder," Mirabel said. "She told me. When I was small. She said she named me for the only thing she couldn't call down out of the sky."

* * *

Sef wanted to stop. Odile's people—what remained of them, nine souls gone down to five after the third vault—wanted to stop. The mathematics were simple and merciless: forcing the remaining vials would destroy him. Aldous could see them arriving at it, one by one, the way mourners arrive at a graveside.

So he called Mirabel to him alone, while the others argued in whispers by the door, and he asked the question he had been turning over beneath the lucidity like a stone under the tongue.

"How does the Regent feed?"

Mirabel blinked. "It eats memories. You know that better than—"

"No. Think. How does it feed *on me*?" Aldous spread his hands. "It has eaten nearly everything. There is almost nothing left. A parasite on a dying host finds new pasture or dies. So why does it stay? Why does it hold on to a mind that is mostly empty air?"

Mirabel frowned. Slowly, the frown became something else—horror arriving in stages, like dawn coming up over a battlefield.

"Because you're not empty," he said. "The vaults. The memories we're bringing back. Every time we return one, it feeds again. We haven't been saving you. We've been—" He stopped. Swallowed. "*Fattening it.*"

"Or waking it," Aldous said. "Either way. It let us come here, Mirabel. Do you understand? It could have stopped us at any gate. It knows every road, every password, every loyalist name—it ate those out of me personally, forty years of dinners. It let us reach the fourth vault because the fourth vault is the largest meal in the kingdom, and because whatever we return, it can drink." He laughed, and the laugh was terrible, thin, a king's laugh with nothing behind it. "I am not a prisoner of the Regent. I am its *larder*. And you have been carrying food into the pantry for a month."

"Then we stop," Mirabel said instantly. "We take the vials and we run—"

"And it takes the crown, and the kingdom, and in thirteen days the last of me dissolves anyway, and it becomes permanent, and every star stays dead forever." Aldous shook his head. "No. Listen to me. There is a way. There is one way, and I have known it since the second vial, and I have been trying to find a gentler version of it ever since, and there isn't one."

He took Mirabel's wrist. The young man's pulse jumped against his fingers—quick, stormy, alive in a way Aldous's own had not been in decades.

"The Regent's roots are in me because my mind has gaps. Gaps are doors. Every place it ate, it left a hole, and it grew into the holes, and now the holes are its home. To drive it out, the holes must close. The memories must return—but they cannot return as *gifts*, poured in and left loose for it to drink. They must return *anchored*. Held fast. Rooted in something living, so that when the Regent pulls, the memory pulls back." He met Mirabel's eyes. "Someone must anchor them. Someone whose mind has no gaps for the parasite to flee into. Someone who will stand inside the flood of forty years of returning memory and hold it in place with their own—while the Regent thrashes, and drinks, and tries to drown them both."

"What happens to the anchor?" Mirabel asked. Very quietly. As if quietness could make the question smaller.

"The anchor gives what it holds," Aldous said. "Memory for memory. Self for self. The returning memories need a foundation to root in, and the only soil available is the anchor's own. Whatever the anchor treasures most—whatever makes them *them*—will be pulled out and spent, woven into the king's foundations, so the king can stand again." He did not look away. He owed the boy that much, at least: to say it plainly, without flinching. "You would not die. But the person you are would end. You would forget your valley. Your mother. The reason you came. You would wake a stranger in a stranger's rescue, and the man you saved would remember everything you gave up, and you would remember nothing at all."

The silence stretched. Above them, through the dome's closed shutter, the sky was black and starless, and had been for a month, and would remain so unless—

"Why are you telling me this?" Mirabel whispered. "Why not Sef? Why not one of Odile's people?"

"Because it must be given, not taken. An anchor forced is no anchor at all—the memory rejects it, the same way mine rejected the vials. It has to be *chosen*, freely, completely, or it won't hold." Aldous's voice roughened. "And because I watched your mother make this same arithmetic twenty years ago. She paid with her whole self to hide you. I will not let her son pay it blind. If you do this, you will do it knowing. That is the only mercy left in my power to give."

Mirabel looked down at the ember-glass pendant. It burned steady now, bright, urgent, like a hand gripping his.

"The thirteenth moon," he said. "How long?"

"Twelve nights," Aldous said. "Perhaps eleven."

Mirabel closed his fist around the ember, and when he raised his head his eyes were wet and furious and certain, and Aldous thought, with a grief that had forty years of practice: *oh, child. Don't.*

But Mirabel only said, "Then we'd better not waste a single one," and went to tell the others—and Aldous remained alone on the cold floor of the observatory, in the dark beneath the dead stars, holding the terrible knowledge that salvation was coming for him, and it would cost a boy everything, and the boy had already begun to smile.
