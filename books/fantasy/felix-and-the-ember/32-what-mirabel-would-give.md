## 32. What Mirabel Would Give

The observatory smelled of dust and old starlight.

That was the only way Mirabel could think of it, standing in the ring of the great dome with the vault open at his feet and the king's memories laid out in their hundreds of vials—amber and green and one deep violet that hummed when he came near. Dust, yes, twenty years of it on the brass fittings and the collapsed orrery. But underneath the dust was something else, something patient, the way a room holds the shape of the people who once watched the sky from it. He had grown up in a valley built by his mother's sacrifice. He knew what rooms remembered.

Sef sat on an overturned crate near the eastern window, her ledger across her knees, cataloguing for the third time what they had already catalogued twice. Her quill scratched. It was the only sound in the dome besides the wind worrying the shutters, and Mirabel understood suddenly that she was not working. She was hiding inside work, the way he used to hide inside roof repairs, inside smallness, inside the deliberate ordinary life of a man who mended shingles and doused storms and never asked why the stars never left the sky above him.

He picked up the violet vial.

It was warm. Not ember-warm—not the living heat that pulsed against his sternum where his mother's last memory hung on its cord—but warm the way a hand is warm, the way a cheek is warm when you press your lips to it without thinking. He turned it in the light of the storm-lamps Sef had rigged, and inside, the memory moved like weather. Clouds forming and unforming. A horizon that would not hold still.

"The anchor," Aldous had said. Briefly lucid, briefly himself, propped against the observatory wall with his eyes clear for the first time in forty years and his voice already fraying at the edges as the Regent's roots pulled him back under. *Someone must anchor the returning memories with their own. The vessel cannot be empty, boy. The flood needs a riverbed.*

Mirabel set the vial down before his hands could shake.

"Sef."

"I'm counting." Scratch, scratch. "There are three hundred and eleven intact vials, nine cracked but usable, and one—" she stopped. She had stopped at the violet one every time, and every time she made herself move past it. "One we don't have a label for."

"It's mine."

The quill went still.

He hadn't meant to say it. Or perhaps he had; perhaps some part of him had known since Aldous spoke the word *anchor*, since the meaning landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well, and everything since—the walk back through the tunnels, the meal Odile's people had pressed on them, the hours of not-sleeping—had just been the long fall before the splash.

"The anchor has to be precious," he said. "That's how it works. That's how my mother's spell worked. The wards weren't strong because she was powerful—they were strong because she paid with the thing she loved most. Her whole self, Wrenna said. Paid with her whole self." He looked at the violet vial, at the weather turning inside it. "I've been trying to make it be anything else. The storm-magic. My hands. Some trick of bloodline. But it isn't a trick, is it? It's a price. And the price is always the same kind of thing."

Sef stood up too fast. The crate rocked; her ledger slid and she caught it without looking, because Sef caught everything without looking, because looking would mean admitting how much she watched. "You don't know that. You're guessing. You heard a dying man say one sentence and you've built a whole theology on it."

"He's not dying, Sef. He's being eaten. There's a difference, and the difference is four days."

"Then we find another way in four days."

"With what?" He kept his voice gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting; he saw her register that, see the gentleness as condescension, and he did it anyway because the alternative was to let them both start screaming and never stop. "We lost the third vault. We lost Odile. Two hundred vials burned to nothing while I threw lightning at a hole in the world. What's left is here, and here isn't enough unless the memories come home anchored to something that can hold them. Something the Regent can't digest." He touched the pendant at his throat, absently, the way he'd done ten thousand times in the valley without knowing why. "Something loved enough to burn."

"Then let it be me."

The words came out of her flat and fast, like coins slapped onto a counter.

Mirabel stared at her.

"You need a riverbed," Sef said, and now her voice was doing the thing it did when she argued with the archive, when she defended a footnote against three centuries of consensus—precise, relentless, a little cruel. "Fine. I am offering you a riverbed. I have spent thirty years inside other people's memories, Mirabel. I have read the confessions of dead traitors and the diaries of forgotten queens until I don't always know which childhood is mine. Do you understand? Half of what I remember of my mother's kitchen is actually a description I transcribed from someone else's letter. I am already a house with borrowed furniture. Take the rest. It costs me less than it costs you, and unlike you, I am not—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—I am not the heir of the woman who built the wards. The memories will come home easier to her son. That's not sentiment. That's mechanics."

"That's exactly why it can't be you."

"Why? Because you're noble?"

"Because I'm *compatible*." He said it quietly, and watched the word land. "Aldous said it. The flood needs a riverbed shaped like the river. These are the king's memories, Sef. They'll pour into whoever anchors them, but they'll only *hold* in someone whose own deepest things rhyme with his. Storms. Exile. A crown you never asked for." He lifted the pendant away from his chest and let it hang in the lamplight between them, glowing faintly, pulsing like a second heart. "My mother hid a king's son once. She spent herself doing it. If there's a rhyme the returning memories will recognize, it's this. It's me. It was always going to be me. I think—I think maybe she knew. Maybe that's why the ember held a picture of him weeping. Not so I'd pity him. So I'd know him. So that someday—" He stopped. Swallowed. "So that someday I could finish her sentence."

Sef crossed the dome in five strides and took his face in both hands, which was so far outside anything she had ever done that Mirabel forgot, for a full second, what they were arguing about.

"Listen to me," she said. "You are twenty-six years old. You have been free for less than two months. You learned the taste of rain off a mountain last week—you told me, you were *delighted*, you laughed like a boy falling out of a boat. You have a valley waiting for you with rebuilt wards that know your name. You have a mother whose voice you finally heard after twenty years of silence, and you have not even begun to grieve her properly, because grief takes years and you have given her weeks. And you want to trade all of it—the valley, her, the self you built out of shingles and thunder—for a man who invited a parasite into his kingdom because he was afraid?"

"He didn't invite it. Odile told us—"

"*Someone* did. Someone opened the door. And maybe it wasn't him, maybe it was worse than him, but either way he is a stranger, Mirabel. You fell in love with a memory of a young man dancing in the rain. That's not a man. That's a vial." Her thumbs pressed hard against his cheekbones. "I have read ten thousand lives. I am telling you as a professional: do not die for a document."

Mirabel put his hands over hers. Held them there. Let himself lean into her palms for one breath, two, because whatever else happened in the next four days, he wanted to remember—or wanted whoever came after him to find, somewhere in the sediment of himself, the shape of this: Sef Vane's cold fingers and fierce ruined eyes, begging him to live.

"There isn't another way," he said.

"You haven't looked."

"I've looked since the wall. Every hour. I've turned it over the way you turn a stone looking for the scorpion underneath. Anchor it with something small—a bad memory, a useless one, the taste of porridge, the name of a neighbor's dog. But the spell doesn't weigh the coin, Sef. It weighs the love. That's the whole engine of it. My mother proved that. The wards didn't run on power. They ran on what she gave up." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "You can't pay a tithe in counterfeit. Believe me. I grew up next door to a woman who collected tithes."

Sef's hands dropped. She turned away, walked to the eastern window, and stood looking out at the starless dark with her arms folded tight, and Mirabel recognized the posture because he'd worn it his whole life: a person holding themselves together at the seams.

"If you do this," she said to the glass, "you won't know you chose it. That's the part I can't—that's obscene, Mirabel. You'll wake up whole in a way, and the rest of us will spend the rest of our lives knowing what you paid, and you won't even have the dignity of missing it. You'll just be *gone* and cheerful about it. Some stranger wearing your hands."

"Then write it down." He came to stand beside her. Below, the capital sprawled under its lid of darkness, tidy and terrible, ten thousand bright-eyed vacant windows. "You're good at writing things down. Write me a letter. Tell the stranger who I was. Tell him about the valley, and the roof I fixed for Tam's family, and the night the stars went out, and the rider who died at the border holding a letter with my name in it. Tell him about my mother. Tell him—" His voice snagged. He breathed through it. "Tell him she sang to him. He won't remember the song. But he should know there was one."

Sef was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her archivist's voice had returned, level and precise, and he understood that this was her way of weeping: she would build him a catalogue instead of a grave.

"What do I tell him about me?"

Mirabel considered the question honestly, the way he considered roof beams and weather fronts.

"Tell him you saved his life twice and insulted him daily," he said. "Tell him you taught him that history isn't what happened—it's what someone refused to let be erased. Tell him that when he whistles thunder, and he will, everyone does eventually, it will sound wrong to him, and he won't know why, and that's because I taught him wrong on purpose, because it made you angry." He paused. "And tell him thank you. For the well. For coming up out of it swinging."

She laughed—one short, wet, furious note—and scrubbed her sleeve across her eyes without turning around.

"And the king?" she said. "What do I tell the king?"

Mirabel looked at the violet vial on the table, at the weather turning inside it, and felt the truth of it settle over him at last, not as dread but as something almost like calm—the stillness at the center of a storm he had spent his whole life learning to stand inside.

"Tell him nothing," he said. "Let him find out the way I found out. From a letter in dead hands. From a friend who lied to give him a childhood. From an ember that stays warm long after it should be cold." He touched the pendant one more time, feeling his mother's single preserved image flicker behind it: a king, weeping, asking her to hide her son. "Some inheritances aren't meant to arrive with instructions."

Outside, far to the north, over the palace where the Hollow Regent sat wearing a dead man's patience, the thirteenth moon rose a finger's width fuller—and in the dome of the old observatory, among three hundred unlit lamps of other people's lives, Mirabel Okafor reached for the violet vial, uncorked it, and let his most precious memory begin to pour.

* * *

It started, strangely, with the smell of cedar shingles.

Not the memory itself—he wasn't in it yet—but the edges of it, the frame, the way a dream announces itself by its furniture. Warm wood. Tar and sun. The particular grit of a roof slope under his palms, forty degrees of pitch, the exact angle his body knew better than language. He was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the observatory with the vial empty in his lap and the first thread of it winding up through him, and he thought, distantly, with the part of him that was still a man taking notes on his own dissolution: *so that's what it smells like from the inside. All these years, and I never once noticed I knew it by smell.*

"Sef," he said. His voice sounded fine. That seemed important to record. "Start the letter tonight. Don't wait for me to forget. Write it while I can still correct your spelling."

"Your spelling is atrocious."

"See? Already irreplaceable."

She knelt across from him, ledger open, quill ready, and her face was doing something complicated that he wished he had time to learn properly—the way you wish, watching a country from a departing ship, that you'd learned its language. "Tell me what to write first," she said.

He closed his eyes. The cedar smell deepened. Somewhere below it, faint as a hearth through a wall, a woman was singing.

"Write that the valley was real," Mirabel said. "Whatever else he doubts—write that."
