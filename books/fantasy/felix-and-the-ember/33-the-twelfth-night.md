## 33. The Twelfth Night

The observatory smelled of dust and old brass and, faintly, of rain that had fallen a hundred years ago and never quite dried out of the stone. Mirabel sat on the floor beneath the great orrery—its rings frozen mid-turn, its planets of painted wood hanging like fruit no one would ever pick—and tried to think about ink.

Ink was easier than thinking about tomorrow.

He had a candle, half a bottle of wine Sef had stolen from the loyalists' stores and then pretended not to have stolen, three sheets of good paper, and a pen whose nib he'd had to cut himself with his knife because everything sharp in this place had gone dull decades ago, waiting for an ending.

Above him, through the broken ribs of the dome, the thirteenth moon hung fat and wrong. It had been growing for months now—not in the way moons grow, waxing toward full, but in the way a debt grows, quietly compounding. The astronomers' charts in the archive called it *the Interloper*. It hadn't been in anyone's sky forty years ago. It had arrived with the Regent, or perhaps the Regent had arrived with it; Sef said even the oldest chronicles couldn't agree which came first, the parasite or the sky that made room for it. Tomorrow night it would complete its turn. Tomorrow night, whatever remained of King Aldous inside that hollowed, courteous face would dissolve like sugar in warm water, and the thing wearing him would be permanent.

Tonight was the twelfth night. Tonight was still his.

Mirabel dipped the pen and wrote:

*Mirabel.*

It looked strange on the page. His own name, addressed to himself, as if to a stranger—which, by this time tomorrow, he would be. He laughed, once, and the laugh came out cracked down the middle.

*If you're reading this,* he wrote, *then it worked. If you're reading this and you don't know why your hands smell of rain, or why there's a valley somewhere north of here that makes your chest ache when you think of it—or if you can't think of it at all—then it worked exactly the way it was supposed to, and I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But also: thank you. You did the bravest thing anyone has ever asked of you, and you did it without hesitating, because you're the kind of person who doesn't hesitate when it matters. Don't let anyone tell you different. Especially not yourself.*

The pen hovered. He realized he was crying only when a drop hit the paper and bloomed the ink, and he swore softly and blotted it with his sleeve and kept going, because there wasn't time to be careful, and because maybe the smudge was fine. Maybe the smudged words were the truest ones anyway.

*Here is what you need to know, in order of importance:*

*One. Your name is Mirabel Okafor. You were born in Ember Valley, in a house with a crooked chimney on the east slope, and your mother's name was Lira, and she loved you more than the whole sky, which is saying something, because the sky used to be full of stars before they went out.*

*Two. You have storm-calling magic. Right now it lives in your hands like a second pulse. If you hold them up on a cloudy day you'll feel it wanting. Don't be afraid of it. It was hers first.*

*Three. There is a woman named Sef Vane who is going to be standing near you when you read this, pretending to organize her notes. She has been with you since a well outside Marrowford. She is bitter and brilliant and she will lie to your face about how much she cares about you, and she cares about you enormously. Do not let her push you away. Push back. She folds under pressure. It's her best quality and her worst one.*

*Four. The king owes you a life. He knows it. Make him pay it forward, not back—you won't want what he'd try to give you, trust me.*

He stopped. Set the pen down. Picked up the wine and drank straight from the bottle, which Wrenna would have boxed his ears for, and the thought of Wrenna hit him like a fist under the ribs, and he sat with it, breathing, until he could see the page again.

That was the thing no one warned you about: you didn't get to choose what you lost. He had thought, weeks ago, when Aldous first told them the shape of the price—that someone must anchor the returning memories with their own most precious memory—he had thought he understood. He had imagined it would hurt the way losing hurts, a clean wound, a grief with edges.

But it wasn't a wound. It was an amputation of everything. The valley. His mother's voice, which he could still hear if he held very still—the low hum of her calling weather, the way she'd said his name like it was two notes of a song. Twenty years of small deliberate life: mending roofs, dousing storms with bare hands, Wrenna's tea, the ward-stones humming at the equinox. The ember at his throat. All of it, poured out like water into a dying man's mouth, and none of it coming back, because memory didn't work that way. Memory spent was memory spent. His mother had taught him that with her whole self, though he hadn't known it until Wrenna told him, and now he was going to follow her down the same road, and the road only went one direction.

He picked up the pen again.

*Five. This is the hard part, so read it twice.*

*You are going to want to know why. Why you gave this up. Why a stranger's life was worth your mother's face, your home, every morning you ever loved. And here is the answer, and I need you to believe me, because I am the only witness you'll have left:*

*It was worth it. Not because the king deserved it—though he did, God help him, he did—but because she would have done it. Because she DID do it. She spent herself to hide me, and I spent myself to save the man she died protecting, and that's not a tragedy, Mirabel, that's a inheritance. That's the family trade. We are people who pay with our whole selves so that other people get to keep theirs. If you feel hollow tomorrow, remember: the hollow is where the love was. It's proof. Nothing else in the world is proof like that.*

His hand was shaking. He set the pen down and pressed both palms flat against the cold floor until the shaking slowed, and above him the orrery creaked in a draft, turning its dead planets through their dead orbits, and somewhere below, in the observatory's lower rooms, he could hear Sef moving among the vault vials, cataloguing, checking seals, doing the thousand small necessary things she did instead of sleeping.

They had argued tonight. Of course they had. Sef had spent three days looking for another way—a transferral rite, a substitute anchor, anything—and had found nothing, and had come up from the archives white-faced and furious at the universe, and had said, *There has to be something we haven't tried*, and Mirabel had said, gently, *Sef*, and she had thrown a book at the wall. A rare book. She'd apologized to the book.

And then, later, quieter, sitting on the observatory steps with the starless sky pressing down like a lid, she had said: "I've lost everyone I ever catalogued. Every name in my care, eaten or burned or scattered. I built my whole life on keeping records because records were the only things that stayed." Her voice had been flat, controlled, the voice she used when control was all she had. "And now the one person I—" She'd stopped. Started over. "Don't make me write your eulogy, Okafor. I hate eulogies. They're always wrong."

"Then write me accurately," he'd said. "That's all I'm asking."

She'd looked at him for a long moment, and the moonlight—Interloper-light, colorless, sourceless—had made her look carved out of the same pale stone as the observatory. "Accurately," she'd repeated, disgusted. "You're asking an archivist for accuracy. You'll get ten thousand words and a footnote apparatus." And she'd gone inside, and he'd heard her writing late into the night, the scratch of her pen steady as rain.

Now he turned to a fresh sheet. This one was harder. This one was for her.

*Sef—*

*Instructions for the care and keeping of Mirabel Okafor (former), by Mirabel Okafor (current), who knows himself better than anyone alive and is therefore uniquely qualified:*

*He likes his tea bitter enough to stand a spoon in. He will say he doesn't want it. Make it anyway.*

*When he's frightened, he gets quiet and helpful. Watch for quiet and helpful. That's when you grab his hand.*

*He whistles when he thinks no one is listening. Old valley tunes. If you hear one, don't comment. Just let it happen. It means he's happy, and he almost never knows he's doing it.*

*He falls in love easily and admits it never. He fell in love with a memory of a young king dancing in a storm, so understand that his standards are not high, but his devotion is enormous. If he looks at you the way he looked at that vial—well. You already know. You've known since the well. I'm just the documentation.*

*Do not let him apologize for what he's about to do. It isn't an apology kind of thing. It's a harvest. He planted himself in that valley for twenty years and tomorrow he comes up ripe, and the whole point of a harvest is that somebody eats.*

*Last instruction, and this is the one that matters: when he forgets, he will be afraid. Not of you—of the gap where everything used to be. People who lose their memories don't miss the memories. They miss being a person who had them. So don't tell him stories right away. Don't bury him in his own past. Just stay where he can see you. Be the one fixed point. Let him build outward from you. He's good at building. He rebuilt a roof once in a storm with his bare hands and lightning in his teeth, and he'll rebuild himself too, given time and one person who refuses to leave.*

*That's you. Refuse to leave.*

He signed it, and then, after a moment, added a postscript in smaller letters, the kind of thing you write when the candle is guttering and honesty comes cheap:

*P.S. — I know what you wrote in the margin of the Salt Cathedral chart. I archivists' eyes too, Sef Vane. I saw it months ago. I was waiting for you to say it out loud. Say it out loud sometime. Even if I can't hear it. Especially then.*

He folded both letters. The first he sealed with wax and wrote on the outside, in block letters: *TO MIRABEL OKAFOR, FROM MIRABEL OKAFOR — OPEN WHEN YOU DON'T KNOW WHO YOU ARE.* The second he wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord, and set apart, weighted with the wine bottle, labeled simply: *SEF. DO NOT READ UNTIL AFTER. YES, THIS MEANS YOU.*

Then there was nothing left to write, and the candle was down to a thumb's width, and the Interloper through the broken dome seemed closer than it had an hour ago, filling the gaps in the stonework with its pale, hungry light.

Mirabel got up and climbed the narrow stair to the dome's rim, where the old astronomers had once stood with their brass tubes pointed at a sky worth pointing at. The wind up here smelled of coming weather—real weather, ordinary weather, the kind that meant nothing—and far below, the city of the Regent lay spread out like a spilled drawer, tidy and dim, its citizens asleep behind scrubbed windows, dreaming whatever dreams remained to people who had sold pieces of themselves year by year.

He raised his hands.

The storm-magic answered instantly, eagerly, the way it always did now—flooding up his arms, prickling his fingertips, gathering in the clouds overhead with a low mutter of thunder. Once it had terrified him, this power flooding back after twenty years of silence. Now it felt like the last honest thing he owned. He let a little of it loose—not a strike, just a shimmer, a ripple of light running along the cloud-belly like a fish turning under ice—and watched it go, and thought: *tomorrow I won't know how to do this either.* The magic lived in the same place as the memories. When he poured himself out, the storm would pour out with him. Some stranger would wake with rain-smelling hands and no idea why.

"Good," he said aloud, to the wind, to the moon, to whoever was listening. "Let it be useful. Let it be hers."

Footsteps on the stair. He didn't turn. Only one person walked like that—quick, clipped, heels striking like punctuation.

"You should sleep," Sef said, arriving beside him, arms crossed against the cold. "Aldous wants us at the observatory floor an hour before moonset. Odile's people are carrying the vials up now. It's—" She stopped. He watched her watch the letter in his hand. "You finished them."

"Finished them."

"Can I—"

"No." He smiled, to take the sting out of it. "Tomorrow. After. You can read yours tomorrow, and if you cry, I will never, ever let you forget it, and you'll have no recourse, because I'll have written it down."

"That's blackmail."

"That's *archival practice*," he said, "and you taught me everything I know."

She laughed—a short, wet, unwilling sound—and then pressed her lips together and looked out at the starless dark, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below them the city breathed. Above them the Interloper turned, patient as a key finding its lock.

"I found something today," Sef said finally, quietly. "In the fourth vault's outer seal. My mother's handwriting is in it."

Mirabel turned. "Your mother? Sef, your mother was a laundress in—"

"The co-spell. The ward your mother wove around the vault. It takes two hands, Mirabel. Two casters. We knew that. What we didn't know—" Her voice caught, steadied. "The second signature. The second hand that helped Lira Okafor seal the king's memories away, thirty-nine years ago. It's *mine*. My mother. She was loyalist. She was one of them, and she never told me, and she's been dead eleven years, and her handwriting is on the door of the thing that's going to save us tomorrow." She laughed, unsteadily. "Everyone I've ever loved turns out to have been braver than they told me. It's infuriating. I'm going to start a chronicle of it."

"Sef—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "I'm fine. I'm documenting. It's what I do." And then, before he could answer, she stepped forward and hugged him—quickly, fiercely, her chin hooked over his shoulder, her fingers digging into his back like she was holding a door shut against the sea—and just as quickly let go and stepped back, eyes bright, jaw set, composed as a ledger.

"One hour before moonset," she said. "Sleep if you can."

She went down the stairs. He listened to her footsteps fade, and then to the silence, and then to the thunder muttering far off over the hills, answering the pull of his raised hands without being asked.

Mirabel stood a while longer under the broken dome, under the fat pale moon, in the last hours of the only life he had ever fully owned. Then he took out the sealed letter one more time, turned it over, and on the back—where his future self might find it, where it might be the first thing he saw—wrote four final words, large and plain, in a hand that shook only a little:

*YOU WERE LOVED FIRST.*

Then he blew out the candle, and went down to give himself away.
