## 34. The Unbinding

The observatory had been built by people who believed the sky would always answer.

Mirabel felt that belief in the stones under his hands as he climbed the last spiral stair—old faith pressed into mortar, a century of astronomers' patience baked into every brick. The dome above them was split down its length like a cracked eggshell, and through the gap he could see it: the thirteenth moon, swollen and wrong, hanging in a sky with no stars to keep it company.

It was nearly full. A sliver of dark remained along its lower edge, thin as a paring of rind.

"Twelve turns done," Sef said behind him. Her voice was steady in the way that cost her something; he had learned to hear the price in it. "When the last sliver fills, it's over. Aldous goes out like a lamp."

"Then we'd best be quick." Odile's granddaughter Tam stood at the vault door, both hands flat against the stone. She was nineteen and had never seen a star, and she had volunteered anyway, because there were only six loyalists left and someone had to stand watch on the stairs. "It's still sealed. Your mother's work, archivist says. It wants blood of her line."

"It wants more than that," Sef said quietly.

Mirabel didn't look at her. He had spent three days not looking at her—not since the night he'd told them what the anchoring meant, and Sef had argued until her voice broke, and he had held her hands and said, *there isn't another way*, and watched her accept it the way you accept weather.

He set his palm against the vault door.

It was warm. That was the first thing—the stone was warm as a sleeping animal, warm as the ember pendant against his chest, and beneath the warmth he felt the co-spell turning like a slow heartbeat. His mother's magic. He knew its texture now better than his own name: rain-smell and iron and a stubbornness like bedrock. She had stood in this room forty years ago—or rather, her magic had, woven into the observatory's bones alongside whatever king's-blood or loyalist oath had completed the lock—and she had left a message the way she did everything: practically, and without asking permission.

The door opened when he touched it. Not swung—*melted*, stone going soft as tallow, and the smell that came out was the smell of the valley after a storm, petrichor and crushed grass, and Mirabel's eyes stung before he understood why.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, it's *full*."

* * *

The vault was not a room. It was a sky.

They descended into a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell, and every surface of it—walls, floor, curved ceiling—was set with vials. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They hung in iron lattice-work like fruit in an orchard, each one stoppered with wax and labeled in a hand Mirabel recognized from the confession Sef carried: *Aldous, aged nine, first snow. Aldous, aged fourteen, my father's funeral. Aldous, aged twenty-two, the night I almost abdicated.* The labels went on and on, decades deep, a whole man filed and preserved against the thing that ate him.

And in the center of the floor, on a dais of white stone, lay a basin the size of a coffin, empty, waiting.

"The anchor-point," Sef said. Her voice echoed strangely, doubled by all that glass. "The memories return through whoever anchors them. The king's mind can't hold them on its own—the Regent's roots are too deep, they'll tear him apart coming out unless something holds the shape of him while he re-forms." She looked at Mirabel. "You hold the shape. You pay for the holding."

"I know what I agreed to," Mirabel said.

He took off his coat. He folded it, absurdly, neatly, and set it on the lowest rung of the lattice, and in the pocket he felt the letter he had written two nights ago by candlelight—*My name is Mirabel Okafor. If you are reading this, you are me, and I am sorry*—and he let himself feel the weight of it one moment longer than was wise.

Sef was watching him fold the coat. "You could still—"

"Sef."

"Don't." Her jaw worked. "Don't do the thing where you say my name like that and I'm supposed to understand that this is fine. This is not fine. You're going to pour yourself into a stranger and forget—" Her voice cracked clean through. "You won't know why your hands smell of rain. You won't know what the valley is. You won't know *me*."

"No," Mirabel said gently. "But I'll have been someone who did know you. That has to count. Somewhere in the ledger, that has to count."

Tam made a small sound and turned to face the stairs.

Mirabel climbed onto the dais and lay down in the basin. The white stone was warm too—everything here was warm, his mother's fingerprints on the whole world—and it fit him the way a riverbed fits water, which should have frightened him and instead felt like being expected.

Above him, through the broken dome, the thirteenth moon's last dark sliver was filling.

"Begin," he said.

* * *

Sef broke the first vial against the basin's rim.

She had insisted on doing it herself—all of them herself—because someone had to, and because, she said, if this was going to be a massacre then she refused to make Mirabel his own executioner. So she stood at his shoulder with the lattice ladder and her sleeves rolled up, and she broke the vials one by one, and each memory fell into him like a stone into deep water.

*Aldous, aged nine, first snow:* a cold so bright it hurt, a boy catching flakes on his tongue, a mother laughing somewhere off-frame—

*Aldous, aged fourteen, my father's funeral:* black wool itching at the collar, the terrible adult discovery that grief is mostly logistics—

*Aldous, aged twenty-two:* a resignation letter written and burned unwritten, three times—

They came faster. Sef worked down the ladder rung by rung, wax cracking, glass singing, and Mirabel stopped trying to hold any single memory and let them pool instead, let the basin of himself fill the way a valley fills in spring, and he understood suddenly why the spell needed an anchor—because a man is not a collection of moments, a man is the *shape* the moments freeze into, and someone had to be the mold.

He was the mold. He was learning Aldous the way water learns a cup.

And it was beautiful. That was the obscene part. He had fallen a little in love with the young king dancing in the storm, back in the salt cathedral, but this was different—this was knowing him whole, the cowardice and the stubbornness and the terrible tenderness, the man who had ordered his own mind scattered because he could feel something feeding on him and wanted to leave nothing worth the eating. *Let it starve,* the young king had written in the margins of his own life. *Let it starve and go elsewhere.*

It hadn't starved. It had learned patience instead.

Mirabel felt the change before anyone spoke—a wrongness in the air pressure, the way the valley felt before lightning. Above, through the dome's crack, the stars-less sky seemed to lean inward.

"It knows," Tam called down, her voice thin. "Mirabel—it *knows*—"

* * *

The Regent came through the dome.

Not through the door, not up the stairs—through the broken glass, pouring downward like smoke poured from a jug, a starless patch of darkness unfolding itself into the vault with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been refused. Where it passed, the vials dimmed. Not emptied—*dimmed*, their light sucked sideways, moths leaning toward a false moon.

It had no face. It wore the suggestion of one, the way a glove wears the shape of a hand: a smooth pale oval that might have been a face if you were generous, crowned with a circlet of black iron that drank the lamplight. When it spoke, it spoke with King Aldous's voice, and that was the worst thing Mirabel had heard in twenty-nine years of living.

"You're cooking my supper in front of me," the Regent said, mild, almost amused. "Forty years I've waited for this meal. Did you think I wouldn't smell it?"

"Keep breaking vials," Mirabel said through his teeth. Memory was rising in him now past his knees, past his chest—he could feel Aldous's whole childhood standing in him like floodwater, and beneath it, deeper, his own self waiting to be given, the valley and Wrenna's kitchen and his mother's voice saying *storm-caller* like a blessing. "Sef. Don't stop."

"I don't intend to," the Regent said.

It moved. There was no other word for what it did—one instant it stood at the chamber's edge, the next it was beside the lattice, and where its shadow crossed the iron, the vials simply ceased, unlabeled, unremembered, gone. Tam screamed. Sef threw herself between the Regent and the remaining vials with a broken flask in her fist like a knife, and Mirabel understood with perfect clarity that it would kill her without malice, the way weather kills, the way a tide kills.

So he did the only thing he had ever been good at.

He called the storm.

It wasn't inside the valley. It shouldn't have worked—his whole life, the magic had died at the border stones, caged and domestic—but his mother's wards were broken, and the ember at his throat was blazing, and it turned out that what she had really given him was not a cage but a *seed*. The power came up through the observatory's foundations, through forty years of her patience packed into the stone, and it came up through *him*, through the flood of Aldous's memories, and lightning answered it the way it answers a tall tree on a bare hill.

The dome exploded outward. Rain fell into the vault—actual rain, in a chamber underground, impossible and glorious—and every drop of it glowed faintly with the light of the vials, so that for one moment the observatory was full of falling stars, the first stars the kingdom had seen in months, and Mirabel laughed even as the power tried to scour him hollow.

"NO," the Regent said, and its voice lost the king's music and became what it actually was: hunger, old and enormous and suddenly afraid.

"Yes," Mirabel said.

* * *

What happened next, he experienced from the inside, and language barely survived the trip.

He gave himself away. That was the mechanics of it—there was no ritual left to perform, the spell had taken over, his mother's co-spell and the king's design and the loyalists' forty-year vigil all converging on this one act—and the giving was not like pouring out a cup. It was like a river reaching the sea. Everything he was flowed toward the man in the basin beside him, who was also somehow himself, who was also somehow everywhere: the valley first, green and small and safe, the roof he'd mended, Wrenna's bread, the border stones humming on the equinox—gone, and he felt it go, felt the place itself lift out of him like a splinter drawn clean—

—and his mother. Oh, his mother. Twenty years of her absence, and the single afternoon of her presence preserved in ember-glass, the weeping king, the plea, the hiding-away, the *live, live, live*—he gave that last, deliberately, saving the sweetest fruit for the end of the meal, and as it left him he thought: *now I will never miss her again, because missing requires remembering, and I am spending the remembering.*

The grief arrived before the forgetting finished. He grieved her with everything left in him, one vast final wave of it, and he aimed the grief like a weapon.

Because the Regent was starving.

That was the truth of the climax, the thing no chronicle would ever quite capture: the Regent did not lose a battle. It got what it had always wanted. Forty years it had fed on scraps—the king's memories rationed, scattered, hidden, each theft a famine ration—and now the floodgates opened and the whole of Aldous came home at once, every vial Sef broke and every memory Mirabel anchored and every fragment the loyalists had bled to protect, all of it returning in a single tide, and the parasite latched on and *drank*.

It drank the king's ninth winter. It drank his griefs, his coronation, his doubts, the dance in the rain, the night he almost abdicated, the day he invited the thing in because his court was afraid and fear makes doors. It drank and drank and found no bottom, because an anchored mind does not empty as it pours—an anchored mind *refills*, the memories flowing through Mirabel's sacrifice like water through a spring, endless, self-renewing, a feast that fed itself.

And a thing made entirely of appetite cannot survive abundance. It had no organ for enough.

The Regent began to unravel. Mirabel saw it—felt it—as the black iron crown cracked like the ward-stones had cracked, like dropped porcelain, and the smooth pale face split along seams no one had known were there, and out of those seams poured everything the Regent had ever eaten and digested and hoarded: the tithe-taken winters, the daughter's faces, the mothers' lullabies, the villages' names, forty years of a kingdom's stolen interior life, released all at once into the rain.

The ghosts on the moors must have felt it. The vacant-eyed citizens in the capital must have felt it. Somewhere, a woman who had sold her daughter's face for bread sat up in the dark with tears running down her cheeks and no idea why.

The last thing the Regent did was look at Mirabel—with the king's eyes, briefly, humanly—and say, in a voice that was almost relieved:

"*Finally.*"

Then it came apart, and the crown fell, ringing on white stone, and rolled to a stop against the empty basin like a coin spent at last.

* * *

Silence, except the rain.

Mirabel lay in the basin and felt the tide going out of him. It didn't hurt. That surprised him most—that the loss was painless, that forgetting felt exactly like falling asleep in a warm room while someone reads to you. He was aware of Sef climbing down the ladder, of her hands on his face, of her saying his name over and over in a voice he wished he could keep.

"Stay," she was saying. "Stay, stay, you stubborn—stay—"

"I wrote it all down," he told her. His mouth was already forgetting words; he could feel them slipping loose like leaves. "In the coat. Tell me—I want to hear it once before—"

"You're Mirabel Okafor," she said, fierce and wet-eyed, gripping his hand. "You mended roofs. You doused storms with your bare hands. You loved a valley you thought was home and a mother you thought was a stranger, and you were wrong about both, and you crossed a starless kingdom anyway. You taught a child at a crossroads inn to whistle thunder. You fell in love with a king you'd never met and then you met him and you loved him anyway, more, *differently*—"

"That's the part I mind losing," Mirabel said. The words came slowly now, each one fetched from far away. "Not the valley. Not her. You. And him. The loving. Nobody warned me the loving was a memory too."

"Mirabel—"

"Tell him," he said. "Tell the king. When he wakes. Tell him the man who paid for him says—" What? What was the message? He reached for it and found his hands full of rain. "Tell him I hope the stars come back. Tell him I hope they come back *for him*."

The last sliver of the thirteenth moon filled, somewhere above the broken dome, unseen.

And Mirabel Okafor smiled at the woman whose name was already fading, and said, politely, the way you speak to a kind stranger:

"I'm sorry—who are you?"

* * *

On the dais, the basin began to glow.

Beside it, the man who had been a hollow king for forty years drew a breath—the first breath in four decades that was entirely his own—and opened his eyes, and they were gray and clear and full of falling light.

"Aldous?" Sef whispered, still holding Mirabel's cooling hand, not daring to hope.

The king sat up. He looked at the shattered crown. He looked at the rain of stars. He looked, last and longest, at the young man lying in the basin who had bought him with everything he had—and King Aldous, restored, whole, weeping without shame, took Mirabel's hand in both of his and said:

"I remember you."

And the stars, outside, began to come home.
