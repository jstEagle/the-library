## 35. The Thing Without a Crown

The first thing Aldous remembered was rain.

Not the word *rain*, not the idea of it, not the dry ceremonial knowledge that water fell from clouds in his kingdom on occasion—he remembered rain itself, remembered being nine years old and standing in the palace garden with his mouth open to the sky while his nurse shouted from the doorway that he'd catch his death, remembered the cold needling his scalp and running down inside his collar, remembered laughing so hard he choked. The memory arrived whole, with its weight and its temperature and its smell of wet stone and crushed thyme, and it settled into him like a key turning in a lock that had been rusted shut for forty years.

He opened his eyes.

The observatory dome above him was cracked open to the sky, its ancient shutter-arms wrenched apart, and through the gap he could see—nothing. A black ceiling where stars should have been. He had a sudden, vertiginous certainty that this was wrong, that the sky was supposed to be crowded, that he had once lain in a meadow at fourteen with a farm girl whose name he couldn't yet recall and counted them until she fell asleep against his shoulder—

*There.* Her name came: Bette. And the meadow, and the smell of cut hay, and the argument they'd had about whether the seventh star in the Plough was really two stars pretending to be one. All of it flooding back, decade after decade, a dam breaking in reverse—not water pouring out but pouring *in*, forty years of stolen self returning to a man who had learned to live as an empty room.

Aldous lay on the observatory floor among broken glass and scattered vials, and he wept without knowing why, which was itself a kind of joy, because it meant he *could*.

Someone was holding his hand.

He turned his head. A young man knelt beside him, dark-skinned, soaked to the bone, his hair plastered to his forehead, his eyes fixed on Aldous with an expression Aldous would spend the rest of his life trying to name. It was not triumph. It was not relief. It was the look of someone standing in a house they had just set fire to, watching the flames take the thing they loved most.

"Your Majesty," the young man said. His voice was hoarse, scraped raw, as if he had been shouting into a storm for hours. Perhaps he had. "Can you hear me? Do you know—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Do you know your own name?"

"Aldous," the king said. And then, because the memory was there now, bright and unbearable: "Aldous Meren Vane-Corvane, third of that name, crowned in the spring of my nineteenth year, in a ceremony I spent most of wishing I were anywhere else." He laughed—a cracked, disused sound. "I hated the crown. It was too heavy. My father said I'd grow into it." His face changed. "I never grew into it."

"No," the young man said softly. "You didn't."

Around them, the observatory was a ruin of light. Every lantern had shattered or burned out; every window had blown inward; frost of some impossible summer storm still glittered on the stones. And in the center of the floor, where the great brass orrery had once turned its patient model of the heavens, something was happening that Aldous's returned memories could not explain but his oldest instincts understood.

The darkness was unraveling.

It rose from the orrery like smoke from a snuffed candle—a column of absolute black, shot through with threads of gray, twisting upward toward the crack in the dome. It did not move like wind or water. It moved like a *thing in pain*, convulsing, reaching, grasping at the air with tendrils that thinned even as he watched. Where it touched the stone, the stone went pale and featureless, smooth as an egg—but the patches healed almost as fast, color flooding back, because there was nothing left for it to eat.

"It can't hold on," said a woman's voice, ragged with grief and fury. Sef Vane stood at the edge of the light, clutching a scorched book to her chest, her spectacles gone, her eyes red-rimmed. "Look at it. There are no gaps left in you, Your Majesty. Nowhere for it to root. It's being—" she searched for the word, found it, and said it with terrible satisfaction— "*digested*. By the very memories it stole."

The black column shrieked.

There was no other word for it. Not a sound made of air—it bypassed the ear entirely and scraped directly along the inside of the skull, a sound like a name being erased from every page it had ever been written on. Aldous felt it clawing at him one last time, felt it probe the restored fortress of his mind for a single unguarded door, and found none. Forty years of theft, undone. Every memory it had swallowed was back where it belonged, and the parasite that had worn his face and ruled through his patience had nothing left to be.

"You should have taken more," Aldous said to it. His voice surprised him—steady, cold, the voice of a king who had finally grown into something after all. "You should have been greedier. If you'd left me *one* memory—one small, stupid, human thing—I'd have had a gap for you to hide in forever."

The darkness convulsed. For a moment—and Aldous would swear this to his dying day, though no one else saw it—the column took shape. A figure. Tall, robed, crowned. Wearing *his* face, the way it had for four decades: his jaw, his brow, his tired royal eyes, rendered in negative, a portrait of the king painted entirely in absence.

Then it came apart.

It did not die, exactly. Things that eat cannot be killed by starvation; they simply stop. The column thinned, shredded, dispersed—and then, instead of dissipating into the observatory's ruined air, it streamed upward, pulled through the crack in the dome as if the sky itself had opened a throat. Aldous watched the last of the Hollow Regent rise past the broken stonework, out over the capital, out over the starless kingdom it had made in its own image, and come apart against the black ceiling of the world like ash against glass.

And the ash began to shine.

* * *

Sef saw it first. She made a sound—a small, broken, involuntary sound, the kind a person makes when a wound they'd stopped believing in suddenly stops hurting—and pointed up through the gap in the dome with a shaking hand.

"Your Majesty," she whispered. "*Look.*"

Aldous looked.

One star. A single point of white fire, burning in the place where the darkness had torn itself apart, hanging in the sky like the first word of a sentence everyone had forgotten how to speak.

Then a second. Then five. Then dozens, blooming across the black in slow constellations, each one flaring into existence with a pulse of light that Aldous felt in his chest like a heartbeat. He knew—did not guess, *knew*, with the certainty of a man remembering rather than reasoning—that these were not the old stars returning. They were new ones. They were *made* of what had just been freed: forty years of stolen memory, released from the Regent's dissolution, rising into the sky and catching fire. Every recollection the parasite had eaten from him and from ten thousand others was becoming light. Somewhere up there was the taste of his wedding wine. Somewhere up there was Bette's laugh, and his father's disappointed sigh, and the name of the gardener who had taught him grafting, and a thousand citizens' first kisses and last words and ordinary Tuesdays—all of it burning overhead, visible to anyone who cared to look.

"The tithe," Sef breathed. "Every memory it ever collected. It's all—" Her voice failed. She pressed the scorched book harder against her chest. "It's all coming home."

Below them, faintly, the city began to make noise. Not the tidy hush the Regent had cultivated—no, this was *noise*, shouting, bells, dogs barking, someone singing badly and someone else weeping and someone else laughing with the wild unhinged joy of a person who has just remembered their mother's face. The kingdom was waking up all at once, forty years of amnesia lifting like fog off a field, and the sound of it rolled up the hill to the observatory like a tide.

Aldous sat up. The motion hurt everywhere, gloriously, the way a limb hurts when blood returns to it. He was old—he was *old*, he could feel every one of his sixty-one years settling into his joints like tenants moving back into a long-empty house—but he was also, for the first time since his thirty-first birthday, entirely present in them.

He turned to thank the young man who had done this.

The words died in his throat.

Mirabel Okafor was still kneeling where he had knelt, hand still resting in the king's grip—but the hand had gone slack. And his eyes, when they lifted to Aldous's face, were the eyes of a stranger. Polite. Blank. Searching, the way a lost traveler searches a landscape for anything familiar, and finding nothing.

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said. His voice was gentle and utterly empty. "Have we met?"

* * *

Later—hours later, after the healers came, after Odile's body was carried down the hill wrapped in a loyalist banner, after the palace guards arrived and found their king sitting on the observatory steps looking older and younger than any of them had ever seen him—Aldous stood alone at the dome's edge and watched the stars come home.

They were still arriving. Whole constellations igniting in clusters now, as if the sky were remembering in batches: here the sweep of the Shepherd's Crook, there the long diagonal of the Salt Road, there a scatter of dim ones near the horizon that the astronomers' charts had never bothered to name. Each new burst of light drew a fresh wave of sound from the city below. Aldous found he could bear to watch only a few minutes at a time. Every star was beautiful, and every star was evidence—of what had been taken, of what it had cost to give back, of the boy kneeling downstairs with rain-smelling hands and no idea why they smelled of rain.

"He gave you everything," Sef said from behind him. She had not left. She stood in the doorway with the confession-book under her arm, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she refused to finish. "The anchor had to be precious. That was the mechanism—you couldn't pour a king's soul back into a hollowed man unless something equally whole held the channel open. He didn't hesitate. I begged him. I told him there had to be another way, and he looked at me—" her voice caught— "he looked at me like I was the one who didn't understand, and he said, 'She hid me so I could matter to someone. This is mattering.' And then he put his hands on your temples and he *poured himself out*, and I stood there and let him, because there wasn't another way, and I will carry that for the rest of my life."

Aldous said nothing. What was there to say? *Thank you* was an insult. *I'm sorry* was a lie—he was not sorry, he was alive, he was *whole*, he was standing under a sky full of his own returned childhood, and no apology could make those facts less true or less obscene.

"He asked me for one thing before he did it," Sef went on, quieter. "He wrote a letter. To himself. He said—if I forget, read this to me. Don't tell me first. Just read it." She looked up at the king, and her eyes behind her missing spectacles were fierce and wet. "He's downstairs asking the healers his own name. And I have his letter in my pocket, and I don't know if I'm strong enough to read it to him, and you're the King of Vane-Corvane again, and I think—" she laughed, a terrible little sound— "I think I'm telling you this because I want someone to order me to do it. So it isn't my choice anymore."

Aldous looked at her for a long moment. Above them, another constellation lit—the Crown, of all things, blazing back into the northern sky, every jewel of it burning.

"No," he said.

Sef blinked. "No?"

"No orders. No decrees. I spent forty years watching a thing rule by taking choices away from people, and I will not begin my reign again by doing the same." He descended the last step and stood before her, an old man in a ruined coat, taller somehow than the crown had ever made him. "Read him the letter because you love him, or because you owe him, or because you're angry at him—which you have every right to be—or don't read it at all and let him write himself anew. But it will be *your* choice, Sef Vane. That is the only gift I have to give tonight that doesn't insult either of us."

Sef stared at him. Then she bowed—shallowly, archivally, the bow of one keeper of records to another—and turned toward the stairs.

"Sef," Aldous said.

She paused.

"When he remembers—if he ever remembers—tell him the debt is real. Tell him the crown of Vane-Corvane owes a man with no memory the whole of itself, and that I intend to pay it in whatever coin he'll take. Tell him—" Aldous stopped. Looked up through the gap in the dome, at the sky that was filling, star by star, with everything the dark had eaten. "Tell him I remember dancing in the rain. Tell him I know who taught me how."

Something flickered across Sef's face—grief and hope tangled past separating. "That's not yours to tell," she said gently. "That's in the letter."

"I know," said the king. "But I wanted to say it aloud once, to someone who'd understand. Some things shouldn't only exist on paper."

She went down the stairs. Aldous heard her footsteps fade, heard the observatory door open and close, and then he was alone under the returning sky, an old man made new, holding a debt he could feel the full weight of for the first time in forty years.

Below, in the lamplit room, a young man with rain in his hands waited to learn who he had been.

Sef reached into her pocket, unfolded the letter, and began to read.
