## 36. The Empty Chair of Mirabel

Sef Vane had catalogued ten thousand deaths in the royal archive. She had written the entries herself in her small, exact hand: *name, date, cause, effects upon the realm.* She had believed, in the arrogant way of archivists, that she understood loss—that it was a matter of record, of inventory, of knowing precisely what had been taken and from whom.

She had never once imagined what it would be to stand at a bedside and watch a living man fail to recognize his own hands.

* * *

He woke on the third morning after the unbinding, in a chamber of the old observatory that Odile's surviving loyalists had made into something like an infirmary. Sef was there when it happened. She had barely slept since the storm—since the night the sky had torn open and given back its stars one by one, like coins counted out of a miser's purse—and so she saw everything.

His eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, human. For a moment they were full of the last thing he had seen before the flood took him: the vault opening, Aldous's face, the light.

Then the light went out of them and left only confusion.

He looked at the ceiling. At the stone walls. At his own hands, which he raised slowly above the blanket and turned over, palm and back, palm and back, as though checking whether they belonged to him.

"Where am I?" His voice was rough from disuse. "Who—" He stopped. He looked at his hands again. "Why do my hands smell like rain?"

Sef had prepared for this. She had rehearsed it on the walk down from the observatory dome, in the grey hours, holding the letter in both hands like a shield. She had told herself: *you are an archivist. You will give him the record. The record is a kindness. The record is all there is.*

What came out of her mouth instead was nothing at all. She stood there with her mouth open like a fool while Mirabel Okafor—the man who had doused storms with his bare hands, who had laughed at her bitterness until she forgot to be bitter, who had poured twenty years of himself into a dying king's empty skull—looked at her with the flat polite wariness of a stranger.

"Do you know me?" she managed.

"No," he said. And then, because he was apparently still Mirabel under all that absence, still courteous in his bones: "I'm sorry. Should I?"

* * *

The healers called it a clean severing. That was their word for it, *clean*, as though cleanliness were any comfort. The memories he had given as anchor had not been destroyed; they had gone into Aldous, woven through the returning flood, and they could not be taken back without unraveling the king again. What remained in Mirabel was a self with its center burned out—a man-shaped room with a hearth-shaped hole where the fire had been.

He remembered how to speak. How to walk, though he swayed like a sailor the first day. How to read, which Sef tested shamelessly, holding up pages of her own chronicle and watching his eyes move across them. He remembered the names of common things. Bread. Rope. Storm—he flinched at the word, some reflex deeper than memory.

He did not remember the valley. He did not remember Wrenna, or the ward-stones, or the equinox hum. He did not remember his mother—not her face, not her voice, not the single image of the weeping king that had lived in the ember-glass at his throat.

The ember itself was still around his neck. Sef had checked it a dozen times in the days he slept, half-afraid it would be cold, half-afraid it wouldn't. It was neither. It sat against his chest like a coal banked for winter, warm but giving no light, and when Mirabel touched it—which he did often, absently, the way a man touches a scar—he frowned each time, as if the warmth were a word spoken in a language he'd forgotten he knew.

"What is this?" he asked her on the second day, lifting the cord.

"It was your mother's," Sef said.

He turned it over in his fingers. "I don't have a mother," he said, not cruelly. Simply. The way you'd say *it isn't raining.* "Do I? I don't—I can't remember anything before waking up here."

"You have a mother," Sef said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated herself for cracking, because he needed the record, not her grief. "Her name was Adaeze Okafor. She was a storm-caller. She died keeping you safe."

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, gently, the way you speak to someone who has confused you with another person: "You're crying."

"I'm an archivist," Sef said. "We cry over damaged records. It's a professional condition."

* * *

On the fourth day, King Aldous came.

Sef had seen the restored king twice since the unbinding, and both times she had to grip something solid. It wasn't that he looked different—the hollow king and the true king wore the same face, the same greying hair, the same long hands. It was that he *occupied* himself now. The hollow Aldous had been a portrait of a man; this one was a man. When he entered a room, the room rearranged itself around his attention, and his attention was vast and warm and terribly sad.

He dismissed the guards. He sat in the chair beside Mirabel's bed, and for a while he simply looked at him, and Mirabel looked back with polite incomprehension.

"You're the king," Mirabel said finally.

"I am."

"Why do you look at me like that?"

Aldous smiled, and the smile hurt to witness, because it was a real smile and it was breaking as it formed. "Because I know you," the king said. "Better than anyone alive knows you. And you don't know me at all." He reached out—hesitated—then laid his hand briefly over Mirabel's wrist. "You gave me everything, Mirabel Okafor. My childhood. My wedding day. The name of my first horse. The sound of my daughter laughing, before she died. Twenty years ago I felt myself being eaten alive, bite by bite, and I chose to scatter what was left of me rather than let it feed a monster. And then a stranger from a valley I helped make into a prison carried those pieces across a starless kingdom and put me back together, and paid for it with—" His voice failed. He mastered it. Kings master things. "With the whole of himself."

Mirabel had gone very still. "I don't remember doing that."

"I know."

"Is it—" He stopped. Started again. Sef watched him grope for the question the way a man gropes along a wall in the dark. "Was it worth it? To you?"

Aldous closed his eyes. "Ask me anything," he said. "Anything at all about who you were. I carry it all now. Every year of the valley. Every roof you mended. Your mother's voice. Ask me, and I'll give it back to you secondhand, and we'll both pretend that's enough."

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. Then: "Did I love someone? In—in whatever came before?"

Sef's heart stopped.

"Yes," Aldous said, and glanced at her, and away again, quick as a thief. "Yes, you did."

"Oh," said Mirabel. He looked down at his hands. "I wish I remembered what that felt like."

And Sef Vane, who had spent forty years believing that the worst thing in the world was a burned book, learned in that moment that she had been wrong. The worst thing in the world was sitting three feet from a man who loved you, watching him ask a stranger's face what love was.

* * *

That night she went to find the letter.

She had kept it since the twelfth night. Mirabel had written it by candlelight in the loyalists' camp beneath the observatory, hunched over the page with the concentration of a man defusing something, and when he finished he had folded it, sealed it with a drop of wax and his thumb, and handed it to her without ceremony.

"For the morning after," he'd said. "If there is one. Don't let me read it too soon. A person should get to be a person for a few days before he finds out what he paid."

She had carried it against her ribs ever since, through the aftermath, through the king's restoration, through the terrible bright mornings when the stars came back in constellations and everyone in the camp wept and cheered and Mirabel slept on like the dead. She had almost decided not to give it to him at all. What right did the dead man have to speak to the stranger wearing his body?

But he had asked Aldous what love felt like. And Sef was an archivist, and archivists believe—must believe, or the whole profession collapses into ash—that a record, however painful, is better than silence.

She found him on the observatory steps, wrapped in a blanket, looking up.

The sky was thick with stars now. They had returned in waves over the past week, fed—so the scholars wildly theorized—on the released memory of the Regent's dissolution, every stolen recollection burning off into the dark like sparks from a bonfire. Astronomers were weeping in the streets of the capital. Children who had never seen stars were naming them after sweets and dogs and each other.

"That one's new," Mirabel said as she sat beside him. "It wasn't there yesterday. Or—it wasn't there yesterday, I think. I keep saying *yesterday* like I have yesterdays." He rubbed his thumb along the ember pendant. "Everything I say has holes in it. Like a roof with bad shingles. Did I used to mend roofs? The king said—"

"He said you mended roofs. Yes."

"Huh." He tilted his head. "That feels true. Not remembered. Just—true. Like a word in a language I can't speak anymore but recognize the shape of." He looked at her sideways. "You knew me. Before."

"Yes."

"How well?"

"Well enough to miss you," Sef said, "while sitting next to you."

He absorbed that. He was good at absorbing things, this stranger; she was beginning to see that the patience had survived the burning, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. "I'm sorry," he said. "For not being him anymore. You all look at me like you're at a funeral, and I'm—here. Sitting here. Eating your soup."

"The funeral's for us," Sef said. "Not for you. You've been spared the entire service." She took out the letter. Her hands were steady. She had practiced that too. "There's something you need to read. You wrote it yourself, four nights before you—you gave yourself away. He gave instructions. He said not too soon. But he didn't know you'd go asking kings what love feels like, so I'm overriding him, and he'd have hated that, and I'm going to enjoy it forever."

Mirabel took the letter. Turned it over. Ran his thumb across the wax seal, where the impression of his own thumbprint still sat.

"This is my handwriting," he said wonderingly. "Isn't it."

"It is."

He broke the seal and unfolded the page, and Sef looked away to give him privacy, and then looked back immediately, because she was weak, and because she had read it eleven times already and knew every line by heart, and wanted to watch it land on him the way it had landed on her.

*Mirabel,* the letter began, in his own careful hand.

*If you're reading this, it worked, and I'm sorry.*

*I don't know what you know yet, so I'll start from the beginning. Your name is Mirabel Okafor. You grew up in Ember Valley, which is the most beautiful place in the world, and I say that with authority because I spent twenty years trying to make myself believe it was small and dull so I'd stop being afraid of leaving it. It didn't work. Nothing about that valley is small. The storms come down off the mountains like gods walking, and you used to stand out in them with your arms open.*

*Your mother's name was Adaeze. She sang when she worked. Badly. She died when you were six, and she died on purpose, trading everything she was for a spell to hide you, and you spent twenty years not knowing that, and when you found out you forgave her in about four seconds, because that's who you are. Whoever you are now. Check. See if I'm wrong.*

*You fell in love on the road. Don't panic. It's fine. It was fine when I did it too. She's sharp and bitter and reads more than any three people, and she pretends she doesn't care about anything, and she cares about everything, and her name is Sef Vane, and if she's reading this to you right now she's probably looking away pretending to study the horizon. Tell her she has ink on her fingers. She always has ink on her fingers.*

*Sef—if you're reading this over his shoulder, which you absolutely are: take care of him. Not like a duty. Like a person. He hates being handled. He likes being argued with. Bring him books. Teach him the whistling-thunder trick again; it took you two weeks last time and you complained the entire time and then practiced alone at night so you'd get it right.*

*One more thing. There's an ember around your neck. It's empty now—or it will be. But it's warm. Don't ask why. Some things you don't get answers to. Some things you just get to keep.*

*You fought for a kingdom that exiled you, and you won, and you won't remember winning. If that seems unfair—it is. It's completely unfair. Be furious about it. Fury is fuel. I'd know.*

*Last thing: the valley wards will know you. Even if you don't know them. Go home eventually. Whatever home turns out to mean now.*

*—M*

The paper rustled in the night wind. Mirabel read it twice. Three times. His lips moved on the third pass, tracking the words.

Then he looked up at the sky, at the impossible crowded stars, and Sef watched his face do something complicated and quiet—grief arriving at a house whose owner had moved out, wandering the empty rooms, finding the furniture gone but the shape of it pressed into the floorboards.

"She has ink on her fingers," he said finally. "Right now?"

Sef looked at her hands. Ink, yes. Always. "Always," she said.

"And the whistling-thunder thing. Did I really teach you that?"

"You taught a child at the crossroads inn first. Then me. I was jealous of the child. I never told you that."

"You're telling me now."

"I'm telling you now," Sef said, "because you wrote down that I should argue with you, and I intend to follow your instructions to the letter. Archivist's habit."

Mirabel laughed. It startled him—she saw it startle him, the laugh coming out of a body that didn't remember laughing—and then it came again, rougher, wetter, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and said, "Gods. Why does that hurt? Laughing hurts. Everything hurts and none of it is mine."

"It's yours," Sef said fiercely. "All of it. It's in trust. We're holding it for you—the king, and me, and the valley, and every star up there. And you'll build new things. That's not a consolation, it's a fact. You're the kind of man who builds things. I have it in writing."

He lowered his hand. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Will you read it to me again?" he asked. "Tomorrow? And the day after? Until I—" He stopped. Tried again. "Until I know him. Until I know me. However long that takes."

"However long it takes," Sef said. "I have nowhere else to be. I burned my career down years ago; it's very freeing."

They sat together under the returned stars for a long time. Eventually Mirabel said, "The letter says go home. To the valley."

"It does."

"But I don't know where that is."

"I do," said Sef. "I've been studying the maps for weeks." She stood, brushing off her robes, ink-stained fingers and all. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we ride north. And Mirabel—" She hesitated at the top of the steps, silhouetted against a sky so full of stars it looked like the archive of heaven had spilled. "When we get there, if the wards sing for you the way Wrenna swore they would—try to look surprised. For her sake. She waited twenty years to meet you, and she's going to want to tell you everything, and you're going to have to let her."

"Who's Wrenna?"

Sef smiled, and it ached all the way down. "Someone who kept your mother's secrets," she said. "You're about to become her favorite person all over again."
