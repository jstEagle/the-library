## 37. The King's Debt

The first thing Aldous remembered was rain.

Not the storm that had unmade the Regent—that came later, in fragments, like a dream told secondhand—but ordinary rain, years ago, falling on the palace gardens while a young man with his own face danced barefoot in the mud and laughed at the thunder as if it were an old friend. That memory had come back to him whole, undamaged, one of the first to return when the flood of himself poured back into the hollowed rooms of his mind. He had stood at the observatory window afterward, soaked to the skin from the storm Mirabel Okafor had called down, watching the sky begin—*begin*—to stitch itself with light.

One star. Then three. Then a scatter of them, tentative as sparks from a struck flint, returning to a sky that had been black for twenty years.

He remembered everything now. That was the cruelty of it. The Regent had eaten slowly, carefully, leaving the shell of him functional enough to sign decrees and smile at ambassadors, and so Aldous could recall every year of his own hollowing—the way his mother's face had gone uncertain in his memory, then vanished; the way he had woken one morning unable to remember why he loved the sea; the way he had *felt it feeding*, that patient cold presence behind his eyes, and understood that one day there would be nothing left of him but the shape a parasite wore to hold a crown.

And he remembered what he had done about it. He remembered ordering his loyalists to steal from him—to scatter his memories across the kingdom in vials and vaults, to make himself a man worth nothing eating. He remembered asking a storm-caller to hide her son, and paying for her spell with a lie: that the valley would be a sanctuary, never a cage.

He remembered all of it. And standing in the wrecked observatory, with the smell of ozone and old stone in his air, he had turned to find the boy who had saved him—a young man, really, gaunt and road-worn, with an ember pendant hanging empty against his chest—looking at him with the polite blankness of a stranger.

"You're the king," Mirabel had said. Not *you're alive*. Not *it worked*. Just: *you're the king*. As though Aldous were a fact he'd been told, not a man he'd bled for.

"Yes," Aldous had said, because there was nothing else to say. "I'm the king."

It had been Sef Vane who read the letter aloud—Mirabel's own handwriting, composed on his last free night, instructions for who he had been. *If I forget, tell me I liked the rain. Tell me my mother sang off-key. Tell me I chose this.* Aldous had listened to every word and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, that the debt now sitting in the throne room of his kingdom was not one a crown could pay.

Crowns paid in gold. Crowns paid in land, in titles, in pardons. There was no coin minted for *a self*.

But he was king again—truly king, the Regent unraveled into the starless dark above the observatory, dragged down by the flood of returned memory until there had been nothing left of it to wear him—and a king who could not repay could still *govern*. He had learned, over forty years of being eaten, exactly what law cost when it served a hunger instead of a people. He intended to spend the rest of his reign learning what it cost when it didn't.

---

The decree was written in his own hand.

Aldous had insisted on that. His chancellors had offered drafts—elegant, measured documents full of the passive voice that let kings hide from their own edicts—and he had sent all three back unread. Instead he sat at the long table in the small council chamber, the one his father had used for hunting plans, and wrote in plain ink on plain paper:

*Let it be known, in the first year of the restoration, that the tithe of memory is abolished. No man, woman, or child of this realm shall again be compelled to surrender what they remember—not to the crown, not to any office of the crown, not in tax, not in tribute, not in trade demanded under duress. What a person remembers belongs to that person, whole and forever, as their breath belongs to them.*

*Further: every memory taken by the crown or its agents in the years of the Regency shall be sought out and returned. Let the vaults be opened. Let the collectors' ledgers be seized. Let the tithe-houses be unsealed and their contents carried back, village by village, house by house, to the people from whom they were taken. Where the owner lives, restore it. Where the owner has died, return it to their kin, that they may know what their dead knew. And where no kin remain, let the memory be read aloud in the town square of the place it was taken, so that the community that lost it may carry it together.*

*This is not charity. This is restitution. The difference is that charity is given from surplus, and restitution is paid from debt. The crown owes this debt. I owe this debt. It will be paid.*

He signed it, blotted it, and looked up to find the council staring at him as though he'd begun speaking in tongues.

"Your Majesty," Chancellor Hesse said carefully, "the logistics alone—the tithe-houses number in the hundreds, the ledgers are—"

"I know what they number," Aldous said. "I signed the orders creating them. Forty years ago." He laid the pen down. "I signed them with a thing wearing my face, Chancellor, which is a distinction I grant you, but the hand was mine and the seal was mine, and I will not pretend the signature belonged to someone else. That pretense is how parasites govern."

Hesse went pale. Around the table, the other councillors found urgent business with their papers.

"The treasury will fund it," Aldous continued. "Not the annual surplus—the *reserve*. Yes, all of it. Melt the coronation plate if you must. A kingdom that cannot afford to give its people back their mothers' faces does not deserve its gold."

"And the collectors, sire?" asked Lady Verrin, who handled justice. "There are hundreds of them. Some were coerced. Some were enthusiastic. The prisons—"

"—will hold the enthusiastic ones," Aldous said. "Trial by jury, in the towns where they collected. Let the people they robbed sit in judgment. I will not have vengeance dressed as justice, but neither will I have mercy issued from above like alms. They took memories at my order—or the order of the thing that wore me. The people will decide what that's worth." He paused. "One exception. Any collector who can lead us to hidden stores, who turns themselves in and surrenders their ledgers complete, gets a public trial and whatever sentence the jury gives—no execution without their verdict, and no pardon without it either. We need the ledgers more than we need heads."

Lady Verrin wrote that down. When she finished, she looked up. "And the valley, Your Majesty? The Ember Valley wards fell the night the stars did. The borderlands reports say the storm-caller bloodline—"

"Is one young man," Aldous said, "who is currently a guest in this palace because he gave me everything he had, and I will hear his name spoken in this room with respect or not at all." The silence that followed had weight. He softened, slightly. "The valley's people were kept inside those wards for twenty years without their consent. Some of them will want to leave. Some will want to stay. Both choices are theirs. Send surveyors, not soldiers. And send word to Wrenna—" he stumbled on the name, pulling it from Mirabel's letter, from Sef's careful chronicle— "Wrenna, who kept the secrets. Tell her the crown owes her a debt too, and the crown pays its debts even when the creditor would rather it didn't."

---

That evening he went down to the guest wing himself.

His advisors would have been horrified. Kings did not walk unguarded through their own palaces; kings did not knock on doors. But Aldous had spent forty years being a door that other things walked through, and he had developed a strong opinion about thresholds.

Mirabel's room was modest—he had refused the royal apartments, Sef reported, with the particular stubbornness of a man who did not trust comfort—and through the half-open door Aldous could see him sitting at the window, watching the sky. Watching the stars come back. They were thicker every night now, fed on the released memories streaming out of the opened vaults, and tonight there were enough of them that a person might almost call it a sky again instead of a wound.

Aldous knocked.

Mirabel turned. For a moment his face did the thing it always did now—the flicker of searching, the instinct to reach for a recognition that wasn't there—and then settled into courtesy. "Your Majesty."

"Aldous," the king said. "When we're alone. You've earned the familiarity if anyone has, and I'd rather you have it as a gift than as a title you don't remember earning."

"That's... kind." Mirabel said it the way people said things they didn't believe. He gestured at the window. "They're coming back fast now. Sef says the northern vaults opened yesterday. She's cataloguing the returns—she says the archivists' guild offered her her old position back. With honors." A pause, and something dry moved through the politeness, some ghost of a humor that belonged to a man Aldous had never met. "She told them she'd consider it after she finished yelling at them."

"She would," Aldous said. He came to stand by the window, at a respectful distance. Below, the city was lighting its lamps, and here and there a window glowed with more than lamplight—with the strange, soft luminescence of a memory freshly returned, still bright from its decades in the dark. Somewhere down there a woman was seeing her daughter's face for the first time in fifteen years. Aldous had ordered it done, and it was the only order of his reign that felt like it weighed anything true.

"I wanted to tell you what I've done," he said. "The tithe is abolished. Every stolen memory is being returned. The ledgers, the vaults, the tithe-houses—all of it. It will take years. It will cost the treasury everything. I don't care."

Mirabel watched him with those courteous, empty eyes. "You didn't need my permission."

"No. But I needed—" Aldous stopped. Started again, plainer. "I spent forty years being eaten, Mirabel. Do you know what I learned? That hunger doesn't feel like hunger from the inside. It feels like *policy*. It feels like necessary measures. It feels like the reasonable voice in the room saying the cost is acceptable. The Regent never once thought of itself as a monster. It thought of itself as an administrator." He looked out at the returning stars. "I signed things. In the years before I understood what was happening to me, I signed things that made the taking easier. I want that in the histories. Not the version where the poor king was bewitched—I was *complicit*, in ignorance and then in cowardice, right up until I chose to scatter myself. Both things are true. Write down both."

"I'm not a historian."

"No," Aldous said. "You're the reason there's anything left to write about." He turned to face him fully. "I owe you a self. Twenty years of your mother. A valley. The reason your hands smell of rain. There is no repayment for that, and I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise. So instead I'm going to do the only thing left. I'm going to build a kingdom where what happened to you can't happen to anyone else. Where memory is not currency. Where a child with dangerous magic is exiled to nowhere, because exile is not a punishment we use. Where the law serves the people it takes from." His voice roughened. "It isn't enough. I know it isn't enough. But it's what a king can do, and I refuse to do nothing just because the true debt is beyond me."

For a long moment Mirabel said nothing. Then: "In the letter. My letter. There's a line I apparently wrote—Sef read it to me. *Tell him the king danced in the rain once, and that's why.*" He frowned, as if the words tasted foreign. "Why what?"

Aldous smiled, and it hurt. "Why I'm doing all of this. Come to the garden tomorrow. I'll show you."

---

He showed him at dawn.

The palace gardens had been the first thing the Regent's stewardship had let go to ruin—too expensive, too frivolous, too full of the old king's sentiment—and Aldous had ordered them restored in his first week, working alongside the gardeners himself, mud to his elbows, to the scandal of the court. Now the beds were turning over, spring coming into the soil, and the fountain played in the center of the old parterre where a younger Aldous had once danced barefoot in a storm while thunder answered him like music.

He stood there with Mirabel in the gray-gold light and told him about it: the storm, the dancing, the feeling of being *alive in a way the hollow king never is*—those words were Mirabel's, from a vault beneath a drowned cathedral, from a moment when a stranger had touched a vial of Aldous's youth and fallen a little in love with a man he'd never met. Aldous knew about that. Sef had told him, gently, in the first days. He carried it now as one carries a flame through wind: carefully, and with gratitude.

"He sounds like someone worth knowing," Mirabel said quietly, when the story was done. "That young man in the rain."

"He is," Aldous said. "He's still here. Mostly." He looked at the young man beside him—at the ember pendant, empty and warm, resting against his chest. "And so is the man who saved him. That's not gone, Mirabel. Memory is gone. The man isn't. Whoever you're becoming, you built him out of the same material. Don't let anyone—including me—tell you he's a lesser draft."

Mirabel's hand rose, without apparent instruction from him, and closed around the pendant. Something crossed his face—grief with no address, mourning without an object—and then, faintly, wonder. "It's warm," he said. "It's been warm since the observatory. Sef says embers go cold when the fire's out. Hers went cold." He looked up. "Why is mine still warm?"

Aldous had wondered the same. He had asked Sef, and Odile's surviving loyalists, and consulted what remained of the royal records on ward-craft, and gotten no answer he trusted. But standing there in the dawn, watching the ember glow faintly against the young man's chest like something waiting rather than something spent, an answer arrived that he hadn't sought and didn't fully understand—one that felt less like knowledge than like recognition.

"Perhaps," Aldous said slowly, "because it isn't finished with you."

Mirabel stared at him. Above the garden walls, the last stars of the night were fading into a sunrise that gilded the wet stone, and somewhere in the eastern sky—faint, new, impossible—a star lingered past dawn, burning low and red-gold, like an ember refusing to go out.

Neither of them spoke. But Mirabel's hand stayed closed around the pendant, and Aldous saw him make a decision—not consciously, perhaps, not yet, but somewhere deeper than decision—in the way his shoulders settled, in the direction his feet began, slowly, to turn.

East. Toward the valley he could not remember, on a road he had never walked in this life, carrying an ember that should have been cold.

"Your Majesty," Mirabel said at last, and there was something in his voice that hadn't been there before—not memory, not yet, but the shape of a space where memory would grow. "Tell me about the valley. All of it. Everything you know."

Aldous smiled, and began.
