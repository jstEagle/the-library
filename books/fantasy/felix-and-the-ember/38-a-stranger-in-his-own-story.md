## 38. A Stranger in His Own Story

The palace had a way of teaching you its silences.

Mirabel learned them one by one, the way he had once learned the valley's sounds—which birds meant rain, which creak of Wrenna's floorboards meant she was awake, which hum of the ward-stones meant all was well. The palace had its own grammar. The hush after the third bell, when the servants finished their rounds and the corridors belonged to no one. The particular quality of quiet that came off the throne room at night, like cold coming off a lake. And the silence around him—his own name spoken carefully by people who knew more about him than he did.

He woke before dawn, as he always had. He didn't know why. There was no valley here to tend, no roofs to mend, no neighbor's goat to chase off the garden wall. But his body kept its old hours, and so he lay in a bed too large for him, under a canopy embroidered with stars that no longer existed, and watched the gray light come up over the Starless City.

That was what they called it now—not just in whispers but openly, in the markets, in the new proclamations nailed to every door. The Starless City. As if naming it made it honest. King Aldous had ordered the tithe abolished in his first week awake; the collectors' ledgers were burned in the same square where they'd once weighed memories like grain. But a kingdom does not heal the way a body heals. It heals the way a field heals after fire—slowly, unevenly, with green shoots coming up through ash in places no one planned for.

Mirabel swung his legs out of bed and felt, as he did every morning, the small wrongness of his own hands. They were calloused in ways that didn't match his life. Rope-wear on the palms. A scar on the left thumb he had no story for. His body remembered labor his mind had never done, and sometimes, reaching for a door handle or a cup, his hands moved with a competence that startled him—knew things, wanted things.

His hands, this morning, went to his throat.

They always did. Before he was fully awake, before thought, his fingers found the cord there and followed it down to the pendant resting against his sternum. He'd tried to take it off twice in the weeks since he'd woken in the observatory with Sef's face above him, wet-eyed, saying his name like a question. Both times his hands had stopped halfway through the knot, trembling, refusing. It wasn't attachment. He had nothing to attach to. It was something older than memory—the way a man who has forgotten how to swim still knows water is dangerous, or holy.

He lifted the ember-glass into the pre-dawn light.

It was empty now. He understood that the way he understood arithmetic—because Sef had told him, patiently, more than once, and because the glass itself confirmed it: a dull grey teardrop, clouded like river ice, holding nothing. When he'd first held it, Sef said, it had glowed like a coal. It had held his mother's last preserved memory—a king weeping, asking her to hide her son—and then Mirabel himself had pressed it to his chest and let whatever remained burn out into him. A new memory taking root where an old one burned. That was the phrase Sef used. *Ember, rekindled.* She said it gently, the way you'd hand someone back a knife they'd dropped.

But the glass was warm.

It had been warm since the first morning. Not hot—warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun leaves it, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm when you rest your palm on its flank. Warmth without source. He had asked three physicians about it. One had examined it with instruments and pronounced it inert. One had suggested, delicately, that grief could produce sensations with no physical cause. The third had simply looked at him for a long moment and said, "Some things keep their own heat," and declined to elaborate.

Mirabel turned the pendant over. Inside the grey glass, if he tilted it against the light, there was the faintest suggestion of movement—a shimmer, not quite a shape, like the reflection of clouds on water when you look for the clouds themselves and find only their passing. He had decided long ago not to decide whether it was real.

*You're mourning,* Sef had told him, in one of their late conversations in the archive, both of them too tired to be careful. *That's what this is. You lost twenty years of yourself and everyone keeps congratulating you on surviving.*

*I don't feel like I lost anything,* he'd answered. *That's the problem. You can't grieve what isn't gone from you. It's just—absence. A room I've never been in, missing furniture I've never seen.*

*No,* Sef had said, with the flat certainty she reserved for cataloguing errors. *Grief without objects is still grief. It's worse, actually. At least when you have a grave, you know where to stand.*

A grave. He had none. No valley—he'd been told about it, the Ember Valley, the wards, the storm-magic that died at its borders, but telling is not knowing, and the word *valley* sat in him like a name from a book, a place in someone else's novel. No mother—Wrenna's letters described her, Odile's testimony filled in edges, but the woman herself was a portrait painted by strangers. No self. The man who had crossed a starless kingdom, who had faced the Regent's eye, who had poured out everything he loved to save a king he'd fallen half in love with through a vial of stolen rain—that man was a stranger whose diary Mirabel had read.

And yet.

He got dressed—servants' clothes, at his own insistence; the royal tailors had despaired of him—and went down to the east gallery, because that was where he went every morning, though he couldn't have said why the first time he'd chosen it. Habit, maybe. Or the body remembering routes the mind had lost. The gallery ran along the eastern face of the palace, all windows, built to catch the sunrise. It caught very little now. The sky did its dutyless work: grey, then lighter grey, then the flat white of a day with no opinion.

There was one other person in the gallery, as there often was.

King Aldous stood at the far window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching a city wake up under a sky that gave it nothing. He was dressed plainly—a grey coat, no crown. He almost never wore the crown anymore. Mirabel had heard the servants speculate about it: some said the crown had been damaged in the unbinding; some said the king couldn't bear the weight of it; one scullery boy claimed, with great authority, that the crown screamed. Mirabel believed the boy. He remembered—or rather, he had been told, in the letter he'd written himself, which Sef kept and read to him in pieces, like medicine dosed out across weeks—that the thing in the crown had worn Aldous's face for forty years, and that faces remember being worn.

"Mirabel." The king didn't turn. "You're up early again."

"I'm always up early."

"I know." Aldous's voice had a texture Mirabel had slowly learned to read: a gentleness laid over something raw, like linen over a wound. "I'm always up early too. Forty years of habit. Even now that I know what those years were, my body insists on keeping them."

Mirabel came to stand beside him at the window. Below, the city: chimney smoke rising straight in the windless air, carts already moving, a line of people outside the Hall of Returns where the restored memories were given back—one basket of vials at a time, each return witnessed, each name recorded. Sef had designed the process. Of course she had. She'd designed it the way she did everything, with ferocious precision and a margin note for every contingency.

"You sleep badly," Aldous said. It wasn't a question.

"How would you know?"

"Because I walk at night. Because the palace is quiet enough to hear a man not sleeping two floors up." The king's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Also because Sef tells me. She tells me everything. I believe she considers it her archival duty."

"She worries," Mirabel said. Then, because the words came out before he could weigh them: "She worries about someone she's known for a season. About me. And I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't understand it. I've read about myself. I've heard the stories. I sound like a person worth worrying about. But I can't find him. I look in mirrors and I see a man with borrowed hands."

Aldous was quiet for a while. Below them, a cart loaded with empty vials rattled past the Hall of Returns, heading back to the vaults for refilling. The returning went slowly. Forty years of theft does not give itself back in a season.

"When I woke," the king said at last, "I remembered everything. That was the cruelty of it—I'd spent four decades being eaten alive and I woke up whole, every year of my life intact, including the years I spent hollow. Do you know what that's like? To remember your own absence? I remember the Regent wearing my smile. I remember signing decrees I don't recall drafting. I remember my people looking at me with love and knowing—I knew, somewhere under the fog—that the thing they loved was a mask nailed to my face." His hands tightened behind his back. "I used to think the worst fate would be forgetting. Now I know better. The worst fate is remembering a stranger wear your life."

"That's not the same."

"No," Aldous agreed. "It isn't. You have it harder." He turned then, and looked at Mirabel directly, and Mirabel saw again what he'd seen the first time in the throne room—the flicker, the focus, the man inside the ruin. Except the ruin was gone now, and the man remained, and somehow that was harder to bear. "I owe you a self, Mirabel Okafor. I have decreed the tithe abolished, I have ordered every stolen memory returned, I will spend the rest of my reign paying debts to a kingdom I barely governed. But yours—" His voice caught, just slightly. "Yours cannot be returned. You paid in the only coin that was truly yours."

"You don't owe me anything," Mirabel said. "I don't even know why I did it. That's the strangest part. Everyone tells me it was brave, it was necessary, there was no other way. And I believe them. But believing isn't remembering. For all I know, I'd make the same choice again. For all I know, I wouldn't. I'm a man standing in the ruins of a decision he can't reconstruct."

"Yes," Aldous said softly. "That's exactly what debt is."

* * *

Sef found him in the library at midmorning, which was where she always found him, because the library was where he went when the gallery emptied and the day demanded shape. He'd taken to reading the kingdom's histories—not the official chronicles, which Sef assured him were ninety percent court flattery, but the marginalia, the letters, the household accounts. The small true things. If he could not remember his own story, he would learn to read everyone else's until the skill became a kind of home.

"You didn't eat breakfast," Sef said, setting a plate beside him. Bread, cheese, an apple cut into slices. She did this every day. She never commented on doing it.

"I ate yesterday."

"That's not how eating works." She pulled a chair around backward and sat astride it, arms folded on the backrest, studying him with the expression she wore when examining a document of questionable provenance. "You slept four hours. Your hands are shaking. And you've been staring at the same page of agricultural records from the reign of Aldous's grandfather for"—she checked, because of course she'd checked—"eleven minutes."

"It's fascinating stuff. Crop rotation. Very gripping."

"Mirabel."

"Sef."

"The ember," she said. "It's warmer today, isn't it."

He looked up sharply. "How did you—"

"Because you touch it differently when it warms. Constantly, when it's cold—checking, like a man checking a purse. But when it's warm you hold it still. Like you're listening." Her voice was careful, stripped of its usual edge. "I've watched you do it for six weeks. I'm an archivist, Mirabel. Observation is the entire profession."

He closed the book. Outside the library window, the grey sky pressed against the glass like a face. "It's warm," he admitted. "It's been warm since the beginning. And I know what you're going to say. Inert glass doesn't generate heat. Grief produces phantom sensation. Some things keep their own heat. I've heard all three."

"What I'm going to say," Sef said, "is that I've spent the last month in the recovered archives cross-referencing every text on ember-glass in the kingdom's history, and there are eleven references, and nine of them are decorative, and two of them say the same thing." She reached into her coat—she still wore the archivist's coat, frayed at the cuffs, though she was Chief Archivist of the realm now—and produced a slim volume bound in cracked leather. "The *Liber Vitae*, pre-Regent. Written by glassmakers, of all people. Listen."

She opened it to a marked page and read: "*Ember-glass holds what is given to it. What is burned out of it is not destroyed—for glass forgets nothing, only ceases to show. A vessel that has given up its flame is not empty. It is full of having-given.*"

Silence. Somewhere in the palace, a bell rang the hour.

"That's not an explanation," Mirabel said finally. "That's poetry."

"It's a glassmaker's technical manual," Sef said. "Four hundred years ago, poetry and technical manuals were the same genre." She set the book down between them. "Here's what I think. I think the warmth is real. I think the glass is doing what glass does—holding. Not the memory. That's gone into you, or gone into the ground, or gone wherever given things go. But the giving itself. The shape of it. Twenty years of a mother's spell, and a son's choice, and—" She stopped. For once in her life, Sef Vane seemed to run out of footnotes. "I think you're carrying the fact that you were loved, Mirabel. And it weighs exactly as much as warmth."

He looked down at the pendant in his palm. Grey glass, clouded, ordinary. Warm.

"Mourning without objects," he said quietly. "That's what you called it. But maybe—" His thumb moved over the glass, slow. "Maybe the object isn't missing. Maybe I just can't read it. Like a letter in a language I used to speak."

Sef didn't answer. But she stayed, and she didn't correct him, and from Sef that was practically a benediction.

* * *

That evening, there was a dinner. There were dinners now, regularly—the king's attempt to rebuild a court out of honest people, which mostly meant dinners where everyone was slightly terrified of saying the wrong thing in front of a monarch who remembered forty years of being lied to. Mirabel attended because Aldous asked him to, and because refusing felt like abandoning a post he couldn't remember accepting.

He stood near the window during the third course, watching the sky do nothing, when a young maid approached him—sixteen perhaps, nervous, holding a tray of wine she clearly didn't want to be carrying.

"Beg pardon, sir." She glanced around, lowered her voice. "Are you the storm-caller? The one from the valley?"

"I'm Mirabel."

"They say you brought the stars back." Her eyes were wide, bright—too bright, the brightness of someone recently returned to herself. He noticed, these days, the ones who'd gotten their memories back. They carried them visibly, like people carrying water through a crowd. "My brother was a tithe-collector. Before. He collected mine by mistake—my wedding day, sir, they took my wedding day—and when the king gave it back, I—" She stopped, steadied herself. "I just wanted to say thank you. Whatever it cost you."

"I don't know what it cost," Mirabel said honestly. "I wasn't there for it."

The girl blinked. Then, with the sudden fierce grace of the young: "Well. Someone was. And they're in you now, aren't they? That's what the priests say. Nothing given is ever gone." She bobbed a curtsy and fled back to her tray before he could answer.

He stood at the window a long time, the noise of the dinner washing around him like tide around a rock.

Later—much later, when the candles had burned low and the guests had gone and the palace had settled into its deepest silence—Mirabel climbed to the roof. He wasn't supposed to; the guards had orders to worry about him. But the guards had also, he suspected, been told by the king to worry quietly, and so they let him pass with the particular salute of men pretending not to see.

The roof of the palace was slate and lead and old ironwork, and from it the whole Starless City spread below, ten thousand chimneys breathing smoke into a sky with no stars. He walked to the northern edge, because his feet took him north without asking, and stood where he could see, far off past the walls, the dark line of hills.

Somewhere beyond them was a valley he didn't remember. Wards rebuilt now—Sef had told him the loyalists had gone back, that Odile's people tended the stones, that the border hummed again. A home, waiting, belonging to a man who no longer existed to live in it.

He took out the ember.

It lay in his palm, grey and warm, and he held it up against the starless sky as if it might show him something—as if the glass might remember for him, might lend him one image, one lullaby, one fragment of a woman's voice saying his name. Nothing came. Glass forgets nothing, only ceases to show. He was starting to hate that sentence.

"I don't know who I was," he said aloud, to the glass, to the sky, to whoever was in him now. "I don't know the valley or the storms or the woman who bought my childhood with her whole self. I don't know why my hands know rope, or why I wake before dawn, or why I go north on rooftops. I'm a stranger walking around inside your story. And I'm sorry. I'm sorry I paid for all of you with the only part of me that knew you."

The wind moved. The pendant warmed against his palm—warmer than usual, warm enough to notice, warm enough that he curled his fingers around it instinctively, the way you'd close your hand around a living thing.

And far away, past the hills, past the dark line where the valley lay hidden—

The sky flickered.

One point of light. Faint, uncertain, at the very edge of seeing, in the north—over the Ember Valley—like a candle relit in a window of a house everyone thought was empty.

Mirabel went very still.

Then another. And another—scattered, sparse, stuttering, but unmistakable: stars, returning first above the valley, above the wards his mother had built, as if the sky itself remembered where to begin.

He gripped the ember, warm as a heartbeat in his fist, and did not know why he was crying—but he cried anyway, standing alone on the roof of the palace of forgetting, a stranger in his own story, watching the first stars come home over the place he had once been willing to die for without knowing why.

By morning, the whole city would be talking about it. By noon, Sef would be at his door with maps and theories and that fierce, footnote-ready certainty. But for now there was only the roof, and the cold, and the warm glass against his skin, and the slow, staggering light of a sky learning, somewhere far to the north, to remember itself.

He should go down. He should tell someone.

Instead, Mirabel Okafor—who did not remember the valley, did not remember his mother, did not remember the reason he had ever fought—stood watch over the returning stars until dawn, the way a man stands watch over a sickbed, the way a son stands watch over a grave, the way—though he had no way of knowing it—a storm-caller stands watch over the borders of home.
