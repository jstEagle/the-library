## 1. The Date Written in Ink

The grimoire had not been opened in eleven years, and it opened itself at 6:47 in the evening, while Marguerite was curling her hair.

She heard it from the bathroom — a sound like a heavy book exhaling, the creak of old spine, the whisper of vellum turning over. She stood with a hot iron an inch from her ear and listened to the house settle around that sound the way water settles around a dropped stone. The cottage had always talked to her. Floorboards announcing visitors, the kettle singing warnings when the weather would turn. But this was different. This was deliberate.

She went down the hall with the curling iron still warm in her hand and found her grandmother's book lying open on the kitchen table, pages spread to a place near the middle where the paper had gone soft as cloth from handling. Marguerite had read every page of that grimoire by lamplight as a girl — or every page she'd been allowed. Her grandmother, Edith Pemberton, had kept it locked in a cedar chest with three locks, and she had said, more than once, in the flat voice she used for serious things: *This book is not for you until I am gone, and perhaps not even then.*

Edith had been gone four years now.

The page before her was written in two hands. The older hand was her grandmother's — small, slanted, ink gone brown with age. Beneath it, in letters that looked freshly dried though no one had touched them, ran a passage Marguerite had never seen:

*When the valley's daughter takes tea with a stranger's son,*
*and the hour turns seven beneath a waning sun,*
*the veil grows thin as breath on glass.*
*What was promised shall come to pass.*

Below it, a line in red, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*His name is written here.*

And there, in the margin, in that same fresh ink: **Daniel Ashford.**

Marguerite's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Because she knew that name. She had known it for six days, since the man from the historical society had called to confirm their plans — Daniel Ashford, visiting lecturer, doing research on warded settlements of the interwar period, which was a polite way of saying he wanted to ask her a hundred questions about Thornvale and why it had spent a century refusing to appear properly on any map. They were meeting tonight. At the café on Mill Street. At seven o'clock.

The clock above the stove read 6:52.

"This isn't funny," she said out loud, to the book, to the kitchen, to whatever had written those words. Her voice came out thin. "I know someone is having a joke. It isn't funny."

The book did not answer. Books never answered. That was the rule she had built her whole life upon: the magic stayed on the far side of the wards, and the things she could do — the small, secret, unspeakable things — stayed inside her own head, behind her own teeth.

Because Marguerite Pemberton spoke with beasts.

Not commanded them. Not charmed them. *Spoke* with them, the way you speak with an old friend across a table — the words arriving whole in her mind, carrying scent and weather and intention. She had done it since before she could remember. A crow had told her, at age five, exactly which hedge hid a lost kitten. The dairy cows had warned her, at fourteen, the morning the river took the lower footbridge. And Bramble —

Bramble. The fox who was not entirely a fox, who had found her in the garden when she was six years old and stayed for twenty years, sleeping under the kitchen window, teaching her which mushrooms lied and which storms meant it. Bramble, who had vanished last spring without a goodbye, leaving only a single white-tipped tail hair caught in the gate latch, like a signature on a letter she couldn't read.

She thought of him now, standing in her grandmother's kitchen with her heart going like a trapped bird, and she did the thing she always did when the world grew too large: she whispered.

Not to the book. To the valley.

*Something is wrong,* she said, in the wordless way that was hers alone, the speech that needed no mouth. *Something has come through.*

The answer came faster than it ever had.

*On my way.*

* * *

The stag came through the north field at a dead run, and it came through the wards themselves — which should have been impossible, which had been impossible for a hundred years, which was the entire reason Thornvale existed.

Marguerite saw it from the garden gate. It moved like a rumor given antlers, its coat the grey-brown of winter bracken, but along its flanks ran markings no earthly deer carried: pale spirals, faintly luminous, shifting as it ran like light moving under ice. Its hooves struck the turf and left prints that smoked faintly in the evening cold, and where it passed, the hedgerows leaned toward it, straining like plants toward a window.

It stopped ten feet from her. Steam curled from its nostrils. Its eyes were enormous, dark, and — this was the part that made her knees loosen — *knowing*. Not animal-awareness. Knowing. The look of something that had crossed a very long distance specifically to find one person.

"You're not from here," she whispered aloud, because some habits didn't care about secrecy.

"No," it said, and its voice arrived in her mind resonant and strange, layered like a chord played on a broken instrument. "And neither, little speaker, are you. Not entirely. You never were."

"I don't understand."

"You will." The stag lowered its great head. The spiral marks along its neck brightened. "The book told the truth. The veil is thin tonight — thinner than it has been since your grandmother's grandmother helped close it. There is a door opening, Marguerite Pemberton, and it is opening *for you*, and if you do not walk through it walking, you will be dragged through it otherwise."

"Then I won't go," she said, and hated how small her voice sounded. "I have a date. I have a *life*. I have a cottage with my grandmother's roses coming up in the beds—"

"The roses will keep," said the stag gently. "You will not."

Behind it, the sky over the north ridge began to do something the sky did not do. The stars there were going out — not clouded, not dimmed, but *going out*, snuffed one by one in a spreading darkness that had edges, that had shape, that advanced down the hillside like a tide of black water flowing uphill. Where it passed, the hawthorn trees stood suddenly bare though it was only April. The wards — the invisible architecture her family had tended for generations, the reason no fae had crossed into Thornvale in living memory — were failing. She could feel them failing, feel the valley's long-held breath stuttering in her chest like her own heartbeat losing rhythm.

And then she felt something else. Something familiar. Something that smelled of woodsmoke and wild garlic and twenty years of sleeping beneath her window.

*Bramble?*

The name left her like a wound leaving blood.

The fox-spirit stepped out of the darkening hedgerow — sleeker than she remembered, his coat gone the deep ember-red of late autumn instead of summer rust, his eyes holding a green fire she had never seen in them before. He sat down between her and the stag, neat as punctuation, and looked up at her with an expression she could not read, and that frightened her more than the dying stars, because she had always been able to read him. Always. He had taught her the language of beasts, and now he wore silence like a mask fitted by someone else's hands.

"Bramble, what's happening?"

He held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever moved behind his eyes, he kept it there.

"You're late," he said finally. "Seven o'clock comes whether you cross the veil or the veil crosses you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm permitted." And there — just for a heartbeat — something flickered through the green fire, something old and aching and horribly like grief, before it shuttered closed again. "I'm sorry, little speaker. For all of it. Remember that I said so."

"Remember— Bramble, *wait*—"

The stag stepped forward. The darkness came off the ridge like a wave. The air split along a seam she couldn't see but could suddenly feel everywhere — along her arms, behind her eyes, in the roots of her teeth — and through the seam poured a light that was not light, a color with no name, the smell of rain on stones that had never known rain.

Her feet left the ground.

She grabbed for the garden gate and her fingers closed on air. She grabbed for Bramble and he was already turning away. The last things she saw of Thornvale, seen backwards, falling, upside-down: the kitchen window lit gold because she'd left the lamp burning; her grandmother's roses, tight-budded, patient; the café on Mill Street, impossibly visible from here, impossibly small, where a man named Daniel Ashford sat checking his watch at a table set for two.

Then the seam closed.

Not slowly. Not gently. It snapped shut behind her like a trap, like a jaw, like a book slamming — and she heard it, felt it in her bones, the sound of a hundred years of wards collapsing inward at once, the sound of a valley sealing itself away forever.

The silence afterward was total. No wind. No birdsong. No hum of the world she knew.

She stood in grass that glowed faintly silver under a sky with too many moons, in a realm whose name arrived unbidden in her mind like a splinter sliding home:

*Aldenmere.*

Somewhere behind her, muffled beyond all reaching, a phone was ringing in an empty cottage.

And somewhere ahead, past the luminous treeline, something with too many eyes was watching her arrive.
