## 2. What the Beasts Remember

The thing with too many eyes did not eat her.

That was, Marguerite decided some hours later, the single encouraging fact of her new existence.

It had instead led her — gliding through the silvered trees on legs like folded umbrellas, its eyes opening and closing in slow, courteous blinks — down to a hollow beneath a hill where the roots of something vast broke through the earth like the ribs of a buried cathedral. There it had spoken, in a voice that arrived inside her skull rather than her ears:

*You are the one the ink named. The beasts have been waiting a hundred years for someone who could hear us.*

Then it had left, and she had been alone with the roots and the moons and the ringing absence of everything she had ever known.

She had not slept. Sleep required believing she was somewhere, and Marguerite could not yet commit to that. She sat with her back against a root wider than her grandmother's cottage, hugging her knees, watching the grass breathe. Grass should not breathe. She was fairly sure about that.

"You're shaking," said a voice near her elbow. "You'll want to stop that. Fear carries."

A hedgehog stood on the root beside her. Not an ordinary hedgehog — though at this point Marguerite's standards for ordinary had collapsed entirely — but one whose spines were tipped with tiny drops of light, like dew that had learned ambition.

"I'm sorry," Marguerite said. "Are you— can I—"

"Speak plainly. That's why you're here." The hedgehog's nose twitched. "I am Quill. I keep the Sunken Court, or what's left of it. And you are either the mender the old prophecies promised, or you are bait dropped by the Crown, in which case we'll all be dead by morning and it won't much matter what I called you."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm not here to reassure you. I'm here to feed you." He nodded toward a patch of moss, where berries gleamed like garnets and a skin of water waited beside them. "Eat. Then listen. What you heard on the other side of the veil — the whisper that pulled you — that was no accident. The beasts of Aldenmere have been calling across the boundary since the night the covenant broke. Most humans stopped hearing us generations ago. Your valley warded itself deaf. But you—" his small black eyes fixed on her, "—you answered."

Marguerite ate because her body insisted, though the berries tasted like rain and something faintly metallic underneath, like licking a bell. "The covenant," she said. "The grimoire talked about a covenant. Humans, beasts, fae. It said it *shattered*."

"It was broken." Quill's spines dimmed, as if he'd drawn the light inward. "There is a difference. A thing shatters by mischance. This was done deliberately, by hands that knew exactly which thread to cut. One hundred years ago, the fae queen came to the Worldroot to renew the covenant, as her line had done every century since the realms were young. And before she could speak the renewal, someone took her voice."

"Took her—"

"Silenced her. Utterly. Not killed — death would have been mercy, and mercy was not the point. The covenant cannot be renewed by force; it must be *spoken*, freely, by all three peoples. With the queen voiceless, the rite failed. The threads unraveled. The wards went up in your world, the truth-wardens went up in ours, and the beasts were forbidden to remember out loud." He paused. "We remember anyway. We simply cannot say it. Speaking the truth of the silencing is treason, and treason is paid for in memory — they take it from you, piece by piece, until you forget why you were ever angry."

Marguerite felt cold despite the mild luminous air. "Who silenced her?"

"No one knows. That is the cruelest part. Every creature who saw it has been emptied of the seeing. The queen herself remembers nothing — or pretends to. She still sits her throne in the Sunken Court, beautiful and mute, while the Crown rules in all but name." Quill climbed down the root, moving with surprising grace. "Come. If you truly are the speaker the ink promised, there are those below who will want to look at you. And one above"—his voice went strange—"who already has."

* * *

Bramble smelled her before he saw her.

Fox-spirits do not forget a scent. A hundred years of Crown service had taught him to bury what he remembered, layer over layer, like snow falling on a trail — but scent goes under the snow, into the earth, into the water table of the self. And this scent was *hers*: woodsmoke and chamomile and the particular warm dust of a cottage in a warded valley where he had once been small and red and loved.

He crouched on the ridge above the Sunken Court's entrance, wearing the shape the Crown preferred — a fox grown large and grey, his true color leached out by oath and duty, a silver collar of woven script tight around his neck. The collar translated. Whatever he thought, the wardens heard. Whatever he saw, they saw. He had not spoken freely in forty years.

Below him, the little human from the prophecy was following a glowing hedgehog down into the dark, and Bramble's heart was doing something he had trained it never to do.

*Report,* said the collar, in the voice of Warden-Captain Vess, which sounded like a key turning in a lock.

"The speaker has crossed," he sent back. "She moves toward the Sunken Court."

*Alone?*

"With Quill."

*A traitor and a stray. You know what happens now.*

He knew. He had known since the moment the ink in the Pemberton grimoire had flared and the wards of Thornvale had screamed. He had been the one sent to watch the valley, after all — the Crown's oldest spy, planted a lifetime ago in the shape of a fox kit with a broken leg, found by a girl who had fed him milk from a doll's saucer and told him secrets no one else could hear.

He had told himself, for years, that it was duty. That the covenant's breaking had been necessary. That the beasts were safer leashed, the fae safer muted, the humans safer behind their wards. He had repeated it so often that it had worn smooth as a river stone, and he had almost — *almost* — believed it.

But the stone cracked now, watching her go down into the dark where his masters' enemies gathered.

*You hesitate,* observed Vess.

"She is more useful watched than taken," Bramble said.

*Is that the reason? Or is it the saucer of milk?*

He said nothing. The collar drank his silence and reported it anyway.

*Listen to me, old beast.* Vess's voice softened into something worse than cruelty: kindness. *When we take her, she will ask for you. They always ask for the ones they trusted. And when she does, you will stand before her and tell her you never knew her. You will say it in your own voice, and she will believe it, because the alternative is unbearable. That is how we break speakers. Not with pain. With denial.*

Bramble looked at the moons — three of them tonight, one of them red — and thought about snow falling on trails, and how eventually the snow gets deep enough that even the fox forgets where the ground is.

"Yes, Captain," he said, and went down to betray her.

* * *

They came for her on the second night.

Marguerite woke in the Sunken Court — a drowned amphitheater of pale stone where outcast creatures had made their homes in the cracks: moles with lantern-eyes, a blind serpent who read the air like scripture, starlings that spoke in unison and finished each other's sentences because they shared one mind between three birds. They had questioned her gently, endlessly, testing whether she could truly hear them. She could. She could hear them the way she'd once heard the fox in Thornvale — not words, exactly, but meaning arriving whole.

And then the stars went out.

Not clouds. *Out* — snuffed in a rolling wave, and through the court's open crown of sky descended shapes in grey lacquered armor, riding constructs of wire and bone that smelled of ozone and old law. Truth-wardens. The creatures scattered; nets of written light fell; and Marguerite was pinned to the wet stone by a ward that tasted of copper and command.

Boots splashed toward her. A woman in a captain's mask knelt, studied her face, and smiled without warmth.

"Marguerite Pemberton. Speaker. Prophecy-bearer." She held up a slim ledger. "Your name crossed our desks before you ever crossed the veil. Did you think the beasts called you alone?" She leaned close. "Someone on *this* side opened the door."

Then she straightened and gestured, and the wardens parted, and out of their ranks walked a large grey fox with a silver collar, moving like something exhausted carrying itself on duty alone.

Marguerite's heart lurched so hard it hurt. Because she knew that gait. She had watched it stalk mice through her grandmother's garden for twenty years. She knew the set of the ears, the hesitation in the left forepaw, the way he—

"Bramble?" Her voice cracked on the name. "Bramble, it's me. It's *Maggie*. You found me when I was seven. You slept on my bed. You—"

The fox stopped. Looked at her with eyes like coins — flat, grey, spent.

"I don't know you," he said.

His own voice. His own mouth. And the collar at his throat glittering with borrowed script, drinking every word.

"I raised you," she whispered. "Or you raised me. Whichever. Bramble, please—"

"I have never been to any valley," said the fox. "I have never known any girl. You are mistaken, human, and your mistake is treason."

The captain laughed softly. "There it is," she murmured. "There's the breaking."

And Marguerite understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the fox was lying — that he had to be lying, that the collar made liars of everyone it touched — and that this was the true horror of the Crown: not that it punished truth, but that it had built a machine for manufacturing doubt, until even love could be edited out of a memory like a stain.

They dragged her up out of the Sunken Court toward the trial-circles, and the last thing she saw was the grey fox turning away, and the last thing she heard — so quiet only a speaker could have caught it, threaded under the wardens' boots and the dying starlight:

*"Forgive me. Remember enough for both of us."*
