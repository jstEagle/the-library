## 4. A Covenant of Small Voices

The Worldroot did not look like a tree. It looked like the idea of one — a trunk wide as a cathedral, silver-barked and veined with light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart, rising out of a hollow where the ground had been worn smooth by ten thousand years of pilgrim feet. Its branches vanished upward into cloud, into sky, into something beyond sky. The air around it tasted of rain on warm stone.

Marguerite stood at its base with her palms pressed to the bark, and the bark was warm, and it was breathing.

Behind her, gathered in the hollow's rim in ragged, watchful silence, stood everything the broken covenant had left behind: outcast beasts from the Sunken Court with their river-slicked fur still dripping; fae who had not shown their faces in daylight since the queen was silenced, their forms flickering between beautiful and terrible; a handful of humans — farmers, hedge-witches, two deserters from the truth-wardens with their grey cloaks turned inside out. And Bramble, at the edge of them all, his fox-fire eyes fixed on her with an expression she could not read and no longer trusted herself to try.

The trial had cracked something open. The beasts' memories, made visible to every soul in that courtroom, had shown the truth: the fae queen's voice stolen mid-song, the covenant strangled in its cradle by those who feared what unity would cost them. It had shown Bramble too — young, desperate, his family held hostage by the Crown, his oath extracted like a tooth. She had watched him break, watching himself. She had not forgiven him yet. But she had understood, and understanding was a door forgiveness could walk through later, if it chose to.

Now there was only this.

"The old law is dead," she said, and her voice carried strangely in the hollow, as if the Worldroot itself were listening through her. "It was written by queens and crowns, sealed in blood and hierarchy, and when it broke, it broke because it belonged to so few hands." She turned to face them all. "I won't rebuild it. I won't write another law that some future coward can strangle while everyone else sleeps."

A ripple went through the crowd. A heron-fae tilted its long head. One of the deserters shifted.

"A covenant isn't a decree," Marguerite said. "My grandmother taught me that much, though she didn't know she was teaching it. A covenant is a promise you make knowing what it costs. So we don't swear to a ruler. We swear to each other. Every creature willing to listen — human, beast, fae — swears their own small vow, and the Worldroot holds them all together. Not one great chain. Ten thousand threads."

She looked down at her own hands. They were steady. That surprised her, given what she was about to give away.

"To reforge what was broken, a speaker must pay in memory," she said. "That was always the price. The old covenant was sealed with the queen's voice." Her throat tightened. "Mine will be sealed with my home."

* * *

She began where all her memories began: the smell of Thornvale in late summer, cut grass and woodsmoke and the green rot of the compost heap her grandmother refused to let her father burn. She gave it up willingly, spoke it aloud so the Worldroot could take it whole, and felt it lift out of her like a bird leaving her chest — the lane behind the cottage, the crooked gate, the way the valley walls caught the last light and held it an hour past sunset.

Then the harder ones. Her grandmother's kitchen, flour on every surface, the radio murmuring weather for a valley that never quite got any. The forbidden grimoire on its high shelf, humming faintly, which she'd been told never to touch and touched anyway at age nine. The stag — the first stag, the one who'd come to the fence line when she was six and spoken to her in a voice like wind through barley, and how she'd told no one for three years because telling would make it real, and real things could be taken away.

The wards. She gave up the wards — the taste of them in the air, the faint pressure like a held breath she'd lived under all her life without naming it. She gave up the night the grimoire opened itself and wrote her date's name in ink a century old. She gave up the café, the terrible coffee, the man across the table whose name she suddenly couldn't —

— couldn't —

She gasped. Something was gone. Something about tonight, before tonight, before the veil — a face, a name, a reason she'd been smiling when the stag came through the wards. It slid away like water through fingers, and she let it go, because letting go was the whole point, because a covenant paid in half-measures was just another cage.

She gave up the sound of her grandmother saying her name. That one fought her. That one dug in like a root in stone, and she pried it loose with both hands, sobbing, speaking it aloud one final time — *"Marguerite, love, come inside"* — and heard the Worldroot drink it in, and felt the hollow fill with light.

Around her, they were swearing. All of them. The heron-fae vowed the marshes' memory to any who asked honestly. The Sunken Court beasts vowed shelter for the hunted. The deserters knelt in the mud and vowed their wardens' secrets to the open air. Humans and fae and creatures with no category at all, each voice a thread, each thread catching light, and the Worldroot blazed like dawn breaking underground, and the magic that had been dammed up for a century came roaring back through both realms at once — she felt it go, felt it pour through the roots beneath her feet into soil she would never stand on, into a valley she could no longer picture.

Because she couldn't. She reached for Thornvale and found a shape where a place should be, a word without meaning, a photograph of a photograph. Safe. Warded. Gone.

She sat down in the mud and cried, and the heron-fae stood beside her, and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in weeks.

* * *

Bramble found her at dusk, when the light off the Worldroot had gone gold and soft and the crowd had thinned to embers of itself.

He came slowly, the way he used to come to the fence line when she was small and angry at the world, low to the ground, tail still, making himself small. He stopped three feet away. He did not sit.

"I'm free," he said. His voice was rougher than she remembered, or maybe she remembered it wrong now; maybe she'd lost that too. "The oath died with the old law. I can say anything. I can say your name. I can say—" His breath hitched, a strange sound in a fox's throat. "I can say I'm sorry. I can say I raised you and I betrayed you and both of those are true and I don't know which one I am."

Marguerite looked at him. The grief in her was vast and quiet, an emptied valley.

"You're both," she said. "That's the whole covenant, Bramble. Everything true at once."

He nodded, once, and then he did something she had never seen him do. He closed his eyes, and opened his mouth, and breathed out — and a thread of silver light left him, coiling in the air between them, and took shape.

Not a shape. A sound.

*"Marguerite, love, come inside."*

Her grandmother's voice, warm and flour-dusted and impatient with love, hanging in the evening air of a world away from anywhere. Bramble opened his eyes.

"Stolen," he said. "From the Worldroot, before it finished drinking. The first theft of my freedom, and I'll answer for it however you judge me. But I thought—" His voice cracked clean through. "I thought you shouldn't lose everything."

Marguerite caught the sound in her cupped hands like a firefly, like water, like the last light on a valley wall. She pressed it to her ear, and her grandmother said her name again, and again, and it would keep saying it, she realized, forever, a small voice that would never leave her.

She laughed, and it came out broken, and that was fine. Broken things were allowed here now.

"Bramble," she said, "what happens to a valley when the wards come down?"

He tipped his head, ears forward, listening to something far below the ground, far along the roots. "It changes," he said. "It opens. Things get in. Things get out." A pause, and then, with the ghost of the old mischief she'd grown up on: "And sometimes, little speaker, things that were warded *out* find their way *in*. Your valley has a hundred years of quiet to make up for. I hear the stags have already crossed the veil."

Marguerite stood, wiping her face, holding her grandmother's voice against her heart. Somewhere beyond the Worldroot, beyond the hills, beyond everything she could no longer remember, Thornvale was opening like a gate at dawn — and coming toward her, changed, wild, alive.

"Then we'd better be there," she said, "when it arrives."
