## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The widow Marta Kell came to my shop on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were when I did my best work and she needed the best.

Not that she knew that. She thought she'd come for a tonic. They always thought they'd come for a tonic, or a tincture, or "something for the nerves," said in the hushed voice people use in apothecaries, as if grief were a condition to be whispered about rather than the only honest thing in the valley.

"Master Pemberton," she said. She was maybe sixty, with hands that had done sixty years of work and a face that had stopped bothering to hold itself together around the eyes. "They say you can help with... heaviness."

I set down the mortar I'd been grinding for appearance's sake. The pestle was clean. Everything in my shop was clean. Cleanliness suggested competence, and competence suggested results, and results were how you stayed fed in a valley where nobody could quite remember why they trusted you.

"Heaviness," I repeated. "Whereabouts?"

She touched her sternum, then her temple, then let her hand fall like it had gotten tired halfway through the errand. "Everywhere. It's been eight months since Corwin passed. Eight months, and I still set out two cups every morning. I still—" She stopped herself, embarrassed, which was absurd. Embarrassment over grief, in front of me, of all people. "I can't keep doing this, Master Pemberton. I'm not sleeping. I'm not eating. My sister says I should see the physicker in Lowmark, but he'll only give me poppy syrup, and I've seen what poppy does."

"And what do you want it to do?"

"I want it gone." She said it simply. No drama. A woman ordering bread. "I want to remember that I loved him without having to feel it every time I breathe. Is that something you can do?"

Here is what I should have told her: that grief is the receipt love gives you, and tearing up the receipt doesn't get your money back. Here is what I actually told her:

"That depends on what you're willing to pay."

* * *

Let me explain the Hollow, since you're new here, and everyone is new here eventually, including the people who were born in it.

The Hollow is a valley in the Spineback Mountains, roughly forty miles end to end, shaped like a cupped hand if the hand had given up halfway. Nothing grows particularly well. Nothing dies particularly easily either — there's a kind of stubbornness in the soil, in the people, in the mule that's refused to die at the mill for eleven years now. And magic, which elsewhere in the world runs through everything like water through chalk, does not run here at all. It stops at the ridgeline the way weather stops at a window. The old gods don't hear prayers spoken in the Hollow. The new gods never bothered trying. Witches lose their gifts crossing the border stones. Cursed swords go dull. Prophecies arrive blank.

For most of history this made us a joke. Pilgrims came to laugh at our dark shrines. Kings forgot we existed, which suited us fine.

Then someone — no one remembers who, which is appropriate — discovered that memory behaves strangely here. In the Hollow, a memory can be lifted out of a person. Not erased. Lifted. Drawn out whole, like a splinter, and held in glass, where it keeps. And a person who's lost a memory can have another one put in its place, or nothing at all, or — and this is where the money is — they can buy their own memory back later, worn smooth, sanded down, the sharp edges filed off until what was unbearable becomes merely sad, and what was sad becomes merely distant.

I sell peace. That's the product. Peace, bottled.

It's not a small trade. There are four of us who work the valley properly — me, old Hesk with his shaking hands, the twins up in Corrie Ford who share one conscience between them and therefore each have half, and a woman called Vetch who works out of a wagon and is either the best of us or the worst, depending on the week. We take grief, terror, shame, the memory of dead children and dead husbands and the war that happened before anyone now living could feel bad about it honestly. We sand them. We sell them back. And the valley forgets, and forgets, and forgets, and calls it healing.

Is it theft? Of course it's theft. I'm a thief. I've never pretended otherwise; pretending otherwise is for the twins. But here's the question I've trained myself not to think about, the way you train yourself not to bite a sore tooth: when a woman comes to me drowning and I sell her a boat made from the planks of her own shipwreck, am I saving her or robbing her?

Don't answer. Neither do I. That's the trick of it. You get very good, in my line, at not answering questions.

* * *

Marta Kell sat in my back room on the good chair, the one with the cushion, because clients who feel cared for pay better and remember less. On the table between us sat a stoppered vial of clear glass, empty, waiting.

"How does it work?" she asked. Her hands were folded in her lap like a schoolgirl's. Underneath the folding, I noticed, they were gripping each other hard enough to blanch.

"You'll sit still," I said. "You'll think about him. Not the death — the death is recent and loud, and loud memories sit near the surface. Think further back. The wedding. The morning he proposed. Whatever morning you'd choose, if you could keep only one."

Her face did something complicated. "Keep?"

"We'll discuss terms after." I drew my chair closer. "What happens next is that I reach into your head, gently, and I lift out what's hurting. All of it — the death, the sickness before it, the two cups, all eight months of it. I hold it in the glass. Then I give you back a version with the pain drawn off, the way you draw venom from a wound. You'll remember you loved him. You'll remember he died. It simply won't cut anymore."

"And the version that cuts? The one in the bottle?"

"It stays in the bottle. Some people come back for it, years later. Most don't." I didn't tell her the third option, which was that some people come back for it within the year, weeping, begging, having discovered that a life without the weight was somehow lighter than they could bear — hollow, like a coat with the lining torn out. Those ones I charge double, because desperation has always been the strongest currency, and because charging double makes me feel like the transaction is honest, which is a lie I've kept polished for so long it passes for truth even in daylight.

"And the price?" she asked.

"For the lifting, ten silver. For the smoothing, five more." I named the number and watched her flinch, watched her do the arithmetic of a widow's purse — the winter stores, the roof tile that needed replacing, the tithe. "Fifteen, then, if you pay today."

"I don't have fifteen."

"No one ever does. That's why I offer credit." I smiled the smile I keep for this part, warm as a banked fire. "Six silver today. The rest against your apple harvest. Everyone in the valley pays me in apples by October anyway."

She laughed — a cracked, startled sound, like a door opening on a room that hadn't been aired in months. And God help me, I felt the little warm flicker I always feel when they laugh, the flicker that says *you did a kind thing*, and I stamped it flat the way you stamp a spark off a dry floor. Kindness had nothing to do with it. Apples had something to do with it. Six silver had something to do with it. I find that if I keep the ledger very clear in my head — service rendered, payment owed, no feelings entered on either side — the work stays clean.

The work stays clean. Say it enough times and it almost sounds true.

"Will it hurt?" she asked.

"The lifting? No. That's the strange thing. It feels like falling asleep in a warm bath." I uncorked the vial and set it upright between us. "The remembering afterward is the part that costs. Are you ready?"

She looked at the empty glass for a long moment. Eight months of Corwin lived behind her eyes, and she was deciding whether to pour him out.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

I won't describe the lifting in detail, partly because trade secrets, mostly because describing it makes people imagine tentacles, and it isn't like that at all. It's quiet. It's the quietest thing I do. I rest two fingers against her temple, and I listen — that's really what it is, listening — until I find the seam of it, the bright hot thread of Corwin: his cough going wrong in autumn, the physicker's face doing the careful face, the bed moved downstairs, the last morning when he tried to joke and couldn't finish it, the funeral with too many lilies, and then the long grey afterward, the two cups, the untouched side of the bed, the nights she'd spent talking to an empty chair because talking to it was the only thing that helped and nothing else did.

Eight months of it, dense as wet wool. Stronger than most. She'd carried it well, which meant she'd carried it fully, which meant it would come out heavy.

I drew it out slow. Her breathing changed — deepened, steadied, went somewhere soft. Her hands unclenched. And the thread of him slid free of her mind and into the glass, coiling, settling, glowing faintly the way memories do when they're fresh-removed, gold at the center where the love was and grey at the edges where the dying was.

I stoppered it. Held it up to the lamp. Good vintage. Hesk would have paid me twenty for it outright — grief this clean, this complete, this *loved*, is rare. Most grief is tangled with resentment, and resentful grief sells poorly. But this was pure. A woman who had loved a man for thirty years and lost him and wanted the loss gone and nothing else.

There are buyers for that. There are always buyers. People who never got to be loved like that and will pay handsomely to know, secondhand, exactly how it felt. Somewhere in Lowmark right now there's a rich man's son who has experienced six weddings, two funerals, and a shipwreck that never happened to him, and considers himself well traveled.

That's the other half of the economy, the half the valley doesn't like to name. Nothing stolen here stays here. Grief flows downhill into wealth the way water flows downhill into the sea.

"Now," I said, and gave her back the smoothed version.

* * *

She woke from it like waking from a nap in sunlight, blinking, momentarily unsure of the room. Then her face changed. Not joy — I want to be precise about this, because the ballads (there are no ballads, but if there were) always get it wrong. What crossed her face was relief, and under the relief, a kind of bewildered guilt, and under the guilt, quiet.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's *quiet*."

"That's the idea."

"I remember him." She pressed her hand flat to her sternum again, checking. "I remember all of it. The wedding, the — he died in the spring. I remember that he died." She frowned slightly, testing the edges of it the way you test a missing tooth with your tongue. "But it's like... it's like something I was told. A story I was told about myself. He was my husband, and I loved him, and he's gone, and..." She trailed off, searching for the word, and found it, and said it with wonder: "*And.*"

And. That was the product, right there. Eight months of drowning, traded for an *and*. A sentence that continued instead of ending.

"Come back in a week," I told her, wrapping the vial in cloth. "If anything feels wrong — gaps, echoes, dreams that aren't yours — come back sooner. Sometimes the mind scabs unevenly."

"Does it hurt? Afterward?"

"Not usually." This was true. What it did instead of hurting was worse and quieter: sometimes, in the weeks after a smoothing, people catch themselves reaching for the feeling and finding the shelf empty. They stand in their kitchens holding a cup and can't locate the grief that used to live there, and they panic, briefly, thinking they've lost something else, something they can't name. Then it passes. Then the quiet wins. Then they sleep.

Marta counted out six silver onto my table, coin by coin, and each coin sounded like a small bell. At the door she paused.

"Master Pemberton. Was I — " She hesitated. "Did I love him very much?"

The question hit the exact spot I keep armored. I gave it the professional answer, the one I give every time, the one that costs me nothing and costs her everything, though she'll never know it:

"You'll have to take my word for it," I said. "But yes. It was very much. Anyone could see that."

She nodded, satisfied, and went out into the street carrying her husband's ghost in a bottle and a story about herself that I had sold her along with the peace. The door closed. The shop was quiet.

I sat there for a while with the wrapped vial in my hands, feeling the faint warmth of it through the cloth, gold at the center, grey at the edges. Thirty years of a marriage, distilled. Worth twenty silver wholesale. Priceless, if you were the sort of person who believed things were priceless, which I had stopped being around age eleven, for reasons I also kept bottled and buried and did not think about, ever, on principle.

I put it in the case with the others. Rows of them, shelf after shelf, each labeled in my neat hand: names and dates and contents, like a physician's ledger. *Kell, M. — grief, conjugal, 8 mo., uncomplicated.* The case held four hundred and six vials. I knew the number the way I knew my own heartbeat. I did not know, and made a point of never learning, how many of those hearts had once beaten in chests standing in this room.

The work stays clean. The ledger balances. Outside, the valley went on forgetting, the shrines stayed dark, the mule at the mill declined again to die, and I locked up shop and walked home through streets where everyone I met smiled at me, because I had sanded the sharp edges off half their lives and they didn't remember enough to resent me for it.

That should have been comfort. Mostly it was.

* * *

I was three streets from home, thinking about supper and nothing else, carefully nothing else, when I saw her.

A girl sitting on the low wall outside the chandler's, legs swinging, watching me with the particular patience of someone who has been waiting and intends for you to know it. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Thin as a fence rail, dark hair chopped short with what looked like a knife's opinion rather than scissors'. Big eyes, watchful, too old for the face around them.

I knew her. Of course I knew her. Wren. The foundling from the temple steps, the one the matrons passed between households like a debt nobody wanted, the one whose childhood I'd lifted seven years ago when she was small enough that the whole of it fit in two vials — a thin, ragged childhood, mostly hunger and cold and the inside of other people's tempers, worth almost nothing on the market and taken anyway, taken because she wouldn't remember being robbed, and I was younger then, and hungry myself, and the man who taught me the trade had a rule about leaving value lying around unclaimed.

I hadn't seen her in years. She'd grown. She'd grown teeth, too, apparently, because the look she gave me wasn't a child's look at all.

"Silas Pemberton," she said. Not a greeting. An identification, like a magistrate reading a charge.

"Wren." I kept walking. She dropped off the wall and fell into step beside me, which was worse than blocking my path. Blocking my path I could handle.

"They say you can take things out of people's heads," she said.

"People say a lot of things."

"And put them in bottles."

"Some bottles."

She stopped walking. I made the mistake of stopping too. She looked up at me — she barely reached my shoulder — and said, in a voice completely level, the way you'd ask the time:

"What did you take from me?"

And there it was. The crack. Seven years of careful silence, seven years of the ledger balanced and the work kept clean, and one orphan girl on a chandler's wall asking one simple question, and I felt the armor move for the first time in years — felt it, actually *move*, like ice shifting on a pond.

Because here's the thing about the Hollow: she was right to ask. Something *was* taken. Two vials' worth. Ragged, thin, nearly worthless — and gone. And she remembered nothing except the shape of the hole where it used to be, the way you remember a tooth long after the tooth is gone, the tongue returning and returning to the gap.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

It was a lie. It was a bad lie, and I'm a good liar, which tells you something about how badly I wanted it to be true.

She studied my face for a long moment. Whatever she saw there, she apparently decided to keep — she tucked it away somewhere behind those too-old eyes, filed for later.

"All right," she said pleasantly. "Another time, then."

And she turned and walked off down the lane toward the temple quarter, whistling, light as anything, while I stood in the street telling myself, firmly, in the tone I use for ledgers and for sparks on dry floors, that it was nothing, that she was nothing, that a girl with a hole in her head and a guess in her pocket was no threat to a businessman with four hundred and six vials and a spotless reputation.

The work stays clean.

I repeated it all the way home. It almost worked.

Almost.
