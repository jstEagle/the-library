## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Door

I want it on the record that I did not sleep well. Not because of the girl — I want that understood too — but because the rain came in under my shutters around the second bell and found the gap in the floorboard by the bed that I have been meaning to fix for three years and have never fixed, because a man who fixes things starts fixing everything, and then where would he be? Comfortable. Content. A man with nothing left to put off until tomorrow is a man who has to look at today.

So: the rain kept me up. The girl had nothing to do with it.

I got up before dawn anyway, which is habit, not nerves, and went down to the shop to do inventory, which is discipline, not avoidance. Four hundred and six vials on the shelves, arranged by vintage the way a wine merchant arranges his cellar. Grief, first pressing. Regret, aged seven years. First love, still bright, best taken young. Homesickness, which nobody buys because homesickness is free if you're willing to just stay sad about it.

The shop is called Pemberton's Restorative Tonics, and the sign says TONICS, and the ledger says memories, and only I say both.

I was halfway through counting the shelf of childhoods — always the hardest shelf to count, because they all look alike in their little glass sleeves, summer after summer of somebody else's mother calling them in from the river — when the knock came.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient the way debt collectors are patient.

"We're closed," I said, because the sign said so, and because it was true for values of *closed* that included *go away*.

The knock came again. Same three knocks. Same patience.

I know every kind of knocker in this city, and I knew before I touched the latch that this one wasn't going to be bought off with a bottle of tonic or a lie about hours. There's a weight to a knock like that. It's the knock of someone who has already decided the door is going to open and is merely giving you the dignity of doing it yourself.

I opened it.

She was standing in the grey light with her hands in her pockets and her chin up, and up close she was younger than she'd seemed in the street yesterday. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Thin in the way of people who grew up eating whatever the temple kitchens had too much of, which is usually bread and opinions. Her hair was dark and cut short with what looked like a knife and no mirror, and her eyes were brown, ordinary brown, except that they were aimed at me with the specific gravity of something falling.

"You're the memory man," she said.

"I'm a purveyor of restoratives."

"You're the man who takes things out of people's heads and sells them back to them." She said it flat, no spit in it, which was worse. Spit I could work with. "My name's Wren."

"I didn't ask."

"No," she agreed. "You didn't."

And then she just stood there, letting the silence do its work, and I'll tell you what I noticed, because noticing is my trade the way lifting is some other men's. I noticed her boots were good boots, temple-issue, resoled twice. I noticed her right hand, in her pocket, was holding something small and hard by the shape of it. And I noticed that she stood like a person who has practiced standing places — doorways, queues, the edge of other people's conversations — until not being wanted stopped being an injury and became a skill.

I know that posture. I invented that posture.

"Whatever you're selling," I said, "I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling." She took her hand out of her pocket. In it was a coin — no. Not a coin. A token, lead-grey, stamped with the sigil of the Foundling House of Saint Verity, worn nearly smooth. "They gave me this when I aged out. It's worth one meal and a lecture. I've had the meal."

I should have shut the door. Let me say again, for the record, for whatever ledger they keep on men like me: I should have shut the door.

Instead I heard myself say, "There's tea," and stepped aside, and she walked past me into my shop like she'd been walking into rooms she wasn't wanted in her whole life, which, I suspected, she had.

* * *

She didn't sit. She stood in the middle of the floor and turned slowly, reading my shelves the way a soldier reads a battlefield, and I made the tea because making tea is something to do with your hands when your hands want to shake and you'd rather die than let them.

"These are all people's?" she said. Not a question. She tapped a vial with one finger — gently, almost politely, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Whole lives. In jars."

"Bottled recollection. Therapeutic extraction. The terminology varies by customer."

"And what do they pay you?"

"To take?"

"To give back."

"Ah." I poured the tea. "That depends on how much of it they want returned, and in what condition. A widow who wants her grief lifted pays one price. If she decides, two years on, that she misses even the grief — because grief is proof she loved him, you understand, and a heart without grief over him feels like a heart that never bothered — then she pays another price to have a measure of it restored. Diluted. Painless grief. Grief with the edges sanded off."

"That's obscene."

"That's medicine. Would you like sugar? I only ask once."

She ignored the tea entirely, which told me she hadn't come to be civilized. "The Foundling House keeps records," she said. "Ledgers. Who came in, who went out, who was placed where. Mine has gaps. Seven years' worth of gaps, from age six to thirteen, and then I turn up again in the records like I'd never been gone, except nobody remembers me being gone either. The matron who signed me out at fourteen swore she'd known me all along. Swore it crying, actually, because she thought I was testing her." Wren turned and looked at me, and her voice stayed level, and I admired it and hated it in equal measure. "But there's one entry. One line, in the whole seven years. Payment received. Services rendered. And the payment column isn't coin."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, the valley went on being the valley — carts, gulls, the bell at Saint Low's marking the hour in its cracked voice.

"What was in the payment column?" I asked, though I knew. I have always known. That's the filthy secret of my trade: the seller always knows, and pretends the knowing away, the way a man pretends the dog he sold isn't waiting at the old house.

"A memory," she said. "One memory, extracted from a ward of the Foundling House, age approximately six years, description matching mine. Sold to a private buyer. Buyer's name redacted." She held up the token. "This is the only thing I own from before fourteen. I don't remember getting it. I don't remember the House. I don't remember a mother, a father, a fire, a flood, a plague, a war — anything that might explain why a child goes missing from the records for seven years. Everyone else in this valley has a hole somewhere. A dead parent they've paid to forget, a shame they've paid to bury. Their holes are chosen. They bought them." Her hand closed around the token until the knuckles went white. "Mine wasn't chosen. Somebody chose it for me. And the ledger says the somebody was you."

Now. Here is where I tell you what I said, and here is where you understand that I am very good at what I do.

What I said was: "Miss — Wren — I extract voluntarily. Every client signs. Every client remembers signing, remembers walking in, remembers the terms. If your name were in my books, you'd have a contract with your own signature on it, and you'd remember the day you signed it, because the consent is the one thing I never touch. It's the whole foundation of the business. Take away consent and I'm not a restorer, I'm a burglar of the skull, and the Hollow has laws about—"

"Do you have my name in your books?"

Silence. The particular silence of a man deciding between lies and finding them all the same weight.

Because here's the thing. Here's the thing I've kept down at the bottom of myself for eleven years, under the floorboards of my own skull, weighted like a body in a river.

Yes.

Not in the current ledgers. I burned those pages the same season, all of them, a dozen extractions across three months, and I told myself it was tidiness. But I remember the work. You don't forget the work. A girl brought in by a man wearing gloves in summer, a man who paid triple and didn't haggle, which should have been my first warning, because haggling is the soul of commerce and a man who doesn't haggle isn't buying a thing, he's buying a silence. The girl was small and dark-haired and bit the gloved man's hand hard enough to bleed, and they gave her something sweet beforehand, the way you'd dose a dog, and I told myself the sweetness was kindness.

Six years old. The extraction took four passes. Children's memories are slippery; they haven't set yet, like wax, like mortar. I remember the shape of what came out — I try not to, I succeed most days — a kitchen, a woman singing low, the smell of woodsmoke and burnt honey, a hand smoothing hair back off a hot forehead. A fever. Someone sitting up all night with a feverish child, singing so the child wouldn't hear her being afraid.

That was the memory. That was what the glove-man paid triple for: not the girl's whole childhood, just that. One night. One woman. One song.

I never asked why. Asking why is how thieves become accomplices.

"I don't discuss individual clients," I said aloud, to the girl standing in my shop with her white knuckles and her knife-cut hair. "Past or present. Professional discretion is—"

"I know what it is." She set the token down on my counter, precisely, square to the edge, like a chess piece. "I've spent three years finding you. Did you know that? Three years. I worked the docks in Casterhale, I carried messages, I saved every copper. I learned which traders came up-valley and which ones whispered. And everyone whispers about you, memory man. They whisper like you're a priest. *He gave me back my wedding day. He took the war out of my husband.* Nobody whispers about the price. Nobody asks what happens to the parts you take out. Where do they go?"

"They're stored. Securely. Nothing is destroyed."

"But they're *sold*."

"Some are purchased. By the original owners, or their kin—"

"By anyone?" She stepped closer, and now, finally, there was heat in her voice, the first crack in her own armor, and I recognized it because it mirrored mine exactly. "Anyone with coin can buy a stranger's childhood? Can buy — I don't know — can buy a dead woman singing to a sick little girl, and own it, and I don't even get to remember I ever had it?"

The kettle ticked. My mouth was dry and the tea sat untouched between us like a truce neither side would sign.

"Wren," I said, and her name came out of me wrong, too familiar, and I saw her catch it, saw her file it away the way I'd have filed it. "If such a transaction occurred — hypothetically — the buyer's identity would be protected. That's the discretion."

"Then I'll buy it back."

"It's not for sale."

"Everything in this room is for sale. It's the entire point of the room." She swept her arm at the shelves, four hundred and six little glass lives catching the grey morning light. "Name a price. I'll work it off. I'll carry, scrub, steal — I'm told I've got a talent for stealing, though I can't imagine where I'd have learned it." Something flickered across her face when she said that, quick as a fish turning, there and gone: a half-second where she looked at me differently. Like a question had moved behind her eyes without her permission.

I felt that look in my chest like a hook set in deep water.

"There is no price," I said, "because there is no vial. Hypothetically speaking, stock from that period is long dispersed. Sold on. Gone."

Something went out of her then. Not hope — I don't think she'd arrived with hope; hope is for people who expect doors to open kindly. What went out of her was the last of the pretense that this was a negotiation. She picked up her token, squared it to her palm, and looked at me with those ordinary brown eyes, and said, quietly:

"You're lying. You're a very good liar, but you breathe wrong when you do it. Little short breaths, up here." She touched her own chest, just below the collarbone. "I don't know how I know that. Isn't that funny? I don't remember learning anything about you, and I know how you breathe when you lie."

And she turned and walked to the door, and I let her, because stopping her meant touching the thing at the bottom of the river, and I have built a whole life on the principle that some things stay sunk.

At the threshold she paused. Didn't turn around.

"The buyer," she said. "The one who paid triple. Gloves in summer. I've been asking about him too, up and down the valley, three years. Do you know what I found out? Nobody will talk about him either. Not the way they won't talk because they're embarrassed. The way they won't talk because they're *afraid*, and can't say of what." She pulled the door open. Grey light, grey street, the bell of Saint Low's beginning to mark the hour. "Seven years missing out of my head, memory man. And now the shrines are lighting up all over the valley, and the priests are weeping in the streets saying the gods are answering again, and do you know what I prayed for last night? At the shrine that glowed? I prayed to whoever's listening. I said: *show me what he took.*"

She stepped out into the lane.

"This morning," she said, "there's a bruise on my wrist shaped like a grown-up's hand, holding it. Holding it *gently*. I never had that bruise an hour ago." She looked back at me then, and her face was pale and steady and absolutely certain. "Your gods are answering, thief. And they're starting with me."

She walked off down the lane toward the temple quarter, and this time she wasn't whistling.

I stood in my doorway for a long time, telling myself the ledger balances, telling myself the work stays clean, telling myself the story I have told myself for eleven years: that I took nothing that wasn't given, that I broke nothing that wasn't broken, that a man who sells comfort cannot be blamed for the shape of the hunger that buys it.

Then I went inside, and I took down the oldest ledger, the one with the burned-out stub of pages near the front, and I blew the dust off it, and my hands were not quite steady, and my breath was coming short, up here, just below the collarbone.

Outside, far off toward the temple quarter, a bell began to ring that I had never heard ring before — high and silver and wrong, a sound like a struck star — and every gull in the valley rose screaming off the rooftops at once, as if something enormous had opened one eye beneath the world.
