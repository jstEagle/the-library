## 3. Prayers Answered

Wren had lived in the Hollow for eleven years and had never once seen the shrine on Tanner's Row do anything.

It was a small thing, the shrine — a squat stone basin crusted with lichen, set into the wall between a cobbler's shop and a house that smelled permanently of tallow. There was a worn groove in the lip of it where generations of thumbs had pressed, back when people still believed pressing your thumb there did something. Nobody pressed anymore. The Hollow didn't allow for belief. That was the whole point of the place. Magic couldn't cross its borders; prayers went nowhere; gods, if they existed at all, kept their distance like respectable neighbors avoiding a bad street. People came to the valley to hide from things that answered back.

Wren passed the shrine twice a day, every day, on her way to the wash-house and back. She'd stopped seeing it years ago, the way you stop seeing a crack in a ceiling. It was furniture. It was nothing.

So when she rounded the corner that gray morning with a basket of wet linen on her hip and saw an old woman kneeling in front of the basin with her hands raised as if catching rain — and saw light moving under the woman's hands, light with no source, green-gold and slow, like something breathing underwater — Wren stopped so hard she nearly dropped the basket into the mud.

She looked over her shoulder. The street was mostly empty. A man was leading a mule toward the farrier's. Two boys were throwing stones at a gutter-sparrow. None of them had noticed. Or none of them wanted to.

"Grandmother," Wren said carefully. "You should get up. The stones are cold."

The old woman didn't turn around. Her face was wet. "It's warm," she said. "The stone's warm. Sixty years I've been coming here. Sixty years, girl." Her voice cracked open in the middle. "It never once answered."

Wren crouched beside her, basket forgotten, and put her hand flat against the shrine's rim.

It *was* warm. Warm like a sleeping animal. And beneath her palm she felt it — not heard it, felt it, the way you feel a drum through a floor — a slow pulse, patient and enormous, like the heartbeat of something very large lying just beneath the surface of the world.

Her skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. Every hair on her arms stood up.

She snatched her hand back.

*That's wrong,* she thought, and the thought arrived already dressed in fear. *That is deeply, badly wrong.* Because everyone in the Hollow knew the rule the way they knew which seasons flooded: magic doesn't come here. Whatever else the valley was — poor, muddy, forgettable — it was safe. It was the one place in the world where nothing watched you. People paid good money for that. People built their whole lives inside that fact the way you build a house inside a wall.

And now something on the other side of the wall had turned over in its sleep.

"Has anyone else seen this?" Wren asked.

The old woman wiped her face with her sleeve. "I ran for the priest first thing. He told me I was dreaming on my feet." She laughed, a dry startled sound. "Me. Dreaming. I buried three husbands and a daughter, girl. I know what's real."

Wren glanced up and down the row again. The boys had given up on the sparrow. The mule-man was gone. Ordinary morning, ordinary mud, ordinary gray sky pressing down on the rooftops like a lid.

Except for the light. Except for the warmth. Except for the pulse under the stone, which she could still feel in her palm now, faintly, like a word stuck on the tongue.

"I'll fetch someone," Wren said, standing. "You stay here."

"Why?" said the old woman, and smiled up at her with a face full of tears and terror and joy all at once, and Wren thought: *that's what it looks like when hope comes back after sixty years, and it looks like a wound.*

"Because if it's real," the old woman said, "then everything we were told about this valley is a lie."

---

Wren went first to the wash-house, because she was paid by the bundle and Mrs. Corrigan docked wages for lateness with the enthusiasm of a woman counting coins in her sleep. She wrung sheets, hung sheets, folded sheets, and said nothing to anyone, but her hands shook so badly on the third line that a pillowcase blew off into the mud and she had to chase it down the alley like a fool.

All the while, the feeling stayed in her palm. Not the warmth — that had faded. Something else. A kind of attention. Like being watched by someone who hadn't yet decided whether to speak.

She hated it. She noticed that she hated it, and was surprised by how much. Wren was not a fearful person by constitution; you couldn't be, growing up the way she had, passed between households in the Hollow like a chore nobody wanted, fed at six tables, slept in four beds, belonging nowhere enough to be missed. Fear required stakes. You had to have something to lose before the dark could threaten you.

But this — this reached past everything. This felt like the ground itself reconsidering whether to hold her up.

By midday she'd made up her mind, and after she'd collected her copper bits she walked, without quite deciding to, toward the one person in the Hollow who might know what a returning god looked like.

Not a priest. The priests here were ceremonial at best, men who swept shrines and recited genealogies and had never in their lives expected an answer. No — Wren went to Silas Pemberton.

Everyone knew what Silas was. He didn't hide it; hiding would have been pointless in a town this size. He took memories out of heads and sold them back in prettier shapes. Grief became peace. Shame became indifference. Bad nights became blank ones. Half the Hollow owed him their sanity and half resented him for it, and the two halves overlapped almost completely.

Wren had her own history with Silas Pemberton. She kept it in a locked room at the back of her mind and did not open the door. But she knew the shape of the lock. She knew there was a door.

His shop sat on Cordwainer Lane behind a curtain of dried herbs that smelled like dust and lavender. The sign above the door read PEMBERTON — REMEDIES, which was either a joke or a legal fiction; probably both. Wren pushed inside without knocking, the way you did with Silas, because knocking gave him time to arrange his face.

He was at his workbench, sorting vials by color, and he looked up with the expression he always wore — mild, amused, faintly bored, a man watching weather he'd already predicted.

"Wren," he said. "You're dripping on my floor."

"It's raining."

"It's drizzling. There's a difference, and my floor knows it." He set down a vial of something amber. "What is it? You only come here when something's wrong, and you always stand exactly three feet inside the door, like a cat testing ice."

Wren realized she was, in fact, standing three feet inside the door. She stepped forward another foot, purely out of spite, and told him about the shrine.

She told it plainly: the old woman, the light, the warmth, the pulse. She watched his face while she talked, and this was why she'd really come — not for answers, but to watch. Because Silas Pemberton had a face like a locked drawer, and Wren had spent years learning locks.

What she saw, when she got to the part about the heartbeat under the stone, was this: his hands went still.

Not dramatically. Not a flinch. Just — stillness, total and sudden, the stillness of a man who has heard a floorboard creak upstairs in a house he believed empty. His expression didn't change at all. His eyes didn't widen. But every part of him that had been casually sorting vials simply stopped, the way water stops when it freezes.

"You felt it," he said. "Through your hand."

"Yes."

"And it frightened you."

It wasn't a question, and Wren bristled anyway. "Shouldn't it?"

"No," Silas said, and picked up his sorting again, and his voice was light and easy and completely wrong. "No, it shouldn't. Stone holds heat from the day. Lichen phosphoresces in damp weather — there are varieties that grow in cellars, ask any cheesemonger. Old women dream. You stood in the rain and caught a chill and now you're inventing miracles." He smiled at her. "The Hollow works, Wren. That's rather the entire point of it."

"The stone was warm at dawn," Wren said. "After a night of rain."

Silas sorted vials.

"Your hands stopped," she said.

"My hands are always stopping. It's a condition." He glanced up, and for half a second — less than half — something moved behind his eyes that wasn't boredom at all. It looked, absurdly, like grief. Then the drawer shut again. "Go home, Wren. Dry your stockings. If the shrine starts preaching sermons, I'll be sure to attend."

She left. She made herself leave calmly, one foot after the other, because running would tell him he'd gotten to her, and she hated — with a sudden clarity that surprised her — the idea of Silas Pemberton knowing he'd gotten to her.

But outside, in the drizzle, she pressed her right palm against her coat and thought: *he believes me. He believes me completely. He's just decided the truth is more dangerous than the lie.*

And she wondered, walking home through the gray streets with the feeling still humming faintly in her bones, how many other lies Silas Pemberton had decided were safer than truths — and whether one of them had her name on it.

---

* * *

By evening, the whole of Millbrook knew.

Millbrook was the largest village in the eastern Hollow, all of nine streets and a mill, and news traveled through it faster than decency. By the time Wren finished supper at Mrs. Corrigan's crowded table, the story had grown a second shrine (false), a weeping statue (unverified), and a child born speaking a dead language (almost certainly invented, though nobody could prove it wasn't). The taproom of the Drowned Ox was full past capacity, and Wren went because everyone went, and because staying alone in her attic room with the humming in her bones had become unbearable.

The talk inside was loud and strange. She'd never heard the Hollow argue about theology before; there had never been anything to argue about. Now the room had split itself cleanly down the middle like a wishbone.

One side said it was nothing. A trick of light, a fever, a prank — someone had planted a firefly lantern in the basin, someone had brewed a glowing draught, someone was selling something. This side was louder, because fear usually is.

The other side said it was the beginning. The gods were coming back. The old stories were true — the Sunfather, the Ninefold Mother, the Bell-Ringer who weighed souls — and the long silence was ending, and the faithful would be rewarded, and the wicked had better start worrying. This side was drunker.

Wren stood near the door with a cup of small beer she wasn't drinking and listened to both halves shout, and felt the cold spreading through her that neither side seemed to grasp.

Because both sides wanted the same impossible thing: for the world to go back to having rules. Either no gods, or kind gods. Either silence, or a voice worth hearing. Nobody in the Drowned Ox that night was asking the question Wren couldn't stop asking, which was:

*What if it's answering, but it isn't kind?*

She'd felt the pulse under the stone. It hadn't felt like a father. It hadn't felt like a mother. It had felt like something vast turning over, noticing, taking an interest. Interest, Wren thought, was not the same as love. A hawk takes an interest in a field.

"Girl. Girl! You're the one who found it, aren't you?"

An elbow, a tankard, a red-faced cooper leaning into her space, breath like cider and onions. "Old Marta's girl. Saw it with your own eyes, did you?"

"I saw light," Wren said. "I don't know what it was."

"Gods, girl, say it plain — the gods are back!" He spread his arms to the room, triumphant. "Sixty years of nothing and now they're *back*, and the first thing they do is answer an old widow. That's mercy, that is. That's the Sunfather remembering his own."

"Or," said a thin voice from the corner, a tanner's wife with sharp eyes, "it's something remembering us. Those aren't the same thing, Tam Cooper, and you'd know it if you'd ever read anything heavier than a price list."

The room roared. Wren slipped out the side door into the yard, where the noise dropped to a muffle and the rain had stopped and the clouds were tearing open in ragged strips.

And that was when she saw the second light.

It wasn't the shrine. It was the well at the center of the yard — the Drowned Ox's own well, a plain circle of stones that had given up nothing but cold water for a hundred years. Light was rising out of it. Green-gold, slow, breathing. The same light. Climbing the wet stones like ivy, pooling on the cobbles, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the pulse — *there, there, there* — steady as a heart.

Wren backed away until her shoulders hit the tavern wall.

It wasn't one shrine. It wasn't a trick, a lantern, a fevered old woman's dream. It was everywhere. It was under everything. The whole valley — the safe valley, the blessedly deaf valley, the one place in the world where nothing listened — was sitting on top of something that had woken up.

And as she stood there, heart slamming, the light in the well brightened. Once. Twice. Deliberate, like a lamp being trimmed. Like attention.

Like something on the far side of a wall, turning to face the knock.

Then it guttered out, and the yard was dark and ordinary, and Wren discovered she was crying without having decided to, great silent tears she wiped away furiously with her sleeve, angry at herself, angry at the well, angry at sixty years of safety that had apparently been a lease, not a deed.

"Bad night for wells."

She spun. Silas Pemberton stood at the edge of the yard, collar up, hands in his pockets, looking at the well with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not amusement. Not boredom. He looked like a man reading a bill of sale for his own house.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." He came closer, slowly, eyes never leaving the water. "It's in the chapel cistern too. And the millrace. Old Marta's shrine was just the first place it bothered to show itself." He crouched by the well's rim, studied the black water, and did not touch it. Wise, Wren thought. "Do you know what the interesting question is?"

"Whether it's a god."

"No." He straightened. "The interesting question is why now. Things like this don't wake on their own. Something called, or something broke, or—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, as if he'd said one word too many.

"Or what?"

"Nothing." The drawer shut. The mild face returned, and Wren could have screamed at him for it. "Go home, Wren. Lock your door. And if you feel the pulse again — don't touch it. Don't answer it. Whatever you do, don't let it know you're listening."

"That's not advice," she said. "That's fear wearing advice's coat."

For a moment Silas said nothing. Rain dripped off the eaves between them, counting seconds.

"Yes," he said quietly. "It is."

He walked out of the yard, and Wren stood alone in the dark with the well, and the last thing she saw before she fled inside was this: on the black surface of the water, faint as breath on glass, the light rose one final time —

— and shaped itself, briefly, unmistakably, into the outline of a road leading east, out of the valley.

Toward the capital. Toward the king.

Toward whatever had broken loose in the world beyond the walls, and was now, patiently, knocking.
