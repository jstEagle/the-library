## 4. The Knight Who Fell From the Sky

The trouble with living in a valley that eats magic was that everything strange had to arrive the ordinary way: on foot, by cart, or — as it turned out on the third night of the first week of answered prayers — out of the sky.

I was walking home from the Widow Marsh's place with her grief still warm in my pocket and a coin purse considerably heavier than my conscience, which is to say I was having a good evening. The Hollow at dusk does its best work then. The mist comes down off the rim like a curtain being drawn on a bad play, and the whole valley goes soft and gray and forgettable, which is exactly how its residents like it. People pay good money to forget. I'd built a life on that simple arithmetic.

Then something screamed overhead.

Not a bird. Not a person. A sound like metal arguing with the air and losing, and I looked up in time to see a shape tumble over the eastern rim of the valley — the rim no one crosses, the rim where the world outside starts and our particular peace ends — trailing smoke and what I would later learn was blood, though at the time I assumed it was just more of the valley's weather.

It came down in the old orchard behind the tannery, through the branches of three dead apple trees, and hit the ground with a sound I felt in my teeth.

Now, here's the thing about me, and I want to be honest about it because honesty is rare currency in my trade: my first instinct was to walk away. Whatever fell out of the sky belonged to the sky's business, not mine. The Hollow had kept me safe for twenty years precisely because I minded my own affairs and helped other people unmind theirs. A man who steals memories for a living develops a keen sense for trouble, and trouble has a smell. This smelled like iron and burning feathers.

But the orchard was between me and home. And the thing was groaning.

So I did what any sensible coward does: I approached sideways, hands visible, ready to run.

* * *

He was a knight. That much was obvious even before I got close — the armor saw to that, scorched plate etched with a sigil I didn't recognize, a sun split by a sword. He'd landed on his back in the mud between the apple trees, one arm flung wide, the other clamped against his chest like a man holding shut a door with something terrible behind it.

His armor was wrong. I want to be clear about that, because it matters. It wasn't dented from a fall. It was *burned*, in patterns — long deliberate lines of scorch that followed no fire I knew, curling across the breastplate in shapes almost like writing. And beneath the burned edges, the steel shimmered faintly, the way water shimmers over a hot road. Which should have been impossible, because nothing in the Hollow shimmered. Magic couldn't live here. That was the entire point of the place.

Whatever he carried against his chest was keeping the magic alive even now, dying or not.

"Hello," I said, because I'm a professional, and professionals open with something bland. "Can you hear me?"

His helmet turned toward me with a grinding effort. The visor slit showed me one eye, and I'll tell you what I saw there, because I've spent twenty years reading eyes — it's most of my job. Fear. Not the fear of a dying man. The fear of a man carrying something that mustn't fall into certain hands, who has just realized he's fallen into the worst possible ones.

"You," he rasped. His voice came out wet and broken. "You're the thief."

"I'm a shopkeeper," I said. "Of sorts."

"The Pemberton man." A laugh that cost him dearly; I heard ribs move in ways ribs shouldn't. "They said you were real. They said the valley really — " He coughed, and the cough brought up red. "Help me up."

"Absolutely not."

"They're coming." His hand spasmed against his chest, and for the first time I saw what he held: a vial. Small, no longer than my thumb, glass dark as smoked mirror, sealed with wax stamped by a signet I also didn't recognize. "Behind me. On the road. Inquisitors. God-touched." He said the words like they were names of diseases. "If they take this — "

"Take what? What is it?"

"A memory." His eye found mine and held it with the last of his strength, and I have never in my life been so thoroughly *seen* by a stranger. "You deal in memories. So you'll know what it's worth. You'll know why they can't have it back."

I crouched down despite myself. Old habits. A memory-vial is a rare thing outside the Hollow — sealing a memory into glass requires either divine craft or the kind of artifice that costs more than most castles. I'd seen four in my life. I'd owned none.

"What's in it?" I asked.

And here is where I made my mistake, if you want to call it that. Because I asked the question the way I ask all my questions — idly, professionally, already calculating resale value — but the knight heard something else in my voice. Or maybe he was simply past choosing. Dying men don't haggle.

"King Aldric," he whispered. "Three years ago. The night before the king stopped being the king."

The wind moved through the dead orchard. Somewhere behind us, the tannery dog started barking at nothing, which in the Hollow usually means something.

"That's treason," I said. "That's the kind of treason that gets villages burned."

"Yes," the knight agreed, with terrible calm. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about King Aldric, because every child in the Nine Valleys knows it. Aldric the Just, crowned young, blessed by all seven of the old gods at his coronation — back when the gods still bothered answering, before they went silent and left the world to fend for itself. Twenty years of good harvests and fair courts and roads you could travel without paying toll to bandits. Then, about three years ago, something changed. Nobody could say what. The king grew quiet. The reforms stopped. The old friends were dismissed, one by one, replaced by pale courteous men nobody remembered appointing. Taxes rose. Prisons filled. And last spring, the Inquisition was founded — an order of god-touched zealots who claimed to speak for the silent gods, burning shrines and questioning anyone who remembered the old days too fondly.

People said grief. People said illness. People said, quietly, in the way people say things when they don't want the walls to hear, that the king had changed, that the man on the throne wore Aldric's face but wasn't quite Aldric anymore.

Everyone said it. No one meant anything by it. That's how kingdoms work.

"I'm Ser Corwin Hale," the knight said, "of the King's Own, formerly, and I am asking you, thief, to take this vial south to Casterhale, to a woman named Odile Voss who keeps a candle-shop near the Tanner's Bridge, and to put it in her hands and no others."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only man in a hundred miles who won't look inside it."

That stopped me. "Why wouldn't I?"

"Because looking is how they find it." His breath was going ragged now, each word bought at cost. "The vial is warded. It shows itself only to the ignorant. The moment someone views the memory inside, it — " he coughed, waved vaguely — "announces itself. Lights up like a beacon in the god-touched senses. Every inquisitor within fifty miles will feel it. That's how they tracked me. My second broke the seal on the pass. We buried him this morning."

I looked at the vial. Dark glass, dull wax, utterly unremarkable. The kind of object you could carry through a checkpoint in your boot and forget you owned.

"And if I just keep walking?" I said. "If I leave you here in the mud with your treason and your beacon, and go home to my supper?"

"Then I die alone in an orchard," he said, "and tomorrow the Inquisition burns this valley looking for what I carried, and they will not be gentle, and they will not be quick, and they will start with the shopkeepers." His eye glinted. "Starting, I'd imagine, with the one who deals in stolen memories. They have lists, Master Pemberton. I've seen the lists. Your name is on several."

I hated him a little, right then. There's a special skill some dying men have, of making their emergency your emergency, and Ser Corwin Hale had it in abundance.

"How long do you have?" I asked instead.

"If I stay still? An hour. If I talk? Less." He managed something that was almost a smile. "So let's not waste it arguing. Take the vial."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," he said. "But you crouched down. Thieves always crouch. It's the crouching that gives you away."

* * *

Here's what actually happened, and I'll swear to this version until the day I die, whatever version history ends up with:

I took the vial because leaving it in the mud with a corpse was idiotic, and keeping it was safer than the alternatives, and delivering it was a problem for a version of me with better options. That's the truth. I was not brave. I was not moved by duty or crown or country. I was a man doing risk arithmetic in a dead orchard while a knight bled out in front of him, and the arithmetic came out ugly in every direction, so I picked the direction that let me sleep — badly, but sleep.

Ser Corwin, naturally, chose to interpret it otherwise.

"Good," he breathed, when my fingers closed around the vial. It was warm. That unsettled me more than anything else that night — warm glass, in the Hollow, where nothing holds heat it isn't given. "Good man."

"I'm really not."

"No one is. That's rather what the vial proves." He tried to lift his head and failed, and settled for turning it toward me. "Listen. The woman in Casterhale — Odile Voss — she was the king's confessor, once. She'll know what to do. She's expecting... she's been expecting proof..." His voice frayed. He gathered himself with visible effort, a man hauling a heavy load uphill. "There are three relics. Of the old gods. Tongue, eye, heart. The rite can't finish without — no. No, that's hers to explain. I'm saying too much. The pain makes one chatty."

"You're dying," I pointed out. "Chatty is allowed."

"Don't tell Wren I died like this," he said suddenly, and the name went through me like a fishhook. "In the mud. Tell her I stood."

"Wren," I repeated, carefully, the way you repeat a word when you've just stepped on something that might be a trap. "Who's Wren?"

"My daughter." His eye had gone distant, fixed on the mist above us, on the rim of the valley where the stars were coming out — real stars, the kind the Hollow's fog usually hides. "No. Not mine. Mine died. She's — we collected her. Off a road. Years ago. She'll want to know I didn't run."

I said nothing. There was nothing honest to say, and I've built a career on never saying dishonest things to dying men — they can tell, somehow. It's the one market where counterfeit goods get detected instantly.

"She has your look," Ser Corwin murmured. "Did you know that? Around the eyes. When I found her begging in Casterhale, I thought — " A cough took him, long and deep, and when it passed he was quieter, smaller, the voice running out of coin. "Strange valley. Strange trade you practice. Selling people their own lives back."

"It pays the rent."

"Does it?" He looked at me then, truly looked, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of a man appraising goods he intended to buy regardless of price. "And what do you sell them, exactly? Peace? Or the comfortable lie that peace is possible?"

"I don't answer theological questions after sundown."

"Smart." His hand crept along the mud, found my wrist — armor gauntlet, freezing, heavier than a man's hand should be — and gripped it. "Master Pemberton. Last words are a currency too. Spend them well." He pulled me closer. His breath was iron and blood. "The crown is empty."

"What?"

"The crown." His eye was very bright now, fever-bright, and behind the brightness something was going out like a lamp starved of oil. "Is empty. Has been empty. Three years. The thing that sits on the throne — it isn't a man. It's what's left when a man is — " his grip tightened, bruising — "hollowed. And it's finishing something. A rite. Midwinter. And if it finishes, there will be no gods left to answer anyone, ever, anywhere, and the silence will be — "

He stopped.

Not dramatically. Not with a speech trailing off into poetry. He just stopped, mid-word, the way a clock stops, and the grip on my wrist eased by degrees until it was only the weight of a dead man's gauntlet resting where it happened to rest.

The orchard was very quiet. The tannery dog had given up. Mist moved between the apple trees like something patient taking inventory.

I sat in the mud beside a dead knight for a long moment, holding a warm vial, listening to the words come apart in my head. *Hollowed.* I knew the word. Everyone in the Hollow knew the word — it was half our name, half our joke, the thing we called the empty-headed and the heartbroken. But the way he'd said it wasn't a joke. The way he'd said it was a diagnosis.

*The crown is empty.*

I should burn the vial, I thought. Drop it down the tannery well. Bury it under the shrine that never lights. Let the Inquisition tear the valley apart and find nothing, let the kingdom keep its comfortable lie, let the world go on pretending its king was merely tired.

That was the smart play. That was the play I'd have advised any client to make, and charged them for the advice.

Instead I pried the gauntlet off my wrist, closed the knight's visor with two fingers — a courtesy, the only one I had to offer — and stood up holding a dead man's treason in my fist.

* * *

I was halfway home, moving fast, when I heard the hoofbeats on the far side of the rim.

A lot of hoofbeats. The measured, unhurried kind, the kind ridden by men who know exactly where they're going and are in no doubt they'll be welcomed when they arrive. Through the thinning mist above the eastern ridge, torchlight bloomed — a dozen flames, then two dozen, descending the switchback road that hadn't seen traffic in years.

And with the torchlight came a feeling. I don't know how else to describe it. A pressure, low in the skull, like the moment before a storm breaks — except storms don't happen in the Hollow, and neither does this, this crawling awareness of being *noticed* by something vast and cold and interested. Magic couldn't cross the valley's border. But whatever rode down that road tonight wasn't waiting politely at the line. It leaned against the border like a traveler leaning on a door, testing the hinges.

The vial pulsed once against my palm. Warm. Patient. Almost smug.

"Oh, no," I told it. "Don't think for a moment this makes us friends."

It pulsed again, and I swear on every stolen memory in my possession, it felt like laughter.

I cut off the road, cut through the marsh path behind the tannery, and ran for home — and it wasn't until I had the door barred and the lamp lit and the vial hidden in the loose stone behind the hearth that I let myself think about the name the knight had dropped, the name I'd been carefully not thinking about the entire way.

Wren. His daughter. Found begging on a road, years ago.

There was only one Wren in the Hollow. There was only one girl in the world, as far as I knew, with that particular stubborn chin and those watchful eyes and that hole in her memory shaped exactly like the childhood I'd sold off piece by piece to buyers whose faces I'd made sure never to remember.

She'd come to my door twice since the shrine flickered. She'd stood in my shop asking questions I'd dodged with a craftsman's grace.

Tomorrow, I suspected, she'd come a third time. And tomorrow, it seemed, everyone in the world was going to want something from Silas Pemberton — the dead, the divine, the damned, and the girl whose past was sitting in my strongbox alongside a dead king's secret.

I banked the fire, checked the bar on the door twice, and lay down in the dark with a dead man's last words circling my head like crows.

*The crown is empty.*

Outside, the hoofbeats reached the valley floor, and began, unhurried, to knock on doors.
