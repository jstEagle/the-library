## 5. What the Vial Showed

The vial was warm.

That was the first wrong thing about it. Memory-glass ran cold — Silas had handled enough of it to know. Cold as river stones, cold as the inside of a dead man's mouth, because memory was the coldest thing a person could carry. This one sat in his palm like a sparrow's egg, blood-warm and faintly pulsing, and he did not want to open it.

He opened it anyway. Curiosity was the one appetite he'd never learned to ration.

The knight's body lay where they'd dragged it, behind the tannery, under a scrap of sacking that Wren kept adjusting long after there was nothing left to adjust. She hovered at the edge of the lamplight now, arms crossed tight, watching him the way people watched a man defuse something.

"You don't have to," she said.

"Course I don't." He thumbed the stopper loose. "That's why I'm going to."

The memory poured out into his mind the way stolen memories always did — a lurch, a falling sensation, the world folding itself around someone else's eyes —

* * *

He was in the throne room.

Not *in* it — he was King Aldric, or riding behind the king's eyes, which amounted to the same horror. The perspective was total, immersive, the work of a master thief or a god; every detail arrived with the weight of lived experience. The smell of candlewax and old stone. The ache in the king's knees from twenty years of kneeling at war-prayers. The specific, bone-deep tiredness of a man who had won everything and found it heavy.

The hour was wrong for an audience. Past midnight. The court was empty except for one figure standing at the foot of the dais, hooded, patient, its shadow lying toward the throne instead of away from the candles.

"Your majesty prayed," the figure said. Its voice was a chorus heard through a wall — many voices, flattened into one. "We have come to answer."

"I prayed years ago." Aldric's voice, older than Silas expected, cracked with grief. "For my son. For my queen. No one came."

"The gods were sleeping. Now we wake hungry, and hunger must be fed before gifts can be given. You wish to be a good king. We can make you a perfect one. There is a difference, and the difference is you."

Silas felt the king's fear rise like floodwater — and felt something worse underneath it, the terrible arithmetic of a ruler doing sums with his own soul. A kingdom needed a king more than a king needed himself. He'd thought that way once, maybe. He'd said those words to himself in smaller rooms about smaller things.

"What would it cost?" the king asked, which was the moment Silas wanted to scream at him not to ask.

"Only what makes the crown heavy."

The figure raised its hands, and its hands were wrong — each finger ending in a different face, tiny, serene, faces Silas half-recognized: a queen, a general, a child, dozens of them, generations of them, all wearing the same mild expression of someone who has agreed to something they didn't understand. Smoke poured from the sleeves. Not smoke — *memories*, unspooling, silver-gray threads streaming out of the king's chest toward those little hands, and each thread that touched a face made the face smile wider.

Aldric did not fight it. That was the worst part. He knelt on his own dais, folded his hands, and let it happen, and through the link of the stolen memory Silas felt what he felt: relief. Sweet, shameful relief, like setting down a load carried too long. The memories went first — his mother's kitchen, his wedding day, the sound of his son laughing, the taste of the wine he'd drunk the night he was crowned — and with each one the king grew quieter, smoother, his face settling into a calm that looked, from the outside, exactly like wisdom.

*This is what peace is,* the thing said, in the king's own voice now, practicing it. *This is what mercy feels like.*

The last thing to go was a small memory, tucked deep, guarded longest: a boy on a wall, flying a kite, his father's hand on his shoulder. The smoke took it gently, the way Silas took a widow's grief — carefully, professionally, leaving no scar — and the king smiled.

And then the king stood up, and the thing settled into the space where he had been, wearing him like a coat, and turned to face the empty hall, and said, in Aldric's voice, perfect and warm:

"Long live the king."

* * *

Silas came back to himself on his knees.

He didn't remember falling. The lamp had guttered low; Wren was crouched beside him with her hand hovering near his shoulder, not quite touching, the way you approach a dog that might bite.

"You went somewhere," she said. "You were gone two minutes and your face was — " She stopped. "I've seen that face before. On people whose memories I've watched you take back out again. The ones who got more than they paid for."

He retched, dry, hands braced on the floorboards. His mouth tasted of candlewax and old stone. That was the filthiest part of the trade nobody talked about — residue. You wore other people's moments on your skin for hours after.

"It's real," he managed. "What's in here. It's not a forgery, not a dream-vial, none of the cheap tricks. It's a true memory, taken whole, from inside the king's own head." He wiped his mouth. "Which means either the king is dead and something else is sitting on that throne — "

"Or the king let it happen," Wren said quietly.

"Or the king let it happen." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "You know what the funny part is? I've done this. What that thing did. Smaller scale, better manners, but the same trade. Take the painful parts, leave the shape of a person standing. I always told myself the shape was enough. That I was giving them peace." He stared at the vial, still glowing faintly, still warm. "I just watched what the shape looks like when it's finished."

Wren was silent a moment. Then: "Who sent the knight?"

"A dying man doesn't carry a message this far by accident. Someone knew. Someone in the palace knew, and got this out, and the knight ran for the Hollow because it's the one place magic can't follow to take it back." He was thinking aloud now, fast, the thief's mind finally outrunning the fear. "Which means whoever sent it knows the Inquisition will come. Because they'll come, Wren. Not for us. For this." He held up the vial. "You don't kill the messenger for carrying bad news. You kill anyone who's *read* it."

Her face went pale in the lamplight. "Then pour it out. Forget it."

"You can't pour a memory out. And I can't forget it." He tapped his temple. "It's in here now, rent-free, and it doesn't fade like ordinary memory does. Stolen memories stick. Believe me, I know. I'm carrying forty years of other people's worst days up here, and not one of them has ever dulled."

"So we run."

"Where? The valley forgets, but it doesn't hide. If the gods are waking — and that shrine flickering yesterday says they are — then the Hollow's protection is a door closing slowly, and we're on the wrong side of it." He stood, legs unsteady, and looked down at the knight under the sacking. Old armor, well-kept, a pilgrim's badge hammered flat so it wouldn't catch light. Someone had spent money and love sending this man.

"He said something. At the end." Wren hugged herself. "'The crown is empty.' That's all?"

"That's all." Silas crouched, drew back the sacking, and looked properly at the dead man's face for the first time. Middle-aged. Kind eyes already clouding. And stitched inside the collar, nearly invisible, a scrap of parchment. He worked it free with a thief's fingers.

Three words, in a fine courtly hand:

*Find the relics.*

He showed Wren. She read it twice. "Relics of what?"

"Of the old gods. The ones who used to answer prayers before they went quiet. Every village shrine, every bell tower, every drowned river — the stories all say the gods left pieces of themselves behind when they withdrew. Bones, eyes, hearts." He heard himself say it and hated how sane it sounded. "And if some loyal fool died getting me proof that the king is hollow, then presumably the person who sent him believes the hollowing can be undone. With the right tools."

"And if it can't?"

"Then I've read a dead man's mail and the Inquisition will bury me in the same hole as him." He pocketed the note, sealed the vial, and blew out the lamp. Darkness was safer. Darkness was home. "Get your boots. We need to move the body to the river and we need to be gone from this town before — "

The knock came at the door.

Three slow strikes, evenly spaced, the kind of knocking that had never hurried anywhere in its life. Then a voice, smooth as poured cream, speaking with a courtesy that was somehow worse than shouting:

"Master Pemberton. The Inquisition regrets the hour. Please open the door, and bring whatever the knight gave you."

Through the wall, Silas heard the other sounds — boots in the street, many, surrounding the house with the unhurried patience of people who already knew how the night would end.

Wren's hand found his sleeve in the dark.

"They're early," she whispered.

"No," Silas said, easing toward the back window, the vial cold against his heart now, the three words burning in his pocket. "They're right on time. Somebody told them exactly when to knock — and it wasn't the knight."

Behind them, under the sacking, the dead man's face seemed almost peaceful, as if he'd known all along that the message would arrive safely — and that safety was never part of the bargain.
