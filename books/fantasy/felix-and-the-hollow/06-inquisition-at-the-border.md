## 6. Inquisition at the Border

Wren woke to the sound of bells she had never heard before.

That was the first wrong thing. The Hollow had three bells — the mill bell, the chapel bell, and the cracked old thing in Duncastle's yard that rang only when someone died. She knew them the way she knew her own heartbeat. These bells were none of those. They came from the high passes, thin and silver and wrong, like ice singing.

She sat up in the loft above Silas's shop, where he'd grudgingly given her a cot after the knight fell out of the sky, and pressed her ear to the cold window glass.

Down in the lane, people were standing in their doorways. Old Marta with her shawl half on. The cooper's boys, barefoot in frost. Nobody spoke. Everybody listened. In the Hollow, where nothing ever happened that the valley itself didn't permit, a strange sound was not an event. It was an omen.

The bells stopped. Then they started again, closer.

Wren dressed fast, pulled her boots on without untying them, and went down the ladder into the shop. It smelled of tallow and dried lavender and the faint copper tang of memory-vials, hundreds of them racked along the walls in rows like wasps in a nest. Silas was already there, dressed, awake, doing something she'd never seen him do: packing.

Not tidying. Packing. He moved through the racks with terrible efficiency, pulling vials, wrapping them in wool, sliding them into a leather case that had clearly been built for exactly this purpose and hadn't been opened in years. His hands were steady. His face was not.

"You heard them too," Wren said.

"I heard them." He didn't look up. "Get your things."

"What is it? Who rings bells in the passes? There's nothing in the passes."

"There's nothing *supposed* to be in the passes." He wrapped another vial. "There's a shrine at Cold Saddle. Way-shrine. It hasn't been lit since my grandfather's grandfather. This morning it's lit, and somebody's ringing its bell, and I promise you it isn't pilgrims."

Wren's stomach did something slow and cold. "The gods," she said. "It's really happening. They're answering."

"Yes."

"And you're packing vials because—"

"Because when the gods wake up, so does everyone who works for them." Now he looked at her, and what was in his face frightened her more than the bells. Not fear exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing a dog bark that he'd hoped was dead. "The Inquisition, girl. The god-touched. You've heard of them?"

Everyone had heard of them, the way you'd heard of wolves. Stories from outside the valley, carried in by traders who never stayed long. Men and women whose eyes glowed faintly when they were angry, who could smell a lie, who took heretics over the border and brought back — no. Didn't bring back anything.

"They can't come here," Wren said. "Nobody comes here. Magic doesn't work here. That's the whole point of the Hollow."

Silas buckled the case shut. "Magic doesn't work here. People do. And the god-touched are mostly people." He slung the case across his back. "Besides, they don't need magic to burn a house down. Get your things. We may need to move."

* * *

By midmorning the whole valley knew, because the whole valley had gone up to look.

Wren went with Silas and half of Duncastle's population, trudging the shepherd track toward Cold Saddle, breath steaming, the crowd quiet in the way crowds are quiet when they're afraid together. She walked close behind Silas, close enough to see the vial's outline through his coat where it hung against his chest — the dead knight's sealed memory, the thing that had fallen out of the sky two nights ago and turned her life into this. The thing Silas had watched inside of, alone, and come away from looking like a man who'd swallowed something that would never stop dissolving.

He wouldn't tell her what was in it. "You're better off," was all he said, and for once his voice had no wryness in it at all, and she had decided, temporarily, to hate him for it.

Cold Saddle was a notch between two shoulders of the mountain, a flat grey place of wind-scoured grass and one small stone shrine with a slate roof. Wren had been up here once as a child — or she assumed she had; she couldn't actually remember much of anything from before she was ten, which was its own wound, but that was an old wound and she'd learned to walk around it.

The shrine was lit.

Light poured out of it. Not firelight, not lamplight — light with no source, gold-white and steady, spilling through the little windows and under the door and pooling on the frozen grass like the shrine had drowned in it. And above the roofline stood the bell, and beside the bell stood men in grey.

Six of them. Maybe eight; the light made it hard to count. Long coats, hoods back, faces ordinary and terrible in the way of people doing a job they believe in. As Wren and the crowd crested the last rise, one of them turned, and even at forty yards Wren saw his eyes catch the light and hold it — a faint glow, banked coals, god-touched.

The crowd stopped moving all at once, like a single animal.

A woman stepped forward from the group. Tall, grey-cloaked, dark hair cropped short, a scar running through one eyebrow. She held up a scroll and her voice carried down the slope without any effort at all, flat and clear as a blade laid on a table.

"People of the Hollow. By authority of the Holy Office of the Question, these passes are closed. No soul leaves the valley by road, pass, or tunnel until our inquiry concludes. Return to your homes. Cooperate, and this will be brief."

Nobody moved. An old man near the front — Tam's grandfather, Wren realized, though she hadn't met the boy yet, wouldn't meet him for weeks; at this moment he was just a stooped shape in a brown coat — called out in a cracked voice: "Inquiry into what? What have we done?"

The woman's gaze found him. Her eyes flickered. Just slightly. Like a lamp behind frosted glass.

"A knight of the realm fell from the sky two nights past," she said. "His body has not been recovered. Certain articles in his possession are property of the Crown and the Church. We will have them."

The crowd made a sound — not words, just a low collective noise, the sound of several hundred people each privately deciding not to say anything.

And Wren felt Silas go very still beside her.

She glanced at him. He was watching the woman on the hill with an expression she'd never seen on him before, and Wren had spent weeks now learning his faces: the merchant's smile, the bored mask, the flicker of guilt he thought he hid when she asked about her childhood. This was none of them. This was a man watching a door he'd bolted twenty years ago swing open.

*They'll kill anyone who knows,* he'd said last night, finally, one sentence about the vial, staring into the fire. *Anyone. Whole villages, if it comes to that.*

Now she understood what he'd meant.

"We should go," she whispered.

"In a moment." His voice was barely air. "Look at her hands."

Wren looked. The woman held the scroll in one hand. The other hung open at her side, and hovering an inch above her palm, turning slowly, was a shard of something — glass, or crystal, or light made solid — and it was pointing. Turning like a compass needle. Turning, slowly, steadily, toward the crowd.

Toward them.

"Oh," Wren said.

"Walk," Silas said. "Don't run. Walk down the hill like we came to see the lights and we've seen them and we're bored. Talk to me about eggs."

"Eggs?"

"Anything. Go."

They turned and walked. Behind them the woman's voice continued, announcing curfews, announcing that the Office would be conducting interviews door to door, announcing that the blessing of the awakened gods rested upon all the faithful. Wren kept her eyes forward and talked about eggs, about how Marta's hens had gone off laying since the frost, about whether oaths or barley made better feed, and her voice only shook a little, and Silas matched her step for step and said nothing at all until they were well down the track and around the first bend and the shrine-light was out of sight.

Then he stopped, put his hand against a boulder, and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

Wren stood awkwardly nearby, guarding nothing, watching the road.

"The crystal," he said, wiping his mouth. "Divining shard. Attuned to whatever the knight carried. It was pointing at us."

"It was pointing at *you*. You're carrying it."

"It was pointing at us both, which means it's attuned broadly, which means—" He straightened, pressed the back of his hand to his mouth, mastered himself. "Which means every interview they conduct, every house they search, we're walking around with a lit lantern strapped to our chests."

"So we get rid of it."

"No."

"*No?*" She grabbed his sleeve. "Silas. They'll burn the valley. You said—"

"I said they'd kill anyone who knows, and I meant it, and getting rid of it doesn't help, because they know it came *here*. If we dump it in a bog, they tear apart every house in the Hollow looking for whoever buried it, and then they find us anyway, because the shard points at the person, not the object — I've seen their kind work before, in Casterhale, years ago." He was talking fast now, thinking out loud, and Wren realized with a strange lurch that he wasn't hiding it from her anymore. Whatever wall he'd kept between them — the merchant and the customer, the thief and the girl whose memories he'd sold — the Inquisition had knocked it flat in one morning. "We can't hide it and we can't keep it. Which leaves one option."

"Which is?"

"We leave with it. Through the smuggler's tunnel under Ravenfell. Tonight, before they seal the passes properly — the roads they'll close first, but the tunnels take days to find if you don't already know them, and I know them."

"Leave the Hollow." Wren said it the way you'd say *stop breathing*. "Outside. Where magic works."

"Yes."

"I've never been outside."

"I know."

"How do you know what I've— " She stopped. Of course he knew. He'd been inside her head; he'd taken whatever he'd taken; he probably knew her favorite color before she did. The anger flared up hot and familiar, and then, absurdly, collapsed, because right now the fact that Silas Pemberton had stolen her childhood was somehow the fourth most frightening thing in her life. "Fine. Fine. Tonight. But Silas — the valley. Everyone. If we run and they don't find what they want—"

"I know." He looked back up the track, toward the invisible shrine, the invisible light. "I'm going to talk to the reeve. Quietly. There are ways to make a search fail — misdirection, planted evidence, a false trail toward the lowlands. It buys time. It might buy enough." He paused. "Or it might not. Either way, Wren — tonight, you and I and that vial go over the mountain. Because the safe valley just became a trap, and I refuse to die in a trap I was born in."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and the valley spent the afternoon pretending to be normal, and both efforts failed in the same way.

Wren went home to pack, and found her landlady, Mrs. Corren, sitting in Wren's own kitchen, hands folded, waiting.

"Heard you were up at Cold Saddle," Mrs. Corren said.

"Everyone was."

"Mm." The old woman's eyes were sharp and dry. "Greycoats up there. God-touched. First time in my life I've seen the like inside the passes, and I'm seventy-one years." She leaned forward. "Girl, I've known you since you were ten years old and the Pemberton man brought you down off the mountain with no name and no history and coin enough to rent a room. I've never asked. I'm not asking now. But I'll tell you what I told your neighbor, and what half the valley is saying over fences today." She lowered her voice. "Whatever they're hunting — some folk are saying give it to them. Some folk are saying hide it. And some folk, the ones who remember the stories from outside, are saying the only thing worse than the Inquisition finding what it wants is the Inquisition leaving empty-handed and angry." She stood, joints cracking. "So. Are you the girl who runs, or the girl who stays and burns with the rest of us?"

Wren stared at her. "I don't know yet."

Mrs. Corren nodded slowly, as if that were the most honest answer available. "Hm. Well. For what it's worth, the Pemberton man came by an hour ago. Left a purse on your mantel and a note. Said you'd know what it means."

Alone, Wren opened the note. On it, in Silas's cramped, careful hand:

*Ravenfell tunnel. Moonrise. Bring nothing you love.*

She read it four times. *Bring nothing you love.* Typical Silas — practical to the bone, and underneath the practicality, a warning shaped like kindness: love things lightly, or they'll be used against you. She almost laughed. Almost threw the note in the stove.

Instead she folded it small and put it in her pocket, next to the one possession she owned that mattered, a smooth river stone she'd had since before she could remember — her only relic of a childhood someone else had sold. Then she packed a bag with bread, a knife, a spare cloak, and stood in the middle of her small room and looked at it, at the bed and the shelf and the window with its view of the mill bell, and understood that she was looking at all of it for the last time.

Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. Terror was allowed; terror was honest. But underneath the terror, something else was rising, something harder, and she examined it with surprise, the way you'd examine a stone you'd picked up that turned out to be iron.

Defiance.

They wanted the vial. They wanted the knowledge in it, the thing Silas had seen and wouldn't share, the truth that had fallen out of the sky into their laps. They wanted to seal the passes and question the valley and leave nothing living that remembered. Well. Wren had already had her memory stolen once, by a man she now traveled with and trusted more than anyone alive, and she had survived being emptied. Whatever the god-touched took from her, they'd find the same thing Silas had found: that what was left after the taking was harder than what had been there before.

"Moonrise," she said aloud to the empty room. "I'm the girl who runs. And then I come back."

* * *

Ravenfell was a black shoulder of rock on the valley's western edge, and the tunnel mouth was hidden behind a curtain of ivy and a slab of stone that Silas shifted with a lever hidden thirty years ago by men whose names he refused to say. By moonrise Wren was there, bag on her shoulder, heart hammering, and so was Silas — and so, to her astonishment, was a third figure, small and bundled and furious, arguing with Silas in a fierce whisper.

"—not staying, and that's final, I know the tunnels better than you do, my uncle ran the north route for six years—"

"Tam." Silas's voice had a strained, ground-down quality Wren had never heard. "Tam, listen to me—"

The boy turned, saw Wren, and pointed at her accusingly. "Tell him! Tell him I'm coming!"

Wren looked at the boy — twelve, maybe thirteen, all elbows and outrage, a shepherd's son from the eastern farms whom she vaguely knew by sight — and then at Silas, whose face in the moonlight was doing arithmetic she couldn't read.

"Who is this?" she asked.

"This," Silas said heavily, "is a problem."

"I'm Tam," the boy said, chin up, eyes bright with a fear he was converting directly into stubbornness. "I followed you from Cold Saddle this morning. I saw the greycoats' crystal pointing at you. I figure wherever you're going, it's away from *that*, and I want to be away from that too, and I know the tunnels, and you can't stop me because I'll just follow you in the dark and probably die, and then it'll be your fault."

Silas closed his eyes. Somewhere far above, on the high passes, the silver bells began ringing again — faster now, urgent, the sound of doors being locked.

"Wren," Silas said. "How do you feel about children?"

Wren looked at the boy, at the tunnel breathing cold air behind the shifted slab, at the moonlit valley she was about to betray by leaving — and heard herself say, in a voice steadier than she felt:

"He eats his share and carries his weight. Let him come."

Something crossed Silas's face — relief, or surrender, or the beginning of a debt he didn't yet know he owed. He shouldered his case of vials, gestured at the black mouth of the tunnel, and said:

"After you, then. All of you. And pray the greycoats are still busy with the roads — because in about an hour, everything we are is going to fit through a hole in the world, and if they're watching it, we're already dead."
