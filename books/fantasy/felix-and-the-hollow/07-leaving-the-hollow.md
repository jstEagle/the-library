## 7. Leaving the Hollow

The tunnel mouth was hidden behind a dead man's chimney.

That was Marlow's joke, back when he'd shown it to me — *a dead man's chimney, Pemberton, because only a dead man would be fool enough to use it twice.* He'd been right about that much. I'd used it once to get into the Hollow, eleven years ago, with a knife, a forged letter of passage, and no plan beyond not dying on the pass. Now I was going to use it to leave, and I still didn't have a better plan.

I stood in the ruin of the old Tolliver house at the valley's northern edge while Wren packed by candlelight, and I tried to remember why I'd ever thought staying was safe.

"The vial goes in the boot," she said. "Not your boot. Mine."

"It's safer with me."

"Nothing is safer with you. That's the whole problem." She wrapped the vial in wool, then in oilcloth, then lashed it against her calf where a smuggler's strap held it flat. She'd learned knots somewhere. Not from me. I'd made sure of that — one more thing I'd taken from her without asking.

She was sixteen now, or near enough. Thin as a fence rail, dark-eyed, all elbows and accusations. She had been following me for three days since the knight fell out of the sky at the valley's edge and died whispering nonsense into my ear, and in those three days she had asked me exactly forty questions and accepted exactly zero answers.

"You could stay," I said. "Inquisition seals the passes, they'll move on eventually. They always do. Gods' business blows over like weather."

"You looked inside that vial."

"I looked."

"And you want to *stay*?"

What I wanted was to unlook. What I wanted was to hand the vial to the first gray-robed inquisitor who came down the pass and say, *take it, take the memory, take whatever piece of me you need to make this go away.* But I'd seen what was in it — King Aldric, the Lion of the Marches, kneeling in his own throne room while something made of smoke peeled him open like fruit — and knowing is a debt. It collects whether you pay or not.

"No," I said. "But I want you to understand the arithmetic. Inside the Hollow, magic can't touch us. Outside—" I gestured vaguely at the world, which lately included gods answering prayers and knights falling from skies. "Outside, everything we've heard stories about is real, and hungry."

"You're scared," she said. Not cruelly. Just noting it, the way you'd note rain coming.

"Terrified. Good. Fear keeps thieves alive. Write that down."

"I don't write things down. Someone might steal them." A ghost of a smile. She was learning my trade's humor, which was either charming or a warning sign.

I checked the chimney. The bricks came away easier than I liked — easier meant someone else had been through recently, and recently meant the Inquisition's maps were probably better than mine. The dark below breathed out cold air that smelled of stone and old water and, faintly, of something sweeter. Incense. My stomach dropped.

"They've been here," I said.

Wren appeared at my shoulder. "How can you tell?"

"Incense. God-touched burn it before they walk anywhere dangerous. They think it protects them." I almost laughed. "It doesn't protect them from me."

"That's not comforting, Silas."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be arrogant. Arrogance is comforting if you do it right."

We went down.

* * *

The tunnel under the Hollow was older than the smuggling trade, older than the valley's name, maybe older than the kingdom whose king had been hollowed out and replaced. The walls were worked stone in places and raw rock in others, and in the raw places there were marks — spirals, eyes, hands with too many fingers — carved by people who had believed in the old gods back when belief was just a thing people did, like farming or dying.

For eleven years those marks had been decoration. Stone was stone. The Hollow ate magic; that was its whole virtue, the reason thieves and debtors and heretics crawled into it and slept soundly. Whatever the carvers had poured into those spirals, the valley had drunk it dry.

Tonight the marks were faintly warm.

I put my palm against one without thinking, the way you touch a wall in the dark, and snatched it back. Warm as skin. And underneath the warmth, so faint I could have imagined it — I told myself I had imagined it — a hum. Like a hymn heard through three walls.

"Don't touch anything," I said.

"You touched it."

"I'm an idiot professionally. You're an amateur. Stay behind me."

She stayed behind me, which lasted about twenty minutes, until the tunnel forked and she said, "Left," with such certainty that I stopped walking.

"Why left?"

"Because the marks point left."

I looked. She was right. Every spiral on the fork's face curled toward the left-hand passage, subtle as a wink. Eleven years ago these walls had been mute. Tonight they were giving directions.

"The valley's protection is failing," I said. It wasn't a question. Since the shrine flickered — since old Marta's candle to the Nameless lit itself blue for the first time in living memory — the Hollow had been leaking. Magic seeping back in through cracks we couldn't see. Which meant the one rule of my whole life, the one law I'd built everything on — *in the Hollow, nothing supernatural can find you, follow you, or take from you* — was expiring.

"How long has it been failing?" Wren asked.

"Since the gods started answering again. Maybe before. Maybe it was always going to end and we just got comfortable."

"Speak for yourself. I was never comfortable. I grew up in a foundling house because someone stole—" She stopped. She did that. She'd stop herself at the edge of that sentence every time, jaw tight, and step back from it like a well. "Someone stole my childhood," she finished, quieter. "You know who."

"I know who," I said.

"And someday soon you're going to tell me why."

"Someday soon I'm going to tell you a lot of things. None of them will help."

We went left.

* * *

An hour in, the whispers started.

At first I took them for water. Tunnels talk — drips finding their pitch, air moving through cracks, the whole place muttering to itself the way old houses do. I'd spent enough nights in cellars and crypts to know the vocabulary. This was different. This had words in it.

*"—the red door on Copper Lane, kick twice, wait for the whistle—"*

My own voice. My own memory, spoken back to me in my own voice, from somewhere ahead in the dark.

I stopped. Wren bumped into me. "What?"

"Did you hear that?"

"Hear what? It's a tunnel. It drips."

*"—count to nine, no more, the ninth knock means run—"*

There it was again. Clearer. And I knew it — gods, I knew it — it was the night I'd learned locks, age twelve, crouched outside a chandler's shop in Casterhale while a man named Corvo Vane counted my heartbeats aloud and taught me which ones meant danger. Corvo Vane. The man who taught me theft. The man who taught me that memories were coin and people were purses.

A memory I had stolen. From myself, in a way — sold years ago to a merchant who wanted to forget a bad apprenticeship, and I'd padded the vial with pieces of my own past because my past was the cheapest stock I had. I remembered selling it. I did not remember having it. Both things were true at once, and the both-ness made my teeth ache.

"Silas." Wren's hand on my arm. "Your eyes are doing something."

"What are they doing?"

"Glowing. Faintly. Green."

"Wonderful." I blinked hard until the glow died. "It's the tunnel. The valley's leaking, and the leak is stirring up things that were stored here. Memories keep badly when magic gets into them. They go... vocal."

"You stored memories in a *tunnel*?"

"I stored memories everywhere. Caches. Insurance. A thief who keeps all his coin in one purse deserves to be robbed." I'd kept caches in seven places through the valley — wax-sealed vials buried in walls, sunk in cisterns, tucked into the nests of birds who never questioned the weight. Most were stock: other people's pasts, inventory waiting on buyers. Some were mine. The ones I couldn't sell and couldn't bear to keep in my head.

The whispers thickened as we walked. Not just mine anymore. A woman laughing at a wedding. A child counting stars. A soldier's last prayer, repeated over and over with the ending worn off. The tunnel was full of them, decades of my inventory humming in the dark like bees in a wall, and every one of them turned toward me as I passed. I felt it. Attention. The specific attention of things that remember being yours.

"Walk faster," I said.

"You're shaking."

"Thieves shake. It's how we stay warm."

We came up out of the tunnel two hours before dawn, through a collapsed kiln on the north slope, and the sky hit me like a wave.

* * *

Here's the thing about the Hollow that no one who lives there says out loud: you stop noticing the silence. Magic can't enter the valley, and magic — it turns out — is most of the noise of the world. Prayers rising. Gods breathing. The vast slow churn of everything unseen grinding against everything seen. In the Hollow, all of that is muffled to nothing, and after eleven years I had mistaken the muffling for quiet, and the quiet for peace.

One step past the kiln's broken lip and the noise came down on me.

It wasn't sound. It was pressure — the sensation of standing in a crowd of enormous things that were all talking at once and none of them cared whether I listened. The night sky over the mountains was *occupied*. Stars sat in it like eyes in a face. Somewhere far off, something answered a prayer — I felt the answer the way you feel a door slam in another room — and the answering thing was vast and indifferent and awake.

And underneath all of it, closer than any god:

*"—the red door on Copper Lane—"*

My stolen memories hadn't stayed in the tunnel. They'd followed me. Or rather — I understood with a lurch — they had never really been in the tunnel. They'd been in *me*, all along, the way a splinter is in you, and the Hollow had been the tweezers holding them quiet. Out here, with magic loose in the world, every memory I had ever taken from anyone — including the ones I'd taken from myself — was whispering up through the roots of my mind.

"Silas?" Wren climbed out beside me, and I watched her feel it too — her breath caught, her hand flew to the vial at her calf. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's *loud* out here."

"Yeah."

"Is it like this for everyone?"

"Everyone's hearing the world. Only I'm hearing my inventory." I sat down hard on a fallen lintel and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. The whispers arranged themselves, uninvited, into something almost like a chorus. *"—kick twice, wait for the whistle—"* *"—nine knocks means run—"* *"—mother, mother, the bread's gone black again—"* That last one stopped my breath. That one was mine. Age six. The kitchen in the house that burned. I had sold that memory four times to four different buyers and kept the original cached in a bird's nest above the chapel eaves, and now it lived in my skull again, whispering, warm as the marks on the tunnel walls.

"Talk to me," Wren said. She crouched in front of me. "What's happening?"

"I'm being haunted by my own stock," I said. "Every memory I ever stole. They're all still... attached. To me. The Hollow cut the thread; out here the thread's reattached itself." I lowered my hands. "Do you understand what that means? Everything I've ever taken, I'm carrying. All of it. Forever. Every grief I lifted from some widow, every sin I pulled out of some penitent, every childhood I skimmed off some orphan—"

"Like mine."

"Like yours." I said it before I could stop myself, and then, because the night was huge and honest and there was no valley left to hide in, I kept going. "Yes, Wren. Yours too. It's in here. All of it. I hear it whispering with the rest."

She went very still. Dawn was still an hour off; the stars gave her face a pale, carved quality. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in a way that frightened me more than shouting would have.

"What does my childhood say? When it whispers."

I could have lied. Lying was my native language. But the whispers were right there, and one of them was hers — a small girl's voice, singing a skipping rhyme on a street I recognized, a rhyme about a magpie and a silver ring — and it would have been obscene to lie with the evidence singing.

"There's a song," I said. "About a magpie. You're singing it. You sound happy. Whoever you were before I found you, you were happy, and then I—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "Then I made you into coin."

The silence stretched. Somewhere on the mountain, a god answered somebody's prayer, and the pressure of it rolled over us like weather.

"Keep walking," Wren said at last. She stood, shouldered her pack, and didn't look at me. "You can fall apart at the next milestone. We're making distance before light."

I got up and followed her. It occurred to me, somewhere around the third switchback, that she had just done the cruelest and kindest thing available: she had refused to give me absolution, and refused to give me an excuse to stop moving. Both at once. Efficient. She'd have made a fine thief.

* * *

We made the tree line by full light and the high road by noon, and that was where we saw the smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke rises. This hung — a gray pillar planted on the road two miles ahead, at the pass called Shepherd's Rest, where the tunnel surfaced and where any reasonable authority would post a watch. Gray-robed figures moved beneath it. Even at that distance I could see the wrongness of their motion, too smooth, too synchronized, like puppets sharing one hand.

"God-touched," Wren whispered.

"Inquisitors. Sealing the pass behind us." Which meant they knew someone had come through. Which meant the dead man's chimney had a second user after all, and he'd reported it. Marlow, you old bastard. I filed the betrayal away with the rest of my inventory, in the drawer marked *later, with interest.*

"We can't go forward," Wren said.

"We can't go back."

"So we go sideways." She pointed east, where the mountains folded down into a green throat of a valley that wasn't on any map I trusted. "Pilgrim roads. If the gods are answering again, the roads will be full of people going to ask them for things. Two more travelers won't be noticed."

I looked at her. Really looked. Three days ago she'd been a stray who wouldn't stop following me. Now she was navigating, planning, carrying the relic that could unmake a king, and choosing our route while I stood there listening to ghosts sing skipping rhymes.

"When did you get competent?" I asked.

"I always was. You just weren't paying attention." She started down the eastern slope. "Come on, thief. Try to keep up."

I followed. And as the high road fell away behind us and the pilgrim valleys opened ahead, the whispers in my head shifted key — softer now, almost companionable, dozens of stolen lives murmuring at the edge of thought like passengers on a long coach ride. One voice rose above the rest, clear and close, and it was not a memory at all.

It was a woman's voice, warm and tired and amused, and it said:

*"Well. Look who finally came outside."*

I had never heard that voice in my life.

I was absolutely certain I knew it.
