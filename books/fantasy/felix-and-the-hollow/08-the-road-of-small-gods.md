## 8. The Road of Small Gods

Wren had never seen a god before. She'd seen the shrine in the Hollow flicker, one impossible heartbeat of light, and that had been enough to last a lifetime.

Then she left the valley and learned that outside, gods were everywhere.

The first one she saw was squatting in a ditch beside the north road, a thing like a heron crossed with an old man, feathers gone gray and patchy, watching carts go by with the patience of something that had been watching since before the road existed. It didn't speak. It just watched, and every cart that passed threw it something — a heel of bread, a button, a lock of hair wrapped in cloth — and the god swallowed each offering without chewing, and the carts rolled on a little faster than before, the drivers not looking back.

"What's it giving them?" Wren asked.

Silas didn't answer right away. He was walking with his hood up, which he'd been doing since they crossed the valley line, like a man with a sunburn trying to stay out of the light. "Nothing yet," he said. "It's saving up. That's how the small ones work. You feed them, they remember you fed them, and someday when you're drowning in the river they'll remember harder."

"That's not a god," Wren said. "That's a debt collector."

Silas glanced at her, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — not surprise, exactly. Recognition. "Now you're learning the world," he said, and walked on.

* * *

The village of Bracken's Ford sat where the road crossed a brown, slow river, and it had a god in a well.

They learned this from a boy selling apples on the bridge, who told them for free because telling travelers was half his sales pitch. The well god had come back three weeks ago, same as all the others. Before that, Bracken's Ford had been like everywhere else — shrines dark, prayers falling like stones. Then the old gods woke up hungry, and the well god woke up hungriest.

"What does it do?" Silas asked.

"Cures," the boy said, with the flat pride of someone reciting a thing he'd said a hundred times. "Fevers. Rot. The miller's daughter had the wasting and now she's plowing fields. You pay what it asks and you get well."

"And what does it ask?"

The boy's pitch faltered, just slightly, the way a wheel catches on a rut. "Depends," he said. "On what you've got."

They went to look. Wren told herself it was curiosity. She knew, even then, that it wasn't. It was the same pull she'd felt at the flickering shrine in the Hollow — the need to see the impossible up close, to put her hand on it and confirm the world had actually changed.

The well sat in the village square, ringed with fresh-laid stone, and the god lived at the bottom of it. You couldn't see it from above. You could only see the water, which was too still, and too dark, and reflected the sky even at noon when the real sky was gray. A line of people waited at the well's edge, and they waited quietly, in the way of people in a place where loudness might be noticed.

A woman at the front of the line held a child — a small boy, gray-faced, breathing in the shallow rattling way Wren had heard once before, in the year the fever came through the Hollow's lower farms. The woman knelt at the well and spoke into it, low and fast, and the water shivered.

Then the water went still again, and Wren felt the air change, the way it changes before a storm, except the sky stayed clear.

The god answered. Not in words. In a voice that wasn't a voice — a pressure behind the ears, a feeling like someone reading over your shoulder. Wren couldn't have said what it said, but she saw the woman's face when she heard it, and the woman's face went through hope and then through something worse than hope.

"I can't," the woman whispered.

The pressure came again. Longer this time.

The woman looked at the child in her arms. The child looked back at her, not understanding, trusting her the way only a fevered child trusts, completely and without anywhere else to go.

"Yes," the woman said. "All right. Yes."

The well drank. Wren didn't see it take anything. But she saw the woman afterward, standing up with the boy in her arms, and the boy's color was coming back, pink rising in his cheeks like dawn, and the woman was crying and smiling and both at once, and she was also — Wren was sure of it, she'd spent years around people whose memories had been pulled at — she was also missing something. Some piece. The way a room is missing a chair. You can't say what's gone from the empty space, only that the space is empty.

"What did it take?" Wren asked the apple boy.

He shrugged, but his shrug was careful. "Her wedding day, maybe. Her mother's voice. The name of her first dog. It doesn't tell you till it's done." He looked at the well, and for a second his sales face dropped and he was just a kid in a village with a hungry thing in the ground. "My da sold it our harvest festival. All of them, every one we ever had. Says it was worth it, on account of my sister living." He picked up his basket. "It was worth it," he said again, to himself, like a man checking a knot.

They walked on. Wren looked back once. The woman was walking home with her living son, and she was laughing at something he said, and she was also searching her own face for something she'd lost, and not finding it, and laughing anyway.

* * *

That night, in the lean-to behind a road shrine — the shrines were all occupied now, gods in every one, and you paid for shelter with a memory of a meal, which Silas said was cheap and Wren thought was a terrible word for it — she couldn't sleep.

Silas sat up, sharpening a knife he never seemed to use on anything. The vial was wrapped in cloth against his chest. The sealed memory of a king being emptied out. Wren had stopped asking to look at it. Once had been enough.

"The gods came back wrong," she said.

Silas didn't look up from the knife. "They came back exactly right. We just spent four hundred years telling stories about what they were like, and we got it wrong." He tested the edge on his thumb, didn't cut himself, looked faintly disappointed. "The old gods were never kind. They were powerful, and sometimes power is useful to you, and people mistake useful for kind."

"The well god cured that boy."

"The well god made a trade. Curing the boy cost it nothing. The memory cost the woman everything she had that it wanted." He sheathed the knife. "That's not cruelty, Wren. That's worse. That's a merchant. Cruelty at least means it noticed you."

She lay back and stared at the lean-to's roof, at the gaps between the boards where the stars came through. "In the Hollow, magic can't follow. That's what everyone says. That's why people go there — to be somewhere the gods can't reach." She turned her head. "Did you ever wonder why?"

Silas went very still. It was a small stillness, controlled, the stillness of a man who has decided not to move, and Wren had learned to spot it because she'd grown up — whatever that meant, for someone with holes in her head — around him.

"No," he said.

"You're lying."

"I'm a thief. Lying is the honest part of the job." But he said it without any of the usual dryness, and after a while, in the dark, he added: "The valley wasn't built to keep gods out. It was built to keep something in. Or so the oldest stories say. And the oldest stories in the Hollow are all about forgetting." He pulled his blanket up. "Sleep. Tomorrow we hit the shrine road, and the gods get bigger the closer we get to the capital."

"Bigger how?"

"Bigger appetites," Silas said, and this time she heard it — under the wryness, under the thief's flat calm — fear. Real fear, the kind he'd been carrying since the knight fell out of the sky. "Small gods take your wedding day. The ones near the capital take kingdoms."

* * *

The shrine road was lined with them, and each one was worse than the last.

Wren kept count, because counting was a thing she could do when the world tilted, a handrail to hold. A god of crossroads that took one shoe from every traveler and left the road ahead mysteriously free of bandits. A god of the orchards, a vast slow presence like a tree that had learned to want, which cured blight in exchange for the memory of the taste of fruit — so that the farmers it protected could no longer taste what they grew, and ate their dinners like men doing sums. A god of a bridge who asked nothing at all, which frightened Wren more than the rest, until she saw the birds avoiding it, a wide clean arc of empty sky, and understood that some gods didn't need to be paid because they were already feeding.

On the fourth day they came to a town called Merton's Cross, and Merton's Cross had a problem.

The problem stood in the town square. It was a god of the old, old kind — not a heron-thing or a well-dweller but something that had come back from before the forgetting, something with a body like a deer and a face like a door, and it had answered the town's prayers so thoroughly that the town no longer knew how to live without it. It healed. It blessed the fields. It told the mayor which travelers to turn away. And it had started asking for more than memories.

"It asked for the Hendersons' baby," a woman told them in the tavern, in the low voice people used now, the voice of the occupied. "Just for a day, it said. To look at him. It gave him back. He's fine. He's fine." She said it twice, the way you say a thing you need to believe. "But it remembers him now. And it looks at the cradle when it passes the window."

"Why does the king let this happen?" Wren asked, and the tavern went quiet in a way that had weight to it.

A man near the fire laughed, one dry note. "The king," he said. "The king's blessed us, girl. Midwinter blessing, every year, whole capital turns out. King Aldric loves his people." He said the name with the careful roundness of a coin being shown on both sides, and Wren watched Silas's hand move, very slightly, toward the wrapped vial under his coat, and then away.

They left Merton's Cross before dawn.

* * *

It was on the road out of the town, in the gray hour when the mist was still deciding whether to be fog or morning, that Wren saw the god look at her.

Not the deer-thing. A small one — a ditch god, a hedge god, one of the patchy feathered beggars that lined the road like mile-markers. It was doing nothing. It was doing what they all did, sitting and watching and waiting to be fed. But as she passed, its head turned, and its eyes — eyes like two drops of well water — followed her, and it made a sound.

It wasn't speech. It was the pressure-voice, the behind-the-ears reading-over-your-shoulder feeling, and what it pressed into her was not words but a shape, an absence, the outline of a thing with the thing removed.

It pressed the shape of her childhood into her.

She stopped walking. The mist moved. Silas was three steps ahead, then four, then he turned because she'd stopped, and he saw her face, and he came back.

"Wren."

"It looked at me," she said. Her voice came out strange. "It looked at me and it — it knew. It knows there's a hole in me. It could *see the hole*, Silas. Like a tooth gone bad. Like it was thinking about whether I'd be worth filling."

The god watched them both now. And she saw Silas look at it, and she saw the god look at Silas, and something passed between them that she had no word for — the recognition of one hollow thing by another.

"Keep walking," Silas said quietly.

"It could give them back." She wasn't walking. Her feet had made their own decision. "My memories. The ones you sold. It's a god, it could just — it knows where the hole is, it could fill it, I could know my mother's face, I could know why I was on that road, I could know *anything*, and all it wants is—"

"Wren." He said her name the way you say a word to a horse standing too close to a cliff edge. "What do you think it wants in exchange?"

And that was the vertigo, right there, the whole world tilting on its side. Because she knew. She knew what the well god took, what the orchard god took, what all of them took, and she understood, standing in the mist on the shrine road with a god's water-drop eyes on her, that the price for having her childhood back would be paid out of the only self she had left — the girl who'd walked out of the Hollow with a thief she hated and trusted, the girl who was, right now, the sum of everything that had happened *after* the theft. Fill the hole and the new girl goes into it. She'd get her past back and lose the person she'd become getting it.

The god waited. It was very patient. They were all so patient.

"Come on," Silas said, and took her arm, and this time she let him, and they walked, and the god's gaze stayed on her back for a quarter mile, she counted her steps to keep from turning around, two hundred and eleven steps before the feeling of being read finally lifted.

They didn't speak for a long time. When they did, it was Silas, and he was looking at the road ahead, at the far blue line of hills that was supposedly the capital's direction.

"The gods came back hungry," he said. "That's the rule now. Everything out here is a price." A pause. "I've spent my whole life being the price-taker. I know how this ends, Wren. Before this road's done, one of those things is going to ask one of us for something we can't pay, and we're going to pay it anyway."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know weather." He walked a few more steps. "There's a river ahead. Big one — the Arren. And there's a thing in the Arren that knows where the first relic is, and it doesn't trade in memories of meals or wedding days."

Wren felt the cold come into her before he said it, some animal part of her already counting what she had left, what she could not lose, what she would not —

"It trades in what you love," Silas said. "And I'm about to go make us a bargain."
