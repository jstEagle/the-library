## 9. The Thief's Guild Remembers Him

Casterhale smelled the way it always had: fish rot and lamp oil and the sweet-sour breath of a river that had drowned more people than any war. Silas stood at the top of the Stair of Nine Hundred Steps with Wren beside him and felt the city reach up and take hold of him by the throat.

He'd told himself he'd never come back.

"You said this place would be safe," Wren said. She had the vial strapped against her ribs under her coat, wrapped in three layers of cloth, and she kept touching it the way other people touched a wound — to check it was still there, still hurting.

"I said it would be *safer*." Silas watched the harbor below. Gulls wheeled over the masts like a thought you couldn't shake. "The Inquisition sealed the valley's passes two days ago. They're crawling all over the border roads. But cities are different. Cities are too big to search properly. You can disappear in Casterhale if you know how."

"Can you?"

"I grew up disappearing here." He started down the steps. "That's not the same as being safe. Nothing is safe. Safe is a story we tell children so they'll sleep."

Wren followed. She was fourteen now, or fifteen — she didn't know which, because he'd sold the years that would have told her, and every time she asked about her own birthday something in his chest turned over like a boat taking water. "Then why are we here?"

"Because Marlow knows things. Marlow knows where the tongue-bone is, or knows who knows, or knows who's already sold that information to someone worse." Silas took the steps two at a time, keeping his hood up, keeping his face angled away from the lamplight. "And because we're out of options, and out of options is where I do my best work."

"That's a lie," Wren said. "You hate this. I can tell. Your shoulders go up around your ears when you're scared, like a turtle."

"Turtles are wise."

"Turtles hide. We're walking *toward* the thing."

Silas didn't answer. She was right, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them on the stairs like a third traveler.

* * *

The Guild Quarter hadn't changed. That was the first horror of it. He'd left seven years ago certain the place would rot without him, and instead it had simply gone on — the same crooked lane, the same shuttered windows with their chalk marks, the same beggar at the corner who was not a beggar but a look-out, and who looked at Silas for exactly one heartbeat too long before looking away.

Silas felt that heartbeat all the way down his spine.

"He saw me," he murmured.

"Who?"

"The man pretending to sleep against the wall. He'll send word ahead. By the time we reach Marlow's door, Marlow will know I'm coming, what I look like now, and how many people I'm traveling with." He adjusted his stride, casual, unhurried. A man with nothing to hide walks slowly. "Which means whatever welcome we get will be arranged. Staged. Marlow doesn't have welcomes. He has performances."

"Is that bad?"

"It means everything he says will be true enough to believe and false enough to sell." Silas turned down Ropewalk Lane, where the smell of tar thickened and the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. "Marlow taught me that rule. Everything has a price. Friendship. Loyalty. Information. Especially information. Information is the only currency that gains value when you spend it on the wrong person."

Wren caught his sleeve. "You're talking like him."

"What?"

"Like someone who trusts nobody. Like it's *smart*." Her grip tightened. "I've been watching you since the valley. You talk like that when you're about to do something you're ashamed of."

He looked down at her hand on his arm. Small fingers, chapped from the road, holding on to a man who had stolen her childhood and spent it. And holding on anyway. That was the thing about Wren he could never account for in his ledgers. Every calculation said she should hate him. Every calculation was wrong.

"Maybe I am," he said quietly. "About to do something shameful. It's been known to happen."

"Just don't do it to me."

The words landed harder than any blade he'd ever taken. He put his hand over hers, briefly, and then walked on, because if he stood there another moment he'd have to say something true, and true things were dangerous in a city full of ears.

* * *

Marlow's shop sat behind a false front — a chandler's, nominally, selling tallow candles that no one bought, because everyone in the Quarter knew the candles were just shelves for the real inventory. Memories, mostly. Vials in wax-sealed racks, glowing faintly through the tallow like flies in amber. Silas remembered the day he'd first walked in here, seventeen years old and starving, carrying his first theft in a cracked vial and his whole future in his fists.

The bell above the door rang the same sour note.

"Well." The voice came from behind the counter, from a man who had grown wider and grayer and no kinder. Marlow looked up from a ledger with the expression of a man finding a dead rat in his soup. "If it isn't the Pemberton ghost. Seven years, and you pick *this* season to haunt me."

"Hello, Marlow."

"Don't 'hello' me." Marlow set down his pen with great deliberation, as though each motion were a legal act. "Do you know what's happened since you left? Do you know what's been *asked* of me? There are god-touched men in this city wearing gray coats, and they ask questions, and when I answer correctly they let me keep my fingers. All ten. It's been a very educational decade."

"I need information."

"Of course you do. You always did. You'd walk into a funeral and ask for directions to the will reading." Marlow's eyes flicked past Silas to Wren, lingered, calculated. Silas watched him do it — watched the old fence weigh the girl, the vial-shaped bulge under her coat, the road dust, the fear — and arrive at a number. Marlow always arrived at a number. "And who's this? Daughter? Apprentice? Hostage?"

"Wren. She's with me."

"With you." Marlow smiled with his mouth only. "Everything's *with* you, Pemberton. Nothing's ever *of* you. That was always your gift and your disease."

Silas stepped closer to the counter. Under the smell of tallow he caught something else now — incense. Gray-coat incense, the kind the Inquisition burned in their waystations, myrrh cut with something metallic. His stomach dropped slow and cold.

"You've had visitors," he said.

"I've had *customers*." Marlow spread his hands. "In this city, those words are married. What do you want, Silas? Say it plainly, pay for it cleanly, and leave before either of us remembers why we stopped liking each other."

"The tongue-bone of the god whose name the valley forgot. Where is it?"

The silence that followed had texture. Silas heard the river through the walls, heard a cart pass on the lane outside, heard Wren breathing carefully behind him. Marlow's face did something complicated — surprise, then greed, then fear, then a smooth professional blankness laid over all three like fresh paint.

"And why," Marlow said softly, "would a memory-thief from a valley where magic can't follow want a relic of the old gods?"

"That's not the question you're selling."

"No. It isn't." Marlow leaned back in his chair until it creaked. "Here's the question I'm selling: who else wants it? Because I've had that question asked twice this month, Pemberton. Once by a priest with ash on his cuffs who paid in silver. Once by a woman in a gray coat who paid in something better than silver." He tapped the ledger. "She paid in *forgetting*. One page of my debts, torn out. Gone. Do you understand what that's worth to a man in my trade? A clean slate. There are people in this city who'd kill their mothers for a clean slate."

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "Vess."

"You know her name. Interesting. That's worth something too, though I won't charge you for it. Call it a loyalty discount." Marlow's smile widened a fraction. "Oh, wait. You don't deal in loyalty. My mistake."

* * *

They negotiated in the back room, over tea Marlow poured himself and did not offer to anyone else. Wren sat by the door with her hand inside her coat, near the vial, near the knife she'd taken to carrying. Silas sat across from Marlow and watched the man's hands, because faces lied but hands were honest, and Marlow's hands kept drifting toward the ledger on the shelf behind him.

Not toward a weapon. Toward the ledger.

Toward the page with names on it. Names and dates and routes. Silas had seen that ledger twenty years ago, when he was a boy learning the trade, and Marlow had shown it to him as a lesson: *every client, every sale, every route, every debt. A fence who forgets is a fence who hangs.* Somewhere in that book was a line item for two travelers off the mountain road, one tall, one small, asking after relics.

Somewhere in that book was the price of their lives, already written, waiting for a buyer.

"Two hundred crowns," Marlow said. "For the location of the tongue-bone."

"I don't have two hundred crowns."

"Then you have a problem, and I have a ledger." Marlow sipped his tea. "Or — alternative payment. I hear you've gotten *good* since you left. Better than good. There's talk in the Quarter about a thief in the Hollow who can lift a memory so clean the victim thanks him for it. Talk like that is worth money to me. So: one job. You steal something for my inventory, I give you the bone's location free, and we part as colleagues."

"What's the job?"

"Ahh." Marlow set down his cup. "There it is. The hesitation. You've changed, Pemberton. The boy I trained would have said yes before I finished the sentence."

"The boy you trained ended up hollowing out half the people he loved. Forgive me for developing standards."

"Standards." Marlow laughed, short and ugly. "Standards are what thieves call fear when they want respect for it. Fine. Then pay coin. Or pay memory. I know you carry stock. Everyone in this room knows it. That girl's been guarding that vial like it's her own heart, and maybe it is, and maybe it's yours, and either way—" he shrugged, vast and terrible in his chair, "—inventory is inventory."

Silas stood. Slowly. "We're done here."

"We're done when I say we're—"

"We're done." He put both palms flat on the table and leaned in, and let Marlow see what seven years in the Hollow had built underneath the wry, detached thief — something cold and patient and very tired of being handled. "You're going to give me the location, Marlow. Not because I'm threatening you. Threats are currency and I'm not spending them here. You're going to give it to me because twenty years ago you told a starving boy that information is the only currency that gains value when you spend it on the wrong person. And you're trying to decide right now whether I'm the wrong person. So let me help you decide."

He reached into his coat and set one vial on the table between them. Not the king's memory. Something smaller — a stolen sunset, a wedding, some stranger's best afternoon, lifted years ago and never sold because it wasn't worth much and it was too lovely to spend.

"One memory. Freely given. Not sold. Given." He pushed it across the wood. "Because once, when I was seventeen and stupid, you fed me for a week and never put it in the ledger. I checked. There's no line item. You did one decent thing and hid it from your own book. So here's mine. Now we're even, and neither of us has to pretend we're friends."

Marlow stared at the vial for a long time. When he picked it up, his hands were not quite steady, and Silas understood suddenly, horribly, that the old fence was frightened — not of him, but of the gray coats, of the clean slate they'd offered, of the arithmetic that said two hundred crowns and a torn-out page were worth more than one thief's gratitude.

"Bell tower," Marlow said finally. "Saint Verity's, in the drowned quarter. The bell's been silent thirty years, but the tower's not empty. There's a priest up there who rings it sometimes at night. Rings it and rings it and screams between the peals. The bone's in the bell. Don't ask me how I know. Ask yourself why a mad priest guards a dead bell." He closed his fist around the vial. "Now go. And Pemberton — if a woman in a gray coat asks you what I told you, remember that I have a ledger, and ledgers can be read aloud."

* * *

They made it four streets before Silas admitted it.

"She's going to sell us."

Wren didn't break stride. "I know."

"You knew?"

"I watched his hands the whole time. He kept touching that book like it was a wound." She glanced up at him, and there was no accusation in her face, which was somehow worse than accusation. "Everyone has a price. You said that. You said it like it was wisdom."

"It *is* wisdom."

"It's an excuse." She pulled her collar up against the river wind. "You use it so you don't have to be angry when people betray you. If everyone has a price, then betrayal is just... weather. It happens. Nobody's fault. Nobody's choice." She shook her head. "Marlow chose. He's probably choosing right now, sitting there deciding how fast to send word. And you're going to let him, because stopping him would mean becoming someone who hurts old men, and you'd rather bleed than be that. Even though bleeding gets us killed."

Silas stopped walking. The lane was narrow here, the river loud beyond the warehouses, the lamps few and far between. He looked at this girl — the orphan whose memories he'd stolen, who had every right to leave him on any road in the world and hadn't — and felt the crack in his armor widen into something structural.

"What do you want me to do?" he asked. "Genuinely. Tell me."

"Go back and buy the rest of it," she said. "Not the location. The *rest*. Who Vess talked to, what she paid, what Marlow promised. Buy his silence outright, tonight, before morning. Spend whatever it costs. Because you're right about one thing — everything has a price. So pay it. Pay it before someone else does."

He looked back the way they'd come, toward the candle shop with its false front and its true inventory, and felt the trap close gently around him, the way good traps do — not with teeth but with logic.

Every ally has a price. Including Marlow. Including, someday, maybe, Wren.

Including him. He'd been bought before. He knew exactly what he cost.

"Wait here," he said, and turned back toward the shop.

He was halfway down Ropewalk Lane when he saw the light — candlelight moving behind the chandler's shutters, wrong, too quick, more than one flame. And beneath the river-smell, faint but unmistakable, drifting from the direction of Marlow's door:

Myrrh, cut with metal.

Gray-coat incense.

Silas melted into the shadow of a doorway, heart hammering, and watched two figures in gray coats let themselves into the candle shop without knocking — let themselves in like men coming home — while somewhere behind the counter, Marlow's voice rose, high and performing, saying words Silas couldn't hear but could already price to the copper.

The ledger was open. The page was being read aloud.

And the first name on it was his.
