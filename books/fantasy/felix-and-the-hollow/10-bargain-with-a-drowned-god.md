## 10. Bargain With a Drowned God

The river god lived under the bridge at Thorn's Crossing, and like everything in Casterhale that called itself holy, it took payment up front.

Silas learned this from a beggar with no eyes who sat at the bridge's foot, palms out, murmuring to travelers: *She hears you coming. She always hears you coming. Bring something you'll miss.* The beggar had been blind before the gods came back, Silas suspected. Now he was blind in a way that looked chosen, his empty sockets turned upward toward the water as if listening for it.

"You want to cross," the beggar said when Silas paused. Not a question. "Everyone wants to cross. Nobody wants to pay."

"We're not crossing," Silas said. Wren stood a pace behind him, hood up, one hand resting on the vial through her coat where it hung against his chest — *his* chest, he'd taken to carrying it himself after the second night of dreams about smoke and borrowed faces. "We need information. A location."

"Same thing." The beggar smiled with too many teeth, or maybe just the right number seen from the wrong angle. "Downstream is a question. Downstream is an answer. She holds both ends of the river, and she doesn't let go of either."

Wren shifted beside him. She'd been quiet since Casterhale, quieter than usual, and Silas had learned to read her silences the way he'd once read locks — by feel, by pressure, by what gave. This silence said: *I don't like this.* He didn't blame her. He didn't much like it either.

"What does she take?" he asked.

"Memories." The beggar said it the way a man says *rain* or *taxes*. "But not the way you do it, thief. I can smell your trade on you. You lift them whole and sell them back gentle. She takes them out by the root. What she drinks stays drunk."

Silas had stolen memories for eleven years. He knew their weight better than anyone alive — which ones were heavy as stones, which were light as breath, which ones a mind grew around like a tree growing around a nail. He'd never once thought about what it meant that he could feel that weight. That the things he pulled from other people's heads had texture, temperature, *taste*.

He thought about it now.

"How do I find her?" he asked.

"You don't." The beggar tilted his head back further, throat working. "You stand on the bridge at moonrise and you say her name into the water. Then you wait to be invited."

"And her name?"

The beggar's smile widened until Silas understood that the answer would cost him nothing because it wasn't worth anything. "You already know it," he said. "Everybody does. It's the name you give the river when you're small and standing on the bank, throwing stones. The first name you ever gave moving water."

---

They found a room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and old smoke, and Silas spent the afternoon doing something he hadn't done since he was nine years old: trying to remember being small.

It should have been easy. Memory was his trade. He could reach into a stranger's skull and pull out their wedding day intact, still warm, still glistening. But his own past was a locked room he'd thrown away the key to on purpose, and now, standing outside it, he found the door wouldn't open even a crack.

He remembered facts. Facts were cheap. His mother's name had been Lira. They'd lived in a house with a blue door — or maybe the door was green; the fact kept shifting when he pressed on it. There had been a river near the house. He was almost sure there had been a river.

He lay on the narrow bed with his hands behind his head and went down into himself the way he went down into other people, gently, professionally, hands raised to show he meant no harm.

And there, at the bottom of him, silted over: water. Moving water. A boy on a bank, five years old maybe, six, throwing stones at the current and shouting a name at it, some made-up name, the kind of name children give to things they think are alive—

He couldn't get the name. It was right there. He could feel its shape the way you can feel the shape of a word on the tip of your tongue, round and wet and certain, and every time he reached for it, it slid.

"Damn it," he said to the ceiling.

From the floor, where she'd claimed the pallet, Wren said, "What?"

"Nothing. Go to sleep."

She didn't. He heard her turn over, heard the rustle of her thinking. "You're going to see her tonight, aren't you. The god."

"Yes."

"Alone?"

"That's the plan."

"I don't like the plan."

"You don't have to like the plan. You have to be somewhere else while I execute the plan."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was approaching something sideways. "When you trade with her. When you give her a memory." A pause. "How do you choose which one?"

Silas stared at the ceiling beams. Somewhere below them the chandler was still working, the scrape of his knife rhythmic as breathing.

"You don't," he said finally. "That's the trick of it. You walk in carrying everything, and she picks. Like a magpie. Whatever shines brightest, that's what she takes."

"And what shines brightest in you, Silas?"

He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing. "Nothing. I'm all dull metal, me. Go to sleep, Wren."

But he lay awake long after her breathing slowed, and he thought about what shone, and he told himself the thing he'd told clients for eleven years: *memories are only weight. Give away enough weight and eventually you float.*

He'd never believed it. He'd just gotten very good at saying it.

---

Moonrise came up copper behind the eastern hills, and the river ran dark beneath the bridge, and Silas stood alone on the old stones with his boots planted wide the way he stood when he expected the ground to move.

He leaned over the rail. Below him the water slid past, black and patient, carrying the reflections of stars it had drowned years ago.

He opened his mouth to call a name he couldn't remember, and what came out instead was the sound a child makes — half word, half splash, a nonsense syllable that his mouth knew and his mind didn't. "*Ammun.*"

The river stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped*, mid-current, the way a held breath stops, and every reflection in it turned to look at him at once — the drowned stars, the drowned moon, the drowned faces of everyone who had ever leaned over this rail and spoken without knowing they were praying.

Then the water rose.

It came up the bridge pilings in a slow spiral, climbing against gravity with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been refused, and it gathered itself at eye level into a shape. A woman, roughly. Tall, roughly. Her body was water holding the memory of a woman the way a glove holds the memory of a hand, and her face was the worst part — because it was beautiful, and because it was *almost* someone, and because the almost-someone it was kept changing. A mother. A sister. A girl he'd never met and suddenly missed.

"Thief," the god said. Her voice came from everywhere the water was, which was everywhere. "You smell of my sisters' griefs. You've been drinking from shrines."

"I've been *stealing* from shrines," Silas said. "There's a difference. It's a professional distinction."

The god laughed. The river laughed with her, a thousand small ripples. "Yes. I know your work. You're famous down here, in the dark places where the drowned gossip. The memory-thief of the Hollow. The man who sells people back their own hearts at interest." Her face settled — settled on his mother's face, young, the blue door behind her, and Silas's stomach dropped through the bridge stones into the river. "What do you want, thief?"

"A location." Keep it business. Keep it dry. "A relic. A tongue-bone of one of the old gods. It hangs in a bell tower somewhere east of here. I need to know which tower, whose god, what guards it."

"And what will you pay?"

"Name it."

"Oh, thief." She drifted closer, and where she passed, the air went damp and heavy as a held funeral. "You misunderstand the transaction. I don't name prices. I *choose*. You brought yourself to my bridge, full of everything you've hoarded, and I will reach into you the way you reach into others — except I won't be gentle, and I won't put anything back, and whatever I take, I keep."

Silas had faced knives, inquisitors, the memory of a king being hollowed out by smoke. None of it had made his hands sweat like this.

"And if I refuse?" he asked.

"Then you climb down these stairs and you spend the rest of your short life running from the Inquisition with a vial you can't open and a girl who hates you slowly learning why she should." The god's borrowed face smiled his mother's smile. "Or you pay, and you learn where the bone hangs, and you go one step closer to unbinding a king and saving a world that has never once saved you back. Your choice, thief. It's always your choice. That's the cruelest thing about mortals — the choosing."

He thought about walking away. He thought about it honestly, weighing it the way he weighed everything, and he found that the scales had already tipped somewhere in the last hundred miles without his noticing. There was a girl asleep above a chandler's shop who deserved answers. There was a dead knight's voice in his head whispering *the crown is empty*. There was no version of him, anymore, that climbed back down those stairs.

"Do it, then," he said.

The god reached out.

Her hand went into his chest — not through his coat, not through his skin, but *into* him, into the place where he kept himself, and she began to sort. He felt it happen. Felt her fingers — cold, thorough, impersonal as floodwater — leafing through him. The widow's grief he'd lifted in the Hollow, still stored, unsold. The taste of the first coin he ever earned. The face of Marlow, younger, before the ledgers got him. The smell of rain on the smuggler's tunnel. Wren's face at eight years old, the day he took her childhood—

"No," Silas said, and grabbed her wrist, and the river laughed at him. "Not that one. Not her."

"Then you shouldn't have brought it," the god said, almost kindly. "Shine less brightly, thief."

She let it go. He would think about that later — that she let it go, that she left him that guilt intact, perhaps because guilt was heavier than any single memory and gods liked their meals to stay heavy. Instead her fingers closed around something else, something deep, something silted, something he hadn't known he was carrying until the moment it began to come loose—

A blue door. Green? Blue. A woman in the doorway with flour on her hands. A boy running toward her across a yard. The sun was— the sun was—

The god drank.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscenity of it. It felt like setting down a bag he'd forgotten he was carrying, like exhaling after a decade underwater, and for one golden instant Silas Pemberton felt *lighter than he had since childhood* —

and then he tried to remember his mother's face, and there was nothing there.

Not darkness. Darkness would have been something. There was simply a shape in him where a face had been, smooth-sided, water-smooth, the exact dimensions of a absence. He knew the facts still: Lira. Flour. A door, blue or green. But the face itself — the particular architecture of her, the nose, the eyes, the way her mouth went when she saw him running — gone, drunk, kept, forever, by a thing under a bridge.

He was on his knees. He didn't remember getting there. The river had resumed flowing around the pilings below, ordinary, indifferent, and the god was already sinking back into it, already becoming current.

"The tower," Silas rasped. "You promised—"

The water paused. Turned. And her voice came up thin through the stones, already distant:

"Bell Tower of Saint Verene, in the town of Ashfall, three days east. Ask for the priest who prays with his eyes open. And thief—" the river chuckled, low and vast, "—the bone answers to the tongue that fed it. Choose your words carefully when you get there. Words are how gods are commanded. And how they're *made*."

Then she was gone, and the river was just a river, and Silas knelt on the bridge in the copper moonlight with a hole in his head shaped like his mother.

---

Wren found him there. Of course she did. He'd told her to stay away and she'd stayed exactly long enough to count to five hundred, and then come anyway, because she was Wren, and obedience had never once survived contact with her curiosity.

She sat down next to him on the cold stones without asking what happened. For a while she said nothing at all, and the river talked to itself underneath them, and the moon climbed.

"She took something," Wren said finally.

"Yes."

"Something big."

"It doesn't matter."

"Liar."

He almost smiled. Almost. "It doesn't matter *tonight*."

Wren hugged her knees. She was fifteen now, nearly sixteen, and in the moonlight he could see the girl she'd been layered under the one she was becoming, and it occurred to him with a fresh, sick clarity that he knew her childhood better than she did — that he was, in a sense, the only living archive of who she used to be, and that he'd sold pieces of it, and that the god under the bridge had shown him precisely what that made him.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"You'll ask it either way."

"When you stole from people. Back in the Hollow. Did it ever feel like this?" She gestured vaguely at him, at all of him, at the shape of what was missing. "Like something's gone and you can feel the edges of it?"

Silas looked at the water. The water looked back, black and patient, holding its drowned stars.

"Every time," he said.

Wren nodded slowly, as if filing that away. Then she did something unexpected. She scooted closer, shoulder to shoulder, and said, "Tell me about her. Your mother. Whatever you've still got."

"There isn't—"

"Facts, then. Names. Anything. If you say them out loud, they're harder to lose." She shrugged, fierce and awkward. "That's what I do. With the bits of me that are left. I say them out loud every night so they stick. So somebody's got to hear them."

So Silas Pemberton, who had built a life on taking memories and calling it mercy, sat on a bridge in the dark and gave away the only thing he had left of his mother, free of charge: *Lira. Flour on her hands. A door that might have been blue.*

And Wren listened like it was treasure, because to her it was, and when he finished she said, "See? Still yours. She can't drink what we both carry."

He wanted to believe her. He was a professional skeptic; belief had never come easy. But three days later, riding east toward Ashfall with the vial cold against his chest and the ghost of a blue door following him like weather, he caught himself reaching for his mother's face one more time — testing the wound, the way you test a missing tooth —

and found, instead of the absence, something new.

A voice. Young, female, furious, alive, saying *still yours, she can't drink what we both carry* — laid over the gap like a patch, like a graft, like a memory that had never happened but was true anyway.

He rode on toward the bell tower, hollowed a little further, and fuller than he'd ever been, and he did not look back at the river, because rivers remember, and he was not finished paying.
