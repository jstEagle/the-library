## 11. The Relic in the Bell Tower

The bell tower stood at the center of a village that had stopped being a village.

Wren saw it first from the ridge road — a crooked finger of gray stone rising out of the mist, and around its base, the shapes of houses with their doors standing open to the cold. No smoke from the chimneys. No dogs barking at their approach. Just the tower, and the silence, and somewhere high above them, a sound like breathing.

"Charming," Silas said. He had his hood up and his hands in his sleeves, which was how he looked when he was pretending not to be afraid. "I've robbed nicer places."

"You've robbed *every* place," Wren said.

"That's what makes me an expert." He didn't smile when he said it. He hadn't smiled much since Casterhale, since the fire, since she'd seen him stand in the middle of a burning street and look back at her with something raw in his face before he turned and ran toward her instead of away. She was still turning that over. She suspected she'd be turning it over for a long time.

They left the road and picked through the abandoned houses. Inside one, a table was set for supper — three bowls, three spoons, bread gone to green mold. The chairs were pushed in neat, as if everyone had simply stepped out. In another, a child's toys lay arranged in a perfect circle on the floor, each animal facing inward.

Wren's skin crawled. "Where did they go?"

"Answered prayers," Silas said quietly. "That's my guess. The priest here prayed hard enough, long enough, that something finally listened. And whatever it gave him, it took the rest of them as payment." He crouched by the circle of toys, not touching. "Or he took them himself. Mad comes in different flavors. Some of them are generous."

"You say that like you know."

"I know what wanting sounds like," Silas said. "It's most of what I hear, in this trade." He stood, brushed off his knees. "Come on. The tongue-bone's in the tower. Let's get it before whatever's up there finishes its nap."

* * *

The tower door was oak, banded with iron, and carved over the lintel with words worn nearly smooth. Wren traced them with her fingers: *WE PRAISED HIM AND HE HEARD US.*

"That's supposed to be comforting?" she asked.

"It's supposed to be a warning," Silas said. "Somebody chiseled it after the fact. Notice the gouge marks. Notice how deep." He tried the handle. Locked, of course. Of course. He produced two thin pieces of metal from his sleeve and went to work, muttering under his breath in the rhythm Wren had come to know — a little song of clicks and scrapes, his whole body leaning into the lock like a lover.

The lock opened. The door swung inward on darkness and a smell like old incense and old blood.

Inside, stairs spiraled up into blackness. Every few turns, a slit window let in a blade of gray daylight, and every few turns, the walls changed. The lower stones were bare. Higher up, someone had begun carving — prayers, hundreds of them, thousands, cut into the rock in a hand that started careful and got wilder as it climbed. *Hear me. Hear me. Hear me. I have been faithful. I have been faithful. I HAVE BEEN FAITHFUL.* By the top of the spiral the letters were half an inch deep and the stone around them was cracked from the force of the cutting.

"He carved all this?" Wren whispered.

"Years of it." Silas ran his torchlight along the wall without slowing. "Prayer is just begging with better posture. Do it long enough into a silence that never answers, and something in you breaks along the seam." His voice was flat, but Wren heard what was underneath it. She'd learned to hear what was underneath Silas Pemberton. It was the only interesting thing about him, and lately it was the only thing about him she trusted.

At the seventh turn, they found the first body.

He was seated against the wall, mummified, hands folded in his lap, face tilted up toward the window with an expression of perfect contentment. His mouth was open slightly, as if mid-word. As if mid-*thank you*.

"Don't," Silas said, when Wren reached toward the man's folded hands. "Whatever killed him, it liked him. That's worse than the ones it didn't like. Trust me."

"How would you—"

Above them, the bell rang.

One note. Deep enough that Wren felt it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the soles of her boots. The dust on the stairs jumped. The blade of light from the window shivered. And beneath the tolling, woven into it like a thread through cloth, came a voice — vast and slow and pleased, the way a landslide might be pleased:

***YOU CAME BACK.***

Silas blew out the torch.

In the sudden dark, Wren heard him move — close, fast, his hand finding her wrist in the blackness and squeezing once. A question. She squeezed back. *Yes. I'm here. I'm not running.* It was strange, how quickly they'd built that language. Strange and terrifying, because languages like that only get built by people who expect to need them.

The dark lasted three heartbeats. Then light bloomed from above — a soft gold radiance spilling down the stairwell, sourceless, warm as candlelight and wrong as a painted sun. It lit the carvings on the walls, and Wren saw that the prayers were glowing too, faintly, each word pulsing like a heartbeat.

And descending the stairs, unhurried, came the priest.

He had been handsome once. Wren could see the bones of it still, under the ruin. His robes were fine vestments gone to rags, crusted with wax and something darker. His hair was white and fell past his shoulders in ropes. But it was his eyes that made her step back — they were full of light. Not reflecting it. *Full of it*, gold pouring out of him from somewhere behind his face, and when he smiled, the light spilled between his teeth.

"Pilgrims," he said, and his voice was gentle, warm, wrecked. "Pilgrims! Oh, bless you, bless you, it has been *so long* since anyone climbed. They all stopped coming, you know. After the miracle. They said I'd gone too far. They said He doesn't answer like that. But He *does*. He answered *me*. Would you like to see?"

"We're just passing through," Silas said smoothly. He'd stepped forward, putting himself between Wren and the priest, and his voice had gone into its merchant register — easy, reasonable, a man with nothing to hide. "We heard the bell. Beautiful tone. We only wanted to pay our respects."

"Respects!" The priest laughed, and the laugh echoed strangely, doubled, as if something behind him were laughing a half-second late. "Respects are free. Everything else costs. That's the new arithmetic, pilgrim. Didn't you feel it? The gods came back hungry. Everyone's learning the prices now." He spread his arms, and the light in him flared. "But for *you* — for pilgrims who climbed all this way — I'll show you the miracle for nothing. Isn't that kind? Isn't that *generous*? Come up. Come and see Him. Come and *hear*."

"See who?" Wren asked, before she could stop herself.

The priest turned those burning eyes on her, and for a moment the gentleness fell away and she saw what was underneath: a man stretched on a rack of his own devotion, years deep, screaming silently forever. Then the gentleness returned, worse than the scream.

"The god in the bell," he said. "My god. My beautiful, attentive god. He lives in the bronze now. I gave Him my voice to live in — did you know that? That's what a tongue-bone is *for*. The old gods kept their speech in a piece of themselves, separate, safe, the way a miser keeps coin. And when the silence came, when the gods went deaf and dumb, I found where mine had hidden His voice. I climbed. I prayed. I *offered*." He touched his own throat, tenderly, the way you'd touch a scar. "And He spoke to me. Every day since. Every hour. He tells me He loves me. He tells me I'm His favorite. He tells me—" the light guttered, just for a moment, "—He tells me the others will come take Him away, and I mustn't let them, and I believe Him, because He has never once lied to me."

Behind the priest, up the stairs, the darkness at the top of the tower pulsed once, like a throat swallowing.

Silas's hand drifted, casual as weather, toward his sleeve. Wren knew that motion. She'd watched him do it a hundred times in a hundred rooms — the little theft, the quick fingers, the world rearranged while you blinked. Whatever he was reaching for, though, the priest saw it too.

The light in the man's eyes went white.

"*No,*" the priest said, and the word hit the stairwell like a hammer blow, and the bell above them tolled again, and Wren's knees buckled. Sound became pressure became pain; the carved prayers on the walls shrieked in resonance; and through it all the doubled voice rolled down the stairs, no longer gentle at all:

***THEY ARE THIEVES. THEY COME FOR MY VOICE. PRIEST — KEEP WHAT IS MINE.***

"Gladly," the priest said, smiling, weeping, both at once. "Gladly, my lord. Gladly."

* * *

What happened next, Wren would remember in fragments for the rest of her life.

She remembered the priest moving faster than a man his age should move, robes billowing, light streaming from his sleeves. She remembered Silas grabbing her arm and hauling her upward — *upward*, which seemed insane, running toward the thing in the bell rather than out the door — and shouting over the din: "The relic's at the top! We run or we die tired!"

She remembered the stairs blurring beneath her feet, the golden light chasing them, the walls of carved prayers strobing past like the pages of a madman's book. She remembered thinking, absurdly, of the circle of toys in the empty house, all the animals facing inward, waiting.

She remembered the belfry.

It opened around them all at once — a high stone chamber open to the gray sky on four sides, wind moaning through the arches, and hanging in the center, enormous, the bell. It was older than the tower around it, older than the village, green-black bronze covered in script that writhed when you weren't looking directly at it. And suspended inside the bell's mouth, held by nothing, hung a bone.

It was small. That was the horror of it. After everything — the tower, the bodies, the mad priest, the voice like a landslide — the relic itself was a bone the length of her forearm, pale as milk, drifting in the bell's shadowed heart and glowing faintly, softly, like a coal remembering fire.

The tongue-bone of a god.

"There you are," Silas breathed.

Then the priest filled the doorway behind them, and the sky went dark.

Not cloud-dark. *Presence*-dark. Something poured up out of the bell — smoke, or sound given weight, a shape of layered voices and ringing bronze that unfolded and unfolded and never finished unfolding. Wren felt her mind slide off it every time she tried to look straight at it, the way your eye slides off the sun. It had no face, but it regarded them. She could feel the regard like a hand on the back of her neck.

***LITTLE THIEF,*** it said, and the words landed inside her skull rather than her ears. ***I KNOW YOUR KIND. YOU TOOK AND TOOK AND TOOK UNTIL THE TAKING WAS ALL YOU WERE.***

Wren saw Silas flinch. Actually flinch — the great unflappable Silas Pemberton, caught like a boy with his hand in the till. And she understood, in one cold flash, that the god wasn't guessing. It had read something off him. It knew about the memories. It knew what he was.

***WHAT WILL YOU GIVE ME FOR MY VOICE?*** the god asked, almost playful now, the playfulness more terrible than rage. ***EVERYTHING COSTS. EVEN YOU KNOW THAT. NAME YOUR PRICE, THIEF. YOUR SECRETS? YOUR YEARS? HER?***

The last word curled toward Wren like smoke finding a draft, and Silas moved — stepped bodily between her and the presence, arms slightly out, ridiculous and gallant and terrified.

"Nothing," he said. "We give you nothing. We're taking the bone and walking out, and you're going to let us, because if you could stop us yourself you'd have done it already. You need him." He jerked his chin at the priest. "You're stuck in the bell, and he's your hands, and your hands are *old*, and I don't think he can keep this up much longer. Am I wrong?"

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere below them, far down the spiral, a door banged in the wind.

***BOLD,*** the god said. ***STUPID AND BOLD. PRIEST.***

"Yes, my lord." The priest had come to stand at the bell's rim, gazing up at the presence with an expression of such naked love that Wren had to look away. It was like watching someone pray with their whole body. It was like watching drowning.

***KEEP THEM.***

The priest raised his hands, and the light came off him in waves, and the air in the belfry turned solid.

Wren couldn't describe it any other way. The air *hardened*, pressing against her chest, her throat, pinning her arms. She heard Silas grunt beside her, straining, his clever fingers scrabbling at nothing. The priest walked toward them slowly across the belfry floor, light pouring from his eyes, and he was smiling, and he was saying, "Don't struggle, it's easier if you don't struggle, He only wants to talk, He only ever wants to *talk*—"

And Wren stopped being afraid.

It happened quietly, the way important things do. She looked at the priest — at the ruined vestments and the burning eyes and the decades of carved prayers rotting the stairwell below — and she thought: *he gave everything to a voice, and the voice gave him nothing back but orders.* She thought of Silas in the burning street in Casterhale, turning back for her. She thought of the memory-vial against Silas's chest, the dead knight's last gift, the truth they were carrying toward a king who might already be a hollow mask. She thought: *somebody has to be the hands that hold on to what matters.*

The pressure on her chest was immense. She leaned into it. Took one step. Then another.

"Wren—" Silas gritted.

"Shut up," she said, "I'm busy."

The priest was three paces away. Two. The light in his eyes was blinding up close, and behind it his actual eyes were wet, and exhausted, and so, so tired. That was the thing she saw, in the last moment — not the god wearing him. The man. A man who had begged into a silence for forty years and finally heard something answer, even if the answer was a leash.

"I'm sorry," Wren told him, and meant it.

Then she moved — dropped low, inside his guard, the way Tam had taught her during the long road weeks when they'd played at fighting with sticks and the boy had laughed at how she led with her shoulder. She drove her palm up under the priest's jaw, hard, the way Tam had shown her, and the priest's head snapped back, and the light stuttered —

— and the pressure holding her vanished, because the pressure was his, and for one blessed second the god had no hands.

"*Now!*"

Silas was already moving. Of course he was. He came off the floor like a thrown knife, crossed the belfry in four strides, and plunged his arm into the bell's dark mouth — into the space where the bone hung — and the moment his fingers closed around it, the whole tower *screamed*.

Bronze screamed. Stone screamed. The god screamed with ten thousand overlapped voices, and the sound tore across the sky, and every carved prayer in the stairwell below ignited at once, gold flame racing down the spiral like fire down a fuse. Silas staggered back out of the bell with the tongue-bone clutched to his chest, and the bone was blazing, and his hands were smoking, and he was laughing — actually laughing, wild and awful — "Got it, got it, *got* it—"

***THIEF,*** the god roared, gathering itself, the dark shape collapsing inward toward the bell like a fist closing. ***THIEF, I WILL TAKE IT OUT OF YOUR CORPSE—***

The priest rose.

Blood ran from his split lip. His eyes had gone dark — truly dark, empty of the god's light for the first time in decades, and he swayed on his feet like a man waking from a very long dream. He looked at his own hands. He looked at the bell. He looked at Wren.

"Oh," he said. Small-voiced. Human. "Oh, it's *quiet*."

And then, before either of them could speak, before the god could finish gathering its fury, the old man walked to the edge of the belfry, spread his ruined arms wide like a man greeting a sunrise, and stepped out into the open air.

Wren lunged — too slow, too far — and caught nothing but wind. Below, far below, the gray mist received him without a sound.

The god's scream changed key. From rage into something else. Something that, in any creature less vast, Wren would have called grief.

***PRIEST,*** it said. ***PRIEST.***

Then the dark shape poured backward into the bell, the bronze swallowed it, and the great bell tolled one final time — a note so low and mournful that Wren felt tears start down her face without her permission — and went silent.

* * *

Afterward there was wind, and gray light, and the two of them sitting on the belfry floor with their backs against the cold stone, breathing.

Silas's hands were burned where he'd gripped the bone. Wren wrapped them without asking, using strips of her sleeve, and he let her, which from Silas was practically a marriage proposal. The tongue-bone lay between them, wrapped in cloth, quiet now. Warm, though. Still warm, like a sleeping animal.

"You dropped it twice on the stairs," Silas said eventually. "Climbing down. I counted."

"You were crying on the stairs."

"I was *winded*."

"You were crying."

He didn't argue. That was new, too. He sat looking out through the arches at the empty village below, at the doors standing open, at the table set for a supper no one had eaten, and his face did something complicated and then went carefully blank.

"It talked to me," he said at last. "Up there. It knew things. About the taking." His jaw worked. "About what I am."

Wren tied off the bandage. She thought about all the things she could say — the cruel ones, the kind ones, the true ones. She chose the true one.

"Yeah," she said. "It also called you bold. Stupid and bold. I'd take stupid and bold over careful and hollow any day."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of trust, which looks the same from the outside. "You saved my life up there."

"Don't make it weird."

"You saved my life," he repeated, stubborn, watching her. "The debt runs the other way now. You know what that means, in my trade."

"It means you owe *me*," Wren said, standing, picking up the wrapped bone and slinging it across her back where it rode warm against her spine. "And I'm a much harsher creditor than you ever were. Now get up. We've got a god's tongue, a map, and six weeks. Next stop—"

Below them, in the silent village, every single door in every single house closed at once.

Silas was on his feet before the echo faded. "Wren," he said quietly, "tell me you didn't hear that."

Down in the streets, lights were coming on in the windows. One by one. All of them gold.
