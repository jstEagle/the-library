## 12. Marlow's Ledger

Marlow still kept his shop on Tallow Row, wedged between a chandler that smelled of rendered fat and a pawnbroker whose window displayed nothing but broken clocks. The sign above the door had been repainted since Silas last saw it — *MARLOW & SONS, FINE CURIOSITIES* — though as far as Silas knew, Marlow had no sons. He'd never had a wife either, or a conscience worth the name. The sign was a lie the way all of Casterhale was a lie: painted fresh over rot.

Silas stood across the street in the shadow of the chandler's awning and watched the door for an hour before he went in. That was the old training. *Watch first. Watch longer than you think you need to.* His teacher — a man whose face Silas could no longer summon, only his hands and his voice and the smell of tallow on his knuckles — had drilled it into him until watching became breathing.

The river city seethed around him. Barges ground against the quays below, and gulls screamed their ownership of everything, and somewhere a fishwife was cursing a customer with the kind of fluency that took decades to build. Ordinary life. It had been four days since the knight died at the valley's edge with a vial in his fist, three days since Silas had watched a dead man's memory show him a king hollowed out like a gourd, and two days since they'd slipped out of the Hollow through a smuggler's tunnel that smelled of bat droppings and old fear. Two days on the road, and the world outside the valley had not yet noticed them.

He intended to keep it that way for exactly as long as it took to buy what he needed: a route south, papers if Marlow could get them, and a name — someone in the capital who dealt in relics without asking where they came from.

He pushed open the door. A bell tinkled, bright and stupid.

Inside, the shop was exactly as he remembered it, which was itself suspicious. Curio dealers' shops changed; stock moved, shelves emptied, dust gathered in new patterns. This one was frozen. The same stuffed cormorant on its perch by the window. The same jar of pickled eels. The same cracked astrolabe on the same shelf, tilted at the same angle, gathering the same gray felt of dust.

*He hasn't touched anything,* Silas thought. *Or he's arranged it to look untouched.*

"Closed," said a voice from behind the counter, and then Marlow looked up from his ledger, and his face did something complicated. Surprise first. Then calculation, fast as a card sharp's shuffle. Then a smile laid over both like fresh paint.

"Well," Marlow said. "Silo Pemberton. They told me you were dead."

"They tell you everything, don't they? That's your whole trade."

"My trade is knowing which lies are worth buying." Marlow closed the ledger. He was older — of course he was; eleven years older, because it had been eleven years since Silas had sold him the contents of a dead courier's satchel and vanished into the valley — but age had only tightened him, the way a rope tightens. His hair had gone from iron to ash. His eyes hadn't changed at all. "You look terrible."

"I've been living somewhere honest. It doesn't suit me."

"No," Marlow said, and something flickered behind the paint — almost fondness, or the memory of it. "It wouldn't." He glanced past Silas at the door, at the street beyond it. "You're alone?"

The question had teeth in it. Silas heard them. "Why?"

"Because the last man who came through here asking after routes south wasn't asking for himself." Marlow leaned back in his chair, and the chair complained. "And because I'd hate to sell you a map to a place that gets you killed when a map to safety costs the same."

"I want a route to the capital. Quiet roads. And a name in the capital — someone who moves relics."

"Relics." Marlow's eyebrows climbed. "Gods' bones, that kind of relic?"

"The kind that gets people killed."

"That kind I have a buyer for already." Marlow said it lightly, but Silas caught it — the half-second delay, the way his thumb pressed flat against the ledger's edge. "Everyone does, these days. Since the prayers started getting answered, there's not a lord or a bishop or a frightened widow in nine kingdoms who wouldn't pay a fortune for a fingerbone of something old and holy. You've picked a profitable moment to come out of hiding, Silo."

"I'm not selling. I'm asking."

"Asking costs more." Marlow opened a drawer, took out a bottle of plum brandy, two cups. He poured without offering choice. "Sit down. You look like a man who hasn't sat down in two days."

"I haven't."

"Then sit." He slid one cup across the counter. "Eleven years, Silo. You owe me at least the courtesy of a drink before you ask me to risk my neck."

Silas didn't touch the cup. He stood in the doorway's light and let the silence do his talking, because silence had always been his best currency. Marlow lasted about ten seconds under it, which was eight seconds longer than most.

"You always were a cold one," Marlow said. "That's why I liked you. All right. Route south, quiet roads, a name in the capital. I can give you all three. Price is fifty crowns and the story."

"The story?"

"What you're carrying. What's got the Inquisition sealing passes and burning waystations between here and the mountains. Don't insult me — you think I don't know why you came out of the Hollow now, of all times? Something fell out of the sky, Silo. Something wearing armor. And every ear in this city with a price tag on it has been listening for footsteps ever since." He tapped the counter once. "I'm not asking you to hand it over. I'm asking what it is. Knowledge is my stock in trade, and right now knowledge of you is worth more than the brandy."

Silas weighed it. He'd known this would be the price; he'd walked in expecting it. The trick was to give Marlow enough truth to satisfy him and not enough to hang anyone. "A dying knight gave me a message for the capital. That's all."

"A message."

"Sealed. For eyes in the palace, not mine."

Marlow studied him for a long moment, and Silas watched him decide — visibly, the way you watch a man count coins — whether to believe it. Whatever he saw in Silas's face, he chose to buy it. Or chose to pretend to.

"Fifty crowns," Marlow said. "Half now, half when you're across the border. And the name in the capital — that's extra. Brother Corvane, at the Chantry of Saint Vellum. Tell him Marlow sent you and mind how you say it. He owes me, and he hates owing."

"And the route?"

Marlow pulled a rolled vellum from beneath the counter and flattened it with both palms. Roads inked in brown and red, the river country stitched with blue veins. "South along the Lea until Harrow Ford, then cut west before the toll bridge — the Inquisition checkpoints the main crossings, but they can't cover the ford. From there, the Shepherd's Road through the hill country. Ten days to the capital if the weather holds, twelve if it doesn't, forever if you're stupid." He tapped the western fork. "Avoid the King's Way entirely. The god-touched ride it night and day now, and they stop everyone. Everyone." He looked up. "Including pilgrims. Including children. There are stories coming down the river about what they take at those checkpoints, Silo. Stories I don't like repeating in my own shop."

"I've seen what they take."

"Then you know better than me." Marlow rolled the map and held it out — and held on when Silas took it, just a beat too long, fingers dry and warm as paper. "One more thing. Free of charge, because I liked your teacher and I miss him. If the Inquisition comes asking me about you — and they will, because they come asking everyone about everything now — I will tell them whatever keeps my shop standing. You understand that. It's not betrayal. It's arithmetic."

"I know," Silas said. "It's the only honest thing about you."

Marlow smiled, and for a moment the paint thinned and something older showed underneath — tired, and maybe sorry. "Drink your brandy. You'll want it on the Shepherd's Road."

Silas left the cup full and walked out into the noise of Tallow Row, and did not let himself think about the word *arithmetic*, or about how easily he recognized the sum.

* * *

Wren was waiting in the room above the Drowned Rat, a tavern so disreputable even its rats had opinions. She'd found them a garret through means she refused to explain, paid with coins she also refused to explain, and had spent the intervening hours sitting by the window with the vial wrapped in cloth in her lap, watching the street below the way a cat watches a hole in a wall.

She looked up when he came in. Thirteen years old, all elbows and suspicion, with her hair chopped short with a knife for reasons she'd also declined to share. She'd been following him for six weeks now — out of the Hollow, through the tunnel, down the river road — and in all that time she had asked him perhaps four hundred questions and accepted perhaps forty answers.

"Well?" she said.

"He came through. Route, papers, a contact in the capital."

"And what did it cost?"

"Fifty crowns and a lie."

"Whose lie?"

"Mine. The good kind. The kind that keeps us alive." He set the map on the table and weighted its corners with coins. "We leave tonight, before the gates close. South along the Lea to Harrow Ford, west into the hills. Ten days."

Wren studied the map, and he watched her read it the way she read everything — completely, hungrily, like a person eating a meal she wasn't sure would be repeated. She'd taught herself letters off tavern signs and funeral notices, she'd told him once, and he'd believed her, because everything about Wren was self-made. Every skill, every scar, every sharp edge. Except the childhood. Except the years he'd taken.

He'd stolen her memories when she was seven. Sold them to a collector in the Hollow who'd wanted a child's wonder, unspoiled, bottled. He'd been nineteen and hungry and very good at not thinking about things, and he had not thought about it for eleven years, right up until a girl with no past knocked on his door and demanded it back.

"We should go now, then," she said. "Not tonight. Now."

"It's midday. Gates are watched hardest at midday."

"Then we go at the change of watch, when they're bored." She was already rolling the map. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you plan like we have time. We don't have time. I watched the street for three hours, Silas. There was a beggar by the chandler's who never begged. Just stood there. And a cart went past twice with the same turnip lying on top, same bruise, same side up, and nobody sells turnips twice on Tallow Row unless they want to be seen doing it." She tied the cloth around the vial and slung it across her body. "Someone's counting foot traffic. Someone's counting *us*."

Silas felt the cold arrive in his stomach, patient and familiar. "You're sure."

"I'm sure that I grew up with nothing and learned to notice everything, and I'm telling you we're being counted." She met his eyes. "How much did you tell Marlow?"

"Nothing true."

"How much did he guess?"

Everything. That was the answer, and they both knew it. Marlow had guessed everything, and Marlow owed favors the way rivers owed the sea, and Silas had walked into his shop anyway because there was no one else — because the map of his old life had exactly one door left standing, and he'd gone through it with his eyes open.

"We move," he said. "Change of watch. Back gate of the Rat, down to the water, hire a barge as far as Harrow Ford. We'll be on the Shepherd's Road by dark tomorrow."

"And if we're followed?"

"Then we lose them in the hills."

Wren nodded once, briskly, and started packing, and Silas went to the window and looked down at Tallow Row and tried to see what she'd seen. The chandler's. The pawnbroker's broken clocks. The corner where a beggar should have been begging and wasn't.

The corner was empty now.

* * *

They made the back gate at the change of watch, as planned, and the plan began dying immediately.

The alley behind the Drowned Rat smelled of piss and low tide. Silas went first, Wren a pace behind with the vial flat against her chest, and they were halfway to the water stairs when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. Not the watch bells. These were deeper, tuned wrong, a sound you felt in your back teeth — and Silas knew that sound, had heard it once through the walls of a waystation, the sound the god-touched made when they worked. When they *sought*.

"Smoke," Wren said.

He smelled it a heartbeat later. Woodsmoke, wrong for the hour, wrong for the weather, and rising from the direction of Tallow Row.

They climbed to the water stairs and looked back, and Casterhale was beginning to burn.

Not the whole city — not yet. Just the row. Just the chandler's shop, whose stock of rendered fat and tallow candles went up like the wick it was, flame climbing the building in a bright orange roar that lit the river red. And beside it, the pawnbroker's. And beside that, the shop with the sign reading *MARLOW & SONS, FINE CURIOSITIES*, its windows glowing from within, its door standing open, figures moving inside with the unhurried purpose of people doing a job.

Three of them. Gray cloaks, hoods back despite the smoke, and even at this distance Silas could see the shine of them — the god-touched gleam, eyes catching light like a cat's, faces too calm for a fire. Inquisition. Not searching the shop. *Emptying* it. One of them carried Marlow's ledger under one arm like a psalter. Another held something small and glass that caught the firelight.

The vial shape of it was unmistakable.

"They're not burning it down for him," Wren whispered. "They're burning it down to erase the sale. To erase that anyone ever—" Her voice caught. "Is he still in there?"

Silas looked. The third figure stepped out of the doorway, and behind it, through the open door, Silas saw a chair. Empty. Ropes cut and coiled on the floor, neat as bookkeeping.

Either Marlow had run, or Marlow had been taken out the back ahead of the fire, or Marlow had never been in the building at all when it lit. He found, examining himself honestly, that he could not make himself care which. That was the part that frightened him — not the fire, not the pursuit, but the clean, cold ease with which he catalogued the possibilities and filed them under *irrelevant*. Eleven years of practice at not feeling things, and here was the dividend.

"Silas." Wren grabbed his sleeve. "The barge. We have to go *now*."

There were barges at the water stairs — three of them, moored and dark. And on the nearest, a figure stood up. Gray cloak. The shine of god-touched eyes turning toward the stairs, toward them, unhurried, certain, the certainty of a trap that has already closed.

"There," the figure said, and its voice carried over the water with impossible clarity, pitched to reach them and no one else. "The thief and the girl. The crown thanks you for leading us to the message."

Silas did the arithmetic. It took less than a second, and he hated himself for the speed of it. The water stairs ran two ways: down to the barges, toward the god-touched, or up along the quay wall, toward the burning row and the streets beyond — streets that would be filling with watchmen and gawkers and, within minutes, gray cloaks. Two exits. One of them had a hunter standing in it.

And Wren — Wren was slower than him. Smaller. The vial heavy against her chest. If they split, if he went up and over the quay wall while she drew the eye below—

The thought arrived whole. He didn't summon it. It simply presented itself, fully formed, the way his best professional thoughts always did: *You could make it alone. You've done harder things with worse odds. She's a debt, not a duty. Run.*

One heartbeat. Maybe two. He stood on the water stairs with firelight on his face and considered leaving a thirteen-year-old girl to the Inquisition, and the consideration was calm, and thorough, and quick.

Then he saw her face. She was looking at him — not at the god-touched, at *him* — and she had already done the same arithmetic. He could see it in her eyes: she knew what he was weighing. She'd grown up with nothing; she knew exactly what she was worth on the open market, and she'd braced for the number.

And she nodded. Almost imperceptibly. *Go. I'll draw them.*

Something in Silas's chest tore along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"No," he said aloud, and grabbed her wrist. "Together. We're worth more together and you know it, so don't argue—"

The god-touched on the barge raised one hand, and the river answered.

The water beneath the stairs convulsed. It rose — not waves, *walls*, a column of river hauling itself upright with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat — and hung there, black and moonlit and trembling, blocking the channel north. Cutting off the water road. Herding them.

Toward the fire. Toward the streets. Toward the net.

"*Up*," Silas snarled, and pulled Wren back up the stairs, and they ran.

They ran along the quay with the burning row at their backs and the risen river at their left, and the world narrowed to cobblestones and breath and Wren's hand locked in his. Behind them, boots on stone — not running, *walking*, the measured tread of hunters who knew the net was closing and saw no reason to hurry. Ahead, the alley mouth that led away from the river, into the tangle of Casterhale's oldest quarter, where the streets forgot their own names and the maps lied.

"Where?" Wren gasped.

"Anywhere crowded. Anywhere with too many doors. The fish market — it runs all night for the morning catch—"

They burst out of the alley into the fish market square and stopped, because the market was empty.

Not thin. Not sparse. *Empty*. Stalls shuttered, lanterns unlit, the flagstones slick and silvered with fish scales and nobody, nobody at all, at midnight, in a city that never slept. And standing at the far end of the square, where the lane narrowed toward the river district, three more gray cloaks. Waiting. Unhurried.

The trap hadn't been the shop. The trap had been the whole night, the whole city, drawn shut around them the moment Marlow's ledger changed hands. Every exit priced and purchased. Arithmetic.

Wren's grip tightened on his hand. "Silas."

"I see them."

"What do we do?"

And Silas Pemberton, who had survived the Hollow for eleven years by never being surprised, by always having one more door, by treating every catastrophe as a problem in logistics — Silas looked at the three waiting figures, and the burning sky, and the girl beside him whose childhood he had stolen and whose trust he had nearly spent an hour ago like a cheap coin — and heard himself say:

"The roofs."

Because there was one thing the god-touched could not buy, and it wasn't loyalty and it wasn't luck. It was a thief's map of a city, drawn over eleven missing years in the only currency he'd ever truly trusted: other people's forgotten things. And somewhere in the jumble of what he'd stolen and sold and lost, he *knew* these rooftops — knew them the way he knew his own hands, because some memory, some dead man's or drunkard's or long-gone boy's, had walked these ridgelines before him and never bothered to mention it until now.

"The roofs," he said again, louder, and dragged her toward the drainpipe at the chandler's back wall as the gray cloaks finally, finally began to run.

Behind them, Marlow's shop collapsed inward in a bloom of sparks, and the ledger burned, and the river stood in its black wall and watched.

Above the rooftops of Casterhale, with the fire painting the clouds orange and the god-touched swarming up behind them like ants up a felled tree, Silas ran — and in his pocket, folded against his heart, Marlow's map fluttered, and the western fork, the Shepherd's Road, the way south, was still inked there in brown and red.

Still real. Still theirs.

If they lived to walk it.
