## 13. The Inquisitor Vess

The cell smelled of river water and old lies, which in Casterhale amounted to the same thing.

Inquisitor Sera Vess sat across from Marlow the fence and let him sweat. She was good at letting people sweat. It was, she had learned over eleven years of service to the Choir Militant, the most efficient tool she owned. Fear did more work than any question she could ask, and it never needed feeding or rest.

Marlow was a soft man gone softer — jowls like wet bread, rings on every finger, a silk waistcoat that had been fashionable when Vess was still a novitiate scrubbing chapel floors with a toothbrush. He'd been talking for six minutes without saying anything, which took talent.

"—and I told them, Inquisitor, I told them plain as scripture: I don't traffic in that kind of merchandise. Memory-work is filthy business. Unsanctified. I keep my ledger clean—"

"Your ledger," Vess said, "lists a sale of three vials to a buyer in Ashford Row two months ago. One of those vials contained a memory of the royal household."

The sweating stopped. That was always interesting, the moment fear changed shape — from the general fog of guilt into something specific and sharp. Vess leaned forward into the lamplight so he could see her face properly. People usually found it disappointing. She was not tall, not scarred, not beautiful in the way that made stories. She had a round, ordinary face, the kind you forgot before the door closed behind her. The only remarkable thing about her were her eyes, and only if you looked closely, and only if you knew what god-touched meant: a faint silver ring around each iris, like frost forming on a windowpane.

Marlow looked closely. Marlow knew what it meant. His hands went flat on the table between them, palms down, as if steadying himself against weather.

"I want you to understand something," Vess said pleasantly. "I don't care about your ledger. I don't care about your smuggling, your fencing, the two men you drowned in the Caster canal in '41 — yes, we know about that too, it's in a file nobody will ever read because nobody above me cares about dead smugglers. What I care about is one vial. One memory. And I care about who bought it, who sold it, and where it went."

"And if I tell you?"

"Then you walk out of here tonight, and the file stays unread forever."

He studied her. She let him. Somewhere above them, through the stone, the city bells of Casterhale rang the hour — nine deep bronze notes, tuned, like all bells in the realm now, to the suppression pitch the Crown had mandated three years ago. The sound pressed down on the cell like a hand. Vess felt it in her teeth. She always felt it. The bells were supposed to make the god-touched comfortable. They made her feel like a bird in a cage built by people who thought they were being kind.

"The buyer," Marlow said at last, "was a courier. Grey cloak, no name, paid in mint coin. He took the vial east."

"East toward what?"

"Toward the capital road. Toward—" Marlow's throat worked. "Inquisitor, I'll say this once, and then I'd like my file to stay unread. Before the courier came, the vial passed through other hands. A knight's hands. Ser Aldous Vane, formerly of the King's own company. He sold it to me desperate, drunk, and bleeding from somewhere under his armor, and he said a thing I've spent two months trying to drink out of my head."

Vess waited.

"He said the crown is empty." Marlow's voice dropped, as though the walls might be listening, and perhaps they were; the Inquisition's cells were warded, but wards were prayers, and lately — no. Lately nothing. Lately the silence held. "He said the king died years ago and something else wears his face, and he'd seen it done, carried it in his head like a coal, and he couldn't carry it anymore. Then he cried. Big man like that. Cried like a boy."

Vess sat back. Her heart was doing something quiet and terrible, the way it did whenever the world confirmed the thing she already believed. She kept her face still. Eleven years of practice.

"Describe the courier."

"Grey cloak. Young. Walked like a soldier but dressed like a clerk." Marlow hesitated. "And there was another one. After. A thief came asking after the vial's origin. Pemberton. Silas Pemberton. You know the name?"

She did. The files knew the name. *Silas Pemberton, memory-thief, resident of the Hollow, unregistered practitioner, method unknown.* A thin file. Thin files bothered her more than thick ones. Thick ones meant a man had been caught often enough to leave a shape. Thin ones meant he was careful, and careful men were the ones who got away with things.

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I'm not a fool."

"You're an enormous fool," Vess said, without heat, "but go on."

"I told him nothing, and then I sold his description to the courier's people, because they paid better than you do." Marlow spread his ringed hands. "That's the whole of my sin, Inquisitor. Greed. Honest greed. I never touched the thing itself."

*The crown is empty.*

Vess stood, gathered her coat from the back of the chair, and paused at the door.

"You'll be released within the hour. There's a purse on the table. Consider it interest on your discretion." She opened the door, then stopped. "One more thing, Marlow. The knight. Ser Aldous. Where did he go after he sold you the vial?"

"East," Marlow said. "Chasing it, I think. Or running from whatever was chasing him." He rubbed his face. "They found him at the valley border two days back, didn't they? Dead. Fell out of the sky, the carter said. Fell out of a clear sky."

"Yes," Vess said. "They did."

She closed the door on him and stood a moment in the corridor, listening to the bells finish their decay.

Fell out of a clear sky. Knights did not fall out of clear skies unless something with wings wanted them dropped somewhere specific — or unless something answered a prayer. And the Hollow was the one place in all the world where nothing answered anything. Which meant Aldous Vane had been thrown at the valley's edge like a stone from a sling, aimed at whoever lived inside.

Aimed at a thief named Pemberton.

* * *

Her name had been Sera Voss then, with the double-s, before the Choir Militant corrected the spelling in their rolls and she'd let them, because by then she'd already let them take everything else worth keeping.

She had been nine years old, and her mother was dying of the lung-rot, and the village of Harrow's Bend had one shrine left — a little moss-eaten thing to Vaela of the Quiet Hours, a minor goddess, a hearth-god, the kind of divinity the theologians called *residual*. The kind that wasn't supposed to answer anymore. The gods had gone silent a century before; everyone knew it; the priests said it with shrugs, the way you mention bad weather. You lit the candle anyway. It was habit, like locking a door against a thief who'd died before your grandmother was born.

Sera had knelt on the cold flagstones with her mother's cough rattling through the floorboards above, and she had prayed. Not the rote words. Real prayer. The ugly kind, the kind with bargaining in it, the kind children are too honest to dress up. *Take me instead. Take ten years off anyone. Take my voice, take my eyes, take anything, just let her breathe through winter.*

And the shrine had answered.

People imagined divine answers as light, as choirs, as warmth. What actually happened was this: the candle flame stood up straight and turned white, and a voice came out of the stone — not heard so much as *installed*, arriving fully formed inside her skull like a memory she'd never made — and it said:

**Yes.**

One word. And her mother breathed easier that very night, and lived through winter, and through spring, and was still alive four years later when the thing wearing Sera's reflection started following her.

Because that was the price, and no one had warned her, because no one alive remembered the terms. The gods had not gone silent because they died. They had gone silent because they were *full* — glutted on a century of unanswered bargains, sleeping off the meal. And when one woke, hungry, and a child offered everything, it took everything, and gave change besides.

It gave her the sight. The silver-ringed eyes. The ability to see the seams in people — the places where memory had been cut, stolen, patched. It gave her dreams of the Quiet Hours that were not quiet at all: a goddess vast and patient and *hungry*, coiled in the dark between seconds, whispering that the bargain was good, the bargain was fair, the bargain would keep, oh, it would keep for a lifetime.

It gave her, in short, exactly what the Inquisition was founded to find.

They'd come for her at fourteen. Not cruelly — the Choir Militant of that era was gentle with children, gentler than the Church that replaced it. They'd taken her to the seminary at Corvale, taught her doctrine and discipline and how to hold a suspect's gaze until his certainty dissolved, and they'd taught her the official history: that the gods were gone, that their absence was mercy, that any flicker of answering power was not miracle but *infection*, and infection must be cut out before it spread.

She had believed it the way you believe a wall is load-bearing: because everything rested on it. Because if the gods could answer — if they were awake, if they were listening, if they were *taking payment* — then every prayer ever whispered by every grieving child in every cold chapel was a contract waiting to be enforced, and the world was not a world at all but a trap baited with hope.

Better the silence. Better the bells. Better a hundred years of dead shrines than one more nine-year-old kneeling in the dark, trading her soul for her mother's breath, never knowing the bill would come due with interest.

For twenty years, the silence had held. The bells rang their suppression pitch, the shrines stayed dark, and Vess rose through the ranks on a simple reputation: she found god-touched before the god-touched found their price. She had burned out covens, silenced false prophets, put down a whole village in the Weeping Marches that had begun leaving offerings of *teeth*, and she had slept well afterward. Mostly.

And then, eight months ago, the reports had started. A shrine in the southern hills flickering. A fisherman on Lake Merren who'd traded his shadow for a full net. A well in Dunmark that granted wishes and collected voices. Small things. Residual things. Gods waking up at the edges of the map like embers catching wind.

And now a knight falling out of a clear sky at the border of the Hollow, carrying a vial that proved the King of the Nine Valleys was a hollowed thing in a borrowed face — which meant either the knight was mad, or the gods were awake enough to *witness*, and both possibilities ended the same way.

The silence was over. The trap was springing. And somewhere east of here, a thief with a thin file was carrying the proof of it in his pocket like a lit match.

* * *

The safehouse the Inquisition kept in Casterhale was a chandler's shop that had never once sold a candle. Vess climbed to its attic and unrolled the map of the eastern reaches on the trestle table, weighting the corners with candlesticks that had never been lit.

Her aide, Brother Callow, a young man whose piety was genuine and therefore slightly exhausting, stood waiting with the dispatch box.

"Orders from Corvale," he said. "Priority seal."

She broke it. Read it. Read it again, because the first reading seemed impossible and the second merely horrifying.

Corvale had received the same reports she had — the waking shrines, the small hungry miracles — and Corvale had reached the opposite conclusion. Not *infection to be cut out*. Opportunity. The Crown's position, relayed through the Church's new leadership, was that the old gods' return was a political crisis to be managed, not a spiritual one: the Hollow King's court had petitioned for expanded authority over all religious matters in anticipation of midwinter, and the Church, eager to stay on the right side of a throne that had grown strange and sudden in its decrees, had agreed.

The orders instructed her to stand down the search for the vial. To return to Corvale. To leave the matter of Silas Pemberton to the Crown's own agents, who would handle it *discreetly*.

Discreetly. The word sat on the page like a fly on meat.

"They're protecting him," Callow said, when she read it aloud. His young face was baffled. "The court is protecting—"

"The court isn't protecting the vial." Vess set the orders down. Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "The court is protecting the rite. Whatever happens at midwinter, they want no Inquisition eyes anywhere near it. They've ordered me blind two weeks before the event." She looked up at him. "Callow, in eleven years, has Corvale ever once ordered me to stop hunting evidence?"

"No, Inquisitor."

"No." She rolled up the map. Outside the attic window, Casterhale sprawled along its brown river, bell towers rising everywhere like masts of a fleet at anchor, all of them singing their suppression note, all of it suddenly sounding less like safety and more like a lie told loudly enough to pass for one. "So we have a choice, Brother. Obey, and go home, and let the Crown handle its own affair discreetly. Or disobey, and follow a dead knight's trail east on our own authority, knowing that when we're wrong, we hang, and when we're right—"

"When we're right?" Callow asked quietly.

"Then we're the only ones looking," Vess said. "And I have seen what answers prayers, Brother. I have been the answer to one. I know precisely what is coming if that vial reaches the capital and the thing under the crown finishes whatever it's building. It isn't a war. Wars leave survivors."

She took her coat off its hook. The silver rings of her eyes caught the grey window-light, frost on glass, the mark of a bargain struck twenty years ago and still, always, keeping.

"We ride east within the hour," she said. "There's a village on the capital road called Mirefen. The carter who found the knight's body says Pemberton and a girl passed through heading that direction. And Callow—" she paused at the stair, hearing herself say the next part, hating how much it sounded like hope, "—the carter said something else. Said the folk in Mirefen have all started smiling. Every soul in the village. The same smile."

Callow frowned. "Happy villagers. Is that a crime now?"

"No," Vess said. "It's a symptom. Get your horse."
