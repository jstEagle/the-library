## 14. Hollowed Men

The road bent east around a hill the color of old bone, and Wren said, "Smoke."

She was right. Not the gray-blue ribbon of a hearth fire, not the black column of something burning — but a thin, wrong haze that hung over the valley ahead like breath on cold glass. It didn't rise. It just sat there.

"Could be nothing," Tam said. He'd taken to walking at Silas's elbow since the checkpoint, close enough that their shoulders bumped on the downhills. He carried a stick he'd whittled into something between a sword and a cross, and he swung it at weeds as they walked. "Villages have smoke."

"Villages have chimneys," Silas said. "This is coming from the ground."

They had been three days out of Casterhale, moving by back roads because Vess knew their route now and Marlow's ledger had probably been read aloud in every garrison from the river to the sea. Three days of sleeping in ditches and eating hard bread, and Silas had almost convinced himself the worst thing behind them was fire. He should have known better. The worst things never chased you. They waited politely ahead.

The village was called Bracken Hollow — or it had been, according to the milestone, which someone had defaced with a chisel until only *BRACKEN* remained. It sat in a fold of farmland with maybe forty houses, a mill, a shrine to some minor harvest god whose name Silas had never bothered learning. The fields were half-harvested. Tools lay where they'd been dropped. A cart stood in the road with its ox still harnessed, standing very still, chewing at nothing.

"No dogs," Wren said quietly.

Silas noticed it the same moment she did. No dogs barking at strangers. No chickens scattering. Even the ox didn't lift its head. The whole place had the quality of a painting of a village rather than a village itself — everything correct, everything still, everything *arranged*.

"Stay behind me," Silas said. "Tam. You too. If I say run—"

"I know," Tam said. "Run and don't look back." He said it the way children repeat lessons they've heard too many times, and Silas hated that he'd been the one to teach it.

They went in through the western lane, past a well with a rope coiled neatly beside it, past a house with its door open to the afternoon. Inside the open door, a woman stood at a table kneading bread. She was mid-motion, hands working the dough, and she did not stop when they passed. She did not look up. Her face wore a smile — small, pleasant, symmetrical — and her eyes were fixed on the middle distance like a woman listening to music no one else could hear.

"She can see us," Wren whispered. "I saw her eyes move."

"I know."

They kept walking. At the second house, an old man sat on his step sharpening a scythe blade with long, even strokes. Same smile. Same fixed gaze. The stone touched the steel in perfect rhythm, *shhh, shhh, shhh*, and he might have been doing it for a hundred years.

At the third house, a boy of perhaps ten sat on a fence rail, and when he saw them he waved.

That was worse. That was so much worse than not looking up. The wave was cheerful, open, exactly the wave a friendly child gives travelers — and it was identical, Silas realized with a crawling sensation at the base of his skull, to the smile on the baker woman, the smile on the old man, the smile on every face they'd passed. Not similar. Identical. The same curve of lip, the same crinkle at the eye corners, copied from one face to the next like a printer pulling proofs.

"Hallo," the boy called down. His voice was warm and easy. "Are you come for the blessing?"

Silas made himself smile back. He was good at smiles; he'd worn them across a dozen border checkpoints. "What blessing would that be?"

"The king's blessing." The boy hopped down off the fence, landing lightly. Up close, Silas could see that his eyes were fine — clear, brown, alive — and utterly empty behind, like windows of a house where all the furniture had been sold. "It came a fortnight ago. The men in gray brought it. Everyone is happy now." He beamed. Everyone-is-happy-now, delivered with that same smile. "Everyone was sad before. Now nobody is sad. Isn't that good?"

"It sounds good," Silas said. His mouth was dry. "Where are your parents?"

"Mama's making supper. Papa's at the mill." The boy tilted his head, and for one flickering instant — one heartbeat — something moved behind his eyes. Something small and drowning. Then the smile reasserted itself, smooth as water closing over a stone. "Everyone is happy now," he repeated, as if answering a question Silas hadn't asked. As if *he* hadn't asked it. As if the question had come from somewhere deep inside him and the smile had been sent down to smother it.

Wren's hand found Silas's sleeve. Her grip was iron.

"The mill," she breathed. "He said the mill."

* * *

The mill stood at the stream's edge, its wheel turning slow and steady, grinding grain no one had brought it. They approached along the waterline, low, and looked in through the loading door.

There were twenty people inside. Maybe more. They stood in rows, facing a raised platform where sacks of flour had been cleared away, and on the platform stood two figures in gray robes with their hoods down — ordinary faces, tired faces, the faces of clerks or tax collectors. One held a ledger. The other held a small glass vial, and Silas's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world, because he knew vials. He'd bought and sold them for fifteen years. This one was different: it glowed faintly, a pale gold light, and it was attached by a fine silver chain to a circlet of the same metal, which the robed man was lowering onto the head of a farmwife kneeling before him.

She knelt willingly. That was the part Silas couldn't look away from. She knelt with her hands folded and her eyes closed, and when the circlet touched her hair she sighed — a long, relieved sigh, the sigh of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried for years — and when she opened her eyes again, she was smiling. That smile. The same smile, transferred now to a fortieth face.

"What are they doing?" Tam whispered. He'd followed them despite the instruction, because of course he had. Silas didn't have the breath to scold him.

"They're hollowing her," Wren said. Her voice shook. "Like the king. Like what was done to Aldric. But they're doing it to—" she counted, lips moving, "—to everyone."

"Not everyone." Silas watched the next villager step forward, a young man with flour on his apron, kneeling as readily as the woman before him. "Look at the ledger. They're taking names. There's a list."

The robed man with the book read aloud, each name clear in the mill's hollow acoustics. *"Osmund Weaver. Come forward."* And Osmund Weaver came forward and knelt and was crowned with the little circlet and rose smiling, and the ledger man made a mark, and the light in the vial dimmed slightly and then brightened again, drinking.

"They're harvesting," Silas said. The word came out flat and clinical and he was grateful for that, because underneath the flatness was a scream. "Grief, fear, want — all the heavy things. That's what the hollowing takes first. Everything that hurts." He thought of the widow in the Hollow, the grief he'd lifted from her, the peace he'd sold back. He had done this. In miniature, clumsily, for coin — he had done this. "And what's left is quiet. Compliant. Happy, if you're generous with the word."

"Why?" Tam asked. "Why would anyone kneel for that?"

"Because it works," Silas said. "Because they were offered an end to hurting, and they took it. Because that's the oldest bargain there is, and most people make it the moment it's offered." He had made smaller versions of it himself, a hundred times. Every memory he'd ever stolen was a version of it. The knowledge sat in his chest like swallowed stones.

Down in the mill, the vial flared. The robed men conferred briefly, and Silas caught fragments on the wind: *quota*, *the eastern parishes*, *His Majesty requires*. Then the taller one said something that turned Silas's blood to ice water:

"The shrine next. The god's still whispering to four of them. We'll finish after the shrine."

* * *

They retreated to the tree line above the village and crouched among bracken while Silas's mind ran the problem the way it ran locks and ledgers — fast, cold, itemized.

Forty villagers. Two agents. One vial, one circlet, one ledger. The agents were not soldiers; they moved like clerks. But the villagers themselves were the danger, because a village full of smiling, obedient people would do whatever the gray men asked, including raise alarms, block roads, hold strangers. You couldn't fight a town. You couldn't outrun a hundred hands.

"We could go around," Tam said. "We don't have to help them."

Silas looked at him. The boy was watching the mill through the branches, and his knuckles were white on his whittled sword-stick, and his jaw was set in a way that meant he'd already decided to help them and was only offering Silas the exit out of politeness.

Gods save me from children who've decided to be brave, Silas thought, and knew, with weary certainty, that he was going to do the stupid thing.

"We go around," he said, and both of them turned to stare at him. "Tonight. After dark. We take the ledger."

Wren got it first. "The names."

"The names, and the vial if we can manage it. The ledger tells us which villages are next, how many agents, which way they're moving. And—" he hesitated, hating the next part, saying it anyway, "—if there's anything left of these people under the smile, the vial might hold it. What's harvested can sometimes be poured back. I've seen it done badly. I've done it badly. But it can be done."

"You don't know that it works here," Wren said. "This isn't your cheap valley magic. This is crown work. God-touched."

"I know." He met her eyes. "But I'm not leaving forty people wearing that smile because the odds were bad. I spent fifteen years telling myself the memories I took were better off with me. I'm done lying."

Something passed over her face — not softness exactly, more like a door opening a crack — and she nodded once.

They waited out the afternoon in the trees, watching the mill disgorge its smiling congregation back into the streets. The villagers returned to their dropped tools and resumed their interrupted lives with eerie precision: the ox was unharnessed and stabled, the half-cut field was half-cut further, the bread was finished and set to cool. Everything correct. Everything arranged. And everywhere, on every face, the smile — patient, identical, waiting.

At dusk, the gray men walked to the shrine. Four villagers followed them, walking with the strange loose gait of people being steered. From the treeline Silas heard a brief sound — a chime, a word in a language that made his teeth ache — and then a silence with edges on it. When the party came back, there were only three villagers behind the gray men.

The fourth was the old man with the scythe. He came last, alone, walking slowly, and halfway across the square he stopped. He stood there a long moment under the rising moon. And then, very quietly, he began to cry — great silent heaving sobs, doubled over, gripping his scythe like a railing — and Silas understood that the shrine's death had cracked something loose, or that something in the man had been holding on until the last whisper of his god went out, and now there was nothing left to hold on *for*.

The gray men noticed. One of them sighed, the sigh of a clerk faced with paperwork, and walked over, and put the circlet on the old man's head, and the crying stopped, and the smile came, and the old man walked home whistling.

Tam made a small sound. Silas put a hand on the boy's shoulder and found he needed the contact as much as the boy did.

"Now you've seen it," Silas said softly. "Now you know what we're riding toward. Not a corrupt court. Not a stolen throne. An erasure. Whole kingdoms, filed away and smiled over." He looked east, toward the capital, toward the crown that was empty and hungry. "Midwinter isn't about gods anymore. It's about whether anyone is left who can grieve them."

* * *

They went in at the hour when even honest villages sleep, and dishonest ones pretend to.

The ledger was kept in the mill, on the platform, weighted with a river stone. Silas lifted it with a thief's reverence — paper was testimony, testimony was power — and read by the light of a shuttered candle stub while Wren watched the door and Tam watched the window, though what a boy with a stick would do against a grown man Silas didn't ask.

The ledger was worse than he'd feared. It wasn't one village. It was a campaign. Parishes listed in neat columns, dates, quotas, agent assignments — thirty villages within a week's ride, two hundred by midwinter, the capital's surrounding counties scheduled for *completion* before the rite. In the margin of the final page, in a different hand, precise and elegant, someone had written a single line:

*The valley is exempt. Nothing there to harvest.*

The Hollow. His Hollow. Marked exempt — not spared, *exempt*, the way you exempt a plot of ground already salted. Because there was nothing in the valley for them to take. Because he and his kind had stripped it bare years ago, memory by memory, grief by grief, and called it a living.

Silas set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down a thing that might go off.

Then the candle guttered — no wind, no draft — and the light came back gold, and he knew before he turned around.

The tall gray man stood in the doorway of the mill. Behind him, filling the lane, stood the villagers. All of them. Forty smiling faces in the moonlight, arranged in rows as neat as the mill's, and every one of those identical smiles was pointed at Silas like a drawn bow.

"You're the thief," the gray man said. Not unkindly. Almost wearily. "From the Hollow. We wondered when you'd come sniffing." He held up the glowing vial, and inside it the golden light swirled, and Silas felt it pull at him — felt it recognize the stolen weight he carried, all those years of other people's yesterdays packed behind his ribs. "His Majesty has such an appetite for a man like you. All that inventory." The smile the gray man wore was his own, human, tired — and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "You can walk away, thief. The ledger says the valley's exempt. Go home. Forget us."

Behind Silas, Wren's hand closed on his arm. Tam pressed against his side.

And Silas Pemberton, who had survived fifteen years by knowing exactly which fights to walk away from, looked at the forty smiling faces and the tired clerk and the glowing vial, and heard himself say:

"I'm afraid I've recently developed a problem with forgetting."

The vial flared white.

* * *

Later — much later, bleeding, running, the ledger stuffed inside his shirt and the taste of gold light in his mouth — Silas would piece together how they'd lived. Wren's knife, thrown before thinking. The candle overturned into spilled flour. The mill going up in a bloom of white heat while forty smiling villagers stood in the yard and did not scream, did not scatter, did not do anything at all except turn their heads in perfect unison to watch them flee — and that image, more than the fire, more than the blood in his boot, was what Silas carried east at a dead run through the night.

They crossed the ford at dawn and collapsed in a shepherd's bothy on the far side, lungs raw, hearts slamming. Tam was laughing, high and shaky. Wren was swearing, fluently, inventively, in a way that suggested hidden depths Silas resolved never to plumb.

Silas pulled out the ledger with shaking hands and opened it to the last page, hunting the columns, the routes, the *next* name — because if the campaign was real, if the map of the erasure was real, then somewhere in these pages was the pattern, and patterns had centers, and centers could be struck.

Instead he found the marginal note again, and beneath it, fresh ink that had not been there in the mill — ink still wet enough to smear, written in that same precise, elegant hand, as though the ledger wrote itself wherever it was carried:

*The blind oracle at Vael's Crossing holds the eye-stone. Take it from her, thief, or we will. Choose quickly.*

Silas stared at it. Wren leaned over his shoulder and read it, and went very still.

"That's the second relic," she whispered. "The eye-stone. How does the king's own ledger know what we need?"

"It doesn't," Silas said slowly, watching the ink dry. "Someone else wrote this. Someone who knows what we're doing. Someone who wants us to get there first." He looked east, where the road ran down toward a river crossing and a blind woman who dealt in memories of love. "Or someone who wants us exactly where they can see us."

Outside, the sun came up over a world that still, for the moment, knew how to frown.
