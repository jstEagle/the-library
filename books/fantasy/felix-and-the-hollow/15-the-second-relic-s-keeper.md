## 15. The Second Relic's Keeper

The oracle lived at the top of a mountain that Wren was fairly sure hated them.

The path switchbacked up its flank like a scar, narrow enough that Silas walked ahead and she walked behind and neither of them spoke much, because speaking cost breath and breath cost footing, and the drop on her left side went down so far that the river they'd followed for two days looked like a spilled ribbon of tin. The tongue-bone rode in a leather case against Silas's chest. The eye-stone — if it existed, if the drowned god hadn't lied to them, which Wren had stopped taking for granted — waited somewhere above.

"You're limping," Silas said, without turning around.

"I'm walking with character."

"Your ankle's swollen."

"My ankle is *enthusiastic*." She caught up to him on a flat stretch and immediately regretted it; the wind up here had teeth. "How much farther?"

"The goat herder said half a day from the shrine."

"That was yesterday."

"He also said the oracle eats liars," Silas said. "I've decided not to worry about either claim."

Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too thin, and she knew why. It wasn't the climb. It wasn't the cold. It was the thing the goat herder had said next, the thing she'd been turning over in her head all night by the fire while Silas slept with one hand on the relic case like it might grow legs: *the blind woman sees everything but herself, and she takes her fee before she shows you anything.*

*She takes memories,* the goat herder had said. *Not any memory. A memory of love. And she doesn't give it back.*

Silas had gone quiet when Wren told him that morning. Not shocked — Silas didn't do shock, he did stillness, a particular kind of careful blankness that Wren had learned to read as loudly as other people shouted. He'd just looked at her across the dead fire and said, "We can find another way."

"There isn't another way."

"There's always another way. Usually worse, but there's always one."

"And we don't have time for worse." Six weeks until midwinter when Vess's map said the rite would happen. Five now. The eye-stone was the only thing that could show them what the rite actually looked like, where the impostor would stand, what the unbinding required beyond three relics nobody had ever assembled in living memory. "Besides," she'd added, lighter than she felt, "it's not like I'm rich in memories of love. What's she going to take? My fondness for stew?"

He hadn't laughed. That was the thing about Silas. Other people would have laughed, because laughing was easy and kind and cost nothing. Silas just looked at her with those gray eyes that held everything behind glass, and said, "Wren," in a voice she'd only heard him use twice — once over Tam's empty bedroll, once in Casterhale when the fires started — and then he'd shouldered his pack and started climbing.

*

They reached the cave mouth at dusk.

It wasn't marked. There were no shrines, no offerings, none of the crude altars they'd passed in the lowland villages where minor gods traded miracles for teeth and years and names. Just a cleft in the rock above the treeline, breathing out air that smelled of stone and something older underneath — not incense, not rot. Rain that hadn't fallen yet. Wren stood at the threshold and felt the hair rise on her arms, and beside her Silas went very still in the way he did when magic was near, because outside the Hollow magic couldn't touch him but it could certainly *notice* him, and being noticed by things like this had nearly killed them twice already.

"She'll know we're here," Wren said.

"She knew we were coming before we were born, probably." But he stepped in first anyway. Of course he did. Wren had stopped keeping count of how many times he put himself between her and a doorway.

Inside, the cave opened into a round chamber lit by nothing — no torches, no fire, and yet she could see, the way you see in dreams, every surface soft-edged and clear. The walls were covered in writing. Thousands of lines of it, spiraling inward, in no alphabet Wren recognized, and as she looked closer she realized with a lurch that some of the lines weren't carved at all. They were *moving*. Slow rivers of script crawling over stone like water finding its level.

In the center sat a woman on a chair made of antlers.

She was old in a way that had nothing to do with years. Her skin was pale as paper and mapped with fine lines, and her eyes — Wren looked, and then wished she hadn't — her eyes were closed and had been closed so long the lids had gone smooth as river stones. No lashes. No scars where eyes used to be. Just smoothness, as if she'd been poured into this shape rather than born.

"Two thieves," the oracle said. Her voice was dry and pleasant, like pages turning. "One who steals. One who was stolen from."

Wren's stomach dropped. Beside her, Silas shifted his weight — toward her, always toward her — and said, "We've come for—"

"I know what you've come for." The oracle's head turned toward him with terrible precision. "You carry a god's tongue in a box and a god's debt in your chest, thief, and both are louder than you think. Sit."

There were no other chairs. They sat on the floor, cross-legged, like children at a story. Wren hated how natural it felt.

"The eye-stone," Silas said. "You have it."

"I keep it. Keeping and having are different trades." The oracle smiled, and it was almost warm. "You want to see the rite. You want to see the hollow king and the crown and the names going into the dark. The stone will show you. The stone shows true — that is its nature and its curse, and mine, once upon a time." One pale hand rose, palm up. "My fee is known to you."

"A memory of love," Wren said.

The smooth lids turned toward her. For a long moment the oracle said nothing, and the moving script on the walls slowed, as if the whole chamber were listening.

"Not any memory of love," the oracle said softly. "A true one. Felt whole, from both sides or from one side fully given. The stone knows the difference. It has refused payment before." Her head tilted. "It has eaten liars before, too. Your goat herder undersold me."

"We know," Silas said. His voice was flat and careful. "And we're not paying."

Wren turned to look at him. He was staring at the oracle with an expression she recognized — the one he wore when he'd already decided something and was building the wall between himself and anyone who might argue.

"We're not—" she started.

"No." He said it fast, final, a door shutting. "No. Find another way to pay. Take something else from me. I have thousands of stolen memories in here—" he tapped his temple, and Wren saw his hand shake slightly, and pretended she didn't, "—take ten, take fifty, take the lot, they're not even mine, most of them—"

"They must be yours," the oracle said gently. "Given freely. That is the shape of the price. Love that is given, not taken. You of all people must understand the difference, thief. Your whole life has been the difference."

"Then take my memory of her." He leaned forward. "Of Wren. Every one. The day she came to my door, the road, the shrine of teeth, all of it — I'm giving them freely, right now, take them—"

"Those are not memories of love," the oracle said. "Not yet. Perhaps not ever, if you keep holding them behind glass." Something moved through her voice that might have been pity. "And they are not hers to give."

"It's my memory," Wren said quietly.

Both of them turned. Silas's face had gone the color of the paper-pale walls.

"Wren. No."

"It's my memory, Silas." Her heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears, but her voice came out steady, and she was grateful for that, grateful for the one small mercy of sounding braver than she was. "Which one do you want?" she asked the oracle. "Tell me what counts. Tell me what the stone will take."

The oracle was silent again. Then, softly: "Show me."

And Wren understood that this was the real gate. Not the climb, not the cave. This. She closed her eyes.

She reached for the memory the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue — carefully, afraid of startling it away. It wasn't hard to find. It was the brightest thing she owned.

The night after the Shrine of Teeth. After the raid, after the guardian's curse had crawled up Silas's arm and he'd hidden it under his sleeve for two days like a coward, after Tam was back and asleep and snoring with his whole body the way only Tam could snore. The fire had burned down to coals. Wren had been on watch, and she'd looked across the embers and seen Silas asleep sitting up, chin on chest, one hand still loosely curled around the hilt of a knife he never needed to use anymore because she was watching — and his sleeve had slipped, and the curse-marks showed, black branches growing up from his wrist, and he'd looked so *tired*, so human, this man she'd spent three years hating and four months unable to hate, and she'd gotten up and crossed the camp and pulled his sleeve down over the marks and tucked the blanket — his own blanket, she'd stolen it off him to give it back to him, which was such a *them* thing to do — and he'd stirred and half-woken and said her name. Just her name. Like it was an answer to a question someone had asked him.

And she'd said, "Go back to sleep, thief," and he'd smiled in his sleep, actually smiled, and she'd sat back down on watch and realized her hands were shaking and realized she was happy. In the middle of everything — the hunt, the burning city, the end of the world on a midwinter schedule — she was happy, sitting by a dying fire, watching a man sleep.

That was the memory. That was the one.

When she opened her eyes, the oracle was crying. Two clean lines running down the paper face, into the seams of her mouth.

"Oh," the oracle whispered. "*Oh.* Yes. That one. That is a true one."

"Don't take it." Silas's voice cracked straight down the middle. Wren had never heard his voice crack. Three years, four months, a burning city, a cursed arm, and she'd never once heard it, and hearing it now was worse than any wound. "Wren, listen to me. Listen. You spent your childhood with a hole in it because of me. You got pieces back and they don't fit and you *still* don't know who you were before me, and you want to hand me the one good thing that happened after? Hand it to *me*? I'm the reason you don't have more of them—"

"You're not listening." She kept her voice level. It was the hardest thing she'd ever done, harder than the tower, harder than the burning rooftops in Casterhale. "You're doing the thing you do. The glass thing. Where you decide what everyone's allowed to feel so nobody gets hurt except you."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what you do. And I'm done letting you pay my debts." She turned to the oracle. "Take it. Take that one. The fire, the blanket, the marks on his arm, all of it. Freely given."

"*Wren.*"

"Silas." She looked at him, and let him see all of it, everything she'd been carrying since the shrine, since maybe since the day she'd knocked on his door with nothing but anger and a name. "If we fail, there's no future. If we succeed, there's a future. Either way, I need you to stop trying to be the only one who bleeds. That's not love. That's just theft with better manners."

The words landed. She watched them land — watched them go through the glass, all the way through, for the first time.

The oracle raised her hand. "Freely given?" she asked Wren. "Say it."

"Freely given."

"Knowing it cannot be returned?"

"Knowing."

"Then hold it one last time," the oracle said, and her voice was almost tender now, "and let it go."

Wren held it. She let the whole memory rise up bright — the coals, the snoring, the black branches on his wrist, her name in his mouth, the shaking happy hands — she held it the way you hold a child, the way you hold a door against wind, and then she opened her fingers.

It left without pain. That was the cruelest part. It left like water through a grate, smooth and total, and afterward there was a shape where it had been — she knew a memory had lived there, she could feel the outline of it the way you feel a tooth that's gone — but the contents were gone. Fire, blanket, name. Gone. She knew she had loved something in that spot. She didn't know what.

She was crying. She noted it distantly, from very far away, the way you note weather.

"Is it done?" Silas asked. His voice was wrecked. She couldn't look at him yet. Looking at him would make it real in a way that broke something structural.

"It is done," the oracle said. "And the stone accepts." She turned her smooth face upward, toward the ceiling of the cave, and spoke a word that Wren's mind slid off of like rain off oilcloth.

The walls answered.

Every line of moving script on every surface began to flow toward the center of the chamber, streaming off the stone and into the air, gathering, braiding, condensing — until above the oracle's antlered chair hung a sphere the size of a fist, made of what looked like smoke and starlight and the inside of an eyelid. It had no pupil. It didn't need one. Wren felt it look at her, all of her, every room she'd ever kept locked.

"The eye-stone," the oracle said. "It will show you the rite. All of it. What the impostor does, and what undoes him, and what stands where you cannot stand." The sphere drifted down and settled into Silas's cupped hands, light as ash, and Silas stared at it like it might bite. "But hear the warning that comes free, thief, because you paid nothing and free things are how I am cruel."

Silas looked up.

"The stone shows true," the oracle said. "True things are not the same as survivable things. When you see what the crown was made from — when you see whose memory sits at the bottom of it — one of you will wish you had never climbed this mountain." Her smooth lids turned, slowly, and settled on Wren. "And one of you will already know."

Wren's mouth went dry. "Know what?"

The oracle smiled, sad as a closed book. "Ask the stone. It's the only thing left in this world that answers honestly." She folded her hands. "Now go. Midwinter doesn't wait, and neither do gods. And girl—" as they rose, as Silas helped Wren up with a touch so gentle it hurt worse than the loss, "—the memory isn't destroyed. Nothing truly loved is ever destroyed. It's only waiting somewhere you can't follow. Yet."

They walked out into the dark under a sky full of indifferent stars, and Wren felt the hole in her chest where the fire and the blanket and her name in his voice used to be, and beside her Silas held the eye-stone like a heart he'd been handed, and neither of them spoke until the cave was far behind.

Then Silas stopped on the path, in the wind, and said, "I'm going to give it back to you. Whatever it costs. I'm going to find a way to give it back."

And Wren, who had just learned exactly what these things cost, took his hand — the cursed one, the marked one — and said, "First let's see what it wants to show us," because the eye-stone in his palm had begun, very softly, to glow.
