## 16. Visions Paid For

The eye-stone was the size of a plum and warm as a sleeping bird.

Silas held it in his palm and did not look into it. That was the first rule of relics — you didn't touch them to your skin, you didn't speak near them, and above all you didn't *look*, not until you'd decided what looking would cost. He'd spent two days in the oracle's hut learning nothing except that everything had a price, and he was tired of paying before he knew what he was buying.

Wren sat across the cold hearth from him, knees drawn up, watching the stone with the flat hunger of someone who had already given it her share. She'd handed over her one good memory of him — the night he'd pulled her out of a flooded culvert when she was nine, though she hadn't known it was him until this week — and she hadn't cried about it. She'd just nodded, like a woman settling a debt at market, and walked outside to be sick in the snow.

Now she came back in, stamping frost off her boots, and said, "Well? Are we going to stare at it all winter or are we going to use it?"

"It shows what it wants," Silas said. "That's the trouble with eyes. Even stone ones."

"The oracle said—"

"The oracle said a lot of things." He turned the stone over. Somewhere inside it, deep down, something shifted like a pupil dilating. "The oracle also charged a memory of love for a look at a rock, so forgive me if I'm slow to trust her bookkeeping."

"You paid too."

"I paid less." He had given it the memory of the drowned god's riverbank, which had cost him almost nothing because the god had already hollowed most of it out of him. The oracle had frowned at that, as if he'd tried to pass a clipped coin. "And I'm the one holding it, which means if it takes something, it takes from me. So we do this my way."

"Which is?"

He looked around the hut. It was a poor place even by the standards of the blind villages they'd been passing through — one room, one hearth, walls hung with dried herbs that had gone gray with age, and everywhere, on every surface, small carved eyes staring out of wood and bone and river clay. The oracle's work, presumably. Or her worship. With gods back in the world, it was getting harder to tell craft from devotion.

"Alone," he said. "You wait outside."

"No."

"Wren—"

"You're going to see something terrible," she said, "and then you're going to decide, alone, in your own head, what part of it I'm allowed to know. That's what you always do. That's the whole shape of you." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; she'd learned argument from watching him, and she used his own weapons better than he did. "So either I sit here and watch it with you, or I stand outside and imagine worse. Pick."

Silas weighed it. He was good at weighing things — it was most of what thieving was, the arithmetic of risk — and every sum came out the same. If he sent her away she'd press her ear to the door, and if he barred the door she'd find another way in, and if he tied her to a post she'd talk her way loose before he finished the knot. There were people you could lock out. Wren had stopped being one of them somewhere on the road, and he couldn't name the mile-marker, which bothered him more than he let show.

"Sit there," he said. "Don't touch me while it's working. And if I start talking to someone who isn't in the room, you hit me. Hard."

"With what?"

"Anything. You're resourceful."

Almost, almost, she smiled. Then she sat, and folded her hands, and waited, and Silas thought: *this is what it would have been like, if I'd never sold her childhood.* A girl who trusted him enough to be afraid in front of him. He put the thought away where he kept the other expensive ones, and pressed the eye-stone against his brow.

* * *

The world went out like a snuffed candle.

Then it came back wrong — vast, and slow, and seen from everywhere at once. He was standing in a chamber he had never entered, high in a palace he had never visited, and he knew both intimately, because the eye-stone did not show him pictures. It showed him *knowing*. For a few endless seconds he simply possessed the room the way he possessed his own hands: the vaulted dark overhead ribbed with black iron, the floor of white stone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling, the seven tall windows through which midwinter light should have fallen and did not, because someone had bricked them up from the outside and left only hairline seams of gray.

And in the center of the floor, a crown.

Not on anyone's head. Resting on a cushion of gray silk, alone, the way you'd set down something dangerous while you went to fetch the rest of the tools. It was Aldric's crown — Silas recognized it from the vial, from the memory of the knight, gold worked into the shapes of oak leaves with acorns at the points — but it had been changed. Someone had cut channels into the metal, fine as veins, and the channels were full of names.

He could read them. That was the horror of it. The eye-stone gave him the language the way it gave him the room, poured it straight into his understanding, and so he stood in a stolen vision reading the true names of gods scratched into a king's crown in letters no living scribe should have been able to write. There were hundreds. There were thousands. Small gods of wells and thresholds and the first thaw, river mothers, hearth-fathers, the green man of orchards, the lady of bees — every minor divinity whose shrine had flickered back to life these past months, whose crude miracles had been bought in blood and memory in a hundred hungry villages. All of them, gathered. All of them, *held*.

And the channels were not full yet.

The vision tilted, and now he saw the rite itself, laid out like a diagram drawn on the inside of his skull. Midwinter night. The longest dark. Seven bells rung backward at the hour of deepest silence, each bell tuned to a note that unmade rather than made — he understood suddenly why the capital's bells had been recast, why travelers spoke of the city's new ringing with unease they couldn't articulate. The bells would open the way. The crown would drink the names. And whatever wore Aldric's face would stand at the center of the web and pull every thread tight, and the old gods would not die, because gods don't die — they would be *unmade*, unwritten, returned to the silence they'd slept in for three hundred years, and this time there would be no shrines waiting, no prayers queued up like beggars at a gate. This time the forgetting would be permanent.

The impostor would not rule a kingdom. It would rule a world with nothing left in it but people. Hollow, obedient, godless people.

Silas tried to look for the figure at the center of the vision and the vision refused him. Every time his attention slid toward the dais, toward the throne-shaped shadow behind the crown, the stone flinched — he felt it flinch, a muscular little contraction against his brow, like an eye blinking away smoke. It would show him the trap but not the spider. Perhaps it was afraid. Perhaps it had been *taught* to be afraid.

But the stone showed him other things. It showed him the crypt beneath the chamber, and the third relic sleeping there — a weight, a warmth, a slow red pulse in the dark like a heart beating under flagstones, and he understood with sick certainty that it was exactly that, and exactly where. It showed him the guards' rotation, the numberless corridors, the one stair that ran from the rite chamber down into the dark. It showed him dates. It showed him the moon's face on midwinter night, thin as a paring, and the hour the bells would begin.

Six weeks. Forty-one days. He counted them without wanting to, the way the stone counted them for him, and the number landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

A thousand miles. Six weeks. Three relics, one of them under the impostor's own floor.

The vision began to close, the chamber folding up around its secrets like a hand, and at the last instant — whether the stone relented or the thing in the palace felt him looking, he never learned — he saw the crown again, close, the names crawling in their channels like insects waking. And one channel, near the bottom, was empty. Waiting. Sized for a single name.

He had time to think: *whose?*

Then the hut slammed back into being around him, firelight and herb-smell and Wren's face inches from his own, her hand still raised from the second blow.

"I said hit me," he croaked. "You didn't have to enjoy it."

"You were talking to the wall. In a voice that wasn't yours." She lowered the fire-poker. "What did you see?"

* * *

He told her. All of it — that was the decision he made sitting there with his skull ringing, the decision that mattered more than any he'd made since the valley: he told her all of it, in order, without editing. The chamber. The crown. The names. The empty channel. The heart under the flagstones. The forty-one days.

She listened the way she did everything now, completely, and when he finished she was quiet for a long moment, feeding a stick to the fire.

"The empty channel," she said finally. "Whose name goes there?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He rolled the eye-stone between his fingers. It had gone cool now, spent, an ordinary rock wearing the memory of seeing. "It's saving space. Last name in, the keystone. Whatever it is, it matters more than all the others combined."

"A big god. One of the old ones."

"Maybe." But the stone had shown him sizes, and the empty channel was small. Small as a coin. Small as a name whispered once, by a child, in a room where a thief was teaching it to pick pockets. He kept that arithmetic to himself. Some sums you don't share until you have to.

"So we go," Wren said. "Tonight. Now. Forty-one days, a thousand miles — that's twenty-five miles a day, every day, no rest, no storms, no broken wheels." She was already standing, already moving around the hut, gathering the few things they owned with the brisk violence of someone who needed her hands busy. "We can't do twenty-five a day. Not with the roads the way they are. Not with checkpoints."

"No."

"Then we go faster than roads." She stopped. Looked at him. "There's a way, isn't there. You've got that look. The look you get when you've already stolen the answer and you're deciding how much to tell me."

He did have a way. He hated it. Two hundred miles south, past the burned farms and the hollowed village, the River Vane bent east toward the sea, and rivers were roads now — had been, ever since the gods woke, because the drowned god of the Vane owed him a debt and debts were the only currency that moved faster than fear. A river would carry them to within a week's walk of the capital. A river would also carry them through water where a god remembered his face, remembered his taste, remembered the memory he'd sold and might come collecting interest.

And there was the other problem, the one he'd been circling since the vision closed. "The stone showed me the crypt," he said. "Guards, stairs, the relic. It wanted us to know. Relics don't want things, Wren. Something in that palace let us see."

"Or the oracle sent us with a key and the lock showed us itself."

"Or." He stood, joints aching, forty-one days old in every one of them. "Either way, the impostor knows someone's coming. Maybe not who. Maybe not when. But the clock it's running isn't just midwinter. It's us. We're on the board now."

"Good," Wren said.

He stared at her. "Good?"

"It's been hunting us in the dark since Casterhale. Burning towns. Hollowing villages. Taking Tam." Her jaw set, and for a second he saw the woman she'd have become if he'd never touched her childhood — hard, certain, dangerous in the clean way he'd never managed. "If it knows we're coming, then it has to stop pretending. It has to turn around and face us. And I want that." She slung her pack over her shoulder. "I want it looking at us instead of at the crown. Let it spend its nights worrying about a thief and an orphan girl instead of finishing its work. Every hour it spends hating us is an hour it doesn't spend ringing bells."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the blind village lay quiet under early snow, shutters closed, everyone asleep except the ones who prayed. Somewhere south, a thousand miles of frozen road and hungry water and god-touched soldiers stood between them and a bricked-up chamber where a crown full of names sat waiting for its last one.

"We leave before dawn," he said. "South road to the ford, then the river. And Wren—" he caught her at the door, and the words came out before he could weigh them, which was happening to him more and more lately and which he distrusted completely, "—what the stone took from you. The culvert night. I can't give it back. But when this is done, if there's anything left of me that remembers it, I'll tell you. Every detail. You'll have it twice."

She considered him in the doorway, snow-light on her face.

"Better plan," she said. "When this is done, we make a new one. Those are worth more anyway. They come with the person attached."

Then she was out into the dark, and Silas stood alone in the hut of carved eyes, and every one of them seemed to be watching him leave. He picked up the eye-stone, weighed it once in his palm — spent, harmless, ordinary — and set it gently back on the oracle's shelf, facing the wall.

Forty-one days. One empty channel in a crown of stolen names, sized for a single word.

He was starting to suspect he already knew whose.
