## 17. Riding With Pilgrims

### Silas

The caravan found them on the third day out of the ash checkpoint's shadow, or perhaps they found the caravan — it was hard to say who was following whom, on a road where everyone moved in the same direction for the same reason.

Midwinter blessing. The king's blessing. Every year the crown opened its gates and let the faithful pour in, ten thousand pilgrims shuffling toward the capital like iron filings toward a lodestone. Silas had spent his whole life avoiding crowds like that. Crowds had eyes, and eyes had memories, and memories were his trade, which meant crowds were full of people who could recognize him.

But a thousand miles was a thousand miles, and Vess knew his face now.

"We join them," he said, before Wren could argue. "We *are* them. Two more pilgrims going to kiss the king's ring."

"You hate the king."

"I hate everyone. That's what makes me convincing."

The caravan master was a broad woman named Odile with a wagon bed full of candle-tallow and a voice that carried over oxen. She looked Silas up and down with the professional assessment of someone who'd been robbed before and intended to know exactly how much this time.

"Pilgrims pay in labor," she said. "You push when we're stuck, you stand watch at night, you don't preach unless your preaching is short. What's your blessing-sickness?"

Silas blinked. "My what?"

"Everyone walking to the capital is sick with something the road can't cure." She jerked her chin at Wren. "Her?"

Wren, to her credit, didn't miss a beat. "She wants to see her mother's grave. In the capital."

"And you?" Odile said to Silas.

He considered lying, then considered that lies were just memories he hadn't been given yet. "I'm looking for something I lost," he said. "I don't remember what it looks like."

Odile snorted. "Half my caravan, then. Get in the back of the tallow wagon. And if either of you is Inquisition—"

"If we were," Silas said, climbing up, "we'd have better boots."

### Wren

The caravan was a village that moved.

Wren learned its shape over the first two days: Odile's tallow wagon at the head, then the Hendersons' cart of dried apples, then a family of bell-founders from Casterhale who flinched every time they passed a shrine — which, since the gods had woken hungry, was often. There was a one-eyed mason who whittled saints out of fence posts and gave them away to children. There was an old man called Brother Anselm who wasn't a brother of anything anymore but still blessed people at dusk, quietly, apologetically, as though apologizing to the gods for bothering them.

And there was a boy named Tam.

He appeared beside Wren on the second morning, walking backward so he could face her, which should have ended in a ditch and somehow never did.

"You're the ones from the checkpoint," he said. "The fire. The one where nobody surrendered their memories."

"Nobody remembers that," Wren said carefully.

"I do." He grinned. He was maybe eleven, all elbows and certainty, with a burn scar on one forearm he clearly wore like a medal. "I remember everything. My ma says remembering's a sin now, but she also says that about dancing, so."

Wren laughed before she could stop herself. It came out rusty, like a hinge in a house nobody lived in.

Tam's grin widened at the sound like he'd won something. "What's your name?"

"Wren."

"Wren what?"

"Just Wren."

"Just Wren," he repeated solemnly, testing it. Then he pointed across the column of walkers, to where Silas sat on the tail of the tallow wagon pretending to sleep. "And him? He watches the road like something's coming up it."

"Everyone watches the road these days."

"Not like him." Tam lowered his voice with the gravity of a boy sharing state secrets. "He counted the shrines. All of them. We passed four and he counted four, and he looked at each one like he was deciding whether to apologize or spit."

Wren glanced at Silas. He had, in fact, counted the shrines. She hadn't known that until Tam said it, and now she couldn't unknow it, and she wondered — not for the first time, but with new weight — how much of Silas Pemberton was visible to anyone patient enough to look.

"He's just tired," she said.

Tam looked at her with eyes too old for his face. "So's everybody. That's why we're all walking."

* * *

### Silas

On the fourth night, the caravan made camp in the bowl of a dead volcano, and the sky did something Silas hadn't seen since he was small enough to believe in it.

The stars moved.

Not drifting — *arranging*. Slowly, in patterns no astronomer would bless, constellations shifted like sleeping men turning over. Somewhere north, faint as a struck match, a light kindled that might have been a god stretching after a long century of silence.

Around the fires, the pilgrims went quiet. Not afraid, exactly. Something worse than afraid: hopeful. Silas had stolen hope before. It was the most dangerous thing in any mind — heavier than grief, sharper than love, and it burned through everything around it looking for fuel.

"They're watching us," whispered the apple-widow Henderson, hands clasped, tears on her cheeks.

"They're always watching," said the old mason, not looking up from his whittling. "Question's whether they like what they see."

Silas kept his back to the sky. He'd learned long ago that wonder was just the front door fear used when it wanted to be invited in.

Except.

Except that Wren was sitting beside him, and she was looking up, and her face in the firelight was open in a way he'd only ever seen once before — years ago, in another life, in a memory he'd taken from her and sold and never gotten back. She looked like a girl being given something.

"The gods are real," she said softly. Not to him. To the sky. "They're actually real, and they're *awake*, and I don't know if that's the best news I've ever heard or the worst."

"Yes," Silas said.

She turned. "Yes to which?"

"Yes."

That got a breath of a laugh out of her. "You're impossible."

"I'm consistent. There's a difference."

She hugged her knees, chin on top, firelight catching the hollows under her eyes. Six weeks of road behind them already, and the eye-stone's vision sitting between them like a stone neither would move: midwinter, the crown, every god's name bound into silence forever.

"Silas." Her voice dropped. "When this is done. If it's done. If we win — what happens to you? You'll have given up—" She stopped herself. She knew better than anyone what he'd already paid. "You don't have anything left to trade except—"

"Don't." 

"—except yourself."

"I said don't."

She held his gaze, unflinching, and he hated how well she'd learned to do that. From him. He'd taught her that without meaning to, the way you teach someone everything by taking things from them.

"Then tell me what I'm supposed to do instead," she said. "Watch? Hope? Pray?" A bitter edge crept in. "Praying works now, remember. Terrible prices, wonderful miracles. Maybe I'll pray you into being brave."

"I'm brave enough."

"No," Wren said, gently, terribly. "You're *surviving* enough. Those aren't the same thing. I know, because I learned the difference from you too."

She got up and went to help Odile mend a harness strap, leaving Silas alone with the moving stars and the terrible arithmetic of what she'd said.

Surviving enough. He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

Across the fire, the boy Tam was showing the mason's youngest how to fold a prayer out of grass, and failing, and laughing about it, and the child was laughing too, and Silas caught himself watching longer than was safe.

He looked away. He looked back.

Damn it.

* * *

### Wren

The rain came on the sixth day, cold and sideways, and the caravan bunched together like sheep, and that was when the found family started — because misery shared on a road is a faster glue than any vow.

Wren found herself under the apple cart's canvas with Widow Henderson and two of the bell-founder daughters, passing around a skin of cider that was mostly vinegar and mostly wonderful. Henderson talked about her son, dead three winters, whose memory the checkpoint priests had tried to take at the border of some province Wren had never heard of.

"They got most of it," the widow said, matter-of-fact, wiping rain off her chin. "His face, his voice, his wedding day. But they couldn't get the smell of him. Isn't that strange? They took everything else and left me the smell of his hair when he was small. Like the gods left me one crumb on purpose." She smiled, wet-eyed. "Or like the priests are just bad at their work."

"One crumb's more than some of us got," Wren said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Henderson's eyes sharpened with the particular kindness of women who've buried things and can spot grief at forty paces.

"What did they take from you, girl?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "Nothing I miss."

Henderson patted her knee and let it lie, which was its own mercy. Under the canvas, in the vinegar-smelling dark, with strangers pressed warm against both sides, Wren felt something she hadn't felt since before she'd walked into a thief's shop demanding answers about a childhood she couldn't remember.

She felt *held*. Not by anyone in particular. By the collective fact of them — twelve wagons, sixty souls, all walking the same direction into the same storm toward the same uncertain city, none of them sure the king was worth the walk anymore, all of them walking anyway.

Faith, she thought. This is what it actually is. Not the shrines. Not the light. Just this — people choosing to keep going together when stopping would be smarter.

She thought of Silas alone on watch, and decided, with the quiet ferocity that was becoming her signature, that by midwinter he was going to learn this feeling if she had to drag him into it bodily.

* * *

### Silas

It was Tam who broke him, in the end. Of course it was.

The boy materialized at Silas's elbow on the eighth night while Silas stood the late watch, and didn't ask permission, just planted himself against the wagon wheel and stared at the dark road with the fierce vigilance of a soldier who has never seen battle.

"You count things," Tam said.

"Go to sleep."

"I count things too. Steps, mostly. Stars, sometimes. Wagons." He held up a hand, fingers splayed. "Sixty-one people in this caravan. Fifty-eight if you don't count the ones who'll turn back at the Sorrow Ford."

Silas looked down at him. "How do you know about the Sorrow Ford?"

"Odile said. The river rises in winter and half the pilgrims lose their nerve." Tam shrugged. "I'm not going to turn back. Are you?"

"That depends on a lot of things you shouldn't worry about."

"My da worried about things," Tam said, to the road. "He worried himself into a box in the ground. Ma says worrying's just praying for bad stuff." He glanced up, suddenly sharp. "You pray?"

"No."

"Liar. Everybody prays now. The gods answer." He said it simply, without the terror the words deserved, and Silas felt the old chill walk up his spine. Children spoke of answered prayers the way they spoke of weather. Nobody had told them yet that weather killed.

"I don't pray," Silas said, "because I've met the things that answer."

Tam considered this with enormous seriousness. Then: "Is that why you watch the road? Waiting for one to come collect you?"

The question landed somewhere beneath Silas's ribs and stayed there.

"Something like that," he managed.

"Well." Tam nodded, satisfied, as though Silas had passed a test whose terms he'd never understood. "When it comes, wake me first. I want to see it." He stood, brushed off his knees, and paused. "You'd fight it, though. Wouldn't you? For the caravan. You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look the heroes have in stories right before they say they're not heroes." Tam grinned, gap-toothed, absolutely certain. "Goodnight, Silas."

He ran back to his mother's wagon, and Silas stood alone in the cold with the stars rearranging overhead and the word *heroes* rattling around his skull like a coin in an empty purse.

Behind him, soft footsteps — Wren, wrapped in a blanket, taking the last hour of watch because she'd stopped asking and started simply appearing.

"Trouble?" she asked.

"A boy," Silas said, "has decided I'm a story."

Wren followed his gaze to the small sleeping shape in the wagon bed. Something crossed her face — tenderness, and warning, in equal measure.

"Then be careful," she said, "what kind of story you turn out to be."

Far up the dark road, past the sleeping fires, past the sentries, where the night lay blackest — a light flickered. Once. Twice. Like something very far away opening an eye.

Silas counted the seconds between flashes, the way he counted everything now.

Three. Then nothing.

He didn't wake anyone. But he didn't sit down again, either, and he watched the road until dawn.
