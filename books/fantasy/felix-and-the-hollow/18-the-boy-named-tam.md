## 18. The Boy Named Tam

The boy had been following Silas for two days before he worked up the nerve to speak to him.

Silas knew, of course. He always knew. It was a professional habit — you didn't survive in his line of work without knowing who was behind you and how far back they were standing. The boy kept a careful distance, maybe twenty paces, close enough to feel part of the caravan and far enough to pretend he wasn't watching. He was maybe ten years old, all elbows and knees, with a cowlick that refused every attempt at order and a pair of boots two sizes too big that he'd stuffed with straw at the toes. Silas could hear them squeaking when he walked.

He'd been told the boy's name on the first night, by the boy's mother, who had also told him her name, her husband's name, the name of their farm outside Millbrook, and the name of the pig they'd sold to fund the pilgrimage. Her name was Sera. She talked the way some people bled — constantly, and with no way to stop it.

"That's Tam," she'd said, gripping Silas's arm with flour-rough hands. "He won't stop talking about you. You gave him water on the first day, when the well at Harrow's Cross was dry."

"I handed him a canteen," Silas said. "It was full."

"You *gave* him water," Sera repeated, with the gravity of a woman describing a miracle. "Nobody gives anything on this road."

Silas had extracted himself from the conversation as politely as he could, which was not very, and spent the rest of the evening trying to remember why he'd bothered filling the canteen in the first place. He hadn't. Wren had filled it. He'd just been the one standing there when the boy looked thirsty, and handing it over had required less effort than explaining why not.

That was the trouble with kindness done by accident. It couldn't be taken back.

* * *

On the second day, the boy finally closed the distance.

They were walking through a stretch of country where the road ran along a dried riverbed, and the pilgrims had spread out into a long straggling line, everyone too tired to talk. Silas had drifted toward the rear out of habit — thieves watched exits, even on a road with no doors — and the boy materialized beside him with the suddenness of a cat deciding it owned your lap.

"You're him," the boy said.

Silas looked down. "I'm someone. That's usually enough."

"You're the one from the story." Tam fell into step beside him, boots squeaking, chin tipped up. "The thief who stole from the gods and got away with it."

"I've never stolen from a god in my life."

"My da says you did. He says you're the one who took the tongue-bone out of the bell tower at Saint Verren's, and the priest there went mad because his god stopped talking, and now the god talks through *you* instead." The boy said this with tremendous satisfaction, as though the theology were airtight. "Is it true? Can you hear him?"

"Your da," Silas said, "sounds like a man who enjoys a story more than the truth."

"He says the same about you."

Silas exhaled through his nose. Somewhere ahead, Wren glanced back over her shoulder, saw who he was walking with, and smiled in a way he chose to interpret as sympathy rather than amusement. She'd been doing a lot of that lately. It was insufferable.

"The bell tower," Silas said carefully, "was empty when we got there. The priest had already gone mad. We took nothing but a bad headache and some bruises."

"But you *went* in." Tam's eyes had gone wide and lit from within, the way only children's eyes can go when they've decided something is wonderful. "Nobody goes in. Old Marta says the tower eats people. She says the bells ring by themselves and if you count the rings wrong you die."

"Old Marta also told me my left eye was cursed. I've checked. It's fine."

"She said that *after* you came out of the tower alive," Tam pointed out. "Which proves she was right about something."

Silas opened his mouth. Closed it. There was no winning an argument against a child who treated logic as a suggestion rather than a rule. He settled for silence, which the boy apparently took as agreement, because he beamed and skipped ahead a few steps, then back, orbiting Silas like a small determined moon.

"What else did you do?" he demanded. "In Casterhale. They say the whole city burned."

"The city burned itself. I was elsewhere."

"They say you carried a girl out of the flames over your shoulder."

"She walked," Silas said. "Under her own power. I was carrying the *bread*."

Wren, ten paces up, made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been laughter strangled at birth.

* * *

That evening the caravan camped in the lee of a ruined waystation, and Silas discovered that proximity to children was contagious, because Tam simply did not leave. He sat near Silas at the fire. He asked questions. He asked so many questions that Silas began to suspect the boy had a list somewhere, written down, and was working through it systematically.

Why did you become a thief. (Hunger, mostly, then habit.) Was it true he'd once stolen a memory from a lord. (Once or twice. Lords have the best memories; they never use them.) Did he know sword-fighting. (No.) Could he teach him anyway. (No.) What was inside the vial. (Nothing. A trinket.) Why did Wren look at him like that sometimes. (Like what? Like *that*, the boy said, gesturing at the entire concept of looking.)

By the time the fires burned low, Silas had a headache and an unwelcome realization: he was answering. Not deflecting, not lying — well, lying, but *engaging* with the lies, elaborating on them, giving them texture. He was building a version of himself for this boy, the way you'd build a set piece for a con. And the horrible thing, the genuinely horrifying thing, was that the version was better than the original. Braver. Kinder. A man who gave away canteens on purpose.

"Tell me the one about the drowned god," Tam said, hugging his knees. The firelight made his face flicker. "Marta says you bargained with something under the river and it took your shadow."

"It took a memory," Silas said, before he could stop himself. Then, cursing inwardly: "Allegedly. If such things existed. Which they don't."

"What did it take?"

"A face," Silas said quietly.

Tam waited for more. Silas didn't provide it. Across the fire, Wren had gone very still, watching him, and he found he couldn't look at her either.

"A face?" the boy prompted.

"Somebody's face. From a long time ago." Silas fed a stick into the flames. "I know I loved them. I know they mattered. But when I try to picture them, it's like reaching into smoke. I know the shape of the hole where they used to be."

Tam considered this with the terrible seriousness of childhood. "That's sad."

"Yes."

"But you still know you loved them." The boy shrugged, matter-of-fact. "So they're not really gone. You just can't see them anymore. Like a house behind a hill."

Silas stared at him.

It was the kind of thing adults said to each other after wine, after funerals, after long silences — the kind of comfort that cost something to give. This boy had produced it in four seconds, between bites of hardtack, without any apparent awareness of having done anything remarkable. Children were like that. They walked past truths that took grown men decades to find, and they didn't even stop to pick them up.

"Go to sleep, Tam," Silas said roughly.

"Will you tell me another story tomorrow?"

"No."

"Is that a story-teller's no or a real no?"

"It's a *go to sleep* no."

Tam grinned, lay down in the crook of his mother's arm, and was asleep within minutes, the way only children and soldiers can sleep anywhere. Silas sat by the fire a while longer, turning the phrase over. *A house behind a hill.* He tried, as he did most nights, to reach for his mother — for whatever remained — and found the usual smoke. But tonight, for the first time, he thought he could almost make out the roofline.

* * *

Two days later, the road climbed into hills, and the hills brought checkpoints.

Not Inquisition ones — those would come later, closer to the capital, and everyone in the caravan knew it and walked anyway, because faith is mostly the art of walking toward the thing that frightens you. These were bandits, or men dressed as bandits, or soldiers dressed as bandits, which amounted to the same arithmetic: a dozen armed men on the high ground, a toll of "donation" levied on every pilgrim, and knives that made it clear the donation was not optional.

Silas assessed them in three seconds and hated what he saw. Too organized. Clean gear under the dirt. Someone was paying these men to slow pilgrim traffic on the road to the capital, and Silas had a guess about who, and the guess wore a crown.

There was nothing to do but pay. The caravan pooled its coins, grumbling, and the line moved slowly past the robbers, who took their cut with bored efficiency. Silas paid for himself and Wren without incident. Tam and Sera were three places behind him.

He heard it before he saw it — Sera's voice going high and thin, the sound of a woman arguing with a man who has already decided. One of the bandits had hold of Tam's shoulder. Another was holding up the boy's boot, the one stuffed with straw, and laughing.

"Empty," the man said. "You travel with nothing, boy? Nothing hidden in the straw?"

"He's a child," Sera said. "He has nothing. Please—"

"Everyone has something." The bandit drew his knife, not threateningly, exactly — idly, the way a bored man plays with a tool. "Maybe we'll take a lock of hair. Maybe a tooth. Something to remember you by."

And here is the thing about Silas Pemberton, the thing the stories got right without ever understanding: he did not decide to move. There was no moment of heroic choice, no internal debate. His body simply arrived, the way it arrived at pockets and locks, faster than his conscience could file an objection. One moment he was three places back in line; the next he was between the knife and the boy, hands open, voice pleasant.

"Now, friend," he said, "let's talk about what remembering costs."

The bandit blinked at him. "Another hero."

"Worse. A professional." Silas smiled the smile he saved for marks — warm, stupid, disarming. "You're being paid to collect coin and slow carts. I know, because I've been on your side of this road. So let me save you some trouble. The child's worth nothing. The woman's worth nothing. But I—" he leaned in, dropped his voice, let the smile sharpen into something older and hungrier, "—I'm worth a great deal, and I'm worth much more to you unharmed than dead. Walk me to your captain. Let the family pass. Everyone profits."

It was, technically, a bluff. He had nothing to offer except the implication that he was valuable, and the deeper implication that he knew things worth knowing. But bluffing was the first language he'd ever learned, and the bandit's eyes did the arithmetic Silas wanted — greed dividing caution, caution coming out slightly ahead.

"Wait here," the bandit said, and stalked off up the slope.

Silas turned back to Sera and Tam. The boy was staring up at him with an expression that Silas recognized, distantly, with horror, as worship.

"You stood in front of the knife," Tam breathed.

"I stood in front of a *man*," Silas said. "Men are negotiable."

"You're not scared of anything."

"I'm scared of everything. That's the entire trick." He crouched to the boy's level, and lowered his voice, and told him the truest thing he'd said in weeks: "Being brave isn't not being afraid, Tam. It's being afraid in a useful direction. Now get behind your mother and stay there."

The bandit returned with his captain, a scarred woman with tax-collector eyes, and Silas talked for ten minutes — about routes, about tolls, about a fictional employer with a fictional purse — and bought the caravan through with nothing but words and nerve. When it was done, and the pilgrims were moving again, and his heart had remembered how to beat at a normal speed, he found Tam walking beside him again, closer than before, matching his stride with fierce determination.

"When I grow up," the boy announced, "I'm going to be just like you."

Something cold slid down Silas's spine, colder than any checkpoint, colder than the vial's memory ever managed.

"Don't," he said.

"Why not?"

Because I'm a liar and a thief and half of what you've heard is invented and the other half is worse than invented. Because the last person who decided to be like me ended up wearing a crown made of my worst day. Because everything I love gets measured against what I'm willing to lose, and I keep losing.

"Because," Silas said, "the story's wrong. The man in the story gives the water away on purpose. The real one just happened to be standing there."

Tam frowned, processing this. Then he shrugged, with the magnificent resilience of the young.

"Maybe," he said. "But I was *there*. I drank the water." He grinned, gap-toothed and certain. "Stories are just what happened, plus somebody who saw it. And I saw it."

He skipped ahead to bother Wren, boots squeaking, and Silas stood in the road watching him go, feeling something shift in his chest that he had no name for and didn't trust.

Behind them, far down the valley they'd climbed out of, a bell began to ring — though there was no town there, and no tower, and the sound carried wrong, flat and deliberate, like a message rather than a noise.

Wren heard it too. She turned, hand already at the strap of the pack where the relics rode wrapped in wool.

"That's not a church bell," she said.

"No," Silas agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Ahead, the road bent north toward the capital, and somewhere past the bend, he was certain, someone had just learned exactly which pilgrims to look for.
