## 19. Checkpoint of Ash

The checkpoint announced itself an hour before we reached it, the way a wound announces itself before you see the blood.

First the road changed. The pilgrim caravan had been following the old king's highway through a country of bare fields and frozen streams, but now the road widened, graded, swept clean of snow — and swept clean of everything else. No beggars. No shrines. No roadside cairns where travelers left offerings for the small gods. Just white stone and white frost and a silence that had been manufactured, not found.

Then the crows. Hundreds of them, perched on the bare trees along both sides of the road like an audience waiting for a show. Wren looked up at them and her breath came out in a thin, frightened stream. "That's not natural."

"Nothing about the Inquisition is natural," I said. "They pay their god-touched in omens. The crows are somebody's idea of a welcome."

And then, around a bend in the road, the checkpoint itself: a wall of black iron and pale stone thrown across the highway like a scar across a throat. Towers at either end. A gate wide enough for six carts. And above the gate, mounted on a spike of white marble, a bell — the largest I'd ever seen, its surface covered in script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts. Even from a quarter mile away I could feel it. A pressure behind the eyes. A flattening, like a hand pressed gently but firmly against the inside of your skull.

The bells of the capital were supposed to be tuned to silence the gods. This one was tuned to silence something smaller.

Us.

"They're searching memories," Mother Hessa said beside me. She was the caravan's leader, a broad, weathered woman with a pilgrim's staff and a voice that could carry across a valley. She'd seen checkpoints before. "Every pilgrim going to the midwinter blessing gets screened. It's law. It's been law for thirty years."

"Screened how?" Wren asked.

"They touch your temples and they look. If you're carrying anything the church doesn't want carried — heresy, god-words, relics — they take it." Hessa's jaw tightened. "They take the memory of it. You walk through the gate and you don't remember what they took. You just feel lighter, and you thank them for it."

I kept my face still, but inside, everything went cold and clear, the way it always did when the job arrived. The relics — the tongue-bone and the eye-stone — were wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the beggar's cart, under turnips and a dead man's coat. The tongue-bone hummed. The eye-stone, since the oracle had given it up, dreamed — I'd felt it dreaming at night, a slow turning attention, like a lighthouse sweeping a dark sea. If a god-touched inquisitor laid fingers on either one, we were dead. Worse than dead. Hollowed, and then paraded through the capital as an example.

"Everyone walks through?" I asked.

"Everyone," Hessa said. "Even the children. *Especially* the children. The church says innocence is easiest to read."

* * *

We had two hours, because the line of carts and pilgrims ahead of us moved at the speed of fear.

I spent the first hour watching. That was the trade, really — watching was ninety percent of any theft worth the name, and the other ten percent was nerve. I sat on the cart's bench with Tam asleep against my shoulder and I watched the checkpoint work.

The inquisitors processed pilgrims in threes. One to ask the questions — name, parish, purpose of travel, have you heard the voice of any god, have you seen any miracle, have you dreamed of the king. One to touch the temples and *look*. And one to stand behind with a long white rod of pale stone, and this was the one I watched most carefully, because the rod was for the ones who failed.

I watched a tinker fail. He was a small, gray man with ink-stained fingers, and when the temple-touching inquisitor — a woman with a blindfold of white silk and a face like folded paper — touched his temples, her head tilted, and she made a small sound, and the man with the rod stepped forward.

The tinker wept. He said, "Please, I only heard it once, I only —"

The rod touched his forehead. There was no flash, no scream. He simply stopped weeping. He stopped everything. He looked at the inquisitors with a mild, pleasant face and said, "Thank you, blessed ones. I feel so much lighter." And he walked through the gate smiling, and I knew — I knew the way a thief knows a locked box is empty — that somewhere behind his eyes a room had been cleared out and the door bricked over.

"They don't kill you," Wren whispered. She'd been watching too. "They just *edit* you."

"They take what they want and they leave the shape of the wanting," I said. "It's cleaner than killing. Killing makes martyrs. Editing makes grateful subjects."

Tam stirred against my shoulder. He was nine, and he'd appointed himself my shadow somewhere outside Casterhale, and no amount of cold shoulder had dislodged him. "What do they take?" he asked sleepily.

"Memories," I said. "The ones they don't like."

"Can they take good ones?"

"They can take any ones they like. That's what makes them the Inquisition."

He considered this with the gravity of a nine-year-old deciding something important. "Then they're thieves," he said, and went back to sleep.

I looked at his sleeping face for a long moment. *Then they're thieves.* Out of the mouths of children, as the priests used to say back when the priests still believed in gods. I felt something turn over in my chest, some old familiar shame with a new edge on it, and I put it away. Later. Feel it later, if there was a later.

Because right now I had a problem to solve, and the problem was this: in two hours, a blindfolded woman was going to put her fingers on my temples, and she was going to see everything. The vial's memory of the hollowed king, burned into me like a brand. The tongue-bone's location in the cart. The eye-stone's dreaming. The curse the guardian at the Shrine of Teeth had left in me, the whispering link to the thing that wore Aldric's crown. And underneath all of it, the whole sedimentary record of Silas Pemberton: every theft, every fence, every widow's grief I'd lifted and sold back to her as peace.

They wouldn't kill me for most of that. They'd take it, though. And what they took, they used — I'd seen what the Inquisition built out of stolen minds. And if they saw the curse, if they heard the whisper of the impostor king in my skull, they wouldn't edit me.

They'd deliver me.

* * *

I found Mother Hessa at the front of the caravan, arguing quietly with the captain of the hired guards, and I interrupted the way I'd learned to interrupt over thirty years of taking things from people: apologetically, and with my hands visible.

"Hessa. A word about the gate."

She looked at me. She'd been looking at me differently since Casterhale burned, since I'd pulled her grandson out from under a collapsing stable roof with one hand while the other held a burning ledger I'd stolen back from the Inquisition's agents. She suspected what I was. Suspecting and knowing were different things, and I intended to keep them different as long as the road allowed.

"The gate takes memories," I said. "You know it. I know it. The question is whether it takes what we can't afford to lose."

"The church's law is the church's law," the guard captain said. He was a big man, ex-soldier, honest in the way of men who'd never had to be otherwise. "We're pilgrims. We go through like pilgrims."

"And lose the blessing you're walking a thousand miles to receive?" I kept my voice gentle. "Hessa. The eye-stone showed us the rite. Midwinter night, the king binds every god's name into the crown, and every prayer in the world goes unanswered forever. Every shrine goes dark. Every miracle dries up. The blessing at the capital this midwinter will be the last blessing there ever is — and it'll be a lie told by a hollow thing in a stolen crown. That's what's behind that gate. That's what's at the end of this road. And if we walk through that gate like honest pilgrims, we arrive in the capital as empty as the man on the throne, and the world ends with us smiling about it."

Silence. The crows shifted on their bare branches, hundreds of small black heads tilting.

Hessa looked at the gate for a long time. Then at me. "You have a plan," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd learned that much about me.

"I have the beginning of one."

"That's what you said in Casterhale."

"And Casterhale burned," I admitted. "So I've improved it. This one has fewer fires."

* * *

The plan had three parts, and all three parts were theft, because I am what I am and the gods — the real ones, the hungry ones — have a sense of humor.

The first part was the simplest and the hardest: the memories had to leave our heads before the inquisitors touched us. A blindfolded woman can't see what isn't there.

I'd spent thirty years taking memories out of people. I knew the geography of a mind the way a locksmith knows the geography of a lock. The trick of it — the trick nobody in the Hollow had ever bothered to learn, because in the Hollow I was the only thief of my kind — was that taking and *giving* were the same door. A memory is a weight. You lift it out of one mind and you can set it down in another, if the other mind will hold still and open. Wren had carried a piece of my childhood for two days once, when the Inquisition nearly caught us outside Casterhale. She'd hated it. She'd done it anyway.

So we gathered in the back of the beggar's cart, under the turnips, in the hour before the gate, and I did the work.

Tam first, because his mind was small and bright and easy as an open window. I took the shrine — the night at the Shrine of Teeth, the raid, the guardian's bone-white face, my curse, everything that connected the boy to what we actually were — and I folded it small and set it into Wren, who held still with her jaw set and tears standing in her eyes and said, "Take it. I'll keep it safe. I'll keep *him* safe."

Then the eye-stone's visions, out of Wren and into me — better they sit in the mind of a man who's spent his life hiding things. Then the tongue-bone's location, out of Hessa, out of the guard captain, out of the three pilgrims who'd helped load the cart. Then the route, the names, the faces. Layer by layer I stripped the caravan of everything that could hang us, and I carried it, and the weight of it was like carrying a second head.

The relics themselves I couldn't carry in my mind. They were objects, and objects show. So the second part of the plan belonged to Hessa, and it was beautiful in the way that only honest things are beautiful.

"Turnips," she said, when I'd explained it. "You want me to hide the bones of a dead god under turnips."

"Under turnips, in a cart, in a line of forty carts, behind a hundred pilgrims, in front of inquisitors who are looking into minds, not baskets. Nobody looks at turnips. Turnips are the most innocent object in creation."

"They'd better be," she said, and wrapped the tongue-bone in a turnip's worth of oilcloth, and buried it deep.

The eye-stone was the problem. It dreamed. It had attention, and attention could be felt — I'd felt it for weeks, that slow lighthouse sweep. If a god-touched inquisitor came within arm's reach of it, they'd feel it like a hand on the back of the neck.

So I did the thing I'd been avoiding doing since the oracle's hut. I opened my own mind and I took the eye-stone's dreaming into myself. Not the stone — the stone went under the turnips with its brother — but the *dreaming*, the attention, the weight of it, the way you might carry a sleeping animal's warmth in your coat while the animal itself sits in a basket. I folded the dream down small and I hid it in the one place in my mind no inquisitor would ever think to search, because no one in the history of theft had ever hidden anything there.

I hid it in the memory I'd already sold.

There's a room in my head where my mother's face used to be. I'd sold that memory to the drowned god outside Casterhale — traded it for the tongue-bone's location, and what was left was the shape of the having, a room with a bricked-over door. The inquisitors would read that room and find what every memory-thief's victim finds: an absence, smooth and finished, nothing behind it. But an absence is a space, and a space can hold something, if you're the kind of man who learned to hide things inside his own wounds.

I put the god's dreaming in the room where my mother's face had been, and I closed the door, and I felt the eye-stone's attention settle there like a bird on a bare branch, patient, watching out through my own loss.

It was the most blasphemous thing I have ever done, and I have done many.

* * *

The third part of the plan was nerve, and nerve was the part I couldn't plan.

We reached the gate at dusk. The bell above it pressed down on us like deep water, and I felt the whisper in my skull — the curse, the link to the impostor — go quiet, flattened, smothered under the bell's silence. For the first time in weeks my head was entirely my own, and I was surprised by how much I'd come to dread the quiet, and how much I'd missed it.

The line moved. Carts were overturned and shaken. Pilgrims were processed in threes, questions, touch, and through. The tinker's empty smile haunted the back of my eyes.

Then it was us. Hessa, Wren, Tam, me, and the beggar's cart.

The question-asker was a thin man with a voice like dry paper. Names. Parish. Purpose. *Have you heard the voice of any god.* Hessa answered like a stone wall answers weather, and the paper voice moved on to me, and I gave him the name I'd been wearing since Casterhale — Aldous Fen, tinker's journeyman, going to the blessing to pray for my late wife — and he wrote it down, and the blindfolded woman stepped toward me with her fingers raised.

And that was when the crows went up.

All of them, the whole audience of them, hundreds of wings at once, a black storm peeling off the bare trees, and every inquisitor in the checkpoint looked up — because you look up, everyone looks up, it's the oldest misdirection in the world and I didn't even do it, the crows did it for me, or something did, some small god with a sense of humor and a grudge against the church that had silenced it.

The blindfolded woman's fingers never touched my temples. The paper voice said, "The omens — report to the tower —" and half the checkpoint's attention went vertical, and in that gap, that beautiful human gap, the guard captain's cart-horse shied — I'd paid a stable boy in Casterhale to teach that horse to shy at the smell of crow — and the beggar's cart lurched against the inspection table, and turnips rolled everywhere across the white stone, dozens of them, and forty pilgrims and six guards and three inquisitors all bent at once to pick them up, because turnips are the most innocent object in creation and nobody lets a good turnip go to waste.

I didn't touch the relics. I didn't need to. I stood in the middle of the small chaos with my hands visible and my face arranged into honest confusion, and I did the only thing left to do: I *watched*. I watched every inquisitor, every guard, every pair of hands, and I marked who saw what. A guard picked up the oilcloth bundle with the tongue-bone in it, felt the hum, frowned — and then a sergeant shouted about the crows and he set it down among the turnips and forgot it, and the forgetting was a door, and I walked through it and lifted the memory of the frown out of his mind as cleanly as lifting a coin from a sleeping man's purse. He'd never know he'd ever felt it. The bundle was just a turnip again.

The blindfolded woman came back to us before the turnips were all gathered. She touched Hessa's temples, then Tam's, then Wren's, then mine — quick, distracted, her head still tilted toward the wheeling crows — and I stood in the room where my mother's face used to be with the god's dreaming folded in my arms like a sleeping child and I was nothing, I was smooth absence, I was Aldous Fen the tinker's journeyman with a dead wife and an empty head, and her fingers rested on my skull for three seconds, four, five —

And then her head tilted the other way. Slowly. Toward me. The blindfold's silk was thin, and beneath it I felt something turn, some attention older and colder than the woman wearing it, and it looked at me the way the eye-stone looked at things.

"You," she said softly. "I know your face."

"I don't think so, blessed one," I said. "Tinker's journeyman. We all have the same face after a season of road dust."

"No," she said. Her fingers tightened. "Not your face. Your —"

And the crows came down.

Not all of them. Three of them, fast and black, straight at the bell above the gate, and they hit it, all three at once, talons and wings against the script-covered bronze, and the bell — tuned to flatten gods, tuned to silence everything holy — *rang*. One clear, enormous, wrong note that went through the checkpoint like a spear through water, and every god-touched inquisitor within a mile staggered, and the blindfolded woman cried out and tore her fingers from my temples, and in the ringing and the staggering and the storm of black wings, Mother Hessa said in her voice that carried across valleys, "Bless you, blessed ones, we'll be late for the blessing," and the caravan rolled through the gate.

I was the last one through. I couldn't help it. I looked back.

The blindfolded woman was standing in the middle of the road, holding her head, and the crows were settling on the bell above her like a black crown. And as the gate's shadow passed over me, she turned her face — blindfolded, tear-streaked, terrible — and found mine in the dark with unerring precision.

"I'll remember you," she called, and her voice carried the way Hessa's carried, the way voices carry when something behind them is doing the carrying. "Tinker's journeyman. I'll remember your face if it's the last thing the church leaves me."

* * *

Two miles down the road, in the dark, with the checkpoint's fires shrinking behind us, Wren handed Tam back his memories. I felt her do it — felt the folded night at the Shrine of Teeth leave her and settle into the boy, who woke with a gasp and immediately said, "Did we get through? Did the plan work?"

"The plan worked," Wren said.

"Mostly," I said.

Hessa snorted from the driver's bench. "Turnips," she said, to no one, to the road, to whatever small god had sent the crows. "The bones of a dead god smuggled past the Inquisition under turnips, and a bell rung by birds. If I live to be ninety I will never stop telling this story."

"Don't," I said. "Not in the capital. Not where anyone can hear."

She glanced back at me, and her face in the firelight from the cart lantern was grim and kind at once. "That's the trouble with you, Aldous Fen," she said, using the false name like a knife between the ribs, letting me know she knew. "You steal everything so well that you've forgotten how to let anyone keep anything."

I didn't answer. I sat in the back of the cart with Tam asleep against my shoulder again and the god's dreaming folded in the bricked-over room where my mother's face had been, and I told myself the woman's parting words were a threat and nothing more.

But I'd spent thirty years reading people, and I knew the difference between a threat and a promise, and her voice had been a promise.

*I'll remember your face if it's the last thing the church leaves me.*

She hadn't seen my face. She'd seen through it. And somewhere behind us, at a checkpoint of ash and turnips and impossible crows, Inquisitor Vess — because I knew, I'd known since the word *remembered* left her mouth, I'd have known that voice anywhere, that patient, terrible, grieving patience — had just found the thread she'd been hunting since Casterhale burned.

And I had a feeling that the next time she pulled it, she wouldn't send crows.

She'd send an offer.
