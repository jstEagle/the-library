## 20. Vess Offers a Deal

She came for him at the hour when even thieves sleep.

Silas woke to the smell of tallow and cold iron and knew before he opened his eyes that he was not alone. The pilgrim caravan lay around him in its usual sprawl — forty souls bedded down in the lee of a ruined waystation, oxen staked, cookfire burned to embers. Somewhere near the wagons, Tam snored with the shameless abandon of a boy who trusted the world. That trust was Silas's fault. He'd let it happen.

The woman sitting on the far side of the dead fire didn't move when he stirred. She sat with her hands open on her knees, palms up, the posture of someone showing you she held nothing. Inquisitor Vess out of armor looked smaller than she had at the checkpoint of ash — a lean, gray-eyed woman in a plain traveler's coat, her god-touched brand hidden under a leather cuff. Only her eyes were the same: patient as a trap.

"You didn't hear me come," she said.

"I heard you." It was a lie and they both knew it. "You want something. People who want to kill me don't sit by my fire first."

"Your fire is out."

"Then we have that in common." He sat up slowly, keeping his blanket over his legs, keeping his hands where she could see them. Around them the camp slept on. That bothered him more than anything else. A woman like Vess should have set every pilgrim's teeth on edge just by existing. Instead the camp breathed easy around her, and Silas understood she'd done something — some small working, some hush laid over the firelight — so they could talk without an audience. Or so no one could come running.

"I've been watching you since the checkpoint," Vess said. "You stole back eleven memories from my clerks while I stood three feet away. Eleven. And I only caught it because one old woman smiled at her husband afterward and he didn't know why."

"It seemed rude to leave them empty-handed."

"Don't be clever with me, thief. Cleverness is how you got here, and here is a place you don't want to be." She leaned forward into the ember-light. Up close, Silas saw what the gray in her eyes really was: not age. Scars. Fine white threads across both irises, like frost on a window. "I'm going to offer you a bargain. Before I do, I want you to understand that I know exactly what you're carrying. The vial. The tongue-bone. The eye-stone. You think you've been quiet. You've been quiet the way a knife is quiet."

Silas kept his face still. Inside, his stomach dropped through the frozen ground. *She knows about the relics.* Which meant she knew about the rite, or enough of it. Which meant everything they'd bled for on the road from Casterhale — the burning city, the shrine of teeth, the curse whispering behind his ear like a second heartbeat — all of it had been a race run against someone already waiting at the finish line.

"How long?" he asked.

"Since the checkpoint. Maybe before. My order has ears in every pilgrim band moving toward the capital this season, and yours has been strange since Casterhale. Two travelers who don't pray. A boy who follows a stranger like a duckling. A beggar's cart that's heavier than it looks." She shrugged. "You didn't need to hide better. You needed to hide differently. Thieves never learn that."

"And yet here you are, alone, telling me instead of taking me." He nodded at her open hands. "Where's your escort, Inquisitor? Where are the chains?"

"Dead men make bad witnesses and worse allies. I'm not here to take you." Her voice dropped, and for the first time he heard something underneath the iron in it. Not softness. Weight. "I'm here because we want the same man dead, and I'm tired of watching other people pay for my failure."

* * *

He made her talk first. That was the oldest rule he had — older than the Hollow, older than Marlow's ledger — *never spend your own coin until you know theirs.* So he fed the embers and listened while Vess told him about the day the gods came back hungry.

"I was nine years old," she said. "A farm girl in the Marches, nothing special. When the silence fell — when the shrines went dark everywhere, the way they did in your valley — my mother took it hard. She'd prayed to Vael the Shepherd her whole life. Lambs, rain, her husband's bad lungs. When he stopped answering, she decided it was her fault. Not the world's fault. Hers. So she prayed harder. She prayed until her knees bled, and one night—" Vess stopped. Her jaw worked once. "One night, something answered."

"The old gods were gone."

"Something was left in the house after the owners moved out. Rats in the walls wearing the family's clothes. It answered in my mother's voice. It gave her exactly what she asked for — my father's lungs healed, whole and strong. And then it took the payment. It took every memory she had of being loved by him. Forty years of marriage, gone between one breath and the next. She woke up in the morning next to a stranger and screamed until the neighbors came." Vess turned her hands over, closed them. "That's what answered prayers look like now. That's what the Inquisition was built to fight. We were farmers' children and priests' bastards and one knight who'd seen too much, and we swore that no god would ever feed on another family like mine. Then we found out who was teaching the rats to wear the clothes."

"The king," Silas said quietly.

"The thing wearing the king." Her eyes came up and fixed on his. "You've seen it. Don't lie to me — I watched your face at the checkpoint when my clerk mentioned the midwinter blessing. You went pale in a way that has nothing to do with fear of us. You've seen inside that crown."

He thought about lying anyway. Habit argued for it; survival usually did. But there was Wren asleep twenty feet away, and Tam, and forty pilgrims who'd shared their bread with two strangers, and the curse behind his ear that meant the impostor could probably already taste this conversation. Lies were heavy currency and he was nearly broke.

"I saw a memory," he said. "Stolen, sealed in glass. King Aldric, eight years ago, on a table in his own crypt, while something made of smoke and borrowed faces poured him out like a jug and put itself on in his place." He watched her flinch — actually flinch, the frost-scarred eyes going bright. "So yes, Inquisitor. I've seen inside the crown. And I know what happens at midwinter if nobody stops it."

"Tell me what you know. All of it. Then I'll tell you my plan, and you can decide how much of a fool you want to be."

So he told her. Not everything — he kept back the curse, kept back the voice in his head that spoke in his mother's tone, kept back the truth about what the impostor was grown from, because that truth was a knife he wasn't ready to hand anyone. But he gave her the rite: the binding of names into the crown, the unmaking of every god still answering, the silence that would fall over the world forever — a silence deeper than the Hollow's, a silence with teeth. Vess listened without interrupting, which frightened him more than shouting would have. Soldiers who listen are soldiers with a plan.

When he finished, she was quiet a long moment. The embers ticked. An ox shifted in its traces.

"Six weeks," she said finally. "Midwinter night, in the rite chamber beneath the palace. Three relics to break the binding — you have two. The third is under their feet already, in the crypt where Aldric was hollowed. They keep it close because it's the anchor. Take the heart away and the whole rite comes apart like wet rope." She said it flatly, factually, and Silas felt the ground tilt under him. She knew about the heart. She knew things about the palace that had taken Wren a night of grave-robbery terror to learn.

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I've been planning to kill that thing for six years," Vess said, "and I have never once gotten closer than a locked door. That's my offer, thief. Listen."

* * *

She laid it out the way a surgeon lays out knives — each one named, each one placed precisely where the next would need it.

"The rite chamber sits thirty feet below the throne room. Its doors answer only to the king's blood and the king's voice, and the thing on the throne hasn't left the palace in four years. Assassins get within a hundred feet and forget why they came — it hollows them before they reach the door. Poison fails; it doesn't eat. Fire fails; the guards don't burn. Every approach I've tried, every agent I've sent, dies or worse." She paused. "But the rite itself needs one thing the impostor cannot fake. It needs witnesses."

"Witnesses?"

"A binding of that scale — every god's true name pulled into one crown — requires belief to hold it. Names are just sounds unless someone hears them. The old rites were performed before congregations for exactly that reason. The impostor knows this. That's why it invented the midwinter blessing. Ten thousand pilgrims come to the capital every year to receive the king's blessing on the longest night. This year, those ten thousand aren't a crowd. They're the congregation. They're the bell it rings the names with." Her voice went very quiet. "Every soul in that square when the rite completes gets hollowed. All at once. Ten thousand people smiling the same smile, the way that village on your road smiled. And the rite doesn't end there. With ten thousand believers feeding it, the binding reaches outward. Every prayer said anywhere, answered or not, becomes a leash. The gods won't be killed, thief. They'll be *owned*. And whoever owns them owns everyone who ever knelt."

The cold that went through Silas then had nothing to do with the night. He thought of the Road of Small Gods, the drowned god's price, the oracle's blind eyes. A world where divinity was a chain and the chain was held by a thing made from his own discarded childhood.

"So the blessing must not happen," he said. "Keep the pilgrims home."

"With what army? The roads are open, the festival is proclaimed, and half the realm believes their king walks on water. No. The blessing happens. It *has* to happen — because it's also our only door." She leaned in, and the frost in her eyes caught the ember-light. "The rite chamber opens from above during the blessing. One moment, when the impostor stands before the congregation and begins the naming, the ward-stones shift and there is a stair, unlocked, straight down into the dark. For the length of a single hymn. I've confirmed it twice through agents who didn't come back but whose reports did. During the hymn, the chamber below is reachable. And during the hymn, the impostor's attention is *upward*, drinking the crowd. That is the only minute in six years when that thing is not looking down."

"So you go down the stair during the hymn," Silas said slowly. "With the relics."

"With the relics, yes. But the relics aren't enough — I told you that six years ago, when I lost my arm trying it alone." She pushed back her left cuff. Beneath it, where a hand should have been, was a smooth cap of pale wood, carved and worn. "The binding has to be *unbound*, not broken. Someone must stand in the circle and speak the counter-rite while the relics do their work, and the counter-rite must be spoken by a memory-thief. By someone who can take a name the way you take a memory — gently, whole, without shattering it. There are perhaps four people alive with the gift to do it. Two are in the Hollow. One is dead. And one is sitting across my fire pretending he isn't interested."

Silas said nothing. His mouth had gone dry.

"That's the offer," Vess said. "Help me kill the impostor king. Stand in the circle at midwinter and unmake the binding. Do that, and I give you Wren."

The word landed like a blade between his ribs. "Wren isn't yours to give."

"She's wanted in four provinces for theft of a holy relic. She's marked in my ledgers as an accomplice of a memory-thief operating outside guild sanction. If I take you both at midwinter, she burns with you — that's the law I serve, and I have never once bent it, not for love, not for pity, not for my own mother begging on her knees." Vess's voice didn't rise at all. "But if you help me kill the thing that murdered my faith, then I will write Wren's name out of every ledger I control, burn the warrants myself, and put her on a boat south with coin in her pocket and no past worth hunting. She goes free. Clean. Forever. That's not a bribe, thief. It's the only honest thing I have to trade."

* * *

And there it was. The devil's arithmetic, laid out neat as a merchant's scales.

Silas looked at the embers and did the sums he was good at. This was the trade he'd practiced his whole life — weighing what a thing cost against what it bought — and he hated how quickly the numbers came.

Because the plan was good. That was the poison in it. He turned it over and it had no flaws he could find. The stair during the hymn, the attention upward, the counter-rite spoken by a thief — it was elegant, it was feasible, and it would work. He could feel it working, the way he could feel a lock's tumblers set right before he ever touched them. Six weeks of terror on the road, and the answer had been sitting in the enemy's own ceremony the whole time.

And the price. The price was written in the same ink.

"Ten thousand people," he said. "In that square. Singing the hymn while we sneak downstairs. If we fail — if anything goes wrong, if I stumble over a syllable of your counter-rite — the rite completes and every one of them is hollowed. You're not asking me to risk my life. You're asking me to build the altar out of them."

"I'm asking you to recognize that they're already on the altar," Vess said. "They were on it the day that thing took the crown. The blessing was designed for this. The congregation was gathered for this. Nothing you or I do puts them in that square — they're walking there happily, singing, bringing their children." Her wooden hand tapped once against her knee. "The only question is whether anyone is standing in the circle when it matters. Right now the answer is no. With you, the answer might be yes."

"And Tam?" The word came out of him before he could stop it. "The boy who thinks I'm a hero out of a story? He'll be in that square too. Pilgrims bring their children to the king's blessing. You know it. Your plan counts on it — a crowd that size needs children in it, belief that fresh." He leaned forward, and for once he didn't care what showed in his face. "You're asking me to use them as bait, Inquisitor. All of them. The whole caravan. The old woman whose memories I stole back. The man who taught Tam to whistle. You want ten thousand people standing in the rain singing while we commit regicide underneath them, and if we fail they stop being people. And you call that honest."

"I call it the only door there is." For the first time, something cracked in her voice — just a hairline, just enough to see the wound underneath. "You think I haven't run this night ten thousand times in my head? You think I don't know exactly what I'm asking? I've spent six years finding a way to save the world that costs nothing, and I've come to the same conclusion every time: there isn't one. There's only the version where fewer people die. Fewer, thief. Not none. Never none again." She stood, slow, joints stiff with cold, and looked down at him with those scarred gray eyes. "You of all people should understand the arithmetic. You've made a living off it. How many widows bought peace with grief you took? How many times have you weighed a person's pain against another person's comfort and called it commerce?"

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same, and you knew it every time, and you did it anyway because the alternative was starving." She pulled her cuff back over the wooden hand. "I'm not mocking you. I'm telling you we're the same animal, you and I. The difference is I stopped pretending the ledger balances."

Silas looked past her, at the sleeping camp. At the shape of Tam curled against a wagon wheel, one arm flung over his face, whistling tunelessly even in his sleep. At Wren's bedroll, empty — she was on watch, up on the waystation wall, a small dark figure against the stars, keeping guard over a man who'd stolen her childhood and never given it back.

If he said yes, Wren walked free. Clean papers, coin, a boat south, no past worth hunting. Everything he owed her, paid in full, by the only creditor who could actually pay it. And all it would cost was standing in a circle beneath ten thousand singing people and gambling their souls on his voice holding true.

If he said no, Vess would try it without him, or die trying, or worse — and the rite would complete, and the gods would become leashes, and somewhere south on a free clean boat Wren would wake up one morning smiling someone else's smile.

There was no door that cost nothing. She was right about that. He hated her for being right about that.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until the caravan makes the crossroads at Millbrook. Four days. After that, the road splits and my path takes me ahead of you to the capital, and the next time we speak will be in the square on midwinter night — with you either beside me or in my chains." She reached into her coat and set something on the stone between them: a thin sliver of pale metal, no longer than a finger. "A key. To the stair, if you say yes. It only turns during the hymn. It only turns once." She paused at the edge of the firelight, a gray shape already dissolving into the dark. "For what it's worth, thief — I hope you say yes. Not for the world. For the boy. I watched him at the checkpoint. Whatever you did to earn that kind of faith, it's the only thing I've seen in six years that thing on the throne can't imitate."

Then she was gone, between one blink and the next, and the camp breathed normally again, and Silas sat alone by dead coals with a key that weighed nothing and everything.

He didn't sleep. He sat turning the sliver of metal over and over until the sky went gray, and when Wren climbed down from the wall at dawn, stamping cold from her boots, she found him still there and read his face in one glance, the way she always did.

"What happened?" she said. "Who came?"

Silas closed his hand around the key.

"Sit down, Wren," he said. "There's something I have to tell you. And then there's something I have to decide."
