## 21. What Loyalty Costs

Wren knew something was wrong before she saw them.

She'd learned to read Silas the way other people read weather — the set of his shoulders, the way his hands went still when they were usually busy. He came back from the treeline alone, which was the first wrong thing. He'd left with Vess an hour ago, walking out of the pilgrim camp under the pretense of checking the shrine road for Inquisition patrols. He came back with his jaw tight and his eyes fixed somewhere past her, like if he looked at her directly the whole night would become real.

Tam wasn't with him either. But Tam was always wandering off — that was nothing. That was just Tam.

"Where's Vess?" Wren asked.

"Gone." Silas sat down heavily on the wagon's tailboard, next to the beggar's cart where the relics slept under sacking and dried fish. The tongue-bone, cold as a coin in winter. The eye-stone, which Wren had paid for with a memory she could no longer name but still felt the shape of, the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore. "Back to her people."

"And?"

"And nothing. We keep moving at first light."

Wren crouched in front of him so he couldn't look past her anymore. "Silas. What did she offer you?"

He didn't answer fast enough. That was the second wrong thing. Silas Pemberton lied beautifully — she'd seen him talk a checkpoint sergeant into apologizing to *him* — and when he didn't lie, it meant the truth had teeth.

"She wants the impostor dead," he said finally. "Same as us. She offered to let us walk away clean. All we had to do was give her the pilgrims."

The words landed one at a time, each one worse than the last. "Give her the pilgrims how?"

"Bait. She stages a raid on the caravan. Draws the hollowed guard out of position while she takes the rite chamber." His voice was flat, reciting. "Two hundred people, most of them unarmed, most of them children or old. She says the casualties would be acceptable."

"Acceptable," Wren repeated.

"It gets worse."

"How could it possibly—"

"She wanted me to pick which ones." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "She said I was good at deciding who loses things. Professional experience."

Wren stood up because sitting felt too much like agreeing. Around them the pilgrim camp breathed in its sleep — cookfires banked to embers, a baby crying somewhere and being hushed, old Marta snoring in the wagon beside theirs with her prayer beads wrapped around her fist. Two hundred people who had shared their road and their bread and their fear for three weeks. Who had taught Tam dice games. Who had given Wren, without knowing it, the closest thing to a family she'd had since before the Hollow took her childhood.

And Vess wanted to spend them like coins.

"You refused," Wren said. It wasn't a question. If he hadn't refused, he wouldn't look like a man carrying his own funeral.

"I refused."

"Then why do you look like that?"

Silas rubbed his face with both hands. "Because she was right about the math, Wren. That's the ugly part. Two hundred against a kingdom. Against every soul in the capital, maybe every soul everywhere, if the rite finishes. I did the arithmetic standing right there in the trees and the arithmetic says take the deal." He looked up at her, and for once there was no thief's polish on him at all, just a tired man in his forties who had spent his whole life surviving and didn't know how to do anything else. "I said no anyway. And I need you to tell me why, someday, when I'm not too ashamed to hear it."

*Because the math is how the world got broken in the first place,* Wren thought. *Because every hollowed man in that village smiled the same smile, and somebody decided their lives were acceptable too.* But what she said was: "You already know why. You just don't trust yourself to mean it yet."

That earned her half a smile. It died fast.

"There's something else," Silas said.

The third wrong thing. Wren's stomach dropped through the frozen ground. "Tam."

"He wasn't at the meeting. He followed us. Little shadow, quiet as anything — I should have heard him, I *would* have heard anyone else—" Silas's hands curled into fists. "Vess found him at the edge of the trees. And instead of sending him back to camp like a sensible woman, she looked at me over his head and said, 'Then we'll call this insurance.'"

The night went very still around Wren. The fire crackled. Somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up.

"She took him," Wren said.

"She took him. Said if I change my mind by tomorrow's dusk, meet her at the ash mill on the river bend and bring the relics. Said Tam would be 'kept comfortable.'" Silas stood, and now the fury was there after all, banked so deep it came out as calm, which from Silas was the most frightening register he had. "I've been robbed by professionals my whole life. I know the difference between a threat and a promise. That was a promise."

Wren was already moving — grabbing her cloak, the knife she kept under the wagon seat, her boots. Her hands were shaking and she let them shake because fighting it would cost time.

"Wren." Silas caught her arm. "Think. She's god-touched. She burned a man's memories out of his head at the checkpoint with two words. If we go charging in—"

"If we *don't* go charging in, she learns that taking children works." Wren pulled free of his grip, not gently. "You want to know why I said the math is wrong? Because the moment you start paying in children, you've already lost whatever you were buying. She doesn't even see it. She thinks she's the reasonable one. She thinks she's *saving* everyone." Wren's voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "I was a child someone decided was expendable. Someone stole everything I was because it was convenient, and I spent years not even knowing what was missing. I will burn that ash mill down around her ears before I let Tam learn what that feels like."

Silas stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes — guilt, old and familiar, the shape of a debt he'd never been able to repay her. For once he didn't try to talk his way around it.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay. Then we do this smart, because brave and stupid look identical in the dark, and I've buried enough friends to know the difference."

* * *

They woke the ones they trusted and no others. Old Marta, who had crossed three wars with a mule cart and a psalter and never lost her nerve. Doss, the tanner with hands like slabs of leather, who asked no questions and brought a boar spear. A young couple, Ferro and Isla, whose son played dice with Tam and who volunteered before Silas finished explaining, which made him flinch like he'd been struck.

Marta listened to all of it — the Inquisitor, the hostage, the ash mill — with her beads clicking through her fingers, and then she said, "God-touched, you say. Marked by answered prayer."

"So they say."

"Mm." Marta's eyes were pale and steady. "My grandmother said the gods came back hungry. Everyone laughed at her. Nobody's laughing now." She tucked the psalter into her belt like a weapon. "The boy prayed at my fire last week, you know. Asked the small gods to make him brave enough to be useful. Children shouldn't pray like that. It invites attention."

Wren felt those words settle into her chest and stay there, cold.

They reached the river bend an hour before dawn, moving through frozen reeds with the mist coming off the water thick enough to drink. The ash mill squatted on the far bank — a derelict hulk of black timber, its wheel long rotted, its windows like empty eye sockets. One window glowed faintly. Candlelight, or something pretending to be candlelight.

Silas counted guards with a stolen spyglass. "Two outside. Maybe more within. Vess won't be far from the boy — hostages are only worth holding if you can produce them."

"Then we go in quiet and—"

The bell rang.

Not the mill. Not any bell Wren could see. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a single deep note that rolled across the river and up through the soles of her boots, and every hair on her arms stood up because she recognized it — the same resonance as the shrine in the Hollow, the impossible flicker that had started all of this. The sound of a god paying attention.

In the mill's glowing window, a silhouette rose. Slim. Female. Unhurried.

"She knows we're here," Silas breathed. "Of course she knows. God-touched. She probably felt us cross the river."

"Then sneaking is done." Wren drew her knife. Her heart was slamming and her mouth was dry and underneath the fear ran something hotter, something that had been building since the checkpoint, since the hollowed village, since a dying knight fell out of the sky and handed them a truth heavy enough to crush the world. "Silas. Whatever happens in there — whatever she offers, whatever she shows you — remember the arithmetic is a lie. People aren't numbers. Not the pilgrims. Not her. Not even her."

"Not even her," Silas agreed softly. "That's the problem with her. She forgot it about herself."

They waded the shallows side by side, the water dragging at their legs like hands, and the mill door opened before they reached it, spilling yellow light across the frosted mud.

Vess stood in the doorway with Tam beside her.

The boy looked small in the vast dark of the mill, but he wasn't crying. He was standing straight, chin up, wearing the expression of someone who has decided to be brave enough to be useful — and Wren's heart broke cleanly in two, because she recognized that expression. She'd worn it once, in another life, in front of another thief who took everything she had.

Behind Vess, in the candlelight, Wren saw the walls of the mill. They were covered in writing — thousands of prayers, scratched and inked and burned into the timber, floor to ceiling, decades of them. And beneath the prayers, half-glimpsed in the shadows, something else moved. Something that had been waiting in this place a long time, listening to all those voices asking for help.

Vess looked at Silas, then at Wren, then at the knife in Wren's hand, and she did not reach for her own blade.

"Good," she said. "You brought your fury. I'll need it. Because the thing behind me has been answering prayers in this valley for thirty years, thief — and it has agreed to help us kill the king." Her voice didn't waver, but her hand, Wren noticed, was trembling. "The price is a memory. And I know exactly which one it's going to ask you for."

Tam's eyes found Wren's across the threshold, wide and trusting and unbroken.

"Don't pay it," the boy whispered. "Whatever it asks. Don't pay it."
