## 22. Rescue at the Shrine of Teeth

The Shrine of Teeth had been a chapel once, back when the old gods still bothered to listen. Now it was a ribcage of black stone on a hill above the pilgrim road, its spire crowned with a dome studded with actual teeth — thousands of them, yellowed and cracked, mortared into the rock like scales. Pilgrims used to crawl up that hill to press their tongues against the door and pray for the right words at the right moment. Nobody crawled anymore. The god who'd lived there was dead or sleeping, and the Inquisition had moved in like rats into an abandoned larder.

Silas lay flat in the frost-brittle grass below the treeline and counted guards.

"Six on the walls," he murmured. "Two at the door. One walking the perimeter with a lantern — no, make that one pretending to walk the perimeter. He's stopped by the well every circuit for the last hour. Meeting someone."

Beside him, Wren shifted her weight without a sound. She'd learned to move quietly somewhere between Casterhale burning behind them and the checkpoint of ash, and Silas tried not to think too hard about what that meant — that she'd learned it because of him, because he kept dragging her toward fire.

"Vess?" she whispered.

"Inside. She wouldn't leave Tam anywhere but close." He'd bet the eye-stone on it. Vess was many things — cold, patient, terrifyingly certain — but she wasn't careless with leverage. The boy was her insurance policy, and you didn't leave your insurance in the cellar down the road.

The tongue-bone hung against his chest inside his shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a swallowed coal. It had been warm ever since the bell tower. Wren said she couldn't feel anything from it. Silas could feel it the way you feel a splinter you can't reach — a thin insistence, a pressure behind the breastbone, like something small and sharp trying to speak through his ribs.

Tonight he intended to let it speak.

* * *

They went over the wall on the north side, where the shrine's shadow pooled deepest and the mortar had crumbled enough to offer handholds. Silas went first, because it was his plan and his sin and if anyone's fingers were going to slip, they should be his. Wren came up after him like water finding a crack, and then they were crouched on the wall-walk between two guards who were watching the road instead of each other, which was the kind of mistake people only made when they believed no one would be fool enough to come for one orphan boy.

Fools. They'd never met Silas Pemberton when he had something to steal back.

He'd spent three days learning the shrine's rhythms. Three days of lying in wet grass while frost formed on his eyelashes, memorizing lantern circuits and shift changes and the exact pitch of the hinge on the refectory door. Three days of Wren asking, once, quietly: *What if we just knock? What if we tell her we'll trade?*

And what would we trade? he'd asked. The relics? Then everything Tam paid for is worth nothing.

She hadn't answered. She'd just looked at him with those eyes that remembered nothing of their shared past and somehow held all of it anyway, and he'd understood that she knew what he was really afraid of. Not the guards. Not Vess. That he'd find a reason, halfway through this raid, to cut and run — the way he'd almost done outside Casterhale, the way his whole body was built to do, the way the man who taught him theft had built him to do.

So here he was. Cutting toward the thing instead of away from it. It felt unnatural, like walking upstairs in a dream.

The perimeter walker passed beneath them, lantern swinging, and Silas dropped onto his shoulders with both knees, put him down into the frost, and had the man's own gag in his mouth before the grunt finished leaving his throat. Wren took his keys, his knife, and — after a moment's consideration — his boots, because hers had soaked through two days ago and cruelty was a luxury the poor couldn't afford to waste.

"You're getting ruthless," Silas whispered.

"I'm getting boots," she whispered back. "There's a difference."

There wasn't, quite. But he let her have it.

* * *

The interior of the Shrine of Teeth smelled of tallow and old incense and something underneath both — a mineral smell, like the air off a cave mouth. Their stolen lantern showed them a nave stripped of its icons, pews shoved against the walls to make a barracks, and, at the far end where the altar should have been, a dais of rough stone with a single iron cage upon it.

Tam sat inside the cage with his knees drawn up, and he was not crying, which broke Silas worse than crying would have. The boy was watching the dark with the particular stillness children learn when stillness is safer than noise. When the lantern light touched his face, his eyes went wide, and his mouth opened, and Silas got there fast enough to press a finger to his own lips before any sound escaped.

Tam nodded. Thirteen years old and he nodded like a soldier. Silas hated everyone responsible for that, starting with himself.

The cage lock was good work — Inquisition issue, a warded mechanism that would shriek like a murdered cat if you forced it. But Silas had spent twenty years among locks, and locks only knew what they'd been taught. He knelt, picked the warden's pocket of patience, and had the shackle open in ninety seconds that felt like ninety years.

Tam came out of the cage and did the thing Silas had been bracing against since the checkpoint: he threw his arms around Silas's waist and held on, face pressed into his coat, shaking. Silas stood there with his hands hovering, useless, and finally — awkwardly, like a man handling something holy and breakable — put one palm flat on the boy's back.

"All right," he said. "All right. We're going."

"He asked me questions," Tam whispered into the wool. "The lady with the grey eyes. She read my memories out loud so I could hear them. She wanted to know about you."

Ice, down the spine. "What did she learn?"

"That you're scared," Tam said simply. "That's all she said out loud. That you're scared, and that scared men either run or come back, and she wanted to see which."

* * *

They made it forty feet toward the north wall before the lights came up.

Not lanterns — the walls themselves, lines of pale script flaring along the stone like veins of lightning frozen mid-pulse. God-touched wards, and strong ones; Silas felt them the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. Every guard in the shrine shouted at once. Boots hammered on flagstones. And from the doorway of the vestry, unhurried, wrapped in a grey travelling cloak with her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a coach, came Inquisitor Vess.

"You came back," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way certain voices do, as though the room were listening to her specifically. "I confess I hoped you would. Scared men who come back are the useful kind."

"Give us the boy and we'll be gone," Silas said, putting himself between her and Tam and Wren both, arms loose, weight forward. Around the nave, six guards with drawn steel were closing like a slow fist. "You don't need him. You needed to know if I'd come. Now you know. Let that be enough."

"It's never enough." She tilted her head. "But you're wrong about what I need. Put your hand in your shirt, thief. Take out what you're carrying. Slowly."

His blood went cold. "You don't know what it is."

"A god's tongue-bone, from the tower at Vell's Crossing, missing since the priest there burned his own records and hanged himself with the bell rope." She recited it like a shopping list. "I know exactly what it is. And I know what it does in a place like this." She gestured, one finger, at the dome overhead with its thousand mortared teeth. "This was his house, thief. The god of Right Words. Dead these hundred years — or quiet, which amounts to the same. But dead gods leave bones, and bones remember being obeyed."

"You want me to use it," Silas said slowly. "Here. Why?"

"Because I've seen what the king is doing," Vess said, and for the first time something moved under the iron of her voice — something that might have been fear, or grief, or both wearing the same coat. "I've read the rite texts. I know what midwinter will cost. And I cannot stop it with warrants and interrogations. I need the relics gathered, and you have two of them, and you have a thief's talent for acquiring the third." Her eyes flicked to Tam, then back. "The boy was never the prize. You were. This—" she spread her hands at the closing ring of guards, the flaring wards, the whole trap of it— "was an interview."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the relics and the boy, and you can explain to him in ten years, if the world lasts, why you chose pride."

Behind him, Wren's breath caught. Silas didn't turn. He was doing arithmetic — six guards, one Inquisitor, wards on the walls, one exit — and the sum kept coming out the same ugly way it always did.

But his hand was already at his collar.

Because here was the thing Vess didn't understand, the thing nobody who'd grown up under open sky could understand: Silas Pemberton had spent his whole life in a valley where gods couldn't follow, trading in other people's pasts, keeping his own locked in boxes. He had never once in his life been *asked*. People had taken from him, bought from him, betrayed him, hunted him. Being asked — even by a woman holding a knife to everything he loved — landed in some starved part of him like rain on cracked ground.

"Say it plainly," he said. "What do you want me to do with the bone?"

"Command the guardian," Vess said. "Every shrine has one. The thing that kept this god's house when the god left. It sleeps under the floor, and it answers the tongue-bone, and I have prayed at this altar for eleven years and it has never once stirred for me." A pause. "I am not the right kind of voice. Perhaps you are. Wake it, command it to stand watch over this road until midwinter — to hold the pass against the king's hollowed men — and I will consider the debt between us settled, and the boy goes free tonight."

The guards stopped moving. Even the wards seemed to dim slightly, listening.

Silas looked at Wren. She looked back at him, and gave the smallest nod — *your call* — and he thought, absurdly, of the widow in chapter one of his life, the first memory he'd ever lifted, and how easy taking was compared to this.

He unwrapped the tongue-bone.

It was smaller out of the cloth than it felt against his chest — a curved white sliver, knee-length, dense as lead, carved all over with script too fine to read. Warmth poured off it like a hearthstone. And now that it was bare, now that it was *here*, in the house whose dome wore a thousand teeth, it began to hum — a low note Silas felt in his molars, in the roots of his hair, in the wet dark of his inner ear.

"Kneel at the altar," Vess said quietly. "Speak a command. Any command. If it hears you, it hears you."

Silas walked the length of the nave with every crossbow in the room tracking him, knelt on cold stone before an altar dedicated to a dead god of right words, and pressed the tongue-bone to the floor.

"Stand up," he said, feeling immediately like an idiot.

Nothing.

He closed his eyes. Thought about the bell tower, the mad priest, the way the bone had sung when Wren pulled it free. Thought about how the priest had screamed that the god only answered the honest. Thought — with a sour twist — about how much honesty cost a man like him, and how he'd been paying it all year anyway, in installments, whether he liked it or not.

"I'm a thief," he said to the floor, to the bone, to whatever slept underneath. "I've stolen from widows and orphans and kings. I stole a girl's childhood and sold it piecemeal and I'm only sorry now because it's convenient to be sorry. I'm scared. The boy in the cage said so and he was right. I don't want to save the world. I want to save three specific people and go home to a valley where none of this can follow me." His voice cracked on the last word, and he let it. "That's the truth. Do what you want with it."

The bone went silent.

Then the floor answered.

It began far away, deep under the hill — a grinding, a settling, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat after a century of sleep. Dust sifted from the rafters. The thousand teeth in the dome chattered softly, all together, like a jaw remembering how to work. Cracks spidered across the flagstones in widening rings around the altar, and through them rose a smell like a grave opened after rain, and something came up.

It was mostly hands. That was the horror of it — a body assembled entirely of grasping hands, hundreds of them, layered over a frame of fused finger-bones, rising out of the earth in a slow bloom, each palm opening and closing like a flower testing the air. Where a head should have been there was a knot of interlocked wrists, and set in the center of the knot, one human tooth, enormous, turning slowly to regard the room.

Every guard in the shrine found religion instantly and loudly. Two ran. One fainted. Vess alone stood her ground, and even she had gone the color of candle wax.

The guardian turned its tooth toward Silas.

"*Voice,*" it said, and the word came from everywhere — from the dome, from the floor, from the bone in Silas's hand, from somewhere behind his own eyes. "*A voice at last. A hundred years of silence. Say what you came to say.*"

Silas's mouth had gone dry as ash, but the words were already chosen; he'd said them once and they'd worked. "Hold this road. Hold this shrine. Let no man serve the empty crown pass this hill until midwinter comes. That's the command."

The great tooth rotated, considering. The hands flexed, a wave running through them like wind through wheat.

"*A command costs,*" the guardian said. "*All obedience costs. Name what you pay.*"

"I don't have anything left to—"

"*You do.*"

It reached for him. There was no hurrying it; the hands crossed the space between them with terrible patience, and Silas found he could not move — not bound, just *outweighed*, pinned by attention the way a moth is pinned by a lamp. One vast palm cupped the side of his face, gentle as a mother's, and pressed its fingertips to his temple, and Silas felt it *reading* him — flipping through him like a ledger, like Marlow's accounts, like a pickpocket's quick fingers through a stranger's pockets.

"*Here,*" it said, almost tenderly. "*This one. This scar. This is the price.*"

"What—"

Pain. White and total, a hot wire driven through his skull from ear to ear, and then — gone. Something gone. He felt the absence before he identified it, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue: a hole in the map of himself, smooth-walled, fresh. He grabbed for it blindly, desperately —

— and found a memory sealed shut behind glass, like all the others, the ones he'd stolen and boxed and sold. But this one was different. This one was *his*, native-born, and the guardian had written across it in letters of fire:

**MARKED. CLAIMED. MINE WHEN CALLED.**

He sagged. Hands caught him — Wren's, Tam's, both of them, hauling him upright. The guardian was sinking back into the floor, satisfied, the cracks sealing over it like water closing over a stone. The last thing he heard from it, faint, from very far down, was almost amused:

"*Midwinter, voice. Keep your appointments.*"

* * *

Vess kept her word. That was the worst part — that she kept it, briskly, without apology, ordering the guards to stand down, unlocking the armory herself to return Wren's confiscated pack, pressing travel bread and a flask of spirits into Tam's arms with the mechanical courtesy of a woman settling a bill.

"The guardian holds the pass," she said, standing in the shrine doorway as dawn greyed the east. "The king's riders won't come this way. You have a clear road to the capital and nineteen days. Don't waste them mourning whatever it took from you — mourn later, if there's a later." She paused. Her grey eyes moved over Silas's face with something that in a softer person might have been called concern. "What did it mark?"

"I don't know yet," Silas said. Which was true. Which was also a lie, because he could feel it — the sealed memory, the claimed one, sitting in his chest like a debt with a due date. He knew which memory it was. Of course he knew. There was only one he'd never been able to sell, never been able to look at, never been able to throw away.

The one of his mother's face.

They walked down the hill in the early light, three silhouettes on a frosted road, and behind them the Shrine of Teeth stood silent on its hill, its thousand teeth gleaming, its new guardian awake beneath the floor with its hundred hands folded, waiting.

Tam walked close to Silas and didn't ask questions, which was its own kind of mercy. Wren walked on his other side, and halfway down the hill she said, quietly, "You're limping on the inside. I can tell."

"Yeah," Silas said.

"When it calls in the debt," she said, "we'll be there."

He didn't answer. He was listening to the silence where the memory used to live, and in that silence — faint, patient, coming from the direction of the capital, from the direction of the crown — something had begun, very softly, to laugh.
