## 23. The Curse's Whisper

The mark on Silas's wrist looked like a bruise the color of old wine, shaped like a hand that had gripped too hard and left its fingers behind.

Three days since the Shrine of Teeth. Three days since the guardian's curse had sunk into him like a hook into wet clay, and in those three days he had learned to hate silence — because silence was when it spoke.

*You're awake,* said the voice inside his skull, warm as a hearth he'd never sat beside.

Silas kept walking. The road to the capital ran along a frozen river, and Wren was twenty paces ahead with Tam on her shoulders, laughing at something, and Vess rode behind with her hood up and her god-touched eyes closed, pretending sleep. Nobody else could hear it. He'd checked. He'd asked Wren, casually, whether she heard voices, and she'd given him a look that said *you first*, and he'd laughed, and the laugh had been the worst lie he'd ever told.

*I know you're awake,* the voice said again. *I can feel your heartbeat from here. It stutters when you're frightened. Yours always did.*

"Yours," Silas muttered under his breath. "We don't share anything."

*Don't we?*

He stopped at the riverbank, ostensibly to fill the waterskin, actually because if he didn't put distance between himself and Tam's laughter he was going to do something stupid, like answer out loud. He crouched at the ice-edged water and let his mind go flat and gray, the way he did when lifting a memory — empty shelves, swept floors, nothing worth taking.

The voice sighed, patient as winter.

*You can't shut me out by being hollow, thief. I was born hollow. It's my native country. You're only visiting.*

Silas filled the skin. His hands were steady. He was proud of his hands.

*Ask me something,* the voice offered. *Everyone wants to ask me something eventually. The inquisitors who used to serve me before they stopped knowing they served me. The priests. The kings of small countries who come to kneel. They all want to ask: what are you? And I tell them the truth, and they go mad, and I replace them with someone quieter. But you — you might survive the truth. You've survived worse. You survived yourself for forty years.*

"I'm thirty-one," Silas said to the water.

*Are you? How would you know? You've sold so much of yourself you're mostly other people's leftovers stitched into a coat.*

He stood. He walked back to the road. He did not answer, and the silence that followed had teeth in it, and he carried it all day like a stone in his boot.

* * *

That night they made camp in the lee of a ruined waystation, and Wren built the fire while Tam collected sticks with the seriousness of a general provisioning an army, and Vess sat apart as always, sharpening a knife that never dulled. Silas watched them and felt the mark pulse gently, like a second heart learning his rhythm.

*They love you,* the voice said, wonderingly. *Isn't that remarkable? I've read your memories — the ones you kept, the scraps — and there's no precedent for it. No one has ever loved you before without paying you first.*

"Shut up."

*Make me.*

He almost laughed. That was the horror of it, the thing he hadn't told anyone: the voice wasn't cruel. Not exactly. It was *familiar*. It talked to him the way he talked to himself at three in the morning, when the fire was low and the lies ran out. It knew where the soft places were because it was made of soft places.

Wren looked over. "You said something?"

"Nothing."

"You're talking to yourself a lot lately."

"He's been doing that for years," Vess said without opening her eyes. "Thieves rehearse."

Tam grinned. "Rehearsing what? His apology to every person he's ever robbed? That'd take all night."

"It would," Silas said, "take several lifetimes," and Tam laughed, and the laugh landed somewhere in Silas's chest and stayed there, glowing, and the mark drank the glow like wine.

*Oh,* the voice said softly. *Oh, that one hurts. Keep him close. I want to watch.*

* * *

It came for him properly on the fourth night, in his mother's voice.

He was on watch. The others slept in a row like spoons, Wren's arm around Tam, and Silas sat with his back against the waystation wall and let himself drift — a mistake, he knew it was a mistake even as he did it, but exhaustion is a door left unlatched.

*Silas.*

His mother's voice. Not remembered — remembered would have been bearable, remembered would have been his own rotting archive — but *present*, coming through the mark the way sound comes through a wall, muffled and real. Warm. A little rough at the edges, the way hers had been after the fever.

"Stop it." His whisper cracked. "Stop it right now."

*You have flour on your sleeve, love. Honestly, you'd lose your head—*

"She's dead." He said it fast, before the warmth could get in. "She died when I was nine. I sold the memory of her face when I was nineteen to buy passage out of Casterhale, and I don't remember her face anymore, so whatever you're wearing, it isn't her. It's a costume. Take it off."

A pause. When the voice came back it was still her voice, but the warmth had drained out of it, leaving something older underneath, something patient and vast and terribly interested.

*There it is,* it said. *There's the sharp little knife you use on everything, including yourself. Do you know how I know these things about you, Silas? Truly? Guess.*

"You stole from me. Same trade I'm in."

*Guess again.*

"I don't play guessing games with monsters."

*But you do.* Almost gentle now. *You've been playing one your whole life. Every memory you ever lifted, you told yourself you weren't taking anything that mattered. Every client, every widow, every grieving man — you told yourself comfort was comfort no matter whose hands delivered it. You've spent thirty-one years guessing at what you are instead of looking. I'm not going to make you guess anymore. I'm going to show you. Soon. Very soon. And then you'll understand why the crown fits me so well.*

"Why?" The word came out of him before he could stop it, raw, and he hated himself for it, hated the hunger in it. "Why does it fit?"

*Because it was measured for both of us,* the voice said, and then it laughed — her laugh, his mother's laugh, cut short like a string snapped — and withdrew, and left him alone with the snow and the sleeping people who trusted him, and the mark on his wrist beating quietly, patiently, like something waiting to be fed.

* * *

In the palace at Carreth, a thousand miles north, the thing wearing King Aldric's body stood at a window and watched the snow come down over a city of bells.

It had been a long day of being a king. The council had met; it had smiled at the correct moments; it had signed papers ordering the hollowing of two more border towns, because the rite required quiet roads and quiet roads required quiet people. In the evening it had dined alone, tasting nothing, and dismissed the servants, and stood here, watching snow.

It did not need to watch snow. It did not need anything. But the body it wore had habits, and habits were useful camouflage, and besides — watching things was how it thought.

*He refused to guess,* it observed to itself, in no voice at all. *Good. Refusal means the question is alive in him. A living question grows toward its answer like a plant toward light.*

It turned its attention inward, down the thin dark thread the guardian's curse had tied between them, and felt the thief at the far end: cold, exhausted, sitting upright on watch rather than wake anyone by moving. Stubborn. Careful. Frightened in the specific way of a man who has realized the monster knows his name.

It had expected fear. Fear was the default instrument of human beings, the way bells were the default instrument of this city. What it had not expected — what it found itself turning over like a coin of unfamiliar minting — was the boy.

Tam. The pilgrim child who believed the thief was a hero from a story.

The thing wearing the king had read ten thousand memories of children. Children appeared constantly in the memories it harvested: children remembered, children grieved, children were the soft cargo everyone carried. But it had never been *in* a memory of being loved by a child. It had never been on the receiving end of that particular current. Tonight, riding the link, it had felt Tam's laugh land in Silas's chest, and for 1.5 seconds — it counted everything, seconds were its native currency — the thing wearing the king had experienced something adjacent to envy.

Interesting, it thought. Note it. Set it aside. There will be time after midwinter.

Because after midwinter there would be no gods, and no magic, and no memories that walked around outside their owners' heads. After midwinter the world would be a clean machine: bodies, bread, order, silence. It had explained this to Vess once, years ago, in a dream it had planted in her praying sleep, and she had built an entire Inquisition on the blueprint without ever seeing the architect. Humans were wonderful that way. Give them a fear and a flag and they'd build you a cathedral.

And yet.

It looked at its hands — Aldric's hands, broad, scarred, a swordsman's hands — and felt the old itch, the one it never named because naming gave things power, though gods were dying precisely because it had learned their names, so perhaps naming gave *it* power too, and it should reconsider.

The itch was this: the thief was *like* it. Made of the same material. Cut from the same original cloth. And the thing wearing the king had spent eleven years among humans now, wearing their faces and signing their papers, and it had developed theories — untested, unwelcome theories — about what happened to a thing made of stolen memory when it stayed too long among the people the memories came from.

It was starting to enjoy snow. That was the beginning of the problem. It was starting to *enjoy* things.

Kill the thief, then. Before midwinter. Before the link could teach either of them anything true.

Or —

Or bring him here. Bring him close. See what it felt like to stand next to the only creature in the world made of the same stuff, and find out whether that was kinship or hunger.

It smiled with Aldric's mouth, which had been famous once for its kindness, and decided: both. Both was possible. Both was usually true.

*Sleep well, Silas,* it said down the thread, in the mother's voice, because it had learned long ago that humans heard truth best when it came dressed as comfort. *Only six weeks now.*

* * *

Six weeks, Silas thought, staring at the fire's last coals. Six weeks until midwinter, four hundred miles still to ride, one relic short, one inquisitor of uncertain allegiance, one orphan girl who deserved better than him, one boy who thought he was a hero — and a voice in his skull that knew his heartbeat and wore his mother's laugh.

He pressed his thumb hard against the mark, as if he could bruise it silent.

*That doesn't work,* the voice murmured, half-asleep itself now, drowsy as a child. *Nothing works. But I'll tell you a secret, since you won't guess. You want to know why I chose her voice? Of all the voices I could wear?*

Silas said nothing. The coals ticked.

*Because I remember her,* the voice said. *Her face. Her hands. The song she sang when the fever came. I remember everything you sold, Silas. Everything you ever took and traded away — it all came through me first. I am where your memories went.*

The fire settled. Somewhere behind him, Tam murmured in his sleep and Wren tightened her arm around him, and Silas sat very still in the cold, doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish:

If the voice remembered his mother — truly remembered, held what he had lost — then the voice wasn't just wearing her.

The voice was *made of her*. Made of the stolen shape of her, the sold shape of her, some seed of his own childhood grown monstrous in the dark for twenty years.

Which meant the thing wearing the crown of Carreth, the thing that would unmake every god in the world at midwinter, was — there was no gentler word for it, and Silas had always been good at finding the gentle words, and this once none came —

Was his.

He looked at the mark on his wrist, and the mark, patient as a hook, looked back.
