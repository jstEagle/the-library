## 24. The Impostor's Truth

The whispering started again at dusk.

Silas sat apart from the fire, the way he'd sat apart from every fire since the Shrine of Teeth, turning a strip of dried mutton he had no intention of eating. Across the camp, Wren was teaching Tam to whittle — badly, on both counts — and Vess stood watch at the treeline with her sword drawn and her god-sense open, which was as close to an apology as she would ever offer.

*You're brooding,* said the voice in his skull. It wore his mother's tone tonight. It always tried his mother's tone first.

"Get out of my head."

*I can't. You made sure of that when you let that guardian scratch you.* A pause, warm and patient, the way she used to be before the fever took her. *Besides, why would I leave? We're only getting acquainted.*

He'd learned three things about the link in the four days since the shrine. First: it went both ways, and the thing on the other end could read the surface of his thoughts like a man reading headlines in a shop window. Second: silence fed it. It wanted him talking, thinking, remembering. Third — and this was the one that scared him — it never lied.

It exaggerated. It seduced. But in four days of listening, Silas Pemberton, professional liar, had not caught it in a single outright falsehood.

That was worse than any lie.

"You want something," he said quietly, under the crackle of the fire, where Wren couldn't hear. "You've been circling for days. Whatever it is, say it."

*Direct. Good.* The mother-voice receded like a tide going out, and what came in behind it was neither male nor female, young nor old — just a voice, smooth as river stone. *I want to show you something. A memory. Yours, technically.*

"I don't have memories anymore. That's rather the point of me."

*You have so many more than you think.* And then it pushed.

---

Not pain. That was the horror of it. The curse-link didn't hurt; it *opened*, the way a key opens a door you didn't know your house had. Silas felt himself tip forward into a scene that was not a scene, because he had no vial for it, no glass, no market stall — just raw remembering, unmediated, the way ordinary people carried their pasts around every day without ever noticing the weight.

A kitchen. Low ceiling, black beams, a smell of tallow and boiled barley. He was small. He knew he was small by the height of the table, by the way he had to reach up for the latch. His hands were small and grubby and there was a splinter in the left thumb he kept worrying at with his teeth.

His mother stood at the hearth with her back to him, singing.

He couldn't see her face. He noted this with the strange calm of a dreamer — the face was turned away, or shadowed, or simply refused to resolve, and some part of him understood that this wasn't the curse's doing. This was the memory itself. This was how it had always been.

She was singing a lullaby about a miller's daughter and a fox, and she was singing it wrong, skipping the third verse, and small-Silas knew all the words and loved her for getting them wrong because it meant the song belonged to them now, their own broken version, theirs—

The door opened.

Cold air. Snow-light. A man filled the doorway, and even through the child's eyes Silas recognized the silhouette — the broad shoulders, the crooked left knee, the hat pulled low. Corvus. The man who had found him begging in Casterhale at nine years old and taught him fingers, patience, and the difference between stealing and *taking*. The man who had sold him his first lesson in memory-work with the words *the mind is just another pocket, boy, and most people never check them.*

In the memory, Corvus smiled at his mother. She smiled back. She knew him — that was the detail that made adult-Silas's stomach drop, because she *knew him*, greeted him by name, set out a third cup, and Silas understood with sickening clarity that this was not a stranger come to rob them. This was a friend of the house. This was someone his mother trusted.

This was how he'd gotten in.

*Watch,* said the voice, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

Corvus drank the tea. Talked. Laughed at something. And then he leaned across the table while the mother bent to lift a pot, and he did it — the technique Silas himself would later use ten thousand times, refined, softened, made nearly painless. Two fingers to the temple. A breath. A pull.

Except Corvus didn't take a memory from the mother.

He reached past her. He reached for the *boy*.

Small-Silas felt it happen and couldn't stop it — a coolness at the crown of his skull, like snowmelt trickling down, and then a piece of him simply *left*. Not a memory of anything. Not an event, not a face, not a name. Something deeper. Something structural. The boy blinked, and the world looked exactly the same, and nothing was wrong, and everything was wrong, because there was a hole in him now shaped like something he'd never get to learn the name of.

Corvus sealed it into a vial. Smiled at the mother. Kissed her hand, gods help him, kissed her hand like a courtier, and left into the snow.

And the memory ended — but the voice didn't release him.

*Do you understand what he took?*

Silas came back to the fire so hard he bit his tongue. The strip of mutton had fallen into the dirt. His hands were shaking. Across the camp, Wren looked up, frowning, and he shook his head at her — *fine, fine* — and she went back to the whittling, unconvinced.

"What did he take?" he asked the dark. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You pushed that at me for a reason. Say it."

*A seed,* said the impostor king. *He took a seed.*

---

Vess relieved him at midnight and he walked the perimeter alone, which was stupid, and he did it anyway, because the thing in his head wanted privacy too and it was easier to hate yourself with no audience.

*When Corvus died,* the voice said, companionable, walking beside him though there was nothing there, *he left an estate. Debts, mostly. And inventory. Forty years of stolen memories in sealed vials, catalogued like wine. You inherited the trade; you never asked what happened to the stock.*

"I burned it." He remembered that night clearly — one of the few nights he still owned. Corvus's back room, the brazier, the smell of burning glass and the strange sweet smoke of evaporated lives. He'd been twenty. He'd told himself it was mercy.

*You burned almost all of it.* A pause, savoring. *One vial you kept. The oldest one. Unlabeled. You told yourself you were saving it to study the old man's technique. You told yourself a great many things about that vial, Silas. You kept it eleven years.*

He stopped walking.

Because it was true. There was a vial. There had always been a vial, riding in the false bottom of his case, moving from hideout to hideout, from the Hollow to Casterhale and back, the one piece of inventory he had never sold and never opened and never quite managed to destroy. He'd stopped thinking about it years ago the way you stop thinking about a debt — not because it's gone, but because thinking about it is unbearable.

"The Hollow," he said slowly. "Magic can't follow into the Hollow. Memory-work is just magic wearing gloves. So if a vial sat in the valley long enough—"

*Nothing,* the voice agreed. *It went dormant. Dormant isn't dead. And then, six years ago, the gods began to stir, and dormant things began to wake, and a seed of stolen childhood — a piece of a boy's soul, cut out whole, steeped forty years in longing — woke up in a valley where nothing else could touch it. And it was hungry, Silas. Do you know what a discarded memory wants?*

"No," he said. "Tell me."

*To be lived.* The voice cracked, just slightly, and underneath the crack was something young. Something nine years old. *It wants a body. It wants a name. It wants the life it was cut away from. It spent six years growing in the dark of your valley, wearing shapes it stole from dreams that drifted too close to the border — and then it found the king. A king who prayed, one midwinter, to be rid of his grief. And it answered. It climbed into the grief and hollowed him out and put itself in the space, and it has been trying to finish itself ever since. Every rite. Every relic. Every god it means to bind and unmake — do you know why?*

Silas stood very still in the cold. Somewhere behind him, the campfire spat.

"Why?"

*Because a thing built from one memory cannot hold a whole soul. It leaks. It needs more. It thought more memories would fill it — it stole thousands, hollowed hundreds — and none of it worked, because you cannot patch a hole with other people's cloth. There is only one thing in the world that fits the shape of what Corvus cut away.* The voice dropped to almost nothing. Almost tender. *The rest of the boy it came from.*

The wind moved through the trees. Silas became aware, distantly, that he was gripping his own wrist hard enough to bruise.

"That's not possible," he said. "I'm not missing anything. I'd know."

*Would you?* A soft laugh. *You're a thief who sells forgetting. You built a career on people not knowing what they'd lost. You think you'd do better? You feel it, though. Don't you. All those years. The sense that you were carrying a package with a hole in the bottom. Why do you think you never opened the vial? Some part of you knew. Some part of you has always known that if you looked inside, you'd find the shape of your own absence staring back.*

He thought of the widow in chapter one of his own life — no. He thought of the widow, weeks ago, buying back her grief. He thought of Wren on his doorstep, knowing something was taken and not knowing what. He thought of every client, every vial, every careful refusal to feel anything about any of it, and the architecture of his whole wry, detached, survivable life suddenly looked like what it had always been:

A man walking very fast away from a room he couldn't afford to enter.

"You're lying," he said.

*I don't lie. I told you.* And then, quieter, in the mother's voice again, in the broken lullaby version, the one with the third verse skipped: *Come to the capital, Silas. Come to the rite. Bring the relics — I know you have two, and I know where you'll dig for the third. Finish it with me. Bind the gods, take the crown, and I'll give it back. Everything Corvus took. You'll be whole for the first time since you were nine years old. Or don't come, and stop me, and spend the rest of your life wondering what I could have given you.*

*Either way,* it said, and here the voice settled into something horribly reasonable, horribly familiar, horribly like his own, *you already know the truth now. You can't unknow it. Isn't that what you people say in the Hollow? Knowledge is a burden?*

*Welcome to mine.*

The link closed like a door.

---

He didn't remember walking back to camp. He was sitting by the dead fire when the sky went grey, and Wren found him there, wrapped in his coat, staring at his own hands.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"No."

"It talked to you again." Not a question. She'd learned to read the signs — the tilted head, the half-heard answers, the way he flinched from kindness like it might be the voice wearing kindness's clothes. She sat down across from him. Tam was still asleep, curled against Vess's sleeping roll, because children sleep anywhere and trust anyone, and Silas envied him with his whole hollow chest.

"Wren." His mouth was dry. "If I told you something about me. Something bad. Something that — that explains what's hunting us, maybe. Explains why it hunts *me*." He stopped. Started again. "Would it change how you look at me?"

She considered him for a long moment. Firelight was gone; there was only dawn, grey and honest, and her face in it — young, scarred at the eyebrow from a fall he'd watched her take two winters ago, a face he'd helped empty once and had never forgiven himself for.

"I spent six years knowing something was missing," she said, "and everyone telling me I was fine. Whole. Nothing wrong with me at all." She shrugged. "So no. Whatever it is — tell me true, and we'll carry it. That's the deal now. That's what the road is."

He opened his mouth.

And the link flared — brief, distant, amused, like a knock from across a city: *Midwinter, Silas. Sixteen days. Don't be late for yourself.*

—and was gone.

Silas closed his mouth. Looked at Wren's open, waiting face. Looked east, toward the capital, where a thing wearing a king's body was waiting to hand him back his own soul at a price he couldn't yet count.

"There's something I have to tell you," he said. "All of it. Before we go another mile."
