## 25. Confession by Firelight

Wren had learned to read Silas the way other people read weather. The set of his shoulders meant rain. A certain tightness around his mouth meant he'd lied to someone that day and hadn't enjoyed it. And when he went quiet — truly quiet, not the performing quiet of a man pretending to think, but the hollowed-out silence of a man trying to disappear — it meant something was coming.

She'd been watching him go quiet for three days.

Since the curse-mark had bloomed on his forearm like a bruise in the shape of a hand, since he'd started flinching at nothing, staring into the middle distance with the expression of a man listening to a voice no one else could hear. Since he'd woken her twice in the night, once shouting a name she didn't recognize — *Mother* — and once saying nothing at all, just sitting up with his eyes open and his breathing wrong.

Tam had noticed too. The boy had stopped sitting next to Silas at the fire, which for Tam was an act of enormous discipline, and had started asking Wren questions instead. *Is he sick? Is it the curse? Are we going to die?* She'd answered honestly: I don't know, probably, eventually, everyone does. He'd accepted this with the terrible resilience of children and gone back to whittling a stick into something that was either a horse or a knife.

But tonight there were no fires to sit beside. They were holed up in the ruin of a shepherd's cot two days south of the shrine, off the road, off every road, because Silas had insisted — *the king can find me when my mind is quiet, so we keep it loud* — and loud meant movement, noise, Tam's endless chatter, Wren singing badly on purpose. Tonight Tam had finally worn himself out and slept in the corner like a folded animal, stick-horse under his arm, and the wind was worrying the loose shutter, and Silas sat across from her with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he hadn't drunk, going quiet.

The bad kind of quiet.

"Say it," Wren said.

Silas looked up. "What?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for three days. Say it or stop making faces at your tea."

He almost smiled. It didn't take. "It's not — " He stopped. Started again. "There's a thing I have to tell you, and after I tell you, you're going to want to leave. Or worse. And I'd deserve both."

"That's a promising opening."

"Wren." His voice cracked on her name, and she felt something in her chest tilt, because Silas Pemberton did not crack. Silas Pemberton was all angles and deflection, a man who could pick the lock on a conversation faster than a strongbox. "Please. Let me get through it."

So she let him. She pulled her knees up and waited, and outside the wind kept working the shutter like a patient thief testing a lock, and somewhere far off a nightbird called three times and got no answer.

"I stole from you," Silas said.

"I know. You've told me. My childhood, when I was eight. You've apologized — badly — about six times."

"No." He set the cup down. His hands weren't steady. She'd never seen his hands unsteady. "I mean yes, but not — I keep telling it small because small is survivable. So here's the whole of it, unsurvivable size." He breathed out through his nose. "You came to me first. Before the Hollow. You were seven, maybe eight, and you'd walked out of a burning house in Casterhale with nothing but a burn scar and a name you couldn't remember, and a man named Corvo found you before I did. Corvo was my teacher. He taught me the trade — lifting memories, bottling them, selling them. And he taught me that the best stock is fresh grief, because grief keeps its flavor."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Go on."

"You were full of it. Whatever happened in that fire, whatever you lost — it was the richest memory I'd ever touched. Corvo wanted to drain you completely. Take everything, leave you a shell, sell you by the piece. And I — " Silas laughed, a horrible sound, no humor in it anywhere. "I told myself I was being merciful. I took only part. Your mother's face. Your father's voice. The smell of your old home. The day of the fire itself. I left you enough to be a person, and I told myself that made me good, and then I sold what I took and used the money to buy my way into the Hollow, where nothing could ever follow me. Including guilt. I thought."

The wind shoved the shutter. Somewhere in the dark, Tam stirred and settled.

"The vial," Wren said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away. "The knight's vial. What the king's memory showed us. The thing of smoke and borrowed faces."

"It wasn't the king's memory. Not originally." Silas met her eyes, and she wished he hadn't; there was nothing behind his composure now, just the raw mechanism of a man dismantling himself in front of her. "Corvo didn't just teach me theft. He harvested me. When I was young. He took pieces of my life and sold them, same as I did to you, and one of the pieces he took was — " His jaw worked. "A memory of my mother. Of her telling me a story by a fire, when I was small. It was the truest thing I owned. He sold it to a collector, and collectors do things with memories, Wren. Old rites. Forbidden ones. Someone grew that memory into flesh. Fed it years of shadow and ambition and borrowed faces until it stood up wearing a crown and calling itself Aldric."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped down a well.

"The impostor king," Wren said slowly, "is made of your childhood."

"A piece of it. The best piece." He rubbed the curse-mark through his sleeve, unconsciously, the way a man touches a wound. "That's why it speaks to me in her voice. That's why it knows things about me no one should know. It isn't hunting me because I have the relics. It's hunting me because I'm its — " He couldn't finish. He tried again. "I'm the reason it exists. Everything it does, everyone it hollows, every town full of smiling people — that traces back to a choice I made in a gutter in Casterhale when I was nineteen and hungry and wanted to believe mercy was the same as theft with better manners."

Wren sat with it.

She was surprised by how quiet her own mind was. She'd expected rage — had been carrying rage for months, actually, ever since the knock at his door, ever since she'd learned something had been taken, a hole shaped like a childhood with her name on the bottom of it. She'd imagined this confession a hundred times, usually ending with her walking out into the rain and never looking back.

Instead she found herself asking: "My mother's face. You have it?"

His face went grey. "Had. I traded a memory to the drowned god in Casterhale for the relic's location. It took — " He stopped. "I don't remember her face anymore. Yours, I mean. Your mother's. I remember that I took it. I remember the shape of the taking. The contents are gone."

"So we're even," Wren said. "Neither of us remembers her."

"That's not — " Silas's voice broke clean through. "Gods, Wren, that's not the same thing at all. You never had a choice. I chose every step of it. I chose it and I profited from it and I ran from it, and the running is why we're here, in a ruined hut, hiding from a monster I made, with a boy asleep in the corner who thinks I'm worth imitating." He dragged a hand down his face. "I've spent twenty years building a man who doesn't feel anything about any of this. It worked. That was the problem. It worked so well I hollowed myself out ahead of schedule, and now there's barely anything left to lose, and I keep thinking that's convenient, and then I look at you and Tam and I realize it isn't convenience, it's just another kind of empty."

The firelight moved on his face. Outside, the wind had finally given up on the shutter, or won.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Wren asked. "Not back in the valley. Not on the road. Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow we start for the capital, and midwinter is close, and if I die out there I wanted one person to know the truth of me. Not the fence's version, not the guild's version. The actual inventory." He spread his hands. "Everything I took. Everything it cost. All of it, laid out, so that when they bury me, somebody buries the right man."

"And if you don't die?"

"Then you'll know exactly who you're traveling with, and you can decide what that's worth."

Wren unfolded herself and crossed the room. She sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, and the silence was different from his silences — it was a shared silence, load-bearing, the kind you build things on.

"When I knocked on your door," she said, "in the Hollow. Do you remember what I said?"

"Every word. I've replayed it more times than any memory I own."

"I said I didn't remember my childhood, but I knew something was missing. Like a tooth. You feel the gap with your tongue all day." She turned to look at him. "Here's what I never told you. After you stole from me — after, when I was eight and alone in the world with a hole in my head — I survived anyway. Somebody fed me. Somebody taught me letters. Somebody found me a place in a caravan and I grew up and became a person who could walk into a stranger's shop and demand answers. That girl existed because of what you left, Silas. Not what you took. What you *left*. You drew a line at shell and stopped there, and that line is the only reason I'm sitting here able to hear this and not bleeding out from it."

"That doesn't forgive — "

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't. Forgiveness isn't a debt you cancel, it's a debt you choose to carry differently." She took his hand — the marked one — and turned it over, and looked at the bruise-shaped curse without flinching. "You're going to pay for what you did. Not to me. To the world. You're going to pay it in the capital, in front of that thing wearing your mother's story, and it's going to cost more than money or comfort or maybe everything. And I'm going to be standing next to you when you pay it, because that's what family does — and make no mistake, thief, you built one whether you meant to or not. Me. Tam. Even Vess, gods help us. You keep collecting people and pretending you haven't."

Silas made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed.

"I spent my whole life," he said quietly, "thinking truth was just another thing you could steal. Something you lifted out of people and sold back in pieces. I never once gave it away free."

"What's it feel like?"

He considered. "Lighter than I expected. And heavier. Both at once. Like setting down a weight and picking up a responsibility."

"That's the trade," Wren said. "Everyone thinks honesty costs. It doesn't. It *invests*." She squeezed his hand and let go. "Now sleep. Real sleep. Tomorrow we walk toward the worst city in the world, and I need you sharp, and you can't be sharp on three days of listening to your mother's ghost."

He lay down by the dead fire without argument, which frightened her more than anything he'd said — Silas Pemberton, surrendering watchfulness — and within minutes his breathing slowed into something like peace.

Wren stayed awake a while longer, watching the dark window, turning the confession over in her mind like a stone found on a beach. *The impostor is grown from his memory.* Which meant the thing in the capital knew Silas the way a scar knows the wound. Which meant it would offer him things. Terrible, perfect, tailored things.

And which meant, she realized with a cold slide of understanding, that when the moment came — the confrontation, the rite, the unbinding — the price would not be paid in steel.

It would be paid in memory. In the last true thing Silas had left.

She looked at him sleeping, this hollowed man who had given her back the shape of herself without knowing it, and she made a quiet promise to the dark: *whatever it asks of you, you won't face the asking alone.*

Far to the north, past the hills and the sleeping pilgrim roads, the bells of the capital began to ring midnight — though no wind carried that far, and no bell stood within fifty miles.

All three of them heard it anyway.

Tam sat up in the corner, eyes wide, stick-horse forgotten.

"He knows we're coming," the boy whispered.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, moving fast and quiet through the grey light, and by the time the sun cleared the hills they could see it on the horizon: the capital, a smudge of towers and smoke, crowned in festival bunting visible even from miles out. Banners for the midwinter blessing. Thousands of pilgrims funneling toward the gates like blood toward a wound.

Silas stood at the crest of the ridge and looked at the city for a long time.

"She's in there," he said. Not the impostor. *She.* His mother's voice, wearing a crown.

"We're all in there," Wren said, and shouldered her pack. "Let's go give the gods back their names."

They walked down toward the bells.
