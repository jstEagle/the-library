## 26. The Capital of Bells

We heard the capital before we saw it.

That was the first wrong thing. Cities announce themselves with smoke, with stink, with the glitter of a thousand windows catching the low sun. Vellharrow announced itself with sound — a deep, rolling thunder that came up through the soles of my boots and settled in my teeth like a stone I couldn't spit out.

Bells.

Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, ringing from every tower in a city that sprawled across the river plain like a spilled inkpot. Not ringing for joy. Ringing *at* something. The tone was tuned, deliberate, the way a man hums to drown out a voice in the next room.

"They're smothering it," Wren said beside me on the cart bench. She had her hood up and her hands buried in the straw around our belongings, as if she could feel what I felt. "The gods' voices. The bells are smothering them."

She'd been hearing them since the eye-stone showed us the rite. Six weeks ago she'd started flinching at nothing, pressing her palm flat against her ear like she was trying to keep something in rather than out. Out here on the pilgrim road, three days from the walls, she said the voices had grown from whispers into a crowd. Prayers, she called them. Millions of prayers, all rising off the world like heat off a summer road, and somewhere above them, things that heard.

The bells were meant to make the city deaf to all of it.

I kept my eyes on the road and my hand near the false bottom of the cart, where three relics of dead-or-dying gods rode wrapped in beggar's wool between a bundle of turnips and Tam's spare shirt. A tongue-bone that let you command what should not be commanded. An eye-stone that showed you what you'd pay never to see. And beneath them both, wrapped thickest of all, a heart the size of a fist that beat when it wanted to, slow and patient, like something sleeping in a cellar and dreaming of waking.

Every bell in Vellharrow was ringing to drown out voices like those.

"Smile," I told Wren. "You're a pilgrim. Pilgrims are grateful."

"I'm not grateful."

"You are now. Grateful people don't get searched."

Tam, wedged between us among the turnips, looked up at me with the naked faith that still made my chest hurt if I let it. "Is it true there's ten thousand bells?"

"There's one bell," I said. "It's very large and it's everywhere. That's how they get you."

He laughed like I'd said something wonderful. Children are easy audiences. It's adults who want the truth, and even then only in small doses, cut thin, like medicine.

* * *

The road funneled toward the Southgate through a river of pilgrims — whole villages on foot, flagellants with their backs laid open and scabbed, fat merchants doing penance on horseback, a troupe of mummers performing the Martyrdom of Saint Esselt on a wagon stage while the actress playing the saint died beautifully every quarter mile. Everyone was filthy, festive, and terrified. Midwinter festival did that to people. You came to the king's blessing because not coming was worse.

The Inquisition knew it too. They had the gate strung like a fishnet.

Three checkpoints, each worse than the last. At the first, gray-cloaked clerics walked the lines with censers, swinging smoke that smelled of cold iron and old snow. Where the smoke curled away from a person, they pulled that person aside. I watched a woman dragged from the queue for nothing more than the way the smoke bent around her shoulders, and I watched the crowd close ranks behind her without a word, everyone suddenly fascinated by their own boots. That's what fear looks like in bulk. It looks like politeness.

At the second checkpoint they took memories. Not all of them — just tithe. A cleric with kind eyes and a silver stylus touched your temple, skimmed the top layer, and handed you a receipt. *Paid in full. Blessed be.* People wept and thanked him. Somewhere in the palace vaults, I supposed, sat shelves of other people's best days, filed and catalogued, and I felt a kinship with the place so ugly it made my stomach turn. I'd been running from what I was since Casterhale burned. Here was an entire city built on my trade, wearing vestments.

"Silas." Wren's voice, low. "Your face."

"What about it?"

"You look like home."

I fixed my eyes on the middle distance and thought about turnips until the expression passed.

The third checkpoint was the gate itself, and the gate was where the bells lived. They hung in the twin towers flanking the archway — two monsters of green bronze, taller than the towers that held them somehow, though I never worked out the geometry and decided not to try. When they swung, the sound didn't pass through you. It passed *over* you, the way weather does, and left a silence behind it shaped exactly like yourself.

Under that silence, I could feel the relics in the cart go quiet. The tongue-bone stopped its faint dry whispering. The eye-stone went blind and dumb. Even the heart slowed its beating, hunkering down under the bronze thunder like a fox under a hawk's shadow.

Good, I thought. Stay down. We're nearly through.

A cleric in white over gray stopped our cart. Young. Tired around the eyes in the way of someone working his fourth festival shift. He looked at Wren, at Tam, at me, at the turnips.

"Purpose in the city?"

"Pilgrimage," I said, and produced the travel-chit we'd bought off a dying man outside Marlow's old district, back when Marlow was still alive and still a traitor. "For the king's blessing. My sister's boy has never seen the bells."

The cleric glanced at Tam. Tam, bless him, gaped up at the towers with his mouth actually open, which required no acting whatsoever.

"Open the cart."

My pulse did not speed up. That's the trick I learned young: the body panics, you let it, and you stand apart from it like a man watching weather come in. I climbed down and folded back the canvas, and the cleric leaned in over the turnips and the straw and the shirts, and beneath all of it, under three feet of false floor that had cost me a week's work and two bribes, the heart of a god lay still as a stone.

The cleric reached into the straw. His fingers found Tam's spare shirt. He lifted it, sniffed it, made a face.

"Boy smells like a goat," he said.

"He's fond of goats," I said.

Something flickered behind the cleric's eyes — not suspicion. Recognition, almost. Like he'd heard a joke once, long ago, and couldn't remember the punchline. Then the bells overhead swung together, both monsters at once, and the sound fell on us like a wave, and whatever he'd been about to think drowned in it.

"Move along," he said. "Keep your chit. Blessing lines form at dawn on midwinter eve. Don't be late. The king doesn't wait."

"The king doesn't wait," I agreed, and drove the cart through the archway into Vellharrow.

Only when the bells' shadow had faded behind us did I realize my hands were shaking on the reins. Wren noticed too. She put her hand over mine, brief and warm, and said nothing, which is the finest thing anyone ever did for me.

* * *

The city inside the walls was a machine for forgetting.

I mean that as craft, not complaint. I've spent my life in the memory trade; I know architecture when I see it. The streets of Vellharrow were laid out in spirals, so that no matter which way you walked, you always seemed to be circling back toward the center, toward the palace on its hill, toward the crown. Every corner held a shrine — but the shrines were empty. Not ruined. *Emptied.* Scrubbed, whitewashed, their niches vacant, their offering-bowls polished and dry. Someone had unmade the gods here with mops and lime and good honest labor, and the work had been thorough, and the work had been going on a long time.

And above it all, the bells. Always the bells. They rang on the hour, the half-hour, the quarter, and at odd unscheduled moments besides, so that you could never settle into any thought deeper than a puddle. I watched a street preacher open his mouth to speak by a fountain and get flattened mid-sentence by a peal from the cathedral, standing there blinking like a man who'd walked into a door he couldn't see. Nobody listened to anybody for longer than a minute. The whole city had the attention span of a startled bird.

"It's a prison," Wren said quietly, as we creaked through a market square where festival lanterns were going up on poles. "They built a prison and called it a capital."

"It's a muzzle," I said. "Prisons hold one person. This holds everything."

Tam stood up in the cart to see the palace. It crowned the hill at the city's heart, white-walled, bannered, beautiful in the way of a knife — and I felt the heart beneath the floorboards answer it. One slow beat. Then another. Like recognizing like. The impostor's anchor and the god's heart, calling to each other across a mile of rooftops, and I put my boot heel against the boards as if that would do anything, and kept smiling at the lanterns.

We lodged that night at the Drowned Lantern, a pilgrims' house in the tanners' quarter where nobody asked names and the beds cost double for the privacy. Vess had arranged it before she left us at the last crossroads — gone to ground inside the Inquisition, wearing her gray cloak again, feeding us what cover she could from the inside. *Trust no message you don't recognize,* she'd said. *Trust me least of all.* She'd given us a sign: a chalked lantern on the doorpost of anywhere safe. The Drowned Lantern's post bore one, small, at knee height, where only someone looking would find it.

The room was one bed, one chair, one window looking out on a wall. I set the wards — chalk, salt, and a stolen habit of watching doors — and then we opened the floor of the cart in the stable below and moved the relics up in relays, Tam on lookout, Wren carrying the eye-stone against her chest like a child, me taking the heart.

It beat against my ribs the whole way up the stairs. Not fast. Patient. And with every step, I heard — not with my ears, with the scar the guardian's curse had left on me — a thread of the impostor's mind, far off on its hill, noticing. The way a spider notices a fly walking the outermost ring of the web. Not alarmed. Interested.

*Come in,* the thread murmured, in a voice I refused to name. *Come in, little thief. I've been keeping your seat warm.*

I set the heart down in the chalk circle and wiped my palms on my coat and did not answer it. Answering is how it starts. I learned that in the weeks since the curse: it speaks in her voice, and if you speak back, you start remembering things you've sold, and if you remember, you start wanting, and wanting is a door.

Wren watched me from the doorway. "It talked again."

"It talks. I don't listen."

"That's not what listening is, Silas."

"No," I said. "But it's what surviving is. For now."

She crossed the room and sat on the bed's edge, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the bells rang the hour, and the heart beat its slow patient beat inside the warding, and somewhere above us on the hill a thing wearing a king's face turned its borrowed thoughts toward the tanners' quarter like a man turning toward a smell.

"We need eyes in the palace," Wren said finally. "Vess can't do it alone. And we need to find where the third relic's kept — the crypt, the anchor chamber, wherever the rite happens. Six days to midwinter."

"Five," I said. "Midwinter eve is when the blessing lines form. The rite runs at midnight. So five days to become people who can walk into a palace."

"And how do thieves walk into palaces?"

I almost smiled. Almost. "Badly, usually. Fatally, often. But there's a version of it that works, and it works because palaces run on the same currency as everything else." I tapped my temple. "Memory. Courtiers sell each other's secrets daily. Guards remember faces they shouldn't. Stewards hoard grudges like dragons. A man who can lift a memory and leave a better one in its place can walk through a palace like a ghost through a wall."

"You'd have to steal from dozens of people."

"Hundreds, maybe. And wear their recollections like coats, one over another, until I'm someone the palace believes in." I looked at the window, at the blank wall beyond it, at the hill I couldn't see but could feel. "There's a servant's entrance on the north side. There's always a servant's entrance. And somewhere in that building is a steward or a seneschal whose memories will open every door worth opening."

Wren was quiet a moment. "And the cost? There's always a cost with you."

"That's the plan's one virtue," I said. "This time the cost lands on other people. Rich ones."

She didn't laugh. Neither did I, really. But it broke the tension enough that we could breathe, and breathe was what we needed, because tomorrow the work began: casing the palace, finding the entrance, mapping the guards' rotations, learning the shape of the web before we touched a single strand.

Tam came back from the common room with bread, cheese, and news. "There's a hanging tomorrow," he reported, mouth full. "A thief. Caught stealing from the palace, they say. They're going to hang him on Festival Green at noon so everyone can watch before the feasting."

The cheese turned to chalk in my own mouth. "What was his name?"

"Dunno. Some river-man. Marlow's crowd, the innkeep said. From Casterhale."

I looked at Wren. She looked at me. Marlow's crowd meant Marlow's ledger meant the Inquisition's net, drawn tight around anyone who'd ever handled our route — and Vess was inside that net now, and the hanging was either a warning or bait, and both meant the same thing.

"Nobody goes to Festival Green tomorrow," I said.

"But—"

"*Nobody.*" I said it gently, which somehow made it land harder. "If it's bait, we don't take it. If it's a warning, we've read it. Either way, we have five days, a cart full of gods, and a palace to rob. Grief keeps. Work doesn't."

Tam nodded and looked at his bread, and I hated myself a little, and put it away with all the rest.

Outside, the bells rang again — unscheduled, urgent, a pattern I hadn't heard before, three-and-one, three-and-one, rolling out over the rooftops from the hill. Wren went pale.

"That's not the hour," she whispered. "That's an alarm. Something got into the palace tonight."

Or something already inside it had been noticed, I thought, and didn't say. The heart in its chalk circle beat once, slow and satisfied, like a sleeper turning over.

Five days. I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the bells, and beneath them, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, I could hear the gods' voices starting to seep back in — the bells tiring, the muzzle wearing thin, the whole vast machine of silence beginning, very slightly, to fail.

The impostor would hear it too. And a thing that built a city-sized muzzle to sleep soundly would do anything, anything at all, to keep it quiet.

Midnight was coming. So was everything else.
