## 27. The Court of the Hollow King

The capital smelled like bells.

That was the only way Silas could describe it — copper and cold iron and something underneath like struck bronze, a taste in the back of the throat that never quite went away. The festival had swallowed the city whole. Lanterns hung from every eave in the shape of crowns, and the streets ran thick with pilgrims, jugglers, roasted chestnut smoke, and the kind of joy that people perform loudly when they're afraid of what silence might bring.

Above it all, the bells rang on the hour, every hour, tuned to a frequency that made Silas's teeth ache and his thoughts go smooth and shallow, like a riverbed worn flat. He'd learned to hate them in three days. Wren had learned to hum over them, which was worse.

"Stop humming," he said.

"You stop flinching." She walked beside him through the festival crowd with her hood down and her chin up, because hiding marked you faster than standing tall in a city this size. Tam rode on Vess's shoulders, wearing the blank-eyed serenity of a boy who had been told he was going to see the king and had decided to believe it would be fun.

Vess moved like she belonged here, because she did. Grey cloak, grey eyes, the Inquisition's sigil turned inside out and sewn against her skin where no one could see it. Three days since she'd broken Tam out of a cell and handed him over without apology or explanation, and Silas still hadn't decided whether trusting her was courage or the slowest form of suicide.

"The audience chamber opens to petitioners at midday," she said now, low, under the bell-toll. "Midwinter blessing week. Thousands come to kneel. You'll be one more face in a river of faces."

"And my face is worth nothing."

"Your face is worth nothing," she agreed. "It's your *purse* I'm counting on."

Silas patted the satchel at his hip. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, sat eleven stolen memories — court memories, lifted from nobles and clerks and one extremely drunk herald over the past two days of work he refused to call theft anymore, because Wren kept looking at him when he used the word. Memories of protocol. Of who bowed to whom. Of the exact cadence of a petitioner's oath, the precise angle of a knee before the throne, the smell of the wax on the seal of an audience-grant from the Lord Chamberlain's office.

He'd taken them from people who wouldn't miss them for weeks. That was the story he told himself. He was getting very good at telling himself stories.

"Eleven vials," he said. "For one look at the man wearing Aldric's face."

"One look," Vess said, "and one thing more. You'll know if he's real."

"He's not real," Silas said. "I've seen the memory. I watched the smoke pour into him eight years ago while he screamed behind his own eyes."

"Then why do you need to see him?"

Silas didn't answer. Because the truth was ugly and simple: some part of him still hoped the vial lied. That the knight had been mad, that the memory had been forged, that the world was the ordinary corrupt place he understood and not the nightmare it kept revealing itself to be.

Wren touched his elbow. Just once. She didn't say anything. She'd gotten very good, these last months, at saying things without saying them.

They joined the petitioner's queue at the palace gates as the midday bells began.

* * *

The line moved for four hours.

Silas spent them doing what he did best: listening, watching, cataloguing. The guards at the gate were hollowed — he could see it now that he knew to look, the way their smiles all bent at the same angle, the way they blinked in unison every ninth second like puppets sharing a string. The clerks processing petitions were not hollowed but were afraid, and fear made people leak memory like cracked barrels. He caught fragments off a undersecretary as they shuffled past: *the king has not slept in six years, the king does not eat where anyone can see, the king asked me last week what rain felt like.*

That last one lodged under Silas's ribs and stayed there.

Tam, meanwhile, had discovered that royal guards would answer a child's questions when they'd ignore an adult's, and was conducting intelligence operations from Vess's shoulders with the shameless efficiency of a born spy.

"How many windows does the throne room have?" Silas murmured.

"Forty-one! And there's a door behind the dais with a lion on it and a guard told me nobody goes through it except the king and he goes down *stairs*, down and down, and—" Tam lowered his voice with tremendous gravity, "—and he comes back up with dust on his knees."

Silas went very still.

Dust on his knees. Going down. Beneath the palace.

He thought of the eye-stone's vision: the rite chamber, the crown drinking names, the dark beneath the capital where the old gods' bones had been buried by men who wanted to build an empire on top of a grave. He thought of the third relic. A god's heart. Kept close. Kept *anchored*.

"Tam," he said carefully, "does the guard know what's down there?"

"No. He said the stairs are older than the palace. He said his grandfather was a mason and even the masons aren't allowed." Tam shrugged. "I think the king keeps his toys in the basement."

Out of the mouths of babes, Silas thought, and felt the cold settle deeper.

By late afternoon they reached the inner gate, where a chamberlain's officer in silver-trimmed livery checked seals and turned away the unworthy. Silas had bought his way past this point in advance — a forged audience-grant built from three stolen memories stitched together, the wax pressed from a mold he'd paid a Casterhale forger half his remaining coin to carve. The officer examined it, examined Silas, examined Wren and the child and the tall grey-cloaked woman who radiated exactly enough bored authority to be unremarkable.

"Purpose of petition?" the officer asked.

"Gratitude," Silas said. It was the safest word. Half the petitioners this week had come to give thanks for the midwinter blessing. "My family wishes to kneel before His Majesty and offer thanks for the season's peace."

The officer's smile bent at the correct angle. "Proceed to the antechamber. You will wait until called. You will not speak unless addressed. You will keep your eyes lowered in the royal presence."

"Of course," Silas said, and meant none of it.

* * *

The antechamber held perhaps sixty souls, packed between pillars painted with scenes of Aldric's coronation — the young king radiant in gold, the old gods' symbols ringing the painted ceiling, stag and serpent and drowned star, all of them watching the mortal below with what the artist had clearly intended as blessing.

Silas looked up at them and thought: *they're not watching him bless. They're watching him suffer.* Eight years ago, in a memory sealed in glass, those same symbols had circled a screaming man while something poured itself into his mouth.

An hour passed. Then another. The crowd thinned as petitioners were called through in groups of ten. Wren dozed against a pillar with Tam curled under her cloak. Vess stood motionless, a grey shadow among shadows.

And Silas worked.

It was almost too easy. Sixty frightened, hopeful, exhausted people in an enclosed space, each of them marinating in their own thoughts, leaking memory like open taps. He didn't even need to touch most of them. Proximity and attention were enough, if you were patient. He skimmed the surface of the room the way a fisherman skims a lake, and what he pulled up made his blood run colder with every pass.

A merchant who had seen the queen — dead six years, officially — walking the west gallery at midnight, smiling at nothing.

A laundress whose sister served in the royal kitchens, who reported that the king's meals came back untouched but the dogs wouldn't go near them.

A minor lordling who had knelt at the midwinter blessing last year and sworn, drunk afterward, that when the king laid a hand on his head he had felt *nothing*. Not warmth, not power, not presence. Nothing. Like being touched by a glove with no hand in it.

And one more. An old priest, half-asleep in the corner, dreaming fitfully — and dreams were doors left unlocked. Silas slipped in gently, apologetically, and found the dream full of bells and a single recurring image: the king alone in a chapel, kneeling before an altar of the old gods, and the altar was *dark*, and the king was whispering *please* over and over in a voice that sounded like a man calling down a well, and no one answered, and the king wept without changing expression, tears sliding down a face that had forgotten how to move.

Silas withdrew so fast he nearly staggered.

"That bad?" Vess murmured beside him, without moving her lips.

"Worse." He kept his voice under the room's murmur. "There's still someone in there. Whatever poured into him didn't fill him all the way. There's a man at the bottom of it, praying."

"Then we're not too late."

"We were too late eight years ago, Vess. We're just not *finally* late."

She turned her head then, just slightly, and looked at him with those grey eyes that gave away nothing. "You know what I prayed for? When I was twelve? I prayed for my father to stop drinking. And something answered. Something answered *so kindly*, Silas. It took his wanting away. All of it. He sat in his chair for nineteen years smiling at the wall until he died, and everyone said how peaceful he looked." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "So don't tell me about too late. I've been living in too late my whole life."

Before Silas could decide whether that deserved pity or a blade, the doors opened, and a herald's voice cut through the antechamber like wire:

"Petitioners for the evening blessing — ten persons — approach and kneel."

Their group was third in the call. Silas gathered Wren with a touch, hoisted Tam onto his hip, and walked with his family of strangers through doors tall enough to admit a siege engine, into the presence of the King of Callowmere.

* * *

The throne room was designed to make you feel small, and it succeeded. Forty-one windows, as Tam had reported, though the winter dusk had drained them all to grey. Banners of every province hung from the vaulted ceiling. Two hundred courtiers lined the walls in tiers, and every one of them wore the same smile.

Not similar. The *same*. Silas had seen hollowed villages, hollowed guards, hollowed priests — but this was different in scale, in *craftsmanship*. Two hundred faces curved upward at identical angles, two hundred pairs of hands folded identically, and when a page coughed near the east wall, two hundred heads turned toward the sound in perfect unison, like a field of wheat bending in wind that wasn't there.

Wren's hand found Silas's and gripped hard.

At the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of gold thread, sat the King of Callowmere.

King Aldric had been forty in the stolen memory — broad, weathered, a soldier-king with laugh lines carved deep around eyes that had seen the border wars. The thing on the throne wore that face the way a coat hangs on a hook. It was the right face. It was even, Silas realized with a lurch, *aging correctly* — someone, something, was maintaining the illusion with terrible diligence, adding wrinkles year by year, greying the hair at the temples on schedule.

But the eyes were wrong. Not evil. Not cruel. *Empty* — and not empty like a vacant house, but empty like a house where someone is standing very still behind a window, watching you pretend not to notice.

"Approach," the king said, and his voice was warm and rich and completely wrong, a voice with no breath behind it, and Silas heard — or imagined he heard, or *knew* he heard — a fainter voice underneath it, far down, calling up through stone: *please.*

They knelt. Ten petitioners on cold marble. Tam wriggled against Silas's side, and Silas murmured, "Eyes down," and the boy obeyed, trembling.

The line ahead of them filed forward one by one. A farmer giving thanks for rain. A widow asking blessing on her son's ship. Each received the same benediction: the king extended one hand, laid it on the petitioner's head, spoke words of season and peace. Each rose smiling that same smile, and walked away lighter, and Silas understood with sick clarity that the touch was taking something — a sliver, a shaving, a tithe of memory or will or self — and that the whole festival, the whole *blessing week*, was a harvest.

Then it was their turn.

Silas rose, guided Wren and Tam forward, and knelt at the foot of the dais — close enough to see the king's hands, and the hands were wrong too, too smooth, knuckles without history. Close enough to smell him. Under the incense and the gold, the king smelled like the inside of the vial. Like smoke and borrowed faces.

The hand descended toward Tam's head.

Every instinct Silas owned screamed at once. He shifted — a small movement, a stumble, a clumsy petitioner's apology — and put himself in the way, so the king's palm landed on *his* crown instead of the boy's.

The touch was cold. Not flesh-cold. *Well*-cold, cellar-cold, the cold of deep places where light has never been. And through the contact, for one instant, something looked *into* him.

Silas had spent his life behind walls of practiced emptiness. But the thing behind the king's eyes wasn't picking the lock. It was simply *looking*, patiently, the way a man looks into a drawer he already owns — and what it found there made it pause.

Because Silas was hollow too. Not the way these courtiers were hollowed — but hollowed by choice, by trade, by thirty years of selling pieces of himself until there was more absence than man. The thing searching him found no clean memories to shave off. It found a thief's cluttered ruin, and in the ruin it found—

—the mother. The seed. The original memory, the one the impostor had grown from, still buried in Silas like a root under a floor.

The king's fingers tightened. For half a heartbeat the warm mask *flickered*, and Silas saw beneath it: vast and thin and hungry, a shape of smoke wearing patience, and it recognized him. Not his name. His *substance*. It knew what he carried the way a drowning man knows water.

"*You,*" the king said softly, in a voice that was suddenly, horribly familiar — and it wasn't the king's voice at all. It was a voice Silas hadn't heard in twenty-five years, a voice singing over a cradle in a house that had burned, and the whole throne room heard it, and two hundred courtiers smiled wider.

Then the pressure withdrew. The hand lifted. The mask reassembled itself, warm and kingly and bland.

"Blessings of the season," the impostor said pleasantly, to the room, to no one. "Next."

Silas got his family out of the throne room on legs that barely worked, past the smiling walls, through the towering doors, into the antechamber's noise and lamplight, and kept walking until they reached a colonnade overlooking the festival square, where the bells were tolling the hour and no one could hear a man shake.

"What happened?" Wren demanded. "What did it say? What was—"

"It knows me." Silas braced both hands on the balustrade. The city glittered below, lanterns in the shapes of crowns, thousands of happy people waiting for a blessing that was eating them alive. "It looked at me and it *knows me*, Wren. It knows what I'm carrying. It's been looking for that memory for — maybe forever. Maybe that's why it grew from it in the first place. Maybe it wants the *original*."

Vess appeared out of the crowd, Tam's hand locked in hers, her face grim. "We leave tonight. New safehouse. If it marked you—"

"It did more than mark me." Silas straightened. His heart was hammering, but underneath the fear, cold and clear as well-water, a thought had assembled itself. "Tam. The door behind the dais. The stairs going down. Dust on the king's knees."

Tam nodded solemnly.

"The heart's down there." Silas looked at Vess, at Wren. "The third relic isn't hidden somewhere we have to quest for. It's *here*. Under the throne. It's the anchor — the impostor keeps its own tether buried in the crypt, close enough to guard. Which means we don't have to steal our way into the palace twice."

Wren stared at him. "You want to go *back*. Down. Under it."

"I want to end this before midwinter night," Silas said. "And for once, the gods — whatever's left of them — are being generous. Everything we need is in one building." He looked back at the palace, black and gold against the darkening sky, its thousand windows lit, its bells tolling, its king somewhere above a buried heart, dust on his knees, whispering *please* down a well.

"But we go tomorrow," he added quietly, "because tonight, the king knows my face — and I intend to spend the hours he spends wondering where I am learning exactly where his stairs go."

Below them, the festival roared on, joyful and doomed, and high in the palace a window lit that had been dark all day — the west gallery, where a dead queen was said to walk.

Silas watched it a moment too long.

Then the bells tolled again, and somewhere beneath his feet, faint as a heartbeat through stone, he felt the palace *wake*.
