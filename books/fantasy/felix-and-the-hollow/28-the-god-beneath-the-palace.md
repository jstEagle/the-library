## 28. The God Beneath the Palace

The crypt door was iron, and the iron was warm.

Wren pressed her palm flat against it and felt the heat of a sleeping animal, a slow pulse coming through the metal like something on the other side was breathing. She snatched her hand back.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

"Everything in this palace knows we're here," Silas said. He crouched beside her in the dark of the undercroft, turning the stolen guard's ring of keys over in his fingers. "That's not the problem. The problem is whether it cares."

Above them, muffled by three floors of stone and festival noise, the bells of the capital rang the hour. The sound came down through the walls like a headache — that was what they were tuned for, Tam had told them, half-proud, half-ashamed, the way only a boy raised in this city could be. *Tuned to keep the gods quiet.* Every bell in every tower struck the same flattened note, and beneath it, Wren realized now, she had been feeling it all week without naming it: a pressure behind her eyes, a silence with weight, like the whole city was holding one long held breath.

Down here, below the bells, the silence was different. Down here it wasn't suppression. It was attention.

"The map says the heart's behind this door," Vess said. She stood at the edge of the lamplight, armor swapped for a pilgrim's grey, her Inquisition signet hidden in her boot where she'd kept it even after everything. "And the map also says there are six guards on rotation, and I counted four passing above us in the last hour, which means either the impostor has thinned the watch because he thinks no one would dare—"

"—or he's pulled them closer to the thing he can't afford to lose," Silas finished. He'd gone very still, the way he did when he was reading a room by its silences. Wren had learned to watch for it. "Which means whatever's down there matters more than the rite itself. You don't anchor your god-killing ceremony to a crypt unless the crypt is the keystone."

"So we go fast," Wren said.

"So we go quiet," Silas said. "Fast is loud. Loud is dead."

She bit back the argument. There was no time for it and no point; she'd learned that much about him in a thousand miles of road. But her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and she hated that, and she hid them in her sleeves where nobody could see, and then hated herself for hiding too, because hiding was his habit, not hers, and she'd sworn somewhere between Casterhale and the burning river that she would never learn to be ashamed the way he was ashamed.

Tam shifted against the wall behind them. They'd argued about bringing him — argued hard, Wren and Silas, in whispers while Vess stood watch — and lost, because Tam had refused to stay with the pilgrims and they had no way to make a boy who'd already been hollowed once and survived it do anything he didn't want to do. He sat now with his knees pulled up, hugging the beggar's cart horse's blanket around his shoulders, and when Wren glanced at him he gave her a thumbs-up that shook slightly at the top.

"You don't have to come," she mouthed.

"I know," he mouthed back. And stayed.

* * *

The keys turned without sound. That was the first wrong thing.

Silas tried three before he found the right one, and each turned smooth as oiled glass, and Wren watched his shoulders climb toward his ears with every silent revolution. Locks were supposed to resist. Locks were supposed to announce you. A lock that welcomed you was a lock that wanted you inside.

"Trap," Vess breathed.

"Or an invitation," Silas said. "With him it's always both."

The door swung inward on darkness that didn't smell like a crypt. Crypts smelled of dust and old incense and the sweet rot of expensive corpses. This smelled like rain on hot stone, like the air after lightning, like the moment in chapter-stories when the god steps out of the whirlwind. Wren's skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. Somewhere in the dark ahead, water dripped — but slow, far too slow, each drop falling with the patience of a heartbeat.

They went in single file: Silas first with the lamp hooded to a slit, Wren behind him with the tongue-bone wrapped in wool against her chest, Vess last with her hand on Tam's collar like a mother hen who happened to be armed. The stairs went down farther than any crypt needed to go. Deeper than foundations. Deeper, Wren thought wildly, than the city itself should allow, as if the palace had grown upward out of a hole rather than being built upon the ground.

The stairs ended in a chamber that took her breath away.

It was round, and vast, and old — older than the palace, older than the dynasty whose banners hung shredded in the corners, older maybe than the kingdom. The walls were black stone veined with gold, and the gold moved. Slowly. Like sap. Like light trapped in amber and still trying to get home. And in the center of the floor, sunk into a basin carved in the shape of cupped hands, lay the heart.

It was the size of a prize bull's, and it beat once every ten seconds, and every time it beat, the golden veins in the walls brightened and dimmed in answer, and the warmth rolled over Wren like a wave off a summer street, and her eyes filled with tears she had no reason for and could not stop.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just *oh.*

"That's not a relic," Vess said hoarsely. Behind her, Tam made a small wounded sound. "That's a prisoner."

The heart was bound. Wren saw it now, through the tears: chains of pale metal ran from the basin's rim into the thing itself, sinking into the flesh without wound or blood, and the chains were taut, and the chains were *strumming* — a low note, below hearing, below the bells, the same flattened note the towers played, drawn out of the heart itself and fed upward through the palace's bones. The whole capital rang with it. The whole capital had been ringing with it for years, and everyone thought it was just bells.

"They're using it as a tuning fork," Silas said softly. "Silence the gods everywhere by playing their own heart out of tune." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "He didn't bury it down here to hide it. He buried it to *use* it. The crypt isn't the prison." His lamp rose, and the light caught the ceiling, and Wren followed it up and felt her stomach drop.

The ceiling was carved with faces. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Round-faced, smiling, identical, radiating outward from a central point directly above the basin — and the central face was larger than all the rest, and it was smiling the widest, and Wren knew that smile. She'd seen it on a village full of people in the north country. She'd seen it on posters in every town square since midwinter was announced. She'd seen it, God help her, in her own mirror some mornings lately, when she caught herself smiling at nothing.

"He's wearing them," she whispered. "All of them. The whole ceiling is — those are the people he's hollowed. He keeps them up here. He keeps *all* of them."

"And the heart keeps them beating," Vess said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the voice Wren had learned meant the inquisitor was frightened and converting fear into doctrine. "Cut the heart loose, the anchors fail. Cut the anchors, the rite fails. This is it. This is why he let us come." She turned, and in the lamplight her face was terrible and certain. "This is a trap with our names on the invitation, and I say we spring it anyway, because in six days he finishes the rite and there is no second chance. Are we agreed?"

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. Silas was already walking toward the basin.

* * *

Later — much later, in the running and the screaming, Wren would try to remember exactly how it started, and she never could. Whether Silas touched the chains first, or whether the heart simply *knew*, the way a dog knows its name spoken kindly after years of kicks. What she remembered, what she kept, was this:

Silas knelt at the rim of the basin, and he did not reach for the chains. He reached past them, and laid his bare palm flat against the heart itself, the way she had laid hers against the crypt door, and he closed his eyes, and he said, so quietly she almost missed it:

"I'm sorry. We're late."

And the heart *answered.*

The pulse stuttered. Quickened. The golden veins flared so bright the shadows fled to the corners, and the strumming note in the chains climbed and cracked and changed key for the first time in years, and Wren heard — everyone heard, it came up through the floor and out through the walls and she would swear until she died that the whole city heard it — a single clear tone underneath the false one. A true note. A god's note, faint as a memory of music, rising through the flattened bells like a swimmer breaking surface.

Every face on the ceiling opened its eyes.

Not the big one. All the others. Hundreds of small carved eyes, rolling in their smiling sockets, finding the intruders below, and the smiles did not change, and that was the worst thing Wren had ever seen, worse than the hollowed village, worse than the checkpoint of ash: a thousand faces grinning down at them with fresh, wet, waking attention.

"Silas," she said. "Silas, the ceiling—"

"I see it." He had the knife out now, the thin one, the one he used for memories, and he was cutting at the nearest chain where it sank into the heart's flesh. The pale metal resisted, sang, resisted — and parted. One link. The true note grew louder.

"Guards," Vess snapped. Above them, through the stone, boots — many boots, running, and a bell overhead began tolling out of rhythm, alarm-pattern, the flattened note abandoned at last because the palace itself was screaming now. "We have minutes. Maybe less."

"Then help me cut," Silas snarled, and Wren tore the wool off the tongue-bone and pressed it into his free hand, and the bone *sang* — actually sang, a wordless command in a language older than kingdoms — and the second chain parted like rotten rope.

Two links left. The ceiling faces were weeping now, golden tears tracking down black stone, and the big central smile had begun, very slowly, to turn downward at the corners, and the temperature in the chamber dropped as if winter itself had taken an interest. Frost feathered across the basin's rim. Wren's breath came out white.

The third chain fought her. She got both hands on it where it entered the flesh, hauling with all her weight, screaming without deciding to scream, and it gave — parted — whipped back across the chamber and shattered a pillar of black stone, and the true note swelled to fill everything, and the last chain—

The last chain was attached to the big face.

It ran straight up, from the heart, into the ceiling, into the open mouth of the largest carving, and as Wren watched, the mouth closed around it and began to *pull* — reeling the heart upward, toward the smile, toward the thing that wore the crown three floors above and had worn it for years and was not finished wearing it yet.

"No," Silas said. Simply, quietly, the way he said everything that mattered. "No, you don't."

He drove the tongue-bone into the final link like a nail.

The chain broke. The heart fell back into the cupped hands of the basin, and the pull from above became a shriek — an actual shriek, from the ceiling, from the big face, from everywhere at once, a sound of pure furious loss that cracked two more pillars and burst every lamp but theirs — and then the palace woke.

Not the guards. The *palace.* Every bell in the capital tolled at once with no hands on the ropes, a chaos of clashing bronze, and the golden veins in the walls went dark one by one like windows going out in a dying city, and the thousand faces on the ceiling slumped back into blankness, empty again, abandoned, discarded puppets with their strings cut. The warmth died. The frost spread. And from far above, muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable, came the sound Wren had been dreading since the shrine of teeth:

Footsteps. Many. Descending.

"Got it?" Vess shouted, already moving, dragging Tam toward the stairs.

Silas lifted the heart out of the basin. It should have weighed as much as a bull; it came up light as bread, light as a child, and it beat against his chest through his coat, quick and alive and *grateful*, and Wren saw her thief — her careful, armored, never-feel-anything thief — press his lips to it once, quickly, like a man kissing a sleeping forehead.

"Got it," he said. "Run."

They ran. Up the endless stairs, the heart tucked inside Silas's coat beating faster and faster as though it knew it was escaping, the tongue-bone howling in Wren's grip, Vess shoving Tam ahead of her with a tenderness she'd deny to her grave. Behind them the crypt door slammed of its own accord. Ahead, torchlight and shouting and the crash of a barred gate giving way.

They burst out of the undercroft into the guard corridor and the first squad of royal guards was already there, six of them, halberds dropping level — and stopped. All six. At once. Their heads tilted, in unison, the identical tilt, and their faces began, slowly, horribly, to arrange themselves into the smile.

"Hollowed," Vess breathed. "He's hollowed his own guard."

"Then they'll be slow," Silas said, and Wren heard it in his voice, the thing she'd heard on the road of small gods and at the checkpoint and in the burning streets of Casterhale: the flat calm of a man who has decided exactly what he is willing to lose. "Slow enough. Wren — the tongue-bone. Give me your hand. Everyone else — *run.*"

The guards smiled wider. Somewhere far above, beneath the clanging bells, beneath the screaming palace, a voice that wore a king's throat and was not a king's began — softly, patiently, in a voice Wren recognized and Silas clearly did, a voice that made Silas flinch like a struck man —

"— *come home, little thief,*" it called down through the stones. "*You're carrying what's mine.*"

The guards charged.
