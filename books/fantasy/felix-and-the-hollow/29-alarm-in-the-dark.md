## 29. Alarm in the Dark

The heart beat in Silas's hands.

That was the only word for it. The relic — a god's heart, black as river stone and warm as a sleeping bird — pulsed against his palms, and every pulse sent a shudder through the floor of the crypt, through the bones stacked in their alcoves, through the marrow of the palace above them. They had taken it from its cradle of silver and chains forty seconds ago, and the palace had been screaming ever since.

Not people. Not yet. The *building*.

Bells that had never been rung were ringing. Stones groaned in their settings like old men waking from nightmares. Somewhere far overhead, a choir of alarms had begun, high and thin, and beneath it all came a sound Silas felt in his teeth: a slow, rhythmic tolling, deep as a funeral, spreading outward through the capital of Bells like a stain through water.

"They know," Wren said. She was already moving, torch held low, her shadow leaping along the crypt walls where ten generations of dead kings lay in state. "Silas. *They know.*"

"They knew the moment I lifted it." He tucked the heart into the padded satchel across his chest, where it sat against his ribs like a second heartbeat, wrong and insistent. "Move."

Vess moved first. The former inquisitor had shed her white-and-gold coat two days ago; now she wore a guardsman's grey, and she wore it well, cutting ahead down the eastern stair with her blade drawn and her voice flat. "Service corridor. We go up two levels, west along the wine cellars, out through the ash-chute. Ninety seconds if nothing's waiting."

"Nothing's ever not waiting," Tam said.

Silas looked back at the boy — thirteen years old, all elbows and stubbornness, carrying the beggar's cart's false bottom over one shoulder because he'd refused to leave it behind — and felt the old familiar ache, the one that had started the day a dying knight fell out of the sky at him. The ache had a name now. He'd spent most of his life refusing to name things. Naming made them real, and real things could be taken.

"Stay behind me," Silas said.

"You always say that." Tam grinned at him in the torchlight, gap-toothed, fearless in the way only children and fools are fearless. "And then you always end up behind *me*."

"Because you're too stupid to run when I tell you to."

"Because you're too slow," Tam said cheerfully, and darted past him up the stairs.

Silas let him go. That was the thing he would carry for the rest of whatever life he had left — he let him go. He had the heart of a god against his chest and a plan half-collapsed in his mouth and he let a child take point on the stairs because the boy was quick and light and good at slipping through gaps, and because Silas was tired, and tired men let other people lead.

The stairs took them up into cold and dark. The wine cellars stretched away in ranks of barrels, each one stamped with the crown's seal, each one older than the impostor sitting on the throne above them. Their footsteps echoed wrong — too loud, too many, as though the cellar itself were counting them.

Then the bells stopped.

All of them. At once.

The silence that followed was worse than the screaming. It was the silence of something drawing breath.

"Incoming," Vess said quietly. "Left side. Six or more. Move to the—"

The lanterns flared.

Every flame in the cellar stood up straight at once, tall and white, and by that terrible light Silas saw them come around the corner of the barrel-ranks: royal guard, a dozen of them, in lacquered black, faces smooth and shining and *identical*. Hollowed men. The same smile on every face, the same empty calm, moving with the terrible economy of puppets whose strings all led to the same hand.

"Hollowed," Vess breathed. "He's hollowed his own guard."

"Of course he has." Silas backed up a step, hand finding the satchel strap. "Why hire loyalty when you can grow it?"

The hollowed guard didn't shout. Didn't warn. They simply drew steel in perfect unison — twelve blades leaving twelve scabbards with a single sound, like one sword — and came on.

What followed took eleven seconds and cost more than Silas wanted to count.

Vess met the first rank like a wall falling uphill. She fought the way she prayed, he realized — with total, joyless commitment, no flourish, no wasted motion, a woman who had once given her soul to gods and now gave everything to the simple arithmetic of keeping three people alive. Her blade took the nearest guard through the throat and she was already turning, shoulder-checking the second into the third, buying space.

Wren went left, low, the way Silas had taught her without ever meaning to — steal, don't fight, *steal* — and her knife opened the hamstring of a guard reaching for Tam. The man went down smiling. They all went down smiling. That was the worst part. Every wound, every death, and not one face changed.

"Chute!" Vess barked. "GO!"

They ran. Down the long aisle between barrels, the hollowed guard regrouping behind them with that awful silence — no orders shouted, no pain cried out, just the soft synchronized rhythm of boots on stone. Silas shoved Tam ahead of him toward the iron door at the cellar's end, the ash-chute Vess had promised, and the boy wrenched it open and black cold air poured out and—

—and there were more of them. Coming up the chute. Climbing hand over hand out of the dark like things born in it, six of them, eight, faces tilted up and smiling, and the door was the only way through and they were filling it.

Tam didn't hesitate.

That was the other thing Silas would carry. The boy looked at the door full of enemies, looked back once at Silas — just once, just long enough to grin, that same stupid brave grin — and said, "I'll hold it."

"TAM—"

"I'm quick. You said so yourself." He dropped the cart panel, drew the little knife Silas had given him outside Casterhale — a thief's knife, a joke gift, a *toy* — and planted himself in the doorway like a fencepost. "Go. I'll be right behind you. I'm always right behind you."

The hollowed guard reached him.

Silas lunged forward and Vess caught him — caught him bodily, arms locked around his chest, dragging him back while he screamed, actually screamed, words he couldn't later remember, and Wren had his satchel strap and was hauling too, and the last thing Silas saw before the chute door swung shut between them was Tam turning to face eight grown killers with a toy knife and a grin, holding the line, holding the door, holding —

The door boomed shut. Iron on iron. On the far side of it, muffled, Silas heard the ring of steel and did not hear a boy's voice at all.

"*No.*" He hit the door with both fists. "*NO.* Open it — Wren, help me — we go BACK—"

"We can't." Wren's voice cracked on it. Her hands were on his shoulders, dragging him away, and her face was wet and set at the same time, grief and iron fused together. "Silas. *Silas.* If we open it, we die and he died for nothing."

"He's not DEAD—"

"He's captured." Vess, breathing hard, blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers. She said it gently, which was somehow worse. "If they'd killed him, they'd have left him. They took him alive. Think about what that means."

Silas thought about what it meant, and the thinking was a blade going in under the ribs.

The impostor read memories. The impostor had been reading Tam's memories since the moment the boy was dragged up those stairs — every stolen apple, every night by the pilgrim fire, every time a foolish child had looked at a gray-eyed thief and seen a hero from a story. Every road they'd walked. Every safehouse. Every face.

Every plan.

"Oh, gods," Wren whispered. She'd gotten there too. "He'll see us. He'll see *everything*."

"Not everything." Vess was already moving, forcing them down the chute, down and out into the freezing dark beyond the palace walls. "The boy's brave. Bravery is noise. Noise drowns signal. It may buy hours." She looked back at Silas, and for one unguarded moment the inquisitor's mask slipped and something almost human looked out. "I'm sorry. For what it's worth."

"It isn't," Silas said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They came out through the ash-chute into the slums below the palace hill, where the festival fires still burned and nobody yet knew the sky had started to bruise. Silas walked because his legs remembered how, and he hated every step, because every step was away.

The guilt wasn't loud. That was the trick of it. He'd expected it to scream. Instead it was quiet and precise, like a knife being sharpened somewhere in another room, and every few steps he felt it test its edge against him.

*You let him take point.*

*You gave him the knife.*

*You taught him that grin. He learned it from you. Everything brave and stupid in that boy, he learned watching you.*

"You're doing it," Wren said quietly. They'd stopped in the lee of a burned-out shrine, the three of them, hearts hammering, the god's heart pulsing in the satchel between them like an accusation. "The thing where you go somewhere else and blame yourself. I can see it."

"Can you." His voice came out flat as coin metal.

"Yes. Because I learned it from you too." She stepped in front of him, forced him to look at her. Firelight from the festival caught the tear-tracks on her face and the fury underneath them. "Listen to me. Tam made a choice. A *stupid*, brave, impossible choice, and he made it because of who he decided you were. So you don't get to make it mean nothing. You don't get to carry it like a stone. You carry it like a *debt* — and debts get paid."

"How?" The word tore out of him rawer than he meant. "How do I pay this one, Wren? Tell me. The whole city will be looking for us by dawn. He'll have everything Tam knows by midnight. And midwinter is tomorrow night."

She flinched. Tomorrow night. The rite. The binding of every god's name into the crown, the silencing of heaven forever, and they had exactly one day and none of their advantages left.

"The map," Vess said suddenly.

Both of them turned. She was crouched over the satchel, pulling out the papers she'd stolen from the Inquisition archive — route maps, rite diagrams, the chamber layouts she'd traded her entire career for. She spread them on the shrine's broken altar with shaking hands.

"Tam doesn't know these exist. He's never seen them — I kept them wrapped, he thought they were laundry lists." Her finger stabbed down on the largest sheet, the rite chamber, the dais, the crown's cradle. "What he knows is faces. Routes. The beggar's cart. He knows we're going for the dais tomorrow night — but the impostor already knew that. Everyone knows that. What the boy *can't* give him—" she looked up, and her eyes were bright and hard, "—is how. Or the third piece."

Silas stared at her. Then at the satchel, where the god's heart pulsed its slow black rhythm against his ribs.

Two relics. They had two. The tongue-bone, cold in its wrappings. The eye-stone, blind and seeing. And the heart, beating against his chest like it wanted to go home.

One relic short. One day short. One child short.

And somewhere above them, in a tower room lit by a hundred candles, a thing wearing King Aldric's face was leaning over a small, stubborn boy and smiling the smile Silas knew better than anyone alive — because he'd worn it first, in a memory stolen thirty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of bread, in a life that had ended the day a man named Corvus taught a child that anything could be taken.

The link stirred. It had been quiet since the crypt — the curse's whisper, the thread that bound Silas's mind to the impostor's — but now it woke, and it spoke, and it used the voice Silas had paid a river god to forget.

*"You're closer than you think, little thief,"* said his mother's voice, gentle and terrible. *"Bring me the rest. Bring me the heart. And perhaps the boy keeps his smile."*

Silas closed his eyes.

When he opened them, Wren and Vess were watching him, waiting, and far off across the sleeping city the palace bells began to ring again — not alarms now, but something slower, sweeter, a lullaby played over a hostage.

"Tomorrow night," Silas said, and his voice did not shake, because he'd spent thirty years learning how to make it not shake, and it was the hardest thing he'd ever done. "We finish it. All of it. And we get him back."

Above the rooftops, the bruised sky began, very softly, to snow.
