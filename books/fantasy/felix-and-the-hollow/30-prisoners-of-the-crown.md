## 30. Prisoners of the Crown

They took his boots first.

Tam didn't understand why until the cold came up through the flagstones of the corridor, through the wool of his stockings, into the bones of his feet, and he understood that it wasn't about the boots at all. It was about making him feel small. Prisoners got their boots taken. Heroes kept them. So which was he?

He decided, walking barefoot between two guards whose faces were smooth as eggs, that he was a hero. It helped to decide things. Silas had taught him that, though Silas had never said it out loud — you learned it from watching him, from the way he'd stand at a crossroads and pick a direction like the world owed him an argument. *Decide fast,* Tam imagined him saying. *Decide wrong if you have to. Just don't let the world decide for you.*

So: hero. Barefoot hero. Cold feet, warm head.

The corridor went down. Torches in iron brackets burned with a flame that didn't flicker, and Tam stared at one as they passed because everything he'd read about palaces said torches flickered, and this one didn't, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. A flame that didn't move was a flame that wasn't alive.

The guards' hands were clamped on his arms, but not hard. That frightened him too. They weren't worried he'd run. Where would he go? He was eleven years old in the belly of the palace, three hundred miles from anyone who loved him, and both of those facts were true at once, and he held them together the way you held two ends of a rope bridge — carefully, or you fell between.

*Silas will come for me.* He made himself think it plainly, no decoration. Not *maybe*. Not *if*. Will. Because Silas Pemberton was a thief and a liar and a coward when it suited him, and Tam had watched him walk back into a burning checkpoint for strangers, and some things you knew the way you knew your own name.

The doors at the bottom of the stairs were black wood with silver hinges, and they opened without being touched, which meant someone or something wanted them open, and Tam's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

The throne room was wrong.

He'd expected gold. Every story had gold — banners, chandeliers, the king's crown catching light like a promise. But the room was grey. Grey stone, grey tapestries, grey light falling from windows so high they might have been painted on. And the throne itself sat on a low dais, almost humble, as if the room were embarrassed by it, and on the throne sat the king.

Tam had seen the king once, from a rooftop in Casterhale, during a procession. He remembered a broad man with a laugh, tossing coins. This was not that. This was a shape wearing a king's posture, a tall figure in grey and silver whose face kept almost resolving and then didn't — familiar in the way your own reflection is familiar, and just as impossible to look at directly.

*It has no face,* Tam thought, and then corrected himself, because that wasn't quite right either. *It has every face. It just can't pick one.*

"Come here, child."

The voice was kind. That was the trap of it — the voice was genuinely, warmly kind, the way a grandmother's voice is kind, and it came out of that unfinished face, and Tam's legs shook and he walked forward anyway. Heroes walked forward. That was most of what heroes did, when you got down to it. The rest was dressing.

He stopped ten paces from the dais. Close enough to be respectful. Far enough to spit, if it came to spitting.

"What's your name?"

"Tam."

"Just Tam?"

"Tam of Nowhere," he said, chin up. "That's what Silas calls me."

Something moved behind the king's eyes — or where eyes should have been. Interest. Hunger. Tam felt it like a draft under a door.

"Silas." The impostor tasted the name. "You love him."

"He's my friend."

"Friend." The word came out curious, like the king had found a coin from a country that no longer existed. "Do you know what I was, before I was this? Neither do I. Isn't that funny? I am made entirely of someone else's remembering, and even that remembering has holes in it. Do you know what it's like, child, to reach for your own beginning and find a thief's fingerprints?"

Tam didn't answer. Answering felt like opening a door.

"You do, actually." The king leaned forward, and the grey light bent toward him. "You know exactly what it's like. Someone took pieces of you once. A girl's memories, sold in a valley where gods couldn't watch. You've heard the story — everyone on that road told stories around the fires. The memory-thief of the Hollow. And you forgave him. That's the part I find delicious. He stole your childhood and you *loved him anyway*, and I want to know how that works, because I was stolen too, and all I've ever managed is rage."

"I don't forgive him," Tam said. "I decided he was worth more than what he took. It's different."

The king went very still.

Then it laughed — a real laugh, delighted, awful. "Oh, you're *wonderful*. Where did they find you? Keep him," it said to the guards, as if Tam were a gift. "Now, Tam-of-Nowhere. Here is what happens next, and I'll tell you honestly, because honesty unsettles people worse than cruelty does. I'm going to look inside your head. All the way in. Every fire you sat beside, every prayer you whispered, every time Silas Pemberton smiled at you like you mattered. I'm going to walk through you like a man walks through a house with the lamps lit, and I'm going to find where he is and what he plans, and then I'm going to finish my rite tomorrow night and unmake every god that ever ignored a praying child. And you—" the kindness sharpened into something ancient, "—you are going to show me everything, because you're eleven, and no one is brave all the way down. No one. I've hollowed kings, child. Kings beg."

Tam stood there with his cold feet on cold stone and thought about the flame that didn't flicker.

Then he thought about the shrine at the checkpoint, and Vess's face when she'd looked at him through the bars, and Wren teaching him to whittle on the road, and Silas asleep sitting up with his hat over his eyes because he didn't trust beds, and the whole ragged, borrowed, found family of them, and he thought: *they got here by deciding things. So decide.*

"No," Tam said.

The word dropped into the grey room like a stone into a well.

"No?" said the king.

"You heard me." His voice cracked on the second word and he hated that, but he kept going, because heroes' voices cracked too, probably, in all the stories nobody wrote down. "You can look. Look all you want. But I'm going to mess it up for you. I'm going to think about nothing. I'm going to think about *boots*. I'm going to count every step from here to Casterhale and back, twice, and when you dig past that you're going to find me thinking about boots again, and I'll tell you something else—" his heart was slamming so hard he could hear it in his ears, "—Silas told me once that memories aren't rooms. They're *smoke*. He said a good thief knows smoke doesn't keep its shape for anyone who grabs at it. So grab."

The king rose from the throne.

Up close, without the distance to soften it, the impostor was worse than faceless — its features slid, a jaw becoming another jaw, eyes settling and unsettling, and for one horrible half-second Tam saw a version of the face that was young and scared and familiar in a way he couldn't name, and then it was gone, smoothed away like breath off glass.

"This will hurt," the king said gently. "I want you to know that I regret it. That's the worst part of me, child — I always regret it, and I always do it anyway."

It reached out and touched his forehead with one finger.

* * *

The pain was not like pain. Pain had edges; this didn't. It was like being opened — like the finger was a key and Tam was a door and behind the door was everything he'd ever been, laid out in lamplight for a stranger to walk through.

He felt it enter.

He felt it find the checkpoint, and the caravan, and the night Silas had pulled him out of a river outside Bellhollow with one arm and called him a *damned anchor* while laughing. He felt it turn those memories over, greedy, thorough, a hand rifling a merchant's strongbox—

—and Tam counted boots.

Left, right, left, right. One pair, two pairs, the guard's boots, the dead knight's boots from the story Wren told, boots in shop windows in Casterhale, boots, boots, boots. Smoke, Silas had said. Don't hold a shape. Be weather.

The presence paused. Puzzled. Pushed deeper.

Tam gave it boots. Gave it the smell of leather and the number seventeen, which was how many pairs he'd owned in his life including the ones currently confiscated, and gave it the sensation of a blister, and underneath the boots, buried like a coin in sand, he set the thing he actually needed to hide — not a place, not a plan, he didn't know any plans, but a *feeling*: the direction Silas had gone, the shape of the map Vess had drawn, folded up small and wrapped in the memory of a boot, of all things, because the king had already seen the boots and would skim past them like a man skimming a book he thought he'd read.

It worked for a while. He could feel it working, feel the great cold attention sliding over the leather and moving on—

And then the king stopped.

"Ah," it said softly, inside his skull. "*Clever.* Who taught you smoke?"

*Silas,* Tam thought, and immediately wished he hadn't, because the presence seized the name like a hook seizing a fish, and hauled—

—but what came up wasn't the map. What came up was the river outside Bellhollow, the cold water, Silas's arm, the laugh, the words *damned anchor*, and the feeling underneath them that Tam had never put into words because he'd been saving it: *he came back for me. Nobody comes back. He came back.*

The king went quiet.

When it spoke again, the kindness was gone, and what was underneath was tired in a way that made Tam's chest ache, tired like something very old standing in a very long dark.

"He loves you," the impostor said. "This boy. This grubby little pilgrim. He came back for you, and he'll come again, straight into my rite chamber, and I will hollow him in front of you, and the last thing he'll feel is your face watching. Is that worth your boots, Tam of Nowhere? Is that what love buys?"

Tam was crying. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain — it was happening to his body, not to him. Inside, where the king was listening, he built a wall out of one sentence and stood on top of it:

*He came back once. He'll come back twice. Count on it.*

And then, because the monster had said *count on it*, and because Silas had taught him that you decide things — Tam did the bravest thing he would ever do in his life, and he did it on purpose, slowly, so the king couldn't miss it.

He smiled.

"That's why you're scared of him," Tam said aloud, wet-faced, barefoot, eleven years old on the grey stones of a dead king's hall. "Not because he'll stop your rite. Because he *decides*. And you can't. You're still waiting for somebody to come back for *you*, and nobody's coming, because you're not a person, you're a leftover. You're what got thrown away."

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. Tam counted them.

On the fifth heartbeat, the king's hand closed around his throat — not squeezing, just holding, the way you hold a thing you haven't decided whether to keep or break — and the unfinished face leaned down until the sliding features filled Tam's whole sky.

"When I unmake the gods," it whispered in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, a woman's voice, warm and worn and gentle, a mother's voice, "I will remember you, little anchor. I keep what I love. Ask Silas what that means. Ask him what he *kept*."

The world went white at the edges. Tam's knees hit the stone. Through the roaring in his ears he heard the king say, distant now, conversational:

"Hollow him. Slowly. I want him smiling when they arrive — I want the thief to see what his decisions cost. And someone bring the boy his boots. Let him walk to his cell like a soldier."

* * *

The cell was underground and dry, which Tam supposed was a mercy, and the walls were grey like everything else in this grey kingdom of grey light. They brought his boots, as promised. They also brought bread and water, which confused him until he understood: the king wanted him healthy. Wanted him *watching*.

He ate the bread. Heroes ate when they could; you never knew when the next meal was coming, and starving was just losing slower.

Then he sat against the wall, pulled his boots on — they'd been cleaned, which somehow made him angrier than taking them had — and started counting.

Steps from the cell door to the end of the corridor: thirty-one, based on the sound of the guard's rounds. Hours since they'd taken him: he guessed six, maybe seven, which meant midwinter eve was tomorrow, which meant Silas was close, because Silas was always close when it mattered, even when he pretended otherwise, especially then.

And there was one more thing. One thing the king hadn't found, because Tam hadn't known he was carrying it until the presence had brushed past it and recoiled.

Down deep, under the boots and the smoke and the counting, there was a moment from the Shrine of Teeth — the night of the rescue, when the guardian's curse had marked Silas and Silas had cried out, and Tam had grabbed his hand. For half a second, through skin contact, the curse had *leaked*. Tam had felt the impostor's mind for one heartbeat — cold, vast, and looking *back*.

The link ran both ways.

The king thought it had been reading Tam. But a door that opens from one side opens from the other, and Tam had spent the whole terrible audience keeping that half-second locked down, buried in boot-leather, because he hadn't known what it was until now, alone in the dark, when he finally let himself touch it.

And through it, faint as a lamp through fog, he felt a mind he knew. Weary. Guilty. Moving fast through the dark beneath the city, with a girl and a traitor-inquisitor and a stolen god's heart, straight toward the rite chamber.

*Told you,* Tam thought at it, pouring everything he had through the crack — *told you you'd come back. Hurry up. I'm fine. I'm not fine. Hurry.*

Far away, faintly, he felt the mind stumble.

Feel him.

Feel *him*.

And in the cell below the palace, Tam of Nowhere put his back against the wall and grinned through dried tears at the grey dark, because somewhere above him the monster on the throne had just gone rigid with a question it had never expected to ask —

*How does the boy know where I am?*

— and the answer was already running up the stairs.
