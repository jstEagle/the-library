## 31. Vess's Crisis of Faith

The cells beneath the palace had no bells.

Vess noticed that first, before anything else — the absence. Above her, the capital rang with a thousand tuned bells, every hour of every day, drowning out whatever still whispered in the world's old places. Down here there was only stone and dripping water and the sound of her own boots, and in that silence she could hear it again: the thin, sweet voice that had answered her prayer twenty years ago, still lodged behind her sternum like a swallowed coal.

She had come down to interrogate the boy herself.

Officially, the prisoner was to be processed by the Crown's own people — the king's men, the quiet ones in grey who didn't wear the sunburst of the Inquisition and didn't answer to its writ. Unofficially, Inquisitor First Rank Vess had spent thirty years building a reputation for getting truth out of anyone, and the boy had been sitting in a cell for two days without saying a word worth the space he occupied. So she'd written the order herself. She'd told herself it was efficiency.

She told herself a great many things these days.

* * *

They brought her to the observation gallery first — a slit of smoked glass above a chamber she hadn't known existed, carved into the bedrock below the crypts. She'd served the Inquisition in this city for eleven years. She had walked these halls ten thousand times. She had never once suspected there was a room like this under her feet.

"Standard procedure," said the grey man beside her. He was called Brother Sallow, which Vess had always assumed was an affectation until she'd seen his face in certain lights. "The subject resists ordinary extraction. His memories are... anchored."

"Anchored to what?"

"You'll see, Inquisitor. His Majesty prefers witnesses for this part. It aids the process." A pause, and then, with something almost like warmth: "You especially. He asked for you by name."

That should have been the first warning. Kings did not ask for her by name. Kings did not know her name. But she was tired — three weeks of hunting a thief through half the country, a burned city at her back, Marlow's broken ledger still haunting her — and the boy was the last thread leading to the vial, and pride is a door that opens easiest when you're too exhausted to check who's knocking.

Below, in the lamplit chamber, they wheeled in a chair. Strapped into it, small and stubborn as a burr, sat Tam.

Vess had seen the boy on the road for six days. He'd sung songs badly. He'd asked her — not knowing what she was — whether all inquisitors were tall or just the ones sent to frighten people. He'd shared dried apples with her at a checkpoint, solemnly, the way children share things, as if the act itself were a small sacrament. When the guards took him at the palace gates he had not cried. He had looked at her across the courtyard with an expression she had spent two nights failing to name, and now, watching him strapped into that chair, she finally understood it.

It wasn't fear. It was disappointment, held in reserve. The look of someone waiting to be proven wrong about you.

"The subject believes the thief will come for him," Sallow said. "This loyalty is the anchor. We will loosen it."

"How?"

Sallow smiled. Below them, attendants in grey brought forward a crown.

Not the crown of Aldric — not the iron-and-gold thing from the coins and the banners. This was older, uglier, a circlet of black metal set with teeth that might have been human once, and it hummed. Vess felt it in her fillings, in the scar tissue of her old wound, in the coal behind her sternum, which flared hot for the first time in years.

"That's not—"

"Crown regalia," Sallow said smoothly. "A working copy. His Majesty finds it useful for... instruction."

The impostor king himself entered then, and Vess watched him the way she'd always watched him — from a distance, through glass or across a hall, never close enough to see what she was now terrified she would see. He wore Aldric's face the way a well-made glove fits a hand. He crossed the chamber and stood over the boy, and Tam spat at him, and the king laughed — a warm, easy, kingly laugh, the laugh from the festival broadcasts, the laugh that made crowds weep with love.

Then he put the black crown against the boy's temple, and began to hollow him.

* * *

Vess had seen hollowing before.

That was the thing she would return to later, in the long dark hours after, turning it over like a stone with something underneath: she had *seen it*, many times, and never once recognized it, because the Inquisition had taught her a different word for it. *Cleansing.* *Purification.* *The mercy of silence.* When a village prayed to something hungry and got an answer, the Inquisition came and made the villagers smooth. When a god-touched child was born screaming with another's voice in its mouth, the Inquisition came and made the child quiet. She had signed the orders. She had stood witness. She had believed — truly believed, the way you believe in the floor beneath your feet — that what they did was amputation. Cutting away the infected part to save the body.

She had never watched it done slowly, deliberately, to one small boy, by the man she served.

Tam's memories came out of him like smoke drawn through a keyhole. Vess could see them — everyone in the gallery could; the chamber was built to make them visible — pale ribbons rising off the boy's head, each one glowing faintly with its own color. A ribbon of gold: his mother's kitchen, bread and song. A ribbon of green: a hillside, a dog, a summer that lasted forever. A ribbon of blue-grey: six days on a road, dried apples shared with a stranger, a story about a hero named Silas Pemberton.

The king gathered the ribbons in his hands like a man gathering laundry. He examined each one. And then he ate them.

Not metaphorically. Vess watched the impostor press a golden ribbon of memory to his lips and inhale it, and his face changed — softened, warmed, filled with something real — and she understood with a lurch that this was how the thing stayed convincing. It wasn't wearing Aldric's face. It was wearing everyone's. It was a patchwork of stolen childhoods, and every memory it devoured made the mask fit better.

"He eats them," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "You told me the subjects were cleansed. You told me the memories were destroyed."

"They are preserved," Sallow said. "Nothing is wasted, Inquisitor. Waste is sin."

*Sin.* The word landed wrong, slid sideways, and in the sliding she saw it — saw the whole architecture of her life tilt two degrees, which is all it takes for a tower to come down.

Twenty years ago she had been a girl in a plague village, kneeling at a cracked shrine, praying to anything that would listen. Something had listened. Something had answered, and the answer had cost her family their faces — she'd woken to find her mother smiling a smile that belonged to no one, her father laughing a laugh borrowed from somewhere else, and Vess alone in the house with strangers wearing her loved ones. That was why she'd joined the Inquisition. Not ambition. Not faith in doctrine. Terror. She had sworn a vow over her family's smooth, happy corpses-that-still-walked: *no god will ever do that again. No power will ever reach into a human head and take what lives there.*

And here it was. Not a god. Worse than a god — a god at least was honest about being hungry. Here was a man-shaped hunger wearing the crown of the realm, eating a child one memory at a time, and around it, holding the doors, carrying its writ, standing witness in galleries like this one —

The Inquisition. Her Inquisition. Thirty years of her life, arranged like kindling around this thing's feet.

"No," she said.

"Inquisitor?"

"This isn't containment. This was never containment." She was gripping the rail hard enough to hurt. "We've been feeding it. Every cleansing, every seizure, every god-touched child we 'made quiet' — where did the memories go? Where did they *go*, Sallow?"

He looked at her with mild, patient pity, the way one looks at a slow student. "To the Crown," he said. "Where else? Order requires a treasury, Inquisitor. You of all people understand treasuries."

*You of all people.* Because she'd been the best at taking. Because her seizures ran clean, her interrogations produced full ledgers of extracted memory, her paperwork was immaculate. She had thought she was a surgeon. She had been a harvest hand.

Below, the king pressed the blue-grey ribbon to his mouth — the road, the apples, the story of the thief — and paused. Tilted his head. And looked up, directly at the smoked glass, directly at her, though nothing should have been visible from his side.

He smiled at her with Tam's mother's kindness layered over Aldric's charm, and spoke, and his voice carried up through the stone as if the room were inside her skull:

*"Inquisitor Vess. I've tasted your work before. You have excellent hands."*

* * *

She didn't remember leaving the gallery. She remembered corridors, her boots striking stone, the coal behind her sternum burning colder now instead of hotter — because the thing that had answered her prayer twenty years ago, the voice she'd carried like a brand and a shame, suddenly made sense. It had never been a god answering. It had been *this* — the crown's hunger reaching through the crack she'd opened, testing, tasting. Her whole catastrophe had been a supply run. Her tragedy had been inventory.

She stopped in an alcove and was sick, quietly, like a professional, and then stood wiping her mouth with the back of her hand while a lifetime rearranged itself around a single fact:

*I built the cage. I am the cage.*

Thirty years. The villages. The children. Sister Anthea, who'd wept after every cleansing and hanged herself in the seminary bell tower, and Vess had reported the suicide as weakness of faith rather than ask what Anthea had seen. The god-touched infants handed over to the grey brothers "for care," whose care she had never once followed past the door. All of it — every act of rigor she'd been proud of — had been a conveyor belt running straight down into this room, into this chair, into this boy.

Tam.

She went back down. Past the gallery, past the startled grey brother at the stair, down into the cold, because there was one question left and she needed to watch it die or live.

In the chamber, the attendants were unstrapping the boy. Most of his ribbons were gone — the gold, the green, the blue-grey, all eaten. What remained hovering above his head was a single thread, thin as spider silk, dull brown, almost invisible. The king stood considering it, and even now, even hollowed past recognition, Tam was glaring at him with wet furious eyes, jaw set, refusing to give them the scream they wanted.

"What is that one?" Vess heard herself ask.

Sallow consulted a slate. "Residue. A low-value strand. The subject's belief that the thief will rescue him." He smiled. "His Majesty leaves those till last. Hope spoils the flavor of everything else if you eat it too soon."

The king reached for the thread.

Later, Vess would try to reconstruct the moment and fail. There was no decision she could point to, no clean line between the woman who stood witness and the woman who drew her blade. There was just the sight of that thin brown thread — a child's hope, worthless, low-value, residue — and thirty years of her life saying *stand still,* and one voice, her own, small and clear as the girl she'd been in the plague village, saying *no.*

Her sword came out of its sheath and took off the nearest attendant's hand at the wrist before the man knew she'd moved.

The chamber erupted. She fought the way she'd taught novices to fight — ugly, fast, economical — backing toward the chair, cutting the straps, hauling Tam's slack arm over her shoulder. The king did not move to stop her. That was the worst part. He stood among the scattered ribbons of a child's life and watched her with polite interest, and said, in a voice like warm bread:

*"Run, then, Inquisitor. You'll only bring me more. You always have."*

She got Tam to the stair. Behind her, alarms began — not bells, the palace had no bells down here, but a low subsonic moan in the stones, the building itself waking. Grey brothers poured into the corridors ahead. She put Tam down behind a cistern, put her back to the wall, and counted her options the way she'd counted them her whole career, and found exactly one.

There was a man in this city who had slipped her checkpoints three times. A thief who stole memories, who owed her nothing, whom she'd tried to recruit with a knife at his throat and then with a bargain he'd refused at cost. Whose face she'd glimpsed at the ash checkpoint and sworn to hunt to the ends of the earth.

She had a map of the rite chamber folded against her heart. She had a boy bleeding hope out of his eyes. She had no apology, because sorry was a coin and she'd spent her whole purse on the other side.

"Inquisitor First Rank Vess," she said to the dark, to the moaning stones, to the girl she used to be. "Defecting."

Then she picked up the boy and ran toward the one man in the capital she knew would hate her enough to help her.
