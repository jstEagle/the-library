## 32. Uneasy Alliance

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and it was wrong in every way a knock could be.

Too soft. Too even. Three raps spaced like a heartbeat that had learned patience. Nobody knocked like that at a safehouse — not guards, who kicked; not thieves, who scratched; not friends, who called your name through the door because they trusted the night. Only one kind of person knocked like that: someone who already knew you'd open it.

Silas lay still on the pallet with his hand around the knife under his pillow and listened to Wren breathing across the room. She was awake too. He could tell by the quality of her silence — sleep made noise, even hers, small sighs and shifting cloth. This was held breath.

"Through the back," she mouthed.

He shook his head. If it was Vess, running would only confirm what she already suspected: that they were here, in the tanner's loft above the old dye-works, three days after Tam had been taken from them on the shrine road. Three days of Silas sleeping in shifts with a knife and hating himself in a rhythm so steady it had almost become comfortable.

The knock came again. Same spacing. Same terrible gentleness.

"Open the door, thief," said Inquisitor Vess, "or I'll open it myself, and then we'll both have to explain the splinters."

Wren was up before he was, knife drawn, positioning herself where the door's swing would hide her. Good instincts. He'd taught her some of them without meaning to, back when teaching her things had been the last thing he wanted to do. He crossed the room, put his eye to the gap in the boards.

Vess stood alone in the alley below, hood down, gray hair plastered to her skull by the drizzle. No escort. No lantern. And over her shoulder, slung like a sack of turnips, limp as laundry —

Tam.

Silas's whole body went cold and then hot, in that order, fast enough to hurt.

He unbarred the door.

* * *

She laid the boy down on Wren's pallet with a care that didn't match anything else about her. Tam was breathing. That was the first thing Silas checked, hands shaking worse than he'd ever let them shake in front of anyone, pressing two fingers to the boy's throat like a man testing whether his own heart still worked. Tam's skin was clammy but warm. There was a bruise blooming along his jaw and a rawness around his eyes, as if someone had been reading him — actually reading him, page by page — but he was breathing, and when Silas touched his shoulder the boy murmured something about horses and turned toward the warmth of the blanket Wren dropped over him.

"He'll wake hungry and confused and furious," Vess said. "In roughly that order."

"What did you do to him?" Wren's voice came out flat and dangerous, and Silas thought, with a strange detached pride, *she sounds like me on my best day.*

"Nothing. That's rather the point." Vess pulled back her hood entirely and sat down on an overturned dye barrel like a woman settling in for a long sermon. Up close, in the guttering light of the single candle Wren allowed, she looked worse than she had at the checkpoint. Thinner. The gray in her hair had colonized new territory. And her eyes — those pale, patient, terrible eyes — had a crack in them now, a hairline fracture running through all that certainty.

"I interrogated him," she said. "Properly. Memory-walk, full depth. I needed to find you, and the boy was the surest map." She said it without flinching, which Silas respected and hated in equal measure. "And what I found in his head was you. Not the thief. Not the smuggler. You, pulling him out from under a collapsing wagon on the pilgrim road. You, giving half your bread to a family that wasn't yours. You, standing between him and my checkpoint guard and lying so smoothly the boy believed every word." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Children remember what we choose to forget, Mr. Pemberton. They keep the receipts."

"You read a child's mind to hunt us," Silas said. "Forgive me if I don't weep at your review."

"Yes." She didn't argue. That was the worst part. She just accepted it, the way stone accepts rain. "I did. And then three nights ago I stood in the king's audience chamber and watched him do the same thing to a grown man — a traitor, technically, though the treason was only that he'd prayed to the wrong god — and I finally understood what I've been serving." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the Inquisitor vanished and there was only a tired woman in a cold room. "It isn't the crown that hollows people, Pemberton. It's the crown that gets hollowed. Something is eating this kingdom from the top down, and I have spent eleven years sharpening its teeth."

The candle spat. Tam shifted in his sleep. Outside, the drizzle kept up its patient work on the city's rooftops.

"The vial," Silas said carefully. "You knew what was in it. At the checkpoint. You weren't hunting stolen memories. You were hunting *that* memory."

"I was hunting proof." Vess reached into her coat — Wren's knife came up fast, and Vess paused, palms out, until the blade lowered an inch — and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, unwrapping it with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Inside lay a sheet of parchment covered in tight architectural script, annotated in three different inks, and a smaller scrap bearing a wax seal Silas recognized from a hundred forged documents in his old life: the crowned sun of Aldric's house.

"A map of the undercroft beneath the throne room," Vess said. "The rite chamber. I've been inside once, two years ago, escorting a prisoner. I didn't understand what I was seeing then. I do now." Her finger traced the parchment's central chamber, a circle within a circle within a circle. "Three concentric rings of binding-script, keyed to the old gods' true names. On midwinter night, when the veil between the world and the divine is thinnest, whoever stands at the center wearing the crown can speak those names into the gold and *close* them. Permanently. Every god, every shrine, every prayer — silenced forever. Not killed. Erased. As if they had never been."

"And the impostor needs the relics destroyed first," Wren said. "That's why the tongue-bone and the eye-stone matter. Why he sent agents after us."

"He doesn't need them destroyed. He needs them *unclaimed*. A relic in mortal hands is a god's foothold in the world. Bring all three together at the center of the rite, however, and—" Vess stopped. Looked at Silas. "You've seen the vision. The eye-stone showed you."

"It showed us the rite," Silas said. "Not the ending."

"No. It wouldn't." She folded the map closed. "Because there isn't one written yet. That's the truth nobody in the Inquisition will say aloud: the outcome depends on choices none of us have made. Which brings me to why I'm standing in a tannery at midnight instead of reporting your location to my superiors."

"Which you know," Silas said. "You've known since you walked Tam out of the palace."

"I've known since I read the boy's memories." She rose, and the tired woman folded herself away again, and the Inquisitor stood in her place — but changed, Silas thought. Cracked. Running on something other than certainty now. Desperation, maybe. Or grief. "Here are the terms, and I'll state them plainly because I know how you think, thief. You don't trust gifts. So this isn't one."

She set the map on the barrel between them.

"I am not asking you to forgive me. I took a child hostage to force your cooperation, and I would do it again tomorrow, because I believed — I still believe — that stopping what happens on midwinter night matters more than any single life, including mine, including his." She glanced at Tam, and something moved behind the crack in her composure. "But I was wrong about one thing, and it has cost me everything I built. I thought the impostor was a usurper using the gods' names. He isn't. He's afraid of them. The way a thief is afraid of the owner coming home. Whatever sits on that throne knows exactly what it is, and it is trying to lock the door before the true owner returns — and I have spent eleven years guarding the door *for* it."

"So you're defecting," Wren said.

"I'm choosing sides. There's a difference. Defection implies I expect shelter." Vess pulled her hood back up, and the candlelight slid off her face, leaving only the pale eyes. "I expect nothing. I go back to the palace tonight. I resume my post. And on midwinter eve, I will unlock the service gate to the undercroft from the inside and leave it unbarred for exactly one quarter-hour. After that, you're on your own, and if you fail, I will deny knowing you with my final breath, and they will hollow me for it gladly."

"Why?" The word came out of Silas rougher than he intended. "Eleven years, Vess. Eleven years hunting people like me, breaking people like Marlow, taking children's memories at checkpoints. One bad day in the throne room and you burn it all? People don't work that way."

For a long moment she said nothing. The drizzle ticked against the roof. Somewhere below, a dog complained about the rain.

"When I was nine years old," Vess said at last, quietly, "my village prayed for rain. We were dying of drought, and we prayed to whatever was listening, and something answered." Her hands, resting at her sides, curled slowly into fists. "It rained for forty days. My mother drowned in the barley field she'd been praying over. Half the village went with her. And when the Inquisition came to record the miracle, they asked me — a nine-year-old girl who'd lost everything — to help them build a world where prayers were never answered again. Where no child would ever kneel in the mud and beg a thing like *that* to listen." She met his eyes. "I said yes. I have said yes for thirty years. And I was right, Pemberton. The gods are hungry and careless and vast, and their attention is a plague. But I was wrong about the cure. You cannot make the world safe by silencing every voice in it. You only make it quiet. And I have seen what lives in the quiet now. It wears a crown."

Silas looked at the map. At Tam, breathing slow and even under Wren's blanket. At Wren herself, knife still bare in her hand, watching Vess with an expression he couldn't fully read — fury and something adjacent to pity, wrestling.

"If this is a trap," he said, "it's an elaborate one."

"All the good ones are." Vess moved to the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame. "One more thing. The boy. When I walked his memories, I left marks. Nothing lasting — he'll dream of gray rooms for a while, nothing more. But the king's readers walked him after me. Deeper." Her jaw tightened. "They found the link. The curse-mark the shrine guardian left on you. They reported it, and the king has been listening through it ever since — and through it, he has felt *you* listening back."

Ice moved down Silas's spine. He thought of the whispers, the mother-voice threading through his dreams, the sense lately of being watched from inside his own skull. He'd told himself it was the curse fading.

"He knows where you are," Vess said. "Not precisely — the mark blurs, and your valley-blood confuses it, thank whatever's left of the old gods for the Hollow in your bones. But he knows the city. He knows you're close. And he has stopped sending agents." She opened the door onto the dark and the drizzle. "He's sending the hollowed. Whole patrols of them. Men who smile the same smile. They don't sleep, they don't eat, and they don't stop. You have six days, thief. Midwinter comes whether you're ready or not."

"Vess." He said it before he could decide whether to. She paused. "Why bring him back? You could have kept Tam. Leverage. Insurance. It's what I would have done."

She considered that with the seriousness she gave everything.

"Because I watched him refuse to break," she said, "for three days, at nine years old, while men twice my skill tried to crack him open. And I thought: *this is what we're spending.* Children like this. To protect a thing that eats them." Her voice didn't waver, but it cost her visibly. "I'm done spending children. Even as leverage. Especially mine."

Then she stepped into the rain and was gone, footsteps dissolving into the drum of water on cobbles, and the door swung shut, and Silas barred it and stood with his forehead against the wood for a long time.

* * *

Tam woke near dawn, exactly as promised: hungry, confused, furious.

"Furious" lasted about four seconds, which was how long it took him to register whose loft he was in. Then he launched himself off the pallet and hit Silas mid-torso hard enough to stagger them both into the wall, arms clamped around his ribs, face buried in his shirt, saying nothing at all, which from Tam was louder than a sermon.

"Ow," Silas said, and put a hand on the boy's head, and discovered his own eyes were burning, and decided very firmly that it was the candle smoke.

Wren fed Tam porridge with honey stolen from somewhere Silas chose not to ask about, and the boy ate like a wolf and talked like a river — the gray rooms, the smiling men, the woman with the pale eyes who had come in the night and cut his bonds and said *"run, and don't look back, and tell the thief the gate opens at moonrise"* — and Silas listened and nodded and felt the map in his coat pocket growing heavier by the hour.

When Tam finally crashed back into sleep, real sleep, dreamless and deep, Wren sat down across from Silas at the little table and put both hands flat on the wood.

"We're not trusting her," she said.

"No."

"She sold out Marlow's route. She took Tam. She's spent thirty years doing exactly what she says she's sorry for."

"Every word true." Silas turned the map over, studying Vess's annotations in the gray pre-dawn light. "And she gave us the only copy of a route into the rite chamber, handed back the hostage she could have used to gut us, and walked back into the lion's mouth to hold a gate open with her own body. Both things are true, Wren. That's what makes her dangerous. People who are all lies are easy. People who are half-true will get you killed in interesting ways."

"So what do we do?"

He spread the map flat. The undercroft unfolded under his fingers: service tunnels, the gate marked in Vess's precise hand, the triple ring of the rite chamber, and at the center, circled twice and annotated in ink gone brown with age — *the anchor. the heart. do not touch it before the rings are broken.*

"The truth outranks the grievance," he said slowly, feeling his way toward it as much as deciding it. "That's what she is. A grievance walking around telling the truth. We use the truth. We watch our backs with the grievance." He looked up at Wren, and saw his own wariness mirrored there, and something else too — the same exhausted arithmetic he was doing. Six days. One gate. Three relics in a beggar's cart downstairs, wrapped in onion sacks. "We go in through her gate. And if she's betrayed us, we die knowing we were right about her, which is a stupid way to die, so let's make sure we're wrong."

Wren snorted despite herself. Then her face sobered, and she reached across the table and tapped the map's edge, where Vess had written one final line in that same brown ink:

*He knows you're coming. He wants you to. Ask yourself why.*

Outside, the bells of the capital began the dawn peal — tuned flat and dead, as always, swallowing every voice but their own. But underneath the bronze, faint as a pulse, Silas heard the other sound. The whisper. The mother-voice, patient and loving and utterly wrong:

*Come home, little thief. I've kept your seat warm.*

Six days.
