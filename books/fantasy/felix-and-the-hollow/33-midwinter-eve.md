## 33. Midwinter Eve

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above a shuttered chandlery in the Tanners' Quarter, and it smelled like it. Vess had chosen it for exactly that reason — no one from the Inquisition came to the Tanners' Quarter unless they were dragged, and even then they complained about it.

Silas sat on a crate of tallow with the three relics laid out before him on a strip of oilcloth, and he found he could not look away from them.

The tongue-bone first: pale as river ivory, curved like a question, humming faintly when you got close — a hum you felt in your teeth rather than heard. The eye-stone beside it, blind and milky until Wren walked past, at which point its surface swirled like a frozen pond remembering spring. And beneath them both, wrapped in three layers of wool because none of them liked to be near it uncovered, the heart.

It beat. Slowly. Once every eleven seconds, Silas had counted. It never varied. Somewhere under the palace, in a crypt full of dead kings, something had been keeping time with this thing for years, and tomorrow at moonrise the impostor meant to bind every god's name into his crown and snuff them out like candles between wet fingers.

"Say it again," Tam said. He was sitting cross-legged on the floor, sharpening a knife he didn't know how to use, because Silas had told him once that busy hands keep fear quiet and the boy had taken it as scripture. "The plan. All of it."

"You've heard it four times," Wren said.

"So say it a fifth time."

Silas looked at him. The boy had lost weight since the crypt; they all had. But there was something set in his face now that hadn't been there in the pilgrim caravan, some load-bearing beam of stubbornness that had been tested by the impostor's fingers in his memory and had not cracked. Silas hated that he'd put it there. He loved that it was there. Both things were true and he'd stopped trying to make them fit.

"The rite happens at moonrise on midwinter night," Silas said. "In the old chapel under the palace — the one they bricked up after Aldric's coronation. The crown goes on an anvil of black iron. The king speaks the gods' names into it, one by one, and each name that goes in is a god that can never answer anyone again." He touched the tongue-bone, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "These are the counterweights. Tongue, eye, heart. Vess gets us into the guard rotation — she still has friends who owe her, or fear her, which is better. Wren carries the eye-stone, because it likes her. I carry the other two. We go down through the cistern tunnel on her map, we reach the chapel before the last name is spoken, and we unbind what's bound."

"And if we're late?" Tam asked.

"Then every prayer anyone ever prays afterward falls into silence," Vess said from the doorway. She'd been standing there a while; Silas had heard her breathing change but hadn't let on. "And the thing wearing Aldric's face rules a world where nothing answers back. Forever. That's the whole of it. No second attempt. No next year."

She came into the light. Out of uniform, in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, she looked less like an inquisitor and more like what Silas suspected she'd been before the Inquisition found her: a farm girl from somewhere flat and cold who had prayed once, hard, and been answered so terribly that she'd spent twenty years making sure no one else ever got what they asked for.

"Vess," Silas said. "Your friends in the guard. When this is done—"

"When this is done I'll hang for it and I know it," she said, mildly, the way you'd discuss weather. "I've made my peace. Have all of you?"

Nobody answered, which was answer enough.

* * *

They ate the last good meal any of them could afford, which was to say Wren stole most of it and Silas pretended not to notice. Roast fowl, black bread, pickled things in a jar, a bottle of plum wine that Marlow had once paid Silas with and that Silas had carried across half a kingdom without knowing why. He knew why now. You carry the bottle to the night before the end, and you drink it with people who have become, without your permission, the shape your life has taken.

Wren poured. Tam got a thimbleful and watered it, over his loud objections. Vess drank hers like medicine. Silas held his and watched the candlelight swim in it.

"I want to do the thing," Tam said suddenly.

"What thing?"

"The thing people do. Where you say—" He waved the knife vaguely. "What you'd lose. What you're willing to lose. Vess said it earlier, about hanging. That's the thing."

"That's not a custom, Tam, that's just Vess being grim."

"It should be a custom." He set the knife down with great ceremony. "I'll go first. I'm willing to lose—" He thought hard, brow furrowed, and Silas braced himself, because the boy had already been hollowed once, partially, painfully, and had every right to name anything else. "I'm willing to lose being afraid," Tam said finally. "Not of dying. Of being small. I used to think if I stayed small and quiet nothing bad would find me, and then bad things found me anyway, and now I know being small doesn't work. So I'm willing to lose that. I want to be someone who stands in front of people."

The silence after it was the good kind. Wren reached over and squeezed the back of his neck, and he pretended to hate it and leaned into it anyway.

"Wren?" he prompted.

She turned her cup slowly. "You know what I'd say."

"Say it anyway. It's the custom."

"I invented the custom five seconds ago," Tam said.

Wren huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then she looked at Silas, straight on, and held it. "I'm willing to lose the version of me that existed before I met you," she said. "The angry one. The one who came to your door wanting her childhood back like it was a coat somebody stole off a line." A pause. "I'm not saying I forgive it all the way. I'm saying I don't want her back. Whoever I am now — the me that knows you, that knows Tam, that's walked a thousand miles with an inquisitor and a thief — she's worth more than the girl I was. If tomorrow costs me everything except that, I'll pay it."

Silas's throat did something complicated. He drank instead of speaking.

"Vess," Tam said.

Vess turned her cup the way Wren had, but slower, like a woman reading leaves. "I'm willing to lose my certainty," she said. "I built my whole life on being right — that the gods were a wound in the world, that answering prayers was predation, that silence was mercy. Tomorrow I go into a hole under a palace to save the gods I swore to unmake, because I learned the silence was never mercy, it was theft." Her mouth twisted. "If I die down there, I die having been wrong about the most important thing I ever believed. I'm willing to lose that. I think I lost it months ago, honestly. This is just the funeral."

"Silas," Tam said.

Everyone looked at him. The candle guttered. Below them, faintly, the capital's bells rang the hour — tuned, all thousand of them, to press down on anything divine, and Silas felt the pressure of them in his molars the way he always did now, like weather coming.

He thought about lying. It would have been easy. He was very good at it.

"I'm willing to lose the memory of my mother," he said.

Wren went still. Vess's eyes narrowed — she knew pieces of it, not all of it.

"It's the seed," Silas said. "The thing the impostor grew from. My teacher stole it out of me when I was nine — the last of her, her voice, her hands, the song she hummed while she worked. Everything that thing is, it's built on top of what she was. And tomorrow, if it comes to it, I feed it back. I starve it with its own root." He turned the cup. "I've been carrying her for thirty years without looking at her. Carrying her cost me the drowned god's price already — I couldn't tell you her face anymore, only that I had one. So yes. That's what I'm willing to lose. What I'm asking—" and here his voice did fail him, briefly, and he pushed through it because Tam had earned the truth, "—what I'm asking is that if I come back up out of that chapel empty, you remind me who she was. Even secondhand. Even wrong. Don't let the absence be the only thing left."

"We will," Tam said instantly, fiercely, before anyone else could speak.

"Yes," Wren said. Quieter. "We will."

* * *

Later, when the wine was gone and Tam slept by the banked stove with the knife still in his hand, Silas stood at the loft's one window and watched snow come down over the capital of bells.

Wren joined him. For a while neither spoke. The city below was bright with festival — lanterns strung across every street, midwinter garlands on every door, ten thousand people preparing to kneel tomorrow for their king's blessing, not one of them knowing what the blessing would cost.

"Do you remember," Wren said, "the first night on the road, after Casterhale burned? You asked me why I was still following you."

"I remember hoping you'd stop."

"I said it was because you owed me." She smiled at the snow. "That was true for about a week. Then it stopped being true and I kept following you anyway, and I never told you why."

"Why?"

"Because you were the only person I'd ever met who was honest about being a coward." She shrugged. "Everyone else pretends. You never did. You'd look at a burning building and say, plainly, I am afraid and I might run. And then you'd walk in anyway. Every time. I figured a man like that was worth following, because at least you'd never lie to me about which parts were hard."

Silas watched the snow fill the streets, softening the edges of everything, making the city look almost kind.

"There's something I haven't told you," he said. "About tomorrow."

Her head turned.

"The impostor offered me a deal. Back in the rite chamber vision — and again through the curse-link, the night before the shrine. A kingdom built from shared memory. Everyone's memories, pooled, given, no more loss, no more theft. He built it out of my mother's memory, Wren. It looks like her kitchen. It smells like bread." His breath fogged the glass. "And I want you to know that when he offers it again tomorrow — and he will — some part of me is going to want to say yes. Not because I believe him. Because I'm tired. So if you see me hesitate down there, that's what the hesitation is. It isn't doubt about the cause. It's just tiredness wearing a beautiful face."

Wren took his hand. Hers was cold and calloused and certain.

"Then I'll stand where you can see me," she said. "And I'll be the ugly real thing, and you can choose it."

Below them, across the sleeping-bright city, the bells began to ring midnight — all thousand of them at once, a wall of bronze sound rolling over the rooftops, pressing down, pressing down. And underneath the bells, so faint that only someone who had spent a lifetime listening to things that weren't there could have caught it, Silas heard something else.

A hum. Faint as a dying fly against a window. Rising from the direction of the palace.

The heart, wrapped in wool on the crate behind them, had begun to beat faster than once every eleven seconds.

It had begun to count down.
