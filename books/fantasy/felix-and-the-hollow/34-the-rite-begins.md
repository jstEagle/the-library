## 34. The Rite Begins

**Silas**

The bells stopped at moonrise.

That was the first sign that something had gone wrong with the world. The capital of Bells rang every hour of every day — Silas had learned that much in three days of hiding in a beggar's cart and a cellar that smelled of onions and old prayer. The bells never stopped. They were tuned to a frequency that pressed down on the divine like a boot on a throat, and the whole city lived inside their sound the way fish live inside water.

At moonrise on midwinter night, they all went silent at once.

Not faded. Not rung out. *Stopped*, forty towers at once, as if a hand had closed around the city's throat.

Silas stood at the slatted window of the safehouse Vess had given them — a deconsecrated shrine two streets from the palace, its saint's alcove empty for years — and felt the silence come down over the rooftops like snow.

"They've turned them off," Wren said behind him. "The bells."

"No." Vess was checking the edge of her sword by candlelight, running her thumb along it the way she did when she wanted her hands busy. "They've been *re-tuned*. All midwinter the bellfounders have been working under royal writ. I thought it was festival vanity." She set the blade down. "It wasn't. They're not suppressing the gods' voices anymore. They're gathering them."

"Gathering," Tam repeated. He sat on the floor with his back against the wall, knees up, trying to look brave about it and mostly managing. He'd been free of the impostor's cells for less than a week. He still flinched at bells. "Gathering how?"

"Sound is just a name said out loud," Vess said. "Every god has one. You ring enough bronze in the right pattern, you can say a great many names very quickly."

Silas looked out at the city. From here he could see the palace rising above the terraced roofs, black against a sky that should have been full of stars. It wasn't. There was a hole in the sky above the palace — not clouds, nothing so honest as clouds. Just an absence, a circle of perfect dark maybe half a mile wide, edged with a thin corona of light that bent the wrong way, inward, like light falling into a well.

He'd seen that shape before. In the vial. In the memory of Aldric being hollowed out, the smoke-thing bending over the king's open mouth while the light around it fell inward.

It was doing it again. To everything this time.

"It's started," Silas said.

No one argued. That was the thing about midwinter night — there was no more arguing left to do. The relics lay wrapped in oilcloth on the table: the tongue-bone, pale as a knuckle; the eye-stone, dark and wet-looking even through cloth; the heart, which no one had touched since the crypt, because everyone who came near it could feel it beating, slow and enormous, like something asleep under the floor of the world.

Wren laid her hand flat on the table beside them, not touching. "Six hours until dawn," she said. "Vess says the rite peaks at the darkest hour. So we go in before that, or we don't go in at all."

"We go in," Silas said.

He heard his own voice and was surprised by it. Not because it was steady — he'd spent thirty years making his voice steady, it was a thief's tool like any other — but because underneath the steadiness there was nothing. He kept reaching for fear and finding a room where fear used to be. He kept reaching for his mother's face and finding the shape where it used to be. He had fed both to the thing in the crown, or he was about to, or some part of him already had — the memory was a coin halfway across a counter, and the whole night felt like that moment, suspended, irrevocable.

*You could still walk away,* said a voice in his head that was not his own. It wore his mother's voice the way the impostor wore Aldric's face. *You always could. That's what I love about you.*

"I know," Silas said aloud.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it out loud.

"The curse," Wren said quietly. "It's talking to you again."

"It's always talking." He turned from the window. "Let's make it stop."

* * *

**Wren**

They went down into the city through the dry cisterns, because the streets were already wrong.

Wren understood how wrong only when they surfaced near the Processional Way and saw the crowds.

Midwinter festival, and the people were out — thousands of them, filling the avenue from wall to wall, all facing the palace, all waiting for the king's blessing. That part was normal. What was wrong was the stillness. No vendors shouting. No children running between legs. No drunks singing. Ten thousand people standing in the falling snow, faces tipped up toward that hole in the sky, and every single face wearing the same smile.

Not similar. The same. Wren had seen the hollowed village weeks ago and told herself it was a trick of exhaustion, that no two smiles could truly be identical. She had been lying to herself kindly. Here it was, multiplied past denial: ten thousand mouths curved to the same degree, ten thousand pairs of eyes reflecting the same inward-bending light, snow landing on their shoulders and eyelashes and none of them brushing it away.

"They're not being held," Vess breathed. She had gone gray. Wren had watched this woman interrogate men without raising her voice, had watched her kill a fellow inquisitor to buy them an hour, and now her sword-hand shook. "I thought the hollowing took — I thought it needed time, rituals, contact. Look at them. He's just *drinking*. The rite isn't building to anything. It's already working. We're watching the first course."

Tam's small hand found Wren's. She squeezed it and hated herself for how much she needed the squeeze too.

A bell began to toll somewhere — one tower, then another, then all of them, but the sound was different now, deeper, layered, each strike carrying harmonics that didn't belong to bronze. Wren felt the names in it. She couldn't explain how she knew, but she knew: each toll was a name being said, and the names were being pulled out of the air and up, up into the crown, and with each name something went out of the world. She couldn't have said what. Only that after the fourth toll, the snow smelled less like snow. After the ninth, she couldn't remember the tune her mother used to hum — except she'd never known her mother, so perhaps it had never been hers to lose, and that was worse somehow, that the theft was so thorough it reached backward into lives that had never happened.

"He's taking the small ones first," Silas murmured beside her. His eyes tracked the sky. "River gods. Field gods. Gods of doorways and thresholds and lost things." A pause. "Gods of thieves."

"You'd mourn him?"

"I'd mourn the job security." But his voice caught on it, and Wren saw — really saw, for the first time since the firelight confession — how hollowed he already was. Not smiling. Never smiling. Just *empty* in patches, like a moth-eaten coat, and wearing steadiness over the holes.

She had given her best memory of him to the oracle. She had thought that was the price, paid and done. Now she understood the price was still being collected, hour by hour, from everyone who loved anyone, and the collector wore a crown.

"The side gate," Vess said, dragging them back. "Crypt stair comes up inside the rite chamber itself — that's why I gave you the map. Once we're in, the relics have to reach the dais. Whatever happens—" she looked at each of them in turn, and Wren watched her decide not to soften it, "—whatever happens to any of us, the relics get there. Swear it."

They swore. Even Tam. Especially Tam, in a voice that cracked and held anyway, and Wren thought: *this is what he's made us into. People who swear things on midwinter night in the snow.*

The hole in the sky widened by a finger's width, and the light falling into it bent a little further wrong, and somewhere beneath the palace, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

* * *

**Tam**

Tam had decided, somewhere in the last mile of cistern-dark, that he was not afraid anymore, and he was sticking to that decision.

It helped that there wasn't time to be afraid. Vess moved like a story — quick, quiet, certain — and Silas moved like a different kind of story, the kind where doors opened before the hero knocked, and Wren kept a hand on Tam's shoulder the whole way, and Tam catalogued everything the way he did when he wanted to keep it forever: the green moss-light of the cisterns, the sound of their boots in shallow water, the weight of the tongue-bone wrapped against his own chest, because Silas had said *you carry this, it likes you, it sang to you at the shrine*, and Tam had nearly cried at being trusted with it and had pretended instead that he was merely cold.

Then the crypt stair, and the smell of stone older than stone should be, and the heart beating louder with every step down — no, up, they were going up, the rite chamber was above the crypt, the monster had built its throne room directly on top of a buried god, which Tam thought was the most cowardly thing he'd ever heard of.

They came up behind a wall of carved saints whose faces had been chiseled smooth. Through the gap between two of them, Tam looked into the rite chamber, and his decision not to be afraid developed a crack in it.

The chamber was the size of a cathedral and shaped like a throat. Every surface was bronze — walls, floor, pillars — and every surface was ringing, silently, visibly, a shimmer passing through the metal like wind through wheat. At the center rose the dais, and on the dais stood the king.

King Aldric, tall and golden and terrible, arms raised, crown blazing. Above the crown hung the hole in the sky brought indoors — a sphere of absolute dark turning slowly, drinking down the silent toll of ten thousand gathered names. And around the dais, kneeling in concentric rings, hundreds of courtiers and priests and guards, all smiling the same smile, all chanting without sound, their lips moving in unison like a field of flowers opening.

And threaded through the dark sphere, visible only in flashes, were *shapes*. Wings. Antlers. Eyes the size of lakes. The gods themselves, being wound in, name by name, like thread onto a spool.

"There," Vess whispered. "The dais. Ninety feet of open floor and every eye in the room—"

"Not every eye." Silas's voice had gone strange. Tam looked up and saw him staring not at the king but at the dark sphere, and Silas's face was doing something Tam had never seen it do. It was breaking.

"It's looking at me," Silas said. "It's been looking at me all night. It saved me a seat."

* * *

**Silas**

The link flared open like a door kicked off its hinges.

He was inside the impostor's attention and the impostor was inside his, and for one vertiginous moment Silas couldn't tell which memories were whose — there was a boy on a dock, there was a woman singing, there was a man with a knife teaching a child to lift a purse, and the images belonged to both of them, because they had both been that child, once, before one of them got stolen.

*Brother,* said the voice that wore his mother's tone. *Look what I built for us.*

*I'm not your brother.*

*You're my seed. My root. Everything I am grew out of what he took from you.* The impostor's gaze did not turn from the sphere, but Silas felt the attention swivel, vast and delighted. *He threw away the parts of you that hurt. Your mother's voice. Her hands. The way she said your name. He sold the rest and kept the grief because grief was heavy and heavy things are useful. And the discarded pieces didn't die, Silas. Discarded things never die. They wait in the dark and they grow hungry and they grow large, and eventually they grow a plan.*

*Your plan is to murder heaven.*

*My plan is to make sure no one ever does to another child what was done to us.* For a moment — one treacherous, awful moment — the sincerity in it was real, and Silas felt it land in the hollow places inside himself like rain on cracked ground. *Every god in that sphere has answered a prayer, and every answer costs more than the prayer was worth. I have read the ledger of heaven, brother. I am going to close it. No more bargains. No more prices. No more mothers taken. A world with no gods in it is just a world — and a world is survivable.*

*So is a lie,* Silas said. *You're wearing a dead man's face and drinking a living city. That's not mercy. That's just hunger with a philosophy.*

The smile he felt through the link was his own smile. That was the worst of it, the very worst: the impostor smiled exactly the way Silas smiled, wry and crooked and a little sad, because it had been grown from the same clay.

*Come take the crown, then,* it said. *Or stand at my right hand and we'll rule what's left. I saved your mother's memory, you know. The original. I never digested it. It's the seed I grew from — I keep it at the center, the way a tree keeps its heartwood.* A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. *Come and take it back. Come and have her say your name one last time.*

The chamber around them — the real chamber, the bronze throat, the kneeling thousands — seemed to lean in. The silent toll of the gathered names climbed a register. Somewhere overhead, the hole in the sky drank another god, and Silas felt the loss as a cold spot in the air, a word the world would never say again.

Wren's hand closed on his arm. Real. Warm. Chapped from the road.

"Whatever it's offering," she said, "the answer's no."

"You don't know what it offered."

"I know you." She lifted the eye-stone from its wrapping, and the dark glass reflected the burning dais and her own steady face. "That's how I know."

Behind them, Vess drew her sword, and the sound of it — plain steel, unmagical, human — rang against the bronze walls, and for the first time all night, something in the chamber faltered. One of the kneeling thousands lifted his head. Then another. Then a hundred, their identical smiles flickering like candles in wind, because somewhere under all the stolen will, a body remembered what it meant to hear a person choose to fight.

The impostor king lowered his arms at last and turned upon the dais, crown blazing, borrowed face serene, and looked directly at the gap between the faceless saints where five small mortals stood.

"Guests," the king said, and his voice filled the bronze throat of the room like a bell struck from inside. "I wondered how long you'd keep me waiting."

Silas stepped out of hiding, tongue-bone in one fist, and the last hour of the world began.
