## 35. Storming the Rite Chamber

The doors of the rite chamber were thirty feet of black iron, and they were already open.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the light — not torchlight, not godlight, but the pale, sourceless glow that came off the hollowed when they stood very still and waited for instructions. It pooled in the corridor beyond like milk spilled across marble.

Silas pressed his back to the cold wall and counted heartbeats. Behind him, Wren checked the edge of her knife with her thumb, the way he'd taught her two hundred miles ago on a road full of small hungry gods. Vess stood with her inquisitor's staff planted, its sigils burning white — she'd stopped hiding what she was three corridors back, when hiding stopped mattering. And Tam —

Tam was supposed to be in the crypt tunnel with the cart. Tam was not supposed to be here at all.

"You're supposed to be in the crypt," Silas breathed.

"The crypt's caved." The boy's chin came up, stubborn as a mule's. "Vess said I could carry the tongue-bone."

"Vess said no such thing."

"I said he could carry it if he kept up and kept quiet," Vess said, eyes forward. "He's done both. Mostly."

Silas wanted to argue. There was no time to argue. Down the corridor, the pale light shifted, and the hollowed guard began to move.

There were forty of them. He knew because he counted, because counting was what he did instead of panicking, and because Marlow had once told him that a thief who couldn't count died rich in other people's ledgers. Forty men and women in palace livery, moving in perfect unison, faces smooth as eggs, swords drawn in a single synchronized whisper of steel. No shouting. No challenge. The impostor didn't waste breath on challenges. It simply reached into them and pointed.

"They'll fight until they drop," Vess said quietly. "They don't feel pain. They don't feel fear. They feel nothing."

"I know." Silas pulled the eye-stone from his coat. It was warm, and it was watching, and it made his skin crawl the way it always did — like being looked at by something that remembered being worshipped. "But they're still bodies. Bodies have knees."

* * *

It started, as most terrible things in Silas's life had started, with him doing something stupid on purpose.

He stepped out of cover, held the eye-stone high, and let it *see*.

The stone had been a god's eye once, before gods got small and hungry and cheap. When he bared it to the corridor, it remembered that job. Light poured out of it — not the hollowed milk-glow but something older, gold shot through with green, the color of the first spring after a hard winter — and every hollowed guard within twenty paces flinched. Not in pain. In *recognition*. Whatever was left buried under the emptiness in them, some animal cellar of the soul, knew what an eye like that meant, and hid from it.

Forty guards. Half of them stumbled. That bought him four seconds.

Four seconds was enough for Vess.

She went through the gap like a lit fuse. Her staff came around in a flat arc and the sigils along it discharged all at once, and the nearest six guards went down as if the floor had been pulled out from under their souls. She didn't stop. She didn't look at what she'd done. Silas had watched her fight twice now, and both times he'd thought the same thing: this is what it looks like when someone has already grieved everything they might lose, and decided it wasn't enough to stop.

Wren took the left flank. She fought the way water gets into a boat — quietly, finding every seam. A heel behind a knee. A knife across a hamstring. She never killed if she could drop, and she dropped three before the first one touched her, and when one did grab her arm she twisted out of it with a little hiss of pain and put her knife through its palm and kept moving.

And Tam — Tam ran straight down the middle of the corridor holding a god's tongue-bone over his head like a lantern, screaming at the top of his lungs, which should have been suicide and instead was genius, because the hollowed turned toward the noise, toward the relic, toward the thing in the boy's hands that their master feared, and every second they spent turning was a second Silas spent putting a stolen memory into a guard's head so violent it dropped him where he stood.

That was his part. That was the only weapon he'd ever really had. He moved among them touching bare skin — wrist, neck, jaw — and *gave*. A memory of drowning. A memory of a funeral in the rain. A memory, dug up from some merchant's mind he'd emptied years ago in Casterhale, of burying a child. The hollowed had nothing to defend with; the emptiness in them had no walls. His memories went in like knives into wet clay, and bodies folded, and he hated every second of it, hated using grief as a club, hated that he was good at it, and did it anyway, again and again, working down the corridor toward the open doors and the light beyond.

A sword opened his sleeve and the arm under it. He barely felt it. Later he would feel it. Everything cost later; that was the whole economy of his life.

"Wren! Left!"

She was already there. The guard coming around the pillar with his blade rising met her boot in the knee and went down sideways, and she stepped over him without looking back, because that was the deal they'd never spoken aloud: you watch my blind side, I watch yours, and neither of us says thank you because thank you would make it a debt and between us there are no debts anymore.

Twenty paces from the doors. Fifteen.

Then the impostor noticed them.

Silas felt it arrive like weather. The pressure in the air changed; the milk-light in the corridor guttered; and every hollowed guard still standing stopped mid-motion, all at once, forty-odd bodies going still as furniture. In the silence Silas heard, faint and far away and inside his own skull, a voice that wore his mother's cadence:

*You brought them to me. How thoughtful.*

"No," Silas said out loud, to the air, to the thing. "I brought them to *you*."

The guards moved again — faster now, no more unison, each one jerking into motion like a puppet whose operator had stopped pretending to be gentle.

* * *

The last twenty paces were the worst fighting Silas had ever been part of, and he had been part of exactly none before this year, so perhaps he wasn't qualified, but his body had opinions anyway and all of them were screams.

Vess held the center. She'd planted her staff and turned herself into a wall, sigils flaring with every impact, and the hollowed broke against her like gray water against a breakwater. He saw her take a cut across the cheek and not blink. He saw her drive the staff's butt through a guard's chest and whisper something — an apology, maybe, or a prayer, or just a name — and keep fighting.

Tam had stopped screaming and started running messages, darting between them with the tongue-bone clutched to his chest, calling positions the way Silas had taught him to call marks in a crowded market. "Two on your right, Wren! Big one behind the pillar, Silas!" The boy's voice cracked in the middle and didn't stop.

And Wren — Wren saved his life, though neither of them would ever say so. He was reaching for a guard's wrist, fingers out, attention on the touch, and the second guard behind it had a clear line on his throat, and he never saw it, because Wren came out of nowhere low and fast and put her shoulder into the man's ribs and drove him into the wall hard enough to crack stone. She looked up at Silas from the floor, hair wild, lip bleeding, and grinned at him like this was the best day of her life.

"You're grinning," he said, stabbing a memory into a reaching hand.

"You're welcome," she said.

Ten paces. Five. The doors yawned ahead, black iron framing a chamber full of wrong light, and beyond them he could see the dais, the crown, the rite — a circle of names written in fire on the air, hundreds of them, thousands, the names of every god small and great, being drawn one by one into the circlet of metal like threads onto a spool.

The sky above the capital had gone dark at sundown. Now he knew why. Every name taken was another star going out.

"GO!" Vess roared, and broke her wall, and spent herself — Silas saw it happen, saw her pour everything the staff had left into one expanding ring of white that flattened every hollowed body in the corridor, saw her drop to one knee afterward, drained, gray-faced, still gripping the staff like a vow.

They went.

* * *

The rite chamber was vast and round and wrong. The ceiling had been removed — not broken, *removed*, peeled back like the lid of a jar, so that the dark sky hung directly overhead, close enough that Silas felt he could touch it, and the stars were going out in slow waves, west to east, like lights being carried away room by room through a great house.

The dais rose at the center. On it stood the crown, floating above a cushion of smoke, drinking names.

And standing beneath it, hands raised, wearing Aldric's face and Aldric's robes and nothing of Aldric at all, was the impostor.

It turned as they entered. It smiled with his mother's smile.

"There you are," it said, and the voice was hers, exactly hers, the voice from a kitchen twenty-five years gone, warm and tired and kind, and Silas's whole body tried to walk toward it. He locked his knees. He bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted blood.

"You're not her."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "I'm you. The part of you that loved her. You threw me away, Silas. Someone fished me out of the gutter and fed me, and I grew, and I learned, and I looked at the world that let a child be carved up for parts —" it gestured, and the circle of names flared, and somewhere a god screamed silently as another thread wound into the crown — "and I decided it needed better management."

Behind him, he heard Wren gasp, heard Tam's small sharp intake of breath, heard Vess dragging herself through the doors. All of them here. All of them spent. Vess empty, the relics nearly silent, the tongue-bone dull in Tam's arms, the eye-stone hot and fading in his own fist.

The impostor spread its hands, wearing his mother's face, wearing a king's crown's shadow.

"Come," it said gently. "We're family."

Silas stepped forward, alone, toward the dais, and the doors boomed shut behind them.
