## 36. Face to Face With Himself

The doors of the rite chamber were taller than a chapel and made of a single piece of black glass, and when Silas put his hand against them they were warm. Not sun-warm. Skin-warm.

"They know we're here," Vess said behind him. Her voice had gone flat the way it did when she was afraid — he'd learned that on the road, in the checkpoint of ash, in every moment she'd stopped performing courage and started rationing it. "They've known since the crypt."

"Then we don't knock." Wren's knuckles were white around the tongue-bone. The relic had been humming since they entered the palace, a low note like a struck bell that never finished ringing, and now it climbed toward something almost like speech.

Tam stood closest to Silas. The boy had been hollowed for three days before Vess got him out, and there were still gaps in him — an afternoon missing, a name he couldn't find, the color of his own front door gone like a page torn from a book. He'd told Silas about it once, at midnight, matter-of-fact as a boy reporting weather: *It doesn't hurt. That's the worst part. It just isn't there anymore.* And then he'd picked up a sword anyway, because in the story Tam believed in, that was what you did.

Silas looked at all three of them — the inquisitor who'd turned, the girl whose childhood he'd stolen, the boy who thought he was a hero — and felt the weight of what he'd spent twenty years avoiding feeling.

"Whatever happens in there," he said, "don't listen to it. Whatever it says. It will sound like sense. It will sound like mercy. That's how it hunts."

"You've heard it," Wren said. Not a question. She'd heard the whispers too, some nights, through the link the curse had carved between Silas and the thing on the throne. She'd heard it use his mother's voice.

"I've heard it my whole life," Silas said. "I just didn't know its name."

He pushed the doors open.

* * *

The chamber beyond was round, and vast, and wrong. The ceiling opened onto midwinter sky, but the sky was not sky — it was a slow churn of darkness, clouds spiraling inward toward a point directly above the dais, as if the night itself were being drunk. Bells hung from nothing, hundreds of them, silent, their clappers bound with black silk. The gods' names, Silas understood, were already half-caught in those bells. He could feel the muffled screaming of them in his teeth.

And on the dais stood the king.

King Aldric — or the shape of Aldric, tall and broad-shouldered, wearing a crown of iron set with stones that drank the light. Up close, through the link, Silas could see what no courtier ever saw: the seams. Faint lines where the borrowed face met the borrowed neck, like a mask fitted by a careful craftsman. Behind the eyes, where a man should have been, there was a depth of quiet so complete it made the Hollow look loud.

Between Silas and the dais, the hollowed guards knelt in ranks, smiling their identical smiles. They did not rise. They did not need to. The impostor raised one hand, and the great glass doors swung shut behind Silas with a sound like a held breath released.

"Thief," the impostor said.

Its voice was the worst thing yet. Because Silas knew that voice. He'd heard it across a hundred miles of curse-link whispering in his mother's cadence, and hearing it aloud, in a body, aimed at him — it landed somewhere below thought.

"You came all this way," the impostor said, descending one step. "Through the valley that forgets. Past the drowned god and the mad priest and the oracle who sees too much. Past my Inquisition — ah, yes, Vess, I see you there, holding your little knife and your new convictions. Past everything I built to stop you." Another step. The crown caught the dark and gave none of it back. "Do you know why?"

"Because you're going to kill every god alive," Silas said, "and I'm going to stop you."

"No." The smile was Aldric's smile; Silas had seen it on coins, on statues, in the memory-vial, on the face of the true king sleeping beneath the palace. "You came because I called you. Every step of this road, thief, has been mine. The knight who fell at your valley's edge — do you imagine that was chance? The vial finding its way into the hands of the one man in the world who could read it? I needed someone to gather the relics. I cannot touch them myself — they burn what I am. But you. You have such clever hands."

The floor seemed to tilt. Silas kept his feet the way he kept his face: through long practice.

"You're lying."

"I am a lie," the impostor said pleasantly. "Lying is the one thing I do perfectly. But test me. Reach into yourself. Tell me — when did you decide to come to the capital? Or did the decision arrive already made, wrapped up in grief and guilt, and you simply carried it?"

And that was the horror of it: Silas couldn't remember deciding. The road unspooled behind him like something written, and he had walked it believing each step was his own.

Wren moved closer to him. He felt her shoulder against his arm, solid, warm, real, and the panic receded enough to think.

"You're stalling," Silas said. "The rite isn't finished. You need time, same as us."

"I need nothing," the impostor said. "But I am offering you time. Listen."

It descended the last steps and crossed the floor, and the kneeling guards parted like water, and Silas made himself stand still while the thing wearing a dead king's body stopped six feet away and studied him with eyes like wells.

"Do you know what you are?" it asked softly. "Not what you do. What you are."

"A thief."

"A hole." The word fell into Silas like a stone into deep water. "You were born in the Hollow, where magic cannot follow, and you have spent your life making yourself more Hollow still. Every memory you stole from another person left a space in you. Every feeling you refused to feel carved out another room. You are the emptiest man in the world, Silas Pemberton. Do you understand what that makes you? It makes you *mine*. I was made from a discarded memory — a child's memory, thrown away by a man who taught boys to steal. I have been hollow from my first hour. And I have spent thirty years filling myself with other people — their faces, their voices, their prayers — and it never holds. It never *holds*, Silas. But you and I together—"

It gestured, and the chamber changed.

* * *

The darkness peeled back and there was a kingdom.

Silas stood on a hill above a city that did not exist — or rather, a city assembled from every city he'd ever seen in stolen memories: Casterhale's river bridges, the capital's bell towers, villages from a dozen pilgrims' home towns, all fitted together seamlessly under a kind gold morning light. People filled the streets below, thousands of them, and he knew their faces. The widow whose grief he'd lifted. Marlow, young again, laughing. His mother — his mother, walking through a market square with her sleeves rolled up, turning to smile at someone out of frame, and Silas's chest seized because he could not remember her face except that the vision had just given it back to him, perfect, impossible—

"It's not real," Wren said beside him, gripping his sleeve. "Silas. It's not real."

"It could be," the impostor said, and its voice was everywhere now, gentle as snowfall. "That is my offer. Walk away. Take your friends, take the relics' worth in gold if you want it, take your lives, and let the rite finish. When the gods are silenced, the world stops *taking*. No more prayers answered with horrors. No more Vesss, broken by what replied to them. No more children hollowed in the night. A quiet world, Silas. A fair world. And in it — this."

The golden city brightened. The people turned and looked up at him, all of them, thousands of faces full of love, and every single face was one he recognized from a memory he had stolen and sold.

"Every soul you ever touched," the impostor murmured. "Every mind you entered. They're all in here. I have been collecting them for you — the widow remembers your kindness, the pilgrims remember your courage, the boy—" it nodded toward Tam, who was staring at the vision with tears running down his face, "—remembers his hero. I can make it all true. A kingdom built from shared memory. Every good thing anyone ever believed about you, made permanent and real. You would be loved, Silas. Completely. Forever. By everyone you have ever known."

His mother turned in the market square and smiled directly at him.

"And all it costs," the impostor said, "is your walking away. While four people you barely know die in a bell-chamber, quickly, painlessly, and are remembered here instead — happy, safe, adored. Isn't that a better ending than the one waiting for them? Isn't it kinder? I am offering you the only honest mercy in this entire rotten century, thief. Take it."

* * *

Silas stood in the false sunlight and let himself want it.

That was the discipline of it — the old thief's discipline, the same one that let him lift a memory without flinching. Don't fight the wanting. Look at it straight. Name it. Because a want you refuse to name will steer you from underneath, but a want you hold up to the light is just a weight, and weights can be set down.

He wanted it. Gods, he wanted it. He wanted his mother's face back, which he'd sold to a river god for a map. He wanted the widow's gratitude and Tam's hero-worship and Wren's forgiveness, all of it guaranteed, all of it permanent, none of it contingent on him being worthy of it. He wanted to stop being a hole.

And that was exactly how he knew the offer was poison. Because the impostor wasn't offering to fill him. It was offering to fill itself — with him, with all of them, with a whole kingdom of remembered love poured into its endless emptiness like water into a cracked jar. It had said it plainly: *it never holds.* Nothing would ever hold. Not for the thing on the dais, and not for the hollowed men smiling in the ranks, and not for Silas either, not if he took love the way he'd taken everything else in his life — taken, taken, always taken.

The Hollow didn't work because it was empty. He'd believed that for forty years, and it was a lie he'd told himself to make the stealing bearable. The Hollow worked because of what people carried into it. The widow's peace hadn't come from the memory he'd lifted. It had come from her neighbors sitting with her, from bread left on her step, from Wren — even then, even as a child — bringing her flowers picked from the shrine ruins. The valley forgot magic. It never forgot kindness. Kindness was the one thing that survived there without any help from the gods.

"Silas." Wren's hand found his. Real. Warm. Contingent on nothing. "Look at me. Not it. Me."

He looked at her.

"No," he said.

The golden city flickered.

"No," Silas said again, louder, and stepped forward off the hill that wasn't there, through the market square that wasn't there, past his mother — and he made himself meet her painted eyes as he passed, and he said, quietly, "I'm sorry. You were real. This isn't you. You taught me better than this," though he couldn't remember if she had, and the not-remembering hurt worse than anything the impostor had shown him.

The vision shattered like a dropped plate.

They stood again in the cold bell-chamber, in the churning dark, and the impostor regarded him with something that might, in a living face, have been disappointment.

"Why?" it asked, and for the first time there was something under the smoothness. Something young. Something lost. "You are empty. I am empty. We are the same thing, you and I — the same discarded child, grown in two different directions. Why do you get to say no?"

And there it was. Thirty years of questions in one raw voice, and Silas understood suddenly, terribly, that the thing had meant every word of the offer — that beneath the strategy there was a genuine, starving hope that its other self would choose it.

"Because I'm not empty," Silas said. "I was. For forty years I did everything I could to be. But empty is a choice you keep making, not a thing you are. Someone finally taught me that." He glanced at Wren, at Tam, at Vess standing grim and steady with her blade drawn. "You were made from a memory of mine. A child's memory. Which means somewhere inside all that smoke and borrowed face, there's a piece of me that knows how this ends. And that piece knows I don't walk away."

The impostor's expression closed like a door.

"Then you die knowing," it said, and raised both hands, and every silent bell in the chamber began to ring.

* * *

The sound was not sound. It went through flesh and bone and memory alike, and Silas felt the relics in their pack answer it — the tongue-bone shrieking against the eye-stone, the eye-stone burning cold against the heart of god they'd dug from the crypt. Above the dais, the spiral of darkness tightened, and through the link the curse had given him Silas felt the impostor's attention split — part of it holding the rite, part of it reaching for him, hungry and furious and afraid.

The guards rose as one, drawing steel, still smiling.

"Wren!" Silas pulled the relics free. "Vess — the dais! Tam—" The boy was already moving, sword up, grinning like the story was finally starting. "Stay behind me!"

"The names are binding into the crown!" Wren shouted over the bell-roar, the tongue-bone blazing in her grip. "We have minutes, maybe less!"

"Then we spend them well!"

The first guard came at him and Silas met steel with the only weapon he'd ever truly mastered — he reached out and *took*, ripping the combat-memory from the man's mind in a flash of silver, and the guard staggered, suddenly a farmer who'd never held a sword, weeping and confused. Around him the chamber erupted into chaos, Vess cutting a path toward the steps, Tam shouting her name, the bells tolling the death of heaven.

And through it all, calm as a hand on his shoulder, the impostor's voice spoke inside his skull, in his mother's voice, one last time:

*Then let us see what you're willing to pay, little thief.*

Above the dais, the last of the sky went black.
