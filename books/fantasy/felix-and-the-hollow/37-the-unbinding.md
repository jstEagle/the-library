## 37. The Unbinding

**Silas**

The rite chamber had no roof.

That was the first thing Silas understood as they came up out of the crypt stair — the impostor had torn the ceiling away, and midwinter sky hung over them like a black lid, stars shivering, snow falling upward in slow spirals where the king's borrowed power pulled at the world's seams. The dais rose at the chamber's heart, a mountain of fused bell-metal, and on it stood the thing wearing Aldric's face, arms raised, crown blazing.

Names poured into it. Silas could *hear* them now, had been hearing them for weeks through the curse's whisper — the old gods' true names, dragged one by one out of the world and wound into the metal like thread onto a spindle. Each name that landed made the sky dimmer. Each name that landed made the snow fall harder upward.

"Silas." Wren's hand found his sleeve. Her knuckles were split from the crypt door; her eyes were steady. "The relics."

He had all three. The tongue-bone cold against his ribs, wrapped in Vess's cloak. The eye-stone in its lead box, humming like a trapped wasp. And the heart — the god's heart — beating against his chest inside his coat as if it had decided his heartbeat was a rhythm worth keeping time with.

Vess moved ahead of them, sword drawn, cutting down two hollowed guards who turned with those identical smiles. She didn't look at their faces. She'd stopped looking at faces three days ago, after Tam.

"The dais," she said. "Wren, the names are anchored at the crown. Silas —"

"I know what I have to do."

He didn't. Not exactly. He knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a room you've only seen in a dream. The eye-stone had shown him: the thing on the dais was grown from a seed, and the seed was a memory, and the memory was his. His mother's kitchen. The smell of bread. Her voice saying his name the way no one else ever had — like it was something she'd been given to keep safe.

The man who taught him theft had cut that memory out of him when he was nine years old and sold it, or lost it, or fed it to something hungry in the dark under Casterhale. And the dark had grown a child from it. Then a boy. Then a king.

Starve the seed, starve the tree.

But the memory wasn't in him anymore. It hadn't been for twenty years. It lived in the thing above him, worn smooth by decades of being someone's whole childhood.

So how did a thief steal back into his own stolen past?

*You walk in the front door,* said the curse-whisper, which these days sounded less like a curse and more like advice from an unwelcome uncle. *You knock.*

The impostor saw him then.

Across the chamber, across the rising spiral of stolen names, the crowned head turned, and the face that was almost his own face — older, smoothed, emptied behind the eyes — broke into a smile of terrible warmth.

"There you are," the king said, and it was his mother's voice, it had always been his mother's voice when it wanted to be loved. "I kept your seat warm."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had watched men die before. She'd watched villages burn and shrines go dark and a knight fall out of the sky trailing smoke. None of it prepared her for watching Silas climb the dais toward the thing that wore his mother's voice, because she finally understood — standing there with the tongue-bone heavy in her hands while Vess held the stair — that he wasn't climbing toward a monster.

He was climbing toward home.

"Don't," she whispered, uselessly, to his back. "Don't listen to it."

But he climbed, and the snow fell upward around him, and the impostor opened its arms like a father, like a mother, like every warm thing a hollowed-out child invents in the dark.

She couldn't follow. That was the bargain they'd struck in the crypt, breathless and bleeding: Silas would feed it, and she and Vess would unbind. Three relics, three anchors, three points around the dais where the stolen names were threaded into the world. Tongue-bone at the north anchor. Eye-stone at the west. Heart at the root, beneath the dais itself, where the god had been buried alive for thirty years.

Vess reached the north anchor first and drove the tongue-bone into the socket like a knife into a loaf. The metal screamed. Every hollowed guard in the chamber dropped at once, smiling faces slackening into nothing, and the scream went up and up until it became words:

*"—liar, thief, unmaker, I gave you quiet, I gave you silence, I answered every prayer you never—"*

"That's not a god," Vess said, teeth gritted, holding the bone down as it bucked. "That's a cage talking. Wren! West!"

Wren ran. The eye-stone burned through the lead box, burned through her gloves, burned through her palms, and she welcomed it — pain meant she was still here, still herself, still the girl who'd walked into a memory-thief's shop demanding her life back and gotten, instead, a family she'd have died for a year ago without knowing what the word meant.

She slammed the stone into the west socket.

The world went white, then clear, and for one impossible second she saw everything: the threads of every name, the shape of the binding, the impostor at the center like a spider grown fat in its own web — and threaded through the web, thin and frayed but *there*, a single golden strand running down into the dais, down into the crypt, down into the buried dark.

Aldric. The true one. Still alive. Still waiting.

"He's in there," she shouted. "Vess — he's *alive*!"

* * *

**Silas**

The impostor's arms stayed open. Snow spiraled between them.

"You came all this way," it said, mother-voiced, kind, "to give me back?"

"No," Silas said. His feet kept moving. Traitor feet. "To take something back."

"It isn't taking if it's given." The smile widened, and beneath the smile he could see the machinery of it, the hunger wearing love like a mask wears a face. "That's the difference between us, little thief. You took. Everything you ever touched, you took. Your friend's childhood. The widow's grief. Your own mother — you let them cut her out of you rather than feel one more winter in that house. I was *given*. Freely. A whole boy's love, discarded, and I grew, and I became gentle, and I became patient, and now I am becoming *eternal*, and all I have ever wanted—"

"—is to stop being hungry," Silas said. "I know. I've heard you say it every night for six weeks."

He reached the top of the dais. Up close, the crown was worse than fire — it was a ring of mouths, small and silver, each one chewing a god's name into smaller pieces. The impostor looked down at him with his mother's gray eyes, and Silas felt nine years old, felt the kitchen, felt the bread smell that wasn't his anymore, belonged to the thing in front of him, had always belonged to it.

"You can't take it," the impostor said softly. "It's mine. You gave it away. Whatever you do to me, you'll never taste bread again. You'll never hear her. Is that worth it? A world of gods who answer prayers with plagues? A king who weeps? You could walk away, Silas. I'd give you a kingdom. I'd give you *her*. I've had twenty years to perfect her — every word, every laugh. Stay, and tomorrow morning you wake up in the kitchen, and she calls you in from the snow, and this time you don't leave. This time you stay nine years old forever, and safe, and—"

"Loved," Silas said.

The word landed between them like a dropped coin.

"Yeah," he said. "That's the offer. That's always been the offer. Safety and love, priced at everyone else." He thought of Wren's split knuckles. Of Tam, hollowed and carried out of the palace, breathing shallow but breathing. Of Vess, who'd prayed once and been answered so horribly she'd spent her life making sure no one else got an answer. Of Marlow, who'd sold them, because everyone has a price, including Silas, whose price had just walked up a staircase wearing his mother's face.

"My mum used to say," he heard himself continue, steady, wry, detached, the old shopkeeper's voice, "that a thing you buy is never as good as a thing you're given. She was wrong about most things. She was right about that."

He opened his coat. Took out the vial — empty since the border, refilled since the shrine, filled now with the only thing he had left that the impostor wanted.

Himself. What remained. Twenty years of carefully hoarded nothing, and underneath the nothing, one last ember: not the memory of his mother, but the memory of losing her. The moment the teacher's needle went in. The moment the kitchen went dark. The grief of the theft itself — the one memory he'd refused to sell, refused to spend, refused even to look at, because it was the only proof he'd ever been loved by anyone.

The original seed wasn't the happy memory. He understood that now, standing in the snow that fell upward. The seed was the wound. The thing below Casterhale had grown from the *loss*.

And grief, unlike love, could be spent.

"You want to be fed?" Silas said. "Open wide."

He pressed the vial to the impostor's lips and poured.

* * *

**Wren**

She felt it before she saw it — a lurch in the web, the golden strand thrumming, and then the impostor *shuddered*, crown-mouths choking, and the voice that came out of it was no longer the mother's voice but a child's, small and enormous, echoing off the bell-metal walls:

"*—cold, why is it cold, where did she go, where did she GO—*"

"Now!" Vess roared. "The heart! The root!"

Wren was already moving, sliding down the dais's flank, driving her hands into the frozen seam at its base where the third socket waited. The god's heart came out of her coat hot as a loaf fresh from an oven — and the absurdity of that, the unbearable domesticity of it, nearly broke her laughing as she jammed it home.

Three relics. Three sockets. The tongue-bone spoke, the eye-stone saw, the heart beat once, twice —

— and the binding tore.

It didn't shatter. It *unraveled*, thread by stolen thread, names flying free into the midwinter dark like birds released from a burning coop, and each name that escaped lit a patch of sky, and the snow stopped falling upward and began, gently, ordinarily, to fall down.

The impostor collapsed inward. Not dying — *shrinking*, decades of stolen growth folding back into the seed it had come from, the crown rolling from its brow, the face cycling through borrowed expressions — the teacher's, the widow's, Wren's own, briefly, horribly — and then settling into the only face it had left.

A boy of about nine, curled on the melting dais, crying without sound.

Silas knelt beside it. Wren couldn't hear what he said. She saw him reach out — hesitate — and then touch the boy's hair, once, the way you'd close a book you'd finished reading whether you liked the ending or not.

The boy came apart like frost in sunlight. Gently. Almost gratefully.

And beneath the dais, beneath thirty years of bell-metal and burial, something golden stirred, and cracked, and woke.

* * *

**Silas**

King Aldric came up out of the ground weeping.

Not the dignified weeping of restored monarchs in ballads. He clawed at the broken metal, sobbing, gray-haired, gaunt as a winter branch, and when Silas hauled him free the king grabbed his collar with both hands and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"How long? How long did I — was it — my wife, is my wife—"

"A long time," Silas said. "I'm sorry."

Aldric looked at him then, really looked, and whatever he saw in Silas's face made him stop asking questions. Because Silas knew what the king was seeing: a man with holes where a life should be. He'd paid the price. All of it. The vial had taken the last ember, and now when he reached for his mother he found — not darkness, darkness would have been merciful — but a *shape* where she'd been, an absence with her outline, the way a glove keeps the shape of a hand.

He knew he'd had a mother. He knew she'd loved him. He knew it the way you know a room you've only seen in a dream.

It was enough. He decided, kneeling in the snow on the ruined dais, that it was going to be enough.

"Silas." Wren's boots crunched up beside him. She was bleeding from the palms, soot-faced, snowflakes catching in her hair, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read — fear, maybe, or recognition. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There's nothing behind them. Like the guards. Like the village."

Ah, Silas thought. So that's what paying in full looks like from the outside.

Above them, freed names wheeled through the recovering sky, and somewhere far off, the bells of the capital — tuned for thirty years to silence — began, one by one, to crack.

Vess limped over, sword point-down in the snow, and looked at Silas for a long moment. Then at the king. Then at the sky.

"Well," she said. "Someone should probably tell the city their god-king just died."

Tam's voice, weak and wondering, floated up from the stairwell: "Is it over? Can somebody come help me carry Marlow? He says he's sorry. He's been saying it for an hour."

Silas started to laugh, and found he wasn't sure how, and tried anyway.

* * *

**Wren**

Later — much later, dawn-gray, in the wreck of the throne room where refugees and freed servants huddled around fires — Wren sat beside Silas and watched him try to remember how to be a person.

He remembered facts. He recited them like inventory: her name, Tam's name, the road to the Hollow, the price of bread in Casterhale. But when Tam hugged him, Silas patted the boy's shoulder with the careful awkwardness of a man handling something fragile he couldn't identify. When Vess thanked him — actually thanked him, gruffly, staring at the wall — he nodded like a merchant acknowledging payment.

When Wren took his hand, he looked at their joined fingers with polite curiosity.

"It's fine," he said, before she could speak. "This is what it costs. I knew the price going in."

"You didn't," she said. "Nobody knows the price going in. That's what makes it a price."

He almost smiled. Almost. The muscles remembered the shape.

She'd spent the walk from the palace thinking about the shop in the Hollow, and the ledger he kept, and the widow whose grief he'd lifted and sold back as peace, and all the careful, surgical thefts of a man determined never to owe anyone anything. And she thought: *he spent his whole life making sure he had nothing left to lose, and tonight he proved he'd lose all of it anyway, gladly, for people he claims he can't remember loving.*

Which meant the love hadn't lived in the memories at all.

Which meant it was still in there somewhere, under the holes, the way the golden strand had run through the impostor's web — frayed, buried, alive.

"We're going home," she told him. "All of us. To the valley. And you're going to sit in that shop of yours, and we're going to come in every day, one at a time, and tell you stories about yourself until some of them stick."

"That's not how memory works," Silas said.

"No," Wren agreed, gripping his hand tighter. "It works better than that."

Outside, the last of the thirty-year bells cracked and fell silent, and in the ringing absence, very faint, from somewhere beyond the mountains — from the direction of the Hollow — a shrine neither of them could see flickered once, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

Silas turned his head toward it. He didn't know why. He'd never remember why.

But he turned.
