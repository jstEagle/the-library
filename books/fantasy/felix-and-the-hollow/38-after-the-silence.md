## 38. After the Silence

The silence came all at once, like a held breath released.

Wren felt it in her teeth before she understood it in her mind — the pressure that had filled the rite chamber, that had filled the whole city, that had filled the whole world, simply *gone*. The gods' names, which had been screaming through the crown for what felt like a lifetime and had actually been eleven minutes, stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Cut off the way a hand cuts the string of a bell.

She was on her knees on the blackened floor of the rite chamber, and Silas was beside her, and Silas was not speaking, and Silas was not moving, and for one terrible stretch of heartbeats she thought they had won the world and lost him in the same instant.

"Silas." Her voice came out as ash. She crawled to him. His eyes were open. That was something. His eyes were open and they were looking at the ceiling and there was nothing in them, nothing at all, and she had seen that look before — in the hollowed village, in the smiling towns, in every person the impostor had emptied. She had seen that look on the dead.

"Silas." She took his face in her hands. His skin was cold. "Silas, look at me. Look at me, you thieving bastard, look at—"

His eyes moved. Found hers. Focused, slowly, the way a man focuses who has just woken in a strange room.

"Wren," he said. And then, after a moment, with a small, terrible politeness: "I'm sorry. Have we met?"

* * *

The healers said he wasn't hollowed. That was the word they used, *hollowed*, and they said it carefully, the way you'd say *plague* in a sickroom. They examined him for two hours by lamplight in the chamber's anteroom while Vess stood guard at the door and Tam slept against the wall with his head on his folded arms, exhausted past waking. The healers turned Silas's eyelids back, pressed silver needles to his scalp, made him count backward and name the months and recite what he'd eaten that morning.

"He gave it away," one of them finally said. The eldest, a woman with burn-scarred hands. "Whatever he fed that thing — it took everything attached. Memories don't come out clean. They come out with roots."

Wren stood in the corner and did not cry, because she had learned, over the past months, that crying was something you did when there was time, and there was no time. There was a city to account for. There was a king — the true king — weeping on the floor of his own rite chamber. There were bells ringing wrong across the capital, and fires in the low quarter, and Inquisition officers standing in the streets with their hands empty and their faces blank, waiting for someone to tell them what they were now, if not the fist of a thing that wore a crown.

"He'll remember," the old healer said gently, to Wren, as if Silas weren't lying right there hearing every word. "Not what he lost. But that he lost. That's the worst of it, usually. The shape of the hole."

Wren knew the shape of the hole. She'd lived inside it since she was seven years old.

* * *

They moved Aldric to a room in the palace that still had working windows. He walked there himself, though Vess offered her arm twice and he declined twice with a courtesy so worn and threadbare it was almost worse than collapse. He was an old man in a young man's body, or a young man in an old man's body — Wren couldn't decide which. The gods had put him back together from whatever the smoke-thing hadn't used, and the seams showed. His hands shook. His eyes went to corners of rooms where nothing was. When a servant brought water he flinched from the pitcher as if it might speak in a voice he knew.

Tam woke in the evening and refused to leave the antechamber, so they let him sit with Silas, and Wren heard him talking — low, endless, a boy's voice filling a silence the way water fills a crack. He told Silas about the caravan. About the checkpoint at Ash. About the shrine of teeth, and the guardian, and how Silas had cursed himself to save him. He told the stories *to* Silas, over and over, and Wren understood what he was doing and it broke her a little: he was trying to give the man back his own history, secondhand, like returning stolen goods one piece at a time.

"Did I really do all that?" she heard Silas ask once, near midnight.

"You did all that," Tam said fiercely. "And more. You're a hero. I always said."

"I'm a thief," Silas said, and there was something in his voice — not grief exactly, because grief requires knowing what you grieve. More like a man pressing on a bruise to confirm it's there. "Everyone keeps saying hero. I keep waiting to remember why that would be a lie, and I can't. I can't even remember why it would be true."

Tam looked at Wren across the room, stricken, and she shook her head slightly: *keep going.* And the boy kept going. God bless him, he kept going.

* * *

Aldric received them on the third morning, in a small room, with no crown.

That was the first thing Wren noticed — no crown, and no throne, and no guards except Vess, who stood by the door out of habit and looked, in that room, like a woman guarding an empty birdcage. The king sat in a plain chair by the window with his ruined hands folded in his lap, and morning light came through the glass and lay across him like something he was being asked to carry.

"You're the ones," he said. His voice was quiet and rough, like a hinge unused for years. "The thief. The girl. The child." He looked at Tam. "And the inquisitor who remembered what she served."

"Your Majesty," Vess began, and he raised one shaking hand, and she stopped.

"I spent years in the dark," Aldric said. "Years. I don't know how many. There was no counting in there — it wasn't a place, exactly. It was the inside of a moment that wouldn't end." His eyes moved to the window, to the city below, where smoke still rose from the low quarter in thin gray threads. "And while I was in there, everything was done in my name. Wars. Taxes. Burnings. That thing wore my face and my seal and my voice, and every cruelty it committed, history will write down as mine."

"History will write the truth," Silas said. "We have witnesses. We have the vial. We have—"

"You have a dead knight's memory," Aldric said, "and a thief's word, and an inquisitor's confession, and a boy who was half-hollowed and healed. Do you truly believe that will stand against twenty years of a king's decrees, signed in a king's hand?" He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had seen in all these months, and she had seen a great many sad things. "The truth is a poor currency, Master Pemberton. I know. You of all people must know."

Silas said nothing. He didn't remember enough to argue, and maybe — Wren thought — maybe that was why the king could say what he said next.

"I am going to abdicate," Aldric said. "This morning, before the assembled court, before the bells stop ringing wrong. I will tell them what I can tell them — that the crown was usurped, that the usurper is destroyed, that I am not fit to rule what was done in my name. And I will not name a successor, because there isn't one. There is no one clean. Let them argue. Let the lords and the guilds and the temples fight it out in the light instead of in the dark, for once." He looked at each of them in turn. "You gave me back my name. I won't dishonor it by using it to hold a chair."

"Your Majesty—" Wren started.

"Thank you," he said, simply, and it stopped her cold, because she had expected ceremony and gotten a man, and a man's plain thanks weighs more than any ceremony. "All of you. I know what it cost. I watched, from the inside of that moment. I saw what the thief fed the thing." His gaze settled on Silas, and there was something in it that Wren couldn't name — recognition, maybe, or grief, or the particular sorrow of one hollowed man looking at another. "I know what you paid."

"I don't," Silas said.

"No," Aldric said. "That's the mercy of it. And the crime."

* * *

The abdication happened at noon, and Wren watched it from the gallery of the great hall, wedged between Tam and a fishmonger's wife who had gotten in on the rumor mill and wasn't going to be moved for any king in creation.

Aldric walked in without a crown and the hall went silent — a real silence this time, the human kind, the kind made of held breath. He stood before the assembled court in plain clothes, and he told them. Not everything. He told them a usurper had worn his shape for twenty years; he told them it was destroyed by four people whose names he spoke clearly, one by one, so that no lord could later pretend not to have heard them; and he told them he would not rule over the ruins of what had been done in his name, and that the crown would pass to whoever the realm could trust, and that he prayed — he said the word *prayed*, and the hall flinched, because the word had teeth again now — he prayed they would choose better than the gods had.

Then he set the crown on the floor of the dais, stepped over it, and walked out through the parting crowd, and no one stopped him, and no one cheered, because there was nothing to cheer. Victory, Wren thought, watching him go. This is what it looks like. It looks like an old man walking away from a chair.

* * *

The gods left that night.

Not dramatically. That was the thing no one had expected — everyone braced for thunder, for the sky to tear, for some final sign. But the old gods had never been dramatic; they'd been *hungry*, and hunger doesn't make exits, it just ends. The shrine lights went out one by one through the evening, quietly, like lamps being snuffed by a servant moving through a house. The river god's presence slid out of Casterhale's waters so gradually the ferrymen only noticed at dawn, when the current felt, in one old boatman's words, "like it was just water again." The minor gods of the road villages went out like candles. By midnight the world was as godless as it had been before the prayers started being answered — except now everyone knew the gods were real, and had been real all along, and had chosen, in the end, to leave.

Wren stood on a palace balcony with Vess and watched the last shrine-light die across the city — a little flicker on a hill in the temple district, there, then not.

"Do you think they were satisfied?" she asked. "Or sated?"

"Does it matter?" Vess said. Her voice was flat with exhaustion. She hadn't slept since the rite; Wren doubted she would sleep properly again for a year. "They took what they were owed and went. That's the oldest story there is." She was quiet a moment. "I spent my whole life hating them for answering me the way they did. Now they're gone and I find I don't know what to do with the hate. It's like a hand that's been clenched so long it's forgotten how to open."

"Silas is the same," Wren said. "Was the same. He held everything at arm's length so nothing could be taken from him. And then it was taken anyway." She looked at her hands. "The gods didn't teach us anything, in the end. They just charged us for lessons we could have learned free."

Vess made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You should write that down, girl. Someone will want it, in a year or two, when they're building the new temples."

"They won't build temples."

"No," Vess agreed. "They'll build courts. Courts are temples where you can charge admission."

* * *

They buried the knight on the fifth day.

They never learned his name — the vial had been his whole testament, and the vial was spent, and the man who might have told them was ash in a border field. So Wren chose the stone herself, standing in the palace crypt where the heart of the god had lain until they pried it loose, and she had the mason cut four words into the granite:

*HE KNEW. HE CAME ANYWAY.*

Tam cried at the burial. Vess stood at attention the way soldiers do. Silas stood beside the grave with his hands at his sides and his face empty, and afterward, walking back, he said to Wren, quietly:

"Did I know him?"

"No," she said. "He crashed into our lives and died in an hour. He was a stranger."

Silas nodded slowly. "I keep feeling like I should be grieving more than I can," he said. "There's a list of things I owe, and I can't read it. I just know it's long."

Wren stopped walking. The corridor was empty, stone and lamplight, and she made herself say the thing she had been carrying for three days like a stone in her chest.

"Your mother," she said. "The memory you fed it. It was her. Wasn't it. The last of her."

Silas looked at her, and for a moment she saw him try to reach for it — she saw the actual motion behind his eyes, the hand closing on emptiness — and then he shook his head, slow and lost.

"I had a mother," he said. "I know that the way I know I have hands. But there's nothing there. There's just... a door, and behind it, nothing." He smiled, and it was the polite, terrible smile from the first morning. "Was she kind? People keep looking at me like I lost something wonderful."

Wren's throat closed. She thought of the confession by firelight, weeks ago, a lifetime ago — Silas telling her about a woman who sang off-key and lied about the flour and loved her son with a fierceness that embarrassed him even in memory. A memory he'd carried like a coal in his chest his whole life, and never spent, and finally spent for all of them.

"She was kind," Wren said. "She sang badly. She loved you more than the world had room for." She took his hand. "And when you're ready, I'll tell you everything I know about her. And when I run out, Tam will tell you what he knows, and Vess, and anyone else who ever heard you talk in your sleep. We'll build her back for you. Piece by piece. It won't be the same. But it will be *yours*."

Silas looked at their joined hands for a long moment.

"Why?" he asked. Not bitterly. Genuinely, like a man asking how a trick is done. "Why would all of you do that? What am I to you?"

And Wren, who had spent her whole life until this winter as a girl with a hole where her childhood should be, who knew exactly what it was to be handed back yourself in fragments by people who loved you — Wren laughed, tired and cracked and real.

"You're the man who paid for the whole world," she said, "with a song sung off-key."

* * *

They left the capital on the eighth morning, in a cart, like pilgrims, because some habits are load-bearing.

The city was already arguing about succession behind them; three lords had declared, two guilds had objected, and the temples — what remained of them — were holding services of thanksgiving that were mostly just people sitting in rooms being glad the gods were gone. Vess had formally dissolved the Inquisition's authority the day before, in a signed order she wrote with the same hand that had signed a hundred seizures, and then she had stood in the empty headquarters and, in her own words, "let the building go."

She came with them. Nobody asked her to. She just appeared at the city gate with a single bag and a face that dared anyone to comment, and Tam made room for her on the cart bench without a word, and that was that.

The road north was quiet. The road shrines stood dark. At the first village they passed, the people were out in the fields, planting, and an old woman at the well looked at their cart, and looked at Silas — whose face was on every broadsheet in the realm now, *THE THIEF WHO UNMADE THE KING*, which was wrong in every particular and right in the way that mattered — and the old woman just nodded to them, the way you nod to neighbors, and went back to her water.

Wren rode the whole first day with Silas's hand in hers, because he asked her, at the city gate, in a voice stripped of all its old irony: "Don't let me drift off. I'm lighter than I was. I might just... blow away." And she had said, "I've got you," and she meant it the way you mean a vow.

Near dusk, as the Hollow's hills came up gray and familiar on the horizon, Silas went still beside her.

"Wren," he said. "There's something I should tell you. About the valley. About what's waiting for us there."

She looked at him. His face in the last light was calm, and empty, and underneath the emptiness — she was learning to see it — something new was being built, slowly, the way Tam built his stories: piece by piece, secondhand, offered freely.

"What's waiting?" she asked.

Silas looked at the hills, and then at her, and said, "I don't remember how to be a thief anymore. I can't recall a single trick, a single mark, a single lie. Whatever I take back with me to that valley — I'll be taking a man who has to start from nothing." He paused. "I think I'd like to start from nothing. With all of you. If nothing will have us."

The cart rolled on toward the darkening valley, and the first star came out over the hills, and Wren held his hand tighter, and said, "Nothing will have us. Nothing will be lucky to have us."

Behind them the capital's bells rang out the hour — still tuned wrong, still ringing the old suppression-chords, though there was nothing left to suppress. Someone would fix them eventually. Someone would tune them to ring for weddings and harvests and the births of ordinary children. But tonight they rang hollow over an empty throne, and ahead of them the Hollow waited, dark and godless and safe, and Wren watched the hills come closer and thought: *this isn't an ending. This is the part where we find out what we saved it all for.*
