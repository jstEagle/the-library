## 39. Memories Worth Keeping

The valley took us back the way it took everything — without comment.

One step I was on the pilgrim road, wind in my face, magic humming against my skin like a wasp trapped under cloth. The next step I crossed the border stone and the hum stopped. Silence. The particular silence of the Hollow, which had always felt to me like a held breath, like the world waiting for me to do something worth remembering.

I stopped on the path and waited for the whispering to start.

It didn't.

That was the thing about being hollowed. I'd spent thirty years listening to other people's memories murmur inside my skull — stolen griefs, stolen first kisses, a stolen childhood that wasn't mine. When I'd fed my mother's memory to the impostor, I'd expected the whispers to stop. They had. All of them. What I hadn't expected was how much else went with them.

I knew my name. I knew my trade. I knew Wren's face and Tam's and Vess's, because knowing faces seemed to live somewhere deeper than memory, somewhere even the gods' hunger couldn't reach. But between those anchors there were gaps. Whole seasons missing. I could remember that I'd taught Tam to whittle, because he still carried the lopsided wooden fox in his pocket and told anyone who'd listen who made it. I couldn't remember doing it. I could remember that Wren had forgiven me by firelight, because she'd said it again on the road home, plainly, so I could hold it fresh. The original night was gone. Burned as fuel for someone else's war.

"You're doing the face," Wren said beside me.

"What face?"

"The one where you're taking inventory." She shifted the pack on her shoulder. She'd filled out over the road; the orphan who'd once stood in my doorway demanding answers had become someone who walked like the ground owed her nothing. "Checking what's missing."

"I'm not—"

"Silas."

"All right. Yes. I'm checking." I looked up the path toward the town, toward the crooked roofs and the shrine that had gone dark again after midwinter, its god withdrawn or satisfied or simply finished with us. "It's a long list."

"Then we'll shorten it." She said it like she was talking about the weather. Like rebuilding a man from the inside out was a chore list. Maybe to her it was. She'd had her childhood taken once too. She knew the shape of the theft.

Behind us, Tam came pelting down the trail with an armful of wild onions he'd absolutely been told not to dig up, Vess walking behind him at the pace of a woman who had decided, somewhere around the second week of travel, that guarding a child was easier than guarding a kingdom.

"The Hollow," Vess said, when she reached us. She said the word carefully, tasting it. A month ago she'd have sealed these passes with holy fire. Now she just looked at the valley the way you look at a house where something terrible happened but people still live. "It's smaller than I imagined."

"Everything's smaller than the Inquisition imagines," I said. "That's why you never find anything."

Her mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. Almost. Vess's smiles were still rationed, one per fortnight, issued grudgingly. But they existed now. That was new since the rite chamber, since she'd watched the thing wearing Aldric's crown try to hollow a twelve-year-old boy and understood, finally, what she'd been serving all along.

We walked down into the valley together, four survivors and one dead man's fox carved from pine, and the Hollow let us in.

* * *

The shop was exactly as I'd left it, which meant it was a disaster.

Dust on the memory-vials. Dust on the brass racks. Dust on the little velvet cushions where clients sat while I slid their own past back into their heads at three times the price of peace. There was a half-eaten apple on the desk that had achieved a kind of geological permanence. My ledger lay open to a page of entries in my own handwriting — *Widow Marsh, grief, partial extraction, paid in eggs* — and I stared at it for a long moment because I remembered the transaction and not the woman. Her face was a blank where a face should be. Another entry on the list.

"We'll need beds," Wren announced, already moving through the room opening shutters. Light fell in. Dust swam. "Four beds. Five if we count the storeroom, which we're counting, because Vess snores."

"I do not snore," Vess said, from the doorway, in the tone of a woman who had been informed of this before.

"You snore like a landslide with opinions."

"I have killed men for less."

"And yet here we are." Wren grinned at her, and Vess — Inquisitor Vess, God-touched Vess, the woman whose prayers had once been answered so horribly she'd dedicated her life to silencing every god alive — turned away to inspect the vials and hid whatever her mouth was doing.

Tam appeared at my elbow. He did that. Quiet as a cat when he wanted to be, which was most of the time now, though the silence had changed quality since the palace. Before, his quiet had been hunger — a boy pressing close to warmth. Now it was watchfulness. He'd read the impostor's attention pass through him. He knew what it felt like to be opened like a book. Some evenings he woke screaming, and some mornings he woke fine, and we had learned not to predict which.

He set the wild onions on my desk with tremendous dignity, as if this settled our food situation for the winter.

"For the soup," he said.

"There's no soup."

"There will be. Wren said." He looked up at me, and I braced myself, because Tam looking at me usually preceded either a question I couldn't answer or a statement I couldn't bear. "Silas. Do you remember the fox?"

I crouched down to his level. His eyes were brown and steady and far too old.

"No," I said. Because lying to him would have been the one theft left available to me, and I was done stealing. "I know I did it. I don't remember it."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, the way he absorbed everything — completely, and then filed it somewhere I couldn't follow. Then he dug the fox out of his pocket and put it in my hand.

"You keep him," Tam said. "Until you remember. That's how it works. If you hold onto a thing long enough, the holding becomes the memory."

"That's not how it works at all."

"It is for me." And he went off to help Wren argue with a shutter that had rusted shut, leaving me standing in my own dusty shop holding a badly carved fox and understanding, with a lurch, that the boy had just handed me the thesis of my entire remaining life.

* * *

They started that first evening, and none of them asked permission, which was the whole point.

I was sweeping — the shop needed it, and sweeping required no memories, only arms — when Wren planted herself in front of me and said, "Sit down."

"I'm working."

"You're hiding behind a broom. Sit down." I sat. She dragged the client's chair around backward and straddled it, facing me, and for a moment she just looked at me with that expression she'd worn since she was nine years old and standing in my doorway: the look of someone who knows something was taken and has come to collect.

Then she reached up and pressed two fingers to her own temple, the way I'd done ten thousand times for clients, and drew out a thread of light.

I flinched. Every instinct in my body read that gesture as a weapon being drawn. But she wasn't pointing it at me. She cupped it in her palm — a small silver shimmer, warm as a coal — and held it out.

"This is yours," she said. "The night you gave me soup. After you stole my memories, before I knew you'd stolen my memories. You thought I was asleep. You sat by the stove and you cried, quietly, into your hands, and then you got up and made soup because you decided that if you couldn't give me back what you took, you could at least feed me." Her voice stayed level. Practiced. She'd clearly rehearsed this. "I kept it. All these years. I told myself it was leverage, evidence, proof of what you were. It wasn't. It was just — him. The version of you that hated himself enough to care. Take it back."

"Wren—"

"It's a gift, Silas. Not a debt. Don't make it a debt."

My hands were shaking. Thirty years of taking, and I had never once been on this side of the gesture. I took the light from her palm.

Memory doesn't return gently. It arrives the way weather arrives — all at once, everywhere, undeniable. One moment I was sitting in a dusty shop across from a young woman with fierce eyes. The next I was *there*: the stove's heat on my shins, the salt taste of tears, the specific weight of guilt so large it had bent me double, and underneath it all the small stubborn animal thought — *soup. Make the child soup.* — because it was the only good act available and I was desperate for one.

I came back to the room wet-faced. Wren pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"I remember," I said stupidly.

"Good." She stood, flipped the chair back around, and pointed at the broom. "Now finish sweeping. We're not animals."

* * *

Vess gave hers the second night, and hers was different — clinical, almost embarrassed, delivered like a report.

She found me on the shop's back step, smoking a pipe I'd found in a drawer and couldn't remember buying. She sat down without asking. Somewhere on the road the Inquisitor had died and a tired woman with a sword had been born in her place, and the tired woman sat closer than the Inquisitor ever would have.

"I've been thinking about what to give you," she said. "Most of what I have is classified."

"Naturally."

"But there's one thing." She looked out at the dark valley. "The checkpoint at Ash. You stole back forty pilgrims' memories while I stood twenty feet away, and I saw your face, and I let you go. Do you remember that?"

"Vaguely. I remember nearly dying."

"What you don't remember — what nobody remembers, because I buried it the same hour — is why I let you go." Her jaw worked. Giving things up clearly cost her more than it cost the others. "Because for six seconds I watched you work, and I thought: *this man is committing treason against my order, and he is better at mercy than any servant of the gods I have ever met.* Six seconds. It was the first crack. Everything after — the boy, the crypt, the rite — grew out of those six seconds. So." She pressed her fingers to her temple, awkward, unpracticed, like a scribe using the wrong hand. "Here. It's yours. I don't want it anymore. It belongs to whoever you became because of it."

The memory flooded in: the checkpoint, ash drifting like gray snow, my hands moving through a crowd's sleeping minds, and above me — I'd never known this — Vess's face, watching, hard, and then not hard. The exact moment a fanatic's certainty developed a hairline fracture. I lived those six seconds from the outside for the first time. From her side.

When it faded, she was already standing, brushing off her coat.

"Don't thank me," she said. "Thanking implies sentiment."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Good." At the door she paused. "The storeroom bed had better be adequate. I'm told I don't snore."

* * *

Tam's turn came last, and I tried to stop it, and failed utterly.

He cornered me in the kitchen — we had a kitchen now, somehow, in the space of two weeks the shop had grown pots and a table and a smell of onion soup that had become permanent — and he climbed onto the bench across from me with the fox in his fist and the gravity of a magistrate.

"You keep avoiding me," he said.

"I don't avoid you."

"You avoid *this*." He set the fox down between us like a chess piece. "You take everyone's gifts except mine. Wren says it's because you think children shouldn't pay for adults' wars. But this isn't paying. This is giving. There's a difference. Wren explained it."

"Wren explains a lot of things."

"She does." He nodded seriously. "So. Mine." And before I could raise a hand, before I could say *Tam, you don't owe me, you paid enough in that palace*, he pressed his fingers to his temple — clumsy, copying Wren's motion, tongue between his teeth — and pulled out a thread of gold.

It hit me harder than the others, because it was his. A child's memory, offered freely by a child who had every right to hoard every happy moment he had left. It poured into me: a campfire on the pilgrim road, stars thick overhead, Tam cross-legged beside me learning to whittle, my hands — my hands, patient, guiding his — shaping a block of pine into something with ears. My voice saying, *No, thinner there. Foxes are thin. Life's hard on foxes.* His laughter when the ears came out crooked. The feeling — and this was the part that undid me, this was the part I hadn't known was in it — the feeling from his side of looking up at me and thinking, with total unguarded faith: *he's the best person I know.*

I put my head down on the kitchen table and shook, quietly, the way he'd once seen me cry by a stove.

Tam patted my arm with a small hand. "There," he said. "Now you remember."

"I remember," I managed.

"And?" He leaned in, urgent. "Was I good? At the whittling?"

"You were terrible," I said, laughing and crying at once. "The fox looks like a dog that lost an argument."

"I *know*," he said, delighted. "Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

Winter deepened. The valley slept under snow, its shrines dark, its gods gone wherever gods go when they're finished with us. And in the shop that had once been famous for taking, a strange commerce began.

Word spread — the way word spreads in small places, sideways and unstoppable — that Pemberton the memory-thief had come back hollowed, and that the people living above his shop were feeding him his own past one gift at a time. Widow Marsh came first. I didn't know her face, but she knew mine; I'd lifted her grief years ago and sold it back to her as peace. She stood in my doorway, looked at the blankness where her transaction should have been in my eyes, and said, "Oh, you poor man. What did they take?" And then she gave me the memory of selling her grief to me — both sides of it, her sorrow and my wry professional detachment, the whole strange transaction preserved like a fly in amber. "You were kind, in your way," she said. "Cold, but kind. I want you to have it. It was ours."

Others followed. The baker whose daughter's fever I'd once eased by lifting the worst night of it. A farmer whose wedding day I'd brightened for coin. Each one came, hesitated in the doorway of the thief's shop, and then stepped inside and *gave*. Not sold. Gave. I tried to pay them. They refused. Tam solved the problem eventually by declaring the shop a gift-house and putting out a sign, and after that people brought bread and eggs and firewood alongside their memories, and the economy of the Hollow tilted, very slightly, on its ancient axis.

Some nights I sat among the vials — my old stock, the stolen goods of thirty years — and wondered what to do with them. Selling them felt obscene now. Keeping them felt worse. Wren caught me staring at them one night and said, "Give them back, obviously. To whoever wants them. Free." As if it were simple. As if it weren't the complete inversion of everything I'd built my survival on.

But then, everything I'd built my survival on had already burned. What remained was this: a kitchen that smelled of soup. An ex-Inquisitor who snored like a landslide. A boy's crooked fox on my desk, keeping watch. A young woman who had chosen, twice, to forgive me — once with words, once with light.

And gaps, still. Whole seasons of silence. But the silence was changing quality. It was starting to feel less like a wound and more like a shelf. Empty, yes. Waiting, yes.

Built to hold things, when people loved you enough to fill it.

On the last night of winter, I sat at the kitchen table while the others argued cheerfully about whether the storeroom should become a proper bedroom or a second gift-room, and I took out my ledger, and beneath the final entry of the old life — *Widow Marsh, grief, partial extraction, paid in eggs* — I wrote, in careful letters:

*Received, freely given: one soup, one checkpoint, one fox, one grief shared, and counting.*

Then I heard footsteps on the road outside — many footsteps, deliberate ones, stopping at my door — and a knock, three slow raps, from someone who clearly wanted me to know they had all the time in the world.

Wren looked at me. Vess's hand drifted toward her sword. Tam hugged the fox.

I went to see who had come calling on the Hollow's newest shop — and what they wanted to trade.
