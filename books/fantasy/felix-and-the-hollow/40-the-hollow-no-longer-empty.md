## 40. The Hollow, No Longer Empty

The shop had a bell over the door that Wren had stolen from a shrine in Casterhale, which was the kind of joke the valley appreciated. It didn't ring for gods anymore. It rang for customers.

Silas kept the counter between himself and the morning light the way he'd once kept it between himself and everything else — out of habit more than need. Three seasons in the Hollow had worn his habits smooth, like river stones. He still woke before dawn. He still counted the exits. But now the exits led to a kitchen where Tam slept with his boots on, and to a back room where Vess sharpened knives she no longer needed to use, and to a street where people nodded at him without flinching.

He opened the shutters and let the light in on purpose. That was new.

The sign above the door read PEMBERTON & COMPANY, RETURNERS OF LOST THINGS, and beneath it, smaller, in Wren's handwriting because she'd insisted: *no thefts performed on premises.* The ampersand was doing a lot of work. The company consisted of a hollowed man, an orphan girl who remembered him better than he remembered himself, a boy who believed in heroes, and a former Inquisitor who had not yet learned how to be anything else. But the sign was honest in spirit, and honesty was the whole business now.

Because here was the thing Silas Pemberton had learned, at a price he could not itemize because the ledger pages themselves were gone: a memory given is worth ten times a memory taken.

* * *

His first customer of the day was the widow Marta, who came every week, though she no longer needed to.

"You're early," Silas said.

"You're open," she said. "That's rarer."

She sat in the chair by the window — the good chair, the one Tam had dragged home from a junk cart and re-caned badly — and closed her eyes, and Silas did what he always did. He reached into the small wooden box on the counter, the one lined with felt, and lifted out one of the little glass vials, and held it up so the morning caught it.

Inside swam a memory of grief. Her grief, or something shaped like it — the shape grief takes when you pour it out of a person and look at it in the light. He had taken it from her years ago, back when taking was all he knew, and sold it back to her as peace, and peace had been a lie with a nice texture. Now she came to hold the vial sometimes. Not to drink it back. Just to remember that she had survived something large enough to fill a bottle.

"Same one?" he asked.

"Same one." She held it in both hands, the way you hold a bird you've decided not to keep. "My husband used to hum when he worked. I couldn't tell you the tune anymore. But I can remember that he hummed, and that I loved it, and that's enough. That's more than enough."

She set the vial down gently, thanked him, left a coin he didn't ask for, and went out into the street where the baker's boy was shouting about the first apples of the season.

Silas looked at the vial a long moment. Then he put it back in the box with the others — forty-odd of them now, griefs and guilts and one terrible beautiful lie about a king — and thought, not for the first time, that he had spent twenty years learning to take things from people and three years learning the far harder trick: giving them somewhere safe to keep what they couldn't carry.

The bell over the door rang. It wasn't a customer.

It was Wren, carrying two loaves and a scowl, which was her way of carrying affection.

"Tam tried to braid the goat's tail again," she announced. "The goat has opinions. Also Vess says if you don't eat breakfast she's going to start confiscating your stock, and she knows exactly which shelf the good memories are on, so don't test her."

"Tell her I fear her deeply."

"She knows." Wren dropped the loaves on the counter and leaned against it, studying him with those sharp eyes that had once been empty of him and were now, infuriatingly, full. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-the-vials thing. The counting-things thing. You do it when you're checking whether you're still hollow." She said it plainly, without pity, which was why he could bear it. "Are you?"

He considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honest answers and he'd promised them. He ran a hand through his hair — grey coming in early now, at the temples, the price of the unbinding written on his body in small installments — and took inventory the way he'd once inventoried a mark's house.

"I don't remember my mother," he said. "I don't remember being a child. I don't remember the man who taught me to steal, except as a story I know happened to someone. There's a hole in me shaped like twenty years, and it doesn't ache anymore, but it's there. I can feel the edges of it when it rains."

"And?"

"And there's a hole in every house in this valley," Silas said. "Every family lost somebody to the forgetting, or to the Inquisition, or just to time. A hole isn't emptiness, Wren. It's a place where something can go."

Wren smiled — quick, crooked, gone again, like most of her smiles — and tossed him a loaf. "Eat. You're philosophizing. That's always a blood sugar problem."

* * *

Midmorning brought the schoolchildren, which was Tam's department.

They came in a noisy clatter, six of them, the eldest maybe eleven, and they crowded around the counter where Tam held court behind a display of memory-vials arranged like sweets. This was the arrangement they'd all agreed on after long argument: children should learn what memories were, what they cost, what they were worth, before anyone offered to trade in theirs. The Hollow had run for generations on the assumption that a person's past was currency. Silas intended to spend whatever was left of his life correcting the exchange rate.

"Show them the king one," said the smallest girl, a gap-toothed creature named Pell who had appointed herself Tam's lieutenant.

"That one's not for showing," Tam said gravely, in the voice he used when he was repeating something an adult had told him, which meant it was something Silas had told him. "That one's history. History stays in the box unless you're ready for it."

"Are *you* ready for it?" Pell demanded.

"No," Tam said cheerfully. "But I'm going to look anyway, someday. That's what brave means. Being scared and looking anyway."

Silas, sorting receipts at the far end of the counter, kept his head down and let the warmth move through him without trying to name it. Naming things had been his old trade. These days he preferred to let some things stay big and unnamed, the way weather stays weather.

The boy had changed him more than the unbinding had, if he was honest. The impostor had been grown from his childhood — from a memory of a boy waiting for a mother who never came home — and when Silas had fed that seed-memory into the thing wearing Aldric's face, starving it, unmaking it, he had assumed he was killing the child he'd been. Instead, somehow, impossibly, Tam had walked into the vacancy. Not replacing. Refilling. The boy followed him around the shop asking questions, believing in him, imitating the way he rolled his sleeves, and every imitation was a small stitch across the hole.

You can't get your childhood back, Silas had concluded. But apparently you can watch one happen to somebody else, close enough to touch, and find that it heals in a direction you weren't expecting.

"Master Pemberton." Pell had detached from the group and appeared at his elbow, holding up a vial she must have palmed from the display — quick fingers, he noted professionally; the girl had a future in legitimate commerce or none at all. "What's in this one?"

He glanced at the label in his own handwriting. *A wedding, remembered by both parties.* One of the return-jobs. A couple who had married during the bad years, when the Inquisition taxed memories of joy, had each sold their half of the day to survive. Silas had tracked both halves down, bought them back at ruinous cost, and rejoined them into a single seamless memory that neither had lived whole. They'd come in together last month to drink it, holding hands, and wept, and laughed, and argued about whether the cake had been honey or plum.

"That one," Silas said, "is proof that two broken halves can make a whole thing. Put it back gently. It's already been dropped once by somebody with quicker fingers than yours."

Pell returned it with the solemnity of a knight racking a sword, and the children clattered out toward the schoolhouse, and Tam lingered at the door.

"He says hi," Tam said. "Aldric. The letter came this morning. Wren hid it from you because you make the face."

"What face?"

"The face you're making right now."

* * *

The letter was on the kitchen table when Silas finally surrendered and went looking for it. Aldric's hand, careful and upright, the writing of a man relearning how to mean what he wrote.

*Pemberton,* it began, because the restored king had never quite managed to call him by his first name, and Silas found he preferred it that way; some distances were structural.

*I write to report that the abdication is signed and the council has stopped pretending to mourn it. I farm now, near the coast. My hands are useless at it. I have never been happier. The gods remain silent — I check the old shrines when my knees permit, out of respect rather than hope — and I find the silence suits me. I heard them answer prayers for too long while wearing another man's face inside my own. Let them rest. Let us all rest.*

*I enclose nothing, because you would only refuse it. But I will say what I did not say at midwinter, when I woke weeping among strangers: whatever you paid, it was too much, and I will spend what remains of my life acknowledging that debt. If the boy ever wishes to see the sea, my door is open. So is my orchard. So, though he will deny it, is Marlow's — he sends word through channels I pretend not to understand, and asks each time whether you've forgiven him. Tell him yes. Tell him every year until he believes it.*

*You gave a kingdom back its king and asked for nothing. I am told that makes you either a saint or a fool. Having met you, I know which.*

*A.*

Silas read it twice, folded it along its existing creases, and put it in the drawer with the other letters, which was also where he kept the ribbon Wren had worn the day they met, and the guard's badge Vess had surrendered with her oath, and a small carved goat of no significance whatsoever that Tam had made him and that Silas would deny owning under torture.

Marlow. He sat with that a moment. The old fence had sold their route to the Inquisition and burned half of Casterhale doing it, and then, when the truth about the impostor came out, had spent his entire treacherous fortune buying food for the displaced, and had sent Silas a letter every season since that began *I owe you* and ended *I know.* Forgiveness, Silas had learned, wasn't a feeling. It was a practice, like sweeping a floor. You did it daily whether or not it showed results.

He got paper and wrote four words: *Yes. Every year. Idiot.* He addressed it through channels he pretended not to understand.

* * *

Vess found him at dusk on the shop's back step, where he went to watch the light die over the valley rim. She sat down beside him without asking, which after three years was its own kind of greeting. She still wore grey. She still carried a knife. Some conversions are partial and permanent.

"Shrine on the north road's still dark," she reported. "I checked on patrol. All of them dark. People leave flowers anyway." She turned something over in her fingers — an Inquisition seal, defaced, which she carried the way Marta carried her grief-vial. To remember surviving something. "I keep thinking I'll feel the gods come back. Some morning. Like weather."

"Do you want to?"

Vess considered it with the seriousness she gave everything. "I prayed once," she said, "and something answered, and it ruined thirty years of my life and nearly ended the world. And I watched a child refuse to break under a monster's reading-gaze, and I watched a thief give away the last of his mother to save people who'd never know his name." She flipped the defaced seal, caught it. "I think if the gods came back, I'd want them to have learned what those people know. Otherwise let them sleep."

"They'd have to learn it from us," Silas said. "We're the only ones who stayed."

"Hm." Almost a laugh. From Vess, that was a hymn. "Wren wants to expand the shop. Take the return-trade up the valley, train people in Harrow's Ford to do what you do. She says you'll resist for a month and then agree."

"Wren knows me disturbingly well."

"She says that too." Vess stood, brushed off her coat, paused. "For what it's worth, Pemberton — I hunted you across half a kingdom to kill you. Best misjudgment of my career."

She went inside. The lamplight through the window laid a warm rectangle on the yard, and from within came the sound of Tam arguing with Wren about dinner, and the smell of bread, and the ordinary racket of the living.

Silas stayed on the step a while longer.

* * *

Night, and the shop closed, and the household asleep — Tam sprawled, Vess silent as furniture, Wren reading by candle even though she'd sworn she'd stop. Silas lay awake in the room above the shop doing the thing Wren called the staring-at-the-ceiling thing, which was the nighttime version of the counting-things thing.

He reached inward, carefully, the way you probe a tooth. The hole was there. Twenty years wide. His mother's face: gone. His mother's voice: gone. The fact of her love: gone, except that everyone had told him about it so many times that he knew it the way you know a country you've never visited — by its borders, its exports, its reputation.

And yet.

Here was the strange arithmetic of his life now. He had entered the Hollow twenty years ago with a head full of stolen goods and a heart he'd locked against audit. He had survived by taking. He had been saved by giving — her memory to the monster, his confession to Wren, his name to a boy, his trust to an ex-Inquisitor, his forgiveness to a traitor, his whole hollow self to a valley that filled it up the way water fills any shape you set it in.

The shrines stayed dark. The houses were full. Somewhere in the mathematics of that sentence was everything he'd ever needed to know.

He was almost asleep when the bell over the shop door rang.

Not the wind — the door was bolted. Not a cat; the bell hung too high. It rang twice, soft, deliberate, the way a person rings when they don't want to wake the house.

Silas was downstairs in seconds, knife from the drawer, lamp unlit, moving along the wall out of habit. Through the dark glass of the shop window he could see the street, silvered with moon, empty in both directions.

Except for the doorstep.

On the doorstep sat a package, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, and beside it — this was the part that stopped his breath — a single glass vial, standing upright in the frost, glinting faintly from within.

He unbolted the door and picked up the vial first, turning it to the moonlight. Inside swam a memory. He recognized the craft of the containment instantly — the fine work, the old work, the kind nobody had made since the gods went quiet. And he recognized the memory itself, or rather he recognized what it was *of*, because the label was written in a hand he'd last seen on a letter that morning:

*Your mother's face. As she looked at you, aged six. Kept safe these twenty years by one who owed her. Consider us even. — M.*

Below, in different ink, newer, shakier: *Found it in his effects. Thought you should decide. — A.*

Silas stood on his own doorstep in the cold for a long time, holding the last piece of his mother in one hand and the wrapping-paper in the other, and the hole in him — the twenty-year hole, the rain-shaped hole, the hole he'd made his peace with — shifted for the first time in three years, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it hears a familiar footstep.

Behind him, upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Wren's voice, sleepy and unsurprised: "Well? Are you going to stand out there all night, or are you going to come tell us what you found?"

Silas looked at the vial. At the door. At the lamplight spilling down the stairs, and the faces beginning to appear in it.

He stepped inside, and closed the door behind him, and reached for the cork.
