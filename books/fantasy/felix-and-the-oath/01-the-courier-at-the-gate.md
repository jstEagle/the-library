## 1. The Courier at the Gate

The trouble with living in a valley that keeps magic out is that you stop believing in it, the way you stop believing in the dark under your bed once you're old enough to know better. Hollowmere had spent three hundred years teaching its children that nothing crossed the boundary stones — no enchantment, no beast of the wild lands, no wound that couldn't be explained by honest causes like falling off a roof or arguing with a plow. We were a people of ledgers and turnips. It was a good life, if you didn't mind being certain about everything.

I minded. But I'd learned young that minding was a thing you did quietly.

That evening I was at the eastern gate with my satchel of herbs, because Old Marta's cough had turned wet and the light was going amber over the ridge, and because — though I'd never have said this aloud — dusk was when the valley spoke clearest. Not in words. In the small grammar of things: the sheep pressing together for no reason, the swallows flying low and silent, the way the pines along the boundary went still all at once, like a room full of people who've heard a knock.

I noticed the stillness before I noticed him.

A man came down from the ridge road, and he came wrong. Not limping-wrong, not drunk-wrong. Wrong the way a puppet is wrong when half its strings are cut. He moved as if his body were a suggestion he was doing his best to honor, and every few steps one leg simply declined to participate until he hauled it forward by main force. His coat had been grey once. Now it was mostly red-brown, and the smell reached me before he did — iron and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a wound that has stopped pretending it will close.

And here is the thing I want you to understand, because it matters: there was no reason for him to be bleeding like that. Men fell off horses on the ridge road. Men got gored by boars, mauled by wolves, beaten by other men over debts and daughters. I had stitched all of those. But those wounds came from *things* — teeth, hooves, fists — and they behaved accordingly. What I saw when he collapsed against the first boundary stone was not that. The tears in his coat showed flesh beneath that looked *burned away*, eaten, as though something had taken bites out of him without using a mouth. There was frost on the wound edges. Frost, in late summer, in a valley where frost had no business arriving for another two months.

Magic wounds. Wounds that shouldn't exist where magic cannot follow.

He saw me and something desperate kindled in his face, which told me he'd been afraid he'd die unwatched, and something in me — some foolish, unschooled part — thought, *oh, you poor creature, you came to the wrong valley.*

"Water," he rasped. Then, as I knelt: "No. No time. Listen."

"I'm listening," I said, already pulling cloth from my satchel, because you can listen and work at the same time; my mother taught me that, or rather taught me the working, and I supplied the listening myself.

"You're — " He coughed, and the sound was wet and terrible. "Whitlock."

I went very still. Nobody said that name. Not *to* me. The village called me Zeph, Zephyrine when the elders were feeling formal, and "that girl" when they weren't. Whitlock was a name that lived in the church record and nowhere else, a name like a splinter everyone had agreed to ignore.

"You're mistaken," I said. "My name is—"

"*Zephyrine Whitlock.*" He said it like a man reciting a debt. His hand shot out and caught my wrist with a strength that startled me, given the state of him, and pressed something into my palm. Folded leather, cracked with age, stiff with what I chose at the time to believe was old wax. "From Felix. From — " another cough, folding him nearly double — "from the one who carried it. Tell them the covenant is failing. Tell them the ward is a lid on a pot, and the pot—" 

His voice failed. I put my arm behind his shoulders and felt how little of him was left, how the great frame of him had been hollowed out around that wound.

"What pot?" I asked. "What covenant? Who sent you?"

"The Sundered Court," he whispered. "Follow the map. Only a — " His eyes fixed on mine, and they were grey eyes going paler by the moment, like milk poured into water. "Only a Whitlock can mend it. Your grandmother knew. Your grandmother *knew*—"

"Who are you?" I demanded, and hated how my voice shook. "What's your name?"

"Felix," he said, and smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was genuinely glad. "Felix. At least — someone should know. Someone should know my name." His grip tightened on my wrist. "They'll come for it. Riders in grey. Don't let them test your blood. Whatever you do, girl — don't let them—"

The sentence never landed. His head tipped back against the boundary stone, and the gladness went out of his face all at once, the way a lamp goes out — not gradually, but completely, so that the man who remained was unmistakably nobody. I have seen death on tables and in beds, in calving barns and fevered attics. I know its signature. This was it.

I sat there in the amber light holding a dead stranger's wrist and a folded piece of leather, and above me the pines began, very slowly, to move again.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I record it without pride: I lied.

Not immediately. First I did the honest things. I closed his eyes. I checked, twice, with fingers at his throat and an ear to his chest, because even now some stubborn part of me wanted him to be merely dying instead of dead. I wrapped the map in my satchel cloth without looking at it — I don't know why, except that looking at it felt like opening a door I couldn't close, and I have always been careful about doors. Then I stood up, brushed the dirt from my knees, and walked back into Hollowmere wearing my face like a well-fitted mask, and told the first person who asked — Tam Voss's mother, at her gate, wanting to know if I'd seen her son — that no, I hadn't, lovely evening though.

The lie sat in my mouth like a coin. Small. Cold. Heavier than it looked.

By full dark I'd reported to Elder Hask, because that's what you do when a man dies at the boundary: you report it, and the elders handle it, and by morning the valley has absorbed the event into its ledger and everything is certain again. Except I didn't report it. I told Hask I'd found nothing at the east gate but a dropped bundle of rags, probably blown over the stones from the wild side, and I watched him nod and write it down, and I understood with a clarity that frightened me that I had just become a different person. There is a self you are before your first real lie, and a self after, and the distance between them is short but you cannot walk back across it.

"He'll need burying, whoever he is," Hask said mildly. "If he turns up. Things come over the stones sometimes. Dead things. The ward sees them off soon enough."

"The ward," I repeated.

"Keeps the wild out," Hask said, in the tone of a man explaining rain. "Has done since the founding. Why do you think we live so peaceful?"

Why indeed. I carried that question home like a stone in my shoe.

I buried Felix myself that night, past the sheep pens where the ground was soft, digging by lantern-light because waiting for morning felt like a betrayal I couldn't name. I buried him deep, and I marked the grave with a fieldstone because he deserved a marker and no name to put on it — except that wasn't true anymore, was it? I knew his name. *Someone should know my name,* he'd said, and I knelt there in the dark and said it aloud over the turned earth, "Felix," like a prayer or a password, and the night took it and gave nothing back.

It was while I was filling the last spadeful that the crows started.

Now, I should explain something here, and I ask you to keep it between us, reader, because keeping it has been the discipline of my whole life: animals talk to me. Not in words — nothing so tidy — but in pressures, images, urgencies that arrive in the mind fully formed, the way you wake knowing you've forgotten something. I have never told a soul in Hollowmere. A girl who hears beasts in a valley built on the principle that beasts are outside and silence is inside — that girl would not last a season. So I have spent twenty years translating my own mind into acceptable silence, and I am very good at it, and I was good at it that night too, right up until the crows' message arrived and refused to be quiet.

*Riders. Ridge. Grey.*

Three images, one urgency. I stood in the open grave's shadow with cold hands and looked east, toward the boundary stones, invisible now in the dark, and felt the first true crack run through the world I'd been standing on for twenty years. Because the crows were wrong, you see. That was the horror of it. Riders in grey belonged to the wild lands, beyond the ward, and the ward kept such things out — Hask had just told me so, in the tone of a man explaining rain. If riders were coming down the ridge road toward Hollowmere, then either the crows were lying —

or the ward had never been a wall at all.

Or — and this thought arrived last and coldest, settling into me like frost finding a crack — the ward wasn't built to keep the wild out at all. Maybe it was built to keep something *in*. Something the valley needed contained. Something with a family name attached.

*Your grandmother knew,* Felix had said, and died with the sentence unfinished, and unfinished sentences are the most dangerous objects in the world.

I filled the grave. I took up my lantern. And somewhere far off along the ridge, faint as a held breath, I heard — or imagined, or was told by the crows, who do not imagine — the sound of many hooves, moving at a walk, patient as a verdict, coming down toward Hollowmere.

I put my hand flat against the satchel, over the folded shape within, and it seemed to me the leather was warm. Almost like a heartbeat. Almost like a compass needle swinging toward something I could no longer pretend I hadn't heard.

The safe world had cracked open, and through the crack, patient and grey and inevitable, the rest of it was coming through.
