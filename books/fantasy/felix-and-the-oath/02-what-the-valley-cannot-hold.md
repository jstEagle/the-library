## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

I did not sleep.

I want to say that as a matter of pride, the way soldiers say it, but the truth is less flattering: every time I closed my eyes I saw the courier's mouth shaping my family name, *Whitlock*, like a man pressing a key into a lock he hoped still worked. And every time I opened them again the satchel was where I'd left it, under the loose floorboard beneath my bed, radiating its faint wrong warmth like a loaf someone had forgotten in the oven for thirty years.

By grey morning light I made myself take it out and look at what I was harboring.

The map was drawn on skin. Not parchment — I know parchment, I've bought enough of it from the tanner down the lane to paper a small chapel — but skin, cured thin and soft, and marked in ink the color of dried blood, which it may well have been. It showed the valley of Hollowmere at its center, our little ring of fields and roofs rendered with loving precision, and then the boundary stones, and then everything beyond them rendered in a hand that grew shakier the farther it went from home. The Ashwood. The Grey Fells. A place near the top edge marked only with a symbol I didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye refusing to blink.

Along the margin, in a different, steadier script, ran a single line:

*The covenant fails where it was sworn. Only the blood that broke it can mend it.*

I put it away. My hands were not entirely steady, and I had work to do, and one of the things I have learned about fear is that it is a terrible foreman but an excellent fuel.

* * *

Felix — if that was his name; he'd never said it aloud, but the compass in his pocket had been warm against his ribs and I had felt, in those last minutes, something like a name pressed against the inside of my skull, patient and ordinary and terribly human — Felix lay where I'd left him, under the willow past the millrace, in ground I had chosen because it was soft and because no one walks there after the first frost.

I had filled the grave in the dark, badly, guiltily, like a child hiding evidence of a broken jar. Now, in daylight, I did it properly. I cut sods and laid them neat. I set a stone at the head, one I carried up from the millrace myself, and I thought about what people carve on stones and decided that anything I carved would be a lie, so I left it blank. He could be anyone. That was the gift I could give him: anonymity, in a valley where anonymity was worth more than gold.

The crows came while I worked. Three of them, then seven, settling along the willow's low branches with the particular silence crows use when they are not merely watching you but *reporting* on you.

"You might help," I told them, without looking up. Talking to birds in broad daylight, in the open, is the sort of thing that gets a Whitlock noticed, and being noticed is the one activity this family has historically avoided with the dedication other families reserve for religion. But the willow shade is deep, and grief makes even careful people careless.

*Deep,* said the eldest crow, who has a voice like a hinge in need of oil. *Cold. Good ground.*

"Thank you."

*He came through the stones,* the crow said, and ruffled its feathers in a way I had learned long ago to read as the avian equivalent of spitting. *Through. Not over. Not around. Through.*

My hands went still on the sod I was laying. "That's not possible."

*No,* the crow agreed comfortably. *And yet.*

I sat back on my heels and looked at the boundary ridge, blue-grey in the morning haze, crowned with its ancient standing stones. Every child in Hollowmere learns the lesson before they learn their letters: the ward keeps magic out. Magic cannot cross. What lives beyond stays beyond, and what we are stays what we are, and this arrangement is the whole reason the valley exists, the whole reason my great-grandparents' generation didn't flee south like everyone else with sense. The ward is the wall and the promise and the prayer, all three.

And yet a dying man had walked through it carrying wounds that bled black at the edges, the way wounds bleed when magic has been in them too long. Wounds that should not have been able to exist inside the ward at all.

Unless.

Unless the ward was not a wall with a crack in it. Unless the ward was a lid, and something underneath had been pushing for a long time, and last night the lid had flexed.

"Riders," I said to the crow. "On the ridge. Last night. Was that real?"

The crows looked at one another. Crows can do a remarkable amount of conversation with a look.

*Grey men,* said the eldest finally. *Many. They walk the horses. They stopped at the high stone and the tall one put his hand on it and the stone* — the crow paused, hunting for the word, and crows do not usually hunt for words — *the stone answered him.*

"What does that mean?"

*It means,* said the crow, with terrible gentleness, *that the stone knows him, girl. And we don't think it should.*

* * *

Elder Maren found me at my gate at midmorning, which was either coincidence or surveillance, and with Maren it has never once been coincidence.

"Zeph." She is seventy if she is a day, built like a fence post that has decided to become a barn, and she has known me since I was small enough to ride her husband's shoulders. Her eyes are pale and quick and miss nothing, which is why I had rehearsed. "You were out late. Bram saw your lantern past the millrace."

Bram. Of course. Bram sees everything and reports most of it, in the manner of all men who have never once been interesting themselves.

"A sick ewe," I said. "One of the north-field ones. I stayed with her till she was through it."

Maren looked at me. I looked back with the serene, slightly bored face of a woman who has indeed spent her night elbow-deep in a sheep, and I discovered something about lying that no one warns you of: it is easy. It is horrifyingly easy. The words come out smooth and pre-fitted, like they've been waiting behind your teeth for years for permission, and some quiet part of you stands off to the side taking notes and wondering what else you've been capable of all along.

"Sheep," Maren said. "At night. In frost."

"They're stupid, not seasonal."

A breath of a laugh escaped her, and then died, and then she did the thing I had not prepared for, which was step closer and lower her voice. "There are strangers on the high road. Grey cloaks. Came down off the fells before dawn, moving slow, counting things." Her pale eyes held mine. "Counting *houses*, Zeph. Counting heads. They say census, and maybe it's census, but the last time grey men counted this valley was before your mother was born, and my grandmother told me what happened after."

"What happened after?" I asked, and hated how much I wanted to know, and hated more that asking was itself a kind of confession.

"People were counted," Maren said, "and then people were missing." She patted my arm once, hard, the way she did everything. "Keep your door barred. Keep your answers short. And Zeph — whatever Bram saw, make sure next time he sees nothing."

She walked off toward the green, where the other elders were already gathering with the grim ceremony of people preparing to be polite to power, and I stood at my gate with my heart going like a hammer and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: I had just lied to Maren, who had fed me stew through three winters of my father's long dying, and I would do it again in the next breath, and this was what my grandmother's blood actually was — not a curse exactly, not a gift exactly, but a *tendency*. A leaning. We lean away from the light.

Second: the riders hadn't come through the ward. They'd come *down from the fells*, outside it, along the rim, the way a man walks the top of a wall looking for the door. Which meant the ward had let them in, or they had a key, or —

Or the ward had never been pointed the way we were taught it was pointed.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the ordinary work of my life with extraordinary attention, because if the grey men were coming to count heads, the safest head in Hollowmere would be the one attached to the most boring body. I mended fence. I hauled water. I sold eggs to Widow Hale and let her tell me the entire genealogy of her late husband's grudges. Inside, beneath the pleasant drudgery, my mind turned the day's pieces over and over like a lockpick.

The courier had come *through* the stones, wounded by magic, carrying a map on skin addressed to a family that officially did not exist anymore. The grey men had come *around* the ward's shoulder, patient as winter, and touched a standing stone that *answered* them. And between these two facts lay the shape of a truth I did not want, the way you don't want the diagnosis even as the physician's face tells you.

The ward doesn't keep magic out, I thought, standing in my yard as the light went long and amber. It keeps something *in*. Or it keeps us in. Or — worst and most likely — it was never a protection at all, only a fence around a field, and fences are built by whoever intends to come back for the crop.

The crows arrived at dusk, all of them, dozens, pouring over my roof like spilled ink and settling on the fence rails, the woodpile, the bare apple tree, until my yard looked dressed for a funeral.

*They're at the lower stones,* the eldest said. *The grey men. Dismounted. The tall one is walking the line of them, touching each stone, and where he touches* — the flock shifted, feathers lifting in a collective shudder — *where he touches, the stones go quiet.*

"Quiet how?"

*You know how a bell sounds after it stops?* the crow said. *It's like that. But worse. It's like the bell deciding it never rang at all.*

I looked east, toward the boundary ridge, invisible now in the gathering dark but as present to me as my own pulse. Somewhere up there a man was walking down a line of ancient stones unmaking them one touch at a time, unhurried, thorough, a clerk closing accounts. And when he reached the end of the row, there would be nothing between Hollowmere and the wild lands except habit.

The compass in the satchel under my floorboard had grown warmer all day. I could feel it through the floor, through my boots, a small insistent heat like a cat sitting on a chest, saying *now, now, now.*

"Not yet," I whispered.

But I caught myself, that night, packing. Quietly. A spare shirt. Dried apples. My grandfather's knife. The motions of a woman preparing to abandon everything she has ever been, performed by lamplight, while below the floorboards a dead man's compass kept its patient warmth and the crows on my roof kept their silent watch, and far off on the ridge the last of the boundary stones — I would learn later — went quiet as a held breath.

In the morning, the grey men would knock on doors and ask their gentle questions, and Elder Maren would give her short answers, and my neighbor Tam's brother would stand in the square and fail whatever test they carried in their grey sleeves, and the safe world would finish cracking open.

But that was morning's business. Tonight there was only me, and the map, and the long dark road beginning quietly, the way all roads do, at the edge of the lamplight.
