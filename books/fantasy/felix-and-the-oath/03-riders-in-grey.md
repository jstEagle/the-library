## 3. Riders in Grey

The crows came first. They always do.

I was mucking out the goat pen when the sky over the eastern ridge went black with wings, and every bird in Hollowmere began screaming a single word into my skull: *riders, riders, riders*. Crows have no gift for subtlety. If they were poets they'd be the kind who write everything in capital letters.

"Thank you," I told them, "I had gathered."

Nine shapes on the ridge road, moving at a walk that was somehow worse than a gallop. Men who hurry are afraid of something. Men who amble are afraid of nothing, and want you to know it.

They wore grey — not the grey of Hollowmere wool, which is honest grey, sheep-grey, grey with dirt in its teeth, but a uniform grey, dyed grey, *decided* grey. Cloaks, half-masks, and on each breast a small silver badge shaped like an open eye. I'd seen the emblem before, once, stamped on the ledger my father kept hidden under the floorboards with the rest of our family's sins. The Oathwardens. I'd assumed they were a story grandmothers told to keep children from whistling at birds.

Apparently they were a census.

That's what they called it, anyway. The tall one who dismounted at the well announced himself as Warden-Recorder Vess, and informed Elder Maren that by order of the Provisional Authority, all inhabitants of the valley would be counted, catalogued, and — this word he said gently, like a man offering sweets — *screened*. For health, he said. For the good of the valley.

Elder Maren, who is seventy and has buried two husbands and most of her illusions, looked at the eye-badges for a long moment and said, "We've never needed counting before."

"All things change," said Vess pleasantly.

Which is the kind of sentence that should make your skin crawl, because it's true, and because men only say it when they're the ones doing the changing.

* * *

Corvane let Vess do the talking. Vess was good at talking; it was the reason Corvane kept him, the way a man keeps a dog that barks so he himself can listen. Corvane stood at the edge of the well-square with his hood back and his hands folded, watching the village of Hollowmere the way a surgeon watches a body on the table: without cruelty, without kindness, with attention.

It was a pretty little lie of a place. Thatched roofs, crooked lanes, a shrine to some nameless local saint that hadn't been a real saint in three hundred years. The ward-stones at the valley's mouth still hummed — he could feel them in his back teeth, faint as a headache coming on. Old work. Whitlock work, if the archives were honest, and the archives were always honest; it was everyone else who lied.

The courier had died four miles east of here with a map sewn into his coat lining and a Whitlock seal burned into the wax. He had also died with wounds no blade made, which meant magic had touched him inside the ward's reach — which meant the ward was failing, or had been breached, or had been *let* fail. Three possibilities, and Corvane disliked all of them equally, because all three ended with him standing in a village square pretending to count farmers while something older than his order came apart at the seams.

"Name?" Vess asked a farmer.

"Hob Tanner."

"Family?"

"Wife. Two boys."

"Roll up your sleeve, Master Tanner."

The blood-test was simple, which was what made it terrible. A needle of grey glass, a drop of blood on a slate that Vess carried chained to his wrist. Clean blood sat on the slate like rainwater. Tainted blood moved. Curled. Reached, very slightly, toward the nearest living thing.

Hob Tanner's blood sat still. Vess gave him a chalk mark on the hand — a small circle, closed, complete — and Hob walked away rubbing at it like he couldn't decide whether to be proud or ashamed.

Corvane watched the villagers watch the test. That was the thing about fear done properly: nobody screamed. They just got quieter. A village full of people deciding, all at once and separately, to be smaller than they were.

He had seen it before. He had been the cause of it before. Somewhere between those two facts lived the man he had become, and he had stopped visiting that country years ago.

* * *

They set up in the meeting-house, because terror travels lighter with a queue. One table, one slate, one line of villagers shuffling forward with their sleeves already rolled, the way you learn fast to do, because fumbling with your cuff in front of the eye-badge marks you slower than the needle does.

I stood in that line with Felix's map sewn into the lining of my own coat, directly over my ribs, where I could feel it like a second heartbeat. Bad idea, sewing it there. Worse idea, standing in the exact line of people being tested for the thing the map represented. But skipping the census would mark me faster than any slate, and besides — I wanted to see how it worked. You can't beat a trap you haven't watched close.

The compass rode in my pocket, silent since the burial. Silent, but warm. Felix had been dead six days and the brass still held heat like a hand.

"Name?" said Vess.

"Zeph Miller." Miller was safe. Half the valley was Millers if you went back far enough; we're not a creative people.

"Family?"

"Goats," I said, "mostly."

Someone behind me snorted. Vess did not. He had the smile of a man who has heard every joke fear makes and filed them all under the same heading. "Sleeve, Mistress Miller."

I rolled it. The needle stung. My blood hit the slate and sat there, red and ordinary, and I watched it with the particular intensity of a liar watching her own face in a mirror. Nothing moved. Nothing curled. Whatever my grandmother's blood had done, whatever the gift in me actually *was*, it didn't show on grey glass.

Vess drew his circle of chalk on my hand. "Clean," he said, and there was — I swear this — a flicker of disappointment in it. These men *wanted* to find something. A hunt with no quarry is just walking.

I walked out into the sun feeling like I'd gotten away with murder, which, given that I was hiding a dead man's map inside my coat, wasn't far off.

And then I saw old Marta Brannigan step up to the table.

Marta, who had taught me letters. Marta, who fed every stray cat in the valley and claimed she couldn't, who swore up and down she had no way with animals even though her yard looked like a parliament of them. Marta, eighty-one years old, hands like tree roots, rolling up her sleeve without a tremor because she didn't know yet what she should be afraid of.

I watched the drop of her blood land on the slate.

I watched it *move*.

Not much. Not dramatically. It leaned — the way a plant leans at a window, the way anything leans toward what it loves. Toward me, as it happened. I felt it, faintly, the way you feel someone say your name in another room. And I saw Vess see it, and I saw his disappointment die.

"Tainted," he said, almost tenderly. "Marta Brannigan. Do you have beasts you speak with?"

"I talk to my cats," said Marta, bewildered. "Everyone talks to their cats."

"Yes," said Vess. "Everyone does."

Two Wardens took her arms. She didn't struggle — she was too old to struggle and too confused to try. She looked back at the line of us, at me, and her eyes went from my face to my chalk circle and something passed over her expression that I will be seeing when I close my eyes for the rest of my life. Not accusation. Worse. *Understanding.* She thought I was next. She was sorry for me.

They put her in the grey wagon with the covered top, and the wagon rolled east toward the boundary stones, and the line shuffled forward one place, and nobody said a word, and the chalk circles on forty hands gleamed like forty little closed eyes.

* * *

That night Corvane read the day's tally by lamplight: two hundred and six souls screened, one taint found, one removal effected. A clean page. In nineteen years of service he had filled eleven thousand such pages, and if you stacked them all together they would tell you exactly nothing about why he did the work.

His brother would have known why. His brother had opinions about everything, right up until the night the unbound magic came out of the hills and took him apart at the age of fifteen, leaving Corvane — sixteen, screaming, holding a hand that was no longer entirely a hand — with a lifelong conviction that the covenant's walls were not cruelty but mercy. Walls keep things out. Everyone remembers that. Nobody remembers walls also keep things *in*, and that both jobs matter, and that somebody has to stand at the wall and be hated for it.

Vess appeared in the doorway of the meeting-house. "The old woman's asking for you. Won't stop asking. Says she'll only talk to the one in charge."

"They always say that."

"She says she knows where the Whitlock girl lives."

Corvane put down the tally book.

The lantern guttered. Outside, the ward-stones hummed their failing hum, and somewhere beyond them, in the dark of the Ashwood, something answered.

"Bring her in," he said.
