## 5. Ink and Ash

The Ashwood earned its name honestly. Everything in it wore a coat of grey — the trunks, the ferns, the light itself, which came down through the canopy like something strained through cloth. Even the birdsong sounded apologetic.

I had been walking for two days since the boundary stones, and I still hadn't decided whether magic was wonderful or trying to kill me. Possibly both. The compass tugged at my hip with Felix's voice caught inside it, fragments surfacing at odd moments — *left at the split oak*, *don't drink the red water* — delivered in that dry, patient tone of his, as if dying had been an inconvenience he was handling gracefully and I was the one making a fuss.

"Left at the split oak," it murmured now, though there was no split oak for another mile. The compass liked to get ahead of itself. Grief does that, I suppose, even secondhand grief belonging to someone else's ghost.

The map, meanwhile, was doing its own kind of talking. Felix's blood had soaked into its western corner, and where it dried, the ink moved — slowly, like things waking up. Roads rearranged themselves when I wasn't looking. A river I'd crossed that morning appeared on the parchment a full day behind me, sulking. And always, always, the same mark waited ahead: a small square stamped with a tower, labeled in cramped script, *Waystation Nine.*

I was beginning to understand that the map didn't show places. It showed intentions. Mine, mostly. Which meant Waystation Nine was somewhere I needed to be, and I'd learned enough in the past week to distrust anything I needed.

The crows found me at midday. Three of them, dropping out of the grey canopy to walk along a fallen log beside me like a delegation of disapproving clerks.

"You're far from home, valley-girl," said the largest, in the rusted-hinge voice crows use when they want you to know they're lowering themselves to speak plainly.

"I'm far from everyone's home."

"Mm. There's smoke ahead." He ruffled his feathers, which is crow for *I have more to say but you haven't paid me yet.* "Smoke that isn't cooking anything. Smoke that's remembering a fire."

I stopped. "A burned building?"

"A burned *everything*. Two days old, maybe three. And something else." The crow hopped closer, head tilting with that terrible avian precision. "Something breathing in the ash. Man-shaped. Smells of iron and of—" he paused, tasting the air with his whole body, "—of *oath*. You want to be careful, valley-girl. Oath-smell means Wardens."

My hand went to the knife at my belt before I could stop it, which was useless and we both knew it. Against a Warden, a knife is a gesture, not a weapon.

"Is he dying?"

"He's man-shaped and breathing. Whether those are the same thing, you'll have to ask him."

I should have walked around. Every sensible instinct I owned — and I'd been told more than once that I owned very few — said to give the ash a wide berth and let whatever was breathing there finish its business alone. But the compass had gone suddenly warm against my hip, and warm meant *yes*, and I had promised myself on the second night out, alone under unfamiliar stars, that I would trust Felix's ghost over my own cowardice.

Cowardice, naturally, objected.

* * *

The waystation had been a substantial building once — stone footings, timber upper floors, a watchtower whose top half now lay across the yard like a felled tree. Fire had taken it from the inside out; I could see that much even without knowing buildings. The doorframe stood alone, charred black, opening onto nothing, and through it drifted that particular smell of long-dead fire: cold smoke, wet ash, the ghost of burning.

The breathing thing was in the ruins of the stable.

He'd dragged himself under the collapsed roof-beam, into the one dry corner, and built himself a nest of straw and half-burnt blanket that spoke of a man planning to stay a while. Or die a while. Both, maybe. He was younger than I expected — thirty at most, sandy-haired, with the grey-and-silver livery of an Oathwarden scorched down one side like a moth had gotten at him. His left arm was wrapped in strips of the same blanket, stained brown, and his face had the waxy look of someone who'd made a detailed arrangement with pain and was honoring it hour by hour.

His eyes opened when my boot crunched the ash. Grey eyes. Warden eyes. They went straight past my face to my hip, to the compass, and something flickered there — recognition, or its cousin.

"Well," he said. His voice was gravel and rust. "Either the map sent you, or my luck has finally turned murderous."

"The map doesn't send anyone. It just... points."

"It pointed me here too." He tried to sit up, thought better of it, and settled for looking less horizontal. "Zephyrine Whitlock."

Every hair on my arms stood up. Not because he knew the name — Felix had known it, the Wardens knew it, apparently everyone but me had been briefed on it — but because of how he said it. Not like a charge. Like a condolence.

"How do you know that name?"

"Because I've spent four years reading files about your family, and three days deciding what to do about it." He gestured vaguely at the ruin around us with his good arm. "This is what I decided. In case the aesthetic wasn't clear."

"You're a Warden."

"I'm a *former* Warden, as of approximately the moment my colleagues set this station on fire with me in it." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Tam. Tam Voss. And before you run — and you should, it's the smart move — consider that they burned me for the same reason they'll hunt you. I read the wrong file. Your file."

The crows had followed me in and arranged themselves along the broken beam above him, watching with the frank interest of an audience that has already seen this play and knows how it ends. The big one muttered, "*Oath-smell,*" reproachfully, as if Tam's having bathed in it were a personal discourtesy.

I didn't run. I've thought since about why. Partly it was the arm, the clumsy field-dressing, the way he held it against his ribs like something borrowed. Partly it was that he hadn't reached for a weapon, and a Warden pretending harmlessness would have reached for one anyway, to sell the lie. Mostly, I think, it was the file. He'd read my family's file. Four years of it. And instead of turning me in, he'd gotten himself burned alive in a waystation, which is either the worst trap ever devised or the loneliest act of defection I've ever heard of.

"Who set the fire?"

"Warden Corvane's company. My company, until recently." His jaw tightened. "Corvane doesn't leave witnesses. I'd catalogued irregularities in the Hollowmere census — blood-test results that didn't match, a courier's death logged as 'natural' by men who weren't there. I filed a query. That's all. A *query*." He laughed, short and bitter. "In the Oathwardens, a query is a confession. Corvane had me assigned to Waystation Nine for 'audit review,' and that night the audit reviewed me."

"And you survived."

"Barely. Badly." He looked at his bandaged arm. "I crawled out through the feed cellar. Then I stayed, because I'm a fool, and because if I died here, at least I'd die knowing why. And then the map—" he nodded at my satchel, and his eyes sharpened despite the fever, "—the map started pulling. I felt it from the moment you crossed the tree line. Whitlock work. I'd know that seal anywhere; it's in the archive, in the restricted drawer, in the file I wasn't supposed to open."

"Whitlock work," I repeated. "You mean my family *made* maps like this?"

"Your family made *covenants* like this. Maps are just what's left when the ink dries." He shifted, winced, kept going. "The Sundered Court, the oath-stone, the terms — your grandmother didn't just break the covenant, Whitlock. She understood it better than the people who wrote it. That knowledge got written down. Sealed. And now her granddaughter is carrying it through the Ashwood with a dead courier's compass, pursued by the only man in the Order who understands what she's actually holding."

"Corvane."

"Corvane." Tam's voice dropped, and for the first time something other than bitterness entered it — something almost like fear, which frightened me more than anything he'd said so far. "Everyone thinks Corvane believes in the law. He doesn't. He believes in *containment*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a believer can be reasoned with."

The light was failing outside the ruined doorway, going from grey to darker grey, and I became aware of practicalities: night coming, fever rising off him like heat off a road, the wound beneath those filthy bandages needing what my grandmother's herbal book would have called *decisive attention* and what I would have called a mess.

"If I help you," I said, "you'll tell me everything. The file. The census. What Corvane wants with the map. All of it."

"If you help me, I'll tell you everything I know, which is less than you're hoping and more than is comfortable." He managed something that was almost a smile. "That's the best offer a defector can make. We deal in partial truths. It's in our charter."

I knelt beside him in the ash, unwrapped the bandage, and immediately regretted every choice that had led me there. The burn ran from elbow to shoulder, angry and weeping, but — and this surprised me — healing. Wrongly, crookedly, but healing, and around the edges of the wound the skin had a faint iridescence I recognized from Felix's wounds, from the thing that had killed him.

Magic burns differently. Magic-burned flesh remembers.

"This isn't ordinary fire," I said quietly.

"No," Tam agreed. "It's oath-fire. They burned the station with a rite. To erase records." His eyes met mine, steady despite the fever. "They were erasing *me*, Whitlock. Wardens don't get funerals. We get expungement."

I cleaned the wound with water boiled over a small fire I built in the yard, under the supervision of three crows who offered commentary throughout ("*lower*, girl, do you want to signal the entire province?"). I packed it with yarrow and comfrey from my satchel, bound it properly, and gave him willow bark for the fever. He bore all of it without a sound except one hiss when the yarrow went in, and afterward he lay back against his straw nest and looked at the broken sky through the missing roof.

"My brother was a Warden too," he said, apropos of nothing, or of everything. "Killed at the boundary stones eight years ago. Official record says beast-attack." A pause. "There are no beasts within forty miles of the boundary stones. The ward sees to that. So either the record lies, or the ward does. I joined the Order to find out which."

"And?"

"And now I'm lying in ash, arm cooked, helping the last Whitlock, so draw your own conclusions." He turned his head to look at me, and in the firelight his face lost the waxy distance and became simply a tired young man's face, stubborn and scared. "Your grandmother broke the covenant to save the beasts. Did you know that much?"

The world tilted slightly. "How do you—"

"It's in the file. Not in those words — the Order doesn't write truths, it writes *findings* — but it's there, between the lines, if you read it angry enough." He closed his eyes. "Get some sleep, Whitlock. Tomorrow we follow your map. And tomorrow night, if my sense of these things is right, Corvane follows *us*."

I sat by the fire a long time after he slept, the compass warm against my knee, the crows folded into the dark like punctuation. Somewhere south, a man who believed in containment was riding toward us with a company and a rite of erasure, and I had just bandaged the hands of the only person alive who could tell me what I was carrying.

The enemy had a face now. That was supposed to make things simpler.

It made them heavier.

* * *

We left at first light, Tam leaning on a staff cut from a burnt sapling, moving with the careful economy of a man renegotiating terms with his own body. The compass pulled east. The map's ink had rearranged itself overnight into a single road, and at its end, where Waystation Nine's little tower had been, a new mark was forming — faint, unfinished, like a word being written by someone not yet sure of the alphabet.

"What is it?" Tam asked, watching me stare at it.

"I don't know yet." I rolled up the map, and the unfinished mark vanished, waiting. "But it's drawing itself in my grandmother's handwriting."

Behind us, far to the south, the crows rose off the dead waystation in a single ragged sheet, screaming one word down the wind:

"*Riders.*"
