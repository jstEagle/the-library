## 6. The Sundered Court

The map said the court was three days' ride past the Ashwood's burned spine, and the map, so far, had not lied to me. It had merely omitted things. Like the fact that the last day of travel would be through country where the grass grew in whispers and the stones hummed if you touched them, like a house where someone had just stopped singing.

Tam rode half a length behind me the whole way, which I'd learned was his way of saying he didn't trust what was ahead and wanted to be between it and me anyway. Defectors, I was discovering, were a strange breed: guilty about everything, loyal by accident.

"You feel that?" he asked as we crested a ridge of black granite.

I did. It felt like standing too close to a struck bell. The air itself had a grain to it, a texture, as though the world here remembered being spoken to.

"The oaths," I said. "What's left of them."

"Left of them," Tam repeated, and something in his voice made me look back. He wasn't being wry. He was being afraid, which from a man who'd walked out of the Oathwardens with nothing but a stolen horse and a bad conscience was its own kind of information.

* * *

The Sundered Court did not look ruined so much as *interrupted*. Pillars stood in rows, most of them whole, some sheared off clean at head height as if a giant's hand had come down mid-gesture. Between them lay a floor of pale stone veined with silver — or what I first took for silver, until I knelt and saw the veins moving. Slowly. Like blood deciding whether to circulate.

At the court's heart rose the thing the map had been leading me toward all along: a monolith taller than Hollowmere's chapel spire, its face carved over entirely with script. Not decoration. Language. Thousands upon thousands of words pressed into stone, and every one of them still faintly, stubbornly glowing.

The covenant.

"It's beautiful," I said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"It's a wound," Tam said. "They don't tell you that in training. They show you diagrams of the covenant lattice, the flow of binding-magic between realms, and they make it sound like plumbing." He dismounted slowly, favoring the leg he'd never told me he'd hurt. "Standing here is different."

Felix's compass had gone strange since we crossed into this country. It no longer pointed north; it pointed *inward*, its needle straining toward the monolith like a dog toward a door it knows is about to open. And sometimes — more often now, each hour — it spoke.

*"—left at the split birch, Zeph, mind the—"*

A fragment. Felix's voice, worn down to gravel and warmth, saying my name like he'd known me all his life instead of one dying minute at a gate. I'd stopped flinching at it somewhere on the second day. I hadn't stopped aching.

"He loved you," Tam had said once, watching me hold the thing.

"I met him for ninety seconds."

"That's enough. Some people you meet for ninety seconds and they rearrange the furniture."

I climbed the low steps to the monolith alone. Tam kept watch at the court's edge, hand on his sword out of habit even though the only enemy for miles was memory.

Up close, the covenant's words resolved into sense — some of it. Old formal language, oath-tongue, the kind the elders used for weddings and funerals. But threaded through the formality were phrases that made my skin crawl:

*...the beasts of field and fen, given in trust...*

*...their voices held in bond, their shapes held in service...*

*...the valley kept clean, the wild kept leashed...*

Given in trust. Held in bond. I read those words and thought of every animal I'd ever spoken to — the crows who warned me, the fox who'd walked me home the winter my mother died, old Bramble the plow-horse who talked in my head like a grumbling uncle. *Held.* As if all of it, every conversation, every friendship, had been happening inside a cage whose bars I'd mistaken for sky.

And beneath the formal lines, cut deeper, cruder — gouged rather than carved — ran a single sentence in a hand I somehow knew before I knew why:

*I WILL NOT GIVE THEM.*

My grandmother's handwriting. My grandmother's fury, fossilized in stone.

* * *

It started as a sound like wind through wheat, except there was no wheat, no wind. Then light — thin, grey, honest light — gathered between two pillars across the court, and the past stepped into it.

She was tall. That was the first thing. Taller than any Whitlock I'd ever seen, with iron-grey hair braided back and a coat that had been fine once and practical twice. She stood exactly where I stood, facing the monolith, and around her the ghost-court filled: robed figures, Warden-grey, and beasts — actual beasts, stags and wolves and something vast and winged at the edges of the echo, all of them waiting.

An oath was being sworn. I watched her mouth move through words I couldn't hear, watched the monolith drink the sound, watched the silver veins pulse brighter with each clause. And then the echo reached the part where everything broke.

The grandmother-figure went still. The robed men leaned in. And she — Elowen Whitlock, though I wouldn't learn the name until later — shook her head.

Once. Slowly. The way you refuse a cup of poison.

Then she raised her hand and spoke the words that were already carved into the stone above me, the ones that had cost her everything: *I will not give them.*

The echo shattered. The pillars cracked. The light died screaming.

I came back to myself on my knees on the cold floor, cheeks wet, compass clutched so hard its casing had bitten crescents into my palm. Felix's voice was murmuring in it, soft and broken: *"—don't let them take the choice away, whatever else, don't let them—"*

"She refused," I said aloud, to nobody, to the stone. "They offered her the covenant whole and she tore it apart rather than give them the beasts."

Which meant the exile, the ward, the generations of Whitlocks raised to think themselves cursed — none of it was punishment for betrayal. It was containment. They hadn't banished us for breaking the law.

They'd banished us because we might break it again.

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet and jagged, halfway to sobbing. Somewhere behind me Tam called my name, sharp, alarmed—

—and then the light changed.

Not grey. White. Hard, disciplined, lantern-white, pouring through the eastern archway in long spears, and with it came boots on stone, many boots, moving in the cadence of men drilled until thinking was optional.

"Ambush!" Tam shouted, and then, worse, quieter, horrified: "How? There's no way they tracked us through the Ashwood—"

There was a way. There had always been a way. I understood it in one sick flash: the compass. Felix's compass, ghost-bound, whispering — and whispering, perhaps, to anyone who knew how to listen along the thread. I had been carrying a lit candle through a dark country full of people paid to hunt candles.

Corvane stepped through the arch.

He looked exactly as he had in Hollowmere — grey coat, grey eyes, a face built for forgetting — except now he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing I'd seen in weeks, because it was patient.

"Zephyrine Whitlock," he said, tasting the name. "You've been a difficult woman to follow. Your grandmother was easier. She came straight here, straight to the stone, and made her choice in an afternoon." His gaze flicked to the gouged words above me. "You've read it, I see. Good. Then you know what refusal costs."

Wardens fanned out behind him — eight, ten, twelve, crossbows levelled, spreading through the pillars like ink through water. Tam drew his sword and put himself bodily between me and the nearest line, which was absurd, brave, and completely hopeless, and I loved him for it and hated him for it in the same heartbeat.

"Stand down, Vess," Corvane said mildly. "You've been a stain on our honor long enough. Come home. Bring the girl. There's a cell with your name on it and a very reasonable hearing."

"There's no hearing," Tam said. "You burned the waystation. You burned Merric alive in it."

"Merric failed his test," Corvane said, and the mildness never cracked. "Procedures exist for a reason."

Everything in me went cold and then hot. This was the man who'd tested my neighbors. Who'd watched a boy vanish for having the wrong blood. Procedures.

"Here is what happens now," Corvane continued, addressing me directly, ignoring Tam entirely, which told me exactly how much danger Tam was in. "You will place the map and the compass on the floor. You will step back. In exchange, I give you my word — and my word has never been broken, ask anyone living — that you walk out of this court alive. The covenant dies unmended. The realms stay sundered. The beasts stay voiceless. And you go home to a valley that will finally, truly be safe."

Safe. The word rang hollow as a dead tree. Safe was a cage with the bars painted to look like sky. I'd spent twenty years in safe.

"And if I refuse?"

His smile thinned by a degree. "Then I'm afraid your defector friend discovers what procedures are for."

Tam didn't turn around. "Zeph," he said quietly. "Run for the stone. Whatever happens — run."

"No—"

"*Run.*"

I ran. It was the only order I've ever taken gladly. I heard the crossbow strings, heard Tam roar and steel ring against steel, heard Corvane's voice crack into something that wasn't mild anymore — "*Take him alive, he's worth more talking*" — and then I was at the monolith with my palms flat against ten thousand glowing words and the veins of silver-light surging up to meet me like a tide recognizing the moon.

The stone knew me. That was the horror and the miracle of it. The moment my blood-warm hands touched the covenant, the whole monolith *woke*, script blazing, and a voice that was not Felix's and not my grandmother's and older than both poured through my skull:

*A Whitlock stands at the stone. A Whitlock stands at the stone after a hundred years. Speak, daughter of the refuser. What will you give?*

Behind me: Tam shouting, cut short. The heavy sound of a body hitting ancient stone.

Cost made flesh.

I turned, and they were dragging him — bound, bleeding from the brow, conscious, his eyes finding mine with terrible steadiness — and Corvane stood over him with a crossbow bolt resting easy in its groove, pointed nowhere in particular, which was the point.

"Last chance, Miss Whitlock," he said. "The terms, or the defector pays the interest."

The stone pulsed under my hands, waiting for my answer. And I realized, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter, that I had no idea yet what I was going to say —

— only that it would not be either of the words they'd brought me here to choose between.
