## 7. The Terms of the Grandmother

The Sundered Court did not want me there. I could tell because the air itself kept trying to hand me back to the world outside, the way a cat deposits a dead bird on your doorstep — not out of malice, exactly, but with a certain firm insistence that you deal with your own problems elsewhere.

I had been inside for an hour, maybe two. Time moved strangely under the broken dome, pooling in the corners like spilled water. The great hall was a ribcage of black stone, its vaulted ceiling cracked open to a sky that seemed too close, too interested. Every pillar bore carvings worn nearly smooth by centuries of wind and grief: figures I took for people at first, until I looked closer and saw antlers, wings, tails braided through human limbs. Beasts and folk, carved together, holding up the roof between them.

Holding it up. Past tense. Half the pillars had fallen, and where they'd come down, the roof had followed, letting in rain and brambles and one extremely judgmental owl who had been watching me since I entered and clearly considered me a squatter.

"I'm not leaving," I told him.

He blinked, slowly, the way owls do when they find your argument beneath them.

Tam was gone. That was the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing, no matter how I tried to occupy my hands with map and compass and the small stubborn business of staying alive. He'd shoved me through a side arch as Corvane's grey riders poured into the court, and he'd turned to face them with his Warden's blade drawn and his face doing something complicated that I suspected he'd spent years practicing not to feel. "Run," he'd said. "Find the stone. It's why you came."

So I ran. Because that's what Whitlocks do, apparently. We run, and other people pay for it.

The compass lay warm in my palm, Felix's voice still flickering through it in fragments — *left, girl, mind the —* and then static, and then nothing, as though even a ghost needed rest. I followed it down a shattered corridor to the heart of the court, where the floor opened into a sunken circle of flagstones, and there it was.

The covenant stone.

It was smaller than I expected. Everything about the legends made it vast — a monolith, a mountain's tooth — but this was a waist-high slab of grey granite, split clean down the middle, the two halves leaning apart like a door left ajar. Words ran across its face in a script older than any alphabet I knew, and the split had torn through them, so that half-phrases hung on either side, amputated mid-syllable. Even without understanding them, I felt them. My teeth ached. The scar where the ward's edge had flayed me on the way out of Hollowmere burned like a brand remembering its shape.

And standing before it, faint as breath on cold glass, was my grandmother.

I didn't know her face. I'd never seen so much as a portrait — the Whitlocks were scrubbed from Hollowmere's records three generations back, and Grandmother Maren most thoroughly of all. But I knew her anyway, the way you know your own hands. She stood with her back to me, silver hair in a braid gone loose, wearing a coat I recognized because I was wearing its granddaughter: patched wool, valley-cut, mended at the elbows. Her feet didn't quite touch the floor.

"You're late," she said, without turning around.

Of all the things I might have said — and I had a whole arsenal loaded, believe me, everything from *how are you a ghost* to *you ruined my life* — what came out was: "You don't know that. Maybe I'm early for something else."

That got her to turn. Her eyes were the same grey-green as mine, or mine were the same as hers, whichever way inheritance runs. They went through me rather than at me, cataloguing. "Zephyrine," she said, tasting the name. "You have her chin. Edith's chin. Stubborn as a mule's opinion of fences."

"You've been watching?"

"Bound spirits watch. It's most of what we do." She drifted closer, and where she passed, the air went cold and smelled faintly of woodsmoke and juniper, which is to say, of home, which is to say, of everything I'd lost. "The rest is waiting. And regretting. There's a great deal of material."

"I buried a man two weeks ago," I said. "A courier named Felix, who died getting this map to me. A Warden named Tam is probably being tortured right now because he helped me get here. And you're going to stand there making jokes about—"

"Yes." The word dropped like a stone into a well. Her humor fell away and what was underneath was old and tired and terribly awake. "Yes, child. Sit down. This will take longer than either of us wants, and shorter than you deserve."

There was a bench along the wall, stone, cold as a grudge. I sat. The compass hummed against my knee, and from somewhere in its depths Felix murmured, *listen, girl,* soft as rain.

"The covenant," my grandmother began, "was sworn four hundred years ago, in this room, by the first King of the Valley and the Queen of the Wild Lands. You've heard the village version. Peace between realms. The ward keeps magic out; the beasts keep their distance; everyone prospers."

"That's the version they teach children."

"It's the version they teach everyone, including the Wardens, including the kings who came after. It's a lie with excellent posture." She moved to the covenant stone and laid her translucent hand above the split, not touching it. Even that nearness made the granite shiver. "Read the terms. Go on. The old script answers Whitlock blood."

I rose and approached the stone. Up close, the carved letters shifted, rearranging themselves like startled insects, and meaning surfaced in them the way a drowned thing surfaces — reluctantly, and changed by the journey.

*I read aloud, haltingly:* "'In exchange for peace within the vale... the wild folk shall render... each seventh year...'" I stopped. My mouth had gone dry. "Render what?"

"Keep reading."

"'...a tithe of their young. Chosen by lot. Delivered to the boundary stones, whence they shall pass beyond return.'" My voice cracked on the last words. "Beyond return to where?"

"Nowhere," my grandmother said simply. "Nowhere at all. The covenant didn't send them anywhere, Zeph. It unmade them. That was the price of the ward. Magic doesn't vanish because you build a wall against it — it has to go somewhere. So the oath fed it. Seven beasts every seven years, calves and cubs and foals, taken screaming from their mothers and walked to the stones and—" She stopped herself, and I watched a bound spirit, who had no breath to lose, lose it anyway.

I thought of every story I'd ever been told about the founding of Hollowmere. The blessed peace. The wise king. The ward like a mother's arm around the valley.

"They never taught us that."

"No. They taught you that your family were traitors. Oath-breakers. That we shattered the covenant out of pride or madness or greed." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like ice cracking on a pond. "Pride. Yes. Let's call it that."

"What did you do?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, the same part that had always known the ward felt less like protection than like a lid.

"In my time, the tithe came due. I was the beast-speaker then — the gift passes down the female line, skipping when it must, but it always comes home. I could hear them, Zeph. The ones chosen by lot. A fox-kit who sang to keep her courage up. An old stag's grandson who asked me if it would hurt, and I lied to him, because I was raised to lie beautifully, the way the whole valley is raised to lie beautifully." Her form flickered, guttered, steadied. "I went to the Wardens. I went to the king's council. I begged. I offered anything. And they told me the peace of thousands outweighed the lives of seven, and that I should be proud to preside over the delivery, as my mothers had before me."

"So you broke it instead."

"So I broke it instead." She turned to face the stone fully. "I couldn't destroy it — oaths that old don't die, they only change their grip. But I could wound it. I spoke a refusal into the heart of it, a Whitlock refusal, paid for with Whitlock blood, and the covenant split the way you see it now. Half-alive. Enough to keep the ward standing, but starving, and angry, and hunting the bloodline that maimed it. Your exile wasn't a sentence passed by men, child. It was the covenant itself, marking us. Every Whitlock born since has been born into debt."

"And the beasts? Did refusing save them?"

"For a while. The tithe stopped. The wild lands rejoiced — briefly. But a wounded covenant is worse than a whole one, Zeph. It leaks. Magic bleeds through the cracks in ways that shouldn't be possible — that's how Felix was wounded inside your valley, that's how the Wardens' tests can fail people who've done nothing. The realm is sick, and the sickness spreads toward the weakest parts. Toward the valley. Toward the beasts who trusted me." Her eyes closed. "I traded seven lives a generation for a slow poison in everything. I have had eighty years to decide whether that was worth it, and I still don't know, and that uncertainty is the truest hell they could have bound me into."

The owl had come down from its rafter at some point and perched on the broken stone, watching us with both eyes now, head tilted. Even the birds wanted to hear how this ended.

I wrapped my arms around myself. "Then we fix it. Reforge the covenant — new terms, no tithe, the realms at real peace. That's why the map led here. That's why Felix died carrying it."

My grandmother looked at me for a long moment, and I watched pity arrive in her face like weather.

"Oh, child. You think I brought you here to finish what I started." She shook her head slowly. "I brought you here — the map, the compass, all of it, everything I set moving before I died — because reforging is the only way to heal the wound. And reforging demands a sacrifice. It always has. The original covenant was sealed in the King's own blood and the Queen's own freedom. The stone doesn't accept metaphors."

"What kind of sacrifice?"

"A Whitlock's," she said. "Two choices, and only two, written into the terms since the beginning. Surrender the gift — let the beast-speaking be cut out of you, root and branch, so no Whitlock ever hears the wild again. Or surrender your freedom — bind yourself to the stone as its keeper, as I am bound, a spirit in this hall until the covenant fails or the world ends." She spread her hands, and the gesture encompassed the ruin, the cold, the endless watching. "Those are the terms, Zeph. Those have always been the terms. Voice or liberty. Deafness or this."

The compass had gone very still against my knee. Somewhere in it, Felix said nothing at all, and the silence was louder than any counsel.

I thought of the crows who warned me of riders. Of the fox at the valley's edge who'd walked beside me for a mile the night I fled, saying nothing, just walking, because presence is also a language. Of Tam's face as the grey riders took him. Of my mother, whose name I barely remembered being allowed to speak.

"And if I refuse both?" I heard myself ask. "If I say no to the deafness and no to the cage?"

My grandmother's expression did something I hadn't seen in it before. Hope, perhaps, arriving late and embarrassed, like a guest to a funeral.

"Then you would be doing what I could not," she said quietly. "I was given these terms and I chose the only rebellion I could imagine: breaking what I could not rewrite. I was a creature of my age, and my age believed oaths were mountains." She drifted closer, until the cold of her was the cold of my own skin. "But you — you were raised outside the walls. You learned the beasts' grammar before you learned the valley's lies. If anyone alive could look at those two terms and see a third word hiding behind them..."

She stopped. The whole court seemed to lean in — the pillars, the owl, the split stone with its amputated sentences.

"...it would be a Whitlock who refused the choice itself."

For a moment, neither of us breathed, insofar as either of us breathed at all.

Then the light at the far end of the hall changed. Not dawn. Not any natural color at all — but the flat, grey gleam of Warden-lanterns, and beneath them, boots on stone, many boots, closing fast.

Corvane had found us.
