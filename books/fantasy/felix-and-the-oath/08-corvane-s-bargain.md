## 8. Corvane's Bargain

I have hanged men for less than what I am about to do.

Not hanged, precisely — the Wardens don't hang anymore. We file. There is a form for everything: Form Seven for a tainted blood result, Form Eleven for a village that burns its own witch before we arrive (they always apologize for the mess), and Form Three, which is the one I have signed more times than any other, the one that reads *subject remanded to custody pending covenant review*, and which everyone in the order knows means *gone*.

I signed three of them in Hollowmere. I remember the names. People assume I don't. People assume a great many things about me — that I am cold, that I am cruel, that I enjoy the work. Only the last is half true, and even that half is a lie I tell myself on bad nights so that the truth doesn't get in.

The truth is this: I am very good at my job because I understand exactly what it costs, and I pay it anyway, every time, without flinching where others flinch. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic.

And tonight the arithmetic has gone wrong.

* * *

Her name is Zephyrine Whitlock. She is twenty-two years old, she speaks to animals the way you or I speak to our mothers, and she is currently sitting across a dead fire from me with a knife in her boot and murder in her eyes, both of which I find — professionally speaking — encouraging.

We are in the ruins of the Sundered Court, or near enough. The great hall collapsed centuries ago; what remains is a courtyard of black stone furred with moss, pillars like broken teeth, and the covenant stone itself rising out of the rubble at the center, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended. The whole place smells of ozone and old rain. Magic pools here in the dark like water finding its level. I can feel it against my skin, greasy and alive, and I hate it, and I hate that I can feel it at all.

She came back for the boy. Tam. My people took him six hours ago when he stepped between my crossbow and her spine, and instead of running — she had hours of lead on us, days, if she'd used her head — she circled back and walked into my camp.

"You could have been over the mountains by now," I said.

"He'd have told you nothing."

"He told us plenty." I let that sit. It wasn't true. Tam Vess had told us nothing but his name and rank, and then he'd smiled at me with blood in his teeth and said, *You're the one who lost a brother, aren't you? I heard the other Wardens talk.* Which was worse than torture, frankly, and I've administered both.

So here we are. Two prisoners of each other, sitting by a fire I permitted because she asked, and because I wanted to see whether she would ask. She did. That matters. Cruelty doesn't ask permission for warmth.

"Your grandmother," I said, "broke the covenant."

"I know what she did."

"Do you?" I leaned forward. "Because I've read the original writ, Whitlock. Not the summary they give recruits. The writ. Do you know what the covenant demanded? Every seventh year, the wild lands owed the valley a tithe of living beasts — not livestock. *Speaking* beasts. Sent through the boundary stones to serve human households as bond-servants. Chained gifts. Collars that burned. Your grandmother stood where you're going to stand, and she looked at that stone, and she chose exile for her entire bloodline rather than hand over one more creature to be farmed for its magic."

Zeph's face did something complicated. Grief and pride fighting for the same ground.

"So you admit it," she said. "The Wardens' law is built on slavery."

"I admit the old covenant was an abomination," I said. "Yes. Freely. And I will also tell you what your grandmother's mercy bought, because no one ever tells you that part. Unbound magic, Whitlock. When the oath shattered, the wards went up as a bandage, not a cure — a wall between everything with power and everything without. And walls fail. Always. Slowly, from the edges, in places like your valley, where the stones go quiet and things leak through." I watched her. "You buried a courier two weeks ago. Tell me — what killed him?"

She didn't answer. Her jaw set.

"His wounds were made by something that shouldn't exist inside your ward," I said. "Something that got in. Because the wall is failing, and because your grandmother, bless her stubborn ghost, traded a managed evil for an unmanaged one. The beasts went free. And the free things that came after — the ones with teeth and appetite and no oath at all — those are what killed Felix of the Courier's Guild in a valley that was promised safety for sixty years."

"That's not—"

"It is not fair," I agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. But fairness is not on offer tonight. Mercy is. Mine, specifically, and I suggest you hear me out before you decide how much of it you need."

* * *

Here is the thing about my brother.

His name was Aldous, and he was four years younger than me, and he drowned in a millpond that had never drowned anyone before, on a morning so ordinary I can still smell the bread our mother was baking. Except he didn't drown. That was the story we were given. What actually happened — what I pieced together over ten years and two promotions and one very long night in the Warden archive — was that the pond sat over a seam of unbound magic, a little vein of the wild that had slipped through before the wards were finished, and something in that water woke up hungry, and Aldous, who was nine and couldn't swim, went in after a duck he'd befriended.

The duck came out. He didn't.

They found him three days later, downstream, and I will spare you the details except to say that whatever held him under had held him *gently*. There wasn't a mark on him. His hands were open. Our mother never recovered, and neither, though I dressed it better, did I.

I joined the Wardens at sixteen. Everyone assumed ambition. It was grief. It has always been grief. Every wall I've raised, every taint-test I've administered, every Form Three I've signed — all of it is a nine-year-old boy standing at the edge of a millpond saying *never again, never again, never again*, in the only language I have left.

You cannot argue with a man like that. You can only make him a better offer than his dead.

Which is why, tonight, I made Zephyrine Whitlock mine.

* * *

"Here is the bargain," I said. "You give me the map and the compass. You walk away — north, over the pass, anywhere, I'll even give you escort as far as the border towns. No Form Three. No cell. You live."

"And the covenant?"

"Dies. Quietly. The Sundered Court stays ruined. The wards stay up. I reinforce them, actually — there are rites, old ones, that can seal the boundary for another century. Another hundred years of your valley sleeping safe behind its stones while the wild lands sort themselves out without human interference." I spread my hands. "No tithe. No collars. No slavery. The abomination dies with the oath. And no unbound magic crossing into human lands to drown any more nine-year-olds in millponds."

"And the beasts outside the wall?" she said quietly. "The ones Felix died trying to help? The ones whose tongues the old covenant cut out of the law entirely?"

"The ones outside the wall," I said, "are not my problem."

"No," she said. "They're just the ones who die."

"They are the ones who *always* die, Whitlock. On both sides of every wall ever built. That is what walls are *for*." I heard my own voice rise and brought it down. "You think I haven't sat where you're sitting? You think I haven't lain awake doing the sum — one world saved, one world spent — looking for a column where nobody pays? There isn't one. There has never been one. Every covenant is a choice about who bleeds, and the only question is whether you choose honestly or lie to yourself while you do it. Your grandmother lied. She called it freedom. Sixty years later a courier bleeds out in your arms, and a wren tells you the sky is full of riders, and somewhere a thing with too many joints walks through a crack in the world your grandmother's mercy made."

She was crying. She didn't wipe her face, and I respected that more than I can say.

"And if I refuse?" she said.

"Then I take the map off your body, I take Tam back to stand trial — he'll hang, boy of his age, defector, they always hang — and I do all of it wishing I didn't have to." I paused. "But you won't refuse."

"Oh?"

"Because I've watched you since Hollowmere. You count costs. You buried Felix with your own hands and you've carried him since. You came back for Tam knowing it might kill you. You are not a girl who spends lives cheaply." I leaned close, and let him into my voice, the boy at the pond, the only honest thing in me. "Give me the map. Let the wall hold. One world sleeps safe, and you — you get to keep your gift, your freedom, your friend's ghost in that compass, and your hands clean. It's the best bargain anyone will ever offer you. It's the best bargain *I* have ever offered anyone, and I want you to understand what it costs me to say that."

For a long moment she said nothing. The covenant stone hummed. Somewhere out in the dark, past the ruined gate, something answered it — a low call, animal and wrong and longing, and I saw her head turn toward it the way a compass turns, helpless, true.

Then she reached into her coat.

My hand went to my sword. Hers came out closed, and opened, and lying in her palm was not the map.

It was a single grey feather, and it was burning without being consumed, and the fire coming off it was the color of the space between stars.

"My grandmother left more than a refusal," Zephyrine Whitlock said, standing, tears still wet on her face and something underneath them that I recognized, God help me, because I'd seen it in mirrors for thirty years. "She left instructions. For whoever came next. In case the wall started failing." She looked at me with terrible gentleness. "You're right, Warden. There's no column where nobody pays. So I'm not choosing a column. I'm writing a third word."

The feather flared. The covenant stone's hum climbed to a note I felt in my teeth, in my brother's name, in the millpond water closing over my head —

and every bird in the Ashwood began, at once, to sing.
