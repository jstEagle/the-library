## 10. Unwritten

The trouble with erasing yourself from the world is that nobody warns you how much paperwork is involved.

Not for me, of course. For everyone else. The covenant stone shattered on a Tuesday — I remember it was a Tuesday because a badger told me he'd been planning to dig out his winter larder that day and now there was light pouring through a hole in the sky and honestly, who schedules an apocalypse before noon? — and in the weeks after, the world went about the business of forgetting me with the quiet efficiency of ants dismantling a beetle.

I watched it happen from the treeline above Hollowmere.

First the ledgers. I didn't see them burn, but I felt it — a small cold snap in my chest each time a page turned blank somewhere in the valley below. The parish record. The mill's debt book, which had listed my father owing eleven crowns to a man who owed him nine, a piece of arithmetic so perfectly balanced it had practically been a marriage. The Whitlock name had been struck from those pages long ago, sentenced into exile by ink; now the sentence simply finished itself. The exile ended because there was no one left to be exiled.

Then the people. That was slower, and worse.

Marta at the bakery, who had traded me day-old rolls for gossip since I was nine years old, looked straight through me when I came down for bread one last time. Not unkindly. She just asked if I was new to the valley, and offered directions to the well, and smiled the way you smile at strangers whose faces mean nothing. I said thank you. I bought the roll. It cost two coppers, which she charged me full price for, which stung more than the forgetting, frankly. Marta would have given me the roll free if she'd known me. Strangers pay retail.

My mother's grave still stood. That surprised me. I'd braced myself to find it smoothed over, nameless, one more stone among stones. But the marker read *Whitlock* in weathered letters, and no one had touched it, because no one remembered why they should. Grief without a name attached apparently just becomes landscaping. I pulled the weeds anyway. Some habits are older than identity.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being unwritten: the animals remember.

Of course they do. They never needed my name. A name is a handle humans use to pick each other up; beasts know you by weight of step, by scent, by the particular way your silence sits in a room. To the crows I had always been *the-one-who-listens*, and listening survived the rewriting intact. To the fox at the valley's edge I was *warm-hands*. To the old mare in Brambleholt's field, who had been ancient when I was a child and was now so old she'd achieved a kind of geological permanence, I was simply *hers* — though hers in what sense neither of us could have explained, and neither of us tried.

So I built a life at the margin of things. A cottage past the last fence, where the valley's tidy order gave way to bracken and birch, close enough to hear the village bell and far enough that no one wondered who lived there. The villagers decided I was some hermit's granddaughter, or a widow, or possibly a witch — villages always decide someone is a witch eventually, and I figured I might as well take the position before it was assigned to poor Marta, who had suffered enough. They left offerings sometimes. Bread, mostly. Once, inexplicably, a boot. I ate the bread. I kept the boot. You never know.

And every morning the valley woke up a little more magic than the day before.

That was the part I hadn't planned — the part I'd written into the oath with my eyes shut and my heart hammering and no idea what *kinship* would actually do once it soaked into stone and soil. What it did was seep. Magic came back to Hollowmere the way water comes back to a dried riverbed: first in trickles, then all at once. Wild roses bloomed through the boundary stones' foundations. The well water tasted faintly of starlight, which sounds lovely until you're trying to make tea. Children started reporting conversations with hedgehogs, and their parents, instead of burning them, mostly just sighed and said *well, your grandfather talked to geese, it's probably genetic.*

The ward was gone. The thing it had kept in — us, our gifts, generations of Whitlocks and half the other families too, muzzled and called safe — was loose, and alive, and learning its own shape again.

Some nights I sat on my doorstep and listened to the whole valley hum like a struck bell, and I thought: *Grandmother, you absolute terror, look what you started.* She'd refused the old covenant rather than hand over the beasts as tribute, and the price had been her family's name and freedom both. I'd refused the new terms too — gift or freedom, the stone had demanded, as if a person were a purse you could empty — and written a third word instead. Kinship. No tribute. No severing. Just the radical, ridiculous proposition that the wild lands and the valley might belong to each other.

The stone hadn't liked it. Stones rarely do. But it had shattered rather than argue, which I choose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

Tam came in autumn.

I knew before I saw him — the crows announced him three ridges out, in the flat, disapproving tone crows reserve for former Oathwardens. *Grey-cloak coming. Grey-cloak walking slow. Grey-cloak carrying something heavy.* Tam walked everywhere slowly now. He'd spent four months in Corvane's cells after covering my escape from the Sundered Court, and whatever they'd done to him down there had left his stride careful, like a man crossing ice year-round.

He stopped at my gate and looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic — the face, almost right; the voice, almost familiar; the memory, gone soft at the edges like everything else.

"You're the hermit," he said.

"I'm the hermit."

"Village says you talk to animals."

"The village says a lot of things. They also say the baker's husband is a werewolf, and he is very clearly not." I opened the gate. "You look terrible, Tam."

He blinked. "Do I know you?"

"No," I said, and found I could say it steadily, which surprised me. "But you look like a man who's walking toward something he's afraid to find. Come sit down. There's tea. It tastes like starlight. You get used to it, or you don't, and either way it's tea."

He sat. We drank. He told me the Wardens had disbanded — not reformed, disbanded, their oaths dissolving along with the covenant they'd enforced, grey cloaks folded into trunks and forgotten. He told me Corvane had vanished north, alone, and that some said he'd gone looking for the place his brother died, and that Tam hoped he found it, whatever *it* turned out to be. He told me the Sundered Court was becoming a pilgrimage site, beasts and humans both drifting there to stand in the rubble of the broken stone and feel, in his words, "like the beginning of something."

"And the map?" I asked, casual as I could manage. "The courier's map. Whatever happened to it?"

"Faded," he said. "Every line of it, the week the stone broke. Like it had finished its work." He turned his cup in his hands. "There's one thing that didn't fade."

He set it on the table between us.

Felix's compass.

It sat there small and brass-battered and utterly ordinary, except that it was glowing — softly, stubbornly, the way embers glow — and its needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at me. It had been pointing at me, I realized, since the moment Tam carried it through my gate.

"He made me promise," Tam said quietly. "At the waystation, before the fire took him — he said, *if I don't come back, find the Whitlock girl and give her this. Tell her it remembers.*" His jaw worked. "I couldn't find her. No records. No one remembered her. I searched two years, and then—" He looked up, and his eyes went wide, and I watched the compass's warmth travel up his arm and into whatever part of a man holds grief. "It's pulling," he whispered. "It's been pulling this whole time, and I thought it was broken, but it isn't. It's—"

"It remembers him," I said. "Ghost-bound. And ghosts don't keep names. They keep *people*."

The needle trembled against its glass, straining toward my hand, and when I finally closed my fingers around the brass it was warm — blood-warm, hand-warm, the exact temperature of a dying man's grip in the dusk at the boundary stones — and Felix's voice came through the way it always had, frayed and fond and slightly annoyed at me:

*Told you I'd get you home, Whitlock.*

I am not ashamed to say I cried. I am slightly ashamed to say I cried into the tea, which improved the flavor considerably.

Tam watched me hold the compass and weep, and I saw him rebuilding the story behind his eyes — the burned waystation, the seal on the map, the girl who'd run into the dark with everything he'd failed to protect. He couldn't reach my name. Nobody could. But he reached the shape of me, the way you recognize a house you grew up in even after the paint's changed.

"It was you," he said. Not a question.

"It was someone," I said, wry, wiping my face. "These days I try not to commit to specifics."

He laughed — a rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter — and outside the window the crows laughed too, because crows are terrible, and the old mare in Brambleholt's field lifted her head toward my cottage, and somewhere in the deep Ashwood a wolf I'd once nearly died speaking to raised her muzzle and sang one low note toward the valley, toward the edge, toward the woman with no name and warm hands and a compass that loved her.

Tam stayed until the candles guttered. When he rose to go, he hesitated at the door.

"Will you be here?" he asked. "If I come back?"

"I'm always here," I said. "Ask the fox. Ask the mare. Ask anything with feathers." I turned the compass over in my palm, warm as a heartbeat. "Names are for records. I'm for keeping."

He nodded, and walked off slow down the moonlit path, and I sat back down on my doorstep with Felix's ghost humming contentedly against my skin, watching the valley breathe its strange new magic under the stars.

It was a good life. Unwritten, unrecorded, unremembered by anyone with a ledger —

which is exactly when the compass needle swung sharply east, tugged by something out in the dark beyond the Sundered Court, and Felix's voice, clear and urgent for the first time in two years, said:

*Zeph. We're not finished.*
