## 2. Proclamation at the Fishmarket

The fish had been dead since dawn, but the market still smelled of the sea, of brine and tar and the sweet rot that no amount of lime could scour from the cobblestones. Vesper Eastwick moved through it the way she moved through everything: quietly, precisely, a woman carrying a ledger under one arm as though it were merely paper and not the most dangerous object in the Gilded Compass.

She had come for salt. That was the joke she allowed herself, some mornings—the sworn servant of a silent god, buying her god's own substance by the sackful from a fishwife with cracked hands. The salt was real enough. What she did with it afterward was between her and the ledgers.

The proclamation went up while she was haggling over the price of coarse grey crystals, and she felt it before she saw it: a change in the weight of the crowd. Fear has mass. It presses down on a marketplace like weather, and Vesper's gift—her curse, her secret, the thing she had spent eleven years burying beneath bookkeeping and routine—read it in her bones before her eyes found the source.

A crier stood on the steps of the tithe-house, hammering a notice to the doorframe with four nails, each strike ringing out over the stalls. The parchment was heavy stock, crown-blue wax, the seal of the Lord Inquisitor pressed deep. Vesper didn't need to read it. She read the crowd instead: the fishwife's hands going still; the rope-seller turning away too carefully, the way a man turns when he wants very much not to look; two children pulled back by their mother's grip without a word being spoken.

*By order of His Majesty,* the crier began, when the last nail was set, *all persons possessing the tainted gift—hereafter called the witch's mark—are to be reported to the Office of Inquiry. A reward of fifty crowns shall be paid for information leading to the discovery of any such person. Sheltering the gifted is treason.*

Fifty crowns. Vesper did the arithmetic without meaning to—that was the trouble with a mind trained on ledgers, it never stopped tallying. Fifty crowns was half a year's wages for a dockhand. Fifty crowns was a neighbor's silence bought cheap. Fifty crowns was every friendship in Lowharbor suddenly priced, weighed, and found purchasable.

The crowd dispersed the way water finds cracks, everyone suddenly busy, suddenly elsewhere. Vesper paid the fishwife, took her sack of salt, and let herself be ordinary a moment longer.

Then she saw Tam.

He was at the far end of the market, near the eel-stalls, doing what he always did on proclamation days—loitering where the news would land first, so he could run it back to the guild before anyone else. Fourteen years old, all elbows and quick eyes, hair the color of wet kelp. He'd been hers in the loose, unspoken way the Compass claimed its strays: she'd fed him his first honest meal, taught him his letters with a stub of charcoal on the backs of old invoices, and never once told him why she'd chosen him. He thought he'd been lucky. Vesper knew better. She'd seen him lift a dropped coin-purse off the cobbles and hand it back to its owner—a dock boy, returning money—and she had understood immediately what he was, because she'd felt the coin's weight through him, a faint bright hum like a struck bell. Tam carried luck the way other boys carried fleas. Small magics, leaking out of him constantly, unnoticed by everyone except someone who could read weight.

Someone like her. And now, apparently, someone like the men in grey who were crossing the market square with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they walked on.

There were three of them. Two were just muscle—Inquisitors' wardens, cudgels at their belts, faces bored. But the third—

Vesper had heard the name Corvane whispered in the guild's upper rooms for a month now, always softly, the way sailors say a storm's name. She hadn't expected him to look like this: a lean man in a plain dark coat, no insignia beyond a small silver pin shaped like an eye, his hands folded behind his back as though the whole world were a document he was patiently reading. He wasn't looking at the crowd. He was looking at the spaces between people, at shadows, at the places where things might hide.

And Tam, bless his reckless heart, was cutting directly across the square toward the tithe-house steps, craning up to read the proclamation, close enough to touch the witchfinder's coat.

Vesper moved. Not fast—speed draws eyes—but with the smooth inevitability of a woman who belonged exactly where she was going. She recalculated her path three times in ten strides, and arrived at the eel-stalls just as Tam turned away from the notice board and walked, oblivious, into the shadow of the Office of Inquiry's men.

"Boy." One of the wardens, the bigger one, put out an arm almost lazily. "Where you running?"

"Nowhere, sir." Tam's voice did what it always did, sliding into the flat dockside drawl that made him sound stupider than he was. "Just looking."

"Just looking." The warden smiled without warmth. "Come here, then. Let's have a look at you."

It happened fast after that. The warden caught Tam's wrist—the wrong wrist, the left one, and Vesper saw the boy flinch because his gift lived in his left hand, a warmth he couldn't always leash—and then the second warden grabbed his collar, and Tam, frightened, did the worst possible thing.

His luck flared.

Vesper felt it like heat across open coals. The big warden's boot came down on a slick of fish-guts and eel-water, and his legs shot out from under him; he crashed into the eel-barrel, which toppled, which knocked the second warden sideways, and in the tangle of cursing limbs and silver fish slapping on the cobbles, Tam tore free and ran.

He ran badly. Panic does that. He ran straight down the mouth of Fishgut Alley, which dead-ended at the rendering sheds, and Corvane—who had not moved, who had watched the entire catastrophe with the expression of a man confirming a suspicion—turned his head and followed the boy with those patient eyes.

Vesper made her choice in the space of a heartbeat, the same heartbeat in which she understood that whatever she did next, the world she had built out of invisibility was already cracking.

She stepped into Corvane's path, ledger held out like credentials.

"Inquisitor." Her voice was steady. Seven years of praying to a silent god had taught her how to sound steady into emptiness. "You'll want this man's report before you chase that boy. He owes the crown duties on forty barrels of pilchard, unpaid since midsummer. If he leaves the square, the debt walks with him."

Corvane stopped. Looked at her. Up close his eyes were pale, almost colorless, the grey of old ice, and they went from her face to the ledger in her hands with an attention that made her skin crawl, because it was the same kind of attention she gave objects—reading them, weighing them, finding the true thing underneath.

"You're a keeper of accounts," he said.

"For the Gilded Compass trading house." She opened the ledger to a page she had prepared years ago for exactly this purpose—a page of invented debts, invented names, a paper labyrinth she maintained the way other women maintained alibis. "Forty barrels, pilchard, landed the ninth of Midsummer. Duty assessed at two crowns six. Unpaid. The debtor is—" she touched a name at random, a name belonging to a man who had died two winters past and would mind nothing now, "—Harrow Beck, of Candlerow Lane."

"That's not my name," said the big warden, hauling himself upright, dripping fish-brine.

"No," Vesper agreed pleasantly. "You're not Harrow Beck. Harrow Beck is the man whose pilchards you've been confiscating without remitting duty, which is a matter I'm sure the Office of Inquiry takes seriously, since theft from the crown's revenues is rather worse than theft from anyone else."

It was beautiful, in its way. She watched the wardens' anger curdle into uncertainty, watched them realize that this quiet woman with her ledger had just handed them a problem larger than a runaway boy. Wardens who skim confiscations were hanged as readily as witches. The big one went pale under his sunburn.

Corvane, though. Corvane simply looked at her.

"The boy ran," he said. "Because you spoke to me."

"He ran because your man slipped in fish guts." She closed the ledger. "I spoke to you because you were about to assault a clerk of a chartered trading house in full view of the harbor, and the Compass is litigious."

Something moved behind the pale eyes—not belief, not yet, but interest. The dangerous kind. The kind that files things away.

"Your name," he said.

"Eastwick. Vesper Eastwick."

"And you keep the Compass's books."

"All of them."

"All of them," he repeated softly, and there it was, the hook going in, gentle as a kiss. "How long have you kept them, Mistress Eastwick?"

"Eleven years."

"Eleven years." He smiled. It was a courteous smile, a well-made smile, and it reached his eyes without warming them at all. "Then you'll know the guild's history better than anyone alive. Perhaps we'll talk again. I find that old records interest me greatly—old records, and old members." He inclined his head. "Thank you for your instruction, Mistress Eastwick. Do give my regards to your guildmaster."

He walked away, unhurried, collecting his wardens with a flick of two fingers, leaving the eels flopping and the crowd pretending it had seen nothing. Vesper stood in the fish-smell with her heart slamming against her ribs like something caged, and made herself breathe, and made herself count—four nails in the proclamation, fifty crowns, two crowns six, eleven years—counting the fear down into numbers where it couldn't reach her.

Tam found her twenty minutes later in the guild's receiving room, breathless, wild-eyed, still smelling of the alley. "They grabbed me," he said. "Ves, they *grabbed* me, and then everything went sideways, I didn't mean—"

"I know what you did." She didn't look up from the page she was pretending to study. "You flared. In daylight. In front of a witchfinder."

"I couldn't help it—"

"I know." Now she looked up, and whatever he saw in her face made him go still. "Tam. Listen to me. From today, you don't carry anything for anyone. You don't touch dice, you don't catch falling cups, you don't walk under scaffolding or near ledges or anywhere luck might want to leak out of you. You are the most unlucky boy in Lowharbor. Do you understand? You trip on flat ground. You lose at cards. You are *dull*, Tam, gloriously, magnificently dull."

"But the reward—fifty crowns—people saw—"

"People saw a boy fall down. People see what it's safe to see." She said it firmly, and hated how much of it was prayer rather than fact. "Go on. Kitchen. Eat something. Don't tell anyone."

He went. Vesper sat alone in the receiving room with the lamps unlit and the afternoon dying grey outside the windows, and finally allowed herself to feel it: cold, rising, the way seawater comes up a hold that's sprung planks. Not all at once. Inch by inch. Inevitable.

Because she had lied to Corvane smoothly, expertly, and none of it had mattered. He hadn't wanted the truth of the pilchards. He had wanted her name, her tenure, her access to eleven years of the guild's oldest records—and he had wanted it while looking at her the way she looked at objects. As if weighing her. As if he could feel, somewhere beneath the ink and the competence and the careful dullness, something that did not balance.

*Perhaps we'll talk again.*

She rose, lit a lamp against the gathering dark, and unlocked the drawer where she kept the ledgers no one else was permitted to see—the deep ones, the ones bound in grey leather older than the guildmaster's grandmother. If Corvane was going to come asking about old records and old members, she needed to know what was in them first. She needed to know what the Compass had been, before she'd trusted it with her life.

She drew out the earliest volume, blew the dust from its cover, and opened it to the first page.

The handwriting was Ash's.

* * *

Her mentor's script, unmistakable even after seven years—the sharp downstrokes, the little hooked flourishes she used to tease him about. *Ash, you write like a spider drowning.* He'd laughed and said ledgers should look like they were struggling; it kept people from reading them.

She'd expected cargo manifests. Bottled grief, lot twelve; borrowed luck, returned with interest. What she had not expected, three pages in, was a column headed in Ash's neat, drowning-spider hand:

*Deliveries — Crown — Gifted.*

And beside it, dates. Decades of dates. And beside the dates, entries written in the guild's own private cipher, which Vesper had been keeping for eleven years and could read as easily as her own name.

She read the first entry twice, because the first time her eyes refused it.

*One (1) gifted, female, age approx. 19. Delivered to Office of Inquiry, Pier Gate. Price: 200 crowns. Note: subject's talent minor (fireflies). No complications.*

No complications.

Vesper sat very still in the lamplight, listening to the building settle around her, to the muffled sounds of the family she loved moving through the floors above—Tam laughing at something in the kitchen, the smugglers arguing over dice they weren't allowed to win, Odile's heels on the stairs like a slow, patient clock.

The Compass had been selling its own people. For decades. To the men with the eye-shaped pins and the courteous smiles.

And somewhere in these pages, in this drowning-spider hand, there might be an entry with a date she knew too well—the date Ash disappeared, seven years ago, the night Salt went silent.

Her finger trembled above the next line of cipher. Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the harbor inch by inch, the way cold water fills a hull.

She began to read.
