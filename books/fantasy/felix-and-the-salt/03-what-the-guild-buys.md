## 3. What the Guild Buys

The oldest ledgers lived in the cold room beneath the counting floor, where the air tasted of iron and old tallow and the guild's history sat in rows like coffins waiting to be opened. Vesper had kept the keys for six years. She had never once needed them until tonight.

She told herself it was routine. An audit, she'd called it when she signed out the lamp and the long-handled taper from the night steward—a reconciliation of the deep accounts, nothing more. Odile had smiled at her across the supper table and said, *Of course, little wren. Mind the dust.* And Vesper had smiled back, and hated herself a little for how easily the smile came, because three days ago Corvane the witchfinder had looked at her across the Fishmarket with eyes like a man reading fine print, and she could not stop wondering what he'd seen.

So: an audit. The truth was that she had gone looking for Ash.

Not deliberately. Not at first. But grief is a kind of clerk too, and it files everything, and when Tam had come to her that morning with his split lip and his question—*Ves, who was the girl in the ledger? The one sold, back before I was born?*—something in her chest had shifted its weight, the way cargo shifts in a hold, and after that there was no pretending the ship sailed straight.

The cold room's shelves ran back two hundred years. The ledgers were bound in salt-cured hide, their spines stamped with the Compass rose, and as she carried the first of them to the worktable her gift woke without permission, the way it always did, reading the true weight of things.

Every object has a weight beyond its weight. A knife weighs steel and also every hand that gripped it. These books weighed ink and vellum and, underneath, something denser—decades of decisions pressed flat into pages. Some of them were heavy with money. Some were heavy with fear. One, near the bottom of the oldest shelf, was heavy with shame so thick it felt like lifting a drowned man.

Vesper set it down and did not open it for a long moment. Her candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were holding its breath.

*Salt,* she prayed, out of habit, out of seven years of habit. *If you're there. If you ever were.*

The god said nothing. The god had said nothing since the night Ash died, and Vesper had learned to pray into that silence the way sailors learn to sleep in storms—out of necessity, not faith.

She opened the ledger.

The early entries were ordinary enough, if ordinary meant trading in bottled storms and borrowed years. *One winter, unspent, to the widow Carrow, against her husband's name.* *Three hours of courage, decanted, to a soldier going over the wall.* The Gilded Compass bought what people couldn't keep and sold what they couldn't bear to lose, and Vesper had balanced these columns for years without once asking herself where the goods came from. Where did one buy an unspent winter? Who grew courage, and what did they have left afterward?

Her finger stopped on an entry dated forty-one years past. The hand was different—older, cramped, the ink browned.

*Received: one gifted child, age approx. 9, talent: finding lost things. Delivered to the Crown's assessors, Whitehall stairs, third bell. Consideration received: 200 crowns, plus standing arrangement.*

*Standing arrangement*, underlined twice.

Vesper read it again. Then again, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something else if she stared hard enough. They did not. The entry sat there in its browned ink, patient as a stone, and around it sat others just like it—one every few years, then one every year, then, in the last two decades, several a year, each recorded in the same dispassionate clerk's hand, each with the same chilling bookkeeping phrase: *delivered to the Crown's assessors.*

Delivered. As though the guild were a midwife. As though children were parcels.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this distantly, the way one notices rain on a window. She turned pages faster now, hunting, and the shame-weight of the book pressed up through her palms until she felt it in her teeth.

And then she found the page with Ash's handwriting.

Ash's hand she would have known anywhere—in the dark, underwater, at the end of the world. The tall confident letters, the little hook he put on his sevens, the way he crossed his t's like a man signing his name to everything he did. He had taught her arithmetic on scraps of wrapping paper. He had taught her that a ledger was a promise you made to the future. His handwriting was the first handwriting she had ever loved.

It appeared here eleven times.

*Received: one gifted woman, talent: mending broken vows. Delivered to the Crown's assessors.* — Ash's hand.

*Received: one gifted man, talent: walking unseen. Delivered.* — Ash's hand.

Eleven entries. Eleven names, some of them people whose faces Vesper still half-remembered from the guild's upper rooms, from suppers, from songs. Old Marta who could sweeten sour milk with a touch. The twins who spoke to moths. All of them *delivered*, all of them entered in Ash's beloved careful script, each column summing neatly at the bottom, because Ash had taught her that a ledger was a promise, and Ash had kept his promises, every one, right down to the crowns.

The candle guttered. Or perhaps it was Vesper who guttered; she couldn't tell anymore. She sat in the cold room with her dead mentor's handwriting under her fingers and felt the ground tilt—not the floor, which was honest flagstone, but the ground under everything, the ground she had stood on for twenty years. The guild had sheltered her. The guild had fed her, named her, given her keys and a room and a family of thieves and dreamers who sang off-key in the kitchen. And the guild had been selling its children to the witchfinders since before she was born, and Ash—the man who taught her to balance a column, the man whose ghost she half-hoped haunted her dreams—had held the pen.

Unless. Unless he'd been made to. Unless the entries were forged. Unless—

Unless the silence of her god and the neatness of these columns were the same thing, and she had been praying all these years into the mouth of the trap.

Somewhere above her, through the floors, she heard the house settling, the night steward's boots, the ordinary sounds of the only home she had. It sounded different now. Every sound sounded like a price being counted.

Vesper copied three entries onto a scrap of paper, folded it small, and hid it in the lining of her boot, where she kept the only other secret that mattered. Then she closed the ledger, and its weight settled back onto the shelf like something satisfied.

Tam knew all the ways through the Gilded Compass that weren't doors.

There was the gap behind the cistern in the washroom, wide enough for a boy built like a whip. There was the stair behind the tapestry of the drowning merchant, which the older runners used when they didn't want the night steward counting their comings and goings. And there was the dumbwaiter, which technically wasn't hiding, but nobody ever looked up, because nobody ever looked up at Tam. That was the whole trick of being small and quick and easy to forget: the world left gaps everywhere, and they were all yours.

He was in the gaps now, three days after the Fishmarket, doing the thing Odile had officially assigned him—carrying a covered dish of stewed eels to Master Quillon on the third floor—and the unofficial thing he'd assigned himself, which was listening.

Because something was wrong. Tam wasn't clever the way Vesper was clever, with her numbers and her quiet eyes, but he'd grown up on the docks where wrongness has a smell, like weather coming, and the Compass smelled wrong. The night steward had locked the cold room and then lied about it, badly. Two of Odile's enforcers who never spoke to each other had been seen speaking to each other. And Mistress Sable, who did the buying, had burned papers in her grate two nights running, and burning paper smells different from burning wood—sharper, sadder, like the ghost of whatever was written there.

Also, Vesper had gone pale as pilchard skin when he'd asked her about the girl in the ledger, and Vesper didn't go pale. Vesper was the steadiest thing in Tam's life. If the steadiest thing tilted, the whole harbor tilted.

He delivered the eels, memorized that Quillon had a visitor whose boots were city guard issue, and took the tapestry stair down, and that was where he nearly walked into trouble, because trouble was standing in the shadow of the landing talking low and fast, and one of the voices belonged to Odile.

"—the wren's been in the deep accounts," Odile was saying. Her voice was smooth as poured wax, but Tam had heard her haggle down smugglers and slavers alike, and he knew the wax had wire in it. "Let her look. A clerk who trusts nothing audits better. What matters is Corvane. Has he come back to the Fishmarket?"

"He's posted men at the tide-gate," said the other voice, one Tam didn't know. "Quiet ones. And there's a list."

"A list." A pause. "Names?"

"Being compiled. Hers will be on it, if it isn't already."

"Then we may need to accelerate certain arrangements," Odile said, and Tam heard in those five words a door closing somewhere, soft and final, the way doors close in ledgers. "The old terms. You know where to leave the token."

Footsteps. Tam flattened himself into the gap behind the tapestry rod, heart slamming, and watched the hem of a grey coat pass close enough to touch.

He stayed there a long time after the landing emptied, doing sums in his head, dock-boy sums, fast and dirty. *Old terms. Accelerate arrangements. A list.* He didn't know what any of it meant exactly, but he knew what it smelled like, and it smelled like the Fishmarket, like the day they'd taken Denny Cobbler's son away in a wagon with barred windows while everyone looked at their shoes.

It smelled like people being sold.

Tam thought about Vesper, down in the cold room or up in her narrow room with her candles and her columns, trusting the guild the way he trusted the tide to come in. He thought about the whipping he'd taken rather than give the witchfinder a single name, and how proud he'd been of the scars, and how the scars suddenly felt like receipts for something he hadn't agreed to buy.

He climbed down through the rest of the dark, quick and silent, a boy-shaped gap in the world, and went to find the only person in the Compass he was sure wasn't selling anyone.

He found her door ajar. Inside, Vesper sat at her desk with an old ledger open before her and a scrap of paper trembling in her hand, and when she looked up at him her face was the face of someone standing on ground that had just remembered it was water.

"Tamsin," she said, and her voice was steady, which was somehow worse than screaming. "Lock the door. I need to tell you what the guild buys."

Behind her, on the desk, the candle flame bent sideways—though every window in the room was shut—and for half a heartbeat the room smelled of sea-salt and old stone, as if something vast had turned over in its sleep and almost spoken.

Then it was only a candle again, and the silence of the silent god, and the two of them alone with the truth between them like a coin neither wanted to pick up.
