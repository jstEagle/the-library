## 5. Salt in the Wounds

Tam Vetch had learned to count in the fishmarket, where everything was counted and nothing was given. Count the crates before the harbor master did. Count the coins twice, because a boy who miscounted got his fingers counted instead. So he counted now, crouched in the dark beneath the Gilded Compass's counting room, listening to boots on the floor above, and the number that mattered was this: eleven witchfinders, maybe twelve, and one of him.

The ledgers were heavy against his chest. He'd wrapped them in oilcloth from the tannery scraps—Vesper's idea, though she wasn't here to know it—and bound them tight with tarred cord, and still they pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat. Nine volumes. The old ones. The ones Vesper had told him, in her flat careful voice that never quite hid how much things cost her, *if anything ever happens, these go into the dark and you go with them.*

*Anything* had happened at half past midnight, when the bell over the Fishmarket gate rang wrong—three strikes, a pause, three more. Tam had heard it from his cot in the eaves and known before he was fully awake. Everyone in Salt Row knew that pattern. It was the crown's knock.

Above him, the counting room floor groaned under boots. A voice like a drawn blade said something he couldn't make out, and then something crashed—a chair, an inkstand, the little brass scale Vesper used to weigh grief by the dram. Tam flinched at every sound as if each one struck him personally, because in a way it did. This was Vesper's room. Her ink stains on the desk edge, her coat on its hook, her god's salt dish sitting empty and patient on the sill. They were up there putting their filthy hands on all of it.

*Down,* she'd said once, tapping the floorboards between her feet. *There's a crawlspace behind the strongroom wall. Old smugglers' run, goes down to the cistern. You're small, Tam. Small is a door.*

He'd hated that, being called small. He didn't hate it now. Small was the reason nine irreplaceable books were riding against his ribs instead of burning in a witchfinder's brazier.

The trapdoor to the crawlspace was behind the wine racks in the cellar, and the cellar was full of people. Tam lay in the dark of the counting room's underfloor and listened to them herd the guild's night workers down the stairs—the laundress Marta weeping quietly, old Fenwick the keymaker swearing in three languages, the twins from the bottling room whose names Tam had never learned because they kept to themselves. Corvane's voice moved among them, smooth as oil on water.

"Names," it said. "That's all I want. Names of the gifted, and everyone walks home before dawn."

Nobody answered. Tam pressed his face against the oilcloth and breathed through his mouth.

Then Odile's voice, and the world tilted.

"Search the lower floors thoroughly." The guildmaster sounded exactly as she always sounded: unhurried, faintly bored, a woman discussing the price of saffron. "The archive especially. And the counting room. Vesper Eastwick keeps the ledgers there."

Tam stopped breathing.

She was giving them the ledgers. Not resisting—not stalling, not lying, not doing any of the hundred things Odile Vane could do in her sleep, the woman who had once talked a customs officer out of confiscating a bottled thunderstorm by convincing him it was decorative. She was handing Vesper's work to the witchfinders like a hostess pointing out the wine.

Unless. Unless it was a trick. Unless Odile was playing some deep game Tam couldn't see, feeding Corvane false volumes while the true ones sat safe against a dock boy's heart. That was possible. Everything was possible until it wasn't.

But then he heard Corvane say, "You were most helpful, Guildmaster," in a tone that held no surprise at all, and Tam understood that whatever game was being played, Odile had been playing it for longer than tonight. Helpful. As if she'd been helpful before. As if this raid had been scheduled the way raids are scheduled when someone sends word ahead.

They found the crawlspace in less than an hour. Of course they did; Odile had told them where to look. Tam heard the wine racks scrape, heard the trapdoor bang open, heard a young finder's voice call up, "Passage down to the cistern, sir!" and he lay in his coffin of floorboards and did the only arithmetic left to him: they would search the cistern, find nothing but water and rats, and conclude the ledgers were elsewhere. Because the ledgers weren't in the cistern. The ledgers were here, under the floor, under their boots, wrapped in the smell of tannery oil and a dead man's luck charm that Tam had added without asking anyone's permission.

It took them until nearly dawn to give up. He heard Marta sobbing as they released her. He heard Fenwick spit. And last, just before the front doors closed, he heard Odile say, softly, almost kindly, to someone remaining behind:

"You see what they bring down on us, keeping secrets. I do try to protect this house."

* * *

They came for Tam at midmorning, which meant they had known about him all along, which meant Vesper's name had been in somebody's mouth.

He was caught in the kitchen, of all places, trying to eat. He hadn't eaten in a day and small bodies keep their own ledgers, and hunger makes fools of everyone. Two enforcers in guild gray—guild colors, Odile's colors—took his arms before he'd swallowed the second bite of bread, and marched him down to the cellar where Corvane waited beside a rack that had probably once held hams.

"The errand boy," Corvane said. Up close, the witchfinder was unremarkable: gray-eyed, gray-gloved, neat as a ledger line. The horror of him was entirely in the hands, which never stopped moving, adjusting cuffs, straightening instruments, always writing something invisible. "Vesper Eastwick's shadow. Where does she go when she leaves the guild?"

"I stack crates," Tam said. "I sweep floors. I don't follow her."

"Everyone follows someone." Corvane lifted a small iron instrument from the table, turned it so the light caught it. "You slept in her eaves. You ran her messages. Last week you carried a parcel to the tannery district and came back lighter by one charm. What was in the parcel, Tam Vetch?"

"How do you know my name?"

Something flickered across Corvane's face that might have been pleasure. "Everyone's name is written somewhere. That is rather the whole of my profession." He set the instrument down and picked up another. "The guildmaster tells me you've been in the household two years. Taken off the docks after the proclamation trouble. Sentimental of her. Or perhaps practical—children see everything and suspect nothing." He smiled without warmth. "Where does Vesper Eastwick keep her private papers?"

"In her head," Tam said, "which is locked, and even if it weren't, you'd need a ladder."

The first blow took the breath out of him. The second taught him about the third. They used a strap weighted at the tip, and they used it the way professionals do anything—without anger, without hurry, pausing between strokes to ask again, patiently, the way rain asks a roof. Tam had been beaten before; the docks had a curriculum. But this was different. This was a conversation, and every stroke was a question, and the terrible thing was how reasonable Corvane remained throughout, how gently he said *names, boy, just names*, as if he were offering Tam a way out of a flooded cellar rather than digging the cellar deeper around him.

Somewhere past the tenth stroke Tam stopped counting, which frightened him more than the pain, because counting was the thing he was for. Instead he held onto other numbers. Nine ledgers. One crawlspace. One tannery cellar with a loose flagstone behind the lime barrels, where the oilcloth bundle now lay in the dark, cool and safe, waiting. Nine. One. One.

"Who else knows?" Corvane asked, somewhere far away. "Who helped her hide them?"

"No one," Tam said into the floor. "She doesn't trust anyone."

"Liar," said one of the enforcers, almost admiringly.

"No," Corvane said thoughtfully. "I think he's telling the truth. Which is more interesting, isn't it? A girl who trusts no one, and yet this child would bleed for her." He crouched, brought his gray eyes level with Tam's swimming ones. "What has she done to earn that, I wonder? What does Vesper Eastwick have that makes a boy lie to me in blood?" He stood. "We'll come back to it. Put him somewhere to think."

They put him in the coal cellar, which was Odile's decision—he could tell because the enforcers hesitated, glanced upstairs, and obeyed anyway. Tam lay on the black floor in a widening pool of his own blood and laughed, or tried to; it came out as a wet rattle. She'd put him in the coal cellar. The coal chute opened onto the alley behind the chandler's shop. He'd used it himself, twice, running messages Odile wasn't supposed to know about.

Either Odile had forgotten, or Odile wanted him to escape, or Odile wanted whoever followed him to lead somewhere interesting. With Odile, all three could be true at once, and that was the thing Tam finally let himself understand, lying broken in the dark: he didn't know which side of her he'd seen tonight. Maybe nobody did. Maybe that was the whole of Odile Vane—a door that opened differently depending on which way the wind blew, and damn whoever stood in the frame.

He crawled to the chute. It took him the better part of an hour, and the whole time he talked to himself in numbers, because numbers were a rope and he needed something to hold. Nine ledgers. One loose flagstone. One friend missing since midnight, out in a city gone mad, not knowing what had happened to her family.

* * *

Vesper found him at dusk in the chandler's back room, and Tam watched her face do something he had never seen it do.

She came in fast, salt-gray cloak still wet with river fog, and saw him propped against the grain sacks, and for one full second her composure simply—left. Her hand went to her mouth. Her eyes went bright and terrible. Then it was back, the ledger-keeper's mask, the still water, but Tam had seen underneath, and he would carry that second with him longer than the scars.

"Who," she said, very quietly.

"Corvane. Eleven of them, maybe twelve. They had the lower floors by midnight." He made himself sit up straighter, because what came next deserved a standing report. "Ves—they knew where to look. Every place. The crawlspace, the archive, your rooms. And Odile—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "Odile walked them through herself. Told them where you keep the ledgers. Called it *protecting the house.*"

Vesper went still in a way that was worse than shouting. He watched her take the words in, weigh them, weigh them again, looking for the error the way she looked for errors in a column of figures. There was no error. He saw her arrive at the sum.

"She sold us," Vesper said. Not a question. A balance struck.

"She sold *you.*" Tam pushed himself forward, ignoring the fire across his back. "Ves, listen—she gave them the lower floors, but she put me in the coal cellar. She left the chute open. That's not nothing. That's not—"

"That's a fisher leaving a gap in the net," Vesper said, "so the big fish swims through and shows her where the school is hiding." She knelt, took his face in both cold hands, and her thumb came away red from his split lip, and something in her jaw set like mortar. "Did you give them anything?"

"Nine ledgers," Tam whispered. "One flagstone. One you."

For a moment she closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were the color of deep water over deep rock, and Tam understood that the girl who believed invisibility was safety had died sometime in the last day, and something else had been born in her place—something with a purpose, and a list.

"They'll move on the tannery district within a day," she said. "Odile will feed Corvane the location if she hasn't already; it's the only thread left dangling. Which means we have until tomorrow's low tide to move nine books, one ghost's map, and ourselves." She rose, pulled him gently upright, and took his weight against her shoulder as if he weighed nothing, as if his blood on her cloak were a debt she'd already entered in a ledger only she could read.

"Where are we going?" Tam asked.

"Down," Vesper said. "All the way down. Ash left me a map, Tam, and the country it draws isn't lost. It's *underneath* us." At the door she paused, looking back through the fog toward the Gilded Compass, where lamplight burned warm and yellow in Odile's high window, and her voice when it came was soft as salt dissolving. "She wanted to flush out traitors. Let's show her what she caught."
