## 8. The Long Descent

The smugglers came at moonset, as promised: a broad woman with river-grey eyes named Brack, and her partner, a wiry man called Finch who smelled of lamp oil and never stopped smiling. They met Vesper and Tam in the drowned chapel beneath the Fishmarket, where the cartographer's ghost hovered over his own unfinished map like a father over a sleeping child.

"You're the ones going down," Brack said. It wasn't a question. She looked at Vesper the way sailors look at weather—measuring, unimpressed, already planning around it.

"We're the ones going down," Vesper agreed. She had Ash's marginalia copied onto her forearm in ink that would wash away with salt water, and the ghost drifting at her shoulder like a cold lantern. "How far?"

"Farther than anyone's gone and come back," Finch said cheerfully. "The archives flooded forty years ago, when they erased the country. Whole districts swallowed. The crown sealed the stairwells and pretended the water was always there." He patted the coil of rope on his shoulder. "We never pretended anything."

Tam stood close to Vesper, still favoring the whip-welts beneath his coat. He hadn't spoken since Odile's enforcers put him against the post in the guild's counting room, but he'd come anyway. That was its own kind of speech.

"The witchfinder?" Brack asked.

"Corvane knows about the door now," Vesper said. "He doesn't know the way down. Yet."

"Then we'd best be quicker than his questions." Brack shouldered a lantern whose flame burned green—the guild's storm-lanterns, fed on bottled wind. "Stay behind me. Touch nothing that hums."

* * *

Tam counted the stairs because counting kept the fear in a box. Forty-one steps down from the chapel floor, and the air changed—thickened, went sweet-rotten with old paper and older water. At sixty-three steps the lantern light found the first shelf, rising out of black water like the rib of some drowned beast, and books sat on it fanned open, their pages swollen soft as bread.

"They didn't burn them," he said, and his voice cracked on the words. He hadn't meant to speak. "They drowned them."

"Drowning is quieter than burning," the ghost said. It drifted past him, and where it passed the water went glass-still. "A country can be erased by fire and everyone remembers the fire. Drowned, and even the drowning forgets." The cartographer's face was a grey smudge above his collar, but his voice carried grief the way a bell carries a crack. "I mapped it while it still had a name. I'm the only one left who remembers what the name sounded like."

"Do you?" Tam asked. "Remember it?"

The ghost was quiet for three steps, four, five. "It sounds like rain on a warm roof," he said finally. "That's all I'll give you for free. The rest costs."

Vesper touched Tam's elbow—*keep moving*—and he kept moving. The water rose past their ankles, then their knees. Brack waded ahead without slowing, testing each step with a long iron pole, and Finch brought up the rear, humming something tuneless and cheerful that Tam suspected was a spell or a prayer or both.

At the bottom of the second stairwell the ceiling came down low enough that they had to duck, and the water reached their waists, and Tam understood with a lurch that they were walking through the tops of buildings. Doorframes passed on either side, submerged to their lintels. Once, far below his feet, he saw a chandelier, still hanging, still holding its candles, in a ballroom no one had danced in for forty years.

His mother had told him stories about the erased country. Everyone's mother had, back before telling them became dangerous—stories of terraced vineyards and bells that rang themselves, of a king who traded his shadow for a harvest. Tam had thought they were just stories. Now he waded through the proof of them, chest-deep in a nation's attic, and the stories pressed against him from every side like hands.

"Keep your mind on the water," Brack said quietly, without turning. "Water this old gets ideas."

* * *

Vesper felt the weight of the archives long before she saw the worst of them.

Her gift had always been quiet—a touch, a glance, the true heaviness of things settling into her like sediment. A coin that had changed hands in a murder weighed more than its twin. A letter written in love outweighed one written in fear, whatever the paper said. She had spent her whole life pretending not to know these things, curating her ignorance like other people curated jewels.

Down here, pretending was impossible. The water itself had weight beyond water—forty years of forgetting, pressing down. And the books, gods, the *books*. Every submerged volume pulled at her awareness, thousands of small dead weights, records of trades the guild had made and unmade and made again. She could feel which ones held true bargains and which held thefts dressed as bargains. Somewhere below, she knew without knowing how, lay the ledger with her own name in it, and her own erased years, and the weight of that knowledge dragged at her worse than the floodwater.

"There." The ghost stopped above a doorway that stood open in the middle of nowhere, its threshold underwater, its frame carved with waves and stars. Beyond it, the passage sloped down into darkness deeper than the lantern could argue with. "The cartographers' undercroft. The door lies past the deep gallery. Half a mile, perhaps more, if the galleries haven't fallen."

"And if they have?" Vesper asked.

"Then you'll find out how much of the capital is held up by habit alone."

Finch laughed. Brack didn't. They went through the doorway, and the water took them to their chests, and the dark took everything else.

* * *

Tam heard the collapse before he understood it.

It began as a sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere behind them—a wet grinding, stones shifting their minds—and then Brack shouted *"Run!"* and the water moved. Not rippled. *Moved*, a single shove of displacement rolling through the flooded gallery like the sea remembering it was the sea, and Tam was lifted off his feet and thrown forward into absolute blackness.

He couldn't tell up from down. The lantern-light was gone—or he was turned away from it, or it was buried. His shoulder struck a shelf; his hand struck nothing. Water forced itself into his mouth and he coughed and coughing under water was dying, he knew that much, everyone knew that much—

Something closed around his wrist. Iron-strong, certain. Brack? No—smaller. Slimmer.

He was hauled, dragged, and then his head broke surface into an air pocket under a vaulted ceiling, and he vomited floodwater and hung on to the arm that held him, gasping like a landed fish.

"I've got you." Vesper's voice, ragged, right against his ear. "I've got you, Tam. Breathe."

"How—" He coughed. "Lantern's gone. How did you find me?"

In the dark, he felt rather than saw her hesitate. Then, quietly: "I read the weight of the tunnel. Where it was about to fall, and where it would hold. I felt where you were, because—" a breath, shaky, "—because you weigh more to me than the rest of it. You always have. Found family has weight too. I just never let myself measure it before."

Somewhere behind them, stone settled into its new arrangement with a boom that Tam felt in his teeth. Ahead, faint and green, two lights bobbed—Brack and Finch, alive, alive, alive.

"You used it," Tam said. "Your gift. Out loud. In front of everyone."

"It was that or lose you." Her grip tightened on his wrist. "Turns out I don't hide very well when it matters."

* * *

Vesper's hands were still shaking when they regrouped on a shelf-platform above the waterline, a stretch of collapsed shelving wide enough for four bodies and a ghost. Brack re-lit the storm-lantern from a spare vial, and green light washed over all of them, making ghosts of the living.

"The gallery's blocked behind us," Brack reported. "No way back. Only way is through, and through means the deep gallery, and the deep gallery is where the water goes to think." She looked at Vesper steadily. "You read the fall coming. Before it happened."

"Yes."

"Odile will hear of it. Corvane will hear of it." Brack's river-grey eyes didn't blink. "I'm not asking if you're gifted. I'm asking why I shouldn't sell you myself, like the guild sold the others."

The question hung there. Tam pushed himself upright despite his welts, planting himself between Brack and Vesper, and Finch—for the first time since the chapel—stopped smiling.

"Because," Vesper said, "you hid those ledgers during the raid. Both of you. You took the whipping risk alongside Tam. If you wanted to sell the gifted, you'd have started with the ones who trusted you." She held Brack's gaze. "And because when we get through that door, the guild stops being a thing that sells people. I'm going to break the ledger, Brack. All of it. You can be standing beside me when it breaks, or you can be standing in the wreckage. Your choice."

Brack studied her for a long moment. Then she snorted—almost a laugh—and handed Vesper the spare vial of bottled wind. "Wreckage, probably. But beside you while it happens." She turned away. "Rest five minutes. Then the deep gallery."

* * *

Tam didn't rest. He sat at the platform's edge with his boots in the water and watched the ghost hover over the dark ahead, and after a while the cartographer drifted closer, as if he'd been waiting to be approached.

"You nearly died back there," the ghost said.

"Nearly's a word for people with options."

The smudge of the ghost's face might have been a smile. "Ash Ravensworth said something like that once. Same stubborn jaw."

Tam went still. "You knew her. Ash."

"I drew maps with her. She paid me in memories I didn't know I was spending." The ghost's voice dropped low, and the water around them went still as glass. "She loved that girl you follow. Loved her the way you love a country you've already lost once—desperately, and in advance."

Ahead, in the deep gallery, something vast shifted in the dark. Not stone. Water, moving with intention, folding around a shape that had begun, slowly, to rise.

"Five minutes are up," Brack called softly. "Whatever that is—it's between us and the door."

Tam stood, and followed Vesper into the dark.
