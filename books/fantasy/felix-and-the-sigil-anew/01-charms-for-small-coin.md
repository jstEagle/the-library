## 1. Charms for Small Coin

The luck-knots sold best on Thursdays.

Oscar had learned that in their first year behind the stall, back when they still counted the years instead of letting them blur together like rain on a window. Thursday was pay-night in Grayhollow's tannery district, and the tanners came to the night market with coin still warm from the foreman's hand and superstition still warm from their mothers' mouths. They wanted knots. Red cord, three loops, a word whispered over it that wasn't quite a word — breath shaped into something older than the city that had outlawed it.

Oscar gave them the whisper and took their copper and told themselves, every single time, that it was nothing. A party trick. A parlor habit. The kind of thing any hedge-wife in the hill country could do with her eyes closed.

It was a lie, and it was a small one, and after fifteen years a person could live inside a small lie the way a snail lives inside its shell — cramped, damp, but alive.

"Two coppers for the knot," Oscar said to the tanner currently turning one over in his cracked hands. "Three if you want it blessed against fever too."

"Blessed by who?"

"By me."

He squinted at them across the candle-lit stall. Oscar knew what he saw: a lean figure in a gray coat gone green at the cuffs, hair the color of old rope tied back with more of the same red cord they sold, a face that might have been forty or fifty depending on how the candle caught it. Unremarkable. Forgettable. That was the whole architecture of the life — built low, built plain, built to survive a storm that had already passed through and might pass again.

"You're the charm-seller everyone talks about," the tanner said. "My wife says your knots actually work."

"Your wife is generous."

"My wife is never generous. She says the knot she bought from you kept our boy out of the fever-house all winter while half his street coughed blood."

Oscar's hands went still over the cord. Somewhere down the row of stalls, a fishmonger was arguing about ice; somewhere above, the moon was climbing past the smoke-haze the way it did every night, pale and blind and pretending not to watch. *Don't,* Oscar told the warmth gathering behind their sternum. *Don't you dare wake up.*

"Cord's lucky," Oscar said. "That's all. Everyone knows red cord turns bad air." They held out their palm. "Two coppers."

He paid three, because tanners on pay-night always overpaid, and walked away wearing his luck knotted at the wrist like a promise nobody had made him.

Oscar exhaled slowly and let the warmth die back down into the cold cellar where they kept it.

Fifteen years ago there had been no stall, no cord, no careful gray coat. There had been a sword that spoke when Oscar spoke to it, and an oath sworn in starlight with six other voices braided through their own, and a war that ended badly enough that the survivors were scattered like dropped coins — some spent, some hoarded, some rolled into gutters and forgotten. Grayhollow had outlawed the speaking-craft before the war even finished cooling. Wards on every border stone, wards in the courthouse walls, wards stitched into the very flagstones of the market square, all of them tuned to one frequency: the frequency of a voice doing what voices were never meant to do.

So Oscar did not speak. Not that way. They hummed the old shapes instead, breath without words, and the magic came anyway, thin and grudging, like water seeping through a wall. Enough for luck-knots. Enough for fever-charms pressed from willow-bark and whispered over. Enough to keep the muscle of it alive without ever letting it stand up and be seen.

Survival as habit. That was all. Habit didn't need to be happy. Habit only needed to repeat.

* * *

The market thickened as the night deepened. Grayhollow's night market ran from moonrise to the third bell, filling a crooked square between the chancery's back wall and the tannery canal, and it smelled the way the whole city smelled: tallow, wet wool, frying onions, and under everything the faint chemical bite of the tan-yards. Lanterns hung on strings between the stalls, and moths orbited them in dumb devotion, and the crowd moved in its ancient patterns — laborers first, then the clerks slipping out of the chancery's side door with ink still on their fingers, then, near midnight, the ones with money and something to hide.

Oscar watched them all. Watching was the other half of the craft. You learned to read a face the way you used to read a battlefield: where the fear sat, where the hope sat, which one would pay more.

The clerks were the interesting ones. Chancery men and women, sworn to the city's ledgers, and lately — this last season, this strange dry autumn — they'd been coming to the market looking worse than the tanners. Paler. Jumpier. Buying charms they'd have sneered at a year ago. Fever-charms, mostly. One woman had bought a knot and asked, in a voice she clearly thought was casual, whether Oscar's charms worked against *debts*.

"Only the kind you can catch," Oscar had said, and she'd laughed wrong and walked away fast.

Tonight the chancery door opened early, and the clerk who came through it was young.

Oscar noticed him the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song. He was perhaps nineteen, narrow-shouldered, with the hunched carriage of someone who'd spent his childhood bending over desks built for bigger bodies. His coat was chancery gray but a cheap weave of it, clerk-class, and mended at both elbows with thread that didn't match. Ink stained his right hand up past the second knuckle — a scribe's stain, permanent, the mark of a trade that owned you body and pen. He moved through the market like a man crossing ice, quick and light and listening to something underneath.

He stopped at three other stalls first. Oscar tracked him sideways, wrapping cord around a wooden bobbin without looking at it. At the pie-seller's cart he stood too long and bought nothing. At the pawnbroker's blanket he picked up a tin whistle, turned it over, set it down with the particular care of someone saying goodbye to it. Then he came to the charm-stall, and stood in front of it, and looked at the luck-knots hanging from their pegs like little hanged men.

"Fever-charm?" Oscar asked. "Or luck?"

The young man blinked, as if he'd expected the stall to be empty. "How much is the fever one?"

Oscar lifted it off its hook: a wax-sealed twist of paper on a cord, willow-bark inside, rosemary, a breath of the old shape folded into it so fine even a ward-stone would read it as folk nonsense. "Four coppers."

Something flickered across the boy's face — the arithmetic, done and lost. Oscar had seen that arithmetic ten thousand times. It was the oldest arithmetic in the world: *what I have* against *what I need*, and the gap between them filled with whatever a person could bear to go without.

"I'll take the luck-knot," he said. "Two coppers. The plain one."

"The plain one's just cord," Oscar said. "No whisper in it. It'll keep your wrists warm and nothing else."

"It's fine." He was already digging in his coat, and Oscar watched him find first two coppers, then hesitate, then add a third, then take the third back with a wince. When he put the two coppers on the counter his hand shook. Not drink-shaking. Hunger-shaking, or fear-shaking, and beneath the fear something else, a current running under ice.

Oscar should have taken the two coppers and handed over the dead knot and let the boy walk away. That was the rule. Fifteen years of rules, each one mortared onto the last: sell small, stay small, care small. Every kindness larger than a copper was a crack in the shell, and cracks spread.

Oscar slid the fever-charm off its hook anyway and laid it beside the luck-knot.

"Market special," they said. "Knot comes with the fever-charm tonight. Don't ask why. The moon's doing something strange and I'm superstitious."

The boy stared at the extra charm. Then at Oscar. His eyes were gray-green and far too tired for nineteen, and they had the specific shine of someone who has been crying recently and in private and plans to keep it that way. "I can't—"

"You can. You already did. Two coppers buys both tonight, and tomorrow they cost normal again, so don't come back expecting charity because there isn't any. I'm a hard-hearted merchant with a pricing error."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face, there and gone like a moth past a lantern. He took both charms. He tied the knot at his wrist with clumsy, ink-stained fingers, and hung the fever-charm around his neck and tucked it under his shirt, and when it settled against his chest something in his shoulders loosened by a degree — the way a person loosens when they've been carrying a weight so long they've forgotten it's not part of their spine.

"Thank you," he said.

"What's your name? For the ledger." Oscar kept a ledger. A real one, ink and paper, columns of coppers — because a hidden life needed paperwork too, needed the costume of ordinary commerce the way a spy needed a false beard.

"Felix." He said it quickly, and then, as if hearing himself, added, "Just Felix."

"Just Felix," Oscar repeated, and wrote it, and did not look up when they felt him still watching. "You're chancery."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it like one. "Junior copyist. Third desk from the draughts window." A pause. "You know the chancery?"

"I know the building. It leans. Everyone in Grayhollow knows the chancery leans."

"It leans more than it used to." He said it quietly, almost to himself, and the current under the ice in his voice surged once, close to the surface. "Everything leans more than it used to."

Oscar looked up then. The boy — Felix — was gazing past the stall, past the market, up toward the sky over the chancery's leaning bulk, where the moon rode high and white and where, on any clear autumn night, a handful of stars should have been pricking through the smoke-haze by now. The haze was thin tonight. The sky was clean.

There were no stars.

Oscar had noticed it earlier and filed it away in the drawer where they kept things too large to think about: weather, they'd told themselves. Ash from some far fire. Fifteen years of practice at making drawers for things too large to think about, and the drawers were full, and the hinges were straining.

But now Felix was looking at that starless sky with an expression Oscar recognized from the inside — the expression of a person watching a door they'd hoped was locked swing slowly open.

"When did they go?" Oscar asked. Kept the voice flat. Merchant-flat. Curious about the weather, nothing more.

"They started fading a month ago." Felix's hand had drifted up to the fever-charm under his shirt. "One or two a night. The almanac clerks say it's atmospheric. The senior archivists stopped saying anything at all, which—" he laughed, that wrong laugh again, "—which is how we know it's bad. Archivists never stop talking. Silence in the chancery is a scream with the volume turned down."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight there are none." He turned back to Oscar, and for a moment the tiredness burned off and something younger and harder showed underneath, something that had made a decision in a back room of the chancery and was carrying it around like a hot coal in his ribs. "You feel it too. Don't you. Under the skin. Like the whole city is a struck bell and the ringing hasn't started yet."

Oscar's hands had gone still on the counter. Behind their sternum, down in the cold cellar where they kept it, the old warmth had turned over in its sleep. Not waking. Not yet. But dreaming, the way a dog dreams, paws twitching, of running.

"Superstition," Oscar said. "Take your charms, Just Felix, and get home before the third bell. The market gets ugly after the third bell."

He went. He went the way he'd come, quick and light and listening to something underneath, and at the mouth of the lane he paused — one heartbeat, no more — and looked back at the stall, at Oscar, as if checking whether a door he might someday need was still where he remembered it.

Then the crowd swallowed him, and he was gone.

* * *

Oscar closed the stall at the third bell, as always. Cord into the box, charms into the box, coppers into the purse, purse into the coat, ledger under the arm. The routine was liturgy; fifteen years had made it sacred. Hands that used to hold a speaking-sword now folded a canvas flap and latched a wooden counter, and the motions were so worn into the body that the body could do them alone, without the mind, which was good, because the mind was busy.

*Tonight there are none.*

Oscar walked home through streets gone quiet, past the canal where the tan-water moved black and slow, past the border-stone at the corner of Tanner's Row — one of hundreds sunk into the city's edges, each one carved with the ward-sign of Grayhollow, each one humming its tuneless note that only certain people could hear. Oscar heard it every day of their exile. Tonight the note was different. Thinner. Off-key. A bell with a crack in it, ringing toward its final fracture.

They stopped at the stone. Put one palm flat against the cold granite, the way you'd check a sick child's forehead.

Underneath the carving, underneath the city's law and the city's fear, something vast and patient was pulling at the ward the way a tide pulls at a sandcastle. And far above, past the smoke and the leaning chancery, the sky lay bare and black and starless, and the moon hung in it alone like the last coin in an emptied purse.

The warmth behind Oscar's sternum turned over again. This time it did not go back to sleep.

"Quiet," Oscar murmured to it, to the thing they'd been starving politely for fifteen years. "Quiet. We're done. We're finished. Whatever's coming, we're not part of it anymore."

The warmth said nothing, because it never did. But it lay there glowing like banked coals, awake now, listening, waiting — and Oscar stood in the empty street with their hand on a cracking ward-stone and understood, with the weary clarity of a person who has finally run out of drawers, that the life they had built low and plain and survivable had exactly one night left in it.

Somewhere across the city, a junior copyist named Just Felix was walking home with a stolen coal burning in his ribs and a fever-charm against his heart.

At moonrise tomorrow, Oscar would learn why.
