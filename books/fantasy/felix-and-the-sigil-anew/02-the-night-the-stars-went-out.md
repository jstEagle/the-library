## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The chancery kept its own hours, and Felix Iversen—no, not Iversen, never again Iversen, that name had been sold along with everything else—Felix Vane had learned to love them. The night shift suited a boy who did not like to be looked at. By lamplight, with his stylus scratching through columns of grain tariffs and bridge tolls, he could pretend he was only a clerk. That he was nobody's collateral. That the ledger locked in the under-croft of Grayhollow's Hall of Debts did not exist, or at least did not have his true name written in it in ink mixed with his own blood.

He was copying out a schedule of tallow duties when the first ward broke.

It did not sound like breaking. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would remember for the rest of his life however long it turned out to be. It sounded like a bell being rung underwater—a deep, mournful thrum that came up through the flagstones and into the soles of his feet and settled in his teeth. The lamp above his desk guttered though there was no draft.

Felix sat very still, stylus lifted, and listened the way small animals listen.

The second ward went a breath later, higher-pitched, almost a shriek, and somewhere across the city a dog began to howl and did not stop. Then a third, and a fourth, and then they were going in sequence, west to east along the border-stones, each one a note lower than the last, as if some vast instrument were being played down the length of the world—and Felix knew, without knowing how he knew, that the wards were not being attacked.

They were being *unmade*. From the outside in. Or perhaps from the inside out.

He stood up so fast his chair went over backward. The clatter of it was swallowed by the thrumming. Around him the other night-clerks were rising too, faces lamplit and confused, one old man clutching his abacus like a talisman.

"Stay at your desks," said Under-Chancellor Hobb from the doorway, but his voice cracked on the word *desks*, and no one stayed anywhere.

Felix ran for the window.

Grayhollow at moonrise should have been silvered—the moon fat and three days from full, the stars scattered thick over the harbor, the border-wardens' watchfires strung along the hills like a necklace of embers. Instead the sky was *wrong*. The moon hung where it should, but its light had gone thin and grayish, like skin stretched over something hollow. And the stars—

The stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone*, every one, as if a hand had reached down and wiped them from the black, leaving a sky smooth and absolute and empty. Felix's mouth went dry. He had grown up on his grandmother's stories about the star-sworn oaths, the old magic that fed on light sworn beneath the stars, and she had always said: *the stars are witnesses, child. Nothing sworn beneath them can be broken cheaply.*

Someone had just removed all the witnesses at once.

"The wards," whispered the old clerk beside him, staring out with milky eyes. "They were star-anchored. Every ward in the Marches is star-anchored. If the stars—"

The rest of the sentence was drowned by the sound of the harbor bell, which had not rung in Felix's lifetime except for drills, and which now began tolling the alarm pattern: three strokes, pause, three strokes, pause. *Wards down. Wards down.* All along the waterfront other bells took it up until the whole city was a carillon of panic.

Below in the courtyard, the chancery guards were running in formation toward the gate, and above them, on the roof of the Hall of Debts directly opposite, Felix saw something that stopped his heart.

A figure stood on the ridgeline of the roof, black against the moonless gray, and it was not standing the way people stand. Its joints bent wrong. Its head turned too far, scanning the streets below with the slow, patient sweep of a thing looking for a particular face among thousands. Even at this distance Felix could see that where its eyes should have caught the lamplight, there was nothing. No shine. No reflection. Just absence, shaped like a man.

A hollow-man. He had seen one exactly once before, seven years ago, from behind his father's shoulder, at the signing. It had stood in the corner of the under-court's delegation chamber the way furniture stands, and his father had wept while the magistrate's borrowed face smiled, and Felix had signed his name in blood-ink because the alternative was watching his family be rendered down to debt-slaves, and a soul pledged was better, the magistrate had said kindly, than a family unmade.

*Collateral,* the ledger said. *Held against the debts of the house of Vane, redeemable at the next new moon.*

The next new moon was in nine days.

And now the stars were gone, and the wards were down, and a hollow-man was standing on the roof of the Hall of Debts turning its empty head toward the chancery windows, floor by floor, like a man reading a ledger line by line.

Looking for him. Because if the Sigil Anew was waking—if the seal beneath the mountain was stirring after fifteen years of stillness—then the first order of business, the first entry in whatever terrible book the under-court kept, would be to collect what was owed.

Felix did not decide to run. His body decided for him, and his mind followed, cold and clear and faster than it had ever been. He snatched his satchel from under the desk—ink, stylus, the wax tablet he used for drafts—and then, because he was a scribe and scribes understood that paper was power, he did the one thing he had planned for years and never truly believed he would do.

He went down to the under-croft.

* * *

The Hall of Debts connected to the chancery by a covered walkway, and the walkway was empty because every guard in Grayhollow was running toward the walls. Felix moved through the dark with his heart hammering and his mind doing what it always did under terror: making lists. *Ledger of Vane debts, shelf fourteen, iron cabinet, key held by the Registrar, duplicate key cut by me two years ago and hidden in the heel of my left boot because I am a coward who plans.* The lock gave with a click that sounded obscene in the ringing dark.

Inside, the record-room breathed dust and lamp-oil. Felix found the shelf by touch, found the ledger by its red spine, and opened it on the table with hands that shook only a little.

There it was. Page 447. His name—not Felix Vane, the name he'd taken, but his true name, the one his mother had given him before the debts ate the house—written in the court's brown ink, and beneath it the seal.

He had seen the Sigil Anew's mark once, pressed in wax on the copy of his contract his father had been allowed to keep. A ring of interlocking shapes, each one a vow made visible, wound around an empty center. His grandmother had gone white when she saw it and had burned the copy and told Felix never to speak of it again. But he had memorized it anyway, because he was Felix, and memorizing things was the only weapon he had ever been given.

The seal on the ledger matched. Of course it matched. And now, in the lamplight, he noticed something he had been too young to see at seven years old: the interlocking vows were not complete. There were gaps in the ring, gaps shaped like missing pieces, and the whole design seemed to lean inward, hungry, toward that empty center.

*Starved,* he thought, with a certainty that felt like memory rather than deduction. *It looks starved.*

Somewhere above, glass shattered. Shouting. The thrumming of the dying wards had stopped, and the silence it left behind was worse—like the silence after a scream stops.

Felix tore the page free. Then, because a stolen page proves nothing and a stolen ledger proves everything, he wrapped the whole book in his cloak and slung it under his arm, heavy as a sleeping cat. He was halfway to the door when the lamp went out.

Not guttered. Extinguished, all at once, as if pinched by invisible fingers, and in the sudden dark he heard, from the top of the stairs, a sound like wet cloth unfolding. Footsteps that made no sound at all, descending.

The hollow-man had come inside.

Felix did not wait to learn whether it could see in the dark. He went out the records-room's back window, the one that overlooked the coal-yard, and dropped into the dark, and ran.

* * *

Grayhollow at night was a city he knew the way a fish knows water, and tonight the water was boiling. People poured from their houses holding candles that wouldn't stay lit—every flame in the city burned small and colorless now, starved of something no one could name. The alarm bells tolled. Wardens shouted for calm and got none. A woman knelt in the middle of Cooper's Lane screaming at the empty sky, and no one stopped to help her because everyone suddenly understood, in their bones, that the sky was not supposed to look like that.

Felix ran against the current, hood up, ledger hugged to his chest, taking the back ways—the tanner's alley, the culvert under the old granary, the stair-cut into the city wall where the watch never bothered climbing because there was nothing up there but gulls. He had one destination and it was absurd and it was the only thought his mind would hold:

*The charm-seller. The night market. The luck-knot woman.*

Because his grandmother had told him one more thing before she died, in the whisper she used for forbidden things: *if the old magic ever wakes in you or near you, find the swordspeaker. There was one in Grayhollow once. There may be one still. Look for someone selling charms too well.*

Felix had spent four years buying fever-charms and luck-knots from the quiet stall at the edge of the night market, paying in coppers, watching the scarred, gray-eyed vendor with the bandaged forearm wrap knots that worked—actually worked, he'd tested them, he was a scribe, he checked things—and telling himself it meant nothing, that some people were just good with string and superstition. Telling himself that because the alternative was hope, and hope was a debt he couldn't afford.

Now the sky was empty and a hollow-man was hunting him through his own city, and hope was all he had left, so he ran toward it.

The night market was chaos—stalls abandoned, a brazier overturned, luck-knots and fever-charms trampled into the mud. But the charm-seller's stall at the far edge was not abandoned. Its owner was there in the dark, methodically packing away spools of cord with steady hands, and when Felix skidded around the corner and collided with them—went down hard on the cobbles, ledger flying, wrist wrenched—they caught him without stumbling. Caught him one-handed, effortless, and set him back on his feet like he weighed nothing.

"Felix." Not a question. The gray eyes in the lamplight were calm in a way nothing else in the city was calm. "You're bleeding."

"It's not mine—it's the ink—" Felix's voice came out in pieces. He grabbed a fistful of the seller's sleeve. "The stars. Did you see the stars? They're *gone*, and the wards are down, and there's a hollow-man, it was on the roof of the Hall of Debts, it was in the *records room*, it knows my name—" He was shaking. He made himself stop. He was a scribe; he delivered information; he made himself deliver this like a report. "My grandmother said to find the swordspeaker. She said look for someone selling charms too well."

Something moved behind the gray eyes—something old and tired and frightened, quickly buried. The charm-seller looked down at the ledger lying open in the mud between them, at the page exposed to the light of the dying lamps: the ring of interlocking vows, the empty center, the true name written in brown ink.

When they spoke again, their voice had changed. Lower. Steadier. The voice of someone picking up a weight they had set down fifteen years ago and praying their back remembered how.

"That seal," they said. "Where else have you seen it?"

"On my contract. When I was seven. My family sold—" Felix swallowed. "Sold me. To the court beneath the mountain. Collateral against their debts. Redeemable at the new moon." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "Nine days. I stole the ledger. I don't even know why, it won't matter, it's binding, everyone says it's binding—"

"It's binding," the charm-seller agreed quietly, "because no one has ever tried to unbind it." They picked up the ledger, closed it, and handed it back to him with a care that bordered on reverence. "My name is Oscar. Get behind me, and don't let go of that book."

"Why?"

Because the air had changed. Because down the lane, at the mouth of the market, a shape was unfolding itself from between two stalls—tall, jointed wrong, head tilting past its shoulder's limit—and every candle flame in the market leaned toward it like grass in wind.

"Because," Oscar said, and drew back the bandage on their right forearm, and beneath it Felix saw lines of old script glowing awake, letter by letter, like coals breathed upon after fifteen years of ash, "it seems my oath has finally decided to wake up. And I intend to ask it some very pointed questions."

The hollow-man took its first step toward them, and the last lamp in the night market went out, and in the absolute dark Felix heard Oscar begin—to his astonishment, to his dawning, desperate hope—to *speak* in a language that made the empty sky itself seem to lean down and listen.
