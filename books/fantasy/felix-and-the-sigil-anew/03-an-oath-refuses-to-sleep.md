## 3. An Oath Refuses to Sleep

The wards fell at moonrise, and Oscar Iversen's arm woke up screaming.

Not the old ache — that they could have lived with, had lived with, fifteen years of dull throb on damp nights like a debt collector who'd learned patience. This was different. This was fire with a memory in it. Oscar dropped the luck-knots they'd been coiling for the morning market and gripped the edge of the workbench as the sword-speech came home down their forearm like a swallow to a nest it had been locked out of.

The words were already forming. That was the horror of it. Not pain first — language. Old syllables rising through the meat of their wrist, each one a blade-shape, each one hungry to be spoken. *Come up. Come up. The border is broken and you are sworn and the dark is coming in.*

"Shut up," Oscar told their own arm, through their teeth.

It didn't shut up. It had never once shut up, not really — it had only gone quiet the way a banked fire is quiet, and now someone had kicked the hearth apart. Light was leaking between their knuckles. Actual light, pale and gold-veined, the color of dawn on drawn steel, glowing out of the lines of an oath scarred into their skin when they were nineteen years old and certain that oaths were the same thing as love.

Fifteen years since they'd spoken a word of it aloud. Fifteen years of biting every syllable back down their throat until the habit wore a groove in them deeper than the scar itself. And now the wards over Grayhollow — the great border lattice, the reason the city outlawed swordspeech in the first place, the reason men like Oscar learned to sell fever-charms instead of battles — those wards had come down all at once, and everything they had ever held back was pouring through the gap, including the thing sealed inside Oscar's own body.

They made it to the window. Outside, the night market was dissolving into panic — stalls overturned, a charm-seller's lanterns swinging wild, people running not away from anything but simply *running*, because the sky above Grayhollow was wrong. The stars were gone. Not clouded. Not veiled. *Gone*, plucked out of the dark like teeth from a jaw, and the blackness left behind had a texture to it, a pressure, like something enormous leaning its face close to see what the little city would do.

Oscar's arm blazed. Down the length of the old scar, letter by letter, the oath-script was lighting itself, and beneath the skin they could feel the shape of a sword that hadn't existed for fifteen years — hilt against palm, weight against shoulder, the sweet terrible rightness of a weapon that spoke back.

"No," Oscar said again, quieter this time, the way you talk to a dog you've had to put down once already. "You're done. We're done. I paid."

But oaths don't take payment. Oaths *are* payment. They sat down hard on the stool by the cold stove and pressed their burning forearm against the iron, and the metal hissed, and the light didn't care, and somewhere out in the ruined dark of the city a boy was running toward their door with death folded under his coat.

Oscar knew that last part the way you know rain is coming — not from evidence but from the ache in old wounds.

* * *

The knock, when it came, nearly took the door off its hinges. Three blows, desperate, arrhythmic — the knocking of someone who has never in his life had to ask a stranger for shelter and is discovering he's bad at it.

Oscar opened the door on Felix Quill, chancery scribe, night-market regular, the boy who bought a luck-knot three weeks ago with coins counted out one at a time and still came up short, so that Oscar had knocked a copper off the price and pretended it was a discount for repeat customers. He stood in the doorway now with ink to his elbows, a smear of it across his cheekbone like a bruise, breathing in ragged pulls. His eyes went past Oscar's shoulder into the shop, cataloguing exits — no, not exits. *Hiding places.* A boy who'd learned to read rooms for cover before he learned to read contracts.

"Master Iversen." His voice cracked on the second word. He swallowed and tried again. "The stars. Did you see the— everyone's saying the wards—"

"I saw." Oscar stepped aside. It wasn't a decision; it happened below decision, in the place where the oath lived. "Inside. Fast."

He came in like a bird thrown into a room, all flutter and crash, and then stopped dead in the middle of the floor, staring at Oscar's arm. At the light. Because of course he was staring — the glow had spread past the cuff now, tracing the whole scarred length from wrist to elbow, and in a city where magic was contraband, a glowing arm was a confession written where anyone could read it.

"That's—" Felix began.

"None of your business."

"It's *beautiful*," Felix said, and then looked horrified at himself, and Oscar felt something twist behind their ribs that they refused to name.

"Sit down." Oscar nodded at the stool. "Tell me why the chancery's second-youngest scribe is out past curfew with ink on his hands and terror on his face, and tell me fast, because my arm is doing something I told it never to do again and I'd like to deal with one catastrophe at a time."

Felix sat. Then, slowly, watching Oscar's face the whole way, he drew something from inside his coat. A ledger. Slim, bound in gray leather gone soft with handling, and even before he opened it Oscar's stomach dropped, because they knew that binding. Every child raised in the mountain-shadow towns knew that binding, the way children know the shape of snakes.

"You stole this?"

"Borrowed," Felix said. "I was going to return it. I was going to fix it. There's a page — my family's account — and it says things it shouldn't say, and I thought if I could just get it out, get it to someone who understood contract-law, maybe there was a clause, maybe there was—" He stopped. His hands were shaking too badly to work the clasp. "I can't read the seal. Nobody in the chancery would tell me what it means. They wouldn't even look at it. Master Corvel saw it and went white and told me to put it back and forget the account existed, and Master Corvel has never in his life told anyone to forget anything, he's a *records man*, forgetting is a sin to him—"

"Felix." Oscar held out their hand. "Give it here."

* * *

The ledger opened to the marked page, and the seal at the bottom caught the light of Oscar's arm and threw it back changed — darker, denser, a small interlocking knot of lines that seemed to move when looked at directly, the way water moves when you stare at it too long.

Oscar had seen that mark twice in their life. Once at nineteen, in a hall beneath a mountain, blazing on a wall taller than any door. Once at twenty-four, in the aftermath of the worst night of their life, branded into the air where a friend had been standing and then wasn't.

The Sigil Anew. The living seal. The thing the old stories called the court's heart and the older stories, the ones nobody told anymore, called the court's *mouth*.

"Where did your family get debts like this?" Oscar asked. Their voice came out flat. Flat was a skill; they'd practiced.

"They didn't." Felix leaned forward, and his finger traced the columns without touching them, scribe's habit, reading numbers the way other people read faces. "That's the thing. My father kept accounts his whole life — honest ones, boring ones, the most boring man in Grayhollow. We owed the wool-guild two hundred crowns. Two hundred. That's a bad year, not a ruin. But then the chancery sent a reassessor, and overnight it was four thousand, and the interest was — look at the interest, it compounds *daily*, no guild charges daily interest, that's not commerce, that's—" He stopped. Looked up. "You know what this is."

"Yes."

"Then say it. Please. Everyone keeps going around it, my parents go around it, the whole chancery goes around it like it's a hole in the floor, and I have spent three weeks telling myself I'm imagining the shape of the thing I'm standing over, and tonight the stars went out and I am *done* pretending there's a floor."

Oscar closed the ledger. Kept their hand flat on the cover, feeling the faint warmth of it, the way you'd feel warmth from a sleeping animal you did not trust.

"The mark is the Sigil Anew," they said. "It's not a stamp. It's not a symbol somebody chose. It's a living seal — the oldest vow in the world, or the newest, depending which liar you ask — and it eats. Not coin. Not land. It eats *sworn souls*. Souls given in pledge. And this page—" they tapped the ledger, once, gently, the way you'd tap a coffin lid, "—is a pledge."

Felix had gone very still. Scribes could do that, Oscar remembered — go absolutely motionless when the news was too large for the room, as if stillness might keep the world from finishing its sentence.

"How much of me?" he asked. Which was the worst possible question, asked in the worst possible way, calm and precise, a boy pricing his own soul like wool.

"I don't know."

"You do. You're lying to be kind, and I'd rather you weren't. How much of me?"

Oscar looked at him. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Ink-stained. Brave in the specific exhausting way of people who insist on knowing things. And underneath all of it — visible now that Oscar let themselves see, visible the way a current is visible in deep water — a pull. A faint tidal drag, centered nowhere, aimed everywhere, tugging at the boy the way the moon tugs at the sea. Oscar had felt that drag before, on themselves, long ago, before the oath anchored them. You never forgot it. It was the feeling of being *owed*.

"Half," Oscar said. "Maybe more. The Sigil doesn't take whole — not at signing. It takes a claim, and the claim grows toward the new moon, and at the new moon it collects. Your soul isn't yours anymore, Felix. It's collateral, and the note comes due in—" they did the arithmetic against the missing stars, against the fallen wards, against the sick certainty settling into their gut, "—eleven days."

The silence after that had weight. Felix put his hand flat on his own chest, over his sternum, as if checking whether the goods were still on the shelf.

"It feels the same," he said finally, and his voice was very small. "Being half-claimed. It feels exactly the same as being whole. Isn't that obscene? I'm half sold and I can't even feel the seam."

"You will. Near the end, you'll feel it pulling. Like homesickness, but for a place you've never been." Oscar stood, and their arm flared in answer, the oath-script brightening, and they clenched their fist until the light squeezed thin between the fingers. "Don't read anything into that. The oath and your claim aren't friends. They're just both creditors circling the same wreck."

"What does it want?" Felix asked. "Your oath. What did you swear?"

"Nothing you need to hear tonight."

"Everything I need to hear is the thing nobody tells me tonight."

Oscar looked at him — at the set of his jaw, the ink on his cheek, the ledger hugged to his chest like a shield made of paper — and felt the old dread rise up and put its arms around them like an old coat. Fifteen years they had kept this shop, these charms, this small careful life, precisely so that no one would ever stand in their kitchen asking questions whose answers got people taken beneath mountains. And here was the flaw in all their careful work: you cannot hide from a war by being kind. Kindness collects strays. Strays carry ledgers. Ledgers carry seals, and seals carry you back down into the dark whether you will or no.

"There's a bed behind the curtain," Oscar said. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we find out who's left in this city worth trusting, and I warn you now, the list is short and half of it hates me."

Felix didn't move toward the curtain. He sat turning the ledger over in his hands, and then said, quietly: "Why are you helping me? You don't know me. You knocked a copper off a charm once."

Oscar's arm burned. The oath-script pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm, and Oscar thought about lying, and found they were too tired.

"Because fifteen years ago I swore an oath to protect people from that seal," they said. "And I failed. And the part of me that failed has been waiting ever since for the chance to fail better." They turned toward the stove, away from his eyes. "Bed. Curtain. Now."

Behind them, after a moment, came the rustle of a boy obeying — but pausing at the curtain, and saying, so softly Oscar was meant to half-hear it:

"The stars will come back. Won't they? Someone always brings them back. That's how the stories go."

Oscar fed a stick of kindling to the dead stove and watched the flame catch, and did not answer, because the true answer was a thing they had sworn never to say aloud again:

*Only if someone pays.*

Outside, far off toward the broken border, something in the dark gave a long, low cry — not animal, not wind, but the sound a ward makes when it dies, the sound of law ending — and Oscar's arm answered it, letter by letter, waking like a wound that refuses to close.

Eleven days. One runaway scribe. Three names Oscar had sworn never to speak again.

They reached for the chalk and began, on the underside of the workbench, to write the first one down.
