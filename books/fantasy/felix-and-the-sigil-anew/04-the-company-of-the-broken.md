## 4. The Company of the Broken

### Oscar

The stars had been gone for three days, and Grayhollow had stopped pretending it was fine.

Oscar walked the drowned streets of the Tannery Quarter with Felix a half-step behind them, the boy clutching the ledger to his chest like a shield that had already failed him once. Rain came down without conviction — thin, gray, tasting of ash from the ward-fires on the walls. The city's lamps burned low. Everyone was saving oil for a darkness no one could name.

"You said you knew people," Felix said. His voice had gone hoarse since the chancery. "People who'd help."

"I said I *knew* people." Oscar turned down a street where the cobbles gave way to mud and the mud gave way to standing water. "Whether they'll help is a different question. Fifteen years is a long time to be dead to someone."

"Are you dead to them?"

Oscar didn't answer. The truth was worse than yes: they were *unspoken*. When the oaths broke at Vell's Crossing and the company scattered like startled birds, Oscar had made a private vow never to speak their names again — because names, in their mouth, still carried weight. Even muffled, even buried under fifteen years of luck-knots and fever-charms, the swordspeech lived in them like an ember under ash, and every name was a breath on it.

They stopped before a building that had been a ward-smithy once. Now it was a tavern called the Cold Anvil, which was either honest or cruel, depending on how drunk you were.

Maren would be inside. Maren was always inside somewhere.

"Wait here," Oscar said.

"She knows me? From before?"

"She knows *of* you." Oscar pushed open the door. "That's the problem."

### Maren

Maren Ovesdotter had a system, and the system was this: drink until the hands stopped shaking, then stop drinking just long enough to remember why they shook, then start again. It had worked for fifteen years. It was working now, mostly, except that tonight her hands wouldn't stop even after the fourth cup, and the reason sat in the doorway of the Cold Anvil wearing a hood and carrying rain like a burden.

She knew the walk before she saw the face. Fifteen years, and she'd have known that walk in the dark, in the deep dark, in the dark under the mountain where she'd last seen it — shoulders set like someone walking into a wind only they could feel.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the cup, to whatever god had finally decided to notice her.

The figure crossed the room anyway. Sat down across from her anyway. Pushed back the hood anyway.

"Hello, Maren," Oscar said.

Maren looked at them. Older. Grayer at the temples. Same scar through the left eyebrow. Same eyes that always looked slightly past you, as if reading something written on the air behind your head. Her chest did something complicated and painful, and she covered it the only way she knew how.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look drunk."

"I'm *consistently* drunk. There's a difference. It's craft." She signaled the keeper for another cup, then, after a war with herself, a second one. She slid it across. "Sit. You're making the floor nervous."

Oscar sat. Didn't touch the cup. Of course not. Oscar never drank; oath-bound bodies kept their own accounts.

"The wards fell," Oscar said.

"I heard. Half the quarter heard. The other half will hear by morning." Maren turned her cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of wet it left. "And the stars went out. That part I didn't believe until I went outside and looked up like an idiot with forty other idiots."

"They're not out. They're *taken*." Oscar leaned forward, voice dropping to the register Maren remembered from war councils, the one that made stone listen. "Something's feeding on sworn light. And I think it's the same thing that took our—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. Maren set the cup down before she cracked it. "Don't say his name. Don't say any of their names. I built a life on top of that grave, Oscar. It's a shallow life and it leaks, but it holds."

"It's not holding anymore. Look at your hands."

Maren looked. They were shaking. Not the drinking-shake. The other one — the one she hadn't felt since Vell's Crossing, the tremor of a ward-smith's hands sensing a lattice under strain. Every smith in the city should be feeling it. The border wards hadn't just fallen. They'd been *unraveled*, pulled apart thread by thread by something that understood them better than their makers did.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "That lattice took nine of us two years to weave. We anchored it in starlight-sworn oaths because nothing else would hold at that scale. If someone's unwinding it—"

"Then they know exactly how we built it." Oscar's eyes finally came around and fixed on hers, and there it was, the thing she'd spent fifteen years not missing: that terrible steadiness. "Which means it's someone who was there. Someone who knew the anchor-words."

The tavern noise receded. Maren heard her own heartbeat, slow and heavy as a forge hammer.

"There were six of us who knew the full anchor-sequence," she said. "You. Me. Tallow. Brannic." She stopped.

"And the two who didn't come home," Oscar finished quietly.

The silence between them filled up with everything they'd never said: the crossing, the breaking, the way Brannic had ridden east and none of them had ridden after him because pursuing him meant admitting what he'd done, and admitting it meant the whole war had been for nothing.

"I need you," Oscar said. "Not for old times. For the lattice. There's a boy—" A pause, a small hitch Maren had never heard from Oscar before. "There's a boy whose soul got sold to the court beneath the mountain. The contract bears the Sigil's mark. New moon, and he belongs to it. And if the Sigil isn't paid willingly, it takes the whole city instead. Maybe the whole country. I've seen what its hunger does to a village, Maren. I need a ward-smith who can read a contract like a lattice."

Maren stared into her cup. Somewhere behind her eyes, fifteen years of careful, leaky, holding-together architecture groaned in its foundations.

"If I say yes," she said, "I'll want a drink for the road."

"Take two."

"That's enabling."

"That's logistics." Almost — almost — a smile. God, she'd forgotten what those cost Oscar, and what they were worth.

Maren drained her cup, stood, swayed, found her balance the way she'd once found the flaw in any ward: by trusting the shake. She grabbed her coat off the peg, and her smithing kit from under the bar where it had gathered dust for a decade, because some part of her had apparently known all along that this night would come.

"For the record," she said at the door, "I hate you."

"Noted," Oscar said. "For the record, the boy's name is Felix. He's waiting outside in the rain. Try not to frighten him."

Too late. Maren stepped out and found a scribe-thin young man pressed against the wall, clutching a ledger, looking at her the way people look at weather they've only read about. She sized him up in one pass: terrified, stubborn, running toward something instead of away, which was rarer than talent.

"Well," she said. "You must be the debt."

### Tallow

The shrine of the Unnamed was a cellar beneath a fishmonger's, which Tallow had always considered fitting: his god smelled about the same.

He was mid-liturgy — which is to say, mid-burglary, prying the lock off the offering box of a rival faith with the delicacy of a man defusing a bomb — when he felt the prayer arrive.

It came the way prayers always came to him: sideways. A pressure behind the ears. A taste like cold iron and candle smoke. He froze, pick still in the lock, and listened with the part of himself he'd kept alive through fifteen years of a banned god and a banned name and a city that would hang him twice if they could manage it.

The prayer was wordless. It was just a door held open, somewhere far off, by someone who remembered how.

"Oh," Tallow said softly, to the empty shrine, to the fish-smelling dark. "Oh, you *clever* old horror. One of them's come back."

His god did not answer in words. It never had. It answered in arrangements — in doors left unlocked, in guards called away, in coins found in coat pockets. The god of thieves and lost things, the god with no name because names could be outlawed and it refused to be outlawed. Its last priest knelt now in a stranger's cellar with stolen silver in his pockets and tears he'd deny to his grave running down his face.

Fifteen years since Vell's Crossing. Fifteen years since the company shattered and the wards fell and Tallow had watched Oscar walk into the smoke carrying a silence where their magic used to live. Fifteen years of small thefts and smaller mercies, keeping the faith the only way it could be kept: alone, in cellars, one candle at a time.

The door at the top of the stairs opened.

Tallow didn't reach for a weapon. He reached for the candle and pinched it out, because a thief-priest trusts the dark the way other men trust armor. Footsteps descended. Three sets. One heavy and uneven — Maren, drinking again. One light and quick and frightened — unknown. And one measured, deliberate, walking like the world owed it money.

"Candle," Oscar's voice said into the blackness. "We know you're there. Your god told us."

"Did it?" Tallow struck a light, and there they were: Oscar, gray and grim as a gravestone with opinions. Maren, flushed and furious and sobering against her will. And a boy — young, hollow-eyed, holding a ledger like it might hatch.

"Who's the chick?" Tallow asked.

"Felix. He's why I'm here." Oscar looked around the little shrine — the scavenged icons of a dozen outlawed faiths, the chalk circle worn smooth by knees, the offering box Tallow had very nearly finished robbing. "Tallow. The stars are gone. The wards are down. The Sigil Anew is calling in debts, and in eleven days it takes this boy unless we get under the mountain and change the terms. I need a door opened that can't be opened honestly."

Tallow let the silence stretch, because priests of his persuasion understood the value of a held breath.

"You understand what you're asking," he said at last. "My god doesn't do favors. It does *trades*. And it's starving, Oscar. Fifteen years underground, one priest, no temples, no tithes. Whatever door you want opened, the price will be paid in the only coin I have left."

"Which is?"

"Everything." He smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked and bright and concealing a wound the size of a war. "But that was always the arrangement, wasn't it? You lot break the world, and I find the way through it."

Maren snorted. The boy — Felix — looked between them with the expression of a man realizing the legends had feet of clay and hearts of iron.

Tallow blew out the candle he'd lit, took up his staff, his cloak, and his god's last candle-end wrapped in oilcloth, and climbed the stairs ahead of them.

"Lead on," he said. "The night's already stolen. Might as well spend it well."

### Brannic

Brannic Torvald saw them coming from the window of the boarding house on Candlemaker's Row, and his first instinct — his honest, cowardly, fifteen-years-practiced instinct — was to go out the back.

He had the bag half-packed before his hands betrayed him. They stopped over the sword on the bed. His sword. The one he'd drawn at Vell's Crossing on the wrong side of the field, the one he'd cleaned every day since as penance, though steel keeps no ledger and neither, he'd learned, does memory.

Through the window he watched them cross the square: Oscar first, straight-backed, terrible and familiar. Maren leaning on Tallow, arguing about something with the ferocity of the truly exhausted. And a boy. A boy with a ledger.

A boy about the age his sister had been, when the letters from the under-court started coming and Brannic had done the arithmetic of love and arrived at betrayal.

The knock came anyway. Of course it did. Oscar had never once in their life let Brannic choose the easy door.

He opened it before the second knock, because he was a coward but not a rude one.

"Hello, Oscar," he said.

"Brannic." Oscar's face did nothing at all, which from Oscar was a scream. "We're going under the mountain. Eleven days. The Sigil holds a boy's soul, and if it isn't unmade properly it takes the city. I'm telling you because you have a right to know. Not because I want anything from you."

"Liar," Brannic said gently. "You came to my door. In fifteen years you never once came to my door. You want something."

Behind Oscar, Maren's hand had gone to her belt knife with the ease of long habit. Tallow was studying the ceiling with theatrical innocence. The boy stared at Brannic with dawning recognition — he'd read the histories, clearly, the ones where Brannic Torvald's name was a synonym for treachery.

"What I want," Oscar said, each word laid down like a stone in a wall, "is irrelevant. What matters is that the anchor-sequence for the mountain's inner wards needs six voices. We are four. The dead are two. That leaves you."

"You'd take my voice into the lattice? After what I—" Brannic's throat closed. Fifteen years of rehearsing this conversation, and every rehearsal ended differently and none of them ended here, in a doorway, being *needed*.

"I'd take your sword," Oscar said. "Your voice stays your own until you've earned otherwise. That's not forgiveness, Brannic. Forgiveness isn't mine to give. Ask the dead, if they'll have you."

Brannic looked past them all, down the stairwell, out the window at the starless sky pressing down on Grayhollow like a lid. He thought of his sister, alive somewhere in the south, paying a different kind of debt. He thought of Vell's Crossing, the smoke, the sound Oscar made when the oath broke — a sound he heard most nights, in the space between sleeping and not.

He took the sword off the bed.

"I'm coming," he said. "Not for you. For the boy." He nodded at Felix, who flinched. "Someone sold him the way I sold you all. I know how that arithmetic feels from both sides. Let me stand on the right side of it once, before I die."

No one spoke. Then Maren, pushing past him into the room, sniffed at the packed bag, the cleaned sword, the boots oiled and waiting by the door.

"He's been ready for years," she said, disgusted. "Look at this. He's been *packed*."

"Fifteen years," Brannic admitted, "is a long time to keep a bag by the door."

Oscar stood in the doorway a moment longer, and Brannic braced for the old wound to open between them, wide and permanent.

Instead Oscar said, quietly, so only he could hear: "The mountain gate takes a memory from each traveler who enters. A true one. Choose yours carefully."

Then they turned and led the way down into the starless streets — a disgraced smith, a godless priest, a traitor with a packed bag, and a boy carrying a ledger full of his own soul — and Brannic followed, because the door he'd been waiting fifteen years beside had finally opened, and it led down.

At the edge of the Tannery Quarter, where the last lamps of Grayhollow guttered out against the dark, the ground began to breathe.

Cold air rose from the cracks between the cobbles, rhythmic, patient, the exhalation of something vast asleep beneath the world — waking.

The mountain had smelled them coming.
