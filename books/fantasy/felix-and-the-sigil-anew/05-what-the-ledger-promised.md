## 5. What the Ledger Promised

The safehouse was a cellar beneath a shuttered dye-works, and it smelled of old mordant and older secrets. Felix sat on a crate that had once held indigo, the stolen ledger open across his knees, and could not make himself turn the page.

Above them, Grayhollow was still screaming. He could hear it even down here — the bells rung wrong, the crowds in the streets, someone weeping two floors up with the steady rhythm of a woman who had been crying since moonrise and did not intend to stop. The stars were gone. Everyone kept saying it like a prayer said backward: *the stars are gone, the stars are gone*, as if repetition might conjure them back.

Felix knew why they'd gone. Or he suspected. And he had known for three days, ever since he'd lifted the ledger from Archivist Corvel's locked cabinet, and he had told no one, because telling made it real, and because — this was the part that shamed him most — some small, cowardly corner of him had hoped the wards would hold forever, that the new moon would never come, that the debt would simply rot uncollected like so many other debts in Grayhollow.

He looked up. Oscar sat across the cellar, cleaning a sword they clearly hadn't touched in fifteen years, whetstone moving in slow strokes that made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than sound. Maren had fallen asleep sitting up against the wall, a bottle loose in her hand, her ward-smith's tattoos crawling faintly even in sleep — little lines of light chasing each other along her forearms like ants that had lost their colony. Tallow was awake, of course. Tallow was always awake. He sat in the corner with his banned god's broken sigil in his palm, watching Felix with the patient attention of a man who heard confessions for a living and had learned to wait out silence like weather.

"You're going to wear a groove in that page," Oscar said, without looking up.

Felix's throat clicked when he swallowed. "It's not — I mean, I took it from the chancery, but it's not what you think—"

"I don't think anything," Oscar said. "That's the trick of it. I stopped thinking about people fifteen years ago. It's very restful." The whetstone paused. "But the hollow-men came through a collapsed ward straight to the chancery district, and they went to *your* desk, boy. They tore it apart looking for something. So either you stole something worth killing for, or you're worth killing for yourself, and either way I'd like to know which before whatever's hunting us finds this cellar."

Maren stirred. Her eyes opened, red-rimmed and sharp, and she set the bottle down with exaggerated care, the way sober people do when they want credit for setting it down. "Let the lad talk," she rasped.

Tallow said nothing. He just turned his palm over, showing Felix the broken sigil, as if to say: *I've given worse news than yours to better men.*

Felix looked down at the ledger. The vellum was chancery-grade, cream-white, expensive — his family had never owned paper this fine. His father had written letters on scraps of tanned hide. His mother had counted their debts on an abacus missing three beads, and Felix remembered, with a lurch, how she would tap the empty places where the beads should have been, tap-tap-tap, like knocking on doors that wouldn't open.

"My family owed money," he said. "Not to a lender. To the court beneath the mountain."

Oscar's whetstone stopped entirely.

"Everyone thought those stories were stories," Felix went on, faster now, the words spilling because if he stopped he would never start again. "The under-court, the Sigil, the souls-as-collateral — nursery tales to keep children from wandering near the mountain road. But the chancery keeps the real accounts. Every noble house in Grayhollow has a ledger there. Debts that can't be paid in coin get paid another way. And three years ago my father—" His voice cracked. He hated it. He pressed on. "My father signed something. My mother witnessed it. And the collateral was—"

He turned the page at last.

The contract filled the spread in a hand so beautiful it looked machine-made, every letter identical, every line ruled true. And beside the terms, in place of a seal, was a mark Felix had stared at until his eyes watered: interlocking rings, dozens of them, nested like the cross-section of an onion, and at the center a single point that seemed — when he wasn't looking directly at it — to be slowly rotating.

"The Sigil Anew," Oscar said quietly. Not a question.

"You know it."

"I know its mark." Oscar set the sword down. In the lantern-light their face was unreadable, but their hands, Felix noticed, had gone very still, the way hands go still when they're holding something down. "Read me the terms. All of them. Word for word."

So Felix read. His own voice sounded far away, a scribe's trained recitation voice, flat and clear, delivering his own damnation:

"*In consideration of the full discharge of all outstanding obligations of the house of Iversen-debtors-none, being four hundred marks, eleven shillings, and the forgiveness of accrued interest in perpetuity, the undersigned does hereby pledge, sell, assign, and convey unto the court beneath the mountain one soul entire, being that of the issue of the body named Felix, to be delivered into possession upon the next new moon following the failure of the border wards, whichever comes later.*"

Silence. Even the weeping woman upstairs seemed to pause.

"Four hundred marks," Maren said at last, disgusted. "They sold you for four hundred marks."

"Three hundred and eighty," Felix said. "There was interest."

"That's not what I—" Maren pinched the bridge of her nose. "Lad. That's not the point."

"It is the point," Felix said, and heard his own voice go strange and hard. "Do you know what my father's abacus sounded like? Tap-tap-tap on the empty places. Do you know how many nights I lay awake doing sums? If I copied ledgers for forty years I couldn't earn four hundred marks. They didn't sell a *soul* to me. They sold a soul instead of me. I was just the cheapest thing in the house."

"Felix." Oscar's voice was quiet. "Look at the clause again. Read the last line."

Felix read it. "*Delivery to be made willingly, or in default thereof, the court shall take satisfaction from the city of Grayhollow entire, at a rate of one soul per household until the debt is discharged.*"

The words sat there. Felix had read them a hundred times in three days and they had never once meant what they meant right now, in this cellar, with these people, with the stars gone dark overhead.

"One soul per household," Tallow repeated softly. He was looking at Felix with something that wasn't pity. Pity would have been easier. "Grayhollow has ninety thousand households."

"Ninety thousand souls," Maren whispered. Her tattoos had gone bright, agitated. "For four hundred marks. That's not a debt collection, that's a harvest."

"That's a *clause*," Oscar said. "Clauses are written by someone who wants them used. Someone drafted this so that if the boy ran — if anyone helped the boy run — the court gets paid anyway. It's not a contract. It's a snare with a soul in the middle of it." They stood, and crossed the cellar, and crouched in front of Felix so their eyes were level with his. Up close, Felix could see the burn-scar running down Oscar's sword arm, old and pale, like a river of wax. "When did you figure it out? The clause. When?"

"Three days ago."

"Three days." Something moved behind Oscar's eyes — not anger. Worse. Recognition. "You've known for three days that your soul is due at the new moon, and that if you don't walk into that mountain freely, they take the city instead. And you said nothing. You let us pull you out of that street, feed you, hide you, put ourselves between you and hollow-men — without a word."

"I was going to tell you!" It came out too loud. Felix made himself smaller. "I was going to. Tonight. I swear it."

"Don't swear," Oscar said sharply. "Never swear. Not here. Not now." They exhaled slowly, and when they spoke again their voice had leveled out into something almost gentle, which frightened Felix more than shouting. "Why tonight?"

"Because the new moon is in six days." Felix's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the ledger to stop it, and felt the beautiful, terrible handwriting under his palms. "And because I've been trying to find a way to fix it alone, and there isn't one. I've read every contract in the chancery archive. There's no loophole. There's no appeal. The sale is binding. The only variable in the whole document—" he tapped the clause, "—is willingness. That's it. That's the only thing in the world I still own. Whether I walk in or they come out."

Maren laughed, short and bitter, with no humor anywhere in it. "Six days. Wards down, stars gone, hollow-men in the streets, and the boy's soul comes due at the new moon like a rent payment." She looked at Oscar. "This is them, isn't it. This is what happened fifteen years ago. This is why you don't swear oaths."

"Maren." Oscar's voice had an edge.

"No, I want to hear him say it isn't." But she was looking at Felix, not Oscar, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Because I've watched that woman sit in that chair for fifteen years flinching every time someone says the word *promise*, and now the same seal shows up on a child's death warrant, and nobody's going to tell me it's coincidence."

Felix looked at Oscar. Oscar looked back, and for a long moment the cellar held nothing but lantern-hiss and the distant broken bells.

"It's not a coincidence," Oscar said finally. "The Sigil Anew and I have history. You'll get the whole of it when I can give it without lying, and not before. What matters now is arithmetic." They stood, and began counting off on scarred fingers. "One: the sale is binding; we don't break contracts, we renegotiate them. Two: the clause requires willing delivery, which means the court needs Felix to choose it, which means Felix has leverage, because a thing that needs your consent cannot simply take it. Three: the magistrates are bureaucrats. Bureaucrats can be stalled, confused, and out-papered. I've beaten them at that game before." A pause. "Once. Badly."

"And four?" Tallow asked.

"Four," Oscar said, "we need Maren sober, Tallow praying, and six days' ride toward the mountain, because the only place a contract like this can be contested is in front of the court that holds it." They looked around the cellar, at each of them in turn, and Felix saw — really saw, for the first time — how tired they were. Fifteen years tired. "Nobody here owes anybody anything. Say so now, and walk, and I'll stand between you and the hollow-men on the way out. That's the offer. Last one I'll make."

Nobody moved.

Tallow closed his hand around the broken sigil and smiled his terrible gentle smile. "My god's banned, my church is rubble, and my prayers haven't been answered in twenty years. I'm coming. Maybe he owes *me* one."

Maren stared at the floor for a long time. Then she picked up the bottle, walked to the drain in the corner, and poured it out — all of it, slowly, watching it spiral away — and the sound it made was the loneliest sound Felix had ever heard.

"I built half the border wards," she said. "If they're down, that's my work failing in public. I want to see what broke them." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "And I want to look at that lattice of theirs. Nobody writes a clause like that without a lattice to enforce it, and lattices," and here something kindled in her face, fierce and ruined and alive, "lattices can be *rewired*."

Oscar nodded once, and turned to Felix last.

"Well?" they said. "You've heard the terms. We're four hundred marks of trouble, minimum, and the interest is already accruing. You can still run. Alone, tonight, you might even live a year or two."

Felix looked down at the ledger. At the beautiful handwriting. At the nested rings turning slowly around their hungry center. He thought of his father tapping the empty places on the abacus. He thought of the weeping woman upstairs, crying for a sky with nothing in it. He thought: *they sold me instead of themselves, and everyone in this cellar knows it, and they're going anyway.*

Something curdled in his chest — shame and fury and grief, all boiled down to one hard grain, like a pearl around a wound.

"No," Felix said. He closed the ledger. His hands had stopped shaking. "I'm done running from a debt I didn't sign. I'm walking in that mountain myself, on my own feet, and I'm going to make them look at me while I do it. Because I've read their contract closer than anyone alive, and I found the thing they didn't want found." He stood, and the ledger felt lighter in his hand than it had in three days. "Willingness cuts both ways. If they need my consent — then I can *refuse* it. Right there, in front of their whole rotten court. Let them explain to their own law why the harvest failed."

Oscar studied him for a long moment. Then, slowly, they smiled — the first time Felix had seen it, thin and crooked and dangerous, like a blade coming halfway out of a sheath.

"Six days," Oscar said. "Then we'd better teach you how to refuse properly. In my experience, the under-court respects exactly one thing more than a signed contract."

"What's that?"

"A party who's read the whole thing." Oscar turned and pulled a dusty tarp off the back wall, revealing saddle-packs, rope, and a second sword wrapped in oilcloth. "Get some sleep, all of you. We leave through the north culvert before dawn, while the hollow-men are still hunting the chancery district." A beat. "And Felix — the ledger stays on your body at all times. If we lose that document, we lose the only proof of terms, and a court without witnesses will rewrite anything."

Felix bound the ledger to his chest with a strip of linen, tight, over his heart, where he could feel it with every breath — a second heartbeat, cold and patient, counting down.

Outside, above the shuttered dye-works, the starless sky pressed down on Grayhollow like a lid.

Six days to the new moon.
