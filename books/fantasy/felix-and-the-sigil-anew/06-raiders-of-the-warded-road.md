## 6. Raiders of the Warded Road

The wards had been dead for three days, and the road out of Grayhollow was already learning to be wild again.

Oscar felt it in their teeth before they saw it — a low, grinding wrongness, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the world. Fifteen years of living inside the city's hum, that patient lattice of oaths and iron that Maren herself had helped weave, and now it was gone. The silence where it had been was not silence at all. It was appetite.

They left by the Salt Gate an hour before dawn, five figures wrapped against the cold: Oscar in the lead with a hood pulled low; Felix behind them, clutching the ledger to his chest like a child holds a stolen loaf; Maren walking with the careful, deliberate steps of a woman who had measured her sobriety in hours and did not trust her own legs yet; Tallow humming something tuneless under his breath, his banned god riding along invisibly in the space between the notes; and Brannic at the rear, broad and silent, carrying more than his share of the packs as if weight could be a form of apology.

The road climbed north through old orchards gone feral, apple trees knotted and black against the graying sky. Frost silvered the ditches. Somewhere behind them, Grayhollow's bells were ringing the alarm for the third day running — not the orderly toll of fire or flood but a ragged, arrhythmic clanging, the sound of a city that had forgotten how its own fear worked.

"Keep your eyes on the treeline," Oscar said quietly. "Not the road. The road is honest. The trees are where things wait."

Felix's voice came thin from behind. "What kind of things?"

"The kind the court sends when it wants something back." Oscar didn't turn around. "Hollow-men."

"I've read about them." A pause. "In the chancery archives. They were listed under *enforcement instruments*. There's a whole classification system. Hollow-men, hollow-hounds, hollow—"

"Felix." Maren's voice, dry as chalk. "Nobody needs the taxonomy right now."

"It helps me," Felix said, with a small, stubborn dignity that made something ache behind Oscar's ribs. "If I can name it, I can put it in a box. Boxes are manageable."

Oscar understood that better than the boy knew. For fifteen years they had lived out of boxes — luck-knots in one drawer, fever-charms in another, the sword-speech folded up so small and buried so deep that some mornings they woke believing they had never been anything but a charm-seller with tired hands. Naming things. Boxing them. Survival as habit, habit as life.

"The short version," Oscar said, "is that hollow-men are what's left when the court takes everything a person is and leaves only the shape. No soul, no hunger, no fear. They don't tire because there's nothing in them to tire. They don't stop because stopping requires wanting something else." They glanced back. "They will come for you specifically. The ledger named you collateral. Collateral gets collected."

"And if they catch me?"

"They won't."

"You can't promise that."

"No," Oscar agreed. "But I can spend everything I have making it true, and I've decided to do that instead of promising. Promises are cheap. Spending isn't."

Ahead, the road bent through a cutting between two hills, the banks high and rooted with hawthorn. Oscar slowed. The wrongness in their teeth sharpened into something almost musical — a dissonance, a chord played badly. They raised a fist, and the company stopped.

"What is it?" Brannic rumbled.

"Company," Oscar said. "The bad kind."

* * *

They came out of the hawthorn without a sound, which was the first horror of it. Six of them, maybe seven — shapes wearing men's clothes, men's boots, faces that moved like masks worn by someone who had only ever been told about expressions secondhand. Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty, exactly. *Filed.* Smoothed over like river stones, all the grain of a person sanded away until nothing remained but the polish.

They spread across the road in perfect, terrible coordination, no signal given, none needed. Whatever will moved them came from far below the mountain, and it moved through them like water through pipes.

"Hollow-men," Tallow breathed. He had stopped humming. His hand found the little tin icon at his throat — a crooked, lopsided thing he'd whittled himself, the sigil of a god whose name could not be spoken in Grayhollow. "Six of them. Why six? They'd only need two to carry a scribe."

"They're not here to carry," Oscar said. Their heart had begun to beat slow and heavy, the way it did before violence, the way it had not done in fifteen years. "They're here to make a point. Kill the escort, take the asset. It's a message to anyone else who might think of running."

"A message," Maren repeated flatly. "To us."

"To everyone watching." Oscar drew the long knife at their hip — plain steel, honest weight, the only weapon they'd allowed themselves since the outlawry. "Get Felix behind the wall. All of you. This part is mine."

Brannic stepped forward instead. "Oscar—"

"This part," Oscar said again, and this time the words came out with an edge that had not been theirs in years, "is *mine*."

And then, because the knife would not be enough — because six hollow-men were not bandits who bled and feared and broke — Oscar reached down into the box where they had buried the sword-speech, and opened it.

It was like opening a door onto a house fire.

The magic came up their arm in a flood of remembered heat, and Oscar nearly wept at the sheer *relief* of it, the way a starving body weeps at the first taste of bread. Fifteen years of rationing themselves down to luck-knots and fever-charms, fifteen years of feeding their gift crumbs when it needed feasts — and now it roared awake, starved and furious and *grateful*, filling every corner of them like light filling a dark room.

The words rose unbidden, in the old tongue that was not a tongue but a grammar of intent, and Oscar spoke the first phrase of the sword-speech aloud:

"*Veth kalen. Veth soran.*"

Edge be true. Edge be swift.

The plain steel knife blurred. Light ran down the blade like water down glass, and the air itself seemed to lean toward it. Oscar moved.

The first hollow-man died before it finished raising its arm — the knife taking it through the throat in a line so clean the body stood swaying for a heartbeat, still wearing its borrowed face, before folding like wet paper. The second came low and fast, and Oscar turned the parry into a kill with a twist of the wrist that fifteen years had not dulled, because the magic remembered even when flesh forgot. *Veth soran,* sang the edge. *Veth soran.*

Exhilaration hit them like wine. Gods, it was *good* — the clarity, the certainty, the world narrowing to a single bright line of purpose. Every stroke was a sentence. Every kill, a completed clause. This was what they had been made for, oath-bound and oath-sworn, the last swordspeaker of a broken order, and for eleven glorious seconds Oscar Iversen was entirely, perfectly alive.

Then the price arrived.

It always arrived. That was the covenant's oldest term, written into the speech itself: *the tongue that commands the blade pays in the coin of the self.* Not blood — though blood came too, warm and sudden from Oscar's nose, dripping off their chin onto the frost. Memory. The magic took memory the way a furnace took wood, and it burned hottest whatever was nearest to hand.

The first thing to go was small: the name of the innkeeper who had sold them breakfast rolls every market-day for nine years. Gone, like a candle pinched out. Oscar barely noticed.

The second was larger. A morning in spring, twenty years ago, teaching a young ward-smith named Maren to laugh — she'd been so grave then, so certain the lattice would fail, and Oscar had juggled apples until she cracked. The memory flared white and went to ash, and Oscar staggered mid-stroke, and the third hollow-man's fist caught them across the jaw.

They went down hard. Frost bit their palms. Above them the filed-smooth face leaned close, incurious as weather, and its hand closed around Oscar's collar with the strength of a winch.

"*Veth kalen,*" Oscar gasped, and drove the knife up through the thing's chest, and the light in the blade guttered like a lamp in wind.

Three down. Three standing. And Oscar on one knee, blood sheeting from their nose, reaching frantically through their own past for something — anything — they could afford to lose. The color of the door of their first shop. The taste of the wine they'd drunk the night the order fell. Their mother's voice saying goodnight. Each one flared and vanished as they fed the speech, and each loss was a small death, a room in the house of themselves going dark forever.

*This is why you stopped,* they thought wildly, parrying a blow that would have caved their skull. *This is why you buried it. It doesn't just cost. It spends YOU.*

"OSCAR!" Felix's scream, raw with terror.

One of the remaining hollow-men had broken from the pattern — no. Had been *sent* from the pattern. It was moving past Oscar entirely, moving toward the stone wall where Felix crouched, moving with the single-minded inevitability of debt collecting itself.

Oscar did not think. Thinking was too slow, and thinking was too expensive anyway — thought required memory, and memory was currency, and they were nearly bankrupt. They simply acted, the way the oath had trained them to act fifteen years ago, the way the oath would keep training them until it had consumed them entire:

They spoke the deep phrase. The one they had sworn never to speak again outside the last battle of the old war. The one that had ended it.

"*Soran veth anna. Soran veth anna. SORAN VETH ANNA.*"

Swift be the edge, thrice-deep, edge that ends.

The knife became a line of white. Oscar crossed the distance between themselves and the hollow-man in a time that was less than time, and the blade went through it spine-to-sternum, and the hollow-man came apart like smoke given a brief and final permission to stop pretending to be solid.

The last two froze. Then, in perfect unison, they turned and walked back into the hawthorn, unhurried, unbothered, mission uncompleted but message delivered. The court did not grieve its instruments. It would simply send more.

Silence fell over the cutting, broken only by Oscar's ragged breathing and the drip of blood from their nose onto the frozen mud.

* * *

Maren reached them first. She knelt in the frost, took Oscar's face in her work-roughened hands, and looked into their eyes with the fierce, clinical attention she'd once given failing wards.

"Say your name," she ordered.

"Oscar Iversen."

"Say mine."

"Maren... Maren." The surname flickered, guttered, held. "Maren Voss. Ward-smith. You built the Grayhollow lattice. You juggle apples badly and laugh worse."

Something in her shoulders loosened half an inch. "What did it take? What's missing?"

Oscar searched inward, walking the corridors of their own past, finding doors open onto darkness. "An innkeeper's name. A spring morning. My mother's voice." They heard how flat their own tone came out, inventorying losses like stock. "Small things. Things near the surface. It burns what's closest to the flame."

"And next time?"

"Next time it burns deeper." Oscar wiped their nose, looked at the red on their knuckles, and laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "You see the arithmetic now. Every time I draw the speech, I get poorer. And the court knows it. Those last two walked away because they didn't need to win today. They only needed me to *spend*."

Tallow crouched nearby, turning his tin icon over in his fingers. "That's why the Sigil hungers, isn't it? Same economy. It eats what people are. Your speech is just... a polite version. A payment plan."

"Thank you for that, priest," Oscar said. "Very comforting."

Felix came last. He stood over them, ledger clutched to his chest, and his face was doing something complicated — guilt and awe and a horror that kept trying to become tears and being refused.

"That was for me," he said. "Those memories. You paid them for me, and I'm not even—" His voice cracked. "I'm collateral. That's all I am. A line item. And you're spending *yourself* on a line item."

Oscar got slowly to their feet. Everything hurt. Whole rooms of their inner house were dark, and would stay dark, and they could feel the edges of deeper rooms waiting, doors standing open on blackness, ready for the next withdrawal.

"Come here," they said.

Felix came. Oscar took him by the shoulders, and their hands were steady, and their voice was quieter than the frost.

"Listen to me. In the old war, I watched better swordspeakers than me burn themselves down to embers paying the speech's price, and do you know what they bought with it? Time. Minutes. Other people's tomorrows." They squeezed. "There is no version of the arithmetic where that's waste. Not one. So you'll not stand there and tell me what you're worth. The court priced you in souls. I price you differently."

"How?"

"As family," Oscar said. "Family is the one debt the Sigil can't collect."

Behind them, Brannic cleared his throat — a rough, awkward sound, the sound of a man assembling courage out of spare parts. "The hawthorn," he said. "They'll report back. There'll be more next time, and heavier. We should move, and we should move off the road. There's a village maybe half a day east, through the orchards. Old place. Off the ledgers."

"Off the ledgers," Maren murmured. "After today, that sounds like paradise."

They gathered the packs. Tallow took point, humming his tuneless hymn again, softer now. Felix fell into step beside Oscar, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and after a while he said, very quietly:

"The deep phrase. The one you swore never to use. What did it cost you? Really."

Oscar walked a few paces before answering, because the answer required walking through the dark rooms first, counting what was gone.

"There was a song," they said at last. "I don't know whose. I don't know the words anymore. But I know I loved it, and I know someone used to sing it to me, and I know that whoever they were, they're still in my heart somewhere, knocking on a door I can't find." They looked east, toward the nameless village and the mountain beyond it. "That's what it cost. Ask me again when we're closer to the mountain, and the answer will be worse."

Felix said nothing. But his hand found Oscar's sleeve and held it, all the way through the orchards, and neither of them let go.

Behind them, far to the south, the bells of Grayhollow rang on — and beneath them, fainter than hearing, something beneath the mountain began, very patiently, to count.
