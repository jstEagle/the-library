## 7. The Village That Forgot Its Name

Tallow had stolen from gods before. It was, in a manner of speaking, his profession.

The god he served — the Unnamed, the Beggar at the Door, the One the Chancery Burned Out of Every Book It Could Reach — had never asked him to stop. That was the joke Tallow carried like a stone behind his ribs: he was a thief-priest of a god who owned nothing, and so there was nothing to steal from Him but faith itself, and Tallow had been spending that coin down for thirty years without ever seeing it replenished.

Three days out of Grayhollow, on a road that wound through hills the maps had stopped bothering to name, he felt the last of it go.

Not all at once. Faith didn't leave you the way a purse leaves your pocket. It left the way warmth leaves a body — slowly, then all at once, and then you were standing in a cold field wondering when exactly you'd died.

* * *

They came to the village at dusk, because Oscar insisted they travel by dusk and dawn and sleep through the bright hours, when the hollow-men could see farther than any living eye. The place sat in a fold of the hills like something set down and forgotten: a mill with no wheel turning, a well with no rope, chimneys smoking thin gray threads into air that smelled of nothing at all.

That was the first wrongness. Tallow noticed it before anyone else, because noticing nothing was his trade too. The village smelled of bread baking and woodsmoke and rain-wet stone, and beneath all of it — nothing. No incense. No shrine-oil. No old blood soaked into a threshold stone. Every village he'd ever robbed had a smell underneath its smells, the smell of people reaching for something above themselves. This one had been scrubbed clean all the way down to the dirt.

"Names," Maren said quietly, as they passed the first cottages. She'd been sober four days now, and her voice had the raw, scraped quality of someone using it for the first time in years. "Look at the doors."

Tallow looked. Above every lintel, where country folk hung horseshoes or carved blessings, each door bore a small blank plaque. Not worn blank — *made* blank, sanded smooth, painted over, some of them still showing the ghost of letters beneath the whitewash like bones under skin.

"They scraped them off," Felix said. He'd gone pale, which for Felix took some doing; he'd been pale since the ledger. "Every one of them."

"Not just the doors," Brannic rumbled. He nodded toward the well, the millstone, the signpost at the crossroads where five directions should have been written and only five smooth blanks remained. "Everything with a name on it. They've been eating their own words."

Oscar said nothing. Oscar had said very little since the road began, and when they did speak it was in that flat, careful tone of someone holding a lit fuse very still. But their hand had gone to the sword at their hip — not gripping it, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on an old dog that growls in its sleep.

A woman came out of the nearest cottage before they could decide whether to knock. She was old, bent like a question mark, with a shawl the color of ash and eyes that were the worst thing Tallow had seen in three days of walking through a world gone wrong: eyes that were perfectly, terribly kind, and perfectly, terribly empty behind the kindness.

"Oh," she said, and her whole face changed. "Oh, *there* you are."

She crossed the yard at a speed her body should have forbidden and took Felix by both hands, and Felix — poor terrified Felix, who flinched from his own shadow these days — let her, because there was nowhere to flinch to.

"Tomas," she said. "Tomas, love, you've come home. I told them. I told every one of them you'd come home when the stars were ready, and now look—" she pointed up, at the black sky where stars should have been, "—look, they're gone, just like I said, and here you are. Come in, come in, I've kept your room. I've kept everything. I knew you'd forget the way but I never doubted you'd remember the wanting."

Felix looked at Oscar over the woman's bowed head. His mouth worked. Nothing came out.

"He's not—" Brannic started.

"Yes," Oscar said softly. "He is. Tonight he is." And there was something in Oscar's voice that Tallow had heard only twice in fifteen years, both times over graves. "Ma'am," Oscar said to the woman, gently, "we're hungry and we've got coin. Will you feed us?"

"Feed Tomas's friends?" She beamed at them all, one after another, and Tallow felt the beam land on him like a physical weight. "Any friend of my son's. Any friend at all. Come in, come in, mind the step, I always say mind the step and nobody ever minds the step."

* * *

The cottage was warm and full of food and utterly, catastrophically wrong.

There were six places set at the table. There were six chairs. On the wall hung six wooden cups, each carved with a different animal — a fox, an owl, a bear, a hare, a wren, a wolf — and when Tallow traced his thumb over the wolf cup's ears, the wood was worn smooth as river stone, polished by years of a child's hands.

But ask the woman her name, and she smiled and poured more stew. Ask her son's name — the real one, the dead or taken one — and she smiled and said, *Tomas, of course, Tomas.* Ask her what year it was, what season, what had happened to the stars, and she smiled and smiled and smiled, and behind the smile was a hole shaped exactly like everything she had ever known, scooped out clean.

"It's the Sigil," Maren whispered, when the woman went to fetch more bread. Her hands were shaking around her cup. "What it feeds on. Sworn souls, starlight oaths — but it's starving now, the wards are down, the stars are out, and a starving thing isn't picky. It's been feeding here. For years, maybe. On names. On memories. Whatever it could reach through the ground."

"The whole village?" Felix's voice cracked. "All of them?"

"You saw the doors. They did it to themselves, at the end. Scrubbed their own names off rather than let it be taken." Maren stared into her cup. "That's not hunger winning. That's people choosing what to lose."

Tallow said nothing. Tallow was looking at the shelf above the hearth, where a dozen little clay figures stood in a row — lumpy, child-made, a shepherd and a soldier and a woman with her arms thrown wide. And beneath them, on a strip of leather gone brown with age, a prayer knot. His own craft, or near enough. A beggar's knot, tied in the style of the Unnamed, three loops for the three questions the god asks every soul at the end of the world: *Who fed you? Who forgave you? Who remembered you?*

Someone in this house had prayed to his god. Recently. The knot was fresh-tied.

His god who had not answered a prayer in thirty years. His god whose last temples were bonfires, whose last priests were Tallow and two corpses and a man in Vhal who'd gone respectable. Tallow had kept the faith the way you keep a lamp in a storm — cupped, guttering, mostly out of stubbornness — and he had told himself for decades that the silence was doctrine, that the Unnamed spoke only in the space between things, that a thief-priest should expect theft even from heaven.

Standing in this scooped-out house, looking at that knot, Tallow felt something he hadn't felt since he was a boy: anger at his god. Clean, hot, human anger.

*You let this happen,* he thought at the dark above the rafters, at the starless sky beyond it. *You let them burn Your books and You called it humility. You let the Sigil eat a village's whole heart and You called it mystery. Well. I'm done calling it anything.*

* * *

The old woman — they took to thinking of her as Mother, because she would answer to nothing else — put Felix to bed in the son's room, and Felix, exhausted past resistance, let her. She tucked the blanket under his chin the way you tuck in a child, humming a tune with no words left in it, and Tallow stood in the doorway watching, and something in his chest hurt in a way he couldn't steal his way around.

"She thinks he's hers," Brannic murmured beside him. The big man had taken the chair by the fire and hadn't moved from it, as if he expected judgment to arrive at any moment and wanted to be seated for it. "Is that mercy or is it cruelty? Because from here I can't tell."

"It's grief," Tallow said. "Grief doesn't care which it is. It just wants somewhere to put itself down."

Brannic grunted. Fifteen years of guilt had made him fluent in grief, if in nothing else.

Later — deep in the dead hours, when even Oscar slept sitting up against the wall with their hand on their sword — Tallow went out into the yard.

The sky was obscene. He'd grown up under stars; every thief had, because stars were the only witnesses who never slept and never testified. Now the black above him was total, a lid, a closed eye, and the village below lay silent in a way villages were never meant to be silent — no dogs, no night birds, no creak of windmill, just the sound of forty hearths breathing in houses that had forgotten why they built them.

He knelt in the dirt by the well. Not because kneeling meant anything to the Unnamed — the god had no use for knees, the burned books said — but because his own body needed the shape of it, needed the old architecture of prayer even if the tenant had moved out.

"Here's the thing," Tallow said aloud, to the empty sky, in the low fast murmur of a man haggling. "I've spent thirty years telling people You listen. I've taken coin for that lie, and bread, and shelter, and once in Vhal a genuinely excellent horse. I'm a fraud. Fine. Established. Everyone in this company knows it, probably including You, especially including You."

The sky said nothing. The sky was very good at it.

"But this village." His voice caught, and he hated it, and he kept going. "This village tied Your knots. Somebody in that house — the mother, the son, somebody — tied a beggar's knot and asked the three questions and got nothing back, same as everyone gets nothing back, and they tied it anyway. They scrubbed their names off their own doors rather than be eaten, and they left Your knot on the shelf. Of everything in that house, they left *Your* knot."

He pressed his forehead to the cold rim of the well.

"I'm not asking for a miracle. I know the exchange rate. I know what miracles cost and I know I'm carrying maybe a copper's worth of faith left in me after tonight. I'm asking — " and here the haggler's voice failed him entirely, and what came out was just a man's voice, cracked and plain — "I'm asking You to look at them. Look at what was done to them. Somebody has to witness it. The Sigil won't. The court beneath won't. The stars can't, they're not even *on*. If You don't look, nobody looks, and then it's as if it never happened, and I cannot — I will not — stand in a world where this village never happened."

He knelt there until his knees went numb. The cold came up through the ground and the dark came down from the lid of sky and between them Tallow waited for the silence he'd earned, the silence that was the only scripture his god had published in thirty years.

Then the well water moved.

Not wind. Not tremor. The water rose — a finger's width, a hand's width — up the inside of the stone shaft, defying every law Maren could have named and several she couldn't have, and it hung there in the moonless dark, and it was not water anymore. It was a mirror. And in the mirror was not Tallow's face.

It was the village. All of it. Every door with its scraped-blank plaque, every hearth, every clay figure on every shelf, every knot on every lintel — and moving among the images, slow as honey, a presence that had no face and therefore used all of them: the mother's smile, the son's laugh lines, the miller's squint, forty faces flickering like a deck being shuffled by someone learning the trick.

And a voice that was not a voice, that arrived the way meaning arrives, directly behind the eyes:

***I see them.***

Tallow's breath went out of him like a blade from a sheath.

***I have seen them every day. I see every theft, every unmaking, every name scraped smooth. I am the god of the forgotten, thief. Where else would I be?***

"You never answered," Tallow whispered. Thirty years of theology collapsed into five words. "Thirty years. I prayed and prayed and You never—"

***You prayed for answers. I had none to give. What answer does a beggar god have for a seal that eats souls?*** The faces in the water turned, all together, toward him — and there was sorrow in them, vast and specific, the sorrow of a god who had watched everything and saved nothing. ***But you did not pray for answers tonight. You prayed to witness. That prayer I can always grant. Witnessing is the one miracle I have never run out of.***

The water sank. The mirror faded. But before it went, the presence gathered itself one last time, and Tallow felt it lean close, closer than any god had leaned in the history of leaning, and it said:

***The mother. The one who calls your friend by her son's name. Her memory is not eaten, priest. It is hidden. She hid it herself, in the one place the Sigil does not reach — in another person. She looked at your friend and gave him her son's face, so that her love would live in him where it could not be taken. That is not a broken mind. That is a vault. And vaults,*** the god said, and something that was almost laughter moved through the yard like wind through wheat, ***can be opened.***

Then it was gone, and the well was only a well, and Tallow knelt alone in the dirt with tears freezing on his cheeks and his faith — his spent, bankrupt, thirty-years-gone faith — kindling in his chest like a match struck in a cellar.

* * *

He told them at dawn, all of it, kneeling in the yard and the well and the voice. Maren stared at him as if he'd grown a second head made of arithmetic. Brannic crossed himself with the wrong god's sign and didn't notice. Oscar stood very still by the door, and in their eyes was the look of a person recalculating a war.

"And the vault," Oscar said finally. "The mother hiding her memories in Felix. You believe it?"

"I believe my god," Tallow said, and was astonished to find the sentence cost him nothing. "First time for everything."

Inside, the mother was already awake, already smiling, already pressing bread into Felix's hands and calling him Tomas, and Felix — who had run from Grayhollow with a stolen ledger and a sold soul and no self worth saving — sat very straight under her gaze, receiving a dead boy's name like a commission, like a trust.

Tallow watched him take the old woman's hands, and watched Felix's lips move, and though no sound crossed the yard, he read the shape of it easily enough:

*I'll carry it. I promise.*

And outside the window, far to the north, under a sky still stripped of every star, the mountain waited — and Tallow felt, for the first time in thirty years, that the road ahead was not a descent into darkness but a walk toward a door that Someone, somewhere, had promised to hold open.
