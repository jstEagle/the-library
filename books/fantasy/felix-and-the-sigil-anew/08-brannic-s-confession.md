## 8. Brannic's Confession

The fire was Brannic's doing, and he hated himself for how long it took him.

Fifteen years ago he could have built it in his sleep — a soldier's fire, quick and clean, ringed with stones and banked against the wind. Now his hands shook around the flint, and the sparks kept dying in the damp, and Maren watched him from across the hollow with that flat, unforgiving stare she'd worn since the warded road, as if to say: *this too. This is also yours.*

He got it lit on the fourth try. Small mercy: nobody laughed.

They had made camp in a fold of hills where the old ward-road dipped beneath a ridge of black pine, far enough from Grayhollow's ruins of light that the darkness felt honest again. Above them the sky was a blank slate. No stars. He kept looking up anyway, the way a man worries a loose tooth, and every time the nothing up there looked back at him and he thought: *I did this.* Not the stars, not exactly. But the shape of everything else.

Oscar sat apart, cleaning their sword with a rag gone gray from use. They hadn't spoken more than six words to him since the hollow-men. Six words, all of them necessary: *left*, *right*, *down*, *hold*, *now*, *run*. It was more than he deserved. He knew that too.

Felix slept near the fire's edge, curled like a comma, the stolen ledger hugged to his chest even in sleep. The boy twitched sometimes, murmuring numbers — debts, dates, clauses — a soul being read aloud by something that hadn't finished claiming it. Tallow had draped a scrap of blessed cloth over the boy's shoulders an hour ago and said nothing, which for Tallow was practically a sermon.

Brannic fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb toward a sky with nothing to receive them.

*Say it,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his sister. *You've been carrying it fifteen years. Say it while they still might listen.*

His sister. Sera. God, but she would be forty-one now, if the court bothered to age its prisoners. She'd been nineteen when he last saw her, laughing at him over a shared pot in a camp very much like this one, telling him he worried too much, that the company would live forever because Oscar swore it so.

*Oscar swore it so.* And then the company died anyway, and Sera went into the mountain, and Brannic learned what his loyalty weighed when the scales were loaded right.

He looked at Oscar across the flames. The swordspeaker's face was half shadow, half ember-light, older than fifteen years should have made anyone. There was a time Brannic would have died for that face without thinking. Then there was a time he'd sold it instead.

"Oscar." His voice came out rusted. He tried again. "Oscar. I need to say something. Before the mountain."

The rag stopped moving.

Tallow opened one eye by the fire. Maren set down the ward-tool she'd been pretending to repair. Even Felix stirred, though he didn't wake — or pretended not to, which amounted to the same permission.

"I'm listening," Oscar said. Their voice gave away nothing. Fifteen years ago it would have given away everything; Oscar had never learned to guard their face until the betrayal taught them.

Brannic stood. It seemed wrong to say it sitting down, wrong to say it from any position that wasn't standing at attention before the person he'd wronged most. His knees cracked. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale.

"Fifteen years ago," he began, "when the wards fell at Karst Bridge — when the under-court took us — you've all spent fifteen years believing it was bad luck. Bad scouting. Bad orders." He swallowed. "It wasn't any of those things."

Maren's jaw tightened. "Brannic—"

"Let him speak," Oscar said quietly. "He came back to do it. Let him finish."

"It was me." The words came out of him like teeth being pulled, one at a time, each with its own little root of blood. "I sold the company's route to the court's agents. I told them where we'd cross, when we'd cross, how many we were. I told them about Oscar's oath — what it was, what it bound, what breaking it would cost. I sat in a tavern in Lowmark and drank their wine and took their coin and drew our own road on their map with my own hand."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the hollow like snowfall.

"Why?" Maren asked. Not gentle. She didn't owe him gentleness. But not cruel either, which was somehow worse — the question of someone who genuinely needed the answer before she could decide what to do with it.

Brannic laughed, and it came out broken. "Because they had my sister."

* * *

Sera had been taken two winters before Karst Bridge. That was the part nobody knew — not Oscar, not Maren, not Tallow, who hadn't yet joined them. The court beneath the mountain didn't always send hollow-men kicking down doors. Sometimes it sent letters. Polite ones. Cream paper, wax seal, a sum written in a fair hand.

*Your sister owes a debt. The debt has been purchased. She will serve until it is discharged. Inquiries may be directed to the Office of Claims.*

"Inquiries," Brannic said now, staring into the fire. "I wrote eleven inquiries. Eleven letters. Do you know what came back? Receipts. Confirmations that my letter had been received and filed. Once, a notice that her debt had been *reassessed* — which meant it had grown. Interest, they called it. Her existence accrued interest." His hands had become fists without his permission. "She was a seamstress. She borrowed forty crowns to keep her shop open through a bad winter. Forty crowns, Oscar. By the time they were done reassessing her, the number had more digits than my whole year's pay."

"So you went to them," Oscar said. Still quiet. Still unreadable. "To bargain."

"I went to them to fight. That's what I told myself, anyway. I marched to the mountain with my sword and my righteous fury and I stood at the gate — the same gate we're walking toward now — and I demanded her release." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what they did? They invited me in. Gave me tea. A magistrate with a borrowed face — beautiful, calm, patient as stone — sat across from me and explained, kindly, that fury was a currency too, and mine had just been weighed and found insufficient. And then she offered me a trade. Not Sera's freedom. Never that. Just... information. Routes, names, weaknesses. Little things, she called them. Pebbles. And each pebble would knock a digit off Sera's debt."

"You could have come to us," Tallow said. His first words all night, low and rough. "We'd have helped. We'd have found another way."

"I know." Brannic's voice cracked clean through. "That's the worst of it, Tallow. I *knew*. I sat in that beautiful room with that beautiful monster and I thought: Oscar would find a way. Oscar always finds a way. And then I thought about the magistrates' faces, and the ledgers, and the way the debt grew just because she breathed, and I thought: what if Oscar doesn't? What if Oscar's way takes a year, and Sera spends that year accruing? What if Oscar fails?" He looked up, finally, and let them see all of it — the shame, the cowardice, the arithmetic of fear that had governed him for fifteen years. "So I didn't gamble with Sera's life on Oscar's goodness. I gambled with everyone else's instead. I paid the court in the only coin I had that they wanted. I paid them in *you*."

"And it bought you what?" Maren asked. "One digit?"

"Three." He said it flatly, letting them hear how obscene it was. "Three digits off a number that still had seven left. And then Karst Bridge happened, and the company broke, and Oscar—" he faltered, forced himself onward, "—and the court came for Oscar's oath itself, because that was the true prize all along, and I understood then that I'd handed them the key to everything. So I ran. I took my three digits and my shame and I ran, and I told myself Sera was safer for it, and I told myself that for fifteen years, and every night I told myself it, and every night it rang false as a cracked bell."

"Is she alive?" Felix asked.

Brannic turned. The boy was awake after all, sitting up with the ledger still clutched to his chest, watching Brannic with eyes that held no judgment at all — only recognition. The look of someone reading his own future in another man's face.

"I don't know," Brannic said. "That's the cruelty of it. The court doesn't kill its debtors. It files them. Somewhere under that mountain, if she still exists, she's a clerk in a bone-white office, stamping papers, forgetting her own name a little more each year." He nodded at the ledger in Felix's arms. "Like you'll be, boy, if the new moon passes."

Felix looked down at the ledger. Said nothing. But his arms tightened around it.

* * *

Oscar rose.

Brannic's whole body braced — some old soldier instinct expecting a blow, and knowing he'd earned it. But Oscar only walked around the fire, slow, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at him with those eyes that had once trusted him completely and now measured him like a merchant measuring suspect coin.

"Fifteen years," Oscar said. "I rebuilt a life on the rubble you made. I taught myself to be small. I unlearned my own name. Every night for fifteen years I lay awake wondering what I'd done wrong — what failure of mine had lost the bridge, lost the company, lost—" their voice caught, just once, and steadied, "—lost everyone. And the answer was: nothing. Nothing except trusting you."

"I know."

"Do you?" Oscar stepped closer. The firelight made their shadow loom up the rockface behind them like something from the old stories. "Do you know what it is to learn that your worst night wasn't fate? That it was a friend's arithmetic? That the people who died at Karst Bridge — Hesk, and the twins, and little Corvin who used to sing while he sharpened — do you know that they died of a *choice*? Yours. Made in a comfortable room, over tea?"

"Yes." Brannic held the gaze. It was the least he could offer: not to flinch. "Yes, Oscar. I know exactly what they died of."

Oscar's hand moved — toward the hilt, Brannic thought, and some traitor part of him relaxed at the idea, because punishment would at least be simpler than this. But the hand passed the hilt and closed on Brannic's shoulder instead, gripping hard enough to bruise.

"I will never forgive you," Oscar said. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Hear me. Not tonight. Not at the gate. Not if we walk out of that mountain with every soul in it freed. Some things don't come back, Brannic. Trust is one. The dead are another. You'll carry Sera's debt and ours both, and I will not lighten it for you with absolution."

"I'm not asking for absolution."

"No," Oscar said. "You're asking for a place in the line. A chance to stand between the others and what's coming, and call it payment." A pause. The grip on his shoulder tightened, then released. "And the hell of it is, that's exactly what you're offering. And the hell of *that* is, I need every sword I can get."

Brannic breathed. It hurt.

"Then take the sword," he said. "Not my apology. The apology's worthless — I've known that for fifteen years. But the sword's still good. The arm behind it is still good. Let me spend both at the gate, and if the price comes due, let it come to me first."

Oscar studied him for a long moment. Behind them, Maren had gone back to her tools, though Brannic noticed her hands weren't moving. Tallow watched with both eyes open now, and there was something in the thief-priest's face that might have been pity, or might have been the beginning of a prayer.

"The watch order," Oscar said finally, turning away. "You take second watch. Alone. If you run, we'll know by morning, and I'll spend no tears on it."

"I won't run."

"Everyone runs," Oscar said, settling back by the fire with the sword across their knees. "Until the night they don't. Second watch, Brannic."

* * *

The camp settled. Felix slept truly now, the ledger's grip on him loosening into ordinary dreams. Maren banked the coals with a ward-smith's precision. Tallow murmured something to his banned god, soft as ash falling.

Brannic took the high ground above the hollow and stood his watch under the starless sky, and did what soldiers have always done on watch: talked to the dead.

*Corvin. Hesk. Twins.* He named them silently, one by one, the roll of Karst Bridge, and ended, as he always ended, with *Sera*, who might not be dead at all, which was worse.

Somewhere below, Oscar slept without sleeping, the way oath-bound people do. Somewhere beneath the earth, a mountain full of clerks filed souls like invoices. Somewhere in the dark between, his sister might have been listening, or might have been nothing but a name in a ledger, accruing.

"Second watch," he whispered to the empty sky. "I'm here. I'm not running."

And the dark answered — not with wind, not with pine-sigh, but with a sound none of them had heard in fifteen years and none of them had ever expected to hear again:

Far to the north, faint as a struck bell, a single star kindled in the black.

Just one. Just for a moment. Like something ancient, deep beneath the mountain, turning over in its hunger — and noticing, for the first time in years, that it was being *watched back*.

Brannic's blood went cold.

Whatever waited under the mountain had just felt them coming.
