## 9. The Mountain Opens Its Mouth

Maren smelled the mountain before she saw it.

Not stone — she'd worked stone all her life, warded it, welded her craft into its grain until the two were indistinguishable. This was something older and wetter, like the breath of a cellar that had never once been opened, cold enough to carry the memory of iron. It came down the road to meet them a full mile before the road ended, threading through the pines in slow gray ribbons, and Tallow stopped walking so abruptly that Felix nearly collided with his back.

"That's not weather," Tallow said.

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't."

She had built wards along this border once. Fifteen years ago, when her hands still shook with purpose instead of drink, she had stood on the shoulder of this very mountain with Oscar at her side and Brannic laughing somewhere behind them, and she had poured herself into the lattice until her nose bled. She remembered the shape of the work. She remembered the *cost* of the work. What she could not remember — what came back to her now only as a hollow place, an ache shaped like a person — was why she had ever thought it would hold.

The trees thinned. The road climbed. And then the mountain opened its mouth.

That was the only way to describe it. The cliff face simply *parted*, a wound in the rock forty feet high and half as wide, its edges smooth as cut flesh, breathing out that cellar-cold air in long, patient exhalations. No door. No gate. Just an opening, dark as a pupil, waiting.

Felix made a small sound. Maren turned and found him staring at it with his ledger clutched to his chest, his knuckles white around the leather, and she felt something twist in her chest that she hadn't felt in years — the old reflex of protection, dusty but intact.

"It's been open before," Oscar said quietly. They stood at the front of the group, sword-hand loose at their side, and Maren watched them study the wound in the rock the way she used to study a failing ward: looking for the seam, the flaw, the place where pressure would tell. "The stone's worn where people walked. Look."

Maren looked. They were right. A path of paler rock ran into the darkness, polished by centuries of feet, and flanking it stood two pillars of black stone carved with script that made her eyes water when she tried to read it. Not because the letters moved. Because they *waited*.

"Toll-stones," she said, and heard her own voice go flat. "Gods below. I haven't seen toll-stones since—" Since the war. Since before Grayhollow outlawed everything she knew how to do. She stepped closer, squinting against the ache behind her eyes, and read the inscription aloud because some part of her craft demanded it be spoken:

**THE MOUNTAIN KEEPS WHAT IS TRUE. EACH WHO ENTERS LEAVES ONE TRUTH AT THE DOOR. THE MOUNTAIN WILL KNOW IF IT IS FALSE.**

"Leaves one truth," Felix repeated. "What does that mean? Leaves it *how*?"

"A memory," Brannic said.

They all turned. He had hung back since the fire, since his confession, since Oscar had looked at him across the flames and said *I don't forgive you* in a voice like a closing door. But he came forward now, and there was something in his face Maren recognized from fifteen years ago — the look of a man who had already done the arithmetic no one else wanted to do.

"I've seen these before," he said. "In the south, on the old pilgrimage roads. Toll-gates. You give up a true memory — a real one, whole, yours — and the mountain takes it into the stone. You walk through knowing you'll never get it back." His jaw worked. "And you walk through lighter. That's the trap of it. It feels like mercy."

"And if you refuse?" Felix asked.

Brannic didn't answer. Oscar did, without turning: "Then you stand outside until you starve, or you turn around, or you find another way in that doesn't exist." A pause. "There's always a toll. At least this one tells you the price up front."

Maren's stomach had begun to hurt, low and cold, and she understood why before she let herself understand it. She began taking inventory. Everyone did; she saw it happen, four faces going inward at once, each of them riffling through their own lives for something they could bear to lose.

Tallow first — he laughed, short and ugly. "Easy. I've got decades I'd pay to forget. My ordination. My vows. The night my god first spoke to me and I wept like a child." He paused, and the laugh died. "Except those are the ones it wants, aren't they. Not the bad ones. The *true* ones. The load-bearing ones."

"The mountain keeps what is true," Maren murmured. "Yes. It won't take trash. It takes the stones your house is built on."

Felix had gone very pale. "I can't," he said. "You don't understand — my memories are all I have left of them. My mother. My sisters. If I give one up, it's like losing them twice." His voice cracked on the last word, and Maren wanted to cross the distance between them and put her hand on his shoulder, but her hands had learned, over fifteen years, to stay at her sides.

"You won't lose them," Oscar said. "You'll lose one day. One moment. Choose a small one."

"There's no such thing." Felix's voice was fierce suddenly, and Maren flinched at the force of it. "There is no small memory. That's the whole point of me. That's the whole point of *this*—" he struck the ledger with his fist, "—they took everything by pretending pieces were small. A signature here. A clause there. Small things. And now I'm collateral." He was shaking. "I'm not giving that mountain anything it wants. Not freely."

No one argued. Maren found she couldn't.

* * *

They camped within sight of the gate, which Maren thought was either courage or cowardice, depending on the hour. She took first watch because sleep had become a country she could rarely afford passage to, and she sat with her back against a pine trunk and her smuggler's kit open across her knees, doing what she always did when her hands needed an excuse: checking her tools.

Ward-smithing wasn't a glamorous trade. People imagined sigils blazing, great works of power. In truth it was files and silver wire and bone needles and patience, and Maren had spent fifteen years letting all of it rust while she poured the cheaper thing down her throat instead. Her kit was thinner than it had been. But it was hers. She counted the needles. She tested the wire's temper against her thumbnail. And she let herself think about the toll.

*Each who enters leaves one truth at the door.*

She had so many truths she'd rather be rid of. The taste of the morning she woke up and couldn't remember why she'd stopped drinking. The sound of Oscar's voice the day the company broke — no, that one she'd keep; anger was a kind of ballast. The look on Brannic's face when they'd found the traitor's mark on the contract, all those years ago, and how he hadn't even denied it—

Her hands stopped moving.

Because underneath all the truths she was willing to burn, one sat glowing like a coal she'd been carrying too long, and she had known, from the moment she read the toll-stone, whose name it wore.

Joren.

She said his name silently, testing whether it still hurt. It did. Good. Pain meant it was still true.

They had married in spring, in a village whose name she could no longer quite trust herself to recall correctly — memory was a liar even unmeddled-with, and she'd spent too many nights drinking it into new shapes. But the wedding itself, that she had kept clean. Kept sacred. Joren in his good blue coat, laughing because the officiant had lost the ring and Maren had produced it from behind his ear with a flourish of sleight-of-hand she'd learned from a thief-priest who was, at this moment, snoring twenty feet away. The rain starting just as they finished their vows, and neither of them running for cover, just standing there getting soaked while everyone shouted at them to come inside. Joren's hand on her cheek. *Whatever comes,* he'd said, *we keep it.*

He had died eleven years later, in the first wave of the purges, because he'd refused to report a neighbor's charm-stall. She had not kept it. She had not kept anything. She had drunk herself into a woman who could walk past the places he used to stand.

But the wedding day — the wedding day she had guarded like a flame in cupped hands, through every blackout, every bad year. The one true thing.

And now the mountain wanted it, and the boy with the sold soul needed to go through, and there was no version of the arithmetic where she got to keep it.

Maren closed her kit. She looked up at the wound in the rock, black against the blacker sky, and she hated it with a clean and professional hatred, the way she'd hate any badly designed system. *A toll of names,* she thought. *A toll of selves.* Whoever built this had understood something terrible about people: that they would pay anything to move forward, and call the payment fair, and walk on lighter and poorer and never quite know what was missing.

Well. She'd been missing things for fifteen years. One more, taken honestly, might almost be a kindness.

Almost.

* * *

At dawn they stood before the pillars, and the cold breath of the mountain stirred their hair and clothes like something sniffing them.

"Order matters?" Felix asked nervously. "Does anyone know if order matters?"

"It doesn't," Oscar said. "I'll go last regardless." They drew their sword — not to fight, Maren noted, but the way a man touches a talisman — and looked at each of them in turn. "One truth. Make it count, and make it *yours*. Don't try to trick the stone. Don't offer something you've already half-let go of. It'll know, and I don't want to learn what it does to liars." A beat. "We go through. We regroup inside. Nobody wanders. Understood?"

Four nods. Five, counting herself.

Tallow went first, because of course he did; the man had never met a threshold he didn't want to dance across. He walked up to the toll-stone, pressed both palms flat against the black surface, and closed his eyes. Maren watched his lips move — a prayer, or the shape of one — and then watched him sway as though something had passed through him and out. When he opened his eyes they were wet and bewildered.

"Gone," he said hoarsely. "The night my god first spoke. I know it happened. I know it mattered. I can't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "It's like a room in my house I've never entered. I know the door's there. There's nothing behind it." He laughed, shaky. "Lighter. Brannic was right. Damn him, he was right."

He walked into the dark and did not come back out, which was somehow worse than screaming would have been.

Brannic followed. He stood at the stone a long time, longer than Tallow, and Maren saw his shoulders set like a man lifting something heavy, and then he was through, and gone.

Oscar looked at Felix. "Your choice. You can wait outside if—"

"No." Felix's voice was steadier than Maren expected. He walked to the stone, hesitated, and then — she saw him do it, saw him choose — he pressed his hands to the surface and gave it something. She never learned what. He came away from the stone touching his temple like a man after a blow, whispering, "I had a dog. I had a dog when I was seven. I know I had a dog. What was its *name*—" and then he squared his shoulders and walked into the mountain, grieving a small true thing, and Maren's chest ached for him.

Which left her and Oscar.

"Maren." Oscar's voice was quiet. They had not spoken of it — there had been no time, no privacy, no words adequate — but Oscar looked at her now with fifteen years of shared history in their eyes, and said only: "You don't owe this mountain anything."

"Neither do you," she said. "And here we both stand."

She walked to the toll-stone before she could argue herself out of it. Up close, the black stone wasn't stone at all; it was dense with script, layer under layer under layer, thousands upon thousands of surrendered truths packed into the surface like sediment, and when she laid her palms against it she felt them — faint, vast, a city of other people's kept moments humming under her skin. The mountain was not hungry, she realized. The mountain was *full*. It was an archive. It was a vault. It was the most enormous ward-lattice she had ever touched, and some distant, smith-brained part of her screamed with the need to study it.

*Give us one,* the stone seemed to say. Not in words. In pressure. In invitation.

Maren closed her eyes.

Spring. Blue coat. Rain on vows. *Whatever comes, we keep it.*

*I'm sorry, love,* she thought, toward wherever he was, toward whatever was left of him in her. *I kept it as long as I could. And I'm going to spend what's left of me making sure no one else gets robbed like this. So maybe — maybe that counts as keeping it.*

She pushed the memory out through her palms.

It went like blood leaving a wound — a long warm rush, a sudden lightness that was also a loss, the specific vertigo of a limb no longer attached. She gasped. She staggered. Oscar caught her arm, solid and silent, and held her up while the world reorganized itself around the absence.

She knew she had been married. The fact remained, dry as a line in a ledger. She knew his name was Joren because she had written it in her mind that morning, deliberately, like a note pinned above the gap. But the rain was gone. The blue coat was gone. The ring behind the ear, the laughter, the vow — gone, gone, gone, packed away into the black stone under her hands, joining ten thousand other truths in the mountain's keeping.

"Lighter," she whispered. Her voice sounded wrong. Thin. "Gods help me, it feels like being forgiven."

"That's how it gets you," Oscar said grimly. "Come on."

They walked into the mouth together, into air that tasted of deep stone and older cold, and the dark closed behind them like water over a dropped coin.

The passage sloped down, wide as a road, lit by nothing and yet not entirely dark — the walls gave off a faint gray luminescence, and as Maren's eyes adjusted she saw why, and stopped walking so fast she bumped into Felix's back.

The walls were inscribed. Floor to unseen ceiling, both sides, receding into depths beyond sight: names. Tens of thousands of names, each one followed by lines of fine script — terms, clauses, conditions — and beneath each entry, a small seal. She knew that seal. They all did. Felix made a sound beside her like a man being strangled slowly.

Every soul the court had ever claimed. Every debt. The mountain's mouth was a ledger, and they were walking down its spine.

"Look," Felix breathed, pointing with a trembling finger. "Look — there are blank ones. Fresh-cut spaces. Waiting." He turned to her, and in the gray light his face was the color of the lanterns they hadn't yet reached. "Maren. My name's going to be on this wall."

Maren looked at the fresh-cut blanks, dozens of them, scores, each one a hollow waiting to be filled — and the smith in her, the woman who had spent her life finding the flaw in every lattice, noticed something else. Something the others hadn't seen yet.

The wall wasn't whole. Far down the passage, where the light failed, a section of the inscription had been damaged — cracked, patched, rewoven in a hurry by hands less careful than the original makers'. Someone had mended this ledger before. Badly.

And badly-mended wards, Maren knew better than anyone alive, were wards that could be broken.

"Keep walking," she said, and filed the crack away in the deepest drawer of her mind, next to the shape of a wedding she could no longer picture, and followed the others down into the dark.
