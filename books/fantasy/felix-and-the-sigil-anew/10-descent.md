## 10. Descent

The stair went down for a day and a night, and Felix stopped counting after that.

He knew it was a stair because his feet told him so — each step worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of descent, the stone polished smooth as the inside of a spoon. Everything else had become uncertain. The walls of the passage were close enough to touch on both sides, and they were warm. Not fire-warm. Skin-warm. The mountain was not hollow; Felix understood that somewhere around the ten-thousandth step. The mountain was *swallowing*.

Maren's lantern threw their shadows long and trembling against the rock. She had not spoken in hours. None of them had. Even Tallow, who prayed under his breath the way other men breathed, had gone quiet, as though words might be overheard down here and *filed*.

That was the thing Felix could not stop thinking about: filing. He was a scribe. He had spent six years in the Grayhollow chancery learning that every power in the world ran on paper — taxes, marriages, executions, pardons. But he had always believed, in some unexamined corner of himself, that paper was a tool. A record of things. It had never occurred to him that paper could be the *thing itself*, that a soul could be an entry in a ledger, that he could be walking down into the dark because of a clause his father had signed with a shaking hand when Felix was nine years old.

"Stop." Oscar's voice came from ahead, low and even.

Felix nearly walked into their back. In the lantern light he could see the swordspeaker's shoulders had gone rigid, one hand hovering near the plain scabbard at their hip — the sword that had not spoken since the wards fell, or rather, that had spoken once, terribly, on the warded road, and left its price written in blood along Oscar's arm.

"What is it?" Maren whispered.

"Listen."

Felix listened. At first there was nothing — just the drip of water somewhere far below, and the soft pop of the lantern's oil. Then he heard it. A sound like wind through wheat, if wheat grew in rows and rustled in perfect unison. A sound like ten thousand quills moving across ten thousand pages.

Writing. Somewhere beneath them, something vast was writing.

"The city," Oscar said. "We're close."

They descended another hour, and then the stair opened.

* * *

Felix had imagined the court beneath the mountain as a cavern, perhaps — dripping, monstrous, lit by torchlight and cruelty. What he saw instead stole the breath from his body and kept it.

It was a city. An actual city, built inside a vaulted dark so high that the lanterns' light died before it found a ceiling. Streets of pale stone wound between buildings of the same bone-colored rock, and hanging from every eave, every archway, every iron post driven into the cobbles, there were lanterns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They burned with a white, steady flame that gave off no smoke and no heat, and their light was the color of old teeth.

Bone-white lanterns, and every single one of them burning above a desk.

Because that was what the buildings were. Felix saw it as his eyes adjusted: the streets were lined with counting-houses, scriptoriums, halls of record, all of them open to the nightless air, all of them filled with desks, and at every desk sat a figure writing.

The clerks did not look up. They wrote with terrible industry, quills whispering, pages turning, and their faces in the lantern light were —

Felix made himself look. Made himself see.

They were calm. That was the horror of it. They were not tortured, not weeping, not chained. They sat in neat rows in the white light with expressions of mild, diligent contentment, and they wrote, and wrote, and wrote. Recording debts. Assessing interest. Entering payments received. Their eyes were the gray of ash, and when one of them paused to dip its quill, it did so with the small satisfied sigh of a person doing work they loved.

"Felix." Oscar's hand closed on his shoulder. "Don't stare."

"They're people," Felix said. His voice came out cracked. "Oscar, those are *people*."

"They were," Oscar said quietly. "Once."

Tallow had gone gray. He crossed himself with the sign of his banned god — thumb to heart, two fingers to lips — and murmured something too low to catch. Brannic stood apart from all of them, arms wrapped around himself, staring at the nearest clerk with an expression Felix couldn't read and didn't want to.

"How do you know which way to go?" Maren asked. Her knuckles were white around the lantern's handle. "This place is a maze."

"I don't," Oscar said. "But I know where the records are. Every debt comes home to the Hall of Names eventually. And if your soul has a file—" they looked at Felix, and their eyes were kind and grim at once, "—it will be there."

They moved. There was no other choice; the stair behind them had sealed itself shut with a sound like a held breath being released, and the only road now was forward, down streets that curved in ways streets should not curve, past windows where the white light pooled like milk.

The clerks ignored them. That was somehow worse than being hunted. Once, passing a long gallery filled with desks, Felix watched a clerk finish a page, blow it dry with a gentle breath, set it in a tray, take up a fresh sheet, and continue — and the motion was so human, so *familiar*, that his stomach turned over. His mother wrote her market lists with that same little flourish. His father signed his name with that same tired satisfaction.

A clerk looked up.

Just one. Just for a moment. Its ash-gray eyes found Felix across the gallery, and it tilted its head — a small, birdlike, *interested* tilt — and then it returned to its page.

Felix walked faster.

* * *

The Hall of Names stood at the center of the city, and it was less a building than a wound — a vast circular chamber whose walls rose out of sight, every inch of them covered in niches, and every niche holding a file. Not books. Files. Thin folders of pale vellum, each one tagged with a strip of ribbon, and the ribbons were every color Felix had ever seen and some he hadn't, a whole taxonomy of obligation dyed into silk.

There were clerks here too, hundreds of them, moving along the shelves on ladders, fetching files, returning files, carrying them to reading desks where petitioners — living petitioners, Felix realized with a jolt, breathing petitioners with shadows and sweat — sat hunched over their own names.

"It lets people visit?" Maren whispered.

"It lets people *owe*," Oscar said. "Come on. Stay together. And whatever you do, don't sign anything."

They split into pairs by silent agreement — Oscar with Felix, Maren and Tallow taking the eastern arc of shelves, Brannic walking the perimeter with his hand on his knife, watching the doors. Felix followed Oscar into the stacks, and the sound of the quills faded behind them until it was just the two of them and the soft tick of cooling lantern-flames.

The files were organized by name. Of course they were. Felix found the section marked with the letters of the common tongue — IVERS, JORVAN, KESS — and ran his finger along the shelf edges, feeling the vellum, cool and faintly damp, like the skin of a fish.

IVERS. His heart stuttered. There was a file there. *Iversen, O.* — tagged with a black ribbon shot through with silver thread, and the silver was tarnished almost to nothing.

Oscar went very still beside him.

"Don't," Felix said.

"I have to know." But Oscar didn't reach for it. Their hand hovered, trembled, and withdrew. "Not yet. Yours first. Yours is the one with a clock on it."

Felix swallowed and searched on. LARSEN. LINDQVIST. And then—

*Merrick, F.*

His own name. His whole name, spelled out in the careful copperplate of the under-court: *Merrick, Felix Aldous*. Tagged with a ribbon the color of dried blood.

It was heavier than it should have been. That was the first thing he noticed when he drew it from the niche — the file weighed as much as a brick, as much as a cat, as much as a *heart*. He carried it to a reading desk beneath its own bone-white lantern, and Oscar stood at his shoulder, and Felix opened it.

The first page was the contract. He knew it — he had seen a copy once, years ago, when his father had drunk too much and wept and shown him. Four signatures. His father's, his mother's, the magistrate's proctor, and at the bottom, in a space he had never noticed before, a fifth line.

*Signature of the Asset:* and beneath it, blank. Waiting.

"He never signed my name," Felix whispered. "They can't— it's not binding unless I—"

"Keep reading," Oscar said softly.

He kept reading. The next pages were assessments. *Asset condition: intact. Asset age: fifteen at conveyance, twenty-one at present. Asset literacy: exceptional. Asset aptitude: clerical.* His school marks. His chancery evaluations, copied out in full, every commendation his supervisors had ever written him, transcribed here in the court's patient hand. *Asset demonstrates strong penmanship, diligence, capacity for sustained repetitive labor.*

Capacity for sustained repetitive labor.

The words swam. He blinked and read on. There was a projection — a forecast, drawn up with the same mild precision as a harvest report. *Projected asset trajectory upon claim: entry-level clerkship, Hall of Records, third circle. Estimated adjustment period: brief. Assets of this temperament historically achieve contentment within [figure illegible] years.*

Contentment.

Felix thought of the clerks in the galleries. Their mild faces. Their little sighs of satisfaction as they dipped their quills. *Assets of this temperament historically achieve contentment.*

"That's what happens," he said. He heard his own voice as if from far away. "That's what the new moon does. It doesn't drag me down here kicking. It just—" He turned a page. There was an illustration, a diagram, precise as an anatomist's plate: a human silhouette, and within it, small labeled regions. *Memory (select). Will (partial). Grief (full remission).* "It takes the parts of me that would mind. And leaves the rest at a desk forever."

"Forever is a long word down here," Oscar said. "Look at the last page."

He looked. The final leaf was different — older, the vellum yellowed, the ink brown. It was a note, appended by some clerk long dead, and it read:

*Addendum, entered [date illegible]: Asset's file flagged for priority processing per directive of the Ninth Magistrate. Note the resemblance. Note especially the hands. Some assets come home twice.*

*Some assets come home twice.*

Felix stared at the words until they blurred. "What does that mean? Oscar. What does *come home twice* mean?"

Oscar wasn't looking at the page anymore. They were looking at Felix's hands — at his fingers, ink-stained, scribe's-callused, resting on the vellum — and their face had gone the color of the lanterns.

"You write with your left hand," Oscar said.

"I'm ambidextrous. Everyone at the chancery said it was a waste, that I should pick one—"

"I knew someone," Oscar said, very carefully, "who wrote with both hands. Who worked in this city. Who swore an oath to me, fifteen years ago, that we would get her out." Their jaw tightened. "We didn't. And the magistrates who took her — the Ninth was one of them."

The lantern above the desk flickered. All around them, in the endless stacks, the files seemed to lean closer on their shelves, ten thousand souls rustling like leaves.

Somewhere in the Hall of Names, a bell began to ring — slow, deliberate, patient as interest accruing. And down every aisle, in unison, the clerks looked up from their desks.

Every ash-gray eye in the Hall turned toward Felix.

One of them rose. It set down its quill with that small, satisfied sigh, straightened its papers, and began to walk toward the reading desk — toward Felix and his open file — and as it stepped into the lantern light, Felix saw its face clearly for the first time.

It was a woman's face. Kind, intelligent, worn smooth as river stone. And she wrote with both hands, a folio tucked under one arm, and she was smiling at him with mild, diligent contentment as she said, in a voice like a page turning:

"You must be the new asset. I've been asked to show you your desk."
