## 11. The Court Beneath

The magistrates did not summon us. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing I distrusted.

We had been in the bone-white city for a day and a night — or what passed for them down here, where the lanterns burned without flicker and the hours had no edges — and no one had come for us. We had walked its avenues of pale stone, past clerks with too-smooth faces who bowed as we passed but never spoke, past archives that went up like wells instead of down, past fountains that ran with something thicker than water and sweeter than blood. And all the while the court had simply *let* us walk, the way a spider lets a fly cross the web it has already felt.

"They're waiting," Maren said, for the fourth time since we'd found our lodging — a guesthouse of white stone with beds too soft and windows that showed nothing but more mountain.

"I know they're waiting."

"Then why haven't they—"

"Because waiting is a weapon." I stood at the window, watching a procession of clerks move below with the synchronized grace of fish turning in a tank. "They want us to come to them. They want us to ask. Every word we beg out of them will be written into the ledger against us."

Tallow sat on the floor in the corner, legs crossed, his banned god's broken sigil resting in his palms like a sleeping animal. He hadn't prayed aloud since the village. I think he was afraid of what might answer. "Then don't beg," he said. "Petition. There's a difference. Begging asks for charity. Petitioning asserts a right."

"We have no rights here," Brannic said from the doorway. He'd barely slept. None of us had, but it showed on him worst — fifteen years of guilt carved deeper by one night under this roof. "That's the whole architecture of the place. You can see it in the stones. This court wasn't built to judge. It was built to *hold*."

Felix was asleep, finally, in the room's second bed, curled small around the stolen ledger like a child around a doll. Even sleeping, his fingers moved — writing, always writing, some clerk's habit sunk so deep into him it lived below dreaming. Watching him sleep, I understood why the old stories warned heroes against love. Not because it weakened you. Because it made the arithmetic unbearable.

I had done arithmetic for three days now. Felix's soul against my oath. A boy of nineteen years against the vow that had kept my heart beating through fifteen of exile and silence. The magistrates would make me do that arithmetic aloud, in front of witnesses, and whatever answer I gave, they would find a way to charge me for the working.

So before they could set the sum, I meant to read the fine print myself.

* * *

The audience chamber found us rather than the other way around. That is the only honest way to describe it. We left the guesthouse intending to walk the long way, to map the city, to stall — and every street we took bent gently, courteously, inevitably toward the same tall doors of black wood banded in silver, until Tallow laughed once, without humor, and said, "All right. We hear you," and the doors opened.

Inside was a hall built to a scale that insulted the body. The ceiling vanished into dark. The floor was polished to mirror-brightness, and when I looked down I saw not my reflection but a ledger page, columns of figures crawling across it in ink that gleamed like oil. Columns with names at their heads. One of them, near the center, read *IVERSEN, O.* in a hand I recognized — my own, from another life, from an oath sworn in starlight with a sword-point pressed to my tongue.

I looked up fast. Too late. Somewhere beneath the floor, a pen scratched, recording that I had looked.

Seven thrones stood at the hall's far end, arranged in a crescent. Six were occupied. The seventh stood empty, and I knew — the way you know a wound has stopped bleeding only when you press it — that the empty throne was mine. Had been mine. Was being kept.

The six magistrates wore borrowed faces, and they wore them badly, the way children wear their parents' clothes. One had a merchant's jowls and eyes that were older than any merchant. One wore the face of a young woman, beautiful and bland, and her hands folded in her lap were knotted with veins and age spots that didn't match. Another had taken the face of a Grayhollow lamplighter I had known, dead now eleven years, and seeing it turned my stomach over slow.

"Oscar Iversen." The speaker sat at the crescent's center: a broad man with a soldier's broken nose and a scholar's soft voice. "Swordspeaker. Oath-bound. Last of your kind, and longest hiding from ours." He smiled, and the smile fit his face perfectly, which told me it was the one thing about him that was real. "Fifteen years. You must be tired."

"I'm comfortable," I said. "Thank you for asking."

Murmurs along the crescent — amusement, or the shape of it. Behind me, I heard Felix swallow. I could feel him wanting to step forward, to speak, to do the brave stupid thing, and I shifted half a pace to keep my shoulder between him and the thrones. Old habit. Shield-arm forward.

"You've come to petition," the central magistrate said. "We received the petition before you finished composing it. Down here, intent arrives ahead of words. It saves such time." He gestured, and a clerk materialized from the shadowed flank of the hall carrying a tray, and on the tray lay a single sheet of paper, blank except for a seal at its foot: the interlocked ring of vows, the mark from Felix's ledger, rendered here in silver that seemed to breathe.

"The boy's debt," the magistrate said. "Nineteen years of interest, compounded. His family's obligations, transferred. The claim is lawful, complete, and — I'll say this plainly, Swordspeaker, because flattery is wasted on you — *binding*. On the next new moon, his soul passes to this court entire. There is no appeal. There is no loophole. We wrote the law, and we are very good at writing law."

"Then there's nothing to discuss," I said. "Good day."

I turned. It cost me everything I had to turn my back on those thrones, and the floor's ledger-ink crawled hungrily at my heels as I did. But behind me the magistrate said, softly, almost kindly:

"Unless the debt were paid by other means."

I stopped. Of course I stopped. They had built the whole hall, the whole city, the whole mountain, to make people stop at exactly that sentence.

* * *

They brought chairs. That was the obscenity of it — the courtesy. Servants who may have been souls and may have been furniture arranged seats for us before the crescent, brought water and dark bread, treated us like honored petitioners, like guests, like family come home after long absence. Tallow refused his chair and stood. Maren sat but kept her smuggled tool-roll against her ribs where she could feel it. Brannic sat like a man before a tribunal, which, in a sense, he was — this court had held his sister's soul these fifteen years, purchased with his betrayal, and I watched him search each borrowed face for hers and find nothing, and hurt, and hide it.

Felix sat beside me with the ledger closed on his knees and his hands flat on its cover, as if holding down a lid.

"Speak the offer," I said. "Plainly. No kindness. Kindness from this court is a hook with the barb filed smooth."

The central magistrate inclined his head, acknowledging the hit. "Then plainly. The Sigil Anew hungers. It was built — we built it, ages past, we and others like us — to hold the oaths of the world in balance. Starlight-sworn vows fed it. It grew fat and the world grew lawful, and everyone slept safe inside their promises." His voice dropped, and for one moment the borrowed face seemed to thin over something vast and weary underneath. "Then the stars went out. Whether above or below, whether by malice or accident, we do not fully know — the wards failed, the sky went dark, and the Sigil began to starve. A starving seal cannot hold. A starving seal begins to eat what it was never given freely. Villages forget themselves. Wards collapse. Debts come due all at once."

"And you need food," Maren said flatly.

"We need *payment*," the magistrate corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the law. The Sigil may take — and everything it takes rots in its keeping, souls gone gray, memories gone to paste, villages hollowed. Or it may be *given*, and what is given freely sustains it as starlight once did." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, soldier's posture, and looked at me with eyes that had watched empires be born and die of their own paperwork. "Your oath, Swordspeaker. Sworn in starlight, at the height of the old war, binding your speech and your life and your name. It is the last living oath of its kind. It is, quite literally, the finest meal remaining in the world."

"And if I give it?"

"Felix Iversen—" the magistrate paused delicately, "—no relation, I assume, though the courts enjoy coincidence—"

"He took my name," Felix said quietly. "Two years ago, when he apprenticed to me. Chose it himself." His ears had gone red. I hadn't known that. Fifteen years of knowing everyone's secrets and I hadn't known that.

"—Felix walks free," the magistrate finished. "His file burned. His family's debts dissolved. The claim voided, root and branch, before witnesses. And you, Oscar, walk free also. We do not want your servitude. We want only the oath itself, unmade, surrendered — a vow freely released back to the Sigil that first received it."

Silence. The hall drank it in. Somewhere far overhead, stone settled with a sound like a sigh.

It was perfect. That was the problem. I had spent three days looking for the trap and here it was wearing mercy's face, and I could not yet see the barb.

* * *

They gave us an hour to confer alone. Another courtesy. Another hook.

We gathered in an antechamber of white stone where a cold hearth held ashes that had never been warm. Felix spoke first, and his voice shook with hope, which frightened me worse than any threat.

"It's everything," he said. "You heard them. You give up the swordspeech — which you hate, which has been *poison* to you, you said so, you said it burns — and I go free. Both of us free. Why are you looking like that? Why is everyone looking like that?"

"Because it's clean," Maren said. She had her tools out despite everything, worrying a ward-pick between her fingers like a rosary. "Nothing in this mountain is clean. I rewired drainage lattices for thirty years, Felix. When a bargain looks seamless, it's because someone's hidden the seam where they mean to split you."

"Then show me the seam," Felix said, rounding on me. "Show me where it lies. Because from where I sit, the only person paying is you, and you've been paying for fifteen years anyway."

I looked at him — this boy who bought luck-knots he couldn't afford, who chose my name for his own, whose soul sat in a file drawer somewhere below us like a coat checked at a door — and I made myself say the thing I'd been circling since the thrones.

"The oath isn't mine to give."

Tallow's head came up. Brannic frowned. Felix just stared.

"When I swore it," I said slowly, feeling my way through the dark of my own memory, "I swore it *on* something. That's how the old oaths worked — starlight-sworn, anchored. You don't just promise; you stake. My speech, my life, my name — yes. But underneath all that, the anchor, the thing the vow actually rests on—" My hand had drifted to my sword-hilt without my noticing. I made myself stop touching it. "The oath is anchored in the Sigil itself. It was sworn *to* the seal, in the days when the seal still answered. My vow doesn't belong to me, Felix. It belongs to the thing starving downstairs. If I 'surrender' it, I'm not paying a debt. I'm handing a starving dog its own leash and calling it dinner."

"But they said freely given," Felix protested. "Freely given sustains it—"

"*If* it's freely given," I said. "*If* the giving is true. And how does a court of ancient hungry things verify a free gift?" I let the question hang. "They don't. They can't. Freedom isn't a thing you can weigh. So they'll take my oath, call it a gift, feed it to the Sigil — and the Sigil will taste the coercion soaked through it like rot in a wall, and it will not be sustained. It will be *enraged*. And then it takes Felix anyway, and the city after, and my oath besides. We lose everything, and they keep the receipts."

The antechamber was quiet. In the silence I heard it again — that faint scratching beneath the floor, the ever-writing pen, transcribing even our doubts.

"Or," Brannic said, very carefully, "you're wrong. And it's honest. And you're going to throw away the one clean exit this mountain has ever offered because you can't stop seeing knives." His voice cracked on the last words. "Some of us have already paid everything to this court, Oscar. Some of us would pay anything to see one soul walk out."

"I know," I said, and hated how gentle my voice came out. "Believe me, Brannic. I know exactly what this court costs. I've seen the bill."

He looked at me sharply — sharper than the sentence deserved — and I realized too late what I'd said, or rather what I'd revealed: that I knew the price of this court *personally*, from long ago, from a debt I'd never spoken of. His eyes went wide. Felix's followed. Even Tallow leaned forward.

But there was no time. The doors opened on their own, courteous as everything here, and a clerk stood bowing in the gap.

"The court will receive your answer," it said, in a voice like dry paper, "now."

* * *

We returned to the crescent. The six faces waited, patient as mountains, and the seventh throne stood empty, and the floor's ledger-ink crawled with my name.

"Well, Swordspeaker?" The central magistrate spread his hands, magnanimous, merciful, magnificent. "A soul for an oath. A boy's whole future, bought with the burden you've cursed for fifteen years. Name one reason to refuse."

And I could have named ten. The anchor. The rot. The trap coiled inside the gift. But standing there under those patient borrowed faces, I understood that naming reasons was losing — that every argument I raised, they had drafted replies to before my grandmother was born. This court did not win debates. It won *time*. It would talk until the new moon, and then the talking wouldn't matter.

So instead of arguing, I did the one thing they had not drafted a reply for.

I curtsied. Deeply. Mockingly. Sword-hand sweeping the floor like a duelist's salute.

"A fine offer," I said. "Generous. Almost believable. I accept — *conditionally*. I'll surrender my oath, freely and gladly, the moment you bring me before the Sigil itself and let me place the vow in its keeping with my own hands. No intermediaries. No clerks. No magistrates handling what they swear they only want to free." I straightened, smiling, letting them see I knew exactly what I was asking. "Unless, of course, the great court beneath the mountain is afraid to let a starving seal stand in the same room as the last swordspeaker alive."

The murmurs along the crescent stopped. For the first time, the six borrowed faces went utterly still — and beneath their stillness I felt something enormous shift its weight in the deeps of the mountain, the way the sea shifts under a ship.

The central magistrate smiled. It did not reach anything behind his face.

"Of course," he said. "The Sigil will receive you. Tomorrow, at the turn of the watch." He rose, and the whole crescent rose with him, and the black doors began to swing open behind us. "Rest well, Swordspeaker. You'll want your strength."

And as we turned to go, the pen beneath the floor scratched out one final line, loud enough, for the first time, to hear:

*A condition accepted. The trap re-set accordingly.*

I did not look down to read it. I already knew it was about me.
