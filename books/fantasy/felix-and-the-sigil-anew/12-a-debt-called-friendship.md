## 12. A Debt Called Friendship

The clerks of the under-court did not sleep, and so neither did their guests.

Felix learned this in the first hour of captivity-that-was-not-quite-captivity: a chamber of bone-white lanterns and a table set with bread that tasted of nothing, while somewhere above them the new moon climbed toward its full dark. The magistrates had given them rooms instead of chains, which was somehow worse. Chains would have been honest.

He could not sit still. He wandered the corridor outside the chamber they'd been assigned, trailing his fingers along walls of pale stone that felt warm as skin, and it was there — in an alcove he had no business opening — that he found the archive.

It was not locked. That should have warned him. Nothing in this court was locked; everything here was *filed*. The under-court did not need locks because it had paperwork, and paperwork was a cage with infinitely finer bars than iron.

Shelves rose into shadow, stacked with ledgers bound in leather gone soft with age. Lanterns burned without flame. And in the center of the room, on a reading stand worn smooth by centuries of hands, lay a single open book, positioned as if someone had recently been consulting it. As if someone had wanted it found.

Felix told himself later that he had only meant to look. He was a scribe; archives were his native country. He crossed the threshold the way water crosses a crack in stone — without deciding to, without any force he could have named.

The open ledger bore no title. Its pages were dense with the cramped, beautiful hand of some clerk long dead, and at the head of the first visible page stood a name.

*Iversen, O.*

Felix's breath went thin.

He knew that name the way he knew his own heartbeat now — fifteen days on the road will do that, will stitch another person into the fabric of your survival until you cannot remember which fears were yours originally. Oscar Iversen, who sold luck-knots for copper and never once let anyone see how much the magic cost. Oscar, who had drawn the sword-speech against hollow-men and bled from the eyes afterward and said *it's fine* in a voice that made it obviously not fine. Oscar, who had walked into this mountain two nights ago carrying Felix's debt like it weighed less than their own life.

He turned the page.

The hand changed partway through — younger, angrier, the letters slashed rather than written. It took him a moment to recognize why the script looked familiar, and when he did, his stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It was Oscar's handwriting.

*Let it be recorded,* the entry began, *that I, Oscar Iversen, swordspeaker, oath-bound under starlight and witnessed by my company, do here swear myself surety for the prisoner Wren Aldous, held beneath this mountain against all law of sky and season. Let my sword-speech stand as bond. If the prisoner is not freed, let my oath be forfeit to the Sigil Anew.*

Below it, in the older clerk's hand, cold as a fish belly:

*Surety accepted. Term: one turning of the moon.*

Felix read it three times. The words did not change. That was the horror of writing, he thought distantly, the thing civilians never understood about his trade: ink was a promise that outlived the mouth that made it. Ink kept speaking after everyone who spoke it was dead or broken or fifteen years hidden in a night market selling charms for small coin.

There was more. Pages of it. He turned them with hands that had begun to shake.

Oscar had come here before. Not just come here — *petitioned* here. Sworn here. There was a whole correspondence, if you could call it that, between the living fire of Oscar's younger hand and the glacial patience of the court. Petitions denied. Appeals filed. A second oath sworn to extend the term. A third.

And then, near the end, an entry in the clerk's hand that made the lanterns seem suddenly very bright:

*Term expired. Prisoner Wren Aldous remanded to the Sigil Anew per contract. Surety's oath forfeited in part. Sigil notes hunger unassuaged. Sigil notes the taste of a freely-sworn vow and requests further.*

*Requests further.*

Felix stood in the archive of the court beneath the mountain, holding the proof that the person he trusted most in the world had done exactly what he was about to ask them to do — had sworn themselves for a prisoner in this place, had failed, had fed the Sigil a taste of something it had been starving for ever since — and had never said a word.

Not on the road. Not at the fire. Not in the village where the old woman had mistaken Felix for her dead son and Oscar had stood in the doorway with an expression Felix had read as pity but now understood was *recognition*. Not once in fifteen years of hiding had Oscar mentioned that the reason the Sigil Anew hungered, the reason the wards had fallen, the reason any of this was happening —

— was them.

* * *

He didn't remember leaving the archive. He remembered corridors, though — the way the under-court's halls seemed to rearrange themselves politely around your despair, offering you shorter paths to wherever you were going because the court found efficient grief useful. He remembered the lanterns, bone-white and patient. He remembered finding the chamber where Maren sat cross-legged on the floor with her smuggled tools spread out before her like a surgeon's tray, muttering to herself in ward-smith's cant, pretending very hard that her hands weren't shaking.

Maren looked up. Her face did whatever Maren's face did — the thing where every emotion arrived and was processed and dismissed within half a second, leaving only a kind of flinty calm.

"You look," she said, "like a ledger that's been audited."

"Where is Oscar?"

Something flickered behind her eyes. "Petitioning. Why?"

"I need to talk to them." His voice came out wrong. Too flat. "Alone."

"Felix." She set down her tools with exaggerated care, which was what Maren did instead of reaching for people. "Whatever you found —"

"It doesn't matter what I found."

"It always matters what you find. You're a scribe. Finding things is your entire personality." She stood, joints cracking, and studied him with those gray ward-smith's eyes that saw the structure underneath everything. "Listen to me. Whatever it is, it'll keep until morning. Nothing in this mountain keeps, but you can pretend for one night."

"No," Felix said. "I don't think I can."

She let him go. He was grateful for that, later — grateful she hadn't grabbed his arm, hadn't called after him, hadn't done any of the things that might have worked. Maren understood, in her flinty way, that some doors you have to walk through yourself even when you know what's on the other side.

He found Oscar in a side gallery off the petition hall, alone, standing before a niche where a lantern burned slightly whiter than the others. They were talking to it. Or praying to it. Or doing whatever Oscar did instead of either of those things — standing very still with their jaw set, addressing the universe in the tone of someone filing a complaint.

"Oscar."

They turned. In the white light their face looked older than it had on the road, carved rather than lived-in, and for one absurd moment Felix thought: *they look like the ledger.* Like ink that had outlived its speaker.

"We need to talk," he said.

"I know that tone." Oscar's mouth twisted. "That's a scribe's tone. That's 'I've read something' tone."

"Wren Aldous."

Two words. He watched them land. Watched Oscar go still in a way that no living person should be able to go still — not frozen, not startled, but *stopped*, like a clock with the weight removed.

"Where did you see that name?" Quiet. Dangerous-quiet, the quiet before the sword-speech.

"The archive. Your petitions. Three oaths, Oscar. Three oaths sworn for a prisoner I've never heard you mention, ending with the Sigil noting —" his voice cracked and he hated it — "*noting the taste of a freely-sworn vow and requesting further.* Do you want to explain that? Do you want to explain that the reason we're all down here, the reason the stars went out, the reason that thing in the heart of this court is starving — is because you already tried this? Because you already *failed* this?"

"Felix —"

"Were you ever going to tell me?" He heard his own voice rising and couldn't stop it, fifteen years of chancery discipline shredding like wet paper. "On the road? After? When I signed my apprenticeship in your shop and you watched me sign it — were you going to mention that you'd stood exactly where I'm standing and sworn exactly what I need you to swear and lost? That I'm not the first person you've —"

He stopped. Because the sentence he'd been about to say had finally become audible to him, and it was:

*That I'm not the first person you've tried to save.*

"That's what this is," he said, quieter now, and the quiet was worse. "I'm the second attempt. I'm the — the revision. You couldn't save Wren Aldous so you found another soul in a ledger, another collateral, another file with a seal on it, and you thought, *this time, this time I'll get it right.*"

"That is not —"

"Aren't I?" His eyes were burning. "Tell me I'm wrong. Look me in the face and tell me I'm wrong, Oscar, and I'll believe you. I'll believe anything you say. That's the problem. That's the whole problem — I *would*."

And Oscar — proud, stubborn, impossible Oscar, who had faced hollow-men and magistrates and the collapse of every ward they'd ever hidden behind — opened their mouth.

And said nothing.

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Felix counted them; he was a scribe, counting was what he did. Three heartbeats of silence from a person who had spent fifteen years teaching him, without ever saying it, that silence was where the true contracts hid.

"There it is," Felix whispered.

"Felix, listen to me. What I feel about you has nothing to do with —"

"Don't." He backed up a step. "Don't say what you feel about me right now. Please. Because if you say something kind I will stay, and I can't stay, I can't *think* in here, everything smells like ink and old promises —"

"Where will you go?"

"Anywhere. I'm a scribe. I'm *collateral*. Apparently I travel well." The joke came out jagged and he hated himself for making it. "I need — I need to not be looked at. That's all. I need one hour where nobody in this mountain knows where I am."

Oscar's hand twitched toward him — toward his shoulder, maybe, or just toward him — and stopped. Fifteen years of practice at not touching people, deployed at the worst possible moment.

"One hour," Oscar said. "Then I come find you. That's not a request, Felix. That's a promise, and I don't make those lightly anymore. You of all people know what mine cost."

Felix laughed — a horrible, wet sound — and turned, and walked away down the bone-white corridor with the lanterns bowing politely out of his path, and did not look back.

* * *

He got lost on purpose.

That was the trick of the under-court, he realized as he walked — it responded to intent. Want to find the archive and it arranged itself for finding. Want to be nowhere and it gave you nowhere: corridors that doubled back, staircases that descended into descending, galleries full of lanterns burning over desks where no one sat. The court was a bureaucracy, and bureaucracies had a genius for empty rooms.

He ended up — or was ended-up, the distinction mattered less the longer he walked — in a records hall vaster than any chancery above ground. Shelves like cliff-faces. Files stretching back further than Grayhollow had existed. Somewhere in this infinite paper country was his own file; Tallow had described it to him on the road, with a thief-priest's relish for other people's damnation: *your whole life, lad, indexed and cross-referenced, waiting for the new moon to stamp it CLOSED.*

His whole life, filed. His whole life, one entry among millions.

And Wren Aldous's file was in here too. Somewhere. Closed, presumably. Stamped and shelved and finished fifteen years ago, the way Oscar's failure was finished, the way — his mind supplied helpfully — *his own file would be finished in three days if he didn't do something.*

He sat down on the cold floor between two shelf-cliffs and put his head in his hands.

Here was the thing he could not stop circling, the wound under the wound: it wasn't that Oscar had lied. Lies he could have handled; lies were currency in Grayhollow, everyone traded in them. It was the *shape* of the truth. That Oscar's kindness had a template. That there had been a before-Felix, a first draft, a prisoner whose name Oscar still prayed at in white-lantern niches — and that Felix had been chosen not because he was Felix but because he fit the pattern. Collateral. Ledger-seal. Soul sold to the court beneath the mountain. *A replacement for a ghost.*

And worse — worse, the truly unspeakable part, the part he kept trying not to look at directly the way you try not to look at the sun:

Wren Aldous had failed to be saved. Which meant the Sigil had tasted Oscar's vow and found it good and hungers for it still. Which meant that if Oscar swore again — for Felix, this time, with fifteen years of grief sharpening the blade — the Sigil wouldn't just take the oath.

It would take *all* of Oscar. Every year of hiding, every charm sold for small coin, every careful not-touching, every dawn they'd survived by habit instead of hope. The Sigil had been starving fifteen years for exactly this meal, and Felix was not a person to it. Felix was *bait*.

He was bait. He was a revision. He was the second copy of a document whose original had been lost, and everyone in this mountain — the magistrates, the Sigil, maybe even Oscar — knew it except him.

"Stupid," he said aloud, and his voice fell flat in the vastness. "Stupid, stupid —"

"You're talking to the files," said a voice. "They never answer. Believe me. I've asked."

Tallow materialized out of the shelf-shadow the way he did everything — soundlessly, irreverently, smelling faintly of incense and pickpocketed silver. The thief-priest lowered himself onto the floor beside Felix with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and produced from some inner pocket a flask, and offered it.

Felix waved it off. "How did you find me?"

"Didn't. The mountain did. It likes Tallow; I'm the most interesting thing that's happened to it in a century." He drank, grimaced, recapped the flask. "Also your Oscar has been pacing the petition hall like a caged wolf telling anyone who'll listen — which is no one, magistrates being famously deaf — that they have an appointment to go fail at. So. Either you told them where you'd be, or the mountain's playing matchmaker again."

"They didn't lie to me," Felix said. "That's the worst part. They just... never said it."

"Mm." Tallow examined his own knuckles. "You want to know a secret about oaths, scribe? Real ones, the starlight kind? They're not promises. Promises are things you make. Oaths are things that make *you*. Oscar's been made by that vow for fifteen years — shaped around the hole where Wren Aldous used to be. You don't tell someone about a hole like that. You build your house around it and you nail the door shut and you pray nobody ever asks what's behind it."

"And I asked."

"And you asked." Tallow shrugged. "Doors get opened. It's practically your job."

Felix pulled his knees up. "I'm bait, Tallow. That's all I am down here. The Sigil doesn't want *me*, it wants what comes running for me. And Oscar's going to give it. They're going to swear again, and this time the Sigil won't take a taste, it'll take the whole —"

"Lad." Tallow's voice went gentle, which was somehow more alarming than his mockery. "Can I tell you what I saw? Night we camped at the warded road, before the hollow-men. You were asleep. Oscar sat up on watch, and they took out that luck-knot you bought from them the first night in Grayhollow — the one you couldn't afford, the one they pretended was mispriced — and they held it like it was the last honest coin in the world. And they said — I wasn't eavesdropping, I was *praying*, it's a different sin entirely — they said, 'I don't care what it costs. Not this one. This one gets to live.'"

Felix stared at him.

"Not-Wren," Tallow said. "Not-a-revision. *This one.* You want to know the difference between you and a ghost, scribe? Ghosts don't buy charms they can't afford. Ghosts don't run back into courts to face their own damnation so a city full of strangers keeps breathing. Ghosts don't walk away from the one person who'd burn the world for them because they're scared of being loved for the wrong reasons." He stood, knees cracking, and looked down with an expression that on a holier man would have been compassion. "You're scared of being loved for the wrong reasons. Congratulations. That means you love back for the right ones. Now — are you going to sit here cataloguing your inadequacies, or are you going to go be found? Because your hour's nearly up, and Oscar Iversen keeps their appointments."

Felix didn't move. "What if they swear again, Tallow? What if saving me costs them everything the way it almost did before?"

"Then that's their oath to break or keep," Tallow said. "Same as yours is yours." He paused at the edge of the shelf-shadow. "One more thing. Maren sent me to find you, and Maren doesn't send messages unless they matter, so listen: she says the lattice is wrong. The ward-work in this court — she says it's built to take two payments and record one. Whatever bargain the magistrates offer, check the arithmetic. Scribes are good at arithmetic."

He was gone between one lantern and the next.

Felix sat alone in the hall of closed files, and slowly, slowly, the shaking stopped — not because the hurt was gone, but because something else had grown up around it, the way scar tissue grows around a wound that isn't finished being a wound.

*Maren doesn't send messages unless they matter.*

*Built to take two payments and record one.*

He was a scribe. Reading contracts was what he did. And somewhere in this mountain, in the next few hours, the magistrates were going to present a bargain — Felix's freedom for Oscar's oath — dressed in mercy, sealed in good faith, and Maren was telling him the numbers would not add up.

The jealousy was still there. The hurt was still there. Being a revision, being bait, being second — none of that would heal tonight, maybe not for years, maybe not with all the honesty in the world.

But he could count.

Felix stood up in the hall of closed files, in the city of bone-white lanterns, three days before the new moon, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and started walking — not away this time, but toward the petition hall, toward the wolf pacing its cage, toward the arithmetic of a bargain that smelled wrong.

Behind him, in the dark between the shelves, his own file stirred on its hook — and stamped itself, softly, with a word no clerk had written:

*PENDING.*
