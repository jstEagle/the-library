## 13. The Sigil Anew

The magistrates led us down through the court's heart the way mourners walk behind a coffin — slowly, in silence, with a reverence that made my skin crawl. I counted the turns because counting was something to do with my hands when my hands wanted to shake. Forty-one steps. A bridge of black glass over a canal of nothing at all. Forty-one more steps. And then the corridor opened like a throat.

I had expected a chamber. What I found was a wound.

The hall was vast and round and wrong, its walls ribbed with pale stone that pulsed faintly, the way a throat pulses when someone is swallowing. Bone-white lanterns hung from chains so long their origins vanished into darkness above, and each one burned with a light that had no warmth in it at all — light like the memory of light, kept too long. The floor sloped gently downward toward the center of the room, and at the center, floating a hand's breadth above the stone, hung the Sigil Anew.

It did not look like what the old stories said. The stories called it a seal, a stamp, a mark pressed into the flesh of the world by gods who wanted their promises kept. What hung before me was a living knot of vows — interlocked rings of script turning slowly around one another, thousands upon thousands of oaths written in no alphabet I knew, each ring bound through the next, the whole of it revolving with the slow patience of something that has been hungry for a very long time. Where the rings crossed, they flared. Where they frayed, they wept threads of grey light that drifted upward and dissolved.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. It was beautiful the way a glacier is beautiful, or a fire seen from across a valley — beauty with no interest whatsoever in whether you survive it.

And it was starving. That was the second thing, and I felt it before I understood it, the way you feel a draft before you find the broken window. The Sigil's hunger came off it in waves, cold and enormous, pressing against my chest like deep water. Fifteen years since the stars went out. Fifteen years since anything sworn beneath starlight had come down here to feed it. It had been eating scraps — sold souls, desperate bargains, Felix's family's debt — and scraps were not enough. The frayed rings told me that. The weeping light told me that. Something built to be fed by oaths sworn under honest stars had been living on table scraps and lies for fifteen years, and it was dying of it, and a dying thing that size does not die quietly.

"Oscar Iversen." The magistrate who spoke wore the face of a gray-haired woman, kind-eyed, grandmotherly. The voice underneath it was older than the mountain. "You are brought before the Seal."

"I'm brought," I said. My voice sounded small in all that swallowed space. Behind me I heard Maren's breath go ragged, heard Tallow murmur something under his tongue that was either a prayer or a curse, and with Tallow it was usually both. They had insisted on coming. The magistrates had allowed it, which should have warned me. Nothing in this place was allowed out of mercy.

"You swore an oath once," the grandmother-face said. "Under open sky. We have kept it. We have kept it *carefully*." And the Sigil turned — the whole vast knot of it, rings within rings, rotating until one thread among its thousands brightened and reached toward me like a finger.

I knew that thread. Of course I knew it. I had spoken it into the dark above Grayhollow on the last night the stars were honest, kneeling in the ruins of the border wards with my sword flat on the stones and my blood still wet from the fighting: *By starlight and steel, I swear my speech to silence until the seal is satisfied.* Fifteen years I had carried those words like a splinter worked down to the bone. I had thought them mine. Standing there, I learned better. They had never been mine. They had been *filed*.

"That's not possible," Maren said behind me, sharp as a struck flint. "Oaths belong to the swearer. That's the whole — that's the entire *point*—"

"Belong," the magistrate repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is what your kind believes." The kindness in the borrowed face did not waver, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "Come closer, swordspeaker. The Seal wishes to show you something. It has waited long to show someone who can understand."

Every instinct I owned — every instinct that had kept me alive through fifteen years of luck-knots and fever-charms and looking over my shoulder — screamed at me to stand exactly where I was. I walked forward anyway. Because Felix was somewhere below us in the clerks' warrens, his soul signed away in a ledger with the Sigil's own mark, and because the new moon was two nights off, and because understanding the thing that held him seemed like a better use of my feet than running from it.

The hunger thickened as I approached. By the time I stood ten paces from the turning rings it had weight, real weight, leaning on my shoulders like snow on a roof. Up close I could see that the script was not inked or carved but *growing*, each vow a living filament, and I could see — this was the part I would carry out of that hall if I carried nothing else — I could see that some of the filaments were people. Or had been. Oaths so thoroughly absorbed into the seal that the words and the souls who'd spoken them had become the same substance, wound together forever, indistinguishable. Somewhere in that turning mass was a thread that had once been a woman named Sera Vane, whom I had failed to save. I looked for her. I couldn't tell one thread from another. That was the revulsion, arriving right on schedule, entwined with the awe like roots through a wall: the Sigil did not keep promises. It *became* them.

"Look," said the magistrate, and all the magistrates said it at once, a dozen borrowed voices in unison, and the Sigil opened.

There is no other word for it. One of the great rings parted like an eye deciding to see, and through it poured not light but *consequence* — a vision laid directly into my mind, vivid and total, the way only true magic is vivid, the way my swordspeech used to be before I buried it.

I saw Grayhollow.

Not the Grayhollow I knew. This one was quiet. That was the first wrongness — Grayhollow is never quiet; it grumbles and haggles and sings badly even at three in the morning. This Grayhollow stood silent under a sky with no stars and no moon, and its people moved through the streets like sleepwalkers, faces smooth, eyes fixed on nothing. I saw the night market where I have sold charms for fifteen years, and the stalls were empty, and the charm-sellers stood behind them doing nothing, because wanting things requires a self and these people had none left to want with. I saw the chancery where Felix had copied his ten thousand pages, its windows dark, its records burning in the street for no reason anyone remembered. I saw a woman standing at a well she no longer knew how to use, holding a rope she no longer knew why she held.

The village we had passed through on the road — the village that forgot its name — but scaled up. A whole city. Forty thousand souls, hollowed like gourds, walking and breathing and serving, every one of them claimed to pay a debt that was never theirs.

"This is the arithmetic," the magistrates said, gentle as lullabies. "The Seal must be fed. It has been fed poorly for fifteen years, and its hunger grows, and when the new moon comes it will collect what it is owed. One soul was promised. But one soul is a drop, swordspeaker, and the Seal is dying of thirst. You have seen what it does when it takes a village. Now you have seen what it does when it takes a city."

"The contract says willingly or the whole city," I said. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that, later. "Maren read the clause. If the payment isn't willing, it takes everything."

"Your ward-smith reads well." A pause, and in the pause the vision shifted, and I saw the second future, and this one was worse because it was subtler. I saw Felix — alive, walking, smiling, working at a clerk's desk in the bone-white warrens below — and he was happy. That was the horror of it. The Sigil did not need to hollow him by force. It could simply wait for the new moon, take what it was owed, and let him keep the shape of himself the way a lantern keeps the shape of a flame after the flame is gone. He would sign his papers. He would smile his smile. And the boy who bought a fever-charm he couldn't afford because an old woman at the stall looked cold — that boy would be a filament in the turning rings, wound forever into a seal that had never once cared about him.

"So," I said. "You show me the stick. Where's the carrot?"

The magistrates smiled, all of them, all the borrowed faces at once, and I understood that I had just made an error, though I didn't yet know what kind.

"There is no carrot," the grandmother-face said softly. "There is only the truth, which you have not yet asked for. Ask it, swordspeaker. Ask what the Seal was *for*."

I asked. God help me, I asked.

And the Sigil showed me.

It showed me the world before the outlawry, before the purges, before Grayhollow nailed its anti-magic laws up like trophies. It showed me why seals exist at all: because oaths are power, and power spoken aloud under starlight does not simply fade — it *accumulates*. Every vow ever sworn beneath the honest stars had gone somewhere. For millennia it went into the Sigil, and the Sigil held it, and the weight of all that kept-word balanced against all the breaking-word, and the balance was called civilization. The Sigil was not a predator. It was a *ledger* — the original ledger, the one all paper ledgers imitate — and it fed on sworn souls because a soul freely given in payment was the only currency it could not counterfeit.

And then the stars went out. Not as an accident. As a *wound*. Someone, somewhere above, had done something to the sky itself, and the starlight-sworn oaths stopped flowing, and the Sigil began to starve, and a starving ledger starts collecting debts instead of recording them.

I thought of the border wards collapsing. I thought of the same night, the same hour. I thought: *someone planned this.* And I thought, in the next heartbeat, colder: *or someone is planning to make sure it looks that way.*

The vision closed. The great ring sealed itself. I stood in the cold light of the bone-white lanterns with sweat freezing on my spine and the taste of consequence in my mouth, and behind me Tallow whispered, "Well," in the voice of a man whose god had just been proven smaller than the problem, "that's put the wind up proper."

"The Seal has shown you its hunger," the magistrates said. "Tomorrow it will show you its price. Rest, swordspeaker. Eat. The court is generous to guests." A beat, and then, almost tenderly: "The scribe-boy asks after you. You may tell him you saw the future, and that in every version of it, he is present. He will find that comforting. They always do, at first."

They walked us back the way we had come, forty-one steps, the glass bridge, forty-one steps, and not one of us spoke until the door of our guesting-chamber closed behind us and the lock — there was a lock, of course there was a lock — clicked home.

Maren sat down on the bed and put her head in her hands. Tallow began, quietly, methodically, to unpack the smuggled tools hidden in the lining of his coat, laying each one out on the blanket like a surgeon preparing. And I went to the narrow window slit, cut high in the mountain's inner rock, where no sky could possibly show — and looked anyway, out of habit, out of fifteen years of habit.

No stars. No moon. Only the dark, and somewhere far above it, the city that would become the vision if I failed.

"It showed me futures," I said to the room. "Plural. Like it was shopping."

"Futures are bait," Maren said into her hands. "My grandmother always said: when a thing shows you what happens if you don't obey, ask yourself what it's not showing you."

"And what's it not showing us?"

Tallow looked up from his tools, and his face in the lantern-light was older than his years and calm as deep water. "The way out," he said. "Nothing in that whole court showed us a door. Either there isn't one—" he set the last tool down, a thin ward-pick that Maren had forged herself, back when her hands were steady, "—or somebody down here doesn't want us thinking about doors. Which means, love, that we'd best start thinking about nothing else."

I turned back to the window slit. Far down the corridor, faint through the stone, I could hear the Sigil turning, turning, the sound less like machinery than like breathing — slow, vast, patient, starving.

Two nights to the new moon. One bargain already offered and not yet accepted. And somewhere below us, Felix was asking after me, trusting me, and I had seen a future where his smile kept working after he was gone.

I put my hand flat against the cold rock and swore nothing at all. After tonight, I wasn't sure I'd ever swear anything again — except, perhaps, one thing, silently, to no seal that could hear it:

*I'm getting him out. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs.*

The Sigil, I think, heard me anyway.
