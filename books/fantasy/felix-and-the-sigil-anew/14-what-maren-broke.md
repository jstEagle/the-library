## 14. What Maren Broke

Maren had spent thirty years learning to read wards the way other people read faces — the small hesitations, the lies told in copper and salt. So when she stood in the gallery above the under-court's great hall, ostensibly admiring the lattice-work that held the mountain's ceiling together, she was not admiring it.

She was listening to it lie.

The magistrates had granted her access with a courtesy that should have been her first warning. *The lady is a smith of some renown,* they'd said, in voices like paper dragged over stone. *She may inspect our works, if it pleases her.* Nothing pleased them. Nothing displeased them either. They wore borrowed faces — a fat merchant's jowls here, a young woman's soft mouth there — and behind every face was the same patient hunger, the same ledger-keeping stillness. They wanted her looking at the lattice. That was the whole point of the invitation.

So Maren looked at the lattice. And while she looked, she listened.

Wards sang, if you knew how to hear them. Not with the ears — with the teeth, with the bones behind the eyes, with the old scar tissue where her first forge-burn had taught her that metal remembered everything done to it. The court's lattice sang low and constant, a drone like a hive of bees packed into iron. It was beautiful. She hated how beautiful it was. Somewhere in her chest, the drunkard who had spent fifteen years drowning the smith in cheap gin stirred and said, *you could have built this, once, if they'd let you.*

*Shut up,* Maren told her. *Count.*

She counted.

The lattice was a knotwork of oaths — she could see them now, glowing faintly through the bone-white lantern-light, each strand a vow someone had sworn and never unsworn. Thousands of strands. Tens of thousands. They braided together into cables, and the cables ran down into the heart of the court, into the chamber where the Sigil Anew hung like a wound in the world. Every cable fed the Sigil. Every oath was a meal.

And there — threaded through the whole braid like a black wire pulled taut — was Felix's name.

Maren's hands began to shake. She put them behind her back, the way she did when the tremor came, the way she'd learned to do in taverns so no one would know how far gone she was. Behind her back, she pressed her thumbnail into the meat of her palm until the shaking had something else to be about.

Felix's strand wasn't just present. It was *anchored*. Anchored twice over — once to the Sigil itself, which was expected, which was the debt, which was the whole reason they had come down the mountain. And once to something else. A secondary clause, woven so fine and so deep that only a smith who had spent three decades untangling other people's sloppy work would ever have found it.

She followed the second anchor with her eyes, strand by strand, breath held, while a magistrate drifted past below wearing the face of a girl no older than Felix and pretended not to watch her pretend not to watch.

The second anchor led to a holding-knot. And the holding-knot led to a clause written in the lattice's own grammar, plain as day to anyone fluent, invisible as sin to anyone who wasn't:

*When the swordspeaker's oath is surrendered, the claim upon the scribe persists. The payment shall be received in full and the collateral retained.*

They were going to take both.

Oscar would stand before the court, would speak their oath aloud and give it up — fifteen years of discipline, of silence, of a magic kept caged like a bird with a broken wing because the cage was all that kept it from tearing its owner apart — and the Sigil would drink it down. And then the magistrates would turn, courteous as executioners, and keep Felix anyway. Both payments. One bargain voided by a clause Felix couldn't read and Oscar wouldn't think to look for, because neither of them was a ward-smith.

Neither of them was her.

Maren walked slowly along the gallery, counting exits she already knew she wouldn't use, and thought about the wedding.

Not her wedding — she'd given that memory to the toll-gate two days ago, handed it over like a coin to a beggar, and now when she tried to recall it there was only a warm shape where it used to be, a doorframe with nothing behind it. She knew she had loved someone. She knew his name had started with a sound her mouth still made sometimes, half-formed, in sleep. The grief of losing the memory of love was a strange grief: it hurt without an object, like a bruise on a limb that wasn't there anymore.

No — she thought about *a* wedding. Any wedding. Because weddings were vows, and vows were knots, and knots could be tied and knots could be cut, and the difference between a marriage and a hanging was often just which end of the rope you were standing at.

Her hands had stopped shaking. That was worse. Her hands only went still when the smith in her woke all the way up, cold and clear and counting.

*You can't fix this,* said the drunkard. *It's their lattice. It's older than you. It's fed on ten thousand years of stolen souls and you're one woman with smuggled pliers.*

*Shut up,* Maren said again. But gentler this time. Because the drunkard wasn't wrong, exactly. She just hadn't finished counting yet.

* * *

Their cells — the court called them *guest chambers*, rooms of smooth grey stone with lanterns that never flickered and doors that locked with a sound like a swallowed word — were clustered along a corridor off the great hall. Maren made her way back along it at the pace of a tired middle-aged woman who had seen enough wonders for one day. Inside her sleeve, wrapped around her left forearm, were the tools.

Smuggled was a strong word for what she'd done. Smuggling implied hiding things past a search. No one had searched her. Why would they? What threat was a drunkard's little kit — awl, nippers, three spools of wire, a striker, a stub of chalk? The hollow-men at the gate had patted her down with hands like cold meat and found nothing worth taking. They didn't know that a ward-smith's real tools weren't steel. They were knowledge, and knowledge doesn't clink.

Well. Mostly not steel. The nippers were good nippers. Brannic had bought them for her in Grayhollow, the week before they left, pressing coins into her palm outside the chandlery without meeting her eyes. *For luck,* he'd muttered. From Brannic, an apology in its native tongue.

She reached her cell. The door sealed behind her. She sat on the stone shelf that served as a bed, folded her hands in her lap like a penitent, and waited for the corridor's sounds to settle into their rhythm: the hollow-men's rounds, slow as dripping water; the distant murmur of clerks; the hum of the lattice running through the mountain's bones like blood through a wrist.

Then she got to work.

The guest chambers had wards too, of course. Containment wards, but gentle ones — the court wanted its guests comfortable until the new moon. Comfortable guests didn't claw at doors. The ward on her room was woven into the doorframe, a simple tripwire of intent: *no soul passes out.* Simple. Sloppy. Centuries old and maintained by clerks who thought of wards as furniture.

Maren took out her chalk and drew, on the floor beside the bed, a diagram only she would recognize as anything but the idle scribbling of a nervous woman. Then she sat within it, closed her eyes, and did the thing she had sworn off doing for fifteen years, the thing that had cost her her trade and very nearly her mind:

She listened to the ward the way a smith listens to metal.

It sang its little song. *No soul passes out. No soul passes out.* And underneath the song, the way there is always a seam under paint, was the flaw: the ward named its constraint as *out*. It had never once considered *in*. Nothing came into these cells; why would it need to guard against that?

But Maren wasn't going to pass out of her cell. Not tonight. Tonight she was going to pass *through* — or rather, something of hers was, something that was not quite a soul, not quite a body. Fifteen years ago, in another life, she had been the finest ward-ghost-smith in the northern reaches: she could split a thread of her own intent loose from herself and send it walking along any conductive line — wire, water, a ward-lattice — like a spark along a fuse. The guild had called it *sending the ghost*. The guild had also called it forbidden, after the third smith burned out her brain doing it, and Maren had watched a friend of hers come back from a sending with her eyes full of static and her mouth full of someone else's name.

One thread. Small. Careful. Along the lattice lines that ran everywhere in this mountain like veins in a leaf.

She bit down on the leather strap she'd prepared, because screaming would bring the hollow-men, and sent the ghost.

* * *

Riding a ward-lattice felt like being poured. There was no body, no weight, only direction and the singing of the strands around her — thousands of oaths, each one a voice, most of them worn down to whispers. She passed through a strand that had once been a farmer's promise to his dying wife and heard only the last two words repeating, *the harvest, the harvest,* like a man pacing a cell. She passed a child's vow never to lie again, frayed to a single syllable. The Sigil ate these. Had been eating them since before Grayhollow had a name.

Don't listen, she told herself. Count.

She followed Felix's black strand to the holding-knot. Up close, the clause was even uglier than it had looked from the gallery — a beautiful piece of work, honestly, the kind of elegant malice she might have admired in a student, if students could still be flayed. The clause didn't just retain Felix after receiving Oscar's oath. It *transferred* him. The moment the swordspeaker's surrender completed, Felix's anchor would shift from the Sigil's hunger to the magistrates' direct keeping — and the magistrates, she now saw, had plans of their own. Threads ran from the holding-knot outward, dozens of them, toward a chamber deeper than the Sigil's hall. She brushed one with the edge of her ghost and recoiled.

They meant to make him a clerk. Not a served soul in the bureaucracy — a *clerk of the claiming*, one of those ancient offices the magistrates themselves filled, wearing his face, using his neat scribe's hand to write the next century of sold children into binding. They'd been short-staffed, she realized, with a nausea that had nothing to do with the gin. The stars going out had starved the Sigil, and the Sigil's starvation had starved *them*, and they needed new blood. Young blood. Blood that could hold a pen.

That was the trap inside the gift. Oscar would smell it eventually — Oscar missed nothing about oaths, it was the rest of life they missed — but eventually would be too late. The exchange was set for the new moon. Two days.

Maren gathered her ghost back along the lattice, careful as reeling in a line over deep water, and came home to her body with a gasp she strangled against the leather strap. Her nose was bleeding. Her hands, when she looked at them, were shaking so hard the chalk rolled off her knee.

*There she is,* said the drunkard, almost fondly. *There's the woman who broke the Vellum Bridge.*

The Vellum Bridge. Another thing she'd lost the details of — no, not lost, *spent*, traded away at the toll-gate along with the wedding, because the toll demanded true memories and she'd chosen which pieces of herself to feed into the dark. She knew she had broken something great once. She knew it had saved people. She knew it was why she drank. The shape of the deed remained; the deed itself was gone, paid out like coin, and she was living on what was left.

Fine. She'd been broke before. You worked with scrap. You always worked with scrap.

* * *

She slept two hours, dreamless, the way the exhausted sleep. Then she rose, relit no lamp, and began to plan the way she'd once planned bridges: assuming everything would go wrong, and building anyway.

The problem was simple to state and monstrous to solve. The clause lived in the holding-knot, and the holding-knot lived at the convergence of nine primary cables, in a junction chamber somewhere below the great hall, guarded — she'd felt them through the lattice — by wards keyed to the magistrates' own wills. She could not cut the clause. Cutting it would alert every magistrate in the mountain before the nipper's echo faded. She could not dissolve it; dissolution required authority she didn't have and time she liked even less.

But she didn't need to break it. She needed to *delay* it. She needed the transfer to fail — quietly, ambiguously, in a way that would take the magistrates hours to diagnose and longer to admit — between the moment Oscar spoke the unswearing and the moment the court moved to keep Felix anyway. Hours. In those hours, Felix could run, or fight, or do whatever brave stupid thing Felix was clearly going to do regardless of what anyone older and wiser advised. Hours were a lifetime, underground.

To delay a clause, you didn't touch the clause. You touched what fed it. The transfer required a clean handoff between anchors — Sigil to magistrates — and a handoff required synchronization. The lattice kept its time the way all great lattices did: with a master pulse, a heartbeat-strand run through the whole structure, every knot ticking to the same silent drum.

If the drum stuttered — if the pulse arriving at the holding-knot arrived *twice*, a hair apart, doubled and inverted so the knot received both agreement and refusal in the same instant — the handoff would deadlock. Politely. Invisibly. The clause would wait for a clean signal that never came, and the lattice would log the delay as maintenance lag, and clerks who thought of wards as furniture would spend their eternity of paperwork hunting a fault that looked, from every angle, like the mountain's own tiredness.

A stutter-loop. She'd built one once, across a river, in another life, to buy a fleeing company ninety seconds against an army. Ninety seconds had been enough then.

This needed more than ninety seconds. This needed a loop that would hold for hours against a lattice ten thousand years old, woven by hands greater than hers, maintained by hunger itself. It was like proposing to slow a glacier with a hairpin.

Her hands were shaking again. She watched them shake, and thought about Brannic buying her nippers and not meeting her eyes. About Tallow praying to a god that had finally answered, and what that answer had cost him. About Oscar, who had carried a broken oath for fifteen years and was about to carry it to the altar and let it be taken — taken, Maren now knew, *and betrayed*, unless she did something. About Felix, who had looked at her two nights ago across a supper of grey bread and asked, earnestly, whether she'd ever built anything that lasted.

"Kid," she'd said, "nothing lasts. You just decide what's worth outlasting."

Time to decide.

She unrolled her spools of wire across the stone floor. Three kinds: copper drawn thin as hair, silver-pated tin from a Grayhollow pawnshop, and — her last treasure, saved for a need she'd prayed she'd never meet — a single length of starfall-iron, cold to the touch even now, even down here where no star had shone in weeks. Starfall-iron remembered light. Fed a pulse of oath through starfall-iron and it rang with every vow it had ever carried, a chord instead of a note. Perfect for doubling a signal. Perfect for making a lattice hear itself twice.

She began to draw the loop on the floor in chalk: not a diagram for reading, but a diagram for *becoming*. Ward-ghosts could carry patterns. If she memorized the stutter-loop completely — every junction, every inversion, every hairpin delay — she could send it riding the master pulse and seed it directly into the lattice's heartbeat, no tools required at the far end. The wire was for rehearsal. The chalk was for rehearsal. The only tool that mattered at the junction chamber was herself.

Which meant she would have to be at the junction chamber. In person. During the exchange. With the magistrates assembled and the Sigil convulsing and whatever happened next happening next.

Her thumbnail found the meat of her palm again. Pressed. Held.

*You may not survive to use it,* the drunkard said, without cruelty. Just inventory. Just counting.

"I know," Maren whispered, to the drunkard, to the dead friend with static eyes, to the husband whose name began with a sound her mouth still made. "I've survived worse odds sober. Barely."

She picked up the copper wire and began the first hairpin bend.

* * *

Dawn didn't exist down here, but the court kept its own clock: the bells of the clerks' shifts, seven to a cycle, each one a little colder than the last. By the fourth bell, Maren had the loop rehearsed forty times and her nosebleed had dried to a brown crust she could feel cracking when she grimaced. By the fifth bell, she had memorized the route from the guest corridor to the junction chamber through the lattice's own map — she'd ridden it twice more, smaller ghosts each time, thinner threads, spending herself like the last of a purse.

By the sixth bell, footsteps stopped outside her door. Not the hollow-men's tread. Lighter. Human. A key turned — no, the lock simply *unswore* itself, which was somehow worse — and Felix slipped inside, pale as the lanterns, a stolen sheaf of papers clutched to his chest.

"Maren." His voice cracked on her name. "I found my file. I found what I'm — and there's something else, something in the marginalia, the magistrates are—"

"I know," she said. She set down the wire. Her hands, she noticed, had gone perfectly still. "Sit down, kid. And start from the beginning, because we've got until the new moon to break a contract that can't be broken — and I've just figured out how to make it late."

Felix stared at her. Above them, faint as a buried heart, the lattice pulsed on — steady, certain, already beginning, though it didn't know it yet, to stammer.

Maren smiled with cracked lips, and reached for the chalk.
