## 15. Tallow's Bargain with a Banned God

The god of small doors had been silent for thirty-one years.

Tallow counted. That was the first thing they taught you in the gutter-temples of the Hollow Faith: count everything. Count your breaths when the wardens come. Count the coins you lift from a drunkard's purse so you can leave one behind for luck. Count the years since your god last spoke your name, because if you stop counting, you have to admit He might be gone entirely.

Thirty-one years, four months, and eleven days since the night the Grayhollow council burned the last shrine and put His name on the banned list between *plague* and *treason*. Thirty-one years since Tallow, then a boy of nineteen with singed eyebrows and his mother's prayer-beads melted into his palm, stood in the ash and swore he would keep the faith alive even if the faith was only him.

He had kept it. Badly, sometimes. Drunkenly, often. But he had kept it — the stolen candles, the whispered liturgy over dice games and deathbeds, the little shrines built inside other people's houses without their knowledge, tucked behind loose bricks where the plaster hid them. A thief-priest. The only kind of priest Grayhollow would allow, because it never knew it had one.

And now he sat in the dark beneath a mountain that ate memories, holding a candle stub against his chest like a knife he meant to use on himself.

* * *

They had made camp in a side gallery off the bone-lantern city, a niche where the clerks' endless footsteps faded to an echo. Maren was somewhere below with her smuggled tools and her shaking hands, doing whatever quiet treason she did. Brannic stood watch on the stair, jaw set like a man paying down a debt one hour at a time. Oscar sat apart, turning their oath over and over like a coin they were deciding whether to spend or swallow.

Felix slept, finally, curled against his ledger as if the thing might be stolen back by the dead.

Tallow did not sleep. Sleep was for people whose gods answered.

He took out the candle stub — tallow, naturally; the god of small doors had always appreciated a joke at His priest's expense — and set it on the floor. No shrine. No altar. Just wax and wick and thirty-one years of counting.

"Right," he said softly. "You know why I'm here."

The flame caught on the first strike. That was something. In the village that forgot its name, when the old woman had wept over Felix's face and called him by her lost son's name, Tallow had prayed — really prayed, not the transactional muttering of a man hedging bets — and for the first time in three decades, something had *answered*. Not words. Not light. A warmth behind his sternum, a sense of attention vast and gentle and unbearably sad, like being looked at through a window by someone who could not come outside.

His god was still there. Starving, but there.

"I've done the sums," Tallow said to the flame. "You taught me that. Count everything. So here's the count."

He held up one finger. "One miracle. One door out of this mountain that opens only for the willing — no hollow-men following, no magistrates slipping through, no toll collecting. A door with our names on the other side of it."

A second finger. "Two: the price. You know what I'm offering. Don't make me say it twice, You never liked repetition."

But he said it anyway, because a bargain unwitnessed is no bargain at all, and even a banned god deserved proper form.

"My priesthood. All of it. Every prayer I ever kept, every shrine I ever hid, every stolen candle and muttered rite. I'm Your last anchor in the world, old friend. The last mouth that knows Your name unburned. When I give that up—" his voice caught, and he let it, because lying to a god was worse than lying to yourself "—when I give that up, You go out. Like a candle in a draft. And I stay here, warm and living and empty, and I won't remember what I'm missing, which is the cruelest part, and I think You planned it that way, You sentimental bastard."

The flame bent sideways, though there was no draft in the mountain. It bent toward him, the way a dog leans into a hand.

Tallow laughed, wet and cracked. "There You are."

* * *

The warmth came up through the stone before the voice did. It came the way it had in the village — not sound but pressure, not language but meaning, pressing against the inside of his skull like a forehead pressed to his.

*Little thief,* said the god of small doors. *You always did lead with the insult.*

"You always did answer to it." Tallow kept his voice low. Felix slept ten feet away; some things should not be witnessed by the young. "I need to know You can actually do it. A door. Out. Only for the willing. Can You?"

Silence. Then, slow as sap rising:

*I am the god of thresholds. Of the gap under the door and the latch that gives. Of every way out that was never meant to be found.* A pause, and the sadness deepened until Tallow's chest ached with it. *Once. Before they burned the doors.*

"They didn't burn all of them," Tallow said. "I know. I checked. There's a door in the chancery cellar nobody remembers, and a gate in the wall behind the tannery, and—"

*You kept count.*

"Count everything."

*My faithful fool.* The warmth wrapped around him, and it felt — this was the unbearable part — it felt *grateful*, and gratitude from a god is a weight no mortal spine was built for. *Yes. I can open such a door. Here, even here, where the Sigil's hunger gnaws the walls. A threshold is a threshold, and I was old when the mountains were young. But You must understand what You are asking me to spend.*

"I understand."

*Do You?* The god's attention sharpened, and for a moment Tallow felt himself seen whole — every theft, every kindness, every drunk night and every dawn prayer mumbled into a cup, all of it laid open like a ledger. *You are not offering Your life. Death I could work with. Death leaves a ghost, and ghosts remember. You are offering Your devotion. The thread itself. When it is spent, there will be no grief in You, because grief requires the shape of what was lost, and the shape will be gone. You will walk out of this mountain free, and You will pass a locked door someday, and You will feel nothing at all. Is that a price? Or is it just erasure with better manners?*

Tallow looked at the candle flame. Steady now. Patient.

"You're trying to talk me out of it," he said. "That's new."

*I am trying to be honest with You. It is the least I owe the last man who kept My name.*

"Well, stop." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the words came out of him the way blood comes out of a wound — steady, warm, unstoppable. "You want honesty? Here's mine. I have been a priest alone for thirty-one years. Do You know what that is? It's being a lighthouse keeper for a ship that sank. I've kept Your feast days in cellars with no one to feed. I've blessed thieves on their way to jobs that got them hanged, and I buried two of them myself, and I said Your words over the holes because no one else in this rotten city would. And every year You got quieter, and every year I told myself: He's resting. He's conserving. A god of small doors knows about conservation.

"But I'll tell You what I figured out down here, in this mountain full of paper souls." He tapped his own chest. "Faith isn't a hoard. I treated it like one — buried it, saved it, kept it safe. That's not what it's for. Felix's family sold him because they thought a soul was capital. The magistrates down here run damnation like a countinghouse. And I nearly did the same thing, just holier about it. Kept You locked in my chest like coins."

The flame flickered. The god said nothing, which from a god was a roar of attention.

"Faith is currency," Tallow said. "It's only worth anything when it's *spent*. So spend me. Spend all of it. One door, opened once, for people who choose to walk through it willing. That's a better miracle than thirty more years of me muttering in cellars. That's arithmetic even a magistrate couldn't argue with."

* * *

For a long time there was only the candle and the breathing of the sleeping boy and the distant rustle of the bone-lantern city, pages turning in an office that never closed.

Then the god spoke, and His voice had changed. Thinner. Closer. The voice of a man kneeling across from you rather than a presence filling the stone.

*If I do this,* He said, *it will not be clean. A door that opens only for the willing — do You know what that costs Me? Every lock in the world has a key. Every ward has a flaw. But willingness cannot be forged, bought, or bled out of someone. It is the one coin even gods cannot counterfeit. To build a door keyed to it, I must pour everything I am into the hinge. My name. My nature. My last ember. And when the door closes behind the last of You—*

He stopped. Gods, Tallow thought, should not have to stop. Stopping was a mortal infirmity. Something in him broke a little, hearing it.

"—then You go out," Tallow finished quietly. "I know. I said. We go together, old friend. That was always the deal, wasn't it? From the first day. You keep my doors open; I keep Yours."

*You were nineteen,* the god said. *Sooty and furious and praying with Your mother's beads half-melted in Your fist. I should have told You to run.*

"You should have. I'd have ignored You."

The laughter, when it came, was barely there — a tremor in the warmth, a candleflame's idea of a chuckle. And underneath the laughter, unmistakable now, unmistakable and terrible: weeping. Not sound. Pressure. The stone itself seemed to grieve, a slow seep of cold sorrow through the floor, through Tallow's knees, into his bones.

*Why?* the god asked, and it was not a god's question. It was the question of something small and cornered. *Why spend Yourself for these four? For a scribe You met a week ago? For a traitor on the stair? For a drunkard and a swordspeaker who outlawed the very faith You kept?*

Tallow considered. He was, by trade and temperament, a fast talker; he had talked his way out of nooses and into beds and past wardens twice his width. But you did not fast-talk a god at the end of things. You gave Him the plain truth or nothing.

"Because fifteen years ago," he said, "Oscar Iversen carried me out of a burning shrine on their back. They didn't believe in You. Still don't, probably. They did it because I was theirs — company, family, whatever word you want. And because Maren gave a stranger her wedding day yesterday without blinking, and because Brannic is standing on a stair right now waiting to die for people who haven't forgiven him, and because a boy down here sold his soul to save his family and never once called it a sacrifice." He shrugged. "I've spent my whole life stealing small things, You know that. This is the first big thing anyone's ever handed me worth giving away. I'm not going to die clutching it."

The silence that followed was different from all the others. It was the silence of a decision already made, gathering its courage to be spoken.

*Then witness Me,* the god of small doors said. *One last rite. The only one that matters.*

* * *

It did not look like a sacrament. It looked like a thief teaching a trick.

"Put Your hand flat on the stone," Tallow murmured, and realized as he said it that his hand was already moving, pressing palm-down against the cold rock beside the candle. "Any stone. Every stone is a door if You're desperate enough — that's the first lesson, remember? You taught me that when I was nine, hiding from the tithe-collectors in a neighbor's crawlspace. *Every wall is a door that hasn't decided yet.*"

*I remember,* the god said. *You were crying. You prayed to Me by accident. You didn't know any gods; You only knew You wanted a way out.*

"And You gave me one. Cracked mortar. Big enough for a skinny kid." Tallow's throat worked. "Cheapest miracle You ever worked. I've been spending the interest on it for forty years."

*Time to spend the principal.*

The warmth began to move.

Tallow had expected fire. He had expected, in his secret theatrical heart, light and thunder, a god going out like a supernova. Instead it was a *pouring* — everything he had hoarded rising out of him like water finding a drain, and each thing named itself as it left, so that he could not pretend not to feel it.

His mother's beads, melting. Gone — not the memory, worse, the *meaning* of the memory, the reason it had ever mattered. The crawlspace and the cracked mortar. The first stolen candle lit behind a loose brick. The liturgy of the Hollow Faith, every line of it, draining out of his skull like sand through fingers — he grabbed for the opening words and found smooth blankness where they had lived. The names of the dead thieves he had buried. The shape of the blessing he put over dice and deathbeds alike. The particular warmth of being *attended to*, of praying into a darkness that leaned close to listen.

"No," he heard himself whisper, and his hands shook on the stone. "Wait. Wait, I take it back, I—"

*No, You don't,* the god said gently, and the gentleness was the cruelest mercy Tallow had ever known. *You taught Me arithmetic, little thief. Hold the sum. Hold the sum, and let the numbers go.*

So Tallow held the sum. He did not know how he knew what the sum was — perhaps it was the last gift leaving, the final coin pressed back into his palm like a tip for good service — but he knew: *a door, out of the mountain, that opens only for the willing.* He held it the way you hold a rope over a drop, with everything else falling away beneath him. His priesthood. His god. His name for the god — gone, already gone, a hole shaped exactly like three syllables. His reasons. His forty years. His faith.

What remained was the sum, and a man, and a candle burning steadily on stone that had begun, very faintly, to glow.

*It is done,* said the god of small doors — said it thin, said it fraying, a voice with one thread left. *Hear the terms, so the mountain hears them too. There is a door in the deep stair, behind the seventh landing, where the rock was broken and badly mended. It will open for those who choose it freely — who walk toward it wanting out, owing nothing, dragged by nothing. It will not open for the coerced, the claimed, or the hunted. What the Sigil holds, the door refuses. What fear drives, the door refuses. Only the willing.*

"And after?" Tallow's voice came out rusty. "After we're through?"

*After,* said his god, *there is no after for Me. Only for You. Live loudly, Tallow. You were the best theft I ever made — a boy who stole his way into priesthood and never gave it back.*

The candle flame stood very tall, taller than a flame should stand, and bowed — actually *bowed*, Tallow would swear it to his dying day, except he would not remember — and went out.

The glow in the stone faded. The warmth lifted out of Tallow's chest like a hand withdrawn, and what it left behind was not pain. That was the horror of it. It left *room*. A great clean emptiness, swept and ready, where forty years of devotion had stood. He knelt there in the dark feeling absolutely nothing about the fact that he could not remember what he was supposed to be feeling nothing about.

Somewhere far above, dim through miles of rock, he thought he heard a latch click.

Felix stirred and sat up, rubbing his eyes. "Tallow? Why are you on the floor? Whose candle is that?"

Tallow looked at the burnt-out stub without recognition. "No idea," he said. "Someone left it. Sloppy of them."

He stood, brushed off his knees, and felt — distantly, the way you feel weather through a window — that he had forgotten something important. He decided it didn't matter. Nothing mattered much, really. Except the sum. He still had the sum: *a door, behind the seventh landing, for the willing only.*

"Tallow," Felix said slowly, staring past him at the cold candle stub, at the faint scorch-mark on the stone in the shape of a hand. "What did you *do*?"

Down the gallery, from the direction of the stair, Brannic's voice rang out — sharp, alarmed, almost hopeful:

"Somebody get down here. There's a door in the wall that wasn't there before. And it's *warm*."
