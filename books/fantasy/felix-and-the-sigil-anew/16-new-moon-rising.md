## 16. New Moon Rising

Felix felt the new moon arrive the way a man feels a hand close around his heart from the inside.

There was no moon to see — that was the point of it, the absence of it — but he had spent fifteen years in chanceries learning to read what was not written, and now he read the sky. The stars were still gone. They had been gone since the wards fell, and in their place hung a darkness so complete it seemed less like night than like an eye that had closed. Somewhere above all that stone and dark, the moon had slipped into its blind phase, and every contract in the world had turned one page.

Below, in the bone-white lantern light of the under-court, the clerks stopped writing.

Felix heard it first: the silence of ten thousand quills lifting at once. Then the sound of the mountain itself shifting, a long mineral groan, as if the court were clearing its throat. He stood at the barred window of the guest-cell they had given him — *guest*, they kept insisting, though the door locked from the outside — and watched the corridor fill with processions of magistrates moving toward the Hall of Seals.

They were coming for him.

He had known they would be. Maren had told him what she'd found in the ward-lattice, her voice low and shaking in the dark of the cell block: *they mean to take you and keep his oath both, boy. The bargain's rigged. Whatever Oscar offers down there, they'll pocket it and collect you anyway.* And Tallow had pressed his forehead to Felix's and said nothing at all, which from Tallow was worse than any warning.

So Felix had done the only thing left to a scribe whose whole life had been other people's words.

He had decided to write his own.

* * *

The cell door did not open for him. That was the first lesson of the under-court: nothing opened for you here; everything was *collected*. But Felix had been raised on ledgers, and ledgers taught him that every lock is just a clause waiting to be misread.

The corridor guard was a hollow-man, tall and wrong, its face smooth as an uninked page. It stood with the patience of furniture. Behind it, the guest-wing's antechamber held a duty-desk where a junior clerk — an actual soul, gray-faced and trembling, some debtor three centuries deep — recorded comings and goings in a hand so cramped it looked like frost damage.

Felix knocked on his own cell door. Politely. Three times.

The hollow-man did not move. The clerk looked up.

"I need to file a motion," Felix said through the bars. "Regarding my own claim."

The clerk blinked. "The claim is already filed. It convenes within the hour."

"Then I'm filing an amendment." Felix let his voice settle into the register he'd used for eleven years in the Grayhollow chancery, the voice of a man who handled paperwork so boring that arguing with it felt like more work than agreeing. "A voluntary appearance. Signed and witnessed. You know what happens if the claimant contests collection at the finalization — don't you?"

The clerk's pen hovered. "Contested claims require... additional hearings."

"Additional hearings." Felix smiled the smile of a man quoting scripture. "Before the full bench. With discovery. Do you want to explain to the magistrates why their clean collection tonight has sprouted *procedural delays*? Or would you rather accept a signed voluntary appearance and have the whole matter concluded before the new moon sets?"

It was beautiful, in a rotten way, how well the machinery worked when you fed it properly. The under-court ran on procedure the way a furnace runs on coal, and no one — not even the things wearing borrowed faces — wanted to be the clerk who slowed the docket.

The cell door unlocked itself. Of course it did. Somewhere in the lattice, a clause had been satisfied, and clauses were the only gods this place truly worshipped.

"Sign here," said the clerk, sliding a form across the desk with hands that shook. "And initial the waiver of escort."

"Waiver of escort," Felix repeated, and initialed it, because walking into the Hall of Seals alone was the entire point, and because a boy who had been sold once without being asked deserved, at minimum, to choose the route of his own delivery.

* * *

They let him walk the corridors unescorted, which told him everything about how certain the court was. Why post guards around a man walking willingly into the seal?

The bone-white lanterns burned along the walls in their endless rows, each one a soul doing its small eternal service, and Felix made himself look at them as he passed. Made himself see them. A lantern shaped like a young woman laughing at something only she remembered. A lantern that flickered in rhythms too much like breathing. His file, when he'd found it in the archive two nights ago, had listed his projected placement: *Clerical, Fourth Rank. Term: perpetual.* He had seen the desk they intended for him, in the vision the ledger pressed into his mind — a desk in a row of ten thousand desks, a quill that never wore out because the hand never died, writing debts forever for a court that never slept.

That was the future he was walking toward. Not away from — toward. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and he held onto it like a rope over a drop.

Because here was the thing he had not told Oscar. Here was the thing that had sent him running two days ago, when he learned about the prisoner Oscar had failed to save, the oath sworn and broken, the ghost whose place Felix suspected he'd been recruited to fill. He had run because he could not bear being a replacement. But running had carried him down through the archive levels, and in the archive he had found something else: the original claim against his family. And at the bottom of it, in the Sigil's own interlocking script, a clause Maren had missed:

*Should the collateral present itself freely at finalization, the debt may be discharged in kind — one willing soul for one willing vow.*

One willing soul. Not a taken one. The Sigil had been built to receive gifts, and everything it had ever been given since had been theft, and theft did not feed it, and so it starved, and so the stars went out, and so the village forgot its name.

Oscar meant to give it a vow. Felix meant to make sure the court couldn't take anything else along with it.

If he contested, they'd delay, they'd rig, they'd take him anyway and keep Oscar's oath besides. But if he appeared voluntarily — if the collateral walked in of its own accord and *asked* to be weighed — then the terms of finalization locked. No amendments. No additions. What was on the scale at the moment of sealing was all there would ever be.

He was going to put himself on the scale and stand on the other pan himself.

* * *

The Hall of Seals had no ceiling. That was the first thing that struck him — after the low warrens of the court, the hall rose and rose into raw mountain darkness, and far above, a shaft opened to the sky, and through it Felix could see the starless black where the new moon hid.

The magistrates sat in a crescent on thrones of fused wax and iron. There were nine of them, or perhaps three wearing nine faces; their features shifted like reflections in disturbed water. Before them, filling the floor of the hall like a lake of frozen light, lay the Sigil Anew.

Felix had expected a seal, a mark, a thing stamped on paper. What he saw was vast and alive: interlocking rings of script turning slowly against each other, lines of vow crossing lines of vow, and between the lines — hunger. It moved the way a starving animal moves, conserving, aching. Up close he could hear it, a sound below sound, like a word trying to remember itself.

"The collateral appears," said the center magistrate, and its voice was his father's voice, and his mother's, and the chancery master who had first taught him to hold a quill. "Freely. Unbidden. Interesting."

"Not unbidden," Felix said. His voice echoed absurdly in the vastness. He made it steady. "I filed a motion. You'll find it in order."

"We find it *unprecedented*," said another magistrate, and the crescent rippled with something that might have been laughter in a healthier organism. "Collateral does not volunteer. Collateral is collected."

"Then amend your definition." Felix walked forward across the hall floor, each step deliberate, until he stood at the edge of the Sigil's outermost ring. The script turned beneath him, and he felt it reading him — actually reading him, line by line, the way he had read ledgers all his life. It found the fear and noted it. It found the guilt and noted that too. It did not find regret, and at that, the great slow rings seemed to pause.

"I've read your founding clause," Felix said. "The one nobody uses anymore. *One willing soul for one willing vow.* You were built to receive gifts. Everything since has been robbery, and robbery doesn't feed you, and that's why the sky above us is empty." He lifted his chin. "I am here to correct the record."

The magistrates leaned in — all nine, or all three, or however many of them there were — and their borrowed faces sharpened with an eagerness so old and so vast it made Felix's knees hum.

"And what do you offer, little scribe?"

"Myself. Freely presented, per clause. Which locks finalization to the terms on the scale at sealing — nothing added, nothing amended. You know the rule better than I do; you wrote it." He swallowed. His mouth tasted of dust and courage. "But I didn't come to bargain. Bargaining is what got everyone here. I came to *testify*."

"Testify," the center magistrate repeated slowly.

"I've read the ledger of this court. All of it. Fifteen years a scribe — I read fast." Felix turned, slowly, addressing the crescent, the Sigil, the dark shaft of sky above. "You are owed a debt. True. Binding. My family incurred it and I honor the arithmetic — I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But the payment you were designed for was *willingness*, and willingness cannot be seized, only given. Every soul in those lanterns was taken, not given. You have been starving on stolen goods for centuries, and the proof is written across the whole sky, in the space where stars used to be."

The Sigil's rings shuddered. The sound below sound grew louder — that word trying to remember itself.

"So here is my testimony, entered freely: I give myself, and I ask in return only this — that when the vow comes, the one already offered to you, you weigh it against me honestly. One willing soul. One willing vow. Nothing else on the scale. No city. No lanterns. No amendments." He drew a breath. "Let the record show the collateral was asked, and answered, and chose."

Silence, of the enormous kind. Above, through the shaft, the blind moon crossed — he could feel it, a pressure like a thumb on the world.

And then the center magistrate smiled with a face that was almost kind, which was the most frightening thing yet.

"The record shows," it said, "that the court accepts the testimony... and notes an irregularity. The vow you speak of arrives *now*."

The doors at the far end of the hall boomed open.

Oscar Iversen stood in them, sword drawn, Maren and Tallow behind — and Felix's heart broke and mended in the same instant, because of course they'd come, of course they hadn't let him do this alone, that was the whole disease of loving people, they insisted on sharing your sacrifices whether you willed it or not.

"Felix," Oscar said, and their voice was the old fire and the new quiet all at once, "step away from the seal."

"No," Felix said. And for the first time in his life, the word was entirely, completely his own. "You had your turn to fail here. Let me have mine to *choose*."

Above them, through the mountain's throat, the new moon reached its zenith, and every contract in the world came due at once — and the Sigil Anew began, at last, to sing.
