## 19. The Starlight Clause

The silence after Oscar's unswearing was not silence at all.

It was a sound I had no name for — the sound of a word being pulled out of the world by its roots. I felt it in my teeth first, then in the hollow of my chest where my soul had been filed away like a deed to a house I'd never seen. The court beneath the mountain convulsed around us, its bone-white lanterns guttering in waves, and the magistrates screamed with voices that had been old when the mountains were young.

I had come back voluntarily. I had walked down that stair with my chin up and my knees shaking, choosing this over letting the Sigil take Grayhollow whole, and I had thought — foolishly, perhaps — that I understood what surrender looked like.

Then Oscar opened their mouth and taught me otherwise.

They stood on the dais of the under-court, in the circle of interlocked vows that was the Sigil Anew, and they had spoken their oath aloud — every word, every clause, fifteen years of it — and then, word by word, they had taken it back. *I swear* became *I swore*. *I will* became *I would have*. Each retraction landed like a hammerblow on an anvil the size of the sky, and each one cost them something visible: a flinch, a grayness creeping up their throat, their sword-hand opening finger by finger as though releasing something it had held so long it had grown into the palm.

And now the oath was unmade, and the Sigil was starving, and everything hung on what happened next.

"Felix." Maren's voice, low and urgent, at my shoulder. Her hands were black to the wrist with lattice-grease, and she was staring not at the Sigil but at the ward-lattice behind it — the vast web of silvered oath-threads she had spent hours rewiring in secret while the magistrates preened and plotted. "Look at the threads. Look at what's happening."

I looked.

The lattice was unraveling. Every thread that anchored a claimed soul — mine among them, a sickly gray filament running from my chest into the heart of the Sigil — was slackening, fraying, going dark. And as each thread died, somewhere far above us, I could have sworn I felt the mountain shudder with released weight.

"It's letting go," I said. My voice came out strange, thin. "It's actually—"

"Don't celebrate yet." Maren's jaw was tight. "A thing that starves doesn't die gracefully. It looks for food."

The nearest magistrate turned toward us. Its borrowed face — a woman's face, kind and forgettable, the kind of face you'd trust with your keys — was sliding now, the skin moving wrong, like water remembering it used to be a river. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the lanterns and the bones of the mountain and the gray thread still hooked into my chest.

*A payment has been made,* it said. *A vow freely unmade. The first in all the long accounting.* A pause, vast and hungry. *But a payment is not an anchor.*

The words dropped through me like stones through deep water.

"Anchor," I repeated. "What do you mean, anchor?"

*The Sigil must be bound to something,* the magistrate said, and other magistrates were turning now, their borrowed faces rippling, their attention converging on me with the weight of an avalanche deciding which way to fall. *It was built to hold oaths. It cannot hold nothing. If it is given no new anchor, it will make one. It will reach for the nearest souls. It will begin again.*

"Over my dead—" Tallow started forward, and Brannic — Brannic, who was supposed to be holding the stair above us, who had bought us these minutes with his own body — wasn't there to stop him, so Maren caught his arm instead.

"You don't understand what you're offering," she snapped. "You die, it takes the next soul. That's how hunger works."

Above us, the lattice groaned. Below us, the Sigil pulsed — and for the first time I saw it clearly, not as the abstract seal on a ledger or the mark that had haunted my family's debts, but as what it truly was: a living knot of vows, interlocked and interlocking, vast as a cathedral rose window and just as beautiful, and just as empty. Starved. Fifteen years since the stars went out, since the border wards fell, since anything sworn in starlight had fed it. It had been eating scraps. Sold souls. Paper promises. Me.

And now it had been offered its first true meal in a generation — a vow freely unmade — and it had swallowed it, and it was still hungry, because that was the nature of seals. They hold. Holding is all they know.

"It needs something to hold," I said slowly. The thought was forming the way frost forms, line by line, inevitable. "Not someone. Something."

Maren went very still beside me. I watched her arrive at the same place I was arriving, watched her eyes go to her own grease-black hands, to the lattice behind the Sigil, to the hours of quiet, trembling work she'd done in the dark while the rest of us bargained and despaired.

"The village," we said together.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the village whose name had been eaten, because Tallow had told us around a fire weeks ago, in the voice of a man describing a wound he couldn't stop touching:

The Sigil's hunger had passed through it once, long ago, on its way to somewhere else. It hadn't taken souls — the village was too poor, too far, too insignificant for the under-court's ledgers. Instead it had taken something smaller and stranger: every spoken memory. Not the memories themselves — the old woman still knew how to bake bread, how to mend a net, how to grieve — but the words for them. The names. The village could not say what it had lost because saying required the very thing that was gone. Children grew up unable to name their parents' graves. An old woman had looked at Felix and called him by her dead son's name because her son's actual name had been eaten, and some hollow shape of love remained, seeking any vessel.

But here was the thing about eaten things, Maren had realized sometime in the dark hours of her lattice-work, with her smuggled tools and her shaking hands: they do not stop existing. They are only displaced. Somewhere in the architecture of the Sigil, in the cold archive of its hunger, those memories still sat — filed, catalogued, owned. Along with every other truth the Sigil had ever swallowed. Along with, I realized, my own family's debts, and the true clauses of my sale, and fifteen years of Oscar's hidden life, and Brannic's confession, and Maren's wedding day, surrendered at the gate as toll.

The Sigil was not merely hungry. It was *full*. Full of everything it had ever taken and never digested, hoarded like a miser's coin, feeding on none of it, because stolen truths have no warmth in them. You cannot live on bread you've locked in a vault.

"A freely unmade vow," Maren said now, stepping forward, her voice steadying as the idea took full shape in her — I could see it happen, the drunkard's tremor burning off her like morning fog. "That was the meal. This is the pantry." She pointed at the lattice. "Your ward-lattice. I rewired it. You've felt it already — you're pulling threads loose from the claimed souls, and the lattice is taking up the slack. But I didn't rewire it to carry souls. I rewired it to carry *truth*. Returned truth. Given-back truth."

The lead magistrate's borrowed face stopped moving entirely, which I understood, in that moment, to be the under-court's equivalent of stunned silence.

*Explain.*

"Maren," Oscar said from the dais. Their voice was wrecked — scraped hollow by the unswearing, barely recognizable. But there was something in it that hadn't been there in fifteen years. Hope, maybe. Or the memory of hope, returned like the stars were supposed to return: dimmer, but honest. "Maren, tell them what you built."

She told them.

She told them about the lattice's original design — how it had been built, ages ago, to anchor the Sigil to the world through sworn souls, because sworn souls were the strongest currency the old courts knew. How the design assumed theft. How every architect since had assumed theft, because theft was easier, because theft required no consent, because the whole rotten system ran on the assumption that souls could be sold and memories could be seized and hunger could be fed by force forever.

"And it can't," she said. "Forever runs out. You're living proof — look at yourselves. Look at your lanterns. You've been starving in a vault full of gold because you never learned to spend it." She pulled a tool from her belt — a small, ugly, wonderful thing of salvaged ward-smithing, still warm from her hands. "I've rerouted the anchor-points. All of them. The lattice can hold the Sigil to the world without a single claimed soul. But it needs mass. It needs *content*. And there's a whole archive upstairs—" she gestured upward, toward the surface, toward Grayhollow and the eaten village and every stolen memory between here and the fallen border wards, "—just sitting there. Owned. Unused. Unloved."

"Give it back," I said. My voice cracked, and I let it. "All of it. Every memory you've ever taken. Release the claimed souls — release *me* — and anchor yourself instead to the returning. To the truths going home. Let the lattice feed you on *return* itself."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. In the under-court, I would learn later, three heartbeats of silence was a geological age.

*Feed on return,* the lead magistrate repeated. Something moved behind its borrowed face — not the sliding wrongness from before, but something older, wearier, almost like grief. *Truths given back are warmer than truths kept. That was known. That was known before the courts were courts.* Its ripple-face turned, slowly, to regard the Sigil — the vast, starving, beautiful knot of vows that had been its master and its prison both for longer than any of us had been alive. *It was built to receive freely given things. One freely unmade vow, and now this. It has waited a very long time to be offered either.*

"And if it refuses?" Tallow asked quietly. He had his godless hands folded, the way he did when he was praying to nothing, which I had learned was its own kind of faith. "If it's too far gone? If hunger's all it remembers?"

"Then it takes us anyway," Maren said flatly. "And we're standing here offering it the only alternative it will ever get. That's not a negotiation. That's a mercy with a deadline."

* * *

The Sigil considered.

I don't know how else to describe what happened next, except that consideration, for a thing like that, is a physical event. The lanterns dimmed. The air pressure changed. The gray thread hooked into my chest — the thread that was my soul, my collateral, my family's debt made flesh — began to hum, and through it I felt the Sigil thinking, vast and slow and terribly lonely, turning the offer over the way a starving man turns a stranger's loaf in his hands, checking for poison, checking for price, checking for the catch that every gift in the long history of the courts had always contained.

There was no catch. That was the whole point. That was what Oscar had taught it, minutes ago, at terrible cost: that a thing freely given back was possible. That a vow could be unmade. That the ledger could balance in a direction no clerk had ever imagined.

The thread in my chest went warm.

Then it went *loose*.

I felt it happen — felt the fraying filament that had run from my soul to the heart of the seal simply... release. Not torn. Not cut. *Unhooked*, the way a boat is unhooked from a dock by someone who intends, for once, to let the boat go. And with it went the weight I had carried since I first read the stolen ledger in the chancery and learned what my family had signed away — the weight I had mistaken, for weeks, for my own spine.

My soul came home to me like a bird to a hand it remembered.

I don't remember falling to my knees. I remember the floor being cold. I remember Tallow's hand on my shoulder, heavy and real. I remember looking up at the lattice and watching the gray threads die one after another after another — hundreds of them, thousands, every claimed soul in the endless bureaucracy above and below, every clerk in the bone-white city suddenly unhooked from their desks, their files, their slow damnation — and I remember the sound they made, all together, which was not a scream and not a cheer but something between a gasp and a dawn.

*It accepts,* the magistrate said. Its voice had changed. Everything about it had changed — the borrowed faces of all the magistrates were settling now, becoming still, becoming almost peaceful, ancient souls who had worn masks so long they'd forgotten their own features and were, perhaps, about to remember. *The claimed are released. The archive opens. The lattice anchors it to the returning — to every truth that goes home. It will feed on lightness. On restitution. On the specific warmth of a thing given back.* A pause. *It will never be full. Nothing that holds is ever full. But it will no longer starve on theft.*

"And the village?" I asked, pushing myself upright. My legs shook. I didn't care. "The memories it ate. The old woman's son's name. Does it — does it give those back?"

The magistrate regarded me for a long moment.

*The lattice carries them now,* it said. *Carrying is not returning. Returning requires hands.* And then, in a voice that had gone quieter than any sound in that court had a right to be: *Yours, scribe. Yours and your companions'. The Sigil can open the archive. Only the living can carry the truth home.*

Above us, faintly, through miles of rock, something shifted in the sky. I couldn't see it. But every soul in the under-court felt it — a change in the weight of the dark, the way a room changes when a lamp is lit in the next room over.

The stars were coming back. Dim, Maren had promised. Dimmer than before, but honest.

And below, all around us, the great archive of the Sigil Anew was opening its ten thousand drawers, and every drawer was full of names, and someone had to carry them home.

Maren was already checking her tools. Tallow was already weeping, quietly, the way he did everything. And I — soul whole, debt dissolved, file closed — took out the one thing I had carried out of the chancery on the night the stars went out, the stolen ledger that had started all of this, and I turned to a fresh page.

Someone had to keep the record. That was the one craft I had left, and it turned out to be enough.

"Start with the village," I said. "Start with her son's name."

Oscar, silent where their swordspeech used to live, nodded once — and pointed up the stair, toward the door Tallow's god had died to leave open, toward the long climb and the collapsing court and whatever waited for us in the honest dark under the returning stars.

We climbed.
