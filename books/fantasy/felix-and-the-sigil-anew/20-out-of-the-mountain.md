## 20. Out of the Mountain

The mountain was dying around them, and it knew it.

Oscar felt every tremor through their boots as they ran — not the slow breathing of stone that a swordspeaker learns to read in childhood, but something closer to panic. The corridors of the under-court had begun to fold inward like a hand closing. Behind them, down the long bone-white galleries, lanterns were going out one by one, and where each light died, the walls simply *stopped*, unraveling into a darkness that had no floor and no far side. The clerks' desks stood abandoned, contracts still pinned beneath inkwells, souls' names still ledgered in silver script that was fading even as Oscar ran past it.

"Left!" Maren shouted from behind them, ward-tools clattering in her satchel. "The lattice map says left at the second arch!"

"The lattice map is *dissolving*," Tallow called back, his voice ragged but steady. He had no god now. Oscar kept glancing at him anyway, the way you keep checking an empty scabbard out of habit. He looked lighter and hollowed at once, like a man who had spent everything he owned on a single throw of dice and hadn't yet seen where the dice landed.

"It's dissolving slower than we are," Maren said. "*Left.*"

They went left. Felix stumbled beside Oscar, his hand gripping their sleeve — not for balance, Oscar thought, but because he still half-believed that if he let go, the court would take him back, would find some clause, some codicil, some forgotten signature to drag him down into the bureaucracy of the dead. His file was ash now. His name was his own. But fifteen years of watching Oscar hide their magic had taught Felix what Oscar knew too well: freedom is a thing you have to keep re-earning from yourself, every hour, or it slips.

The stair rose ahead of them, cut into living rock, spiraling up toward the place Tallow had bought with the last of his god. *A door that opens only for the willing,* the banned god had promised, weeping as it spent itself. Oscar had seen many miracles in a long life and trusted few of them, but they trusted this one, because it had cost more than any miracle Oscar had ever witnessed cost anyone.

Behind them, the screaming started.

Not the magistrates — those ancient souls in borrowed faces had gone quiet when the Sigil convulsed, quiet in the way of things that are deciding whether to exist at all. This was worse. It was the sound of the court's machinery failing: the hollow-men, released in their hundreds, pouring up through the collapsing galleries with their empty faces and their tireless limbs, ordered to stop five fleeing thieves by any means remaining to a world that was coming apart.

"They're not chasing us," Brannic had said, an hour ago, before he turned back at the stair's foot with his sword drawn and fifteen years of debt in his eyes. "They're chasing the *door*. If the door closes—"

He hadn't finished. He hadn't needed to. They all understood what he meant to do, and none of them had been given time to argue him out of it, which Oscar suspected was exactly how Brannic had planned it. The coward's final trick: die so fast there's no room for forgiveness or refusal.

Oscar ran, and did not look back, and hated themselves for both.

* * *

The stair narrowed. The rock grew cold enough to burn bare skin, and then, strangely, warmer — as though they were climbing out of winter into the memory of spring. Felix gasped and pointed upward, and Oscar saw it: a seam of grey light high above, thin as a knife-cut, widening as they climbed.

"That's not the door," Maren said, breathless. "That's just the mountain's skin."

"No," Tallow said quietly. "Look again."

Oscar looked. The light wasn't grey. It was silver, shot through with something fainter, something that flickered — and Oscar's heart, which had spent the whole descent learning new ways to break, broke in a small and different way, because they recognized that flicker. Starlight. Real starlight, leaking down through a crack in the world like water through a roof.

"The Sigil's release," Maren whispered. "It's letting the sky back in."

They climbed faster. The screaming below grew fainter, then changed pitch — and Oscar, who had spent a lifetime listening to the grammar of violence, heard what it meant: the hollow-men had found something at the stair's foot that stopped them. Something willing. Something holding.

Oscar's legs nearly gave. Felix caught their arm this time, and said nothing, and that silence was the kindest thing anyone had done for Oscar in fifteen years.

* * *

The door was exactly where Tallow's god had promised it would be, and it was nothing like a door.

It stood at the top of the stair in a chamber of raw stone — a simple rectangle of darkness, taller than a man, edged in a thin line of gold that pulsed like a heartbeat slowing. No frame. No wall around it. Just a standing absence in the middle of the mountain's gut, and beyond its threshold, visible as through deep clear water: night sky. Open, endless, scattered with stars.

Dim stars. Stars worn thin, like coins passed through too many hands. But stars — honest ones, returning ones, each one burning with the stubborn modesty of a thing that has come back from being unmade and knows it will never shine quite as brightly as it did before, and has decided to shine anyway.

"Oh," Felix said. It was barely a word. It was the sound a person makes when grief and relief arrive in the same instant and cancel each other out into something that has no name yet.

Tallow walked to the door first. He put his palm flat against the darkness, and the gold edge brightened under his touch, and Oscar saw his shoulders shake once, twice, and go still.

"It knows me," he said. "Or it knows what I paid." He laughed, wet and cracked. "Fifteen years I prayed to a banned god and got silence. One trade and it builds me a door with its last breath." He turned to the others, and his face in the starlight was older than his years and younger than his grief. "It opens for the willing. That was the bargain. So when you step through — mean it. Whatever you're walking toward, whatever you're leaving, mean it, or the door won't have you."

"And if it doesn't have us?" Maren asked.

Tallow looked at her for a long moment. "Then the mountain keeps us," he said. "And the mountain is currently having opinions about keeping things."

Below them, far down the spiral, the screaming changed again — closer now, climbing. The hollow-men had gotten past whatever Brannic had become for them. Or perhaps they had simply run out of Brannic to fight, and the thought of that was a weight Oscar chose, deliberately, to carry later, in some quiet room, over some quiet drink, when there was time to fall apart properly. There was no time now. There was only the door.

Maren went first. She stepped up to the darkness, closed her eyes, and Oscar watched her lips move — not a prayer, not a spell, just a name, spoken once, softly. Herself, maybe. The woman she intended to be on the other side. The gold flared, and she stepped through, and was gone, and the starlight beyond rippled like a pond accepting a stone.

Tallow followed. Then Felix — but Felix hesitated at the threshold and turned back, and grabbed Oscar's sleeve again, hard.

"You're coming," he said. Not a question. Fifteen years ago it would have been a question; the boy who'd bought a charm he couldn't afford had learned, somewhere between a stolen ledger and a living seal, to make demands of the people who loved him.

"I'm coming," Oscar said.

"You'd better." Felix's voice cracked. "You don't get to do the noble disappearing act. Not after everything. I signed an honest contract with your stubbornness, Oscar Iversen, and I intend to enforce it."

For the first time since the stars went out, Oscar laughed — a short, broken, real laugh. "Get through the door, scribe."

He went. And then Oscar stood alone before the threshold, with the mountain groaning around them and the hollow-men's footsteps ringing up the spiral like a bell tolling backward, and did the thing they had been afraid to do since the Sigil screamed and the oath came undone.

They reached for their swordspeech.

Fifteen years it had slept coiled along their arm like a banked fire — the old words, the binding syllables, the language that lived in steel and spoke through blood. Even dormant, even outlawed, even buried under charm-knots and fever-luck and small coin, it had been *there*. A companion. A curse. The last living piece of who Oscar had been before Grayhollow, before the hiding, before the long quiet.

Oscar reached for it the way you reach for a hand in the dark.

And found silence.

Not dormancy. Not sleep. Silence — the specific, absolute silence of a room where a voice used to be. Oscar reached deeper, past habit, past desperation, down to the place where the words had lived since childhood, and found only smooth, clean emptiness, like a riverbed after the water is gone. The oath was unmade. The magic had gone with it. Every syllable, every binding, every burning remembered fire — spent, all of it, poured into the vow freely unmade, and the Sigil had drunk it down, and there was no taking it back. That had been the price. They had known the price. Knowing a price and paying it, it turned out, were separated by the whole width of a life.

Somewhere below, the hollow-men were close enough that Oscar could hear the wrongness of their breathing.

Oscar Iversen, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, stood at a door made of a dead god's love, and was — for the first time since they were six years old, since the first word had woken in their blood like a struck match — simply a person. A middle-aged charlatan with scarred hands and a bad knee and a shop full of luck-knots that worked by craft and coincidence and nothing else.

It should have felt like dying.

Instead, terrifyingly, it felt like setting down a weight so old they'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Mean it," Oscar said aloud, to themselves, in Tallow's borrowed words. And they meant it — meant the dim stars and the ruined city and the drunkard sobered and the thief-priest godless and the traitor dead on the stairs and the boy waiting on the other side of the dark — and stepped through.

* * *

The world on the other side was cold and enormous.

Oscar came out onto a mountainside under the open sky, knees hitting frost-stiffened grass, and the first thing they did — before breathing, before checking the others, before anything — was look up.

The stars were there. All of them, or nearly all, hung across the black like embers raked from a fire — dim, yes, thinned, some missing entirely where the Sigil's long hunger had eaten deepest, but *there*, real, wheeling in their ancient courses as though the past fifteen years had been a single cloud passing. Light fell on the mountainside. Honest light, from honest distances, asking nothing of anyone.

Felix was standing a few yards away with his head tipped back and tears freezing on his face, laughing without any sound in it. Maren sat in the grass with her ward-tools spread out around her, not working, just looking up, her hands finally still. Tallow stood at the treeline facing the sky with his hat in his hands, and when Oscar drew level with him, the thief-priest said, quietly:

"It's smaller than I remembered. The heaven part." He put his hat back on. "But it's realer. I'll take the trade."

Behind them, the mountain shuddered one final time. The peak above the hidden door groaned, slumped inward, and sealed itself with a sound like a giant exhaling — rock closing over rock, the under-court's mouth shutting at last, its debts uncollectable, its magistrates dissolved into whatever becomes of ancient souls when the contract that holds them is torn up. Somewhere inside, Oscar knew, Brannic's body lay in the dark on a broken stair, and no toll of names would ever be paid at that gate again, because there was no longer a gate, and no longer a toll, and the man who ended both had paid for it with the only coin he had left.

Oscar looked at the sealed mountain for a long moment.

"He held," Felix said softly, coming to stand at their shoulder. "I heard it stop. The fighting. It just... stopped, all at once, like—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. They had all heard it stop.

"We'll come back in spring," Oscar said. Their voice came out rough, unused to hope. "When the rock's settled. We'll bring proper tools, and we'll cut him a marker worthy of the door he died holding. And we'll carve it ourselves, because I'm not trusting any stonemason in Grayhollow to spell his name right."

Maren huffed something that was almost a laugh. "He'd hate that. He'd want something grand and awful, with too much gilt."

"Then we'll put too much gilt on it," Oscar said. "Let him haunt us about it."

The wind came down off the peaks, smelling of pine and snowmelt and distance, and Oscar turned their face into it and did the last thing, the hardest thing, the thing they had been circling since the door.

They reached for the swordspeech one more time. Gently now. The way you touch a scar to learn its shape.

Silence. Clean and total and permanent. The riverbed, dry. The room, empty. Somewhere in Oscar's chest, a grief the size of a lifetime opened its eyes — and then, beside it, equally large, equally awake, something else stirred. Not magic. Not words of power. Just the ordinary human arithmetic of survival: *you are alive, they are alive, the sky is lit, the road home exists.*

Fifteen years, Oscar had told themselves they were surviving. Standing under the returned stars, swordless and powerless and free, they understood for the first time that they had never once been doing it alone — and that the family scattered around this frozen hillside, looking up at the same dim constellations, had survived them right back.

"Grayhollow's a week's walk," Maren said at last, standing, brushing frost from her knees. "Less if the roads have gone quiet with the wards down. More if they haven't." She looked at Oscar, and in the starlight her face was open in a way it hadn't been since they were young and stupid and invincible together. "Well, swordspeaker? Which way?"

The title landed like a hand on an old wound — tender, not cruel. Oscar flexed their empty right hand, felt the silence where the fire had lived, and found that answering it didn't hurt the way they'd feared.

"Not swordspeaker," they said. "Just Oscar. Just... the one who knows the way home."

They started down the mountain, four abreast, under stars that burned dim and honest and theirs — and it was Felix, walking at Oscar's elbow with the ledger-ash still on his cuffs, who said what all of them were thinking:

"When we get back... what do we tell them? About the wards? About what magic *is*, now that everyone's seen the sky go out and come back?"

Oscar considered the question the whole length of the switchback, watching the lights of Grayhollow flicker awake in the valley below — more lights than there should have been, doors opening, people spilling out into streets to stare upward at a sky they'd mourned.

"I expect," Oscar said slowly, "they're going to want someone to tell them what to do about it."

And far below, at the city gates, they could already see torches gathering — a crowd, a council banner, and figures climbing the mountain road to meet them.
