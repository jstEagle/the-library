## 22. The Family We Chose

The contract was one page long, and Felix read it four times.

He sat at the counter of Iversen's Charms & Small Repairs — the sign had been repainted twice since spring, because Oscar kept insisting the ampersand was crooked and Maren kept insisting it gave the shop character — with a stub of graphite, a pot of ink gone thick with age, and the most honest piece of paper he had ever held.

*This agreement is made freely,* it began. *No soul, sworn or otherwise, stands as collateral. No debt survives the signing. The undersigned gives only their labor and their learning, and receives in return the same.*

It was, by the standards of every document Felix had touched in six years at the chancery, almost insultingly simple. There was no clause beneath clause beneath clause. There was no seal that breathed. There was no line, hidden in the eighth paragraph of sub-language, where his name became someone else's property.

There was just his name, waiting for him to write it.

"You're stalling," Oscar said from the back room, where they were sorting dried feverwort into paper twists. Their voice carried the particular dryness it had acquired over the past year — the voice of a person who had lost everything magical about themselves and discovered, to their visible annoyance, that they still knew exactly what they were talking about. "You've read that page more times than you read your own confession."

"My confession was shorter."

"Your confession had better punctuation." Oscar came out wiping their hands on their apron, and leaned against the doorframe. They looked older this year. Not worn — settled, like a house after the ground stops shaking. The scar down their right arm had faded to a pale seam, and when they moved their hand there was nothing in the motion but motion. Felix had watched them reach for the swordspeech exactly once since the mountain, on the night the first stars came back, an old reflex rising toward something that wasn't there anymore. He had seen the silence land on their face like weather. He had never mentioned it. Neither had they. Some losses you honored by not making them perform themselves.

"The ink's fine," Oscar said now. "I checked it myself. No sigils, no seals, no clauses. It's just ink. Worst thing it can do is stain your sleeve."

"That's what worries me," Felix said. "A contract that can't hurt me. I don't know how to hold one of those."

Oscar was quiet a moment. Outside the window, Grayhollow was doing its evening work: lamplighters on the corner, the market stalls folding up, two council wardens arguing cheerfully about drainage ditches. A year ago the wardens had been hollow-eyed men who didn't meet anyone's gaze. Now they argued about gutters. The city had come back the way stars did — dimmer than before, honest about it, and somehow brighter for the honesty.

"When I signed my first oath," Oscar said, "I was seventeen, and I thought a vow was a wall you built around yourself so nothing could get in." They turned the inkpot slowly with one finger. "Took me fifteen years and a mountain to learn a vow is a door. You build it so the right things can get through. That's all this is. A door."

"And if I walk through it and I'm terrible at charm-craft?"

"Then you'll be terrible at it under my roof, where I can watch, and in ten years you'll be mediocre, and in twenty you'll be good, and you'll complain the whole way, and I'll pretend to be annoyed." Oscar shrugged. "That's the trade. That's the whole trade."

Felix picked up the pen.

His hand did not shake. That surprised him most of all. A year ago his hands shook constantly — in the chancery, in the dark streets, on the warded road, all the way down into the bone-lanterned dark where clerks with borrowed faces had slid his file across a desk of debt and told him, gently, what he would become. His hands had shaken until the night he walked back into the court on his own feet, choosing it, and discovered that terror and agency could live in the same body after all.

Now his hand was steady, and he wrote *Felix Arden Voss* in the space provided, and the letters came out even and unremarkable, the handwriting of a boy who belonged to himself.

The ink dried. Nothing happened. No cold, no hunger, no whisper from beneath any mountain. The contract just lay there being a contract, and Felix found his eyes stinging anyway.

"Well," Oscar said gruffly, and thrust out their hand. Felix took it. The grip was warm and calloused and entirely ordinary, and it was the best thing Felix had felt in years.

"Apprentice," Oscar said.

"Swordspeaker," Felix answered, and then winced. "Sorry. Habit."

"Don't apologize." Something moved behind Oscar's eyes — not grief, exactly. More like the way you look at an old coat that no longer fits. "Say it like it's history. That's all it is now. History we survived."

* * *

Maren arrived at sundown, which meant she arrived late, because Maren Ovist had never once in her life arrived anywhere early, sober or otherwise.

She came through the door with sawdust in her hair and a set of plans under one arm, and she was — this was the part Felix still caught himself staring at — steady. Not the brittle steadiness of a drunkard white-knuckling an hour. Real steadiness, the kind that came from eleven months of mornings she remembered afterward. She'd rebuilt the border wards herself, or rather taught the council to rebuild them, standing in front of the assembled magistrates of Grayhollow with her hands shaking and her voice level, saying: *you outlawed the craft, not the laws of it, and the laws don't care what you're afraid of.* They'd hired her within the month. She'd made them wait three.

"Plans are done," she announced, dropping them on the counter next to Felix's contract. "New ward-lattice for the north quarter. Self-feeding. It draws on returned memory instead of stolen souls — same principle as the Sigil's new diet, scaled down, no starving seal required." She tapped the drawings. "The village sent a delegation last week. They're remembering things. Slowly. Names first, then faces, then the small stuff — what bread tasted like, who taught them to whistle. The lattice will help. Give the memories somewhere to live besides inside people, so losing one doesn't mean losing it forever."

"Like a library," Felix said.

"Like a granary." Maren grinned, and the grin was the same grin that had once hot-wired a god-cursed ward-lattice in a screaming courtroom while three magistrates tried to kill her. "You store the harvest so winter doesn't eat you. That's all magic ever was, before people got grand about it."

She looked down then, and saw the contract, and saw the signature, and her grin softened into something quieter.

"He signed?"

"He signed," Oscar said.

"About time." Maren cuffed Felix's shoulder — gently, for her; a full-strength cuff from Maren had once knocked Tallow off a cart. "Welcome to the family business, scribe. Fair warning: the pay is bad, the hours are worse, and the senior partner is insufferable."

"I'm standing right here," said Oscar.

"I know," said Maren.

Tallow arrived last, as he always did, because Tallow believed — with the theological seriousness he now applied to nothing else — that a man should enter a room like a punchline. He came in through the back, as usual, because doors were complicated for him now, and dropped into the chair by the stove with the sigh of a man settling into the only furniture in the world that fit him.

He looked thinner this year. He would always look thinner; you spent your priesthood buying a miracle, you wore the cost. But his eyes were clear, and his hands, folded on his knee, were quiet. Godless hands. He'd told Felix once, on the road home, what it was like: *like setting down a weight so heavy you'd forgotten it was heavy. You keep reaching for it. Your shoulder keeps bracing. And then one morning you notice you're walking upright and you don't remember when that started.* He missed his god the way you miss a limb. He also slept through the night now, which he hadn't done in thirty years of service, and when Felix asked him once whether the trade had been worth it, Tallow had considered the question with genuine care and answered: "My god is free, the door works, and everyone who wanted out got out. Ask me again when I've thought of a way that could have gone better. I've been trying for a year. I can't find one."

"Contract signed?" Tallow asked now, eyeing the page.

"Signed," Felix said.

"Witnessed?" Tallow raised an eyebrow. "Because I'll tell you what I told the council about their new charter: an unwitnessed agreement is just two people lying to each other politely."

"We're witnesses," Maren said.

"One witness is a fluke. Two is a coincidence. Three—" Tallow spread his empty priest's hands, "—is a congregation. I'll count as the third. Old habit. Somebody ought to get use out of it."

So they witnessed it, formally, absurdly, Maren scrawling her name with a carpenter's pencil and Tallow signing with a flourish he absolutely did not deserve, and Oscar pressing their thumbprint beside the whole mess because Oscar had never trusted signatures, even their own. Four marks on one page. No collateral. No clauses. No seal.

Felix looked at it and understood, finally, why his eyes had stung earlier. Every contract he had ever touched had been a machine for taking. This one was a machine for keeping.

* * *

They ate upstairs, the way they did every seventh day, because the tradition had started by accident and nobody had been willing to name it and thereby risk killing it. Maren brought bread from the baker on Cooper's Lane who remembered her order without being told — a small thing, a returned thing, worth more than any ward. Tallow brought wine he didn't drink and stories he did. Oscar cooked, badly and generously, and complained about both.

Halfway through the meal, Tallow nodded at the ceiling beam above the door.

"You going to tell him, or am I?"

Oscar set down their spoon. "Tell him what?"

"The beam." Tallow pointed. "Over the door. He walks under it forty times a day and hasn't looked up once. Scribes. No observation skills."

Felix looked up.

Carved into the beam, in deep clean strokes that had been filled with dark oil to make them legible, were four words:

**BRANNIC HELD THE STAIR.**

Below them, smaller: *so we could climb.*

Felix had known, of course. Everyone in Grayhollow knew; Brannic's stand had passed into the city's story within a season, the way true things do, growing truer in the telling. The council had wanted a statue. Oscar had refused a statue with a vehemence that had startled the entire chamber — *"he doesn't need to be bigger, he needs to be remembered at actual size"* — and so instead there was this: a beam, in a shop, over a door, where everyone who entered had to pass beneath the name of the man who betrayed them and then bought it back at full price.

"When did you—"

"Winter," Oscar said. "Maren held the light. Tallow kept watch. Took me four nights. I'm a swordspeaker, not a joiner." They paused. "Was. Whatever."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"What was there to say?" Oscar shrugged, uncomfortable, the way they always were with anything that mattered too much. "He held the stair. That's the whole eulogy. Everything else is commentary."

Felix looked at the carving a long moment. He thought about Brannic at the fire on the warded road, confessing with his head down like a man awaiting a blow. He thought about the stair, and the hollow-men coming up it like a tide, and the sound Brannic had made — not a battle cry, nothing so clean, just a roar of pure spent fury, fifteen years of guilt burning off in one stand. He thought about how Oscar had refused forgiveness and accepted the sword, both at once, and how that had turned out to be the only mercy Brannic could carry.

"It's a good eulogy," Felix said quietly.

"It's an accurate one," said Oscar. "Same thing, if you do it right."

Maren raised her cup — water, these days, always water, held with the same ceremony she'd once given wine. "To Brannic. Who owed us everything and paid it."

"To Brannic," they echoed, and drank, and the beam stood over them, holding up the roof the way its namesake had held the stair, which Felix suspected was exactly the kind of joke Brannic would have appreciated and exactly the kind Oscar had intended.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and Maren had fallen asleep sitting up mid-sentence about load-bearing runes and Tallow had covered her with a blanket without comment, Felix stood at the shop window and looked out at the sky.

The stars were back. Dimmer — everyone said so, the old ones especially, the sailors and the star-priests and the farmers who'd steered by them for generations. Maybe half their old brightness, maybe less. But they were *honest*, and honesty turned out to have a light of its own. The Sigil fed on returned truth now, down in its remade court, and the sky above fed on whatever the Sigil let go, and the whole arrangement worked the way Maren's granary worked: nothing stolen, everything shared, winter survivable.

Somewhere up there, Felix knew, was the starlight clause. Somewhere up there was the shape of the bargain that had saved them all — a vow freely unmade, memories freely returned, a door that opened only for the willing. He'd helped write the plain-language summary for the council archives. It ran to nine pages. Oscar had read all nine and pronounced them "adequate," which from Oscar was a parade.

"You're doing it again," Oscar said from behind him. "Staring at the sky like it owes you money."

"Just checking the interest."

Oscar came to stand beside him. For a while neither spoke. Below, the street emptied toward home; above, the dim stars kept their posts. In the window's reflection, Felix could see the room behind them — the sleeping ward-smith, the godless priest, the carved beam, the contract drying on the counter with four names on it, the whole improbable architecture of a family that had been broken apart by oaths and debts and betrayal and reassembled itself anyway, not because it was given back but because they had chosen, day after stubborn day, to keep it.

"Fifteen years I hid," Oscar said at last. "Fifteen years selling luck-knots to people who needed real luck, pretending I was surviving when I was just... pausing. Waiting for a war to finish without me." They turned the lamp down low. "You know what I learned? Home isn't the place you hide. It's the people you stop hiding from."

"That's very wise," Felix said. "Did you get it from a fortune slip?"

"I got it from a runaway scribe who bought a charm he couldn't afford." Oscar bumped his shoulder. "Worst coin I ever spent. Best trade I ever made."

Felix laughed, and outside, above Grayhollow, the honest stars burned on — dim, returning, enough.

And on the counter behind them, in ink that would never claim anything, his name dried in the lamplight: not a debt, not a sentence, not a sale.

Just a promise, kept.

*THE END*
