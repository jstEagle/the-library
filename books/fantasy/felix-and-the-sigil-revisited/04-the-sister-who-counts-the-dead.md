## 4. The Sister Who Counts the Dead

Maren Voss had counted the dead for eleven years, and she had learned one thing above all others: grief was a ledger too.

The dead did not settle their own accounts. Someone had to. In her village they had called her morbid for it—*the sister who counts*, her mother used to say, half in sorrow and half in warning—but Maren knew what she was doing. Every name entered into her little book of mourning was a debt recorded. And debts, unlike the dead themselves, could be collected.

She had begun the book with her father, drowned when she was nine. Then her grandmother, taken by the winter fever. Then old Tam Wicker, whose cough had finally won its long argument with his lungs. Forty-one names by the time she left home, each one dated, each one closed—a life summed, a line drawn beneath it, the account balanced as best as any living person could balance it.

Felix's name was the forty-second. And it was the only one she could not close.

Because Felix's account had no date of settlement. It had no body. It had only a letter that arrived three weeks after he stopped writing, carried by a drover who didn't remember where he'd picked it up, written in a hand that shook so badly Maren could barely read it:

*Maren—if I don't come home, go to Vellum Street. Count everything. You're the only one who counts properly.*

That was all. No farewell. No explanation. Just an instruction, in her brother's voice, from wherever he had been when he wrote it—and then silence, stretching on until silence became certainty, and certainty became the thing she carried now like a stone behind her ribs.

So she had come to the city. She had come to count.

* * *

Vellum Street announced itself before you saw it, the way rich things always did. First came the smell: ink and vellum and something sweeter underneath, something like honey left too long in the sun, which Maren would later learn was the scent of bottled grief—the Guild's most famous trade, the one that made beggars of whole provinces so that the wealthy could weep beautifully at funerals without the inconvenience of having loved anyone. Then came the sound: the soft, ceaseless rustle of paper, as though the street itself were turning pages. And last came the sight of it—the weighing door.

She had heard about the weighing door on the road north, from a carter who spoke of the Guild the way farmers speak of weather: with resignation, and a kind of fearful respect. Everything that entered the Vellum Guild passed through the weighing door, he said. Grief, years, names, secrets—all of it weighed against brass standards older than anyone could remember, stamped with the Guild's seal, entered into ledgers, and owned thereafter. Nothing given to the Guild was ever given back. That was not cruelty; that was bookkeeping.

Maren stood across the street in the shadow of a chandler's awning and watched the door for two days.

She learned its rhythms. The clerks went in at dawn, gray-robed, gray-faced, carrying gray satchels. The sellers came later—poor people mostly, hollow-eyed, clutching whatever they had left to sell: a widow with a jar of her husband's last words; a boy selling three years of his childhood, presumably because his family needed bread more than he needed to have been young; an old man who stood at the door a long time before going in, and came out lighter somehow, emptier, walking like a man who had set down something heavy and could not remember what it was.

Maren watched them all and wrote down none of it, because none of it was Felix. But she watched the door itself, and the guards beside it—two of them, big men in the Guild's dove-gray livery, with the bored patience of people paid to stand still—and she noted that the door opened inward, that the hinges were on the inside, that the guard on the left favored his right knee, and that the alley running along the Guild's eastern wall was wide enough for a cart but dark enough after sundown to hide one.

This was how Maren mourned. Not with wailing. With observation. Her mother had died of it eventually, or something near it—some slow surrender that began the day Felix left and never reversed—and Maren had promised herself at the graveside that she would not do the same. She would not fade. She would *count*. She would find out what her brother had been carrying, and who had made him carry it, and why his last letter had sounded less like a goodbye than like a warning.

*If I don't come home, go to Vellum Street.*

He had known. Whatever it was, he had known it would be the Guild, or he had feared it would be, and he had sent her here anyway—not away, not to safety, but *here*, to the mouth of the thing that ate him. Because he trusted her to count. Because counting was the only weapon their family had ever owned.

On the third morning, the blood arrived.

* * *

It happened fast, and it happened in plain view, which was the first thing that told Maren this was not ordinary. The Guild did nothing in plain view. The Guild conducted even its cruelties behind vellum and brass and doors that opened inward.

A man came up Vellum Street at a stumbling run, just past dawn, when the clerks were filing in and the fog still lay in the gutters like something poured. He was young—younger than Maren—with road dust to his thighs and a stain spreading down his left side that she recognized before her mind finished naming it. He ran like a man who had been running for days, the run itself worn down to a shuffle, arms pumping out of habit rather than strength. And he was holding something against his chest with both hands, curled around it, the way a farmer curls around a lamb in a storm.

The guards moved toward him—not quickly, not alarmed, just moving, the way water moves toward a low place—and the man saw them and changed direction, staggering toward the weighing door itself, and Maren understood with a cold, clarifying shock that he was trying to get *in*. Not away from the Guild. Into it. He was bleeding to death on their doorstep on purpose, because there was something inside him that needed to be delivered, and the weighing door was the only door he trusted to weigh it.

Then a woman came out.

Maren never learned afterward why her eyes had gone to that door at that moment—call it the hunter's instinct, call it the ledger-keeper's sense for an entry about to be made—but she saw the woman clearly, and she never forgot her. Tall, dark-haired, dressed in the gray of a senior clerk but wearing it wrong, or rather wearing it *heavily*, like someone who felt the weight of cloth more than other people did. There was a mark on her left palm that Maren couldn't see at distance but would dream about later: a sigil, seared into the skin, old enough to have gone silver.

The woman took one step toward the dying man and stopped, and Maren saw her face change—saw recognition move through it like a crack through ice. Not surprise. Recognition. As if she had been expecting this exact stranger at this exact door, though she could not possibly have been.

The man fell before he reached her. The woman knelt. And then—this was the part Maren played back a hundred times in the nights that followed, hunting it for meaning—the man said something to her. Maren was too far to hear it. But she saw the woman's face when she heard it, and what moved across that face was not confusion and not pity.

It was fear. A very particular fear: the fear of being named.

The man's hands found the woman's and pressed something into them—a flat shape, wrapped, sealed—and then his hands went slack, and the woman stayed kneeling over him, and the guards came, and the fog swallowed the details, and by the time Maren crossed the street—she was moving, she realized, she had been moving since the man fell—the weighing door was open and clerks were spilling out and someone was shouting for a physiker who would arrive too late and know it.

They let Maren through. That was the second strange thing. She put on the face she had practiced for eleven years of funerals—the face of a woman merely concerned, merely neighborly—and the crowd's sympathy parted for her like water, and suddenly she was at the front, looking down at her brother.

Except it wasn't Felix.

Of course it wasn't Felix. She had known that from the street, from the way he ran, from the set of his shoulders. Felix had a runner's build and a coward's luck and a laugh you could hear through walls. This man was a stranger, dark-haired, road-worn, dead. But kneeling over him, seeing the blood pool black in the gutter seams, seeing the wrapped thing pried gently from his stiffening fingers by a clerk in gray gloves, Maren felt the forty-second entry in her book tear straight down the middle, because this was how Felix had died too—alone, running, delivering something to people who would weigh it and own it and give nothing back—and the stranger's blood on the stones was Felix's blood, every drop of it, spilled in the same place, at the same door, for the same silent masters.

"Did you know him?" a clerk asked her, and she realized she was still crouched, her hand hovering over the dead man's sleeve.

"No," she said, and it was almost true.

* * *

She followed the woman.

Not immediately. First she waited through the ugly theater of the aftermath—the physiker arriving, the pronouncement, the guards carrying the body inside through the weighing door (of course inside; even the dead belonged to the Guild's ledgers now), the clerk with the gray gloves sealing the wrapped thing into a document case with wax and wire. Maren stood in the crowd and memorized faces. The clerk with the gloves: narrow, careful, a man who touched dead strangers' property the way a miser touches coins. The head guard: the bad knee, favoring it even standing still. And the woman—the tall one, the one the dying man had named with his last breath—standing apart from all of it, her bandaged left hand held close against her ribs, watching the case with an expression Maren recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone holding a debt she hadn't agreed to owe.

Then the crowd thinned, the door closed, and Maren went to work.

She worked the way she always worked. She bought breakfast from the chandler's boy and asked idle questions and learned that the tall woman was called Ansel Nystrom, keeper of ledgers, a Guildswoman of some standing and no society—"keeps to herself," the boy said, "talks to her books more than to us." She learned where Nystrom lodged: rooms above a bindery two streets east, close enough to walk to work, far enough to sleep alone. She learned that Nystrom had come out of the Guild only minutes before the courier arrived—as if summoned, as if scheduled—and Maren wrote that fact down in the back of her mourning book, under a new heading she drew herself, ruling the line straight with a knife-edge: *Questions.*

Under *Questions* she wrote: *Why her? Why that door? What did he say? What did he give?*

And beneath all of it, in letters pressed hard enough to score the next three pages, she wrote the heading that governed everything else: *Who killed my brother?*

Because that was the question under all questions, and Maren had already answered it to her own satisfaction. Oh, the road might have done the killing—fever, or a blade, or bad luck; roads were generous with all three. But roads didn't make men run bleeding through a city at dawn clutching sealed packages. Roads didn't teach men to trust a weighing door with their dying breath. Something had sent Felix to Vellum Street, and something had chased him, and the something wore gray. She was certain of it the way she was certain of arithmetic. The Guild had weighed her brother's life and found it worth less than whatever he'd carried, and they had spent it.

Now they had spent a stranger's too, and the stranger's blood was still wet between the cobblestones, and the woman who had caught the delivery was walking home as if nothing had happened.

Maren followed her through the morning market, keeping a cart and a crowd between them, noting the route, noting the turns. She watched Ansel Nystrom stop once at a fruit stall and not buy anything—just stand there, staring at her own bandaged hand as if it had spoken to her. She watched her flinch when a bell rang somewhere. She watched her take the long way to the bindery, doubling back once, checking for tails—and missing Maren entirely, because Maren did not follow like a person follows; she followed like a shadow counts, at fixed intervals, from fixed positions, patient as interest accruing.

At the bindery, Nystrom went in. Maren took a room across the street, paying for three nights with coin she could not afford, and sat by the window with her mourning book open on her knees, and began, methodically, to plan.

She was not a fighter. She knew this and did not pretend otherwise. She was small and quick and had once broken a man's nose with a ladle, but a ladle was not a strategy. What she had instead was worse, for people like Ansel Nystrom: she had arithmetic. She had eleven years of counting the dead, and she knew what everyone who trades in death forgets—that the dead leave records. Receipts. Witnesses. Patterns. Felix had left a letter. The stranger had left a body and a delivery and a name spoken aloud to a woman who feared being named. Somewhere in the Guild's gray vastness there was a ledger with Felix's name in it, or a receipt for whatever he'd carried, or a page torn out—and torn pages were entries too. A missing record told you what someone wanted hidden, and what someone wants hidden is the truest thing about them.

She would get inside. However long it took. She would get inside the Guild and she would read its books until she found her brother's name, and then she would find the name of whoever had spent him, and she would write that name in her book, and she would close the account.

She looked across the street at the bindery windows, at the lamplight coming up behind them, at the silhouette of Ansel Nystrom moving past—pausing, Maren noticed, pausing again, as if unable to stop looking at her own hand.

"You knew him," Maren said to the glass, quietly. "Or you knew of him. Or you're the reason for him. One of those three."

The silhouette paused again. Then the lamp went out.

Maren sat in the dark a while longer, listening to the city settle, feeling the stone behind her ribs shift into something sharper. For eleven years she had counted the dead and closed their accounts, and it had been bearable because the dead were finished, because grief with a date on it has edges. But Felix had no edges. Felix was an open entry, and open entries festered, and the only cure she had ever known was the one she'd sworn at her mother's grave: *count everything.*

Tomorrow she would start on the Guild's public ledgers—the ones they let commoners read, the tax rolls and trade manifests, the respectable surface of the thing. She would read them the way she read everything: looking for the number that didn't fit. Somewhere in those columns was a discrepancy shaped exactly like her brother.

And if the ledgers wouldn't yield him—if the Guild's books were as clean as the Guild's conscience pretended to be—then she would stop reading and start prying, and the woman across the street, the one with the bandaged hand and the frightened face, would learn what Maren Voss did with unanswered questions.

Below, the bindery door opened. Maren leaned forward.

Ansel Nystrom stepped out into the evening, alone, hood up, moving fast—not toward her lodgings, but back the way she'd come, back toward Vellum Street, toward the weighing door, hours after the day's business should have ended. She walked like a woman going to commit a crime, or prevent one, and either way she walked like a woman who expected not to be seen.

Maren was already pulling on her coat.

She gave her a hundred-count head start. It was, after all, only fair to keep the books balanced.
