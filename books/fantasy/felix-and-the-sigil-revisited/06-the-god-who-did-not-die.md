## 6. The God Who Did Not Die

Sleep did not come to Ansel Nystrom so much as it was taken from her.

She had meant to stay awake. She had lit three candles against the dark of her narrow room above the counting-house, laid the map beneath her mattress where no honest searcher would think to look, and told herself she would sit until dawn with the Guild's ledgers open before her like a shield. There was comfort in columns. There was safety in sums. A number could not lie to you the way a map could, the way a dying man's mouth could shape a word—*Sigil-Bearer*—and leave it hanging in the air like smoke that would not disperse.

But exhaustion is its own kind of debt collector, and it came for her sometime past the second bell, between one line of figures and the next.

The last thing she saw was her own hand on the page, and the sigil on her palm catching the candlelight—the seven-pointed star with its broken ninth ray, the mark seared into her skin at her swearing-in eleven years ago, the emblem of a god who had not answered a single prayer since before her grandmother was born.

The first thing she saw when she woke was snow falling upward.

* * *

Ansel stood in a field she knew and had never seen.

That was the nature of it—she recognized every detail and none of them belonged to her memory. The field was white with frost, but the frost rose from the ground in slow spirals, drifting up toward a sky the color of old vellum, and each flake caught the light as it climbed so that the whole world seemed to be ascending, particle by particle, into something waiting above. In the distance stood mountains she knew from the Guild's oldest maps: the Teeth of Varn, drawn in ink that predated the founding, back when cartographers still admitted how little they understood of what lay beyond them.

Except here the mountains were wrong. Or rather, they were *honest*. On the founding maps they were rendered as clean jagged lines, but here Ansel could see that their peaks were broken off, sheared away as if something vast had passed over them long ago and clipped them like a gardener's hedge. No living map showed them that way. No living person had ever seen them that way.

Because no living person had been alive when it happened.

"Where am I?" she said aloud, and her voice did not echo. It simply stopped, absorbed by the upward-falling snow, as though the air itself were listening and preferred not to be interrupted.

There was no answer. But there was a path.

It ran through the frosted field toward the broken mountains—a road of packed earth that should have been gray but was instead the deep red-brown of dried blood, worn smooth by the passage of feet. Thousands of feet. Tens of thousands. And Ansel understood, with the terrible clarity that dreams grant and waking denies, that this was a road made by pilgrimage. That people had come here once, in numbers beyond counting, walking toward those shattered peaks with their hands open and their voices lifted, and the ground itself remembered them even though the world had forgotten why they came.

Her own feet began to move.

She did not decide to walk. She simply discovered that she was walking, her boots leaving prints in the frost that filled themselves with rising snow, and somewhere far below her—below the field, below the earth, below whatever floor the dream had built for itself—she felt a pull. Gentle at first. Then less gentle. Like a hook set behind her sternum, reeling her forward one slow turn at a time.

The sigil on her palm was warm. Not burning—not yet—but warm the way a stone is warm when someone has only just stopped holding it.

"You're late," said a voice, and Ansel's heart stopped.

It was not a voice she knew. It was not a voice at all, exactly—it was more like the sensation of being *addressed*, poured directly into the space behind her eyes without troubling itself with sound. It was low and vast and patient, the way a cathedral is patient, and it carried an undertone of something she had never expected to hear again in her life, because she had never actually heard it before. She had only read about it. In the founding texts. In the oldest hymns, the ones the Guild kept locked in the archive and sang at no service she had ever attended.

Reverence. The voice was full of reverence—for *her*, which made no sense, which was impossible, which was the most frightening thing about it.

"Who's there?" she called, and hated how small her voice sounded against all that ascending snow.

Silence. The kind of silence she had lived inside for eleven years. The kind she had sworn herself into, kneeling on cold stone while an Auditor pressed the heated seal to her palm and spoke the words: *You serve a god who does not speak, and you will keep his silence as your own.* She had thought, then, that the silence was grief. That the god had died or fled or simply turned his face away, and the Guild's oath was a promise to mourn him properly, forever, without the indignity of complaint.

She had built her whole life inside that silence. She had furnished it. She had hung her loneliness on its walls like pictures.

And now the silence was looking at her.

"I know you," the voice said, and this time there was wonder in it too, wonder braided through the reverence like two threads of one cord. "Not your name. Names are the Guild's currency, and I have none of it left. But I know the shape of you. You were sealed in my mark. Eleven years ago. You knelt on the third step of the weighing hall, and your knee cracked on the stone, and you did not cry out, because you believed crying out would be a debt."

Ansel stopped walking. The field stretched around her, endless and white and climbing into the vellum sky.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "You've been silent for a hundred years."

"Yes," said the voice, and the word landed on her like a physical weight, like a hand pressing down on the crown of her head. "Yes. I have."

* * *

The field changed.

It happened the way things change in dreams—one moment she was standing in frost, and the next the frost was gone and the blood-dark road had widened into a plaza, and the plaza was full of people. They moved without faces, their features smoothed away like thumbprints wiped from wax, but their bodies were vivid, particular, alive: a woman carrying a child on her hip; a soldier with his helmet under his arm; an old man kneeling with his forehead to the stones; a thousand others, ten thousand, flowing past her like a river in flood, and every single one of them was facing the same direction.

Toward the mountains. Toward the broken peaks, which now loomed close enough that she could see the scorch marks on their flanks, the glassy fusion where rock had melted and refrozen in shapes no wind or water could make.

"They prayed to me," the voice said, and it was everywhere now, in the stones and the sky and the smooth-faced crowd, in Ansel's own bones. "Do you understand what that means? Not at me. To me. They gave me their grief and their gratitude and their dead, and I held all of it, because holding is what I was. I was not a god of war, whatever the founders wrote afterward. I was a god of *witness*. Everything that happened in the light of the world, I saw, and I kept, and nothing that was suffered was ever suffered alone, because I was there, and I remembered."

The crowd flowed around her. One of the faceless women paused beside Ansel, and though she had no eyes, Ansel felt herself being looked at with unbearable tenderness.

"And then?" Ansel asked. Her voice shook. "What happened then?"

The tenderness curdled.

"Then they learned what my witness was worth," the voice said, "and men being men, they decided it was worth owning."

The plaza shuddered. The faceless pilgrims flickered like candleflames in a draft. And Ansel felt the pull behind her sternum strengthen sharply, almost painfully, dragging her forward across the stones toward the mountains' roots, where the scorched rock split apart into a crevasse she had never seen on any map but recognized instantly, completely, with a recognition that preceded thought—

Because she had dreamed of it before. All her life. Since childhood. A black seam in the world, breathing out cold, and always in the dreams she had stood at its edge and been afraid, and always she had woken before she learned what was inside.

She was not going to wake this time.

"This is where they brought me," the voice said, quieter now, and the quietness was worse than the vastness had been. "This is where they negotiated. Do you want to know what the war was? Your Guild's histories call it the Sundering of the Nine Banners, and they give it dates and generals and causes, tidy as ledger entries. Here is the true entry: the war was fought in my name by men who claimed to speak for me, and it ended not with victory but with atrocity—villages burned with everyone inside them, offered up to me as sacrifice, as proof of devotion. Ten thousand souls in a single season, murdered upward, sent to me screaming. And I received them. What else could I do? I was made to receive. I held every screaming soul and I could not put any of them down, and the weight of it—" 

The voice broke.

A god's voice, breaking. Ansel stood at the edge of the crevasse with her heart hammering and her sigil blazing now, truly blazing, light pouring from her palm as though her hand had become a window onto something enormous and imprisoned, and she thought: *this is what Felix heard. This is what he died carrying.*

"—the weight of it bent me," the voice finished, barely a whisper now. "I would have done anything to make it stop. And they knew it. Seven of them. The founders. They came to me with mercy in their mouths and chains in their hands, and they said: sleep, and the killing stops, because without your witness the fanatics have nothing to point at. Sleep, and we will carry your remembrance for you. Sleep, and we will keep the truth until the world is ready."

Ansel's breath came out ragged and white in the frozen air. "And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them." For the first time, the voice sounded old. Ancient. Worn down to the bedrock of itself. "That is my confession, Sigil-Bearer, if you will take it. I wanted rest so badly that I mistook a vault for a bed. They buried me under their first foundation stone, and they sealed the burial with oaths—oaths like yours, sworn on my own mark, thousands of them over a century, each one another lock—and they told the world I had died. And then they opened a trade."

The plaza dissolved. The pilgrims dissolved. The mountains fell away like scenery struck by stagehands, and suddenly Ansel was nowhere—in darkness, in cold, in a blackness so complete it had texture—and the only light in all that void was her own burning hand.

"They traded my silence," the god said. "Grief in bottles, because I was not there to hold it freely. Years sold by the measure, because who would guard time when its keeper slept? Names unspoken, truths unwitnessed, every absence I left behind them carved up and sold. They built the Vellum Guild in the shape of my grave, Ansel Nystrom, and they staffed it with the faithful, and they taught the faithful to call the grave an altar."

The darkness pressed in. And Ansel—who had spent eleven years keeping books for that grave, balancing its columns, auditing its profits, praying each night to a god she believed dead and taking the silence as her answer—felt something crack open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close again.

"Why me?" she asked. The question tore out of her raw. "Why show me this? Why now?"

"Because the locks are failing," the god said. "Because the map is moving. Because a boy with my mark on a scrap of leather bled out on your weighing-stones, and his blood was the last key turning. I did not choose you, Ansel. I have not been able to choose anything for a hundred years. But the seal was made of oaths, and oaths are made of people, and you—" the reverence returned, unbearable, tender, terrible— "you are the last oath still kept. Everyone else swore to me and forgot. You knelt on the third step and you have never once, in eleven years, missed a night's prayer. Even unanswered. Especially unanswered. Do you know what that makes you?"

"No," Ansel whispered.

"The hinge," said the god. "The last honest word in a building of lies. I was not abandoned, Ansel. Hear me. Whatever else you learn, whoever tells you otherwise, however gently they tell it—I was not abandoned."

The darkness gathered itself around her like a closing fist, and the voice dropped to its final register, the one that shook the frost from the sky of a dream:

"*I was buried.*"

* * *

Ansel woke on the floor of her room with the overturned chair beneath her and all three candles burned to pools, and the first grey light of morning was seeping through the shutters.

Her palm was unmarked by flame but glowing faintly, fading even as she watched, and when she pressed it flat against the floorboards to steady herself, she felt it: a pulse. Slow. Deep. Patient as a cathedral.

Beneath her. Beneath the counting-house. Beneath the Guild's oldest vault, down through stone and clay and a century of carefully kept lies.

Something down there had spoken to her, and it had used her name.

Ansel got to her feet on legs that did not feel entirely her own, crossed to the washstand, and looked at herself in the small warped mirror. A woman looked back—pale, hollow-eyed, thirty years old and suddenly feeling every one of them. She watched her lips move.

"I was not abandoned," she said aloud, testing the words against the daylight, against reason, against everything the Guild had taught her. "I was buried."

The words fit. That was the horror of it. They fit like a key.

And downstairs, three floors below, muffled by oak and stone, she heard the front bell of the weighing door ring—and ring again—impatient, urgent, insistent, as though whoever stood outside already knew she was awake, and had not come to trade.
