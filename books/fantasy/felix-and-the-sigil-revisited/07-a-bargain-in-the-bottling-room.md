## 7. A Bargain in the Bottling Room

The bottling room sat at the heart of the Vellum Guild like a secret kept from itself.

Ansel had walked past its door a thousand times in her eleven years of service—past the brass plaque reading *APPRAISALS, DIFFICULT*, past the two enforcers who stood watch with the patient stillness of men paid by the decade—and she had never once been invited inside. No auditor below the rank of Third was. The room was where the Guild kept its most delicate inventory, and delicacy, in the Vellum Guild's arithmetic, meant anything that could ruin you if it broke.

Tonight the door was open for her, and the enforcers looked away when she passed, which was how she knew that someone above her had already signed whatever needed signing.

The room beyond was smaller than she had imagined and warmer, lit by lamps of frosted glass that gave off a light like late afternoon in autumn. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling on every wall, and every shelf was crowded with vials—not the plain stoppered phials of an apothecary but vessels that seemed almost alive, each one breathing a faint color into the air around it. Grief, bottled, was a slow grey-green, like pond water in winter. Borrowed years ran gold at the bottom and clear at the top, the way wine separates when it has waited too long. A name that had never been spoken hung in its glass like a held breath, colorless and utterly absorbing of light.

Ansel stopped just inside the threshold and let herself feel it—the hum of the place, low and constant, like a hive heard through a wall. Every vial here was a life somebody had decided they could not afford to keep whole.

*And tonight,* she thought, *one of them will be mine.*

"You came."

The Bottler emerged from between the shelves as if he had been assembled out of them—a narrow man, old in the way of furniture, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles so thick his eyes seemed to float behind them, unanchored. His name, officially, was Master Corvane. Everyone called him the Bottler anyway, the way sailors call the sea *her*.

"Master Corvane." Ansel inclined her head. "I'm told I need an appraisal."

"I'm told you already know what you want to trade." He studied her over the rims of his spectacles, and there was nothing unkind in the look, which somehow made it worse. "Three years. That's what the map wants. That's what the ledger says."

"The ledger says," Ansel repeated. She had not shown the map to anyone. She had hidden it inside the false spine of a hymnal for six days, checking twice a night that it was still there, still shifting, still refusing to be read. And yet here was a bottling room prepared for her, a price already entered in a book she had never seen. "Whose handwriting is in that ledger, Master Corvane?"

He smiled gently, the way one smiles at a child who has asked exactly the right question at exactly the wrong time. "That depends on whose hand you believe in."

* * *

They did the appraisal standing at a workbench of black oak scarred by centuries of small ceremonies. Corvane laid out the instruments with the tenderness of a priest preparing an altar: a scale of white bronze whose pans were shallow cups of crystal; a tuning fork of some metal Ansel didn't recognize; and a single empty vial, taller than the others, its glass so clean it seemed less like a container than like a window into a room that hadn't been built yet.

"Explain it to me," Ansel said. "All of it. I've audited these transactions for eleven years. I know the entries. I don't know the *doing*."

Corvane nodded, unsurprised. Most guildsmen never asked. Asking made the trade harder, and hard trades were bad trades—or so the founders' manuals said, in their tidy, terrible prose.

"A memory is not a thing," he said. "It's a debt the past owes you. When we bottle one, we don't take the past away—we take your claim on it. The years remain. They happened. You lived them. You simply... can no longer collect." He tapped the empty vial. "Three years of yours. Chosen or unchosen?"

Ansel's mouth went dry. "There's a difference?"

"There is always a difference. Chosen, you select which three. Unchosen—" he shrugged, a small, awful motion, "—the vial selects. It tends to take what you'd least expect and miss most. Love does poorly in unchosen trades. So do mothers."

*Felix's dying words.* They were already fraying at the edges—she had noticed it two nights ago, reaching for them the way you reach for a dream on waking, and finding only the shape of where they'd been. *Sigil-Bearer,* he had said, blood on his teeth, and then something more, something after, and she could no longer—no, there. *The vault beneath the vault. Tell her the vault beneath the vault.* Still there. Still hers.

For now.

"Chosen," she said.

"Then choose."

She had expected this to be the hard part, and it was, but not in the way she'd expected. The difficulty wasn't finding three years she could spare. It was admitting that any year could be spared—that she was the kind of person who could stand at this bench and perform this arithmetic on her own life. Eleven years of auditing other people's griefs, and she had always told herself the ledgers were about them. Standing here, she understood that every entry she had ever written had been practice.

Her mother's last summer—no. Not that. Not yet. There would be time, after the map was read, to decide what else the truth required. She would pay what the map demanded and nothing more.

"I choose the three years I spent at Thornharrow," she said. "My posting before the city. The border audit."

Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "A dull choice. Cold country, cold work."

"It was quiet," Ansel said. "I learned the ledgers there. I can spare what taught me my trade." It was even mostly true. What she did not say was that Thornharrow was also where she had spent three years praying every night to a silent god, alone in a garrison chapel with frost on the windows, learning exactly how silence could hollow a person out. If she was going to lose something, better to lose the years that had taught her how to lose.

"Very well." Corvane lifted the tuning fork. "Hold the vial. Both hands. And Ansel—" he paused, and for a moment the gentle appraiser fell away and she saw the man beneath, ancient and tired and very good at his terrible work, "—whatever rises, do not fight it. Fighting is how people leave pieces of themselves in the glass. Pieces we cannot return."

"And if I wanted to fight it?"

"Then you should have chosen the unchosen trade. At least then you could tell yourself it wasn't your hand on the knife."

* * *

The fork sang—one clear note, then another beneath it, then a third that she felt in her teeth rather than heard—and the vial woke in her hands.

It began, strangely, with smell. Pine resin and woodsmoke and the iron tang of the garrison well water. Then cold—the specific cold of Thornharrow, the cold that got into the mortar of the walls and stayed all summer—and then the frost-ferns on the chapel window, and the ache of her knees on stone flags, and her own voice, younger, saying *answer me* to a god who did not—

"No," Ansel breathed, because this was wrong, this was not the border audit, this was the *chapel*, the nights in the chapel were braided through those years like thread through cloth, you could not pull the posting without pulling the prayers—

*Do not fight it.*

She didn't fight it. That was the horror and the mercy of it: the memory left the way sleep leaves, without violence, without even a seam. One moment she was kneeling on frozen flags begging the dark for a sign, and the next moment she knew only that somewhere in her there was a shaped absence, the way you know a tooth is missing not by pain but by the strange new thinness of the air against your tongue.

Three years went. She felt each one go and could not afterward say what had gone, only that the vial in her hands had grown heavy, and warm, and faintly gold at the bottom, clear at the top, and that Corvane was corking it with wax and pressing a lead seal into the wax with a stamp she recognized from the founding ledgers—the sigil of the Guild, the scales, the closed book.

Except that it wasn't only the Guild's sigil. Beneath the scales, so faint she might have imagined it, lay another mark. A mark she knew from the inside of her own palm.

"Master Corvane," she said carefully. "Your seal."

"Old stamp," he said, too quickly, wrapping the vial in black cloth. "Older than me. We keep using it because the wax remembers it best." He set the wrapped vial on a shelf behind him, in a row of identical wrapped shapes, dozens of them, hundreds, and Ansel understood suddenly that she was looking at a century of traded lives and that somewhere in that row, perhaps, were three years belonging to a courier named Felix.

"Now," Corvane said, turning back to her, and his voice had changed—there was something like awe in it now, and something like fear. "Show me the map."

* * *

She had carried it against her skin for six days, and it had behaved like a living thing: ink that migrated, coastlines that sulked and reformed, a sigil at its center that burned her scarred palm whenever she lied about what she was doing. She had tried candlelight, moonlight, the Guild's lens-room, salt water, blood. The map had refused all of them with the serene immovability of a locked door.

But Corvane did none of those things. He simply laid the map flat on the black oak bench, set the freshly sealed vial beside it, and stepped back.

The map drank.

There was no other word for it. The shifting ink stilled—all at once, like a crowd falling silent—and then began to move with purpose, rivers straightening into lines, lines resolving into architecture, and the paper itself seemed to exhale, going soft and supple as skin. Ansel watched three years of her own life settle into the vial's glass like sediment, and watched the map accept the payment, and felt the transaction complete itself somewhere behind her sternum with a click she would hear again in dreams.

"That's not decoding," she whispered. "That's a toll gate."

"Everything in this building is a toll gate," Corvane said. "Read it now. Quickly. Opened maps don't stay open."

She read.

The map was of the city—her city—but a city she had never seen, drawn in section, cut open like an anatomical figure. Streets she knew were rendered as thin surface scratches, afterthoughts. The true drawing went *down*. Stairways spiraled through the bedrock like the chambers of a shell. There were vaults beneath the Guild's vaults, older than the founding, older than the ledgers, and at the bottom of everything, at the root of the page where roots belong, was a chamber drawn not in ink but in something raised, embossed, pressed into the paper from behind—as though the map were not depicting the place but *braille-reading* it, remembering it by touch.

And around that deepest chamber, ring upon ring of them, were sigils.

Hers. The mark seared into her palm at her swearing-in, the mark Felix had died naming her for, repeated in concentric circles like the rings of a felled tree—dozens of them, scores, each one a lock, and every lock turned to face inward, toward the center, toward the thing they held.

"Beneath the vault," Ansel said. Her voice sounded far away to her. "Felix said *the vault beneath the vault*. This is under the Founders' Vault. Under the oldest part of the Guild. This is—" She stopped, because the next word was *him*, and saying it aloud in this room felt like striking a match in a powder store. "This is a prison."

Corvane had gone very still. In the lamplight his floating eyes looked almost wet. "Eleven years you've audited for us," he said quietly, "and you never once asked what the Guild was founded *against*. Only what it was founded *for*."

"What is it founded against?"

"I bottle memories, Auditor Nystrom. I don't keep them." He began gathering his instruments with small precise motions, refusing her eyes. "But I'll tell you what an old man notices. Every hundred years or so, the Guild takes in a trade it doesn't record. Something comes down those stairs"—he nodded at the map, at the spiral stairways descending through the drawn bedrock—"something goes into the deep vault, and the ledgers show nothing, and the wax seals change, and everyone above stairs agrees very firmly not to have noticed. My master did it. His master before him. It's called the Quiet Audit." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression Ansel had ever seen on a living face. "You're the first person in forty years to bring a key down here."

"A key?"

"Look at your hand."

She looked. Through the sigil-scarred skin of her palm, faint as a coal's heartbeat, light was moving. Not burning—*answering*. The rings of marks on the map pulsed once, softly, in rhythm with it, and settled.

"The map didn't open because you paid three years," Corvane said. "The map opened because the payment proved you were willing. Locks like this one were never made to keep out thieves, girl. They were made to wait for someone who would give something up to reach them." He wrapped the map and pressed it back into her hands, and his ink-stained fingers trembled against hers. "The founders built a door that only opens for a kind of person they hoped would never exist. Someone sworn to the god. Someone who loved him enough to break the rules keeping him buried. Congratulations. You are the rarest inventory this Guild has ever produced."

Ansel stood in the humming lamplight with a prison drawn on paper in her hands and a hole in her life shaped like three cold years, and she found, examining herself the way she examined a ledger line, that she was not afraid. That was the strangest part. Somewhere in the space where Thornharrow used to be, some younger version of her was kneeling on frozen flags asking a silent god for a sign.

She finally had one.

"Thank you, Master Corvane," she said, and moved toward the door.

"Auditor." She paused. Corvane did not turn from his shelves. "When you go down there—when you go down under the vault, because you will, it's written all over you the way it was written all over Felix—I bottle what people can't afford to keep. But I keep what they can't afford to lose. Understand? Whatever you trade from here on, trade it *here*. Some things survive the glass that don't survive the forgetting."

The enforcers outside the door looked away again as she passed. The corridor beyond was dim and ordinary, smelling of lamp oil and vellum, and Ansel walked it slowly, one hand closed around the map, the other curled around her own scarred palm like a fist around a coal.

Behind her, in the warm and breathing dark of the bottling room, Master Corvane took down a fresh vial, uncorked it, and set it waiting on the black oak bench—for himself, though he did not say so, and for the conversation he knew was coming, though he did not say that either.

Above stairs, in her office at the top of the Guild, the First Auditor finished writing a single line in a ledger no one else would ever see, blotted it neatly, and rang for tea.

*"The key has found the door,"* the line read. *"Prepare the descent."*
