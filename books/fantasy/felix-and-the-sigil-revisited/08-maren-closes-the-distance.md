## 8. Maren Closes the Distance

Maren had learned to count in her brother's absence the way other people learned to pray.

One: the number of letters Felix sent each month, always on the first, always written in that slanted hand that leaned like a man walking into wind. Two: the coins he owed her and would never repay, which she kept track of not because she wanted them but because debts were a way of keeping someone alive in arithmetic when you could not keep them alive any other way. Three: the days since she'd stood over the bloodstained stones outside the Vellum Guild's weighing door and watched a woman with ash-grey eyes walk away carrying something Felix had died to deliver.

Three days. She had counted every hour of them.

Now she stood in the shadow of the Saltmarket's awnings, where fishmongers shouted prices over the reek of brine and the morning crowd moved in its slow river of wool and basket-handles, and she watched that same woman cross the square.

Ansel Nystrom. She'd gotten the name from a dockhand who asked too few questions for coin. A ledger-keeper of the Guild, the man said. One of the quiet ones. The ones who never left the counting rooms except to be sworn in or buried.

Maren turned a copper between her fingers — Felix's habit, not hers, but grief made thieves of all our gestures — and followed her.

The woman walked like someone who had learned to be unremarkable. Not the soldier's walk, not the merchant's strut, but a deliberate smallness, shoulders drawn in, gaze fixed at nothing. It was a good disguise. It was also, Maren thought, a lie told so often it had worn grooves into the body, the way water wears stone. You did not learn to disappear like that unless you had something worth disappearing from.

She let the crowd carry her closer. Close enough to see the ink stains on the woman's right hand, the callus on the middle finger of a lifetime of pens. Close enough to see that she was thinner than she'd looked in the doorway three days ago, that there were shadows under her eyes like bruises that hadn't decided yet.

*You carried my brother's blood out of that doorway,* Maren thought, and the thought had teeth. *You took whatever he gave you, and you walked away, and you did not even look back.*

She had looked back. That was the thing that haunted her about the memory — she had been across the square, half-hidden behind a fruit cart, close enough to see the woman pause at the edge of the Guild's steps and turn, just once, toward the stones where Felix had fallen. Just once. And then gone inside, and the doors had closed, and Maren had spent the rest of that day learning what it felt like to scream without sound.

The market narrowed ahead, the stalls crowding together where the square funneled into Copperlane, and Maren knew the geometry of this city well enough by now to know what came next: an alley behind the tanners' row, short and crooked, the kind of place where a person could be pulled out of the world for the length of time it took to ask a question.

She went in ahead of her. She had always been better at waiting than chasing. Felix used to say she could out-stubborn a mountain, and mountains, he said, were just patience wearing a very large coat.

* * *

Ansel felt the presence before she saw anything — a prickle at the nape, old mercenary instinct waking up under the ledger-keeper's skin like a dog lifting its head. Three days of reading the map in stolen hours, three days of the ink refusing to hold still, three days of the First Auditor's gentle questions coiling around her ankles like smoke, and now this: the animal certainty of being hunted.

She turned into the alley behind the tanners' row because turning anywhere else would have been worse, and because the alley had two exits and she knew both of them.

The woman was already there.

Not large. Not armed, as far as Ansel could see — a knife at her belt, but her hands were empty, hanging loose at her sides in a way that meant they were not actually loose at all. Red-brown hair tied back with a leather cord. Eyes the color of wet slate, and in them a grief so concentrated it had become something else entirely, the way pressure turns coal to diamond or sorrow to purpose.

"You've been following me since the fishmongers," Ansel said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that.

"Since the weighing door," the woman said. "You just didn't see me until today."

"Who are you?"

"You know who I am." The woman stepped forward, and the light from the alley's mouth caught her face, and Ansel saw the family resemblance arrive all at once — the same sharp jaw, the same lean build, the same way of standing as though the ground owed them money. "Or you've guessed. People who guess wrong don't usually stop walking."

"Maren," Ansel said quietly. "Felix's sister."

Something flickered across the woman's face at the name — pain, quickly mastered, folded away like a letter tucked into a coat. "He wrote about me, then."

"He didn't write about anything. He was dying." Ansel heard her own voice harden and did not soften it. "He had maybe a minute of words left in him and he spent them on a title I've never held and a map I can't read. Whatever else you came here to believe about me, believe that I didn't want any of it."

"No." Maren shook her head slowly. "I don't think I will. Because people who don't want things don't hide them. People who don't want things hand them over to their superiors and wash their hands and sleep at night. But you—" she pointed, one finger, steady as a compass needle, "—you've been carrying that map for three days, and the Guild doesn't know, and you haven't slept, and you walk through the Saltmarket like a woman listening for footsteps. So either you're a thief, or you're a coward, or you know something about how my brother died that you're keeping behind your teeth. And I've come to find out which."

The alley was narrow enough that the tannery smell lay over everything like a second skin — lime and salt and something older, the sweetish rot of hides. Somewhere above them a gull screamed and was answered by another. Ansel measured the distance to both exits and found, without surprise, that the woman had chosen her ground well; the far end was blocked by a stacked delivery cart, and the near end was where Maren stood.

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Ansel said. "I count things. Bottled grief, borrowed years, names nobody spoke aloud. I keep accounts for an institution I swore an oath to when I was nineteen years old, and until three days ago I believed in that oath the way you believe in the floor under your feet — without thinking, because thinking about it is how you fall. Your brother put something in my hands that makes the floor feel thin. That's all you'll get from me."

"That's nothing," Maren said. "That's a wall with a door painted on it."

"It's more than I've given anyone."

"Then give me the rest, or take what comes instead." Maren's hands finally came up — not fists, exactly, but ready, the stance of someone who had grown up scrapping in harbor towns and had never entirely unlearned it. "I've spent three days imagining what I'd do if I found the person who was there when he died. I imagined worse things than this. I'm offering you the cheap version. Truth instead of blood. Take it."

Ansel did not move. Under her glove, against her palm, the sigil had begun to warm.

It did that sometimes now — a low heat, like a stone kept near a fire, since the night she'd first unrolled the map. She had told herself it was her own pulse, her own fear, her own body betraying her the way bodies do. But it was getting warmer, and it was getting warmer *now*, in this alley, facing this woman, and that was not coincidence. She had stopped believing in those somewhere between the excised ledgers and the dream she refused to name even to herself.

"I didn't kill him," Ansel said.

"I know you didn't kill him." Maren's voice cracked on it, just barely, a hairline fracture in the stone. "If you'd killed him you'd have taken the map and run, not stood in a doorway looking like the world had ended. I've watched killers. They get lighter after. You got heavier." She took another step. "But you know something. And everything I've learned about the Guild in three days says that knowing something and surviving are two different trades in this city. So either you tell me, or they find out you told me nothing, and then we both learn what they do to people who stand too close to my brother's ghost."

"He was already dying when he reached the door," Ansel said. "Wounds. Old ones, days old, badly tended. Something chased him a long way and nearly caught him. He spent his last strength getting that map to the Guild — no. No, wait." She stopped. Heard herself. Corrected. "Not to the Guild. To *me*. He looked past everyone else at that door. There were six of us within arm's reach and he crawled to me."

The heat under her glove flared.

It was not warmth anymore. It was a brand coming awake, a wire of fire drawn across her palm, and she gasped — she couldn't help it — and tore the glove off, and the sigil blazed into the dim air of the alley like a coal breathed upon: the interlocked lines, the closed eye at its center, seared into her skin since her swearing-in, inert for eleven years, suddenly lit from beneath as though the flesh itself had turned to parchment over flame.

Maren went still.

Not afraid-still. Worse than that. *Recognizing*-still. Ansel watched the fight drain out of the woman's stance and something else flood in behind it, something that made her step backward until her shoulder met the tannery wall, her eyes fixed on Ansel's burning palm the way sailors fix on a wave that is about to break over the deck.

"That mark," Maren whispered. "That's — I know that mark."

"You can't. It's the Guild's oath-mark. Every sworn member carries—"

"*No.*" It came out ragged. Maren fumbled at the collar of her shirt, dragged out a cord, and on it hung a small oilskin packet, soft with handling, opened with fingers that had begun to shake. Inside: a fold of paper, creased to lace, covered in Felix's slanted handwriting. She thrust it at Ansel without letting go of it, so that they held the page between them like a rope over a drop. "Read it. Read what he wrote me in spring. He said he'd found work carrying for a collector, and the collector paid in secrets, and the last job was a map. He drew the seal that was stamped on the case. He drew it twice because he thought his eyes were playing tricks. He said—" her voice failed and she pushed through it, jaw set, tears standing in her eyes without falling, "—he said it was the mark of a god that isn't dead, and if anything happened to him I should find whoever carried that mark, because they'd know why he wasn't coming home."

The sigil burned. The page trembled between their hands. And there it was, in her dead brother's careful ink, drawn twice, annotated in the margins with measurements and doubts: the interlocked lines, the closed eye.

Ansel's mark. Exactly. To the line.

The fire in her palm guttered down to embers, then to warmth, then to nothing, as though satisfied — as though it had done what it came to do. The silence afterward rang louder than the burning had.

"That's impossible," Ansel said, and heard how hollow it sounded. "Eleven years I've worn this. It's a formality. It's ink and ritual and a vow of silence. It doesn't *do* anything."

"Then why did it just light up like a signal fire?" Maren snatched the letter back, cradling it, and her grief had transmuted again — not cooled, never cooled, but redirected, channeled, a river finding new banks. "My brother bled out on your doorstep holding a map sealed with the mark on your hand. He named you Sigil-Bearer with his last breath. And you're telling me it's a *formality*?" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and short. "Nothing about my brother was ever a formality. He didn't crawl to you by accident, ledger-keeper. He chose you. Out of six people. He chose you, and whatever that mark is, it chose you first."

The alley seemed to tilt. Ansel put her bare hand flat against the tannery wall to steady herself and felt the wood cool and ordinary and real, and hated how much she needed it.

"I've been reading the map," she said. The confession came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh — relief and pain in the same motion. "At night. In my room. The ink moves. It won't hold a shape. When I lied about it to myself — when I tried to pretend it was just a surveyor's chart — it burned me. Same hand. Same burn." She flexed her fingers. "Your brother died carrying something that only answers to this mark. To me. And I don't know why. I swear to you, by whatever's left of my oath, I don't know why."

Maren studied her for a long moment. The gulls argued overhead. From the market square came the ordinary music of the city — a bell, a cartwheel, a vendor crying the price of eels — the whole indifferent world going on with its morning while two women stood in a stinking alley trading pieces of a truth neither of them could yet lift alone.

"My brother believed in impossible things," Maren said at last, quieter now. "Our mother called it a sickness. He believed the sea had a memory. He believed names could be lost and found again. He believed—" she pressed the letter briefly to her sternum, "—he believed gods could be buried but not killed. I thought it was poetry. I thought it was the same fever that made him leave home at sixteen with a borrowed boat and no plan." Her slate eyes came up and fixed on Ansel, and there was no mercy in them, but there was something adjacent to mercy, something that had decided to wait. "I'm not leaving this city until I know what he died for. You can help me or you can hinder me. Hindering me will cost you, because I have nothing left to lose and people with nothing left to lose are very expensive to fight."

"And if I help you?"

"Then I'll help you. I'm good at finding things. I found you in three days in a city that swallows people whole." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile, the memory of one, worn smooth by grief. "And I'm good at keeping watch while other people read. My brother said I had the patience of a mountain. He meant it as an insult. I've decided to take it as a profession."

Ansel looked down at her bare palm. The sigil sat quiet now, but changed — she was almost sure it had changed, the lines finer, the closed eye at its center seeming less closed than it had that morning, as though something behind it had shifted its weight.

A hundred years of silence. Excised pages. A god who dreamed at her. A courier who had crossed half a country to die at her feet, calling her a title she'd never earned — or hadn't earned *yet*, said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like the First Auditor at her most gently dangerous.

"The founding ledgers," Ansel said slowly. "Pages cut out the year the god went silent. If the map leads anywhere, it leads through what was removed. There's a depository under the Guildhall where the oldest records are kept. Getting in requires a sworn member." She met Maren's eyes. "Getting in with a stranger who has every reason to burn the place down requires a miracle."

"I've been told I qualify," Maren said dryly, and wound the oilskin packet back into her collar, over her heart, where it clearly lived. "Tonight?"

"Tomorrow. Tonight I have to sit across from the First Auditor at supper and lie to her face, and I need to practice the lying." Ansel pulled her glove back on, and the gesture felt different now — not concealment but armor, and armor for a battle she was only beginning to understand she'd already joined. "There's a shrine to the Drowned Saint off Cooper's Stair. Unaffiliated. Nobody from the Guild prays there — they consider it sentimental. Be there at the ninth bell tomorrow. Come alone."

"I should say the same to you," Maren said. Then, as Ansel turned toward the alley mouth: "Ledger-keeper."

Ansel paused.

"When the time comes — and it's coming, I can see it in how you stand — when you have to choose between your oath and the truth," Maren said, and her voice did not waver at all, "remember that my brother chose the truth with his last breath, and it bought him sixty seconds and a stranger's doorstep. Make it worth more than that."

Ansel did not answer. There was nothing honest to say that wasn't either a promise she couldn't keep or a warning Maren already knew.

She walked out of the alley into the noise and light of the Saltmarket, and behind her she felt the woman's gaze follow like a hand resting lightly between her shoulder blades — not pushing, not yet. Guiding.

Under her glove, faint as a heartbeat remembering itself, the sigil pulsed once.

Twice.

As though, somewhere far below the city, something buried had felt her hesitate at the edge of the dark — and had begun, patiently, to count along with her.
