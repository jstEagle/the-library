## 10. What Ansel Forgot

The morning began with a cup of tea Ansel could not finish, because she could not remember why she had made it.

She stood in the narrow kitchen of her rooms above the counting-house, the clay mug warm in her hands, and stared at it as though it might confess. Chamomile and honey—her mother's recipe, or so the habit of her hands insisted. Her hands knew the proportions. Two pinches of dried flower, one spoon of honey stirred widdershins, three times, no more. But when she reached for the memory behind the habit—the kitchen where she'd learned it, the woman who had taught her, the sound of her voice saying *stir it widdershins, love, or the sweetness won't settle*—there was nothing.

Not fog. Not distance. Nothing. A hole shaped exactly like her mother's face.

Ansel set the mug down on the sill with great care, the way she handled ledgers whose entries frightened her, and pressed her palm flat against the cool stone of the wall. Beneath the skin, the sigil lay quiet. It had been quiet since the bottling room, since the vial of amber light—three years of her, decanted and sealed and traded away like any other impossible thing the Guild kept on its shelves—had passed out of her keeping and into the map's service.

*It's only three years,* she had told herself that night. *Three years out of thirty-one. A tithe.*

But the years had not come out cleanly. That was the thing no one warned you about, trading in borrowed time: memory was not a ledger with neat columns. It was a weave, and pulling three threads from the middle unraveled the edges too. The map had taken her twenty-third through twenty-sixth years—and with them, apparently, the kitchen where chamomile was learned, because her mother had died in her twenty-fourth year, and every memory of her lived inside the stolen span like coins in a purse now locked in a Guild vault.

Ansel breathed slowly. Counted, as she always counted. One for the oath, two for the silence, three for the god who did not answer. It was an old rhythm, older than her swearing-in, and it steadied her the way arithmetic always had.

Then she tried to remember Felix's dying words, and could not.

She knew that he had spoken them. She had been there. She could see the weighing door, the blood spreading black across the threshold stones, his fingers closing around hers with terrible urgency. She could see his mouth moving. But the words themselves were gone—not blurred, not faded, *gone*, as if a clerk had ruled a clean line through them and blotted the page. And in their place, written in the handwriting she had found weeks ago in the Guild's own ledgers, in handwriting that was hers and was not hers, was a single sentence she had never written:

*The door remembers what the bearer forgets.*

Ansel had copied that line into her private notebook eleven times now, hoping repetition would summon context. It refused. It sat on the page like a splinter, working deeper each time she looked at it.

She poured the tea out the window, into the gutter, and went down to work.

* * *

The Vellum House at midmorning smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath both, the faint mineral tang of bottled grief from the cellars below. Ansel moved through the audit floor with her ledger-board under her arm and her face arranged into the mild neutrality the Guild prized in its clerks. Around her, the day's trade murmured along its accustomed rails: a widow purchasing an hour of her husband's laughter, priced by weight; a merchant selling six months of a bad harvest year to a collector who liked his sorrows vintage; a young man, gray at the temples though he couldn't have seen thirty summers, buying back a single afternoon he'd sold in desperation and regretted ever since.

Ansel audited them all. Weighed the vials against the entries, checked the seals, initialed the margins. Her hand did not shake. Her hand never shook. That was the first thing they taught you at the swearing-in: *the ledger does not tremble, therefore neither do you.*

But when a bottler came up from the cellars carrying a tray of new stock—small vials of amber light, each one glowing with the particular warmth of a lived hour—Ansel caught herself searching the tray. Looking, absurdly, for herself. For some glint of her own color among the inventory.

*Somewhere below your feet,* she thought, *there is a shelf with three years of you on it. Labeled. Categorized. Priced.*

She wondered what the label said. *One (1) lot, memories, assorted, 3 yrs, seller: Nystrom, A., condition: lightly wept.* She wondered whether anyone had browsed them yet. Whether some stranger had already purchased an afternoon of her mother's voice without knowing what they carried, the way one buys a book for its binding.

The thought made her ill in a way she could not show. She initialed the tray's entry and sent the bottler on his way.

"You're pale," said Odellin, the senior auditor, passing behind her with his eternal cup of inked tea. He was sixty, dry as vellum, kind in the way of men who had spent forty years watching people sell pieces of themselves and had decided long ago that noticing was as far as his conscience would stretch. "Eat something."

"I ate," Ansel said, and then, horrifyingly, could not remember whether she had. There had been bread, she thought. This morning? Yesterday? The habit of eating persisted; the specific meals were thinning like ink washed in water.

Odellin grunted and moved on. Ansel wrote a small note in her margin—*check food stores, self*—and hated how much it looked like an entry in someone else's ledger. Like she was auditing herself into existence.

* * *

Maren found her at dusk, in the alley behind the counting-house where Ansel had taken to standing when the walls felt too full of listening ears.

The swordswoman leaned against the opposite wall, arms crossed, watching her with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Since the market alley—since the fight that had ended not in blood but in recognition, Maren's blade stopping a finger's width from a sigil she'd seen sketched in her brother's letters—they had met here perhaps a dozen times. Allies of necessity. Sharers of a secret that grew heavier each week.

"You look worse," Maren said, by way of greeting.

"Thank you."

"I mean it. You look—" Maren pushed off the wall and came closer, studying her face the way she studied terrain before a fight. "You've gone thin somewhere. Not the body. Behind it."

"The map is opening," Ansel said, which was true and also an evasion, and Maren's expression said she knew both things. "Another layer gave way last night. We're close to the vault route. Close to the founders' testament, if it exists."

"That's not what I asked."

"You didn't ask anything. You observed."

Maren's jaw tightened. She had Felix's stubbornness, Ansel was learning, and Felix's habit of circling a truth until it had nowhere left to hide. "How many," Maren said quietly. "Since the vial. How many things are gone?"

Ansel opened her mouth to say *a few, only a few, nothing that matters*—and discovered she could not lie. Not because of any geas, but because Maren was looking at her the way Felix had looked at her at the end, and the parallel struck her somewhere beneath the ribs.

"I don't know my mother's face," she said.

The alley went very still. Somewhere above, a pigeon shifted on a ledge.

"I know I had one. I know her name was Sera—I wrote it down after the bottling, in case." Ansel heard her own voice go flat and clerical, the tone she used for damaged goods. "I know she taught me to stir tea widdershins. I know she died when I was twenty-four, which means everything of her lives inside the years I sold. When I try to see her, there's a shape where a person should be, and the shape is empty, and my hands remember her recipe while I stand in my kitchen unable to remember her face." She stopped. Steadied. "That's what's gone. So far."

"And Felix?" Maren asked, and her voice cracked on the name despite all her armor. "His words. At the door. You told me once you remembered them—that they'd matter."

"They would have." Ansel looked down at her hands. "They're gone, Maren. I can see him dying. I can see his mouth move. The words are a ruled-out line. Something took them, and I think—" she pulled out her notebook, opened it to the eleventh copy of the sentence, held it out. "I think this is what's growing in the space where they were."

Maren read it. Read it again. Her lips moved over the words without voicing them.

"*The door remembers what the bearer forgets,*" she said finally. "What does that mean?"

"I believe it means the map is not merely opening. I believe it is *writing itself*, using whatever space it's given. And I am giving it space." Ansel closed the notebook. "Every layer it sheds costs me something. I assumed the price was fixed—three years, paid once. I was wrong. The price is ongoing. It takes as it needs."

"Then stop paying."

"It isn't a choice anymore. The sigil burns when I withhold. The map goes dark. And we are—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the city, toward the vaults, toward the prison beneath the oldest stones, "—we are the only ones who know what the Guild actually is. If I stop, the truth stops with me."

"So you'll hollow yourself out to carry it." Maren stepped closer, and now her voice had lost its edge entirely, which from Maren was more alarming than any threat. "Listen to me, ledger-keeper. I tracked my brother's killer across four hundred miles to put a knife in her. I know what obsession looks like from the inside. It looks exactly like this. It looks like a person telling herself the cost is acceptable because the purpose is holy."

"This isn't vengeance."

"No. It's worse. It's *duty*. Vengeance at least admits it's selfish." Maren's hand rose—hesitated—and then, with a gentleness that seemed to surprise them both, gripped Ansel's shoulder. "You're paying for the truth with the person you were. Do you understand that? Not with coin. Not with years in the abstract. With *yourself*. And the person you're becoming doesn't remember her mother, doesn't remember the man who started all this, doesn't remember why any of it matters except that the sigil says it matters. What happens when it takes the reason you wanted the truth in the first place? What's left to carry it?"

Ansel stood very still under the grip. The wall was cold at her back. The sigil, treacherous thing, was warm.

"There has to be a way to take the years back," she said at last. "The vial still exists. It's in the deep inventory—I checked the cross-indexes. Vault nine, tier three, lot designation—" she recited the number automatically, then faltered, because for a moment she could not recall whether she had memorized it or written it down, and where she had written it, and whether the place she'd written it still existed in her memory either.

"You see?" Maren said softly. "Even your rebellion is being filed away."

They stood together in the dusk, two women bound by a dead man and a buried god, and the lamps of the counting-house flickered alight in the windows above them, one by one, indifferent.

"We get the vial back," Maren said. "Before another layer opens. Before it takes something you can't survive losing. Promise me, Ansel. Swear it on whatever's left of your god."

"He isn't mine to swear on," Ansel said. "He's buried under the Guild. But yes. All right. The vial. Before anything else."

Maren nodded once, satisfied, and turned toward the alley's mouth—and stopped.

Ansel saw it a heartbeat later. Down at the far end, where the alley opened onto the lamplit street, a figure stood in the shadow of the drainpipe arch. Small. Still. Watching them with the patient attention of someone who had been there long enough to hear everything worth hearing.

The figure raised a lantern, and the light fell on a face Ansel knew from a hundred audits and one gentle, dangerous conversation: the First Auditor, wrapped in plain wool, holding her lamp as steadily as she held her ledgers.

"How touching," the First Auditor said, in the tone she used for closing an account. "Do come inside, Auditor Nystrom. Both of you. There are matters on tonight's books that concern you"—and her eyes, kind and terrible, settled on Ansel's scarred palm—"more than you know."
