## 12. Maren's Reckoning

Maren had learned to read a room the way other people read letters: by what was missing from it.

The safehouse Ansel had chosen—a decommissioned tallow-rendering shed behind the chandlers' row, all stone and old fat-smell and one high window that let in a blade of gray light—was missing three things. A second chair. Any sign of the Guild's watchfulness. And any sign of Felix.

That last absence was the one Maren kept circling, the way her tongue kept finding the gap where a tooth had been. Three days since the alley. Three days since she'd put a knife to the throat of the woman who'd walked away from her brother's blood and learned instead that the mark on Ansel's palm matched the sketch Felix had sent her half a year ago, pressed flat between two pages of a letter, with the words *if I don't come home, find the bearer*.

She hadn't come here for truth. She'd come for a name to hate. Hating was a craft she knew; grief was just weather.

But the woman across from her—the apostate, the Sigil-Bearer, this Ansel Nystrom with her ink-stained fingers and her ledger-keeper's stillness—had spent those three days doing something Maren had not expected. She had been showing Maren everything.

The map, unrolled on the tallow-stained table, its ink shifting like fish under ice whenever Maren looked at it directly. The founding ledgers, copied out in Ansel's careful hand because the originals were watched. The excised pages, their stubs like amputations. And last night, by candlelight, the testament itself—the founders' confession, pulled from a lead box beneath a shrine to a god nobody prayed to anymore, written in a hand that shook with either age or shame.

Maren had read it twice. She had not slept. She did not intend to sleep until she understood what it meant for Felix, and so far all it had given her was a century of lies and no closer to the only fact that mattered: who killed her brother, and why.

"You keep looking at me like I'm an entry you can't balance," Ansel said. She sat with her scarred palm flat on the table, as if she'd learned to stop hiding it. "Ask."

"I've been asking."

"You've been accusing. It's different." Ansel's voice wasn't sharp. That was the worst part of her—nothing about her was sharp, she was all patience and dry arithmetic, and it made Maren's fury feel like shouting at a wall built specifically to absorb shouting. "You want to know how he died. Not who. How."

Maren's hands curled on the table. "He was found at your weighing door with his ribs opened and his blood cooling on Guild stones. I don't need philosophy. I need the order of events."

"Then let me give them to you in order." Ansel turned her palm up. The sigil caught the gray light, a brand of interlocking lines that seemed, as always, almost to move. "And then you can decide whether to finish what you started in that alley."

* * *

She told it plainly, which Maren came to understand was the only mercy she offered: without ornament, without softening, without once asking to be believed.

Felix had come to the city four months ago. Not as a courier—not officially. He'd carried letters for a circle of people the Guild called *irregulars*: archivists, a disgraced bottler, an old priest of the silent god who kept a chapel no one attended. They had been piecing together what the founders' testament confirmed—that the Vellum Guild had not been built to serve the god whose mark its mercenaries wore, but to bury him, and to build a trade on the burial. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names never spoken. All of it priced against a silence the Guild itself had manufactured.

"And my brother," Maren said, when Ansel paused, "was running messages for them."

"He was more than a courier by the end." Ansel's thumb moved over the sigil, unconscious, like a tongue finding the tooth-gap. "The irregulars trusted him because he was fast, and cheerful, and nobody watches a cheerful man. But he read everything he carried. He asked questions. By midwinter he knew more about the prison than most of the Guild's own enforcers—he just didn't know where it was. The map was the last piece. He stole it from the First Auditor's own archive."

"The map that's on your table."

"The map that burned me every time I lied about it." Ansel said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "It's keyed to the sigil. To me. Which means—" she stopped, and Maren watched her decide to say the rest, "—which means somewhere in his theft, Felix learned what a Sigil-Bearer was. What I was. He didn't press it into my hands because I was nearest. He chose me."

Maren stood up. Her chair went over backward and neither of them reached for it.

"He chose you," she repeated. "A stranger. A Guildswoman. And he died for it, and you want me to be grateful."

"I want you to know it." Ansel hadn't moved. "There's a difference, and I think you're the kind of person who knows it even when it hurts."

* * *

The order of events, then, as Ansel laid it out, cross-checked against the irregulars' surviving letters and the Guild's own gate-logs, which she could still read because no one had thought to revoke her access yet:

Felix had left the city on the first of Thaw-Month with the map sewn into his coat lining. He was making for the shrine outside Correnvale where the old priest kept records older than the Guild—records that might say whether breaking the seal would free the god or simply open the cage onto a war. He never reached Correnvale.

The road got him first. Not enforcers. The road.

"There were bandits on the ferry crossing," Ansel said. "Three travelers died that week. The gate-log at Correnvale notes a man matching Felix's description brought in half-dead, fevered, robbed of everything except—" she tapped the table, "—except the thing sewn into his lining, which they never found, because he'd moved it into the wound itself. Wrapped in oilcloth, packed under the bandage. He let them cut him and rob him and search him, and they found nothing, because he'd hidden the map inside his own body."

Maren made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from herself.

"He turned back toward the city because the shrine was watched—he'd seen enforcers on the road ahead, waiting. He was already dying. So he did the arithmetic couriers do. Fastest route, least watched gate, and one person inside the walls who bore the mark and might be reached before the Guild reached him." Ansel's voice stayed level, but her hand had gone white around the table's edge. "The enforcers didn't hunt him down, Maren. They followed the blood. He collapsed at the weighing door with the map already in his fist, and they finished him because a dead courier can't testify, and because the First Auditor wanted the map back and wanted no questions about why a dying man had been calling for the Sigil-Bearer."

"So they murdered him." Maren heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used on men who beat their wives and called it discipline. "They stood over a dying man and—"

"They finished what the road began," Ansel said. "Yes. And I have spent every night since asking myself whether walking past that door, an hour earlier, would have changed anything, and every night the answer is no, and every night it doesn't help."

Something in Maren broke—not loudly. It broke the way a rope breaks, fiber by fiber, under a load it was never rated for. She had built six months of vengeance on a simple structure: *the Guild killed Felix, therefore destroy the Guild, therefore the pain has a shape.* And now the structure was still true—the Guild had killed him, she wasn't letting them off that—but underneath it lay a worse truth, load-bearing and terrible: *the Guild hadn't needed to chase him. He had run himself to death carrying a cause, and they had merely arrived in time to take credit.*

Her brother hadn't been a victim stumbling into knives. He had been a soldier in a war she hadn't known existed, and he had died winning his piece of it, and the map was in her sight right now because he had gotten it through.

"Felix," she said, and her voice cracked on the name like ice on a pond. "You absolute, stubborn, cheerful idiot."

Ansel said nothing. She picked the fallen chair up and set it upright, and waited, because she was apparently a person who understood that some griefs need the floor cleared before they can fall.

* * *

Maren walked. That was her way—she'd walked the length of two counties tracking rumors of her brother, walked out of her marriage, walked out of her village when they buried an empty coffin and expected her to weep prettily over it. She walked the perimeter of the rendering shed, past the old boiling-coppers crusted with a century of tallow, and let the truth settle into her the way cold settles into a house: room by room, corner by corner.

She thought about the letters. Felix had written her every month, always the same mixture of gossip and nonsense and, threaded through it, thin as wire, the real news. *The Guild pays well for tears, Mare. Actual bottled tears. Who weeps enough to fill a vial, do you suppose? Who's being harvested?* And later: *I met a priest today who says our grandfathers' grandfathers didn't worship nothing. They worshipped something so big the Guild had to sit on it.* And the last letter, the one she'd read forty times looking for a code that wasn't there: *If I don't come home, find the bearer. Trust the ledger-keeper. She doesn't know yet what she is.*

*Trust the ledger-keeper.* He'd known. Months ago, dying on a road, he'd already chosen Ansel Nystrom, a woman he'd never met, because of a mark and a title and something the irregulars had divined that Maren still couldn't see.

And Maren had put a knife to that woman's throat.

She came back to the table. Ansel had not used the time to rest; she'd copied another page, her pen moving in small precise strokes, and there was a cup of tea pushed to the far side of the table—pushed toward Maren's side, still hot, set there without comment sometime during the walk. Maren stared at it. Small kindnesses were going to undo her faster than any knife.

"Tell me about the war," she said. "The one he was caged to end. Tell me what my brother died carrying."

So Ansel told her. The genocide committed in the god's name—or rather, committed by men wearing his name while he screamed into a silence that wasn't death but burial. The founders who had offered him a bargain dressed as mercy: sleep, and the killing stops. The trade empire raised on top of the sleeping god like a city on a mass grave. And the part that made Maren's blood run cold and hot at once: the ledgers were empty now. The silence was running out. The Guild was dying of the very thing it had stolen, and it knew it, and it needed a Sigil-Bearer to break the seal on its own terms.

"And if the seal breaks wrong?" Maren asked.

"If it breaks wrong, we learn whether the war is really over, or whether it was only waiting." Ansel finally looked tired. "Your brother believed it could be done right. The priest believes it. I don't know what I believe. I know what I owe."

"What do you owe?"

"A dead man chose me. That's not a debt you can decline." Ansel folded her hands. "I'm going to break it, Maren. The sigil. The seal. Whatever it actually is. I'm going to do it carefully, and I'm probably going to die doing it, and none of that requires you to forgive me. But it would go better with you."

Maren laughed—one short, ugly bark, surprise catching her off guard. "Go better with me. You've seen me fight."

"In the alley. Yes." For the first time in three days, something moved through Ansel's face that wasn't patience—it was almost humor, dry as old paper. "You fight like someone who's already forgiven herself for whatever she's about to do. It's terrifying. I'd rather it be pointed at the Guild."

Maren sat down. She took the tea, because refusing it suddenly felt like refusing Felix, and drank it scalding, and let it burn a path down through her chest where the cold had been living.

Here was the thing she understood now, sitting in a tallow-shed with an apostate and a shifting map: vengeance needed a target, and the Guild was still a target, but vengeance alone wouldn't carry her where Felix had died trying to go. You couldn't stab a lie to death. You couldn't knife a century of silence. What Felix had carried wasn't a grievance—it was a truth, and truths needed bearers, plural, needed sisters and strangers and stubborn ledger-keepers, needed people willing to pay in memory and blood and years.

"My brother stole a map from the most powerful woman in this city," Maren said slowly, "hid it in his own wound, walked himself half to death, and delivered it to exactly the right person. That's not luck. That's Felix." She set the cup down. "So here's what happens now. We finish it. Not for revenge. Revenge is what I came with; I'm leaving it in this shed. We finish it because he asked, and because a god is buried under a counting-house, and because somebody has to remember all of it out loud."

Ansel extended her hand across the table—the scarred palm up, the sigil bare between them, an offer and a warning in one gesture.

Maren took it. The mark was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything about this was going to be warm and terrible and true.

"Then we should sleep," Ansel said, "because tomorrow we go down."

"Down where?"

Ansel's eyes went to the high window, to the gray light beyond it, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet in a way that made Maren's skin prickle.

"The undercity. Where the Guild keeps the borrowed years." A pause. "And where, last night, my sigil started burning hard enough to wake me. Something down there knows we're coming, Maren. I think it's the door."
