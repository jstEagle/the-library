## 13. Apostate

The First Auditor's office smelled of cedar and old decisions.

Ansel stood before the great desk with its surface of polished vellum-wood, and she understood, finally, why the Guild built its seats from the same material as its ledgers. Everything here was written. Everything here could be rewritten. The woman behind the desk—Serah Voss, First Auditor of the Vellum Guild, keeper of a hundred years of careful silence—watched her with the patient expression of someone who had already counted every possible answer and found none of them troubling.

"You would be restored," Voss said again, gently, as if Ansel were a ledger entry gone smudged rather than a person standing on the far edge of her own life. "Your memories returned in full. The three years you traded in the bottling room, poured back into you like water into a dry cup. Your rank confirmed. Your name entered in the Roll of Honor, which has not been opened since the Founding. And the map—" Voss extended one hand across the desk, palm up, an open invitation that was also a cage door held wide. "The map comes home to us, and all of this is forgiven before it was ever committed."

*Before it was ever committed.* As if forgiveness were a currency the Guild could mint in advance, spend against sins not yet chosen. Ansel almost admired it. It was exactly the kind of impossible thing her Guild traded in: absolution sold at retail, guilt bought back at a discount.

Her sigil burned under her glove. It had been burning for two days now—not the flare of recognition she'd felt in Maren's alley, not the hot pulse of the dream where the god had spoken, but a constant low fire, like a coal wrapped in wool against her skin. *I know where you are,* it seemed to say. *I know what you are deciding.*

She thought about the three years in the vial. She could not remember them—that was the nature of the trade—but she knew their shape the way an amputee knows a limb. There had been a winter market, she was almost certain, and someone who had laughed at her terrible haggling. A teacher whose name lived now in glass instead of in her. Small things. The kind of small debts her ledgers were named for.

And she thought about the undercity. The people stacked like cordwood in the borrowed-years vaults, their time leaking out of them century by century so that merchants in the upper city could buy an extra decade of youth, an extra season of profit. She thought of the founders' testament, hidden beneath the salt flats, its ink still wet with confession after a hundred years: *We did not serve him. We buried him. We built our fortune on his silence.*

"We did not serve him," Ansel said aloud.

Voss's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted—the smallest recalculation, a clerk moving a figure from one column to another. "Ansel."

"He agreed to be buried," Ansel went on, and her voice surprised her with its steadiness. "To stop a war. To stop a genocide carried out in his name. And your founders took that mercy and turned it into a monopoly. They've been selling his silence for a hundred years. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names that were never spoken." She pulled off her left glove and laid her bare hand on the desk, sigil upward, seared and shining faintly in the lamplight. "This is not a mark of service, Auditor. It's a brand. It always was. I just didn't know what animal I belonged to."

"Sit down, Ansel." Still gentle. Still terribly kind. That was the horror of Serah Voss; she had never once needed to raise her voice, because the entire architecture of the Guild raised it for her. "You are tired, and frightened, and you have been listening to ghosts. The god is dead. Whatever spoke to you in your dreams was memory, or madness, or the last residue of a seal decaying—which is precisely why the map must come back to us. If the prison fails, everything the Founders purchased with their lives comes undone. The war returns. Do you understand what that means? Cities. Ansel. Whole peoples, erased, because a mercenary with a scarred hand read a dead man's map out loud."

"The war doesn't return if he's remembered," Ansel said. "That's what he asked for. Not freedom. *Remembrance.* You could have given him that at any point in a hundred years. Instead you cut the pages out of your own founding ledgers."

Something moved through the room then—a pressure, a silence with weight to it—and Ansel realized the lamps had dimmed, though no one had touched them. Voss looked at the sigil on Ansel's hand for a long moment, and when she looked back up, the kindness was still there but it had gone thin as gilt over iron.

"I offered you restoration," Voss said. "I want it noted—I want it *entered*—that I offered you restoration first."

"Noted," Ansel said. "Enter it. And enter this too."

She turned her hand over and pressed her sigil flat against the desk's vellum-wood surface, and the burn flared white, and where her palm touched, the wood darkened as though scorched by a seal of its own. Not a message. Just a mark. Proof of presence. *I was here, and I refused.*

When she lifted her hand, Voss was already reaching for the small brass bell that sat beside her inkwell—the bell that summoned enforcers, that sealed doors, that began, in the Guild's cold liturgy, the process of unmaking a person's name.

Ansel was moving before the bell finished its first note.

* * *

The bottling room was chaos when she burst through it, shelves rocking, vials chiming against one another like a thousand tiny bells in mourning. She had perhaps four minutes. Perhaps less. Enforcers in the Guildhouse moved like ink spreading through water—quietly, everywhere at once.

Maren was waiting where Ansel had told her to wait, wedged in the shadow between the grief-shelves and the wall, arms crossed, a knife loose in her grip the way other people held opinions. Her eyes found Ansel's immediately, and read them just as fast.

"You refused."

"I refused."

"Then we're running." Maren pushed off the wall. No argument, no *are you sure*, no recrimination. Felix's sister had a talent, Ansel was learning, for arriving at the necessary thing without wasting a single step on the way there. "Which way?"

"Down and east. The scribes' stair, then the tannery lane, then—" Ansel stopped. Her sigil had flared, sudden and specific, a hot wire drawn tight toward the north wall. Toward the oldest part of the Guildhouse. Toward the vaults above the vaults, above the place the map pointed to, deep beneath the city where a buried god lay dreaming his long dream of being remembered.

"What?" Maren said. "What is it?"

"It knows I've decided." Ansel flexed her burned hand, watching the light move under the skin of the sigil like a fish under ice. "The god—or the prison, I don't know which anymore. But it knows. It's been pulling since I walked into her office. Like a tide going out." She met Maren's eyes. "It wants me to come down."

"Then we go down." Maren said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. "After Felix died I spent four months learning to follow the things that pull. Roads. Rumors. Letters in my brother's handwriting that arrived after he was dead." Her jaw tightened. "Everything that pulls is worth following. Everything else is just waiting to be buried."

They ran.

* * *

The scribes' stair was a spiral of worn stone behind the copying hall, and they took it two steps at a time while somewhere above them the Guildhouse began, floor by floor, to close itself. Ansel heard it happening—the great weighing door dropping its counterweighted slab, the portcullis over the delivery arch singing down its channel, the shutters of the counting windows slamming one after another like a slow round of applause for the condemned. The Guild sealing its own skin. By morning there would be no legal way out of the building, and by evening, if Voss moved quickly, no legal way out of the city at all.

"They'll declare you by sundown," Ansel said, taking the turn at the bottom so fast her shoulder struck the wall. "Apostate. It hasn't been declared in ninety years. The last one was a bottler who tried to sell stolen years back to the families they came from."

"What happened to him?"

"He's in the undercity. What's left of him. They don't kill apostates, Maren. They *file* them." She shoved open the stair door into the tannery lane, where the smell of lime and hides covered the sound of their footsteps, and the afternoon light fell slantwise through drying skins like amber through honey. "Every record of you becomes a debt record. Every friend becomes a liability. Every door you ever knocked on gets a visit from an auditor with a very polite voice and a very full ledger. They erase you by making you expensive to know."

"Then we'd better become cheap," Maren said. "Fast."

They crossed the tannery yard dodging racks of stretched hide, slipped through the gap in the east wall that Ansel had used twice in her life for reasons she could no longer remember—one of the lost years, probably, some errand or escape now sealed in a vial in the bottling room—and came out into the warren of streets below the Guild quarter, where the city changed its accent and its smell and pretended, as it always did, that the towers above it were weather rather than power.

By the time they reached the Fountain of Small Debts—a chipped marble basin where the poor of the district traditionally left IOUs folded under stones, a folk custom the Guild had never managed to tax—the bells had begun.

Not the Guild bells. The *city* bells. Gate bells, quarter bells, the deep bronze voice of the Citadel ringing the pattern Ansel had only ever heard described in contingency ledgers she'd audited herself, years ago, without understanding what she was reading: *Seal Pattern Nine. Seal Pattern Nine. All gates closed. All traffic stopped.*

"They're sealing the whole city," Maren breathed. "For one apostate?"

"For one Sigil-Bearer." Ansel said the title out loud for the second time in her life, and this time it didn't feel like a stranger's word. It felt like a key turning. "Felix didn't die carrying a map, Maren. He died carrying a coronation. Voss knows what I am now. What I might do. The city isn't a trap for me—it's a *seal*. They're going to hold me inside the walls until they can take me down to the vault themselves, on their terms, with their chains, and put my hand on that door the way *they* want it done."

"Then we get to the door first."

Ansel looked at her. In the slanting light, with the bells rolling overhead and the fountain muttering its small debts, Maren looked nothing like a grieving sister and everything like something older—like the women in the pre-Founding woodcuts Ansel had catalogued and dismissed, the ones who had followed the god before he had a Guild to speak for him. Barefoot believers with knives in their belts and no institution between them and the sky.

"There are six gates," Ansel said slowly. "But we don't need a gate. We need the undercity entrance in the Saltmarket. The old cistern stair. It's how the borrowed years move—carted down at night, away from eyes. If the Guild hoards time, Maren, then the undercity is where they keep it, and the cistern stair goes down past the year-vaults to the foundations. To *his* foundations."

"And the enforcers?"

"Some will already be below. Voss won't wait for night." Ansel flexed her hand again. The burn had settled into a steady rhythm now, and the rhythm was familiar in a way that made her throat tighten, because it matched—exactly, patiently—the pace of a walking heart. Hers. The sigil beat when she beat. It wanted what she wanted. Or it had decided, sometime in the last hour, to make sure she wanted the right thing. "But most of them will be looking for us *above*. Apostates run for the gates. That's the pattern. That's what the ledgers say apostates do."

"And you?" Maren asked. "What do the ledgers say you'll do?"

Ansel thought of the entry in her own handwriting she had never written, a hundred pages ago, in a ledger of small debts: *Paid in full — the bearer comes down.* She had spent weeks wondering whose hand that was. She wasn't wondering anymore.

"The ledgers say," she said, "that I stop reading them."

* * *

The Saltmarket at dusk was a country of its own: fish scales and lamp oil, hawkers crying the last of their stock, children weaving between cart wheels with the speed of things that had never been counted in any ledger. Above them the gate bells were still tolling, and the crowd's mood was curdling—sealed gates meant shortages, shortages meant prices, prices meant fear. Ansel kept her gloved hand tucked in her coat and walked the way she'd been taught to walk through any audit: unhurried, unremarkable, a woman with nowhere particular to be.

Maren walked half a step behind and to the left, covering her blind side, and neither of them had discussed it. Some alliances write themselves.

The cistern stair opened off the Saltmarket's northeast corner behind a shuttered dye-house, a stone arch choked with two generations of graffiti, and Ansel had her hand on the cold iron of the door when the crowd around them went quiet—not silent, but *quiet*, the particular hush of many people pretending not to look at something.

Three enforcers stood at the mouth of the lane. Grey coats, grey gloves, faces like filed documents. The one in front held a scroll, and even at this distance Ansel recognized the format: a Declaration of Apostasy, still warm, the wax seal not yet fully cooled. Voss worked fast. Voss had probably drafted it before Ansel left the office, the way the Guild minted forgiveness in advance; apparently it minted condemnation the same way.

"Ansel Nystrom," the enforcer read, and his voice carried the flat clarity of a man reciting in a room designed for recitation. "By order of the First Auditor, you are declared apostate to the Vellum Guild. Your accounts are frozen. Your name is struck. Your debts are called." He rolled the scroll closed. "Come quietly, and the declaration can be amended. The Guild amends things. It's what we do."

Behind her, Ansel felt Maren shift—weight to the balls of the feet, knife hand loose—and she put out her arm without looking, a bar across Maren's chest.

"No," Ansel said.

The enforcer tilted his head. "No?"

"No amendment. No quiet." And here was the strange thing, the thing she would carry with her down every stair that followed: she was not afraid. Under the burn of the sigil, under the bells, under the eyes of a street full of strangers who were memorizing her face for the reward that would surely follow, Ansel Nystrom felt—for the first time since Felix had bled out against the weighing door—entirely, terrifyingly *unburdened*. Every duty she had owed was owed to a lie. Every oath she had sworn had been sworn to a jailer. The Guild had spent this whole day stripping things from her—her rank, her name, her accounts, her future—and what remained underneath was lighter than anything she had carried in years.

Just the truth. Just the map. Just the door.

"My debts are called?" she said. "Good. Then let the ledger show they're paid." She pulled off her glove, and held her bare hand high, and let the sigil blaze in the dusk light where three enforcers and two hundred Saltmarket strangers could see it—the mark of the god who did not die, burning on the hand of the woman the Guild had just disowned. Gasps moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Somewhere a child asked her mother what the light was, and the mother had no answer.

"That mark," Ansel said, loudly enough for the whole lane, "is older than your Guild. And it's coming home."

Then she wrenched open the cistern door, and Maren slipped through ahead of her, and Ansel followed into the dark—down the stair that smelled of salt and centuries, toward the year-vaults and the foundations and the buried patience of a god who had waited a hundred years to be remembered.

Above her, muffled by stone, the gate bells rang on.

Below her, in time with her heartbeat, the sigil burned brighter with every step.
