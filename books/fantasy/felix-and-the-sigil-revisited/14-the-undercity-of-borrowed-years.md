## 14. The Undercity of Borrowed Years

The stair went down farther than the city had a right to be deep.

Ansel counted the steps at first—mercenary's habit, auditor's habit, a way of keeping the world legible when everything else had come loose from its bindings—but she lost the count somewhere past four hundred, when the walls stopped being dressed stone and became something older, veined with pale mineral light that seemed to seep out of the rock itself rather than fall upon it. The air changed too. It grew thick, sweetish, like the air inside the bottling room, like the smell of a vial uncorked, and Maren noticed it at the same moment she did because Maren stopped on the step above her and said, quietly, "It smells like him."

"Like Felix?"

"Like the last hour of him." Maren's voice did not waver. Ansel was learning that it never did; grief in her had been smelted into something harder than the original ore. "They keep it, you know. Grief. I've seen the bottles in the market stalls—*vintage sorrow, gently wept.* I always thought it was a figure of speech."

"It wasn't," Ansel said.

The map led them down. It had led them through the sealed door behind the tannery row, through the corridor where the Guild's oldest vaults began their patient descent, and now through this stair that no ledger she had ever audited mentioned—which was itself an entry of a kind. Ansel Nystrom had spent eleven years learning what the Guild chose to write down. She was only now beginning to learn the shape of what it chose not to.

Her sigil burned. It had not stopped burning since the gates sealed behind them, since the First Auditor's voice had followed them through the streets in the form of criers and bells and the soft, terrible courtesy of enforcers asking doorwardens to report two women traveling together, one marked. But here, descending, the burn changed character. It was no longer the flat heat of pursuit. It pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. Like something breathing under a great weight.

"He knows we're coming," Maren said. She had seen Ansel flexing her hand against her thigh, again and again, the way a person worries a wound.

"I don't know if it's him," Ansel said. "Or the prison. I'm starting to think they might not be the same thing anymore. A man buried alive for a hundred years—" She stopped. Swallowed. "A god buried alive. He might be mostly prison by now."

Maren looked at her sidelong, and in the pale mineral light her face was all angles and shadow. "That's the cruelest thing you've said yet."

"It's the kindest thing I know how to say."

* * *

They came out of the stair into a gallery so vast that the mineral light failed before it reached any wall, and for a long moment Ansel thought they were looking at the night sky underground—a field of dim stars arranged in rows, in terraces, in long receding avenues that descended in gentle tiers toward a darkness below.

Then the nearest star moved, and sighed, and coughed.

"They're people," Maren breathed.

They were. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, perhaps—the gallery was a city of cots and pallets and low stone bunks carved directly from the living rock, tier upon tier, and each bunk held a sleeper. Not dead. Ansel's mercenary eye knew death intimately and this was not it. These people breathed. Their chests rose and fell with terrible slowness—one breath to every ten, to every twenty, to every thirty of Ansel's own—and their faces were slack with an age that did not match their bodies. Some looked young. Young men with smooth cheeks and hair going white at the temples. Young women whose hands, folded on their breasts, were spotted and knuckled like their grandmothers'. One child—Ansel made herself look, made herself count the years written on that small sleeping face—looked eight years old and had the eyes of someone who had watched eighty winters pass from inside a dream she could not wake from.

"Borrowed years," Ansel whispered. The words came out of her like something pulled with forceps. "I audit them. Line item. *Years, borrowed, interest accruing.* I've signed off on transfers. Hundreds of them. Thousands." Her voice cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there. "I never asked where the years came from. I never asked who they came *out of*."

"You didn't know," Maren said.

"I signed my name next to it. Eleven years. My name is in those ledgers, Maren. My handwriting is on the receipts of this." She gestured, and her arm shook, and she hated it. "You wanted vengeance for your brother. I want to know what I am."

Maren took her wrist—not the sigil-scarred one, the other—and held it hard enough to mean it. "You're the woman who walked down here to look. That's more than anyone up there has done in a hundred years."

But even Maren's grip could not steady what Ansel saw when they stepped off the stair and onto the first terrace, and a sleeper near the edge stirred—not waking, never waking, but turning, the way the dying turn toward a voice—and Ansel saw the mark on his throat.

Not her sigil. Something else. A small stamped seal, neat as a wax impression on a letter: the Vellum Guild's weighmark, pressed into the skin itself, over the artery.

"They're tagged," Maren said. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Ansel had learned was worse than loud. "Like cargo."

"Like accounts receivable."

There was a placard set into the stone at the head of each bunk. Ansel knelt beside the nearest one and read it by the light of her own burning palm, holding her scarred hand up like a lamp, which was a thing she would have found blasphemous a month ago and now did without thinking. The placard was brass, engraved in the Guild's tidy bookhand:

*Account 44,117. Debtor: unnamed (consent waived per Founders' Charter, §9). Principal: 34 years. Interest: compounding. Status: dormant. Harvest schedule: see Annex.*

*Consent waived.*

She read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves the way the map's ink did; they simply sat there, being true.

"They sold their years," she said slowly, working it aloud because saying it was the only way to make herself believe it. "No—that's the story we tell upstairs. That's the fair trade, the honest contract: years for coin, freely given, the Guild's whole founding virtue. *We do not steal, we lend.*" She touched the brass edge of the placard. It was warm. Everything down here was faintly, horribly warm, as if the whole gallery were a body. "But these ones didn't sell. §9. Consent waived. Which means somewhere in the Charter there's a clause that lets the Guild take years without asking, and I have read the Charter, Maren, I have *audited* the Charter, and I never—"

"You never looked for it," Maren finished. "Because you trusted the hand that wrote it."

Ansel said nothing. There was nothing to say that the sleeping man beside her was not already saying better, with his young-old face and his stamped throat and his thirty-four harvested years ticking away in some vault above them, earning interest for strangers.

* * *

They walked the terraces.

It was Maren who first understood the arrangement of them—she had Felix's mind for patterns, though she'd have driven a knife into anyone who told her so—and she stopped at the third tier and stood with her arms folded, staring down the long descending avenues of sleepers, and said, "It's sorted. By year. Look—the top rows breathe fastest. The deeper down, the slower."

Ansel looked, and saw it. The uppermost sleepers drew a breath every few heartbeats. The lowest, far below in the dark, might draw one breath an hour. Might draw one a day. Time pooled down here the way water pools in a cellar, and the Guild had dammed it, tiered it, *banked* it.

"The deepest ones," Ansel said. "Those are the old debts. Century-old. The founding harvests." And then, because the thought arrived whole and terrible: "Some of these people were put under before the god went silent. They've been asleep longer than he's been buried."

"They'll wake," Maren said. It was not a question, but it was close to one, and Ansel heard the plea folded inside it—the sister in Maren reaching for the mercenary in Ansel, both of them needing the answer to be yes.

"When the debt clears. When the principal's repaid and the interest runs dry." Ansel's mouth was dry as ledger-dust. "Which means most of them wake owing nothing, having lived nothing, into a world where everyone they knew is dead. If they wake at all. Dormant accounts get written off, Maren. I've written off dormant accounts. It's a Tuesday."

Maren turned and looked at her fully, and for a moment Ansel braced for the blade of her—for the cold fury that had cornered her in a market alley and put a knife to her ribs—but what came instead was worse. Pity.

"You're doing it again," Maren said. "Turning it into numbers so you don't have to feel it."

"That's what I'm *for*. That's what the Guild made me. I'm the person who makes horror arithmetic so decent people can sleep."

"Then stop being what they made you." Maren nodded down the terrace, toward a figure moving between the bunks. "Start being what you broke the oath to become. Because someone down here is awake."

* * *

The warden of the gallery—if warden was the word—was a thin old woman with a lamplighter's pole and a face like crumpled parchment, and she did not startle at the sight of them, which told Ansel she was either blind or had seen stranger things descend this stair. She carried a small brass bell that she rang, once, softly, at the foot of certain bunks as she passed. Not an alarm. A lullaby. Or a vigil.

"You're the marked one," the woman said, when they drew near. Her eyes went to Ansel's hand, still glowing faintly, and she did not flinch. "Word travels faster than fugitives. Even down here."

"You live down here?" Maren asked.

"I keep it." The woman lifted her chin at the fields of sleepers. "Someone has to ring the hours. Someone has to turn them, so the flesh doesn't fail before the term does. My mother kept before me. Hers before her." She smiled, and it was the saddest economy of a smile Ansel had ever seen. "We're part of the operating costs. Line item. You'd know."

Ansel felt the words go into her like a splinter. "How many keepers are there?"

"One per gallery. There are nine galleries."

Nine galleries. Ansel did the arithmetic before she could stop herself—nine times the thousands she could see, tiered and banked and breathing at quarter-speed—and the number came out to something north of a hundred thousand souls. A city. A buried city the size of Greyharbor, sleeping off debts most of them had never agreed to.

"Why do you stay?" Maren asked. "You could walk up that stair. Tell the world. Who'd stop you?"

"Tell them what?" The keeper rang her little bell at a sleeper's bunk—tink, tink, gentle as rain—and moved on. "That the price of their comfortable years is paid by people who never got to spend theirs? Madam, half the city *knowS*. The Guild sells bottled grief in the open market. Nobody asks where the bottle comes from because nobody wants to be the answer." She looked back at them, and her old eyes were not dull at all; they were the sharpest things in the gallery. "You want to know what a century of hoarded years does? Don't look at the sleepers. Look at the ones who sold. Go up two levels, to the Waking Ward. See the ones who got their coin and came back hollow. Then you'll understand why the Guild needed a god to bury."

* * *

The Waking Ward was smaller, and louder, and worse.

These were the sellers—the voluntary ones, the ones whose contracts Ansel had audited with a clean conscience and a clean pen. They had traded five years, ten years, twenty, for coin or cure or a sibling's ransom, and the Guild had been scrupulously fair about it, and they had come back from the borrowing with the exact number of years restored to their bodies and something else subtracted entirely.

They sat in rows in a warm stone hall, doing nothing. Not resting—doing *nothing*, in the particular way of people who have forgotten that hands can be for making things. A woman sat braiding and unbraiding the same strand of rope, over and over, her fingers quick and her eyes absent. A man who could not have been forty sat telling a story to no one, and Ansel caught fragments of it as they passed: a wedding, a daughter, a storm at sea—and then the story looped, seamlessly, back to the wedding, and the daughter was new again, and the storm was coming for the first time. He had sold ten years and the ten years he'd bought back were not the same ten. The Guild returned principal. It could not return the living of it.

"They gave me the years back," the rope-braiding woman said suddenly, to Ansel, without looking up. Her voice was pleasant, empty, a room after the furniture's gone. "Every one. I counted. I count everything now. Counting is easy. Counting doesn't need the parts they took." She looked up then, and smiled with perfect sweetness. "Do you know what they take? Everyone asks. They take the *wanting*. Ten years of wanting things. Wanting is heavy. They skim it off so the years store well." She went back to her rope. "I don't miss it. That's the trick. You can't miss what wanting feels like. Isn't that restful?"

Ansel walked out of the Waking Ward with her hand pressed flat against the wall and her breath coming wrong, and Maren followed, and neither spoke until the sleepers' silence closed around them again.

"My brother," Maren said at last, "sold two years. Did you know that? Before he ran messages for the Guild, before the map—he sold two years to pay our mother's fever-debt. He came back and he was *lighter*. I thought it was relief. I thought the debt had been weighing him." Her jaw worked. "He was lighter because they'd skimmed him. And then he found out what the skimming was, didn't he. That's why he stole the map. That's why he died carrying it."

"Maren—"

"Don't." But she let Ansel put a hand on her shoulder anyway, and for a moment the hunter and the apostate stood together in the breathing dark, two women tallying a debt that had no ledger large enough to hold it.

* * *

It was the sigil that ended the argument about whether to go deeper.

It flared—truly flared, for the first time since the gates, light pouring between Ansel's fingers like blood from a clenched fist—and down in the lowest dark of the ninth gallery, something answered. Not a sound. A pressure. A slow tidal shift in the warm air, as if the whole buried city had turned over in its sleep, or as if something beneath the sleepers had noticed, at last, the approach of its key.

The keeper rang her bell, agitated now, and the sleepers' breathing stuttered—every one of them, a hundred thousand chests catching in unison—and released.

"He's under them," Ansel whispered. "Under the galleries. They built the vault of years on top of the prison. All this stolen time, stacked like sandbags over a door."

"No," said the keeper, and her old voice shook. "Worse than that, marked one. They built it as a *counterweight*. The founders' design. Every year hoarded up here presses down on him. His strength bleeds into the stored time—that's why the bottled years work at all, that's where the power comes from—and the weight of the harvest keeps him under. Free the years, free the god. Free the god..." She looked out across her sleeping city, her hundred thousand charges, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she had refused to do for a lifetime. "Free the god, and every debt in this place comes due at once. Every sleeper wakes. Every account calls in its principal. And the Guild—" she laughed, a small cracked sound, "—the Guild has never once, in a hundred years, planned for the possibility of repayment."

Above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the keeper's small lullaby-bell. A great one. The alarm of the vaults above.

And through Ansel's burning sigil, clear as a voice against her ear, came words in a language she had never learned and understood completely:

*They are counting my chains, little bearer. Come and see how few remain.*
