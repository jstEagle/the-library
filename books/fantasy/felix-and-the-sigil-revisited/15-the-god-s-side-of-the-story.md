## 15. The God's Side of the Story

Sleep did not come so much as it was taken.

Ansel had meant to keep watch. She and Maren had made camp in the shell of a tollhouse above the river road, a day's walk from where the founders' testament lay hidden in its lead-lined chest, and she had sworn—sworn aloud, with her hand pressed flat against the sigil-scar on her palm—that tonight she would not close her eyes. The last two dreams had left marks. The first had given her a voice. The second had given her a question she still could not put down: *I was not abandoned. I was buried.*

But exhaustion is a creditor more patient than any Guild auditor, and somewhere between one breath of the fire's smoke and the next, it collected its debt. The tollhouse fell away. The fire fell away. Even the ache in her palm fell away, and Ansel Nystrom stood in a place that was not a place, under a sky that was not a sky.

It was a field of vellum.

That was the only way her mind could render it—the whole world stretched flat and pale as prepared calfskin, faintly grained, faintly warm, going on farther than sight could follow. No horizon. No sun. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, the way light comes through the pages of a closed book held up to a window. And across this endless page, things were written. Not words. Not at first. As she walked—she was walking; she had legs again, or the memory of them—the writing resolved into roads, into rivers, into the blackened skeleton of an orchard, into a city whose towers were drawn in the careful hand of someone who loved them.

She knew that city. Every ledger-keeper in the Vellum Guild knew that city. It was the city she had slept in last night, rendered as it had been a hundred years ago, before the war took its eastern quarter and the Guild took everything else.

"You are reading me," said a voice, "when I meant for you to be read."

Ansel turned.

She had expected—she did not know what she had expected. Fire, perhaps. A throne. The terrible geometry of something too large to be contained by a dream. What stood before her instead was a man of ordinary height, dressed in the undyed wool of a farmer, with dust on his sandals and a face like every face she had ever audited and forgotten: unremarkable, kind around the eyes, tired in the way of someone who has been listening to arguments for a very long time. Only his hands were wrong. They were scarred—not battle-scarred, but *ledger*-scarred, the fingers stained faintly silver at the tips as though he had spent centuries turning pages written in ink that bit.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Ansel said, because her mouth had always operated slightly ahead of her judgment.

The god smiled. It was a tired smile, but it reached his eyes. "So is a seed," he said. "And yet."

He gestured, and the vellum-field rippled. Somewhere far off—or near; distance was negotiable here—a sound like a great many people breathing out at once passed over them, and the writing on the world shifted, rearranged itself, became a different scene entirely.

"Come," he said. "You have read everyone else's account of my death. It seems only fair you hear mine."

* * *

They walked together across the page of the world, and as they walked, the god told it—not with the cadence of scripture, which Ansel had expected, but with the cadence of testimony. The flat, careful voice of someone who has rehearsed being believed.

"I was not always silent," he began. "I was not even, at the end, particularly loud. Do you know what I was, Ansel Nystrom? I was a god of accounts."

She stopped walking. "That isn't in any text."

"No," he agreed mildly. "The texts were written by people who needed me to be something else. But think about what your Guild remembers of me, even now, distorted through a century of careful forgetting. That every debt must be named. That nothing is owed without record. That a promise spoken is a thing that exists, and can be called upon, and cannot simply be... unwritten." He turned his stained fingers over, examining them. "I kept the world's ledgers. Every oath ever sworn in my name went into my keeping. Every vow, every bargain, every quiet promise made over a sickbed or a grave. I did not judge them. Judging was never my office. I only *held* them. I was the memory of the world, when the world preferred not to remember itself."

"And the war?"

The field darkened around them, the way ink spreads when water touches it, and suddenly they were standing in it: the war, drawn vast and terrible across the vellum. Ansel saw armies rendered not as figures but as columns of names, thousands deep, marching toward each other—and she understood, with a lurch, that this was not metaphor. In this place, names *were* bodies. Each name marched because someone, somewhere, had sworn something in her god's name and could not take it back.

"They swore themselves to me," the god said quietly. "Both sides. Kings who wanted their conquests sanctified. Generals who wanted their slaughters recorded as holy work. And because I hold all oaths, their oaths bound them—to me, and through me, to each other. A soldier sworn in my name could not strike down another soldier sworn in my name without breaking his own vow." He paused. "For six years, that held the line. Six years in which the war was fought with sieges and starvation and slow attrition, because open battle was impossible. Six years of stalemate, because I had accidentally built a peace out of other people's promises."

"What broke it?"

"Not a weapon." His voice did not change, but the field around them went cold. "A forgery."

* * *

They stood now in a hall Ansel recognized from the Guild's oldest engravings: the Founders' Hall, before it was the Founders' Hall, when it had been a counting-house. Nine figures sat at a long table, and the rendering was so precise she could see the grain of the wood, the wax seals stacked like small red fruits, the nervous way the youngest of the nine kept touching her collar.

"The nine founders," Ansel said. "The First Auditor's predecessors."

"The nine founders," the god confirmed. "In those days they were my auditors. My clerks. The finest of those who kept oaths in my name—the ones I trusted to hold the copies of the world's promises while I held the originals." He looked at the table with something that was not quite anger and not quite grief. "And in the third year of the war, they came to me with a proposal. The northern king's army was starving. The southern coalition was winning by simply waiting. And the north had begun to whisper a solution: if the southern soldiers' oaths could be *transferred*—if their vows to me could be bought, sold, reassigned to some other god, some dead god, anything—they would no longer be bound, and the killing could begin in earnest."

"Oath-transfer," Ansel whispered. She had seen the term in the excised ledgers, a single fragmentary line: *transfer protocols, see Appendix (removed).*

"A genocide requires logistics," the god said. "You cannot murder a hundred thousand sworn soldiers while their oaths protect them. So the north needed a way to unmake the protection. And my auditors—" he touched the youngest founder's shoulder, and she flinched in the rendering, as though she could feel him still—"my auditors discovered that they could. They came to me not to ask permission but to offer a partnership. Transfer the southern oaths. Sell them north. End the stalemate. Profit beyond the dreaming of gods."

"And you refused."

"I refused." He said it simply, without pride. "And then they did something I had not thought mortal minds capable of. They did not steal the oaths. You cannot steal an oath; it must be surrendered or broken. Instead they forged one. A single oath, written in a hand indistinguishable from mine, binding *me*: an oath of silence, of abdication, of self-imprisonment, sworn by me, to me, witnessed by no one—for there was no one left with authority to witness a god. And because I am the keeper of all oaths, their forgery went directly into my own keeping. I received it. I filed it. And the moment I accepted it into my ledgers, it became true."

Ansel felt the ground tilt beneath her, though the vellum-field did not move. "That's monstrous."

"It is elegant," the god corrected softly. "That is worse. They used my own nature as the instrument of my imprisonment. I could not refuse the oath, because refusing an oath is breaking it, and I do not break oaths—I *am* the not-breaking of them. I could not contest the forgery, because the only court that could hear the case was me, and I was already silenced. They found the one prison in all creation that requires no walls." He smiled again, and this time the smile was terrible. "A filing error."

"But the sigil." Ansel raised her scarred palm. "The mark. If you were imprisoned by a forged oath, why does the sigil exist? Why does it burn? Why did Felix call me Sigil-Bearer?"

The god looked at her palm for a long moment. "Because I was not entirely alone in my keeping," he said. "When the founders sealed me away, they needed a guarantee—a failsafe, in case their forgery was ever discovered. So they created the sigil: a counter-seal, burned into the skin of whoever among my faithful showed the strongest... attunement. The sigil does not imprison me further. It does something they did not intend. It *remembers*. It holds, in the body of a living bearer, a true copy of the original oath-ledger—the real one, the one that existed before the forgery. Every true vow. Every genuine promise. Including," and his voice gentled, "the truth of what was done to me."

"So the sigil is evidence."

"The sigil is *testimony*," the god said. "Living, breathing, walking testimony. The founders could not destroy the original ledger—it would have unraveled every oath in the world, including their own power. So they hid it in the one place no auditor would think to search: inside a person. And then they built a church of silence around it, and called the silence piety, and waited for the bearers to die out."

"How many bearers have there been?"

"You are the ninth."

The number landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She counted backward—one per decade, roughly, since the silence—and thought of eight strangers across a century who had carried this burning secret in their skin and died, presumably, without knowing what they carried. Or perhaps knowing. Perhaps that was why the Guild's bearer-selection rites had grown so sparse, so ritualized, so empty. Perhaps the Guild had been culling them.

"Why am I alive?" she asked. "If the sigil is testimony, why hasn't the Guild destroyed me?"

"Because destroying the bearer destroys the testimony," the god said, "and the testimony is the only copy of the original ledger. Destroy me and destroy the ledger both, and every oath in the world comes loose at once. Every debt, every promise, every marriage-vow and mercenary-contract and dying wish—unwritten. The founders could not risk that. Their entire empire is built on the trade in obligations. They need the ledger intact. They need *me* intact. They simply need me unheard." He spread his scarred hands. "So they built the Guild atop my prison the way you build a house atop a well, and drew the water, and called the well theirs."

* * *

The field shifted again, and now Ansel stood at the edge of a scene she would have given her remaining years never to see: the war's final month, rendered in names rather than blood.

The transfer had worked. She watched it happen in the language of ledgers—columns of southern oaths migrating northward, entry by entry, each transferred vow leaving a blank space where a life's protection had been. And in the blanks, the names began to thin. Not all at once. Ledger by ledger. Village by village. She watched a town of four hundred names become a town of forty names become a town of no names at all, and the god stood beside her and did not look away, because he could not look away; he was the record of it.

"I heard every one of them," he said. "Every name as it was struck. Do you understand what that means, for a keeper of accounts? I could not stop it. I could not intervene. I could only *witness*, and file, and witness, and file. Four hundred thousand names, Ansel. I hold them still. They are the heaviest entries in my keeping, heavier than the forgery, heavier than the prison. And in the end—" his voice finally frayed, just slightly— "in the end, the founders came back to me. The youngest one. The one who touched her collar. She came alone, at night, to the prison they had built, and she knelt, and she said: *We have done a thing that cannot be survived. Undo us.*"

"And what did you say?"

"I said I could not undo them. Oaths do not bend. But I offered her a bargain, the last bargain I ever brokered: let me speak once more. Let me speak the war's true history into the world, once, completely, so that it can never be forged again. Let the truth of the transfers and the genocide and the forgery of my own imprisonment stand as a permanent entry in the world's memory. And in exchange—" he paused— "in exchange, I asked to be remembered. Not freed. *Remembered.* One honest telling, preserved past the reach of any auditor's knife."

"And she agreed?"

"She agreed," the god said, "and then she went back to her eight colleagues, and they voted, and they decided that a god who tells the truth is more dangerous than a god who tells nothing. So they buried her confession with her—she died within the year; guilt is a solvent—and they buried my request with her, and they wrote the history you were taught: that the god died nobly at the war's end, that the Guild rose from his ashes as a service to his memory, that silence is sacred." He looked at Ansel then, fully, and his eyes were the color of old ink. "A century, Ansel. A hundred years of your Guild trading in bottled grief and borrowed years, and every coin of it minted from the silence they forged to cover a massacre."

* * *

They stood a long time after that, in the pale field, saying nothing. The vellum-world had gone quiet. Somewhere beneath them—if beneath meant anything here—Ansel could feel the weight of the ledgers, the true ones, pressing upward like roots under a road.

"You haven't asked me to free you," she said finally.

"No."

"Maren will want to know why not. Felix died carrying a map to your prison. He gave his life for your freedom. And you're telling me you don't want it?"

The god was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the weariness of a man setting down a load he has carried so long his shoulders have grown around it.

"Child, I have had a hundred years to consider what my freedom would cost. The oaths I hold are the mortar of your world. Half the contracts in the Guild's vaults are written in my name, however ignorantly. Break my prison carelessly and you do not release a prisoner—you collapse a load-bearing wall. Kingdoms have treaties sworn on my authority. Debts are collected, borders held, marriages honored, all resting on the assumption that oaths mean what they say. If I rise wrongly, the war does not return—the war was only the fever. What returns is the *plague*: a world where promises are negotiable, where every vow can be forged or transferred or sold, where the strong write and the weak are written upon. That world existed once. I was invented to prevent it." He shook his head slowly. "Freedom, imposed from outside, may be a second violence. I have seen enough violence."

"Then what do you want?" Ansel's voice cracked on it. "What have you brought me here to *do*?"

"To remember me," the god said. "That is all. That is everything. I do not need a liberator, Ansel Nystrom. I need a witness. Someone to carry the true account out of this place and set it down where no auditor can excise it. Someone to say, plainly, in the world's hearing: *this god was not abandoned; he was buried; here is what was done, and here is what it cost.*" He stepped closer, and up close she could see that his eyes were not eyes at all but tiny, dense fields of writing, whole histories compressed into the space of a glance. "Whether the prison stands or falls afterward—that is not the question I am asking you. The question is whether the truth survives the tellers. Everything else follows from that."

"And if freeing you *is* the truth?" Ansel asked. "If the testimony, once spoken, makes the prison untenable? If remembering you breaks the seal whether we intend it or not?"

The god regarded her for a long, long moment. Around them, the vellum-field stirred, and Ansel felt—distinctly, unmistakably—the sensation of a page being turned somewhere very far away, and a new entry beginning to write itself.

"Then," he said at last, "you will have to decide what kind of violence you can live with. The violence of a lie sustained, or the violence of a truth released. I cannot make that choice for you. I was made unable to make choices, a century ago." His tired smile returned, and this time there was something in it that might have been hope, or might have been apology. "But I will tell you this, bearer, and then you must wake, because your sister-in-arms is stirring, and she has been dreaming of knives:

"The founders thought they were building a vault. They were building a *door*. And doors, unlike oaths, swing both ways."

The field folded. The light went out like a lamp behind glass. And Ansel Nystrom woke in the ruined tollhouse with Maren's hand already on her knife-hilt, staring at the doorway, where the pre-dawn grey showed the silhouette of a rider who had dismounted and was standing very still, waiting to be noticed.

The sigil on her palm was burning like a brand, and the rider wore the grey-and-silver of the Vellum Guild.
