## 16. The Choice Before the Door

The door was older than the Guild.

That was the first thing Ansel understood when the last stair gave out beneath them and the passage opened into a chamber that no ledger had ever recorded. The vault door did not belong to the architecture around it. The undercity's borrowed years had been stacked above it like sediment—decades of stolen time pressed into stone and shelving, vials glowing their faint amber in the dark—but this door predated all of it. It was black iron gone soft with age at the edges, and across its face spread a sigil she knew better than her own heartbeat, because it *was* her own heartbeat. It lived in the scar of her left palm, had lived there since the day she swore her oath at nineteen and felt the brand go through her like a nail through water.

Maren stood beside her, breathing hard from the descent, a knife loose in her grip out of habit rather than intent. Torchlight—or what passed for it down here, the pale gold seep of bottled years in their racks—moved across her face and made her look briefly like Felix. Ansel had noticed it before, in the market alley with the blade at her throat: the same set of the jaw, the same way grief sat on them like weather. She had spent three years of her memory to learn what that grief was for. She would not spend more. But she carried it now as if it were hers, which in some accounting she supposed it was.

"That's it," Maren said. Not a question. Her voice had gone quiet the way it always went quiet before violence or truth, and Ansel had learned there was little difference between the two for her. "That's what he died carrying."

"It's what he died *reading*," Ansel said. "There's a difference."

She held up the map. In the light of the vials it looked less like parchment than like skin—the ink shifting, refusing to settle, the route they had followed through the founders' testament still faintly warm where her fingers touched it. It had led them true. Through the excised pages, through the hidden testament behind the founding ledgers, down through the undercity where the Guild kept its hoard of other people's time. Every step had cost something. Ansel had stopped keeping count somewhere around the moment she could no longer picture her mother's face, and had started again only because Maren had forced her to—*you're paying for the truth with the person you were*—and the counting was its own kind of wound.

Now they stood before the door, and the counting was about to become a choice.

"Open it," Maren said.

Ansel didn't move. "You don't know what's behind it."

"I know what my brother believed was behind it." Maren turned to face her fully, and the torchlight caught the dried blood on her knuckles from the enforcer they'd left groaning two levels up. "Felix spent four years of his life on this. Four years of letters I thought were nonsense, sketches I burned because I thought he'd lost his mind. He wasn't mad. He was *right*, and the Guild killed him for it, and you want to stand here and tell me we've come all this way to knock politely?"

"I want to tell you what I saw."

"Then say it fast."

Ansel closed her hand over the map. The sigil in her palm answered it—a low heat, familiar now, almost companionable, the way an old injury is companionable. And behind the heat, fainter than breath, the presence she had been dreaming since the bottling room. The god. Buried, not dead. Buried not by enemies but by his own consent, twisted by the men who buried him into the foundation of an empire.

"I saw the war," she said. "Not the version in the Guild histories. The real one. Cities that traded sides every season until no one remembered which side they'd been born to. Priests doing things in his name that would have made a butcher sick—and he *let* them believe he wanted it, because the alternative was telling them to stop and watching them do it anyway in someone else's name. He agreed to be buried, Maren. He asked for it. The founders just never told anyone it was mercy. They sold it as silence, and then they sold the silence."

"Then freeing him is justice."

"Freeing him is *risk*." The words came out harder than she meant, and she let them stay hard, because softness here would be a lie. "A hundred years ago the world nearly ended because people believed a god wanted things. What happens when he walks back into it? Do you think the kingdoms that fought that war have gotten wiser? Do you think the Guild will simply hand over its ledgers and apologize? The moment that seal breaks, every priest, every king, every desperate soul who ever wanted a god on their side comes running—and he spent a century learning what it costs to be wanted like that."

Maren was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to something almost gentle, which from her was worse than shouting.

"You sound like the First Auditor."

Ansel flinched. It was a small flinch, quickly mastered, but Maren saw it—she saw everything; grief had made her a lens—and Ansel knew the comparison had landed exactly where it was aimed.

"The Auditor wants to own the aftermath," Ansel said. "I want to prevent one. That's not the same thing."

"No. It's worse, in a way. She's honest about wanting power. You're dressing fear up as duty and calling it prudence." Maren stepped closer. Up close her eyes were Felix's eyes exactly, and Ansel had to hold very still not to look away. "My brother bled out against a weighing door pressing this map into a stranger's hands. He chose you. Out of everyone in that building, out of every mercenary and auditor and clerk, the dying man with the truth in his fists picked the one person marked with the god's own seal. You think that was chance?"

"I think dying men don't choose carefully."

"I think gods do." Maren's voice cracked on it, just slightly, and she steadied herself the way she did everything—brutally, immediately, without acknowledgment. "He called you Sigil-Bearer. He named you. And you've been running from the name ever since, trading pieces of yourself away so you won't have to answer it. Well. Here's the door, Ansel. Here's the end of every road that map could take. Answer it or don't. But if you walk away, walk away knowing my brother's blood is on the stones behind us and you're the reason it bought nothing."

* * *

They argued for another hour. Ansel would remember that afterward—not the argument itself but its duration, the strange intimacy of standing in a buried room with a woman who had tried to kill her, trading futures back and forth like coins neither could spend.

Maren laid out Felix's case piece by piece, letter by letter, sketch by sketch reconstructed from memory. The god had been buried alive in the oldest sense: not slain but *unremembered*, and unremembering, Felix had written, was the only violence that never healed. Every year the Guild traded in stolen time and bottled grief, the world grew a little more hollow, a little more willing to forget anything that inconvenienced it. The war hadn't been prevented by the burial. It had been *postponed*—and postponed wars, Felix believed, came due with interest.

Ansel countered with the visions. The genocide committed in the god's name. The villages that had burned themselves rather than wait for his judgment. The terrible, patient sorrow of a being who had chosen imprisonment over complicity and been rewarded with a monopoly built on his silence. Freedom might be a second violence, she said. Mercy might be the cage. Who were they—a fugitive apostate and a grieving sister—to overturn a bargain struck by gods?

"And who were the founders," Maren shot back, "but merchants? They took a god's sacrifice and made it a product line. You'd honor that?"

"I'd honor *him*. His choice. He asked to be buried."

"He asked to be buried so the killing would stop. The killing stopped. A hundred years, Ansel. The killing has stayed stopped. Maybe the bargain's served its term."

"Or maybe the bargain is the only thing holding the world together, and we're two people in a hole with a knife and a scar, about to find out."

It went nowhere. It went everywhere. They wore grooves in the argument like water wearing stone, circling back to the same points, wearing each smooth, finding nothing new—until Ansel, exhausted past caution, pressed her palm flat against the vault door simply to steady herself.

The sigil flared.

Not the low companionable heat of the journey down. This was a *recognition*—the mark on her skin meeting its twin on the iron and answering it, and through the answering came a flood so sudden and total that she gasped and would have fallen if Maren hadn't caught her arm.

She saw the door from the other side.

She saw it as the god saw it: not a wall but a *page*. The sigil was not a lock. It had never been a lock. Locks keep things in; this kept something *unread*. The founders had not sealed the god behind the door—they had sealed the *truth of him*, the whole account, the complete and honest ledger of what he had done and what had been done to him, and the god behind it was less prisoner than archivist, waiting a century for someone authorized to open the record. The sigil-bearers had not been wardens. They had been *readers*. Every generation, one marked hand, one living oath, one chance to speak the account aloud and let the world decide what it owed him.

And the Guild had known. Had always known. Had spent a century ensuring that every bearer either died ignorant or was bound so deep in the institution's lies that they'd never dare read. Ansel was not the first. She was only the first to get this far.

She came back to herself on her knees, Maren's hand hard on her shoulder, the map fallen open on the floor between them, its ink finally, perfectly still—as if it too had been waiting for her to understand it.

"Ansel. *Ansel.* What happened?"

She looked up at Maren, and the argument was already dead; she could feel it lying between them like something whose funeral neither wanted to conduct. Because the realization rearranged everything, and they both knew it.

"It's not a lock on him," Ansel said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It's a lock on the truth. On the reading. The prison isn't the door—it's the *silence*. He's been in there a hundred years keeping the accounts, waiting for a bearer to come and read them aloud. The war doesn't come back because I open a door. The war comes back—or doesn't—because of what the world does when it hears the truth. And the world can't hear it unless someone speaks it."

Maren stared at her. "Then speak it."

"If I'm wrong—"

"You've been wrong before?"

"About everything that mattered, apparently." Ansel laughed, and it came out ragged. She looked down at her palm, at the sigil that was not a brand but a *commission*, and felt the full weight of the title Felix had given her settling onto her shoulders at last—not as a burden handed to her by a dying stranger, but as a duty she had been carrying since she was nineteen and had never once been told its name. "I'm the last key, Maren. Me. Not the map, not the door. If I die before I read it, the silence holds another hundred years, and the Guild wins forever. If I read it wrong—" She shook her head. "If I read it wrong, I don't even know what I break."

"Then read it right."

"You make it sound simple."

"My brother made it look simple," Maren said quietly. "He walked into the Guild's heart carrying this and didn't hesitate once. Not once, according to every letter. I used to think that was recklessness." She crouched, picked up the map, and held it out to Ansel across the space between them—an offering, or a surrender, or both. "Now I think it was faith. He had faith in you before he ever met you. The least you can do is catch up."

Ansel took the map. Their hands overlapped on it for a moment—Maren's scarred and steady, hers branded and burning—and in that touch was everything the argument had been circling: Felix's blood, Maren's grief, Ansel's oath, the god's century of patience, all of it converging on a single sheet of shifting ink and a single scarred palm.

And that was when the light changed.

Above them, far up the shaft they had descended, the amber glow of the stored years flickered—then began to go out, rack by rack, level by level, a darkness descending toward them with the unhurried certainty of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Maren was on her feet with her knife out before Ansel finished rising. "Tell me that's not them."

Ansel watched the dark come down and felt her sigil burn hotter with every rack of vials that died. He knew. Of course he knew—the Auditor had known about the map from the beginning, and now she knew where it ended.

"They're coming," Ansel said. She turned back to the door, to the sigil that waited for her voice, and drew a breath that tasted of iron and old paper and a hundred years of patience. "And I'm out of time to be afraid."

She raised her scarred palm to the door.
