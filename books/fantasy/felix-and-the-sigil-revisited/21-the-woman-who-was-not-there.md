## 21. The Woman Who Was Not There

Maren had counted the dead before. She had knelt beside her brother's body in the road outside Vell's Crossing and done the arithmetic of grief—two days cold, one wound, no ring, no letter, boots still good enough to be worth taking. Counting was how she kept herself from drowning. Numbers were a floor you could stand on when everything else gave way.

But she had never counted a living woman who was not there.

Ansel Nystrom woke on the ninth morning after the Breaking, in a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and honey, and she opened her eyes on Maren as though Maren were a stranger who had wandered in from the street.

"You're bleeding," Ansel said. Her voice was rough, unpracticed, like a door long shut.

Maren touched her own temple, where the bandage sat. "It's nothing. It's already healing."

"Was I there? When it happened?"

The question landed like a stone dropped down a well. Maren waited for the splash and heard none. "You were there," she said carefully. "You've been asleep for nine days."

"Nine days." Ansel said it the way an auditor says a figure—testing its weight, finding it plausible or not. Then her eyes moved to her own hands, turned them over, palms up. The sigil-scar was gone. Where it had burned for twenty years, seared into her skin at her swearing-in, there was only smooth flesh, pale as new paper, with the faintest ghost of silver beneath it, like a watermark no one could quite read.

Ansel stared at it for a long time. Maren watched her stare and understood, with a slow and terrible clarity, what had been paid.

"I don't remember," Ansel said at last. Not frightened. Not weeping. Just stating it, the way she would have entered it in a ledger. *One sigil, broken. One god, released. One bearer, emptied.* "I don't remember any of it. I don't remember—" She stopped. Her brow furrowed, and Maren saw her reach for something and find the shelf bare. "I don't remember why my hands know how to do this."

She had picked up, without looking, the stub of charcoal and the scrap of paper on the bedside table, and she was making small neat marks—not words, but tallies, columns, the little crosses-and-fives of a bookkeeper's shorthand. She looked at them as if they belonged to someone else.

"Habit," Maren said. "You kept ledgers. For the Guild."

"The Guild." Ansel tested the word. Something moved behind her eyes—dread, maybe, or distaste—but no memory followed it up. "I don't trust that word," she said finally. "I don't know why."

"That's all right," Maren said. "You don't have to."

* * *

The physicians of the low quarter—the ones who asked no questions about apostates—had explained it as best they could. The oath had held her memories the way a dam holds water, they said; when the sigil broke, the whole structure came down at once. Everything she had traded away in vials, everything the oath itself had sealed: gone. Not destroyed, perhaps. The oldest among them, a woman with milk-white eyes who smelled of feverfew, had said quietly that nothing given to a god is ever truly lost. But where it went, and whether it could come back, she could not say.

What remained was stranger than nothing. Ansel remembered language, arithmetic, the weight of a coin, the proper way to hold a knife. She remembered *how* to be Ansel Nystrom without remembering being her. She could walk into a market and haggle like a native. She could read three scripts. When Maren once left a ledger open on the table, Ansel picked it up and found two errors in the sums before she caught herself doing it and set it down like something hot.

"It's like wearing someone else's coat," she told Maren on the twelfth day. They were sitting in the chandler's back room because Ansel could not yet bear the street crowds—they pressed against her like hands, she said, full of faces she almost knew. "Everything fits. Nothing is mine."

"Do you want me to tell you?" Maren asked. She had asked before. She asked every few days, because it seemed important to keep asking rather than decide for her. "About who you were. I knew you for—well. For a while. I could start anywhere you like."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past, and the chandler downstairs called out his prices in a voice like gravel in honey.

"No," she said. "Not yet." And then, because honesty seemed to cost her less now, as if the person who had guarded her truths had also been spent: "I'm afraid of what I'll hear. I can feel it. There's a shape in me that expects bad news. Like a scar that aches before rain."

"There was some bad news," Maren admitted. "There was also—" She stopped. *Felix,* she thought. *Your name was Felix, and she carried your map, and she broke the world open for you, and she doesn't remember you died.* "There was good too. A lot of it, near the end."

"Tell me one good thing," Ansel said. "Just one. Small. So I can see if it fits."

Maren thought about it. She thought about the bottling room, and the vial, and the moment Ansel had pushed three years of herself across the counter like a merchant paying a debt. She thought about the vault door, and the argument, and the way Ansel had finally said, *the sigil isn't a lock on him, it's a lock on the truth, and I am the last key*—and how her voice hadn't shaken.

"One night," Maren said, "you laughed. Really laughed. You'd just done something impossibly brave and incredibly stupid, and afterward we sat on some steps eating fried dough off a scrap of paper, and you laughed until you couldn't breathe. You had a gap in your teeth you don't have anymore—you chipped it later, you won't remember—and you said, *if this is what the end of the world tastes like, it needs more sugar.*"

Ansel listened with her head tilted, the way one listens to music through a wall. When Maren finished, she was silent a while.

"I can almost feel it," she said. "Not the memory. The—the shape where it was. Like a tooth that's pulled. My tongue keeps going back to it." She looked down at her hands. "It doesn't fit. But it doesn't hurt either. That's strange. I'd have thought it would hurt."

"Grief usually does," Maren said.

"Yes," Ansel said, and frowned slightly. "How would you know?"

And there it was—the question Maren had been dreading and rehearsing for twelve days. *Who are you? Why are you here? Why does a stranger sit by my bed every morning?*

"We met," Maren said, "because of your work. Before the end. We weren't friends at first." That much was true. "We became friends. And then things happened, and now you're here, and I stay because—" She found, to her surprise, that the true answer was simple. "Because someone should. Because you shouldn't wake up alone in a world that took everything from you. Because I promised someone I'd look after you."

"Someone who isn't here anymore," Ansel said. Not a question. She read people the way she read ledgers; whatever else had been taken, that remained.

"Yes," Maren said. "He isn't here anymore."

Ansel nodded slowly. Then she reached over and, with the unconscious precision of habit, straightened the crooked stack of papers on the table between them.

"Then we should keep accounts," she said. "You and I. Of what I owe you. I don't like owing."

Maren laughed—a short, wet sound, half sob. "You always did say that."

"I did?" Ansel smiled faintly. "Good. At least I'm consistent."

* * *

Winter came down off the hills and settled over the city like a creditor. In the streets, the world was still rearranging itself around the truth. The Guild's great weighing hall stood empty, its doors chained by the city magistrates; crowds still gathered some mornings just to look at it, the way people look at a house where something terrible happened. The bottled griefs had been poured out into the harbor on the Auditor's orders—or what remained of the Auditor's orders; she had not been seen since the night the sky filled with the war's true history, and some said she had walked into the sea after her empire. Borrowed years were being returned, household by household, in a process so tangled that three separate committees had already formed to argue about it.

Ansel watched none of it. Or rather, she watched it as weather—rain, rumor, noise. It passed over her and left no mark, because there was nothing in her for it to cling to.

But sometimes, Maren noticed, it clung anyway.

Once, passing the fountain in Tanner's Square, Ansel stopped without warning and stood very still, watching the water. "I've been here," she said. "I sat on that edge. Someone was with me." Her hand drifted to her collarbone, touching the place where the sigil had been. "I can't see their face. But I remember—" she hesitated, hunting the word, "—being *afraid*, and being glad of the fear, because it meant the thing I was about to do mattered."

Another time, in the market, a fruit-seller called out a price and Ansel answered in a dialect neither of them had taught her—one of the river-tongues, old and soft—and then stood blinking at her own mouth as if it had spoken without permission.

And once—only once—she dreamed aloud. Maren woke in the narrow bed across the room to hear her murmuring, rapid and urgent, in a voice stripped of sleepiness: *"I was not abandoned. I was buried. I was not abandoned. I was buried."* Over and over, like a man tapping on the inside of a coffin lid. By morning Ansel remembered nothing, but she was quiet all day, and twice Maren caught her looking east, toward the hills, toward the place where the prison had been, with an expression Maren could not name and did not dare ask about.

Grief, Maren was learning, could be gentle. That was the discovery of that winter, the one thing the season gave her that she had not expected. She had known grief as a blade—she had crossed half the country with it sharpened against her hip, looking for someone to kill. But watching Ansel relearn her own hands, her own habits, her own laugh, Maren found another kind of mourning growing in her, slow and green as moss. She grieved Felix every day, but she had stopped counting the days since. She grieved the woman Ansel had been—the sharp, stubborn, secretly kind auditor who paid for truth in pieces of herself—and she grieved her without bitterness, because that woman was not dead. She was only elsewhere. Sleeping somewhere under the smooth skin and the tidy sums, the way the god had slept under the vault.

And some mornings, the elsewhere came close.

* * *

On the first day of thaw, Ansel announced over breakfast that she wanted work.

"I can't keep sitting here," she said, buttering bread with the economical strokes of someone who hated waste. "I owe you rent. I owe you—more than rent. And I'm good at numbers. Apparently." She tapped her temple. "Whatever else got taken, the arithmetic stayed."

"There's a shipping concern on Rope Walk," Maren said. "They're honest, which is rare, and drowning in paperwork, which is rarer still. I could introduce you."

"Introduce me as what?" Ansel asked, and there it was again—that flinch, that flicker of the shape expecting bad news. "I don't even know my own name's worth anything. If the Guild kept records—and institutions like that always keep records—isn't there a file with my face in it? Won't someone recognize me?"

"The Guild's records burned," Maren said. It was mostly true; the enforcers' registers had burned the night of the Breaking, when the truth flooded the city and men who had spent careers erasing people decided, in one collective convulsion of conscience or terror, to feed the evidence of themselves to the fire. "As far as the world knows, Ansel Nystrom died in the autumn. Apostate. Hunted. Very tragic."

"And who am I now?" Ansel asked. Genuinely asking. She spread her hands, empty palms up, the gesture of a merchant showing clean scales.

Maren considered her across the table—the strong hands, the watchful gray eyes, the small scar on the chin she'd gotten falling out of a tree at fourteen (Maren knew this; Ansel did not), the way she sat with her spine straight as a ruled line. A woman built entirely of habits, standing at the beginning of herself.

"Whoever you decide," Maren said. "That's the one mercy in all of this. Nobody gets a clean page. You actually have one."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something that undid Maren completely: she laughed. Not the almost-laugh of the steps outside the vault, not an echo of anything—but a real, present-tense laugh, startled out of her, at the absurdity of a woman with no past being handed a blank ledger.

"A clean page," she repeated. "Do you know how obscene that sounds to a bookkeeper? Do you know what we'd pay for a clean page?" She shook her head, still smiling, and something in her face shifted—settled, the way a sum settles when it finally comes out right. "All right. Rope Walk. Introduce me. As your friend."

"As my friend," Maren agreed.

"Good." Ansel rose and began stacking the breakfast things with brisk efficiency, then paused, her back to Maren, and stood a moment in the grey window light. When she spoke again her voice was quieter. "Maren. Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"Ask."

"That person you promised. The one who isn't here anymore." Ansel turned. In the flat winter light her eyes were the color of the sky before snow. "Did he love you? Whoever he was. Because you love him—I can see it, it's all over you, you carry it the way I used to carry—" she touched her collarbone again, absently, "—something heavy. And I think whoever he was, he must have been extraordinary, because you turned all that grief into—" she gestured vaguely at the room, at the bandage on Maren's temple, at the weeks of patient mornings, "—into *this*. Into staying. Most people turn it into knives."

Maren's throat closed. She thought of Felix at eleven years old, teaching himself to juggle stolen apples. Felix at nineteen, writing letters full of maps drawn in the margins. Felix dying in the road with the map against his heart, and the last thing he'd said to her in his final letter: *some things are worth carrying even if you never get to put them down.*

"He was my little brother," she managed. "His name was Felix."

"Felix," Ansel repeated softly. She said it carefully, tasting it, and Maren watched her tongue go, once more, to the empty socket where a memory had been. Something crossed her face—brief, unreadable, gone. "That's a good name," she said. "It suits the kind of person who leaves that much light behind."

And then she carried the dishes to the basin, humming—humming, tunelessly, a snatch of something that might have been a market song or might have been older—and Maren sat at the table with her brother's name warm in a stranger's mouth, and wept quietly, and let it be gentle.

* * *

That evening, Maren took out the notebook.

She had started it in the second week, in the worst hours of the night watches, when the silence in the next bed was louder than any accusation. Not the book—the other book, the private one, the one she wrote in by candlelight with the door barred. She had filled pages already: the fight in the market alley, the founders' testament, the vault door, the breaking of the sky. She wrote because Felix had written, and because someone in this story had to keep accounts that were true.

Tonight she wrote:

*Day forty-one. She asked about Felix today. I told her his name. She said it suited him.*

*She hums when she thinks no one is listening. She still hates owing. She found both errors in the chandler's invoice and corrected them without being asked, and when I thanked her she looked pleased and embarrassed in exactly the order she used to.*

*The white-eyed physician says nothing given to a god is ever lost. I have started believing her. Not because I have proof. Because Ansel reaches for things that aren't there, and her hand always knows the shape of them.*

*Some nights I want to shake her and shout it all back into her—every name, every debt, every step of the road. Some nights I nearly do. But you don't pour a year back into a vial that cracked. You wait. You keep the accounts. You become, if you have to, a friend from scratch.*

*And here is the thing I cannot write to anyone but this page: I think she is happier. God forgive me, I think the woman with no past sleeps better than the woman who had one. Is that the price working, or the cure? I don't know. I only know that when she laughed yesterday, it was hers. New-made. Nobody's echo.*

*Tomorrow I take her to Rope Walk. Tomorrow she starts her clean page.*

*And tonight, for the first time since the road outside Vell's Crossing, I wrote my brother's name and did not cry. Progress, of a kind. He'd have mocked me for the sentimentality. He'd have been right to.*

She closed the notebook and blew out the candle, and in the dark she heard, from the next room, the small sounds of Ansel settling—blankets, a sigh, the creak of the bedframe—and then, faintly, half-asleep, humming again. That same snatch of melody. Tuneless. Patient. Old.

Maren lay still and listened, and felt the hair rise gently along her arms.

She knew the song. Every child in the river valleys knew it—it was a counting song, a skipping rhyme, older than the Guild, older than the war. Mothers sang it to children who would never know what it counted. *One for the sorrow, two for the vow, three for the buried, four for the plow...*

Except that the version everyone sang ended at seven verses, and the version Ansel was humming, drifting down through the dark like silt settling in clear water, had eight.

Maren had never heard the eighth verse in her life.

She lay in the dark and memorized it, line by line, her heart beating slow and hard, and when the humming finally faded into breathing, she got up, lit the candle, and wrote it down before it could escape.

At the top of the page, above the eight verses, her hand steady, she wrote the date. And beneath it, because she was her brother's sister and could not help herself, she wrote the question the verse had left sitting in her chest like a coal:

*If nothing given to a god is ever lost—what else is he keeping?*
