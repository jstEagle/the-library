## 1. The Valley Without Magic

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Cyrus Blackwood noticed the mistake.

She sat at the long table beneath the window, where she always sat, with her good pen — goose quill, cut by her own hand, because nothing in this valley could be bought that was worth buying — hovering above a coastline that did not exist. The map was called *Thessaly of the Nine Tides*, and it was the finest lie she had drawn in three years: an island realm ringed by shifting waters, its harbors appearing and vanishing with the moon. She had invented its rivers from memory of rivers she had once charted in a world she could no longer enter. She had invented its mountains from longing.

But the southern cape was wrong. The promontory she'd named Cape Vellum curved too gently, sheltering a bay that any self-respecting storm would swallow whole. A coast like that would never survive its own weather. Coasts were honest things; they recorded every argument the sea had ever had with the land, and this one told a coward's story.

Cyrus set down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark — ordinary sparks, chemical ones, the only kind permitted here.

"Twenty years," she said to the empty room, "and you still draw like a woman afraid of deep water."

The room did not answer. It rarely did. It was a good room, as rooms went — low-beamed, stone-floored, smelling of ink and tallow and the dried lavender she hung from the rafters against moths. Shelves climbed three of the four walls, sagging under ledgers and folios and rolled charts tied with string. The fourth wall held the window, and the window held the valley.

Beyond the glass, dusk was settling over Hollowmere like ash over a banked fire. The valley ran eight miles end to end, cupped between two ridgelines the color of old iron, and Cyrus had walked every yard of it so many times she could have mapped it blindfolded — had, in fact, done exactly that during her first winter, when the silence threatened to drive her mad and she needed something, anything, to measure. Eight miles of pasture and pinewood, one village of forty-seven souls, one river that moved too slowly to bother naming, and at the northern mouth, where the valley narrowed to a stony throat, the boundary stones.

She could see them from here on clear days: seven grey monoliths leaning together like conspirators, older than any kingdom, older than the road, older perhaps than the idea of roads. The villagers called them the Quiet Watchers and left offerings of bread and wool at their feet twice a year, though none of them could have said why. Cyrus knew why. She knew precisely what those stones were for, because she had been carried through them face-down across a mule's back, twenty years ago, while the Order's clerks read out her crimes in voices dry as ledger paper.

No spell crosses the stones. That was the whole of it, the entire architecture of her exile, stated in a single sentence. Whatever power moved the world beyond the ridge — the dragon-fire and the binding-words, the sigils and the covenants, all the vast machinery of law and wonder the Order of the Quill administered with its ink-stained fingers — stopped dead at that line of standing rock, as if the valley lay under a bell jar of perfect silence. Inside, a person could live and die without ever feeling the world's true weight. It was meant as a punishment. Some mornings it felt like mercy. Most mornings it felt like being buried alive in a place with very good light.

Cyrus rose, joints announcing themselves the way they had begun to do around her fortieth year, and went to the hearth to feed the fire. Her cottage stood at the valley's western edge, half a mile from the village, close enough to trade eggs for bread and far enough that no one came calling without purpose. The villagers were kind in the flat, incurious way of people who have decided not to ask questions. They called her *the map-woman*. Children were told she drew pictures of places that weren't real, which was true, and that she had been sent here for her own protection, which was a lie so comfortable everyone involved had agreed to keep it warm.

She fed the fire a split log and watched the flames take it. Outside, the first stars were pricking through over the eastern ridge.

There had been dragons up there once. That was the thought that came, as it came most evenings, uninvited, wearing its familiar weight. There had been dragons beyond that ridge, whole flights of them, and she had put their roosts on paper.

*Stop.* She said the word inside her own skull the way one closes a door on a draft. Twenty years of practice had made the motion almost smooth. Almost.

She returned to the table and fixed the cape. Three strokes of the quill — the coast jutted harder, the bay deepened into something a storm would respect — and Thessaly of the Nine Tides became, in that small way, more true than it had been five minutes before. This was the strange arithmetic of her exile: she could make false worlds truer, but she could not touch the true world at all. Somewhere beyond the ridge, the real maps were redrawing themselves without her — borders shifting, roads dying, coastlines arguing with the sea — and Cyrus Blackwood, once the finest cartographer the Order had ever licensed, learned of it only when a peddler came through with secondhand news a season stale.

She blew out the candle out of habit rather than need; the firelight was enough. Then she did what she did every night before sleep: she took down the atlas.

It lived on the highest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was the one object in the cottage she had brought through the stones. Her masterwork. *The Complete Survey of the Northern Reaches,* forty-one plates in her own hand, each one countersigned by the Order's seal in gold leaf that had once gleamed and now had gone the dull brown of old blood. She unwrapped it the way other people might unwrap a wound, turned to no particular plate — tonight it was Plate Thirty-One, the Roosts of the Veyra Flight, though she had not chosen it, her hands had — and looked at what she had made.

Even now, even knowing everything, some traitor corner of her mind admired it. The contour lines fell down the mountainsides like water finding its level. The annotations were precise and terrible: *thermal updrafts, reliable; approach from the west obscured by cloud-shadow after midday; nesting ledges suitable for nine adults, four juveniles.* She had written *juveniles.* She had written it in her beautiful, disciplined hand, and she had delivered it to the Order, and eleven months later the sky over the northern reaches had filled with fire that was not the dragons' own.

Cyrus closed the atlas. Wrapped it. Returned it to the shelf. The ritual took less than a minute and cost what it always cost, which was a portion of herself she did not get back.

*You were trying to end the war.* That was the other voice, the kinder one, the one that sounded like her old mentor Aldous. *You believed a map could do what armies couldn't — show both sides the shape of the thing, make the war small enough to hold in two hands.*

*I made it small enough to burn,* she answered, as she always did, and went to bar the door.

The bolt was iron, the door oak, and neither had ever mattered. In twenty years, the knock had come perhaps a dozen times: the reeve collecting the hearth-tax, a shepherd with a lame dog, once a boy selling pins who had gotten lost in the dark and wept with relief when her light showed. The valley was quiet the way the grave is quiet, and she had made her peace with that, or something shaped enough like peace to pass for it at a distance.

She was reaching for the kettle to make her evening tea — nettle and dried mint, bitter, hers — when the knocking began.

Not at the front door.

At the *window.*

Three soft taps, spaced like a heartbeat slowing. Cyrus went still with the kettle in her hand. The window at the table faced west, toward the ridge and the last light, and the glass there was old and wavy and showed her nothing but her own reflection and, behind it, the fire. She set the kettle down without letting it clatter — twenty years of living alone teaches a body to move quietly — and crossed the room.

Nothing. The slope below the window was empty, silvered with starlight, the pines black and motionless. No footprints in the frost that had begun to feather the ground. No breath-smoke hanging in the cold air.

She was telling herself it was a branch, a bird, the house settling on its stones, when the knocking came again — and this time she heard it truly, and understood it was not at the window at all.

It was under it. Low. At the height of a child.

Cyrus opened the door.

For a moment she saw nothing but the night, wide and cold and glittering. Then her eyes dropped, and there on her doorstep, mud to the knees and shivering so hard the shudder seemed to come from the bones outward, stood a girl of perhaps nine years. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if with a knife. A coat too big for her, adult-sized, belted twice around. Bare feet, blue with cold, on the frozen stone.

And in her cupped hands, held out toward Cyrus the way one holds out a wounded bird or a burning coal, a scrap of hide.

Cyrus had time to think, absurdly, *the village is half a mile away, no child walks half a mile barefoot in frost, where is the snow broken between here and the road* — and then the girl looked up at her, and Cyrus forgot the cold and the questions and twenty years of careful silence all at once.

Because the girl spoke. One word, maybe two, in a voice cracked thin with exhaustion — and the language was wrong. Not wrong the way a foreign tongue is wrong, foreign sounds arranged in unfamiliar patterns. Wrong the way a fossil is wrong: old, impossibly old, a shape of speech that predated the shapes around it. Cyrus had studied tongues for her craft — the high cant of the Order's scriptoriums, the trade-pidgins of six coasts — and she knew, with the certainty of the mapmaker who knows a coastline has no business existing, that no living kingdom spoke what this child had just spoken.

And yet she half-understood it. That was the worst part. The syllables slid past comprehension and landed somewhere deeper, the way music lands, the way a name called in a dream lands, and some buried chamber of Cyrus's heart opened a door it had kept shut for twenty years and answered:

*Here. I am here.*

The girl swayed. Cyrus caught her — the child weighed nothing, was ice-cold, was burning up all at once — and the scrap of hide tumbled from her hands onto the threshold stone.

Cyrus looked down at it.

Firelight spilled through the doorway and fell across the hide, and across the mark worked into it in lines of silver-grey that no ink she owned could match. It was a sigil. She knew sigils; she had catalogued hundreds for the Order, the binding-marks and covenant-seals that were the grammar of all lawful magic. She knew their families and their lineages the way she knew river systems.

This one belonged to no family she had ever charted. It was older than the Order's oldest seal. And as she stared at it, heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out, the sigil did the one thing a drawn mark must never, ever do.

It moved — a slow pulse along its lines, like blood returning to a numb limb —

— and in the silent valley, where no spell could cross the stones, where magic had not breathed for twenty years, the mark on the scrap of hide began, very softly, to glow.
