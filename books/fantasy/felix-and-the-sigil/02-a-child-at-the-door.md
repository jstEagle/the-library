## 2. A Child at the Door

The knocking came at the hour when the light went thin and grey, when Cyrus Blackwood had already banked the fire and set her pen down for the night.

She did not move at first. Twenty years in the valley had taught her the grammar of sounds: which creaks belonged to the cottage settling, which rustles to wind through the pines, which silences meant a fox had paused on the fencepost to consider her chickens. Knocking was not part of that grammar. No one knocked. The valley's few dozen households kept to their own lanes and their own suspicions, and the nearest neighbor was a forty-minute walk downhill through birch scrub that turned treacherous after rain. Travelers did not come here. That was rather the point of the valley.

The knock came again — three small blows, spaced with the strange deliberateness of someone who had thought about how knocking was done but never actually done it.

Cyrus rose from her chair, took the iron poker from beside the hearth out of old habit rather than any real fear, and opened the door.

The child on her doorstep was perhaps nine years old, small for it, standing in a puddle of her own making. Mud had claimed her from boot to brow — a fine grey-brown silt, river mud, valley mud, the kind that clung and dried in pale cracks. Her hair was dark and cut badly, hacked short with something dull, and her eyes were the color of wet slate. She was shivering. She was also, Cyrus noted with the detached precision of long habit, entirely unremarkable except for two things: she stood as though the doorstep were a threshold she had crossed on purpose, and she was clutching something against her chest with both hands, wrapped in her arms the way one holds an injured bird or a live coal.

"Are you lost?" Cyrus asked.

The girl looked up. And then she spoke.

The sound of it stopped Cyrus's breath. It was not the valley dialect, rough and vowel-heavy. It was not the lowland trade-speech she'd heard through travelers' letters. It was not anything. The syllables moved like water over stones — rounded, liquid, older-feeling than any tongue Cyrus had ever studied, and she had studied dead languages the way other people studied dead relatives, hungrily and without hope of answer. There were clicks buried in it, soft ones, and a rising cadence at the end of each phrase that made every sentence feel like a question asked of the sky.

But here was the impossible thing, the thing Cyrus would lie awake turning over for years afterward: she half-understood it.

Not the words. The words were nothing. But beneath them, under the language like bedrock under soil, ran meaning — a shape she could almost trace. *Cold,* said the voice inside the voice. *Long walking. Door. You are the door.*

"You're freezing," Cyrus heard herself say, which was not what she meant to say at all. She stepped back. "Come inside before you die on my step. I've enough trouble with the magistrate over my fences; I don't need a corpse."

The girl tilted her head, listening to the tone if not the words, and then she crossed the threshold — carefully, lifting her feet high, as though stepping over something invisible that lay across the sill.

* * *

Inside, by firelight, the mud resolved into detail: scrapes on her shins, a healing cut above one eyebrow, hands that were small and cracked with cold. She let Cyrus work without protest while Cyrus fed her bread softened in broth, wiped the worst of the grime from her face with a damp cloth, and held her own questions behind her teeth like coins in a closed fist.

The girl ate the way children eat when they have recently been very hungry — fast, watchful, one eye always moving. She watched the fire. She watched the maps.

That was inevitable. The cottage walls were papered with them, floor to rafters: coastlines of countries that had never existed, mountain ranges Cyrus had invented and named and loved, cities drawn in ink so fine it looked like lace. She had spent twenty years mapping realms no one could enter — first because exile left a mind with nothing else to do, and later because invented worlds were kinder than the real one, which she remembered too well. The girl's slate eyes tracked across the drawings with an expression Cyrus could not read, and did not like trying to.

"What's your name?" Cyrus tried again, slowly, in valley speech, then in lowland trade, then — feeling foolish — in Old Imperial, the liturgical tongue every educated child of the capital once memorized.

At *Imperial*, the girl flinched. A small flinch, quickly mastered, but Cyrus saw it, and filed it away where she kept everything: in the vast cold archive of things noticed and not yet understood.

Then the girl pointed at herself with one muddy finger and spoke a single word in that water-over-stones language.

"*Felix.*"

It was the only word that needed no translation. Something in its sound — the bright open vowels, the way it seemed to arrive smiling — told Cyrus it was a name, and more than a name. In the old tongues she knew, roots like that meant luck. Fortune. The fortunate one.

"Well, Felix," Cyrus said. "You're a long way from anywhere."

Felix answered at length, gravely, in the liquid syllables, and though Cyrus understood perhaps one word in twenty, she understood the shape of it: an account. A report. *I walked. I was sent. I found you.* The girl's chin lifted slightly on the last phrase, and her eyes — those wet-slate eyes, far too steady for nine years old — fixed on Cyrus with an expectation that made the hair rise along Cyrus's arms.

*Sent.* Sent by whom? Sent how? The boundary stones ringed the valley for thirty miles, old granite monoliths carved with wards so deep and so total that magic itself could not cross them. Cyrus had chosen this place precisely because of that. Here there were no sigils, no spells, no dragons, no Order of the Quill with its grey cloaks and its ledgers of the condemned. Here a woman could map imaginary worlds in peace and pretend the real one had ended.

And yet something had walked out of the world and up her hill, speaking a language older than kingdoms, looking at her as though she were expected.

*You are the door,* the voice beneath the voice had said.

"I don't know what you think I am," Cyrus murmured, mostly to herself, "but I can promise you I'm not it."

Felix frowned at her — the frown of someone who has understood perfectly well, whatever the words — and then, slowly, unwound her arms from around the thing she had been holding since the doorstep.

* * *

It was a scrap of hide. That was the first impression: leather, tanned thin, ragged-edged, no larger than Cyrus's spread hand. It might have been torn from a satchel or a drumhead or a saddlebag. It was stained dark with old handling, and it was warm — Cyrus felt the warmth before she even touched it, radiating off the girl's cupped palms like a stone pulled from a hearth.

On its surface, burned in — not inked, not dyed, *burned*, the way a brand burns into a beast — was a mark.

Cyrus had spent her life among marks. She had copied the ward-scripts of eleven nations, the merchant glyphs of the southern seas, the grave-runes of empires that had drowned before the Order of the Quill ever learned to write. She knew marks the way a shepherd knows sheep.

This was none of them.

It was a spiral, but a spiral that moved — or seemed to, in the firelight, the way certain patterns shift when the eye tires. Its outer curve broke into seven strokes that were not decoration but structure, load-bearing strokes, the way the ribs of a vault bear the roof. At its center sat a single point, a dot of darkness so absolute it looked less like pigment than like a hole burned clean through the world.

And Cyrus knew — with the same cold certainty with which she knew north from south, the certainty of her whole ruined profession — that this mark did not exist. Not in any script, any archive, any age. It was not forgotten. It was *unwritten*. It belonged to no human hand.

It belonged to the sky.

Her mouth had gone dry. "Where," she said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "did you get this?"

Felix looked at the hide, and then at Cyrus, and began to speak.

The liquid syllables poured out of her, quiet and relentless, and this time the shape beneath them was not a report but a story, and the story was terrible. Cyrus caught fragments the way a drowning swimmer catches debris. *Fire* — yes, fire, unmistakably, the word arriving with heat still attached to it. *Falling* — a great falling, a slow enormous descent, the way a mountain would fall if mountains fell. *Running* — small feet, long grass, ash coming down like grey snow. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a bell struck at intervals, a word that made the hairs on Cyrus's neck stand upright, because she had heard it only once before in her life, twenty years ago, in a room full of men in grey cloaks deciding who lived and who was erased:

The word for *dragon*.

"No," Cyrus said. She said it in three languages. She pushed back from the table hard enough that her chair legs shrieked on the flagstones, and Felix flinched, and Cyrus hated herself for the flinch and could not stop. "No. Whatever you were told, whoever sent you — there are no dragons. There haven't been dragons for twenty years. I would know. I'm the reason—"

She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, dangerous, the way a blade hangs when you catch yourself before it falls.

Felix watched her. The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with its long valley sigh, and somewhere down the slope a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Then the girl did something that undid Cyrus more thoroughly than the language or the hide or the eyes too old for her face. She reached across the table, took Cyrus's hand — her small cold fingers closing around Cyrus's wrist, gentle, deliberate — and pressed the scrap of hide into her palm.

The warmth of it went through Cyrus like a swallowed coal.

And for one instant — one heartbeat, no longer — Cyrus *understood*. Not the fragments, not the shapes beneath the words, but everything: the fire, the falling, the running, the sending. A roost burning. A shape descending through smoke, vast and wounded and deliberate. Small feet carrying something precious away from the ashes because everyone else who could carry it was dead. A dying voice, patient even in its ending, giving a child two instructions: *keep this, and find the mapmaker.*

Find the mapmaker.

The vision — if it was a vision, if it was memory, whose memory? — snapped shut like a book slammed closed. Cyrus snatched her hand back. The hide scrap lay on the table between them, warm and dark and spiral-burned, its center-hole drinking the firelight.

Cyrus became aware that she was shaking. Twenty years of careful, ordered, imaginary life, and she was shaking like a novice.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "The stones. Nothing crosses the stones. Nothing *magical* crosses the stones. Whatever you're carrying, it shouldn't be able to exist in this valley, let alone walk up my hill and sit at my table eating my bread."

Felix, naturally, did not answer in any language Cyrus commanded. She simply looked at her — at Cyrus, at the maps on the walls, at the pens and inks and the half-finished coastline of a country called Ilvaren that Cyrus had been inventing for six years — and then she lifted one small muddy finger and pointed, with terrible precision, at the wall behind Cyrus's head.

At the oldest map in the room. The one Cyrus had never taken down. The one of the *real* world, drawn from memory in the first bitter year of her exile, its edges worn soft from handling, its dragon-roosts marked in a young cartographer's confident hand — roosts that had all been empty ash for twenty years.

Except that as Cyrus turned to look, she saw — and her heart stumbled, and stopped, and started again wrong —

— that the map was changing.

There, along the northern coast, where the parchment had lain blank and faded for two decades, a line was *moving*. Ink-dark, fine as spider silk, drawing itself with the unhurried confidence of a master's pen — redrawing a coastline Cyrus had once known better than her own face, a coastline that should have been beyond the reach of any power in this valley.

Beyond the reach of anything, in fact, except the small mud-caked girl sitting at her table, watching her with wet slate eyes, holding out a piece of a dead dragon's skin.

The line kept drawing. Cyrus did not move. Outside, the last light failed over the boundary stones, and the valley settled into night around the one cottage where, for twenty years, nothing had ever happened — and where, tonight, the impossible had come in from the cold and was drying itself by her fire.
