## 4. The Sigil on Her Skin

Felix woke in the dark and did not know where she was.

The bed was too soft. The blanket smelled of wool and of a woman, not her mother — of ink and candle smoke and something green, like leaves pressed between pages. She lay very still and listened. A house ticked around her, wood settling, wind pushing at the shutters. Somewhere below, an ember shifted in a stove.

Not home. Home was gone. Home was fire.

She pulled the blanket up to her chin and looked at the window. Snow came down past the glass in slow white lines, and beyond the snow the hills were black shapes, and beyond the hills there was nothing she could name. This was the far edge of everything. The woman had said so. *No magic crosses the stones,* the woman had said, in words that were hard like river pebbles but that Felix could mostly hold onto, one at a time, before they slipped away.

Under her pillow was the scrap of hide.

She had kept it there because keeping it near made the bad dreams smaller. Not gone. Smaller. When she held it, the dreams were only fire at a distance instead of fire all around, and the falling shape fell somewhere else, somewhere she wasn't.

Felix slid her hand under the pillow.

The hide was warm.

It should not have been warm. The room was cold enough that her breath made little ghosts above the blanket. But the scrap lay against her fingers like a sleeping bird, and when she drew it out into the moonlight from the window, the mark on it was moving.

She knew the mark. She had known it since before she could remember knowing anything, the way you know your own heartbeat. It was a shape like a flame folded over on itself, with a line through its middle like a river through a valley, and small teeth of light along one side. The old man who had given it to her — running, bleeding, pressing it into her hands with both of his — had said words she hadn't understood then and didn't understand now. But she understood the mark. The mark was hers. Or she was the mark's. She had never been sure which way it went.

Now the lines of it were crawling.

Slowly, like worms coming up after rain. Slowly, like honey poured from a jar. The dark lines of the sigil lifted off the surface of the hide and stood up out of it, thin as spider silk, and they turned toward her hand the way flowers turn toward sun.

Felix should have dropped it. She knew that later, thinking back. Any sensible person would have thrown the burning thing across the room and run down the stairs to the map-woman and shouted, or tried to shout, because shouting was another thing that didn't work right anymore.

But she was nine years old, and cold, and lonely, and the mark had been the only thing in the world that stayed with her. So she didn't drop it. She watched.

The threads of it touched her palm.

Cold.

Not cold like snow. Cold like the middle of winter, cold like holding an icicle too long, except the cold went *in*, past skin, past the meat of her hand, down into the bones, and it kept going, down her wrist, up her arm, into her chest, and there it stopped, right behind her heart, and settled.

Felix's mouth opened. No sound came out. Her breath came out instead, a long white ribbon in the moonlight, and the whole time the sigil sank into her palm line by line, drinking itself into her the way water drinks into dry ground, until the hide was blank and brown and empty as any piece of an old saddle, and her hand —

Her hand was full of light.

Not bright light. It wouldn't have woken anyone. It was the light of coals under ash, a red-gold shimmer sitting just under the skin of her palm, tracing the same shape: the folded flame, the river-line, the teeth. It pulsed once, twice, and then dimmed to almost nothing, a warmth-cold thing buried deep, beating slowly like a second heart.

Felix sat in the cold bed and held her hand up to the window and looked at it for a long time.

*Oh,* she thought, in the wordless way children think big things. *Oh no.*

* * *

She didn't cry. That surprised her too. There had been a lot of crying, in the beginning — weeks of it, or days, time had gone strange since the fire — crying until her throat gave out entirely, which was maybe why the words had gone with it. But tonight the tears just sat behind her eyes like rain that wouldn't fall, and she was too busy being full of the cold-burning thing to let them out.

She got up. The floorboards were icy. She crossed them on tiptoe anyway, clutching the empty hide in one fist and holding the other hand out in front of her like it might bite someone.

In the little square of moonlight by the window she studied it again. Under the skin, the lines had gone quiet, but they were still there. She pressed her thumb against the biggest curve of the folded flame and felt it press back — gently, like something asleep rolling over.

And then the pictures came.

They came the way they always came, but stronger now, the way a radio comes in clearer when you stand closer to it. Fire first. Fire was always first. Not the fire that had taken her village — this fire was older, bigger, a fire like weather, a fire that had a *shape*, and the shape was long and winged and turning in the smoke, and it was falling, the great shape, folding as it fell the way a moth folds when you touch it, and there was screaming that wasn't sound so much as color, orange and black and a green so deep it hurt—

Felix squeezed her eyes shut. The pictures kept going behind the lids.

Running. Always running after the falling. Small feet, her feet, younger than now, mud to the ankles, and a hand putting the hide into her hands, an old hand with burned fingertips, and the old voice saying the words she couldn't understand, and the words were heavy, heavier than gold, heavier than the sky, and then more running, and then the valley, and the stones, and the woman with ink-stained fingers opening the door.

Felix opened her eyes. She was breathing fast. The mark on her palm was glowing harder, answering the memory like a dog answering its name.

She looked at the empty hide. Then at her hand. Then at the hide again.

*You live in me now,* she thought at it. She didn't know if it heard. But the glow softened, just slightly, the way a horse softens when you speak low to it, and Felix decided that it had.

* * *

Morning came grey and quiet. Felix lay in bed listening to the house wake up: footsteps overhead — no, the footsteps were downstairs, the ceiling here slanted wrong, she kept forgetting — the clink of the stove door, water being pumped, the scratch-scratch of a pen. The map-woman worked early. Felix had learned that on the first morning. The map-woman worked early, ate little, talked in straight lines, and looked at Felix sometimes with an expression Felix couldn't read yet, like a person reading a page written in a language almost like her own.

Felix got dressed. She practiced in the mirror while she braided her hair, the way she did every morning: open mouth, push air, shape it. *Guh.* Nothing. *Mmm.* Almost nothing. Her voice had gone into the fire with everything else, or maybe into the hide, maybe that was the trade, maybe that was what the old man's heavy words had cost. She didn't know. Knowing things was hard lately.

Downstairs, Cyrus Blackwood sat at her worktable with four maps pinned open and a look on her face that Felix had never seen before.

Felix stopped on the bottom stair.

The woman wasn't drawing. That was the strange part. The woman was famous — well, she wasn't famous, but she was a *mapper*, a maker of maps, and makers of maps drew. Instead she stood with her pen capped and her arms folded, staring down at the maps as if the maps had said something rude.

Felix crept closer. On the nearest map, coastlines curled across the parchment in fresh dark ink, still wet-shining at their edges. Felix couldn't read the labels, but she knew coastlines when she saw them. She'd seen the sea once, from a cliff, before.

The woman's head came up sharply. "You're awake."

Felix nodded.

"Did you—" The woman stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a stone for crossing a stream. "Did you sleep."

Felix nodded. Then shook her head. Then made a wobbling motion with her flat hand, *so-so*, and the woman's mouth did a thing that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't.

"Sit. Eat." There was bread and honey on the side table, cut for her already. Felix took a piece and ate standing up, watching the woman watch the maps.

Then the woman did something new. She turned, took a slate from the shelf, wrote three quick words on it, and held it out.

*SHOW ME YOUR HANDS.*

Felix's stomach dropped, cold and fast, like stepping off a high root.

She looked at the slate. She looked at the woman's grey eyes, which were patient and sharp and missed nothing, which had probably already missed nothing, which had seen the wet ink moving on the maps overnight and was now looking at the nine-year-old girl who had arrived out of the snow with a scrap of old hide and no voice and no story anyone could read.

Felix could have run. There was a door. There were hills. But the hills were full of snow and the men on horses, and the woman had given her bread and a bed and had not asked questions yet, and besides — besides, the mark in her palm pulsed, gently, and it felt like the mark wanted to be shown. Like hiding it made it itch.

Slowly, chewing her last bite of bread, Felix held out her left hand, palm up.

Cyrus Blackwood went very still.

The grey eyes fixed on the faint red-gold shimmer under the skin — quieter in daylight, but there, unmistakably there, the folded flame, the river-line, the teeth. The woman's lips moved without sound. Her hand rose halfway toward Felix's palm and stopped, hovering, not quite touching, the way you hover your hand over a stove to ask it how hot.

"That's not possible," the woman whispered. Then, catching herself, she glanced at Felix and her voice changed, gentled, slowed. "Does it hurt?"

Felix considered this honestly. She turned her hand over. She pointed at her palm, then made the sign she'd invented for the cold-fire feeling: fingers spread, then closing slowly into a fist, like grabbing something invisible.

The woman frowned. "Cold? It burns cold?"

Felix nodded, pleased. *Burns cold.* Yes. Exactly that.

"And when did—" The woman stopped herself again, visibly reorganizing her words. She picked up the slate and wrote, unhurried, block letters: *WHEN DID IT COME TO YOU.*

Felix pointed at her palm. Then she pointed at the empty scrap of hide, which she had brought downstairs tucked into her sleeve without deciding to, the way you carry a tooth that fell out. She laid the blank brown leather on the table between them.

The woman looked at the hide for a long moment. Then she picked it up, tilted it to the light, ran her thumb across its emptiness, and set it down with a care that told Felix everything: the woman knew exactly what it had been, and exactly what it was now, and the knowing frightened her.

Felix reached out and touched the woman's wrist. The woman flinched — actually flinched, this composed grey woman with her straight lines — and Felix snatched her hand back.

But then the woman exhaled, and crouched down so their faces were level, which nobody had done since the fire, and said, very slowly and clearly:

"You are safe here. Do you understand? Whatever that is. Whoever gave it to you. While you are under my roof, you are safe."

Felix searched the grey eyes for the trick in it. There didn't seem to be one. Grown-ups' eyes usually had tricks in them, or doors closed behind the words, but these were just tired and honest and afraid, afraid for her, maybe, or afraid of her, and Felix found she couldn't tell the difference and wasn't sure it mattered.

She nodded.

Then, because the mark was warm in her palm and the pictures were pushing at the backs of her eyes again, she did something she had never done for anyone. She took the slate and the chalk stub from the woman's loose fingers, and she drew.

She drew fast, the way the pictures came: a great winged shape, long-necked, folding as it fell. Smoke. A tower of it. Little running figures. And beneath it all, pressing down from the top of the slate like a lid, she drew the sigil itself, the folded flame with the river through it, and she tapped it twice, hard, and looked up at the woman with her chin trembling and her jaw set, the way you look at someone when you are handing them half of the heaviest thing in the world and cannot even say *help me carry it.*

Cyrus Blackwood stared at the slate.

The color left her face the way tide leaves a shore. Her eyes moved from the falling dragon to the sigil to Felix's palm and back, and her hand came up slowly and pressed flat against her own mouth, and above her fingers her eyes had gone wide and wet and old with some knowledge Felix didn't have yet.

Outside, far off on the northern ridge, something glinted. Neither of them saw it. The wind had come around, and the snow had thinned, and down out of the grey hills, small as beetles, riders were picking their way along the skyline — but that was still hours away, and the house was still warm, and the woman was still crouched in front of Felix with her hand over her mouth, saying, barely aloud, in the pebble-hard words:

"Oh, child. What did they give you?"
