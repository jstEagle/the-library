## 5. Riders on the Ridge

The knock had come at dusk, and by full dark Cyrus had almost convinced herself the world beyond the valley had forgotten her entirely.

Almost. She was a cartographer, after all, and cartographers did not deal in almosts. They dealt in what could be measured, and what could be measured was this: twenty years of silence, and then, in a single week, a child who spoke in the tongues of dead empires, sigils blooming on her maps like frost on glass, and now — she pressed her palm flat against the cold pane of the eastern window — a line of torchlight moving along the northern ridge.

She counted the lights out of habit, the way she'd once counted cairns on survey routes. Seven. No. Eight. They moved slowly, deliberately, spaced like beads on an abacus, which meant they were not travelers. Travelers bunched together against the dark and the cold. These lights were spread for coverage. A search pattern.

Cyrus had drawn a thousand search patterns herself. She knew their grammar the way other people knew prayers.

"Felix." Her voice came out low and even, and she was grateful for that, because everything beneath it was not even at all. "Come away from the fire."

The girl looked up from the hearth, where she'd been toasting a heel of bread on a forked stick with the absolute seriousness of someone performing surgery. Flour still dusted one of her sleeves from the afternoon's disastrous attempt at teaching her to knead dough — Felix had instead sculpted something that might have been a duck, or a fist, and then eaten half of it raw. The sigil-marked hide scrap lay folded in her pocket; Cyrus could see its outline pressing against the fabric, and she had stopped pretending to herself that she didn't check for it every hour.

Felix tilted her head. *Why?* was not a word she had yet learned in valley speech, but she had a hundred ways of asking it — the lift of her chin, the narrowing of those dark eyes that seemed older than her nine or ten years, the small impatient gesture she made with two fingers, turning them over and over: *explain, explain.*

"Because," Cyrus said, crossing the room and taking the bread stick gently from her hand, "there are people on the ridge, and I would rather they didn't see us."

Felix considered this. Then she pointed at the window, and then at Cyrus, and made the two-fingers gesture again.

*Explain.*

"I don't know who they are yet." That much was true, and Cyrus clung to it. It might be tax collectors from the valley township, though no tax collector rode eight abreast along a ridge at night in early winter. It might be lost shepherds. It might be —

But she had already seen, as the lead torch crested the spine of the ridge, the way the rider sat his horse: upright, unhurried, the torch held high and steady in a gloved hand, sweeping the slope below in slow arcs. Not looking for a path. Looking for signs of life. And when the wind gusted down off the heights and carried the smell of woodsmoke to her window, she saw — briefly, unmistakably, silhouetted against the flame — the grey cloak, cut long and severe, and pinned at the shoulder with a shape she would have known anywhere, in any light, in any year of her life or death.

A quill. Silver, crossed through by a single ruled line.

The Order of the Quill.

The bread stick slipped from her fingers and clattered against the hearthstone. Felix flinched, then stared at her — at the sudden stillness that had come over Cyrus, the kind of stillness she imagined prey animals achieved, every part of the body agreeing at once to stop existing.

"No," Cyrus said quietly, to the window, to the ridge, to the twenty years between. "No, you're dead. You were finished. I buried you."

* * *

They came down off the ridge within the hour, and Cyrus spent that hour doing the most important work of her life, and she had once charted the Dragonspine coast in a storm.

Her mind, that treacherous, magnificent instrument, laid it all out for her in clean survey lines while her hands shook so badly she dropped the lamp twice. *Grey cloaks, eight riders, search formation, descending north slope. They will reach the valley floor by midnight. The boundary stones will stop their magic — if their magic is still what it was — but nothing stops horses, and nothing stops questions.* Questions were the Order's true weapon anyway. Magic was only ever the flourish at the end of the sentence.

Where could a child hide?

Not the cellar. First place anyone looked. Not the loft — same. The cottage had one room and a lean-to and a root cellar, and Cyrus cursed the honest geometry of her own home, built by a woman who had wanted nothing hidden from anyone, least of all herself.

Then her eyes fell on the maps.

They covered the east wall, floor to ceiling, twenty years of imaginary realms rendered in ink and watercolor and stubborn devotion: the Meridian Isles with their tide-road cities, the Kingdoms of Glass, the great inland sea of Vhal that she had invented in her third winter here because the loneliness had grown teeth. Drawn on heavy paper, mounted on wooden battens, hung with iron hooks.

And behind the largest of them — behind Vhal, her favorite, her most beloved lie — the wall was stone, and the stone had a hollow where the original builders had cached salt and tools, and it was deep enough for a thin child to stand in, turned sideways, breathing through her nose.

"It will be dark," Cyrus told her, pulling the map of Vhal free from its hooks with hands that steadied themselves the moment they touched paper, the way they always did, the way they hadn't in twenty years until a mute girl walked into her life carrying a dead language and a living brand. "Dark, and close, and you must not sneeze, and you must not make a sound, no matter what you hear. Do you understand me?"

Felix looked at the hollow. Looked at Cyrus. Then she reached up and took Cyrus's face in both small, flour-dusted hands, and studied her with that terrible ancient attention, and made a sound — not a word, not in any tongue, just a soft rising note, a question mark made of breath.

"They are bad men," Cyrus said. Or tried to say. What came out was quieter and truer: "They are men who once believed they were good, and it made them worse than any villain, and I was one of them, and I do not know yet whether they have come for you or for me, but either way—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she let it. "Either way, I will not give you to them."

Felix's eyes went wide. Then she did something she had never done before: she pressed her forehead against Cyrus's breastbone, brief and hard, the way a bird strikes a window, and stepped back into the hollow, and pulled the darkness around herself like a cloak.

Cyrus hung the map of Vhal back on its hooks. Her handiwork. Her lie. Her sea that did not exist, hiding a child who should not exist, inside a life that had officially ended two decades ago.

Then she banked the fire, set out two cups instead of one — no, she thought, then corrected it, three cups, because a household of one suspiciously hosting two cups looks like a woman expecting company, and a household of three cups looks like a woman who lives alone and has simply never thrown anything away — and went to answer the knocking that had already begun at her gate.

* * *

There were eight of them, and the leader dismounted at her fence line rather than riding through it, which was either courtesy or theater, and with the Order it was always theater.

He was younger than her. That was the first wound of the night, before he'd said a word: the man wearing the grey cloak and the silver quill was perhaps thirty-five, which meant he had been a boy in novitiate robes when Cyrus Blackwood stood trial, which meant he had learned the Order's catechisms from men who had learned them partly from her own published treatises on survey-magic. She had helped build the instrument that was now pointed at her door.

"Mistress Blackwood." He inclined his head. His voice was pleasant, trained, pitched to carry warmth without containing any. "Forgive the hour. I am Scrivener-Second Aldous Vane. We're conducting inquiries in the valley."

"At midnight," Cyrus said.

"The Order keeps its own hours." He smiled. It was a good smile. She remembered having one like it. "May we come in? The wind up on the ridge is bitter."

"You may stand where you are," she said, "and tell me what the Order wants in a valley where none of your art works. You know that, surely. Your superiors drew the boundary themselves. I watched them do it."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — not anger. Interest. The interest of a man updating a ledger. "You've been informed, then. Good. It saves time." He produced, from inside his cloak, a folded paper, and did not offer it across the fence, merely held it where the torchlight caught the seal. Black wax. The quill, crossed through. "By decree of the Grand Scrivener himself: the Order's writ now runs within the boundary stones. All magic lawfully flows where the Quill commands it. You'll forgive me if I don't explain how. Some knowledge is above a valley exile's station."

Above her station. Cyrus felt the old rage stir in its twenty-year grave and made herself look bored instead, because boredom was armor and rage was a scent, and these men hunted by scent.

"What do you want, Scrivener?"

"A child." He said it plainly, and the plainness was the horror of it. "A girl, nine years old, perhaps ten. Dark-haired. Mute — or feigning muteness, which amounts to the same thing for our purposes. She may bear a mark on her skin. A sigil." He paused, delicately. "You will report to us at once if such a child has sought shelter with you. This is not a request, Mistress Blackwood. It is a census question. Refusal to answer a census question carries penalties under the new writ."

Behind him, the seven other riders had spread out, casually, the way men spread when they intend to seem casual. One was looking at her chimney smoke. Another at the tracks by her woodpile — small tracks, bootless feet, days old, half-froze. Cyrus watched him crouch beside them and felt her heartbeat move into her throat.

And she understood, in that moment, with the clarity of a coastline appearing through fog, that they had not come for her at all. Twenty years of exile, twenty years of being a closed file, a spent asset, a name struck from the rolls — and tonight she was nobody, a footnote, a local eccentric to be questioned and dismissed. They wanted Felix. They wanted the vessel, though Vane hadn't used that word yet, and Cyrus knew the word because she had read it in Order dispatches during the war, in reports about dragon eggs and dragon-blooded captives and things too valuable to kill and too dangerous to leave alive.

Her old life didn't want her dead. That was the shock of it, arriving cold and total, worse than any accusation: her old life didn't think about her at all. She had been punished, filed, and forgotten. But this child — this child they had crossed mountains for.

Which meant everything Cyrus had suffered had never been about justice. It had been bookkeeping. And the same institution that had discarded her without a second entry now intended to collect a little girl the way one collects a debt.

"Well?" Vane asked.

Every instinct Cyrus owned screamed at her to tell the truth — not from honesty, but from terror. Truth was safe. Truth was what she'd been trained in, mapped in, married to for thirty years. Lie to the Order and they smelled it the way sharks smell blood; she had personally taught junior scriveners the interrogation tells, the micro-hesitations, the way a liar's eyes go to the upper left—

She made herself yawn.

It was, she thought later, the finest piece of cartography she had ever done: mapping a lie onto her own face, accurate to the scale required. "A child," she repeated, rubbing her eyes. "In this valley? Scrivener, I have lived alone for twenty years. The last child born here was Hesta Marlowe's boy, and he's fourteen now and breaks my fence rails monthly. If a mute girl wandered in from the passes, the whole township would have told me before they told you — they tell me everything, mostly unsolicited. Nothing has come through the boundary. Nothing comes through the boundary. That was rather the point of building it."

Vane studied her. The torches crackled. Somewhere behind the map of Vhal, in a stone hollow the width of a coffin, a child was holding her breath, and Cyrus held it with her.

"You were a Surveyor-General," Vane said suddenly. "Before."

"I was a great many things before. Most of them are buried."

"The Grand Scrivener remembers you."

"The Grand Scrivener," Cyrus said, "signed my exile. I trust his memory of me is warm."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment, the length of a drawn breath — she thought Vane would push. She saw it assemble behind his eyes: the follow-up question, the casual request to inspect the house, the order to his men to walk the property with lanterns. She catalogued her defenses and found them pitiful: one locked door, one nonexistent sea, and a lie less than a minute old.

Then the rider crouched by the woodpile straightened up, and called out something in the clipped cant of the Order's field-sign, and Vane's attention slid away from her like a blade being sheathed.

"Tracks," the man said. "Old. Fox, maybe. Or dog."

"Or nothing." Vane turned back to Cyrus, and the pleasant mask reassembled itself. "Thank you for your candor, Mistress Blackwood. We'll continue our inquiries south along the valley. Someone will call again — the Order does like to be thorough." He remounted, gathered his reins, and paused. "One more thing. Curiosity, nothing more. Your chimney smoked unusually late tonight. Baking, at this hour?"

"Bread keeps better than I sleep," Cyrus said. "Good night, Scrivener."

He held her gaze a moment longer — a surveyor's gaze, measuring, plotting coordinates — and then the eight of them wheeled away, torches swinging south, and the dark swallowed them by degrees, first the cloaks, then the lights, then the sound of hooves, until the valley was silent again and the silence was worse.

* * *

Cyrus stood at the gate until her fingers went numb on the latch. Then she walked back inside, barred the door, and lifted the map of Vhal from its hooks with hands that shook only now, only afterward, the way a wall shakes after the earthquake rather than during.

Felix tumbled out of the hollow, grey-faced, dusted in stone grit, and grabbed the front of Cyrus's shirt with both fists and held on, and made sounds — rapid, urgent, wordless, the language of terror that predates all languages — and Cyrus knelt and wrapped her arms around her and let her.

"It's all right," she said, into the girl's hair. "It's all right. They're gone."

They were not gone. They were south along the valley, asking the same questions at every door, and they would be back, because the Order liked to be thorough, and because somewhere in that pleasant young voice there had been a hook she'd only half-heard: *someone will call again.*

And there was another thing, the thing she turned over as she fed Felix the last of the bread and watched her eat it ravenously, the thing that sat in Cyrus's chest like a stone dropped down a well:

Vane had said the Order's writ now ran within the boundary stones. Their magic flowed where the Quill commanded it. Which meant the valley was no longer a sanctuary. Which meant the one law of Cyrus's world — the iron, absolute, load-bearing law of her exile — had been repealed while she wasn't looking, and no one had thought to inform her, because no one remembered she existed.

She looked at Felix, asleep at last on the pallet, one hand curled around the hide scrap even in sleep, the sigil faintly visible through the worn leather, glowing like a coal under ash.

Twenty years ago, the Order had taken everything from her and called it justice. Tonight they had come for a child and called it a census.

Cyrus sat down at her worktable, pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, and began — because her hands needed work or they would betray her — to draw. Not an imaginary realm. For the first time in twenty years, she drew the valley itself: the boundary stones, the northern ridge, the southern road, and eight small marks moving along it, each one labeled in her precise, merciless hand.

If they were going to hunt the girl, they would do it across ground that Cyrus Blackwood had charted first.

At the bottom of the page, without quite deciding to, she wrote a title. Two words, in the old survey style, the style she'd been exiled for:

*Living Terrain.*
