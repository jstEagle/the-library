## 6. Lessons in Old Tongues

The first word Cyrus taught Felix was *bread*.

She set the loaf on the table between them that morning and tapped it with one finger. "Bread," she said slowly, the way one might speak to a foreign merchant or a slightly dim dog.

Felix looked at the loaf. Looked at Cyrus. Then she said a word back — a low, rolling syllable with a crackle in its middle, like a fire settling in a hearth — and touched the bread almost reverently, as though Cyrus had introduced her to something holy rather than something baked from valley barley three days past its best.

"No, that's *bread*." Cyrus tapped it again. "Bread."

"*Vessh-ka.*"

"That is not bread. That is a noise."

Felix's mouth twitched. It was the first time Cyrus had seen anything like a smile on the child's face, and it transformed her entirely — the wariness falling away for a heartbeat, leaving just a girl of perhaps nine with flour still somehow in her hair from who knew where. Then the smile vanished again, tucked away like contraband, and Felix pointed at the bread and repeated, patiently, "*Vessh-ka.*"

Cyrus sighed and reached for her notebook. If she was going to be out-argued by a mute-seeming child over breakfast, she was at least going to document it.

That was how it began: two languages meeting across a kitchen table like armies negotiating a truce neither fully trusted.

* * *

By the third week, the cottage had developed a rhythm that Cyrus would have found unrecognizable only a month before.

Mornings were for words. They sat at the table — Cyrus had long ago stopped thinking of Felix as a guest; guests left — with the notebook open between them, and Cyrus would teach the plain vocabulary of the valley: *water*, *wood*, *cold*, *door*. Simple nouns first, then verbs, then the treacherous territory of prepositions, which seemed to offend Felix on principle. The girl learned with an unsettling speed, her dark eyes fixed on Cyrus's mouth as though reading the language off her lips before her ears could catch it. Within days she was naming things. Within a fortnight she was correcting Cyrus's pronunciation of her own tongue, which Cyrus chose to find charming rather than humiliating.

Afternoons belonged to Felix's language.

It had no name that Felix could give — or rather, when Cyrus asked what it was called, Felix pressed her palm flat against her chest, against the place beneath her shirt where the sigil had settled into her skin like a scar that had decided to stay, and made a sound deep in her throat that was less a word than a resonance. Cyrus wrote in her notes: *the name of the language is the sound of the sigil itself. Or possibly I am asking the wrong question.*

Dragon-speech, if that was what it was, did not behave. It had no alphabet Cyrus could chart, no grammar she could diagram in her careful cartographer's hand. Words changed shape depending on who spoke them — not their meaning, but their actual sound, as though the language were less a code than a weather system each speaker walked through differently. When Felix said *vessh-ka*, the word meant bread, warmth, the sharing of food, and — Cyrus pieced this together over several baffling days — *the person you trust enough to eat beside*. One word carrying four meanings, braided together so tightly they couldn't be separated without killing all of them.

"It's efficient," Cyrus muttered, writing. "I'll grant it that."

Felix, who had learned to read Cyrus's moods faster than either language, pushed the honey pot toward her. A peace offering. Or a bribe. Cyrus took the bribe.

The mistranslations were the worst and best part. Felix once spent an entire evening trying to explain a concept that involved her hands circling each other, rising, and Cyrus guessed *birds? smoke? prayer?* before Felix seized the pencil and drew — badly, furiously, with a child's impatient strokes — a spiral of embers climbing off a bonfire into a night sky. *Ember-rise*, the moment when sparks stop being part of the fire and become part of the air. There was a word for it. Of course there was a word for it. A language old enough to have watched dragons sleep would have a word for everything worth watching.

Cyrus sat back and felt something she hadn't felt in twenty years: the vertigo of genuine discovery. She had mapped imaginary coastlines for two decades, inventing seas because no real sea would have her. Now a real mystery sat at her table eating her bread with her spoon, and every day it gave her a new country to chart.

She should have been more afraid than she was. Later, she would remember that.

* * *

The sigil grew stronger with every lesson. Cyrus noticed it the way you notice a draft — small at first, deniable, then undeniable.

It started with the hide scrap. Cyrus kept it locked in the cedar chest with her oldest maps, but some mornings she'd open the chest for ink or paper and find the scrap warm to the touch, its burned lines faintly luminous, as though something beneath them were breathing. On lesson days it glowed. On days when Felix laughed — rare, precious, always surprising — it positively shimmered.

Then it moved to the maps.

Cyrus woke one grey morning to find her map of the Southern Reaches — a realm she had invented whole cloth, down to its invented rivers — marked with a thin line of gold that had not been there at bedtime. The line traced the course of her invented river Vael with perfect accuracy, which was impossible, since Cyrus herself had never been certain where the Vael bent near its delta. The map knew. Something in the cottage knew her maps better than she did.

And then, on the coldest morning of the month, it moved to skin.

Felix came to the table rubbing her left palm, and when Cyrus took the hand to look — the girl offered it readily now, another small surrender that made Cyrus's chest ache in ways she didn't examine — the sigil had spread. Where before it had been a single mark, a closed spiral the size of a coin, now fine lines extended from it like roots, or rivers, branching toward her wrist. They were not red or angry. They were gold, faint as breath on glass, and warm.

"Does it hurt?"

Felix considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, then made the gesture she used for *a little, but not the bad kind* — a pinch of the fingers, followed by a shrug, followed by a look that said *don't make it a thing*.

"It is absolutely going to be a thing," Cyrus said, and got up to fetch the salve she used for chapped hands, because it was the only medicine she owned, and because doing something — anything — was better than sitting with the knowledge that a nine-year-old was being slowly rewritten at her kitchen table, and that Cyrus's own lessons were the ink.

Because that was the pattern, wasn't it. She'd charted it now, in her notebook, in her neat and damning hand: *sigil activity correlates with language exchange. The more we teach each other, the stronger it grows.* Not magic leaking through the boundary stones anymore. Magic *learning*. Feeding. Every word of dragon-speech Cyrus absorbed seemed to pass through Felix like current through wire, and every valley word Cyrus gave away seemed to give the sigil somewhere new to go.

They were teaching each other, and the teaching was building something, and Cyrus had no idea whether what it built would be a bridge or a fuse.

* * *

Winter deepened. The lessons continued. Cyrus stopped pretending she might send the girl away.

There was an evening — she would keep it always, the way she kept the good maps flat and dry — when snow came down thick and silent beyond the windows, sealing the cottage in white, and Felix discovered roasted chestnuts.

Cyrus had bought them from the peddler in autumn more out of nostalgia than appetite, and they'd sat in their basket by the hearth ever since. That evening she remembered them, scored the shells, and set them in the fire's edge, and the smell that filled the cottage made Felix sit bolt upright with an expression of such naked wonder that Cyrus laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled them both.

"*Vessh-ka,*" Felix breathed, pointing at the chestnuts, at the fire, at Cyrus, apparently unwilling to let the word be limited to bread when the world was offering so many applications.

"Close enough." Cyrus peeled one, blew on it, and held it out.

Felix ate it with her eyes closed. Then she opened them, took a second chestnut, and instead of eating it, placed it carefully in Cyrus's hand and folded Cyrus's fingers around it.

The gesture needed no translation. Cyrus's throat went tight. Twenty years in this valley, twenty years of meals eaten alone at this table with the wind complaining at the eaves, and here was a child giving her a chestnut as though it were a medal, as though Cyrus had done something worthy of a chestnut, and Cyrus had to turn to the fire and pretend to tend it until her face was under control.

Later, full of chestnuts and drowsy with warmth, Felix fell asleep in the chair with her head tipped against the wing, and Cyrus sat across from her mapping the sleeping face the way she mapped everything — the chapped lips, the smudge of ash on one cheek, the small hands curled loose in her lap, the left palm turned upward where the golden lines caught the firelight.

*Family,* thought a voice in her head that sounded dangerously like hope. *This is what it looks like.*

And underneath it, quieter, colder, the other voice — the cartographer's voice, the one that measured and recorded and refused comfort: *and this is what a weapon looks like, too. You are sharpening her every evening. You can feel it happening. What are you making, Cyrus Blackwood, and who will use it?*

She had no answer. She banked the fire, carried the girl to the cot — lighter than any child had a right to be, as though she were built mostly of secrets — and told herself tomorrow she would think about it properly.

Tomorrow she taught Felix the word for *home*, and Felix taught her the dragon-word whose closest meaning was *the place your fire is safe*, and the sigil on the girl's palm brightened like a lamp being turned up, and Cyrus lay awake long after, listening to the wind, telling herself the light was fading when she knew perfectly well it wasn't.

* * *

The trouble came, as trouble does, wearing ordinary clothes.

It was a market day. Cyrus needed salt, lamp oil, and honesty about her stores, and Felix — who had grown restless through the shortening days, pressing her face to windows like a cat denied — begged with her whole body to come along. Cyrus weighed it. The village knew a child lived with her now; hiding Felix entirely would raise more questions than bringing her. And the sigil was hidden under mittens. And the girl deserved, surely, one morning of the market, of cinnamon steam and fishmongers' shouting and the small human circus Cyrus herself had avoided for years.

"One hour," Cyrus said. "You stay beside me. You do not speak to anyone — your words sound strange, and strange words bring strangers asking questions. Nod if you understand."

Felix nodded, and held out her mitten, and Cyrus took it, and they walked down into the village together like any guardian and ward, and for nearly forty minutes it was wonderful. Felix's eyes drank everything. She pointed at a hanging carp and made the dragon-word for it, delighted; she stood transfixed before the bell-ringer's cart as though it were a cathedral; she bought — Cyrus bought, at Felix's insistent tugging — a twist of honey candy, and the vendor's wife smiled and said the child had her mother's manners, and Cyrus, who had no answer for that, smiled back and felt the lie fit her like an old coat.

It was the baker's boy who ruined it. Eight years old, gap-toothed, bored, trailing his mother's stall. He sidled up to Felix while Cyrus haggled over oil, and snatched at her mitten — children do, the way cats do, testing the world — and pulled it half off, and for one bare second Felix's palm was open to the winter light, gold lines gleaming, before she clamped her fist shut.

The boy stared. Felix stared back. And then Felix said a word.

Not a shout. Not even loud. Just one syllable of dragon-speech, low and resonant, the way she said *vessh-ka* — but this word had a different weight, and the air around them *bent*, very slightly, the way heat bends above a forge, and every pigeon within thirty feet burst upward at once, and the candle flames in the chandler's stall next door all leaned toward the girl like iron filings finding north.

Then it passed. The pigeons resettled. The candles straightened. The baker's boy backed away with his eyes enormous, and his mother asked what was wrong, and he said *nothing*, because whatever he'd seen had already filed itself away in the place children keep the things adults won't believe.

But Cyrus had seen the faces. Three or four of them, scattered along the row — the farrier, the widow Hesk, a grey-eyed man selling furs whom Cyrus had never noticed before and would now never stop noticing. Faces that had turned toward the bending of the air the way old soldiers turn toward distant thunder. Faces that had *recognized* it.

She paid for the oil without counting her change, took Felix's hand, and walked home at exactly the pace of someone with nothing whatsoever to hide.

Only at the door, with the latch thrown and the curtain drawn, did she kneel down and take both of Felix's hands in hers.

"What did you say to him? The boy. What word was that?"

Felix worked her mouth around the valley-tongue, hunting. "*Angry,*" she offered finally. Then, shaking her head, dissatisfied: "*Protect.*" Another headshake. She pressed her sealed fist to her chest, over the sigil, and said the resonant wordless sound that meant the language itself, and then pointed at the boy, and then made a gesture Cyrus understood completely and wished she didn't: *mine. Keep away. Mine.*

"You told him I was yours," Cyrus said quietly. "You told him I was under your protection."

Felix nodded once, fierce and solemn, a knight swearing fealty with honey candy still in her pocket.

Cyrus sat back on her heels. Somewhere out there, a grey-eyed fur-seller was remembering the way the air had bent, and the Order of the Quill had eyes in every market town in the province, and a nine-year-old had just declared herself the guardian of a disgraced cartographer twice her age, and the sigil on her palm was glowing through the mitten seams like a coal refusing to die.

"Well," Cyrus said, standing, reaching for her notebook with hands that were not quite steady. "We are going to need better rules."

Outside, far off on the northern ridge, too far to be sure, too shaped to be mistaken — something moved against the sky.
