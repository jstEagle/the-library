## 8. Fire Under the Frost

The cold came first as a sound.

Felix heard it in the night, a thin whistling at the window's edge, like the valley itself breathing through its teeth. She lay in the small bed under the eaves, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar and ink, and listened to the wind walk around the cottage, testing every seam the way a wolf tests a fence.

She did not mind the cold. Cold was only another word to learn. Cyrus had taught her *snow* that morning, drawing it in the air with one finger — *snow*, and Felix had said it back, and Cyrus had smiled the small smile she made when Felix got a word right, like a door opening just a crack.

But the sigil minded the cold.

Felix felt it wake in her palm as the storm deepened, a slow turning-over, like a bird shifting in its sleep. It was warm now. It was always a little warm, ever since the night it had left the hide scrap and crawled onto her skin, but tonight it was warmer than that. Tonight it felt like holding a stone that had sat all day in summer sun, except the warmth came from inside her, from somewhere behind her wrist, her elbow, her ribs.

*Fire,* said the feeling, in the old language that had no words. *Fire is sleeping here.*

Felix pressed her palm against her chest and waited for morning.

* * *

Morning did not come the way mornings usually came.

Usually Felix woke to grey light and the smell of porridge and the scratch-scratch of Cyrus's pen, the cartographer already bent over her maps like a heron over still water. Usually the world outside the window was quiet and white-edged with frost.

This morning the window was blind. Snow had piled against the glass in the night, and the light that came through was blue and strange, underwater light, and the wind had grown teeth overnight. When Felix pushed open her door, the cold hit her face like a handful of gravel.

The whole valley had vanished. The hills were gone. The boundary stones were gone. There was only white, falling and falling, thick as feathers plucked from some enormous bird, and the silence underneath it, so complete that Felix could hear her own heart.

She stood on the doorstep in her too-big coat — one of Cyrus's old ones, sleeves rolled three times — and held out her hand, and let snow land on her palm beside the sigil.

That was her mistake. Or maybe it was not a mistake at all. Maybe the sigil had been waiting for exactly this: for her hand to open, for the sky to come down and touch it.

The snowflake landed.

And melted.

Not slowly, the way snow melts on skin. Instantly. The flake touched down and became a bead of water, then steam, and the steam curled up off Felix's palm like a tiny ghost, and the sigil drank the cold out of the air around her hand the way dry ground drinks rain.

Felix frowned at it. The warmth was spreading now, up past her wrist, into the bones of her arm. It did not hurt. That was the strange part. It felt *good*, the way standing near a hearth feels good after a long walk, and that goodness frightened her more than pain would have, because she knew — in the wordless way she knew things, the way she had known to run, to run, to run through the dark with the hide scrap clutched to her chest — that good feelings like this one were not hers. They belonged to something older. Something that remembered fire the way Felix remembered running.

*Go inside,* she told herself, in Cyrus's words. *Go inside, Felix.*

But her feet stayed where they were, and the snow kept falling, and the sigil kept drinking.

* * *

By midday the storm had swallowed the whole world, and Cyrus declared it a day for maps.

They sat at the big table by the fire, Cyrus with her pens and her fine paper, Felix with her own smaller sheet and a stub of charcoal. This was their game, or their lesson — Felix was never sure which. Cyrus drew coastlines and Felix copied them, and sometimes the lines turned into animals, and sometimes the animals turned into letters in the old language, and Cyrus studied those letters for a long time afterward with an expression Felix could not read, an expression like hunger and fear sharing one face.

Today Cyrus was drawing mountains. "These," she said, tapping the page, "are the Teeth of Sorrel. Say it."

"Teeth of Sorrel." Felix liked the words. They tasted sharp.

"And these are the Emberreach." Cyrus's voice went quieter on the second word, the way it always did, and her pen hovered without touching paper. "Far south. Where the fires used to live."

Felix looked at her palm. The sigil pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Fires," she said carefully, shaping the new word. Then, because the sigil was warm and the room was warm and the truth was sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal: "One fire."

Cyrus set down her pen.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fire in the hearth crackled. The wind moaned at the windows. And Felix watched Cyrus's face do several things very quickly — go still, go pale, go careful — before settling into the calm mask that Felix was learning to see through.

"One fire," Cyrus repeated. Her voice was level, but her hands, folded on the table, had gone white at the knuckles. "Felix. What do you know about fires?"

Felix didn't have words for it. Not valley words, not yet. So she did what she always did when the old language rose up in her and there was no one who could hold it: she put her hand flat on the table, sigil up, and let it speak.

The warmth poured out of her like water out of a tipped cup.

It happened fast — faster than she could stop, faster than she even understood until it was done. The table wood warmed under her palm. The teacup beside her hand began, very softly, to steam. And the sigil flared — not bright, not loud, nothing anyone outside the cottage would notice — but Felix felt it turn over inside her like a great beast rolling in its sleep, and the warmth ran up her arm, across her shoulders, down her spine, pooling low and deep, and for one moment she was not in the cottage at all. She was somewhere hot and high, looking down at a world stitched with rivers, and something enormous breathed beside her, and the breath smelled of stone and lightning—

Then it passed. The teacup steamed. The wood under her hand was almost too warm to touch.

Cyrus had stood up so fast her chair fell over.

"Felix." She crossed the room in three strides and took Felix's hand in both of hers, and her fingers were shaking. "Does it hurt? Are you hurt?"

"No." Felix shook her head. "Warm. Only warm."

"Warm." Cyrus laughed, but it was not a real laugh; it was the kind that comes out when a person is frightened and does not want to show it. She fetched a cloth and wrapped Felix's palm in it, gentle, gentle, the way you handle a bird with a broken wing. "All right. Warm. We'll keep it warm and we'll keep it quiet, and when the storm passes—"

The floor creaked overhead.

Both of them froze. Felix's ears, sharper than Cyrus's, caught it clearly: footsteps in the loft above, where no one should be. Then a muffled curse, and a thump, and a familiar voice pitched low through the floorboards:

"Cyrus? Your roof's shedding slates into my yard and I've half a mind to send you the bill!"

Tomas. The shepherd from two fields over, red-faced and loud, with a beard full of yesterday's supper. Relief went through Felix like warm milk — and then, a heartbeat later, fear, because Tomas never climbed onto anyone's roof, and he certainly hadn't climbed onto theirs, which meant—

"The back," Cyrus mouthed. But it was too late. Boots thundered down the outside stair, fists hammered the front door, and voices came with them, several voices, muffled by wind and wool but unmistakable: the village had come to Cyrus Blackwood's door in the middle of the blizzard, and it had not come alone.

* * *

There were nine of them crammed into the cottage when Cyrus opened the door, shaking snow off their coats like a flock of wet sheep. Felix counted from the shadows of the kitchen doorway, where Cyrus had put her with one look and one word — *stay* — and where Felix stayed, mostly, peeking.

She knew most of their faces. Marta the baker, whose bread Felix loved. Old Hollis, who walked with a stick and talked to his donkey. The miller's sons, big and awkward. And Tomas, dripping on the mat, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has been elected to say something unpleasant.

"Cyrus." He said her name the way people said it lately — flat, careful, a coin tested between teeth. "Sorry about the hour. Storm being what it is. But we thought you'd want to know straight away."

"Know what?" Cyrus's voice was perfectly pleasant. Felix marveled at it. Under the table, hidden by the fold of her skirt, Cyrus's hand found Felix's shoulder and squeezed once: *quiet, be quiet, be small.*

"There's a patch of spring," Marta said. She couldn't help herself; her eyes were shining. "Up past the well. In all this—" she gestured at the window, where the blizzard howled like something hungry— "there's a circle, maybe ten paces wide, and the snow won't touch it. Grass coming up green in it. Crocuses, Cyrus. *Blooming.* In midwinter, in a whiteout. Hollis says his grandmother told of such things, back before the war, but nobody living has seen—"

"It's witchcraft," said the miller's elder son, and spat into the fire, which everyone ignored.

"It's a miracle," said Marta.

"It's trouble," said Hollis, "is what it is. Nothing blooms in this valley. Nothing *grows* wrong here. That's the whole point of the place, isn't it? That's why the stones are there." His eyes, pale and watery, moved around the cottage — over the maps on the walls, over the rafters, over the shadowed doorway where Felix stood — and Felix shrank back into the dark and made herself a piece of the wall. "Things don't cross the boundary. Everyone knows it. So either the boundary's failing—"

"—or something inside it is leaking," Tomas finished grimly.

The silence that followed had weight. Felix felt it press on her chest, on the sigil, which pulsed once — softly, traitorously, warmly — as if it liked the attention.

Cyrus moved into the silence like a swimmer into deep water.

"You're all welcome to search my home," she said pleasantly. "Every corner. Though I'd point out that if magic were leaking in this valley, the first place it would show is my maps, and you've all seen my maps — badly drawn coastlines of places that don't exist, paid for in bread and firewood. Unless someone believes my imaginary kingdoms are blooming crocuses?" A few uneasy laughs. "As for the circle past the well — I walked that way yesterday. Groundwater runs shallow there, near the old spring channel. Warm water rises, snow melts. My guess is the freeze has driven the warm vein upward. Ask any miner."

Hollis squinted at her. "You're no miner."

"I'm a mapmaker. Water is half my trade." Cyrus shrugged, easy, unhurried, and Felix understood suddenly that this was a performance, a whole invisible architecture of calm built in front of the villagers' eyes, and that beneath it Cyrus was terrified. "I'll walk up with you now if you like. We can stand in your miracle together and catch our deaths."

Murmurs. Shuffling. The miller's son muttered something about women and maps. But the tension in the room loosened, the way ice loosens at noon, and one by one they filed back out into the white roar, calling thanks and warnings and, from Marta, an offer of seed bread left on the step.

The door closed. The wind filled the silence.

Cyrus stood with her forehead nearly touching it, shoulders rising and falling. Then she turned, crossed the room, and knelt in front of Felix, taking both her hands — the cloth-wrapped one and the free one — in her own.

"That was close," she said quietly. "That was very close. Do you understand what happened today?"

Felix nodded. Then, honestly, shook her head.

Cyrus exhaled. "Neither do I. Not entirely. But listen to me." Her grip tightened, gentle and fierce at once. "When it warms — the sigil, when it wakes — you come find me. You don't touch snow, you don't stand near the window, you don't let it *drink*. You come to me. Can you remember that?"

"Yes." And then, because the question had been building in her all day, since the footsteps on the roof, since Hollis's pale eyes crawling over the shadows: "Cyrus. Will they make me go?"

Something moved through Cyrus's face then, quick and painful, like a fish under ice.

"No," she said. "No one is making you go anywhere."

But she said it while looking at the window, at the storm, at the direction of the village and the well and the impossible circle of green, and Felix — who could not lie and was learning, slowly, to read the people who could — saw the truth underneath the words like a stone under shallow water.

Later, after the bread, after the maps, after Cyrus tucked her in and trimmed the lamp, Felix lay listening to the wind and felt the sigil turn over in her sleep-warm palm, contented, drowsy, *loud*.

It was not afraid. That was the thing she understood last of all, drifting down into dreams of heat and height and enormous patient wings. The sigil was not afraid of being found.

Somewhere far south, it seemed to say, in the language before words, *something else is waking too. And it is looking for us.*

Down in the village, in the storm-dark, a rider in grey was asking questions at the inn.
