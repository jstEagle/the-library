## 10. Crossing the Stones

The decision came to Cyrus not as a thunderclap but as a slow thaw, the way ice decides, all at once and without ceremony, to become water.

She had spent three days watching the northern ridge from her attic window, a spyglass balanced on the sill, counting the grey cloaks that moved along it like ink seeping through paper. Seven riders the first day. Nine the second. On the third morning she counted fourteen, and among them a figure who did not ride but walked, robed in white beneath the grey, carrying a staff crowned with a quill of beaten silver. A Scrivener's caduceus. Not a hunting party, then. An embassy.

Or an ultimatum with horses.

"They're waiting," Cyrus said that evening, setting down the spyglass. Her voice came out flatter than she intended, worn smooth by twenty years of talking mostly to herself. "Waiting for us to run out of bread, or nerve, or both."

Felix sat at the kitchen table, cross-legged on the chair because sitting properly was a concept she had rejected early and permanently, pushing a cold potato around her plate in patterns that were — Cyrus noticed, and felt the old cartographer's chill walk up her spine — recognizably sigils. The girl drew them everywhere now: in spilled flour, in breath fogged on glass, in the condensation ring left by a cup. She could not seem to help it. The mark on her palm had spread in the weeks since the snowstorm, thin silver lines climbing her wrist like frost growing in reverse, toward the heart instead of away from it.

Felix looked up. She did not speak — she rarely spoke in any tongue Cyrus knew, though their shared language had grown by dozens of words, built out of meals and mistakes and the particular intimacy of two people learning each other from scratch. She pointed at the ceiling, toward the ridge. Then she pointed at herself. Then, with terrible simplicity, she pointed at Cyrus, and made a small gesture Cyrus had come to understand as *together*, two fists pressed knuckle to knuckle.

"We can't stay," Cyrus said. It was not a question, and Felix did not treat it as one. The girl nodded once, the grave nod of someone much older wearing a child's face, and went back to arranging her potato into what Cyrus sincerely hoped was not a summoning.

That was the thaw. Not courage. Not resolve. Just the quiet arithmetic of a woman who had spent two decades learning that waiting was also a choice, and usually the worst one.

* * *

They prepared for three more days, because preparation was the only prayer Cyrus still believed in.

She packed as a cartographer packs, which is to say obsessively and with an eye toward catastrophe. Ink in wax-sealed vials, wrapped in wool so they wouldn't freeze. Six pens, four quills, a folding rule of brass. Her old field satchel, its leather gone stiff and pale as old bone, its straps re-stitched twice. Maps — not the blooming ones, those stayed hidden under a floorboard, but honest maps of the lowlands drawn from memory and from the letters smuggled to her over the years by one sympathetic supply driver, a man named Odo who asked no questions and was paid in maps of imaginary coastlines for his daughters. Dried meat. Hard cheese. A flint. Two blankets. The small tin of willow-bark and feverfew that constituted her entire pharmacopoeia.

And the hide scrap. That went last, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked against her ribs where she could feel it like a second heartbeat. Felix had offered it to her once, solemnly, palms out — *here, you carry it* — and Cyrus had refused, and the look on the girl's face had taught her something she was still digesting: that Felix did not experience the sigil as a burden. She experienced it as a companion. To take it from her would have been theft of a strange and intimate kind.

On the last evening, Cyrus did something she had not done in twenty years. She stood before the small cracked mirror above her washbasin and looked at herself properly. Grey in the brown hair, more every year. Lines around the eyes like contour marks on a map of difficult country. She looked, she thought, exactly like what she was: a woman who had made a fortress of her guilt and lived in it so long she'd mistaken it for a personality.

"Whatever you were," she told her reflection, "don't be it again."

Her reflection declined to promise anything.

Felix slept that night — actually slept, deeply, curled in the bed with one hand hanging off the edge, the sigil-lines on her wrist catching the banked firelight and holding it, faintly, the way snow holds the last blue of dusk. Cyrus sat up watching her and did not sleep at all. She catalogued instead. It was what she did with fear. *One boundary crossing, north-northwest, past the third stone. Fourteen riders on the ridge, but the ridge watches the road, and we are not taking the road. Moonset at the third hour. Frost tonight; frost means sound carries, so no talking after the tree line.* She filled pages of it in her head until the fear was buried under inventory, and when the moon finally slid behind the western shoulder of the valley, she touched Felix's shoulder and the girl woke instantly, completely, eyes open and clear as if sleep had been a door she'd only been leaning against.

They dressed in silence. They took nothing they could not carry or lose.

At the door, Cyrus paused. She looked back at the cottage — the crooked shelves, the kettle on its hook, the walls she had papered with twenty years of invented worlds because real ones were forbidden to her. It had been a prison. It had also, she realized with a pang that surprised her by its size, been a home. The first one she had ever kept clean.

She closed the door and did not lock it. Let whoever came find a warm hearth and empty shelves. Let them wonder about the woman who had lived there.

* * *

The valley at night was a country Cyrus knew better than her own hands, and she moved through it accordingly, Felix's small hand locked in hers, following sheep paths and drainage cuts that no rider would bother with. The frost lay thick and glittering, and their breath plumed behind them like ghosts trying to keep up.

Felix, to her credit, understood the silence absolutely. She moved well — better than Cyrus expected, light-footed, instinctively placing her weight where the ground would not betray her. Once, when a fox broke cover ahead and went bounding through the bracken, Felix froze without being told, and Cyrus felt something in her chest that was dangerously close to pride. *Stop that,* she told herself sternly. *You are not keeping her. You are delivering her somewhere safer than here, and then you are going to figure out what in God's name comes next.*

The boundary stones began at the valley's northern throat, where the hills pinched together around a stream too shallow to name. There were nine of them visible, ancient menhirs taller than a man, leaning together like conspirators, furred with moss that grew only on their southern faces. In daylight they looked like nothing — farm walls, waymarkers, the sort of stones every countryside accumulates. But at night, up close, they hummed. Not audibly. Cyrus felt it through her boots, a vibration below hearing, the sensation of standing near a struck bell that has fallen silent but not yet finished.

Twenty years she had lived inside their circle. Twenty years since the Order's wardens had walked her, wrists bound in quill-cord, through the gap between the fourth and fifth stones, and the world had gone *quiet* — magic cut off like a limb set down on the far side of a wall. She remembered screaming. She remembered that what frightened her most was not the pain but the silence afterward, the sudden absence of the low constant music she had heard all her life without knowing it was there, the way you never hear your own heartbeat until it stutters.

Now she stood before the gap again, and this time she was walking through on her own feet, holding a child's hand, and the humming rose through her boots like water rising in a lock.

Felix tugged her hand. The girl was staring at the stones with an expression Cyrus had never seen on her — not fear, not wonder, but *recognition*. She pulled free of Cyrus's grip, walked to the fourth stone, and laid her marked palm flat against the moss.

The stone lit.

Not blazed — *lit*, softly, from within, veins of pale gold waking along the menhir's grain like dawn coming up through fog. The hum deepened into something almost melodic. And across the surface of the stone, beneath the moss, lines appeared — carved lines, ancient, worn nearly smooth, and Cyrus's cartographer's eye read them in a single horrified heartbeat.

A map. The stone was carved with a map. And the map showed the valley, and the river, and the ridge — and beyond the ridge, coastlines she knew intimately, coastlines she had redrawn in her own hand on parchment while she slept, coastlines that had been blooming across her maps for weeks as if the world itself were remembering where it kept its edges.

"Oh," Cyrus said aloud, very quietly, into the frozen dark. "Oh, you clever old thing."

The boundary wasn't a cage. It was a *seal*. And seals, her new understanding whispered, are drawn by someone, for a purpose, and can be broken by the right key. Felix's hand on the stone was a key turning. The girl looked back at Cyrus, and her expression said plainly: *it knows me. I know it. Come.*

Cyrus came.

* * *

Crossing the line between the fourth and fifth stones took four steps. It took the rest of her life.

The first step was nothing. The second step, the hum climbed into her teeth. On the third step the world *tore* — there was no other word for it — a seam of silence splitting down the middle of the night, and Cyrus stepped through it, and magic hit her like a wave hitting a swimmer who had forgotten the sea.

It flooded back into her the way blood floods into a limb that has slept too long — that first horrible rushing *prickle*, pins and needles become knives become fire, and she would have fallen if Felix hadn't caught her, this child of nine catching a grown woman's weight without apparent effort, steadying her with both hands as Cyrus gasped and staggered and heard herself make a sound she did not recognize as her own voice.

Because it wasn't just sensation. It was *everything*. Every sense arriving at once, doubled, trebled. She could smell the frost, and under the frost the deep mineral breath of the mountains, and under that something older, a scent like hot iron and rain on stone that she knew, with sick certainty, was dragon-country breathing far to the south. She could hear the stream, and under the stream the groundwater moving through rock strata, and under *that* the slow conversation of roots beneath the snow. Light behaved differently. The darkness was not dark; it was layered, readable, a document she could parse. She could see the wind.

And underneath it all, faint as a pulse in a wrist, she could feel the low constant music again. The song she had been exiled from. It had never stopped playing. It had simply stopped playing *for her* — and now, unbidden, unwelcome, it gathered her back in like a current gathering a leaf.

It was ecstasy. That was the corrupting truth of it, the thing she had forgotten and the Order had counted on: exile hurts, but return *ecstasies*. For one long, shameless moment Cyrus Blackwood stood in the frost outside her prison and let the power pour into her and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any rider ever could: *I could take it all back. Everything they took. I could go to Vellum Hall and peel it apart like fruit.*

She saw it, is what she saw. Saw it whole and tempting. Herself restored, robes and rank and the silver quill, the archive bowing open before her, the Grand Scrivener's face when he realized the asset he'd discarded had come home as a creditor. She saw herself rewriting the ledger of her own life, striking out the exile, striking out the massacre, striking out the twenty gray years, and it would be *easy*, it would be as easy as the first lie she'd told the riders on the ridge, and that ease was precisely how she knew it was poison.

The vision lasted perhaps three heartbeats. It cost her considerably more than that to put it down.

"Cyrus." Not spoken — *signed*, Felix's small hands shaping the word they'd built together, *Cyrus, look at me*. And when Cyrus looked, the girl held up her own palm, the sigil glowing faintly silver, and pressed it over Cyrus's heart, and through the contact came not power but something else: a feeling like cold clean water. Like being *witnessed*. The flood receded from a roar to a river to a stream, and Cyrus found herself kneeling in the frost, breathing, alive, her ears ringing with the ghost of a temptation she would not be telling anyone about.

"Thank you," she rasped.

Felix shrugged, in the universal language of children who have done something enormous and wish it noted briefly and never mentioned again.

* * *

They walked south through the rest of the night, because north was the ridge and the riders, and because the hide scrap — Cyrus checked it twice — had begun, very faintly, to glow. South, it seemed to say. South, toward the Emberreach, toward the legend of the last dragon. The scrap tugged at its oilcloth wrapping like a compass needle finding north, except that it pointed south, and Cyrus had long ago learned to trust instruments over her own preferences.

Dawn caught them in the foothills, in a hollow of black pines with the valley already falling away behind them, a cup of shadow and chimney smoke shrinking in the distance. Cyrus allowed them a fire, small and shielded, and hard cheese, and watched Felix fall asleep mid-chew with the total commitment of the young.

Then Cyrus sat alone with the map she had unrolled across her knees — the lowlands, Odo's smuggled letters rendered into lines — and did what she always did with unbearable things: she studied them.

Magic was in her still. Not the flood of the crossing, but a residue, a warmth in the fingertips, and when she held her pen over the parchment her hand *wanted* things. Wanted to embellish. Wanted to reach. She watched, fascinated and appalled, as the nib hovered and the lines beneath it seemed to lean toward her like plants toward sun, as if the map were asking to be more than a record, as if it wanted to become a *command*.

*This is how it starts,* she thought. *This is exactly how it started, the first time. You draw a coastline and think: wouldn't it be better if I decided where the harbors go?*

She capped the ink. She wrapped the pen. She sat with her arms around her knees and watched the sky go from ash to rose over the war-scarred lowlands she was about to enter — the burned villages, the dragon-scarred fields, the whole ruined ledger of the war she had helped write — and she made herself a promise with all the gravity of a woman signing her name.

*I will carry it,* she promised the dawn, the frost, the sleeping child, the dead. *Whatever it costs. Whatever it offers. I will carry it, and I will not drink.*

Behind her, in the pines, a branch snapped — deliberately, heavily, the snap of something that wanted to be heard.

Cyrus was on her feet with a pen-knife in her hand before her mind caught up with her body, and Felix woke without a sound, sigil flaring silver, both of them staring into the trees at the place where the shadows had recently acquired a shape, and the shape had recently acquired eyes.

The eyes blinked. Slowly. Unhurried. Golden, slit-pupiled, and very large.

And from the treeline, in a voice like dry leaves and distant thunder, in the old tongue that predated kingdoms — the tongue Cyrus half-understood, the tongue Felix spoke — came a single word.

"*Follow.*"
