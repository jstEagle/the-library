## 12. An Honest Thief

The river town was called Brackwater, and it smelled of fish and tar and bread.

Felix had never smelled so many things at once. In the valley there had been woodsmoke and snow and Cyrus's ink, and that was all. Here the smells came in crowds, pushing against each other, and the sounds did too — wheels on stone, a man shouting about eels, a dog barking at nothing, a bell somewhere ringing the wrong hour. Felix held Cyrus's hand tighter than she needed to. Cyrus pretended not to notice, which was one of the things Felix loved best about her.

"Stay close," Cyrus said. "Towns are like rivers. They look slow from the bank."

Felix nodded, though she didn't understand. Rivers looked fast to her. Everything here looked fast.

They had been walking for nine days since the stones. Nine days of ash-fields where trees stood up black with no leaves on them, of villages where people stared at Felix's wrapped hand and whispered behind their doors. In one village a woman had pressed a dried flower into Felix's good hand and wept without saying why. In another, a man had spat at their feet. Felix still didn't know which one had been right.

But Brackwater was not afraid of anything. Brackwater was too busy.

Cyrus found them a room above a chandler's shop, paid for with coins counted out one by one, her mouth thin. "We rest two days," she said. "I need paper. I need to think. You—" She stopped, looking down at Felix, and something in her face went soft and worried at the same time. "You stay where I can see you."

Felix nodded again. It was easier than talking. Talking made her tired, because every word came out in the old tongue first, the fire-words, and then she had to drag it through her mouth and turn it into valley-speech, and sometimes the meaning got bent in the middle like a nail hammered crooked. *Hungry* came out as *hollow*. *Friend* came out as *warm-near*. People laughed when she talked. Or they flinched. There didn't seem to be much between.

* * *

It was Wren who taught her that there could be something between.

Wren appeared on the second morning, sitting backwards on the chandler's fence like she'd grown there, a thin girl with brown hair chopped short by somebody who hadn't been careful and eyes the color of the river after rain. She was maybe twelve. She was eating an apple.

"You're the ash-walkers," Wren said. "Everyone's saying it. A tall woman and a little mute girl come out of the dead lands." She took a bite. "You're not mute, though. I watched you talk to the baker. Your words are all wrong but you do talk."

Felix stared at her.

Wren stared back, chewing, entirely unbothered. Then she held out the apple. Half-eaten, offered anyway.

Felix took it. That was how it started, later — with an apple half-eaten, given without ceremony, which Felix would think about for a long time afterward and understand only a little.

* * *

Wren showed her the town.

That was what Wren said, anyway — *showing you the town* — though what she mostly showed Felix was ways of moving through it. How to walk so that carts missed you. Which gutters were safe to step in and which were lies. How to stand under the awning of the fishmonger's stall during a sudden rain without being chased off, because the fishmonger owed Wren's mother a debt nobody talked about. How to look at a person just long enough to see if they were kind.

"Why are you showing me?" Felix asked, on the second day, in her crooked words. It was the longest sentence she'd managed in front of anyone but Cyrus.

Wren shrugged, walking backwards along the quay rail with her arms out like a tightrope walker. "Because you're new. New people are interesting. Also you have a funny hand-wrap and everyone's scared of you and I don't get scared, so." She wobbled, caught herself, grinned. "Also you're going to fall in the river someday and someone should be there to laugh."

Felix felt her own mouth do something strange. It took her a moment to know what it was. She was smiling.

She hadn't smiled at anyone but Cyrus since the mountain. Since the fire and the falling shape and the running, running, running. The memory lived under her ribs like a swallowed coal, always warm, never cool, and she had learned not to touch it. But here on the sunny quay with the gulls screaming and Wren wobbling on the rail, the coal felt smaller. Not gone. Smaller.

That should have been the first warning. Things that get smaller when you stop watching them are not gone. Cyrus would say that later, in her flat cartographer's voice, drawing lines on paper: *a map is only true until the world moves.* Felix knew it now in her body instead.

* * *

On the third evening they sat on the roof of the boathouse with their legs hanging over the edge, sharing a bag of roasted chestnuts Wren had acquired in a way she declined to explain. The sun was going down orange over the water and the whole river turned to hammered copper.

"What's under the wrap?" Wren asked suddenly.

Felix went still.

"Not the hand. I mean what's under it. On it." Wren wasn't looking at her; she was looking at the water, being casual the way cats are casual before they pounce. "My mum says there's a mark on some children, born in the burning years. Says it's luck. Old Marta down the lane says it's a curse. The Order says it's contraband." She turned her head. "Which is it?"

Felix pulled the wrap tighter. Under the cloth, the sigil lay against her palm like a sleeping thing — it slept more now, in towns, away from high places, away from snow. But it was always there. Warm-cold. A coin of ice left in the sun.

"It's mine," Felix said.

Wren considered this. "Fair enough." And she handed over the last chestnut, the biggest one, the one Felix had been pretending not to want.

That was the thing about Wren that Felix couldn't stop thinking about, even later, even after. Wren took things — apples, chestnuts, a copper hairpin off a lady's hat right in front of Felix once, smooth as breathing — but she also gave things, and the giving seemed to cost her nothing, seemed to be the same motion as the taking, one gesture with two faces. Felix had never met anyone like that. Cyrus gave everything and took almost nothing. The villagers either gave or took, never both. Wren was a door that swung both ways.

Felix liked her. That was the whole of it, simple as a stone in the pocket. Felix liked her, and trusted her the way you trust weather that has been fine for three days.

* * *

The last morning, Cyrus went to buy paper.

She was gone longer than she meant to be — Felix learned this afterward, piecing it together — because the stationer argued with her about the price of vellum and then a clerk from the tollhouse recognized her description and asked questions, and Cyrus, who could lie to riders of the Order without blinking, could not lie quickly to a bored clerk about why a cartographer needed forty sheets of good rag paper. So she was late. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty.

Twenty minutes is a very long time to a child on a dock with a friend.

They were playing a game Wren had invented called *whose pockets*, which was exactly what it sounded like: each tried to slip a small object into the other's pocket without being felt doing it. Wren was very good at it. Felix was terrible, all elbows and honesty, and lost every round, and laughed every time, because losing to Wren didn't feel like losing. It felt like being included in something.

"I win again," Wren said, breathless, holding up the button she'd planted in Felix's coat. "You're the worst thief in Brackwater. You'd starve."

"I wouldn't steal," Felix said. "Stealing is—"

She didn't finish, because the word she wanted was *wrong*, and the old tongue rose first and gave her *unbound*, and the valley-word got stuck behind it, and while her mouth fumbled, Wren hugged her goodbye — quick, fierce, the way Wren did everything — and stepped back, and said she had chores, and went off along the quay with her hands in her pockets.

Felix stood there smiling at nothing for a moment. Then she reached into her own pocket for the hide scrap — she checked it a hundred times a day, the way other children sucked their thumbs — and her fingers found only lining.

She checked the other pocket. Then both again. Then she turned the coat upside down and shook it over the boards of the dock, and a button fell out, and nothing else.

The world went very quiet, the way it had gone quiet on the mountain before the falling shape. Sound came back in pieces: gulls. Water. Her own heart, loud and stupid.

*Wren.*

Felix ran. She ran the way she had run from the fire, without thought, her boots hammering the boards, past the fish stalls and the rope-walks, scanning every brown-haired head. The quay was long and crowded and Wren was small and quick and knew every gutter and awning and alley in this town, and Felix knew none of them, and it did not matter, because Felix did not run with her head. She ran with her hand.

Under the empty wrap, the sigil woke.

It had never done this before — reached, leaned, *pulled* — and it hurt, a cold wire drawn through her palm and up her arm and into her chest, and it pointed. Not north or south. It pointed the way a compass points, except the compass was her own blood, and the direction was *stolen*, and she followed it blind, around the chandler's, up the fish-ladder steps, into the tangle of lanes behind the salt sheds where tourists never went and honest people had no reason to.

She found Wren in a doorway with two older kids, bigger kids, a boy with a broken tooth and a girl with a shaved scalp, and Wren was holding out the hide scrap and the big boy was holding out three silver coins, and everyone was talking fast and low, and nobody had seen Felix yet.

"—don't know what it is, some heathen scribble, but the Quill men at the chapterhouse pay for old script, any old script, they've got a list—"

Felix didn't remember crossing the space between them. She remembered the sound she made. It wasn't a word in any language. It was the sound the sigil made when it burned, translated through a child's throat, and it stopped all three of them like a hand on the chest.

The scrap was in the big boy's fist. Felix held out her hand. Her unwrapped hand.

For one heartbeat the sigil lay bare in the grey daylight, and it was not sleeping anymore. The lines of it moved, slowly, the way smoke moves, and the air around her palm shimmered like the air over a forge, and frost crept up the doorframe beside her in the shape of feathers, and the big boy's face went the color of porridge, and he dropped the scrap into her hand the way you drop something that bit you, and then all three of them were running, even Wren, especially Wren, Wren running fastest of all and not looking back.

Felix stood alone in the alley with the hide scrap pressed against her bare palm.

It should have been a triumph. It was not. The sigil settled against her skin, warm-cold, satisfied, and Felix looked down at it and understood, in the wordless way she understood fire and falling, that it had known. It had felt Wren take it hours ago, felt the scrap leave her pocket, and it had waited, patient as winter, because it did not care about Wren. It cared about itself. It would let Felix be robbed, lied to, left — it would let anything happen to her — so long as it came home.

And Felix had liked her. That was the part that broke. Not the coins, not the chapterhouse, not even the running-away. The apple, half-eaten, offered anyway. The last chestnut, the biggest one. *New people are interesting.* Felix sat down on the wet cobbles of the alley and cried the way she hadn't let herself cry since the mountain, great ugly silent sobs, because she had learned already that loud crying brought people, and the wrong people were worse than no people.

Loyalty must sometimes be chosen, not given — she was too young to know the words for the lesson, but the lesson went in deep, deeper than the sigil, deeper than the burn: *somebody can give you an apple and still sell your name. What holds is what stays chosen. Choose slowly. Choose with your eyes open.*

When Cyrus found her — twenty minutes late, forty sheets of paper under her arm, terror written all over her face — Felix was still sitting in the alley, and the wrap was back over her hand, and she told her everything in crooked, breaking words: *Wren. Took. Sold. Quill. List.*

Cyrus went very still. Felix watched the color leave her face and understood, without knowing how, that the words *the Quill pays for old script* meant something enormous and terrible to her, something with maps in it, something with her own name in it.

"Felix," Cyrus said quietly. "Did she say where? Did she say *which* chapterhouse?"

Felix wiped her nose. Down at the end of the alley, past the salt sheds, she could see the grey slate roof of a building she'd noticed before without noticing — a building with a quill cut into its doorstone, and guards at the gate who stood too straight, and a wagon being unloaded with crates marked in old letters that made the sigil itch against her palm even from here.

Felix pointed.

Cyrus looked where she pointed, and her face did something Felix had never seen it do. It was not fear. It was older than fear, and colder, and it looked almost like coming home.

"Get your coat," Cyrus said.
