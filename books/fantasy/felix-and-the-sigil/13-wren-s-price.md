## 13. Wren's Price

The river town of Marrowford announced itself by smell before sight — tar and fish-rot and the sweet char of the tanneries — and then by its crooked skyline, chimneys leaning like old men in conversation. Cyrus stood at the crest of the lowland road with Felix beside her and studied the sprawl of slate roofs spilling down toward the brown water, and she felt the familiar tightening behind her ribs that meant the Order was near.

She had learned to smell them over twenty years of exile: not a scent at all, really, but a pressure, a flattening of the air, as though somewhere nearby a great hand pressed down on everything living and held it still. The boundary stones had kept that feeling out of the valley for two decades. Out here, it seeped back into her like damp into wool.

"You feel it too," she said.

Felix didn't answer — Felix never answered in words Cyrus could parse — but the girl's hand found hers, small and cold, and squeezed once. That was answer enough. The sigil had been restless all morning, Cyrus could see it in the way Felix kept flexing her left palm, opening and closing the fist as though trying to shake water from it. Since Wren had lifted the hide scrap three days ago in the market at Thistlebridge, the mark on Felix's skin had dimmed to an ember-gray, and the girl had grown quieter even than silence itself, if such a thing were possible. She grieved. Cyrus recognized the shape of it. She had worn that shape herself for twenty years.

"We'll get it back," Cyrus said. She knelt so their eyes were level. "And when we do, I'm going to teach you a word. It's called *retribution*, and it looks a great deal like paperwork."

Felix's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. But close enough to walk on.

* * *

Marrowford's chapterhouse did not announce itself the way the great halls of the capital did — no white façade, no carved quill above the door, no lines of penitents waiting to confess their unlicensed thoughts. Here the Order wore plain clothes. The building they'd chosen was a counting-house on Candlemaker's Row, three stories of grey brick with shuttered windows and a sign reading *Harrow & Sons, Importers*. Only two things betrayed it: the windows were shuttered on a bright afternoon, and the men who came and went through its door walked with the particular gait of people who had been taught never to hurry and never to dawdle.

Cyrus watched it for six hours from the loft of a dyer's shed across the lane, sharing stale bread with Felix and marking every arrival in the margins of a map of the town she'd drawn from memory of the rooftops. Eleven men. Two women. One of the women wore grey beneath her merchant's coat — the riding habit of the Quill's field scriveners, cut for the saddle. Riders. The same order of riders who had come to the northern ridge above the valley, whose leader had looked at Cyrus's cottage with eyes like a man reading a ledger.

"They have the scrap," she murmured. "They wouldn't keep this many hands on a counting-house otherwise."

Felix tapped the map. Her finger stopped on the third floor, where one window sat open despite the cold, venting a thin ribbon of smoke that smelled, even at this distance, faintly of vellum and hot wax. Then she drew a circle around it in the air, over and over, insistent.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "I see it. They're studying it." She chewed the inside of her cheek. Studying meant scholars, and scholars meant records, and records meant — her stomach turned over slowly, the way it always did when ambition dressed itself in duty's clothing — records meant the Order's own hand, written in its own ink, preserved by its own vanity. The Order never destroyed its correspondence. That was its pride and its disease both: a bureaucracy that believed history would vindicate it, and so kept every receipt.

"I'm going in," she said.

Felix grabbed her sleeve. The girl's face had gone fierce, the sigil flickering pale green at her wrist like a warning lamp.

"Not alone," Cyrus agreed. "You'll be my eyes outside. If anyone who isn't me comes out that door, you run to the dyer's loft and you wait. Can you do that?"

Felix stared at her for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she raised her small hand and pressed it flat against Cyrus's cheek — the gesture she made sometimes instead of speech, the one that said *I hear you* in whatever language lived between them — and nodded once.

* * *

The credentials were still good. That was the bitter joke of it.

Twenty years of exile, and the Order's seals did not rot; they only waited. In a locked box beneath the floorboards of her valley cottage — carried there the day she crossed the boundary stones, because even the banished cannot bring themselves to burn their own name — Cyrus kept her journeywoman's warrant of the Cartographic Corps, countersigned by the Office of Scriveners General, bearing the signature of a man named Aldous Vane who had been Third Undersecretary then and was, if the riders' insignia meant anything, Grand Scrivener now. The wax had gone amber with age. The ribbon had faded from scarlet to the color of dried blood. But the cipher-knots woven into the border were current-year knots — she had checked against the riders' satchels on the ridge — because the Order changed its ciphers rarely and arrogantly, believing no counterfeiter alive could match them.

No counterfeiter needed. Only a cartographer who had once *made* them.

She went in at the changing of the watch, in the grey hour when the tannery bells rang and the street emptied, wearing a borrowed widow's cloak over her traveling dress and the warrant in an inner pocket, folded to show only the seal. The door of Harrow & Sons was answered by a young man with ink-stained fingers and the flat, assessing stare of a clerk deciding what category to put you in.

"Business?" he asked.

"Cartographic survey," Cyrus said. "Corps business. I'm told you have materials requiring authentication." She let the warrant peek from her pocket, no more than a corner of ribbon and amber wax.

Something shifted behind the clerk's eyes — not suspicion; deference, quick and reflexive, the way a dog's ears move at a whistle. Twenty years ago that reflex had been aimed at her daily, and she had drunk it like wine without ever tasting how intoxicating it was. Now it tasted of ash.

"Third floor," he said, standing aside. "Brother Cassian has the materials. You're expected?"

"I'm expected," said Cyrus Blackwood, liar, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

The archive occupied the whole of the third floor, and it smelled like her childhood: vellum, hot sealing-wax, lamp-oil, and the faint mineral tang of iron-gall ink. Racks of pigeonholes lined the walls, each labeled in the Order's precise hand. Long tables ran down the center under green-shaded lamps, and at the farthest table, alone, hunched over a scrap of hide pinned flat beneath glass weights, sat a thin man in Quill grey with spectacles pushed up into his thinning hair.

He did not look up. "If you're from the Corridor office, the transmittal's not ready. The resonance mapping is incomplete. Tell Vane the artifact resists standard notation and I will not falsify my findings to suit his timetable."

"Brother Cassian," Cyrus said mildly, "has anyone ever told you that you complain exactly the way a man complains when he's frightened of being right?"

That brought his head up. He blinked at the widow's cloak, at her face, at the warrant she now laid openly on his table beside the glass weights. She watched him read the seal, the countersignature, the cipher-knots, and watched the arithmetic finish behind his eyes.

"Journeyman Blackwood," he said slowly. "The exile."

"The exile," she agreed. "Whose roost-maps your Order used to burn the sky empty. Yes. That one."

His throat moved. He glanced toward the stairs. "There are eleven—"

"Nine," said Cyrus. "Two are drinking at the Anchor and Chain; I counted them an hour ago, and they count their cups more carefully than they count their watches. And the two on the door rotate at the bell, which gives us perhaps four minutes before the new pair wonders why the clerk is chatting with a widow." She pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, unhurried, as though she owned the table and the lamps and the twenty years between. "You said the artifact resists standard notation. Show me."

For a moment she thought he would call the guards, and she measured the distance to the window, the drop to the tannery roof below, the weight of the letter-opener on his blotter. But curiosity is the Order's true religion, and Cassian was a priest of long standing. His hand moved — not to the bell-cord, but to the glass weights — and he lifted them off the hide scrap one by one.

It lay there under the lamp, small and cracked and brown, and even drained of its fire it hurt to look at. The sigil's lines had gone dark, but the geometry remained: interlocking spirals wound around a central eye-shape, a pattern that no human hand had drawn, because human hands could not draw it. Cyrus knew that now. Every attempt to copy it produced something subtly wrong, the way a map of a coastline is wrong — faithful to every measurement and false to the living thing itself.

"It doesn't take ink," Cassian said, and his voice had dropped to the register scholars use in archives, the hush of the confessional. "We've tried. Tracing compounds, sympathetic dyes, blood — don't look at me like that, Journeyman, we've done worse. The lines won't hold pigment. As though the sigil decides what may be written about it." He laughed, short and unhappy. "Vane thinks it's a weapon. A key to dragon-fire, some means of reigniting the breeding stocks. He wants it weaponized before the Emberreach expedition—"

"The Emberreach expedition," Cyrus repeated.

Cassian stopped. She watched him hear his own sentence, watch him understand what he'd given away, and she leaned forward into the lamplight and gave him her oldest professional smile — the one she'd used on generals and guildmasters, the one that said *we are colleagues, and colleagues share*.

"Brother Cassian," she said softly, "you and I are going to trade. You're going to tell me everything in this room, and in exchange, I am not going to tell you what happens to chapterhouses that stand between me and that child. It's a generous rate. I suggest you take it."

* * *

He took it. Of course he took it. Men like Cassian always took it, because men like Cassian had spent their lives believing that knowledge was neutral, that filing a thing was different from doing a thing, and when the filing finally caught up with the doing they had no practice at all in refusing.

What he showed her filled two hours and turned her blood to ice-water and then to something hotter.

The resonance studies first: measurements of the sigil's emanations taken with instruments she half-remembered from her training, improved past recognition. Charts showing the sigil's output rising in proximity to certain bloodlines — and Cyrus felt the floor tilt gently under her when she understood that the bloodlines marked on Cassian's chart included her own surname, entered in a spidery hand, dated *before her birth*.

Then the deeper files, the ones Cassian opened with a key from around his neck, hands shaking. The war correspondence. The roost surveys — her own maps, her own linework, annotated in margins by men whose names she still knew. And threaded through them, dated decades before the massacre, memoranda in the Grand Scrivener's ascending ranks of office:

*Re: Sentience determination, drake clutches, western range. Field observers report tool-use, mourning behavior, and structured vocal communication consistent with language. Recommend reclassification of target designation from vermin to belligerent.*

And beneath it, in a different hand, the reply that had ended the argument:

*Classification noted and set aside. Belligerents negotiate; vermin may be culled without treaty obligation. The covenant requires the dragons be legally voiceless. Proceed per schedule.*

The covenant requires the dragons be legally voiceless.

Cyrus read it four times. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the tannery bells rang the hour, and the watch changed, and the world went on conducting its ordinary business above the buried fact that the war — the war that had emptied the sky, the war that had killed Vessith's kin and orphaned a child and burned a cartographer's name off the rolls of the living — had not been a tragedy of misunderstanding or an escalation of fear. It had been a clerical decision. Someone had *known*. Had known the dragons spoke, mourned, reasoned, named their dead. Had known, and had filed the knowing away in a pigeonhole, because a negotiated peace would have constrained the Order's power, and a voiceless enemy could be exterminated cleanly, without treaty, without guilt, without record.

Except there was a record. There was always a record. The Order's pride and its disease.

Her hands were steady as she copied. That surprised her. She had expected rage to shake her, and rage was there — a vast, silent pressure behind her sternum, like the hand she'd imagined pressing down on Marrowford — but the steadiness rode on top of it, cartographer's steadiness, surveyor's discipline. She copied the memoranda word for word. She copied dates, names, countersignatures. She copied the marginal note in Vane's own youthful hand beside the sentience finding: *Unfortunate. Unavoidable. Deniable.* Three words, and each one a confession.

When she finished, she looked up at Cassian, who had watched her copy in silence like a man watching his own funeral procession go by.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why any of this? You're exiled. You're free of us."

Cyrus folded the pages into her cloak, next to the warrant that had opened the door.

"Because," she said, "your Order believes the law is whatever it writes down. Very well. I am going to write it down differently — and I am going to use your own archive to do it. Every pigeonhole, every chapterhouse, every clerk who ever filed a truth and called it nothing. Your network reaches into every city in the nine provinces, Brother Cassian. By spring, every one of those cities is going to know what you knew." She rose, and settled the widow's cloak, and allowed herself, at last, the smile she'd been saving. "You built the perfect machine for spreading the truth. You just never imagined anyone would load it with this."

At the door she paused. Behind her, Cassian sat motionless among his lamps, a thin man in grey with a lifetime of filed atrocities stacked in neat rows around him, and she thought — not kindly, but precisely — that he would either report her within the hour or spend the rest of his life listening for her footsteps on the stairs. Perhaps both. The Order made people like that.

"One more thing," she said. "The child. The vessel. When Vane finds her — and he will find her, because you'll tell him, or someone will — what are his orders?"

Cassian's voice came out very small. "Containment. The sigil must be joined to a willing bearer to bind a new covenant — or forced onto an unwilling one to break the old seal entirely. Vane prefers the second. A broken seal can be... renegotiated." He swallowed. "He needs the child alive, Journeyman. That's the only mercy in the orders. Alive."

Cyrus stood in the doorway a moment longer, looking at the scrap of hide under its glass weights, dark and quiet as a closed eye.

Then she went down the stairs, out past the startled clerk, into the street where the tannery stink hung thick and Felix waited in the shadows of the dyer's shed with her fist clenched around empty air where a scrap of hide used to be — and Cyrus took the girl's cold hand in hers and began, silently, methodically, to plan a theft of her own.

Not the scrap. She would take that too, before the night was out, if the gods were kind and the locks were honest.

But first, and larger, and forever after: the truth. All of it. Filed, indexed, cross-referenced, and delivered — to every door in the nine provinces — by morning post.

The Order had taught her everything she knew about paperwork.

It was time it learned what she knew about revenge.
