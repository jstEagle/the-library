## 15. What Power Offers

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first insult, and Cyrus understood it as such within three heartbeats of waking. Her wrists lay free on the armrests of a chair too fine for a prisoner, upholstered in leather the color of dried blood, and her ankles were likewise unencumbered, and when she tested the truth of this — flexing her fingers, shifting one boot against the floor — nothing stopped her. Nothing needed to. The room did what ropes could not.

She was somewhere beneath Vellum Hall. She knew it the way she knew her own handwriting: by the walls. They were lined with shelving, floor to vaulted ceiling, and every shelf was crammed with ledgers, folios, scroll-cases of lacquered cedar, bundles of correspondence tied in the Order's grey ribbon. A library, then — but not a library for reading. There were no tables, no lamps positioned for study, no ladders. It was a library for *keeping*, the way a man keeps trophies or teeth. The air smelled of vellum and lamp-oil and, faintly, underneath it all, of cold iron.

And the wards. She could feel them now that she was inside them, now that magic had been poured back into her like wine into a cracked cup. They pressed against her skin from all sides, a lattice of written law so dense it hummed, and every thread of it terminated in the chair where she sat. She was not in a room. She was in a sentence, and she was its subject.

*You let yourself be taken,* she reminded herself, and it was even true — mostly. She had seen the ambush forming in the market square at Duskmere, had seen the grey cloaks folding inward like a hand closing, and she had chosen to walk into the palm rather than run, because running would have led them to Felix, and Felix had been fifty yards away buying apples with Wren's stolen coin. Better my name than her trail. That was the arithmetic she had done in the space of a breath, standing among cabbages and fish-sellers while six Scriveners closed around her with quiet, apologetic efficiency.

But arithmetic done in fear is still fear, and now, sitting in the humming dark with her hands free and her freedom gone, Cyrus allowed herself to feel the full weight of what she had traded. Not her liberty — she had lived twenty years without that and called it peace. Her *purpose*. Somewhere beyond these walls, a child with a burning hand was waiting for a woman who had promised — out loud, kneeling in the ash of a burned waystation, looking Felix in the eye — *I will come back.*

The door opened without sound.

He came in alone, which surprised her. She had expected retinue, ceremony, the Grand Scrivener's power displayed in bodies. Instead: one man, grey-robed, unremarkable, carrying a single candle and a single ledger. He set the candle on the shelf beside the chair, adjusted it twice until the light fell exactly as he wanted it, and then sat down across from her in an identical chair, arranging his robes with the fussy precision of a man who had never once in his life been interrupted.

"Cyrus Blackwood," he said. "Cartographer. Exile. Asset."

"The last one's new," Cyrus said. "I've heard the others before."

"Have you?" He smiled without warmth, the way a ledger balances without joy. "You've heard *traitor*. Traitor is what we told the world. Asset is what we wrote in our own books. You've read our own books now, I'm told." His eyes went to her hands, to the ink stains twenty years deep in the creases of her knuckles. "Yes. We know about the chapterhouse archive. We know what you copied. I confess I was impressed. Your cipher-work has improved since Vellum Hall last had the pleasure of your services."

"Then you know why I won't be staying."

"I know why you *think* you won't be staying." He folded his hands. "May I show you something? You may say no. You may, in fact, attempt to leave. I'd rather you didn't — the wards are old and they were written by better Scriveners than either of us, and they do not distinguish between escape and assault — but you may certainly try. I mention it only because I want our understanding to be honest. Honesty is the whole of what I have to offer you tonight, Cyrus Blackwood. I wonder if you'll recognize it. It's been so long since anyone offered it to you."

Everything in her wanted to spit at him. Everything in her also wanted, terribly, shamefully, to hear what came next. She had spent two decades telling herself the Order was a machine of lies, and here was its engine sitting across from her promising truth, and the worst part — the part she would carry afterward like a stone — was that some starved corner of her leaned toward him the way a frost-burned hand leans toward fire.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

He opened the ledger on his knees and turned pages with a scholar's tenderness. "Do you remember the war's last year? Of course you do. You made it possible, or so the histories say — the histories we permitted. Here." He rotated the book toward her.

It was a map. Her map. The roost-chart she had drawn at thirty-one years old, every hidden eyrie of the northern dragons rendered in her own confident hand, the work she had believed would end the war by making the dragons findable and therefore negotiable. She had not seen it in twenty years. Seeing it now was like meeting a younger self in the street — the same face, wrong eyes.

"You believed it would force a peace," the Grand Scrivener said. "You said as much, in your petition. *A war cannot be ended against an enemy no one can find.* Those were your words. Beautiful words. And here—" he turned the page "—is what we did with them."

Not orders. Not strike-plans. Correspondence. Months of it. And threaded through it, in the margins, in the Grand Scrivener's predecessor's hand, annotations that made Cyrus's stomach turn slowly over: discussions of dragon-fire yields, of hide-values, of how many eggs a roost held and what a hatchling's blood fetched per ounce. The Order had known. Had known the dragons were people — mothers, singers, keepers of law older than any kingdom's — and had priced them anyway.

"You're showing me this because you want me to hate you," Cyrus said quietly. "Why?"

"Because hatred is honest, and I need you honest for what follows." He closed the ledger. "The men who did this are dead, most of them. The war emptied us too. What remains of the Order is smaller, poorer, and more frightened than you imagine — and more useful than you know. Because here is the truth of the present, cartographer: the covenant is broken, the dragons are gone, and the sigil that child carries is the last living seal of the old law. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means you want her."

"It means the world wants her, and does not know it yet." He leaned forward, and the candlelight moved on his face and made it briefly, horribly kind. "Magic is dying, Cyrus. You've felt it — you lived outside it for twenty years, you felt it come back like blood into a numb limb. But you felt it *thin*, didn't you? Thinner than it was. Dragon-fire sustained the old covenant; the dragons' consent is what kept wild magic lawful, bound, survivable. With the dragons gone, the binding decays. Another decade, perhaps two, and magic goes feral everywhere at once. Plague-winds. Rivers that forget their courses. Every ward in every city failing on the same night. Unless the covenant is reforged — willingly, justly, with a bearer who consents. Or unless—"

"Unless the Order takes the sigil and binds it by force."

"And reignites a war against a world that no longer has dragons to fight for it. Yes. That is the other road, and there are men in Vellum Hall who want it, and I am not one of them, and I am running out of years and allies to prevent it." He spread his hands. "So. My offer."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, and that was the obscenity of it — how plainly. Restoration of her name, published in every chapterhouse, the word *traitor* struck from every record and replaced with the truth of what the Order had done with her maps. Full rank: Master Cartographer, First Chair, access to every archive, every sealed map, every locked history of the last three hundred years. And the thing he saved for last, leaning forward, voice dropping as though the shelves might overhear:

"The complete record of the war. Every order, every annotation, every name. Including the names of the men who gave them. You could publish it, if you wished — through our own network, the way you stole from our archive. We would not stop you. We would *help* you. Justice, Cyrus Blackwood, real justice, delivered by your own hand — and in exchange, all we ask is the vessel. Bring us the child. Unharmed. We will not force the seal; I swear it on the Office itself. We will ask her, as we should have asked the dragons. But she must be asked *here*, under law, under ward, where the asking can be witnessed and recorded. Surely you see. Surely a woman who has read our archives knows that an unwitnessed covenant is worth nothing. The valley cannot hold this. A cottage and a child and good intentions — that is not law. That is sentiment wearing law's clothes."

He sat back. "Take whatever time you need."

And Cyrus took it. That was the second obscenity: she took the time, and used it, and found herself *thinking*.

Because the offer was plausible. That was the poison in it. Not the flattery — she had swallowed enough flattery at thirty to be immune. Not the pardon; she had stopped wanting her name back sometime in year nine of exile, when she realized the name had never been the wound. It was plausible because it was *almost right*. The covenant did need witnesses. Law did matter; she had learned that in the worst way, learning what happened when clever people acted outside it. And the Grand Scrivener's account of thinning magic matched everything she had felt crossing the boundary stones, everything the maps had whispered, everything Vessith's dying embers implied. If he was lying, he was lying with the truth, brick by true brick, building a false house.

And Felix — Felix would be *safe*. Fed, taught, protected, asked rather than hunted. No more snowstorms melting circles of spring while villagers whispered. No more riders on ridges. No more running through ash-country with a bounty on her small burning hand.

Cyrus looked at her own hands, free on the armrests, and hated how reasonable they looked. Twenty years ago another version of her had sat in a room like this, across from men this reasonable, and drawn maps that killed the sky. She had told herself then, too, that she was doing the right thing the right way. Reasonable hands. That was how the world actually ended — not in fury, but in plausibility, in good process, in witnessed and recorded and sworn-on-the-Office.

"Your offer is generous," she said at last, and her voice came out steady, which frightened her more than trembling would have. "And I believe most of it. That's what makes you dangerous. But answer me one question honestly — truly honestly, the honesty you're selling — and I'll consider everything else."

"Ask."

"When the dragons were asked — in the beginning, when the first covenant was made — who asked them? Kings? Scriveners?" She watched his face. "Or did they choose their bearer themselves? Because I've stood in a caldera, Scrivener. I've seen what's left of the last one who trusted your kind with the asking. The seal doesn't go to whoever builds the best room for it. It goes where it *goes*. And it went to a nine-year-old girl who walked out of the ash on her own feet, speaking a language older than your Office, and chose — *chose* — a disgraced mapmaker in a valley where magic couldn't follow. You can't witness a covenant into being. You can only betray one."

The silence stretched. The candle guttered and steadied.

"You genuinely considered it," the Grand Scrivener said at last, and there was something almost like respect in it, which was the worst thing yet. "Good. Then you understand that I will make this offer again, and again, and each time it will be slightly better, and one day — perhaps not today — you will be tired, or she will be hurt, or the road will be hard enough that safety will look like love. Power is patient, cartographer. It can afford to be plausible forever."

"I know," Cyrus said, rising, feeling the wards tighten around her like a closing fist. "That's why I stopped listening an hour ago. The considering was real. So is the answer."

"And the answer?"

She thought of Felix's hand, small and burning cold. Thought of the promise made in ash.

"No," said Cyrus Blackwood. "Find another house to build."

The Grand Scrivener sighed, and closed his ledger, and for the first time looked tired — an old man in a room full of other people's secrets. "Then I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, which was somehow worse than if he hadn't. "Guards will escort you to the lower cells. Perhaps time will refine your thinking."

As they took her arms, the candle finally drowned in its own wax, and the last thing Cyrus saw before the dark took her was the Grand Scrivener already writing — recording, always recording — and the terrible, patient certainty in his posture that the story was not finished, that offers like his never die, they only wait.

Somewhere above her, past floors of stone and shelves of teeth, a child was waiting too.

*Hold on,* Cyrus thought, as the cell door boomed shut. *I considered the devil's arithmetic and refused it. Now I only have to survive long enough to be worth refusing for.*

In the dark, faint and far away, something began to burn — and it was not the candle.
