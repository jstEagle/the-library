## 16. The Escape from Vellum Hall

The sigil woke Felix before the sun did.

It was not a burning, exactly. It was more like a hand pressing against the inside of her chest, patient and enormous, the way a dog presses its nose against a door it wants opened. Felix lay in the straw of the stable loft behind the tanner's house and watched her own palm glow faintly in the dark, gold light seeping up between her fingers like water through a floorboard.

*Cyrus*, said the sigil, in the way it said things — not with words but with knowing.

Felix sat up. Her heart was going fast, the way it went when she dreamed of fire and a falling shape and running through snow that was red at the edges. But this was not a dream-thing. This was a now-thing. Cyrus was in Vellum Hall, behind walls of paper and stone, behind wards that had swallowed her like a fish takes a fly, and the sigil had been pulling toward those walls for two days, tugging at Felix's ribs like a hook in her sternum.

She had tried to be good about it. She had tried to wait, because Wren had said *wait, we need a plan, plans are how you don't die*. Wren knew buildings like Felix knew hiding places. Wren could pick any lock in the river town and had shown Felix how, laughing, using hairpins on the tanner's back door while the tanner's dog snored.

But Wren had also taken the hide scrap. Wren had carried it into Vellum Hall in her coat pocket like it was nothing, like it wasn't the last living piece of the last living dragons, and she had done it for coins, and Felix still remembered the exact feeling of reaching into her own bundle and finding it gone — the cold that went all the way down to her toes, colder than the snowstorm, colder than anything.

Felix did not have words for most feelings. She had never needed them; Cyrus read her face the way other people read letters. But she had words for this one, learned from Wren herself, said cheerfully over stolen apples: *betrayal*. It sounded small when Wren said it. In Felix's chest it was the size of the falling shape in her dreams.

The sigil pressed again. *Cyrus.*

Felix climbed down from the loft, pulled on her boots, and went out into the blue-dark morning without waking anyone, because there was no one to wake. Wren slept across town now, in whatever room the Order's coin bought.

Vellum Hall stood on the hill above the river town like a grey tooth. Felix had seen it only from far away before, but the sigil had been pointing at it since they arrived, steady as a compass needle, and now, walking the muddy road with the sky going pink behind her, she felt the pull change. It stopped being a tug and became a current. It flowed through her legs and made them walk faster. It flowed into her eyes and made the Hall look strange — she could see the wards.

They were beautiful. That was the worst part. They hung over the Hall like curtains of frozen rain, layer upon layer of them, silver-white script turning slowly in the air, and each layer hummed a note, and together the notes made a chord that hurt her teeth. Grown men had died touching those curtains. Cyrus had told her so, once, in the low voice she used for true things: *No one crosses the wards of a chapterhouse, little one. No one alive.*

Felix stood at the bottom of the hill and looked at the curtains of frozen rain, and the sigil looked with her, and then the sigil did something it had never done before.

It laughed.

Felix felt it as warmth spreading up from her palm, up her arm, into her throat, and if she had a voice she would have laughed too, because she suddenly understood what the sigil understood: the wards were made by people who thought magic was a language you could learn. Rules. Grammar. Ink and order and law. The sigil was not a word in that language. The sigil was the thing the language had been invented to talk *about*. The wards were a fence built around a word, and Felix was carrying the fire the word meant.

She walked up the hill.

* * *

The first ward touched her at the gate and broke like ice on a pond.

There was no pain. There was a sound, a high clean ringing, and the silver curtain nearest her shivered and went dark, and the dark spread outward from where she stood in a circle, ward after ward guttering out like candles pinched by wet fingers. The humming chord faltered, lost three of its notes, lost five. Somewhere inside the Hall a bell began to ring — she heard it thin and far away — but the sigil was already moving her forward, quick and certain, a child running through a house she knows better than the grown-ups do.

Guards came out of a side door with drawn blades, and Felix looked at them, and the sigil showed them what it was.

She didn't decide it. She barely even saw it happen. One moment there were four men with swords, and the next moment the air between Felix and the men was full of dragon — not flesh, not wing, but the *idea* of dragon, vast and golden and old, a shadow of a shadow thrown across the courtyard, and the men dropped their swords and fell to their knees and one of them began to pray. The shadow faded almost at once, like breath off a window. The men stayed kneeling.

Felix stepped over the swords. She was sorry, a little. She hadn't wanted them to be afraid. But the sigil was not sorry, and the sigil was bigger, and she was learning that being loved by something enormous meant sometimes being carried places she would not have chosen to go.

Inside, the Hall smelled the way Cyrus's maps smelled — ink and dust and something sharp underneath, like fear kept in a jar. Corridors of grey stone. Doors with iron locks. And everywhere, on every wall, framed documents under glass, seals in red wax, the Order of the Quill's whole history written down and nailed up like trophies.

Felix could not read the words. But the sigil could read the *ink*.

It pulled her left, past a staircase, past a door where tired clerks were shouting at each other about bells and breaches, down a corridor that got older as it went — the stones rougher, the lamps fewer — until it stopped her in front of a door banded in black iron, and the sigil poured itself into her palm and said, clearly, for the first time in days, an actual word in the old tongue:

*Open.*

Felix put her hand flat against the door.

The lock did not melt. Nothing so simple. The iron *remembered* — she felt it remember — remembered being ore in a mountain, remembered heat, remembered the long slow patience of stone, and it sighed and slid apart like a fist unclenching, and the door swung inward on darkness and the smell of wet parchment.

And there, chained to a chair in the middle of the room, grey-faced and furious and alive, sat Cyrus Blackwood.

* * *

Cyrus had been counting the hours by the drips of water somewhere behind the wall. She had counted four hundred and eleven when the door unlocked itself, which was impossible, and she had decided she was finally going mad, which was almost a relief, when the doorway filled with a small mud-caked figure holding out both hands.

Felix's palms were glowing. Both of them now. Gold light, steady as lamplight, and around her wrists thin lines of script crawled like slow golden snakes, writing and unwriting themselves.

Cyrus forgot every language she knew except one, which was Felix's name, said silently, because her voice was ruined from refusing to answer questions.

Felix crossed the room in three steps and put her glowing hands on the chains.

"Wait—" Cyrus started, because the manacles were warded, she had *felt* them drinking at her whenever she pulled, they were designed to burn through skin and bone—

The manacles bloomed like flowers.

That was the only way Cyrus could ever describe it afterward. The black iron split along seams no smith had cut, petals peeling back, and the wards inside them — wards that had cost the Order a fortune, wards keyed to bloodline and oath — simply knelt. She felt them kneel. She felt the magic in the room bow the way grass bows under wind, toward the small girl with the burning hands, and Cyrus Blackwood, who had spent twenty years believing herself the only person in the valley who understood what power was, understood in that moment that she had never once seen it before.

The chains fell. Felix caught them before they hit the floor, quietly, because Felix was careful that way, and then she looked up at Cyrus with an expression Cyrus had never seen on her face before. Not fear. Not wonder. Something harder and brighter.

"You shouldn't have come," Cyrus whispered, and her voice cracked on it. "Little one, if they catch you here—"

Felix shook her head. Then she took Cyrus's scarred cartographer's hand in her small burning one, and pressed it flat against her own chest, over the sigil's glow, and let Cyrus feel what she felt: the pull, the certainty, the fierce simple knowing. *Cyrus. Mine. Go.*

Cyrus had argued with generals. She had argued with the Grand Scrivener himself, for six hours, in this very building, years ago, and won points she was later stripped of. She looked at Felix's face and did not argue at all.

"The stairs are watched," she managed instead. "There are forty guards between us and the—"

The wall exploded.

Not all of it. A section of it, roughly person-shaped, outward, into the corridor, showering the far end of the hall with rubble — and standing in the hole, covered in plaster dust and holding a crowbar, breathing hard, was Wren.

"I *knew* the cells were under the east range," Wren said, coughing. "I *knew* it. I've been trying to get down here for two days, do you know how many locked doors this place has? Do you know I had to let them *catch* me stealing to get thrown in the holding cell on this floor?" She took in the scene — the bloomed manacles, the glowing child, the open door — and her face did something complicated. "Or. Apparently I didn't need to bother."

Felix stared at her. The sigil flared hot against her ribs, and it remembered too — it remembered the empty bundle, the missing hide scrap, the cold toes — and Felix felt the wanting-to-hurt rise up in her like floodwater.

Wren met her eyes. Wren, who lied easily and smiled easier, did neither. She just stood in the hole she'd smashed in an Order fortress, dust in her hair, and said, "I stole from you. I'd steal again tomorrow to eat, that's just true. But not *that*. Not you." Her voice wobbled and she hated it, you could see her hating it. "They're going to use that scrap to hunt your kind, Felix. Whatever your kind is. I heard them talking. So we're square when I get it back, and not one minute before."

Felix held the floodwater. It took everything she had. But Cyrus's hand was warm on her shoulder, and Cyrus said softly, "People are allowed to be worse than their best moment, little one. If we weren't, I'd have been dead to you the day we met."

Which was such a strange, sad, true thing that the floodwater went down.

Felix nodded. Once.

Wren exhaled like she'd been holding her breath for two days. "Right," she said. "Guards are coming, by the way. Lots. That bell's been ringing since someone's precious wards started dying. East stair's blocked, so we go up through the map room, and Cyrus, you're going to love this, the map room has a *window*."

"A window," Cyrus repeated.

"Onto the roof. Onto the *sloped* roof. Over the *river*." Wren grinned, wild and guilty and alive. "I may have planned our escape route before I planned my apology. Priorities."

* * *

They ran.

Felix ran in the middle, always, Cyrus's hand or Wren's on her shoulder, and the sigil ran ahead of her through the corridors like a lantern, dimming the wards before they reached them so that alarms slept on as they passed. Twice they heard boots behind them and once ahead, and Wren pulled them through a servant's passage so narrow Cyrus had to turn sideways, muttering that she had mapped half the known world and never once mapped a building she was actually inside.

The map room was exactly what its name promised and nothing like what Cyrus expected: not charts of the world but charts *of people* — ledgers, lists, names in columns, and Cyrus went white as she passed the open pages, because she knew what those columns were. Assets. Expenditures. Her own name was in one of them; she'd seen it in the archive below, in ink that had changed her life twice, once when written and once when read.

"Cyrus." Wren, at the window, prying at the frame. "*Later.*"

Later. Cyrus filed the ledgers away in the place where she kept her rage, which was getting fuller every day, and turned to help with the window.

It opened onto grey morning air and the long slate slope of the roof, slick with dew, dropping away toward the river thirty feet below. Wren looked at it and lost her grin. Cyrus looked at it and did some fast arithmetic involving angles, friction, and death.

Felix looked at it and the sigil sang.

She stepped out onto the slate first. The others cried out — both of them, at once — but the slate did not slide. Under her bare-souled feet the roof *froze*, gently, frost feathering out from her footprints in golden-edged stars, gripping her soles, making a road where there had only been a slope. She turned and held out her hands, one to each of them, and her palms glowed like two small suns.

Behind them, in the map room, boots and shouting. Ahead, the river, bright and cold and free.

"Wren," Cyrus said, taking one small burning hand, "when we reach the bottom, remind me to never forgive you properly. It seems to motivate you."

"Noted," Wren gasped, taking the other.

They ran down the roof of Vellum Hall as the bell tolled and the wards died behind them star by star, and when they hit the slope's steepest pitch Felix laughed — really laughed, soundless, her whole body shaking with it — because the sigil was laughing too, and because below them the river waited, and beyond the river the road bent east, toward ash, toward embers, toward the last fire in the world.

And far behind them, in a tower at the top of the Hall, a lamp flared in a window. Someone had seen them. Someone was already writing.

The sigil felt the pen scratch and pulled Felix faster.
