## 17. Emberreach

The mountain announced itself long before they saw it.

For three days they had climbed through country that grew stranger with every mile — forests of blackened pine standing like mourners at a funeral no one had attended, rivers that ran warm and smelled faintly of struck flint, soil the color of old blood where nothing green had grown in twenty years. Cyrus had mapped this region once, from a distance, from rumor, from the half-remembered testimony of refugees. She had drawn its outline in careful ink and labeled it *Emberreach — uninhabited* — and she had never imagined she would walk it herself, her boots crunching over ash that lay ankle-deep in the hollows like unmelting snow.

Now the map was wrong, and she was glad of it. No cartographer's line could have prepared her for the way the light changed here. The sun still rose and set as it always had, but beneath it the ground itself glowed — not brightly, not anymore, but with the deep sullen radiance of coals raked over and left to die. Fissures in the rock breathed heat against her ankles. In the crevices between stones, tiny crystals of sulfur caught the light and threw it back in colors that had no name she knew: amber shading into rose, rose into something almost blue, the way a fire's heart looks just before it gutters out.

Felix walked ahead of her, and the sigil on her palm had begun to sing.

Cyrus could not hear it — she had never been able to hear it, though she had learned to read its effects on the child the way sailors read weather — but she could see it now, plainly, in the way Felix moved. The girl's usual wariness had fallen away. Her chin lifted. Her steps quickened, then slowed, then quickened again, like someone being called by a voice only she could hear and unable to decide whether to run toward it or away from it. When Cyrus called her name, Felix turned, and for a moment her eyes were not entirely her own — there was a gold fleck in them, deep in the iris, that had not been there a month ago.

"You feel it," Cyrus said. It was not a question.

Felix pressed her marked palm flat against her own chest, then extended it toward the mountain, then drew it back and clenched it, hard, the way children do when they are frightened and determined not to show it. *Yes,* said the gesture. *And I don't know what it means.*

"We're close," Cyrus said. "Whatever happens up there — whatever we find — you remember that I'm beside you. Whatever speaks to you, whatever asks anything of you, you don't answer alone. Do you understand me?"

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, deliberately, she reached out and took Cyrus's hand — the scarred one, the one that had held pens and instruments for twenty years and weapons for barely two months — and squeezed it once. It was the first time she had initiated contact since Wren. Since the chapterhouse. Since Vellum Hall, and the burning of wards, and the flight down the mountain with alarms shrieking behind them and Wren's thin figure waving them onward from a doorway full of smoke.

Cyrus squeezed back and did not let go until the path steepened and they needed both hands for climbing.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of an outcropping of vitrified rock — sand fused to glass by some ancient exhalation of dragon-fire, smooth and dark and faintly warm even through their bedrolls. Above them the caldera rim was a jagged black line against the stars, and from somewhere within it came a sound that Cyrus felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears: a slow, vast rhythm, like breathing, if breathing could be measured in minutes rather than seconds.

"It's alive," she whispered, though Felix could not have understood the words. "After everything — it's actually alive."

She did not sleep. She sat up instead with her journal across her knees, the one honest book she owned, and tried to write down what she was feeling, and found that language failed her. She had spent her life translating the world into lines and labels, reducing the infinite complexity of terrain to symbols a stranger could read at a glance, and here, on the threshold of the last wonder left in the world, her symbols abandoned her. What symbol existed for grief? For awe? For the particular shame of a woman who had helped destroy something magnificent and was now crawling back to beg its forgiveness?

In the end she wrote only: *Tomorrow we meet what remains.* And beneath it, after a long pause: *I do not deserve what we may find.*

Felix stirred in her sleep and murmured something in the old tongue — a soft rising phrase, a question, perhaps, or a greeting. Even asleep, the girl was speaking to it. To him. To whatever slept inside the stone.

Cyrus closed her journal and watched the stars wheel above the broken rim of the world, and waited for morning the way one waits for a verdict.

* * *

The climb to the rim took the whole of the next day. The path — if path it could be called — wound through fields of obsidian shards that rang faintly underfoot like broken bells, past vents that exhaled breaths of hot mineral air, past the petrified remains of things Cyrus chose not to examine too closely. Twice they crossed expanses of ground so hot that the ash on its surface shimmered, and Felix led them around these without being told, her marked palm held out before her like a diviner's rod, steering them away from danger by instincts older than any map.

Toward late afternoon they crested a shoulder of rock, and Cyrus stopped, and forgot how to breathe.

The caldera opened below them like the cupped hands of a god — three miles wide, its walls streaked with mineral rain in bands of rust and gold and bone-white, its floor a plain of cooling lava cracked into vast hexagonal plates. And at its center, visible even from the rim, lay the dragon.

He was smaller than she had expected, and that realization broke something in her. The dragons of the old maps — the ones she herself had drawn for the Order, the roosts she had charted with such meticulous care — had been mountains given wings, creatures whose shadows had covered valleys, whose passing had bent trees like grass. Vessith, if this was Vessith, was perhaps sixty feet from snout to tail, curled within a shell of cooling stone that had risen around him like a cocoon, or a tomb. The shell was cracked in places, and through the cracks glowed the last of his fire: a dim red pulse, steady as a dying hearth, slower than the rhythm she had felt in the night. Slower than it should have been.

"He's dying," Cyrus said aloud, and her voice sounded obscene in that vast silence. "Oh, gods. He's dying, and he's been dying for twenty years."

Felix was already moving. She scrambled down the inner slope of the caldera with a sure-footedness that made Cyrus's heart seize — loose scree, glass-edged rock, heat-shimmered air — and Cyrus followed as fast as she dared, calling warnings the child ignored, slipping once and catching herself on a ledge that seared her palm, descending into the bowl of the world while the light died overhead and the red glow below grew stronger, warmer, more alive with every step.

The floor of the caldera was warmer than any summer day Cyrus had ever known. The air tasted of iron and cinnamon. And up close, the shell of stone around the dragon resolved itself into something neither wholly mineral nor wholly flesh — ridged and scaled in pattern, veined with channels of residual heat that pulsed in time with that terrible slow heartbeat. Up close, Cyrus could see his eye.

It was open. It had been open, she realized, watching them descend. A slit of molten gold, large as a shield, set in a face of gray stone scales gone dull as old pewter. It tracked them — tracked *Felix* — with an attention so total, so ancient, that Cyrus felt like an insect beneath a scholar's lens.

Felix walked straight up to the shell and laid her marked palm against it.

The effect was immediate and terrifying. The red glow in the cracks flared — brightened, steadied, turned from ember-dull to furnace-orange — and the great slow heartbeat stumbled, quickened, found a new rhythm. Heat rolled off the shell in waves that made the shimmering air dance. And from somewhere deep within the dragon's chest came a sound that was not quite a roar and not quite a voice: a low resonant tone that Cyrus felt in her sternum, in her fillings, in the soles of her feet, a sound like a cathedral organ sustaining its deepest note.

Then words. Not in the air — in her *mind*, arriving fully formed, in a voice like gravel rolling downhill in slow motion, each word taking an age, weighted with weariness beyond human reckoning.

***Little flame. You came.***

Felix's eyes went wide and wet. She pressed both hands to the stone now, forehead touching it, shoulders shaking, and answered aloud in the old tongue — a flood of it, tumbling, urgent, the most Cyrus had ever heard the child speak at once. Cyrus caught none of the meaning and all of the emotion: grief, and relief, and a question asked over and over, and something that might have been apology.

***Yes,*** the dragon said, and the word landed in Cyrus's mind like a bell tolling, though she knew — she *knew* — it was not meant for her. ***I am the last. I have been the last for a long time.***

Felix spoke again, softer. The dragon was silent a long while. When he answered, his voice had changed — gentler, sadder, addressed now as much to the empty air as to the child.

***Ask her. The mapmaker. Ask her if she knows what she is standing on.***

Cyrus went cold despite the heat. "I can't understand him," she said to Felix. "What is he saying?"

Felix looked back at her over her shoulder, and her expression was strange — caught between the child Cyrus had fed and sheltered and taught, and something older looking out through those gold-flecked eyes. She said something in the old tongue, gesturing at Cyrus, and the dragon's gaze shifted, and Cyrus stood under the weight of it like a woman under a falling sky.

***The traitor-cartographer,*** Vessith said. ***I remember your kind. Your ink. Your little lines. You drew our homes for them, and they came with their quills and their fire-oil, and my mate burned for six days before she died.*** The great eye did not blink. ***Why have you brought her here, little flame?***

Felix's answer was immediate, fierce, protective — she stepped between Cyrus and the dragon, small hands spread wide, and spoke with a vehemence that needed no translation. *She is mine. She is ours. She came back.*

***Coming back is not the same as being worthy,*** Vessith rumbled, but the fury in him banked, the way fire banks when starved of air. ***Still. She is here. And the seal is here. So we will speak of what must be spoken, before my fire goes out and takes the last chance with it.***

"What is he saying?" Cyrus asked again, hating her helplessness, hating the twenty years of exile that had let the old tongue pass her by. "Felix — please."

Felix hesitated. Then she took Cyrus's hand and pressed the girl's own marked palm against it, and said one word in the old tongue, gently, like a key turning in a lock —

— and the world *opened*.

Sound poured into Cyrus's mind: the caldera wind, the crackle of cooling stone, her own hammering heart. And beneath it all, vast and tired and impossibly patient, the dragon's voice, no longer distant but *present*, filling her skull:

***— hear me now, mapmaker? Good. Then listen, because I will not have the strength to say this twice.***

"I hear you," Cyrus whispered. Her legs had gone weak. She knelt on the hot glassy ground without deciding to, because standing suddenly seemed presumptuous. "I hear you. I —" The words she had carried for twenty years rose in her throat, all the speeches she had composed in the valley's silence, and collapsed into rubble. "I mapped your roosts. I gave them to the Order. I believed — I told myself it would end the war, that knowing where you slept would make them merciful —" Her voice broke. "There is nothing I can say. There is nothing I can do. But I am sorry. I have been sorry every day of my life, and it has never once been enough, and I know that."

The silence stretched. The heartbeat pulsed. Somewhere high above, a night bird cried once and fell silent, as if ashamed.

***Yes,*** Vessith said at last. ***You are sorry. I can taste it; sorrow has a flavor, and yours is old and well-kept.*** A plume of sulfurous breath escaped a crack in the shell, sighing across the caldera. ***But sorrow is not what I need from you, mapmaker. Understanding is. You gave them our sleeping places because you believed knowledge would tame them. Now you will learn what knowledge is *for*.***

***The child carries half a seal,*** he continued, and Cyrus felt Felix stiffen beside her. ***The covenant your Order shattered was bound by two halves: one held in dragonfire, one held in mortal flesh, freely given. They burned the fire-half out of my kin one by one, hunting the vessels, until only this scrap remained, and it fled into a child who could not speak to tell anyone what she carried.*** The great eye closed, then opened again, and Cyrus saw that it was wet — molten gold gathering at the corners like tears of brass. ***A joined seal can bind magic to law justly. Law that serves the land, not the men who write upon it. Or, forced — torn, taken, compelled — it ignites everything my dead kin died trying to prevent. Your Grand Scrivener knows this. It is why he hunts her. He does not want justice, mapmaker. He wants a weapon with a trigger he controls.***

"The Order is coming," Cyrus said. It was not a question either. She had known it since Vellum Hall, since the alarms, since Wren's face in the smoke. "How long do we have?"

***Longer than you fear. Shorter than you hope.*** The dragon's voice was fading now; she could feel the effort of speech draining him, the glow in the cracks dimming degree by degree. ***I have one fire left in me, little flame. One. When the time comes, I will spend it, and what happens after —*** the great eye drifted shut, then forced itself open, fixed on Felix with unbearable tenderness — ***what happens after is not mine to see. Come closer, child. Let me look at you properly. Twenty years I have slept, dreaming of the smell of another of my kind, and instead the world sends me a human whelp with my mother's seal in her hand.*** A sound moved through him that might, in a healthier creature, have been laughter. ***The world has a cruel sense of humor. I think I liked it better when it feared me.***

Felix pressed her face against the stone shell and wept — silently, the way she did everything, her small shoulders heaving against the vast patient bulk of the last dragon in the world. And Cyrus knelt beside her on the burning glass of the caldera floor, one hand on the child's back, one hand flat against the warm stone, and felt through her palm the slow failing pulse of a heart that had outlived everyone it had ever loved.

Above them, on the caldera rim, the first grey shapes appeared — small at this distance, indistinct against the darkening sky, but unmistakable to a woman who had once worn their cloak. Figures, standing in a line along the edge of the world, looking down.

One of them raised a lantern. Its light caught paper — pages, many pages, fluttering on the wind like a flock of pale birds released over the caldera.

Vessith's eye opened. The glow in the cracks beneath Cyrus's hand surged, sudden and furious, and his voice filled her skull one final time, no longer weary at all:

***They are early. Little flame — mapmaker — the choice is now.***
