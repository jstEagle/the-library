## 19. The Battle of the Last Fire

Dawn came to the Emberreach the color of old iron.

Cyrus stood at the caldera's eastern rim, her breath pluming in the cold, and watched the Order's banners unfurl along the distant ridge like wounds opening across the sky. Grey cloth, white quills stitched at the shoulder — she had sewn one of those banners herself, once, twenty-three years ago, with hands that had believed they were doing good work. Now the same hands held a spyglass that had no glass in it, only a disc of polished obsidian Vessith had breathed upon, and through it she counted the shapes descending the switchback trail: two hundred, perhaps two hundred and fifty. Lancers on foot, since no horse would come within a league of a dragon's grave. Sappers with coils of black powder cord. And at their head, walking with the unhurried gait of a man attending a lecture he himself was giving, the Grand Scrivener.

He had not waited. She had thought he would negotiate first, posture, send terms under a white rag as he had in the valley. Instead he had simply arrived in the night with everything he had, because he understood — as she understood — that what slept below them could not be permitted to wake twice.

"They're moving faster than I hoped," Cyrus said.

Felix stood beside her, small and still, her palm wrapped in linen strips that did nothing to muffle the light bleeding between her fingers. The sigil had been burning all night, gold through white, pulsing in time with something far beneath the stone. With Vessith's heart, Cyrus realized. The child and the dragon were keeping time together now, two clocks wound by the same hand.

Felix looked up at her and signed — quick, urgent, the vocabulary they had built over months of road and river: *How many?*

"Too many for Wren alone," Cyrus said. "But the pass is narrow, and Wren is exactly the kind of problem too many becomes."

Below them, where the caldera's rim broke into a throat of shattered rock — the only approach wide enough for two hundred men to walk abreast in column — Wren was making her preparations. Cyrus could see her only as a smudge of movement among the boulders: a slight figure in a stolen Order cloak, dragging stones into place, laying out the sapper's cord she had lifted from a dead man at Vellum Hall with the cheerful shamelessness of someone who considered theft a form of scholarship. Seventeen years old, half-starved, a liar and a pickpocket and the bravest person Cyrus had ever mapped a battle around.

*She'll hold,* Felix signed.

"She'll hold," Cyrus agreed, "because she doesn't know any better than to think she will." Then, gentler: "And when she can't hold anymore, we will already have won or lost, and it won't matter what she held."

Felix frowned at her — the fierce, offended frown of a child being told that love has arithmetic in it — and turned back to the ridge.

Cyrus unrolled her maps across a flat shelf of stone and weighted the corners with cinders. This was the work. This had always been the work, before the Order made it monstrous: the patient, loving study of ground. The Emberreach's eastern approaches were drawn on three sheets, cross-hatched where the rock was sound, stippled where it was rotten, annotated in her own cramped hand from a survey she had made in another life — *overhang, unstable; scree field, active; seam of yellow clay, water-cut, treacherous after rain.* Twenty years ago these notes had been how the Order's sappers chose where to plant their charges against the roosts at Cindervale. She had drawn the map that told them where the mountain was weakest, and they had gone to its weakness like surgeons, and the sky had emptied.

Now she read her own traitor's handiwork the way a general reads scripture, and found, in the margin of the oldest sheet, in ink faded to the color of dried blood, the words she had written the night before her exile:

*The vanguard trail crosses the Yellow Seam at the third switchback. If the seam fails, nothing above the fifth switchback can stand.*

She had written it as a warning. A cartographer's duty note, meant for travelers. The Order had never read it, because the Order had never needed to — they were the ones who would be walking, not falling.

Today the note was a weapon, and she was the one who would fire it.

* * *

The attack began with trumpets, which struck Cyrus as obscene. Two hundred men announcing themselves to a dying dragon, as though courage were something you could declare rather than spend.

They came down the switchbacks in good order — she had to grant them that, watching through the obsidian disc. The Scrivener had drilled them well. Shield-line in front, sappers behind, skirmishers fanned along the flanks where the rock allowed it. At the third switchback the column compressed into the throat of the pass, and there Wren let them have their first taste of the morning.

The first charge went off with a crack that Cyrus felt in her teeth rather than heard, a flat concussion that rolled down the caldera and came back up again. Half the switchback simply ceased to be a path. Six men went with it, tumbling in their grey cloaks like ash off a log, and the column stopped, and reformed, and came on — because the Scrivener was at its head, and the Scrivener did not believe in stopping.

Wren fought the way she stole: fast, low, without honor and without hesitation. Cyrus watched her loose the second charge into a clump of skirmishers who had tried to climb past the blockage, watched her roll behind a boulder as crossbow bolts splintered the stone where she'd been standing, watched her pop up elsewhere entirely, screaming insults in three dialects, and lay the third charge under a shelf of rock just as four lancers reached it. The shelf came down. The lancers did not climb out.

But two hundred is two hundred, and a pass is only a pass until it isn't. By midmorning the Order had learned Wren's rhythm — charge, bolt, charge, bolt — and began feeding men forward in pairs while the main body chiseled at the blockage with picks. Cyrus saw the moment the arithmetic turned. It was in the way Wren moved: shorter distances between cover, longer pauses, the particular economy of a body spending its last coin. A bolt caught her across the shoulder; she staggered, laughed — Cyrus could see the laugh even at this distance, the thrown-back head of it, defiant and terrible — and kept working.

"We go now," Cyrus said.

Felix grabbed her sleeve. *Vessith,* the girl signed, then pressed her bandaged palm flat against the stone. Beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the mountain answered: a slow pulse of warmth rising through the rock, like a heartbeat heard through a floor.

"Not yet," Cyrus said. "She rises when the sigil calls her, and the sigil calls her when we're clear of the blast line. That's the whole plan, Felix. Everything else is just timing." She knelt so their eyes were level. "I need you to do the hardest thing anyone down here is doing today. I need you to wait."

Felix's jaw set. For a moment Cyrus saw in the child's face every argument she couldn't speak — and then, one by one, Felix set them down. She signed, slowly: *You come back.*

"I come back," Cyrus said. It was the first promise she had made in twenty years that she believed she could keep, and saying it felt like setting a bone.

* * *

The Yellow Seam ran beneath the fifth switchback like a vein of cowardice through the mountain's spine — clay and rotten tufa, water-cut, honeycombed by frost. Cyrus had mapped it at nineteen, lying on her belly at the lip of the cut with a plumb line and a notebook, while below her the dragons of Cindervale wheeled in the thermals and she had thought, with the pure stupid joy of the young, *I am the first person ever to draw this place.* She had been right about that. She had been wrong about everything it meant.

Now she came at the seam from above, alone, with the last of Wren's powder cord and a length of fuse she had cut to a length measured not in seconds but in lives. Below her, the vanguard was crossing. She could hear them — boots on scree, the jingle of mail, a sergeant's voice calling the count. One hundred and forty men on the switchbacks between the fourth turn and the pass mouth, packed shoulder to shoulder in the only corridor her maps said they could use.

Her hands did not shake. That was the thing she noticed, kneeling in the cold with the fuse in her fingers — that they did not shake, that some part of her had done this arithmetic long ago and was calm about the answer. *One hundred and forty men, or the last living sigil of the dragons. One hundred and forty men, or a world where Felix grows up hunted.* The Order had taught her that sums always balanced. They had simply never taught her what to do with the remainder.

She thought of the archive beneath Vellum Hall. Of her own name in the asset ledger, filed between a shipment of ward-ink and a requisition for candles. *Asset, spent, discarded.* She thought of the Scrivener's voice, reasonable as arithmetic itself: *the vessel must be secured for study.* She thought of Cindervale, which she had never seen burned because she had been safely exiled when it happened, and which she had nonetheless walked through in dreams for twenty years — the roosts empty, the eggs cracked open like the maps she'd once loved, every coastline of the sky erased.

"Fury," she said aloud, testing the word, finding it fit. "This is what fury is. It's just grief that's finished waiting."

She lit the fuse.

Then she did the thing that no one in the Order's manuals accounted for, because no one had thought to write it down: she stayed. She stood at the lip of the cut, in full view, grey-haired and unarmed, an exile in a patched coat, and she raised both arms above her head so that every man on the switchbacks could see her.

"Hold!" she shouted, and her voice carried the way voices carry in mountains, clean and enormous. "In the name of the covenant you've forgotten — hold! Look up! Look at what your Scrivener is spending you on!"

For three heartbeats, they actually did. A hundred and forty faces tilted upward toward a woman on a cliff, and in those faces Cyrus saw everything: fear, confusion, faith, and here and there — worse than all of it — doubt. Doubt was the thing she could give them. Doubt was the one weapon the Order had no counter for, because the Order's whole architecture was built on certainty, and certainty, once cracked, let in exactly the light she wanted.

"The dragons knew your names!" she called. "They sang them! Read your own archives — I have — ask your Scrivener why the ledgers list them as *persons of treaty!* Ask him what he spent!"

"SILENCE," roared a voice from below, and there he was, the Grand Scrivener, pushing through his own vanguard, quill-badge blazing white on his shoulder, his face twisted into something no longer reasonable at all. "That woman is a convicted traitor! Her words are sedition! Forward — FORWARD—"

The fuse reached the charge.

The Yellow Seam did not explode. It *failed* — quietly, massively, the way bad ground always fails, the whole cut sloughing inward and downward with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. The fifth switchback went first, then the fourth, a cascade of clay and tufa and grey-cloaked men pouring into the caldera's throat in a single rolling avalanche of everything the Scrivener had brought to win the day. Cyrus felt the shockwave through her boots, saw the dust rise in a wall, saw the column cease to be a column and become debris.

She did not watch the men. She made herself not watch the men. She watched instead the one figure who had been at the rear, beyond the blast line — the Scrivener himself, scrambling back up the trail with perhaps thirty survivors, his white badge streaked with clay, his mouth open on a scream she could not hear over the mountain's grinding.

*Thirty left,* she thought. *And me, and a child, and a thief, and a dragon who hasn't opened her eyes in a hundred years.*

It would have to be enough. It was going to have to be enough.

* * *

The dust was still settling when the sigil called.

Cyrus felt it before she heard it — a pressure change, a warmth flooding up through the stone like spring arriving in a single hour — and then Felix's voice, high and wordless and ringing with harmonics no human throat should own, echoing off the caldera walls in the Old Tongue at full strength for the first time since the girl had come knocking on a cottage door in a valley without magic.

The shell of cooling stone at the caldera's heart split like an egg.

What rose out of it was not the dragon of tapestries. Vessith came up out of her century of sleep on wings that were more memory than membrane, translucent at the edges, guttering flame showing through the holes in her like light through moth-eaten cloth. She was vast and she was failing and she was magnificent, and when she opened her jaws the fire that came out was thin and blue and utterly real, washing across the rubble of the fifth switchback, sealing the pass in a rampart of fused glass that no pick in the world would breach before winter.

The remaining Order soldiers broke. Cyrus watched them run — not routed, not yet, but *leaving*, which was different and worse for the Scrivener, because leaving was contagious. Thirty became twenty-five became scattered figures climbing back toward the rim, and above them the Grand Scrivener stood alone on a spur of rock, refusing with his whole rigid body to move, staring up at the impossible fact of Vessith with an expression Cyrus recognized from mirrors: a man watching his life's certainty die in front of him.

Vessith's head swung toward him. The guttering flame gathered in her throat.

"No!" Cyrus was running along the rim, waving her arms, shouting in the Old Tongue now, badly, the few words Felix had taught her scraped raw with urgency. "*Vessith! Sha-veth! Sha-VETH!* Not him — not yet — the truth first — *the truth FIRST!*"

The dragon's eye — huge, ancient, filmed like a cataract beginning to clear — found her. Held her. And in it Cyrus saw a recognition that stopped her breath: not of her face, but of her crime. *The mapper,* that eye said. *The one whose lines showed them the way in.*

"Yes," Cyrus said, and did not look away. "Me. And I've come to finish the accounting."

Behind the Scrivener, on the shattered trail, the last of his soldiers fled into the smoke. Above them all, Vessith drew breath — thin, blue, insufficient, and yet somehow enough — and the heat of it rolled over the caldera rim like a tide.

The Scrivener did not run. He straightened his ruined coat, lifted his chin, and met the dragon's gaze with the stubborn dignity of a man who had read every book except the one that mattered.

Cyrus started toward him across the broken ground, the wind of Vessith's breathing at her back, knowing that whatever happened next would decide not just who survived this day, but what the day had *meant* — and that the hardest battle of the last fire was still waiting for her, one man and one truth away, at the top of the world.
