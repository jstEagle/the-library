## 21. The Burden Accepted

The caldera was quiet in the way Felix thought a church must be quiet, if a church were the size of the sky.

She sat on Vessith's foreleg, her small hands pressed flat against the cooling stone of the dragon's hide, and she listened. Not with her ears — with the thing in her palm. The sigil had stopped burning days ago. Now it hummed, low and patient, like a kettle just before it sings, and when Felix held still enough, she could feel what it wanted.

It wanted to be finished.

Vessith's breath moved through the ground beneath her, slow as seasons. The great head lay on its side among the embers, one eye half-open, that eye like a door into a room where a fire had gone out long ago but the hearth was still warm. When Felix looked into it, she did not see a monster. She saw tiredness. She saw the last light left in a very old lamp.

*Little bearer,* Vessith said, and the voice did not come through the air but up through Felix's bones, through the stone, through the soles of her boots. *You are thinking loudly again.*

"I'm not thinking anything," Felix said out loud, because her mouth-words still felt new and precious, even after all these weeks. "I'm deciding."

*Ah.* The eye blinked, slow as a door closing. *Deciding is heavier than thinking. I remember.*

Felix looked down at her palm. The sigil lay there like a brand made of night — lines within lines, a shape that hurt to look at too long, the way the sun hurt. It had come to her from a scrap of hide she'd carried across half the world without knowing why. It had burned cold when it first took root in her skin. It had melted snow around her in a circle of false spring. It had opened wards no grown person could touch, and it had pulled Cyrus Blackwood out of a prison cell like a key turning in a lock.

It had done all of that for her. And now it wanted something back.

*The covenant wants a keeper,* Vessith said, gentle in the way mountains are gentle, which is to say: enormous, and unmoving, and true. *Not a vessel. A vessel can be broken. A keeper chooses.*

"I choose," Felix said.

*You are nine winters old.*

"Eight," Felix said. "Almost."

Something moved through the old dragon's body that might have been laughter, if laughter could be made of stone settling deeper into stone. *Almost eight. And you would carry this until your last day? You would live where magic cannot follow? You would be the lock, little one, and locks do not get to walk through doors.*

Felix's chin came up. She knew what exile meant now. She had learned it from watching Cyrus — Cyrus, who had lived twenty years at the edge of a valley with no magic in it, mapping worlds she could never touch, eating supper alone every single night, keeping her pens in perfect rows because order was the only friend she had left. Felix had seen the look on Cyrus's face the first time she crossed the boundary stones and magic flooded back into her, painful and bright, like blood returning to a foot that had been asleep for two decades. She had heard Cyrus weep afterward, quietly, behind a screen of brush, thinking Felix couldn't hear.

Nobody should have to go back to that. Especially not Cyrus. Cyrus had already paid.

"It's my turn," Felix said. "Cyrus always says — fair shares. Even when they're bad ones."

*Little bearer—*

"I'm almost eight," Felix said, "and I'm the only one it fits."

That was the truth of it, and both of them knew it. The sigil had chosen her back on the night it crawled off the scrap of hide and into her palm. It sang under her skin in a language older than kingdoms, the same language she'd spoken before she could speak anything else, the language nobody else in the whole world understood except dragons and dying things. Wren said it was creepy. Wren said a lot of things, but Wren also stood in a mountain pass with a stolen sword and held back forty quill-soldiers, so Felix forgave her most things now.

Footsteps crunched on ember-grit behind her. Felix didn't turn. She knew the rhythm of those steps the way she knew rain before it fell — a little uneven on the left, from an old injury Cyrus never talked about, quick and purposeful on the right, like the woman was always late for something important.

"Felix." Cyrus's voice. Rough, the way it got when she'd been arguing with someone. "We need to talk about what Vessith told you."

"You talked to Wren," Felix said. "Wren told you."

"Wren tells me everything. It's very annoying." Cyrus came around into view, and Felix saw that her face was doing the thing it did when she was trying to be calm and failing at it — jaw tight, eyes moving everywhere except where they needed to be. There was ash in her grey hair and a fresh burn along her forearm where a ward had snapped back at her during the battle. The Grand Scrivener's fall had cost them something even in victory. Everything cost something. That was another thing Felix had learned on the road.

"He told you what the binding requires," Cyrus said.

"He told me somebody has to carry it forever. In the valley. Where nothing magical works." Felix held up her palm. The sigil pulsed once, like a heartbeat. "It has to be me. It picked me."

"No," Cyrus said.

"Yes."

"No, Felix." Cyrus crouched down in front of her, which put their faces level, which was a trick Cyrus used when she wanted Felix to really listen, and Felix hated how well it worked. "Listen to me. You are a child. You have already carried more than any child should — you crossed the lowlands alone, you survived the Order's hunters, you carried the last living piece of the dragons through a war zone strapped to your hand like a bandage. That is more than enough. More than any of us. The binding does not require *you*. It requires a willing bearer who understands the cost. And I—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—I understand the cost better than anyone alive."

Felix felt the understanding arrive in her chest like a stone dropped into still water.

"No," she said.

"Yes," said Cyrus.

"That's not fair!"

"Very little of this has been fair," Cyrus said. "That is rather the point."

* * *

They argued on the rim of the caldera while the sun went down and the embers below turned the clouds the color of healing wounds.

Felix argued that the sigil was hers, that it had chosen her, that taking it away would be like taking her name. Cyrus argued that choice made by a child was not the same as choice made freely, that the Order had spent twenty years making decisions *for* people and look where that had gotten the world. Felix argued that Cyrus was always doing this, always putting herself between Felix and things, like a wall. Cyrus said yes. Yes, exactly, that was precisely what walls were for.

Then Felix ran out of arguments, because she was almost eight and Cyrus Blackwood had been arguing with governments since before Felix was born, and so Felix did the only thing left. She held out her hand and called the sigil up.

It rose out of her palm like smoke given shape — the lines lifting free of her skin, hanging in the air between them, beautiful and terrible, humming that kettle-song, waiting to be told where to live.

And Cyrus moved faster than Felix had ever seen her move.

Her hand closed over Felix's small fist, pressing it shut, and her other hand — her right, her map-drawing hand, the hand that had drawn the roosts of dragons and signed away their lives — she pressed it flat against the floating sigil and said, in the old language, in the language older than kingdoms, the words Felix had taught her lesson by lesson over shared meals and mistranslations:

*I take it willingly. I know the price. Let it be mine.*

The world went white.

* * *

Felix woke on her back with Wren's anxious face above her and the taste of copper in her mouth and the sigil gone from her palm — gone, the skin smooth and unmarked, as if it had never been there at all. For one horrible moment she thought it had died, that the last living sigil of the dragons had flickered out between one heartbeat and the next, and she started to cry before she could stop herself.

"She's fine," Wren said, hovering. "She's fine, she's just — out cold. They're both out cold. Is that normal? Nobody told me this was going to involve anybody being out cold."

Felix rolled over.

Cyrus lay ten feet away on the ember-grit, on her side, curled like a question mark. Her right hand was extended, palm up, and across it — seared into the skin in lines of silver and shadow, sunk deeper than ink, deeper than scars — blazed the sigil. Whole. Complete. Half of it still sang with dragon-fire, and the other half, the half that had been missing for a thousand years, had closed over it like a hand closing over another hand.

As Felix watched, Cyrus's fingers twitched. Curled. Closed around the mark as if holding onto something in a strong wind.

"Cyrus!" Felix scrambled over on her knees and took the big hand in both of her small ones. "Cyrus, wake up, wake up—"

The grey eyes opened. For a moment they were unfocused, swimming, full of something Felix couldn't read. Then they found her face and focused, and Cyrus smiled — a real smile, crooked and exhausted and achingly fond, the kind Felix had only seen a handful of times, each one hoarded like treasure.

"There you are," Cyrus rasped. "Knew you'd try something."

"You *stole* it," Felix said, and hit her on the shoulder, which was like hitting a sack of maps. "You stole my — my *name*, you stole the — it was MINE—"

"It was never yours, little one." Cyrus pushed herself up on her elbows, wincing. Wren rushed over to help and got waved off. "It belonged to the covenant. To the dragons. To everyone whose sky was emptied. We were only ever its custodians." She flexed the marked hand slowly, wincing again. "And custodianship is a burden for grown shoulders. Yours have years yet to grow."

"But the valley," Felix said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "You'll have to go back. Forever. No magic. No — you just got it *back*, I *saw* you, you cried—"

Cyrus went very still.

"I did not cry," she said.

"You did. Behind the bushes. After the stones."

A long silence. Below them, Vessith's breathing moved the ground. Somewhere far off, in the pass, Wren's borrowed sword glinted where she'd planted it as a marker, and beyond the pass the world waited — the reformed world, the world that would need cartographers, honest ones, ones who had learned what maps could do in the wrong hands.

"Felix," Cyrus said quietly. "Twenty years ago, I lost everything because I gave away knowledge that wasn't mine to give. Tonight I gave away something that was entirely mine to give — my freedom, my magic, the rest of my life — and for the first time in twenty years, I don't regret a choice I've made." She reached out with the marked hand and touched Felix's cheek, and the sigil was warm, warm as a hearth, warm as spring under snow. "Exile isn't the worst thing, little one. I know that better than anyone. The worst thing is exile *without* something worth coming home to. And now—" her voice did the catching thing again, worse this time, "—now I will have letters. From a girl who speaks dragon-tongue and argues like a barrister and eats jam with a knife like a heathen. I will have maps to draw of a living world. I will have a valley that finally has someone in it worth waiting for."

Felix threw herself forward and hugged her, hard, the way she'd never dared hug anyone before Cyrus, arms tight around the thin shoulders, face buried in the ash-grey hair that smelled of woodsmoke and ink.

"This isn't fair," she said into Cyrus's collar, muffled and furious and grieving.

"No," Cyrus agreed, and her arms came up around Felix, and the sigil on her palm glowed softly against the small of Felix's back like a second heartbeat. "But it's a fair share. And you taught me that."

Above them, vast and slow, Vessith lifted her ancient head for the first time in a hundred years, and the last fire in the world reflected in her eye like dawn.

*It is done,* the dragon said, and every stone in the caldera rang with it. *The covenant is sealed. Bearer — come. There is a vow yet to speak, and a sky yet to mend, and I would have us finish before my fire fails.*

Cyrus rose, pulling Felix up with her, and together they walked toward the dragon across the cooling embers — the exiled cartographer with the covenant burning in her hand, and the mute-born child who had given her the words to accept it.

Behind them, at the caldera's rim, the first riders of the new accord appeared against the darkening sky — not the Order's grey cloaks, but envoys in white and gold, come to witness the sealing.

And in Cyrus's marked palm, the completed sigil began, very softly, to sing.
