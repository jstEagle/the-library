## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

The snow came down the way it always did at Thornfell — sideways, mean, and full of opinion.

Tamsin Quintana stood in the doorway of the gatehouse with a tin lamp held high, watching it scour the valley below. Twenty years of winters had taught them the moods of this weather: which gusts meant a buried road by morning, which silences meant wolves moving low along the tree line, and which particular quality of dark meant that no one — no one at all — would be climbing the switchbacks tonight. This was the third kind. The kind you could trust.

They counted the ritual anyway. Grandmother had insisted on rituals the way other households insisted on bread: daily, without discussion, and woe to the one who skipped it.

Lamp lit. Bolt checked. Ward-stones walked.

Tamsin stepped out into the cold and made the circuit of the gate itself, boots crunching on the packed snow of the threshold. Thornfell was not a castle gate, whatever the maps pretended. It was a wall of grey stone older than any kingdom's memory, set into the throat of the pass where the mountains pinched together like a fist closing. Two pillars. A lintel cracked by some violence no history book recorded. And between them, doors of black oak banded with iron gone green as riverweed, shut now for longer than Tamsin had been alive.

They laid their palm against the left pillar, as required. The stone was cold, but beneath the cold there was the faintest tremor — or Tamsin imagined there was, some nights, the way a sailor imagines the sea still rocking after landfall. Grandmother had called it *the hum*. She'd said it was nothing. She'd said it with her hand pressed to the same pillar, eyes closed, listening so hard her jaw went tight, which was how Tamsin had learned, young, that *nothing* could be a very heavy word.

"Still shut," they told the stone, because the ritual wanted saying aloud. "Still quiet. Still ours."

The stones did not answer. They never answered. That was rule one, chalked inside the gatehouse door in Grandmother's cramped, furious hand:

*Never answer what hums.*

There were eleven rules. Tamsin could recite them in order, asleep, drunk, or bleeding, and had once done all three simultaneously to prove a point to a smuggler who doubted their commitment. Rule two: *No fire above the second stair.* Rule three: *Salt the sill on the new moon.* Rule four through ten concerned the smugglers' roads — which trails were safe, which were watched, and which one, the narrow goat-path behind the Hanging Meadow, existed on no map and must never be spoken of within earshot of the valley. Rule eleven was written last, in ink darker than the rest, and Tamsin had never once needed reminding of it:

*Whatever knocks after midnight is not knocking for you.*

Tamsin blew out a breath that steamed and froze midair, glittering down like the mountain sifting itself to dust. Below, the valley lay white and blind. Somewhere under all that snow was the Salt Road, and beyond it the low country, and beyond that the capital where King Aldous sat his throne — if he sat it still; nobody Tamsin trusted had seen him in years — and past the capital, whole countries full of people who had never heard of Thornfell and never would, because that was the entire point of Thornfell.

A keeper's life was a small life. Tamsin had made peace with its size the way one makes peace with a well-worn coat: not loving it, exactly, but knowing every seam.

They went back inside. They banked the fire. They ate barley stew with the last of the autumn onions, standing up, out of the pot, because there was no one to see and manners were a currency best saved. They oiled the hinges of the little postern door that faced the smugglers' trail, though it hadn't swung in months. They wound the clock that Grandmother had brought from God-knew-where, a fat brass thing that ticked half a beat too slow, so that Thornfell lived forever slightly behind the rest of the world.

That suited Tamsin fine. Behind the world was where the world kept its mistakes.

* * *

Midnight came and went the way it did most nights — unremarked, unbothered, just the clock ticking its lagging count into the dark. Tamsin sat by the banked fire mending a sock with more patience than skill, and let the silence settle over the gatehouse like a second blanket.

It was in that settled silence that the wind changed.

Not stronger. Not weaker. Just — *wrong*. It came down off the high crags carrying a smell that didn't belong to winter: warm stone, and something green underneath it, like a meadow opened in the middle of January. Tamsin's needle stopped mid-stitch.

Then the pillar outside hummed.

Not imagined this time. Not the sailor's ghost-sway. An actual sound, low and round, felt in the floorboards before it reached the ears, the way thunder is felt in the teeth. Tamsin sat very still. The fire ticked. The clock ticked, half a beat late, as though even it hesitated.

And then — soft, uncertain, unmistakable — a knock at the great oak doors.

Three knocks. Small ones. A child's knuckles, if knuckles at all.

Tamsin did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Rule eleven stood up in their chest, tall as Grandmother herself, arms crossed, eyebrows raised in that terrible patient way she'd had. *Whatever knocks after midnight is not knocking for you.* In twenty years, the rule had never been tested. Wolves scratched. Wind knocked branches against the lintel. Once, memorably, an avalanche had struck the outer wall hard enough to ring the whole gate like a bell. But no hand had ever fallen on those doors, because no living soul came to Thornfell's front face. The smugglers used the postern. The valley folk believed the gate led to a haunted ruin. Even the king's tax collectors, in the early years, had taken one look at the black oak and ridden on.

So whoever — whatever — stood on the far side of the doors had climbed the switchbacks, in this weather, at this hour, to a gate that stayed shut.

The knock came again. Three more. Weaker now.

And beneath it, threaded through the humming of the stones, Tamsin heard something that undid twenty years of discipline in a single syllable: a voice. Thin, exhausted, chanting — no, *counting*, counting the way children count when they are frightened and holding onto numbers like a rope —

Words Tamsin did not know and knew absolutely.

The sounds were nonsense to any ear raised in the king's tongue. But they fell into some cellar-room of Tamsin's mind, a room bricked over and forgotten, and each syllable found its shelf. A lullaby's shape. A cadence from before memory, from a kitchen long cold, from a woman's voice singing over a stove while she thought no one listened — except Grandmother had never sung in the king's tongue in her life, and Tamsin had been told, firmly and often, that the songs she sometimes caught her humming were *nothing*, old nonsense, forget them —

The stones were humming louder. The lamp flame leaned though there was no draft.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to the room, to the rule, to the dead. "No, no, no."

But they were already up. Their body had decided before their mind finished arguing; their feet crossed the flagstones with the certainty of a thing done in a dream, and their hand — their traitor, obedient hand — took down the iron bar from its brackets.

Rule eleven said: do not open.

Rule eleven had never heard that voice. Rule eleven had never been a child in a kitchen, half-asleep, letting a song wash over it that it was forbidden to remember.

Tamsin set their shoulder to the door, and the oak swung inward on hinges that had not moved in decades without so much as a groan, as though the gate had only been waiting to be asked.

Snow swept in across the threshold. Beyond it, hunched against the left pillar — the humming pillar, the one Tamsin touched every night, which now sang like a struck bell under the storm — stood a child.

Small. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Wrapped in a wool cloak gone grey with road-dust, hood crusted with ice, face so pale it seemed borrowed from the moon. Barefoot. *Barefoot*, in this, and yet the snow around the small feet had melted in a neat circle, and where the meltwater touched the threshold stones it steamed.

The child looked up. Eyes the colour of deep water under ice.

And spoke — clearly, deliberately, the way one speaks the single sentence one has carried a hundred miles — in the language Grandmother burned books to bury:

"*Eska veth il Spire. Eska veth il dommen.*"

The stones rang. Every one of them, pillars and lintel and the hidden ward-stones sunk in the earth, ringing together like a chime the size of the mountain, and Tamsin stood in the doorway of the life they had built and felt it crack straight down the middle.

Because they understood. God help them, they understood both sentences.

*I bring the word of the Spire. I bring the word of the dead.*

The child swayed. Tamsin caught them — instinct again, hands moving faster than fear — and the small body was furnace-hot despite the bare feet, burning with something that was not fever, or not only fever. From the folds of the cloak, a token slipped free and swung on its cord: a scale, broad as a coin, black-green, veined with gold that glowed faintly, warmly, like a coal deciding whether to wake.

Dragon-scale. Tamsin had seen one drawing in a burned book, once, through a crack in a cellar door they should never have been near. One drawing. One lifetime ago.

The child's fingers closed weakly around the scale. Their lips moved again, and this time the word that came out was simple, hoarse, and in the plain tongue of the kingdom — the last of their strength spent on translation, on making sure:

"Spire."

Behind Tamsin, the great doors stood open to the storm, and the stones of Thornfell gate went on humming — not the drowsy nothing-hum of twenty quiet years, but something with intent in it, something like recognition, something almost like joy.

Far down the valley, faint beneath the wind, Tamsin thought they heard hooves.

They pulled the child inside and barred the door.
