## 3. Riders on the Salt Road

Ilex had learned, in the two days since the gate opened for them, that Tamsin Quintana's house breathed in rhythms. The kettle sang at dawn. The floorboard by the hearth groaned at dusk like an old man settling into a chair. And at night, when Tamsin thought the child was sleeping, the keeper stood at the window with a lamp turned low and watched the road — the salt road, white as a scar across the valley floor — for a long time before allowing themselves to sleep.

Ilex watched them watching.

The child did not sleep much anyway. Sleep was where the words lived, the old words, rising up like fish to break the surface of dreams. *Vess-ah-lume. Keth-ra-iol.* Words Ilex had never been taught by anyone, words that had simply arrived one morning inside their mouth the way birds arrive inside a chimney, and after that there had been no going back to the house where they'd been born, because the woman there — not mother, not aunt, something else — had gone pale as milk and said, *You must run, and you must never say those words where anyone can hear.*

So Ilex ran. And the words came too.

Now the child lay on the pallet behind the hearth, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar and woodsmoke and something older beneath it, a smell like cold stone and iron, and listened to the house breathe. The fire ticked. Somewhere under the floor, water moved through pipes that were older than any kingdom Ilex could name, though Ilex could not name many kingdoms, having never been permitted to learn of them.

Then the dogs began.

Not Tamsin's dogs — the keeper kept no dogs, only a cat with a torn ear who regarded everything with contempt. These were far-off dogs, down in the valley, and their voices carried up through the frozen air in ragged chorus, and beneath them came another sound: hooves. Many hooves, muffled by snow but unmistakable, moving along the salt road at a pace too disciplined for weather, too slow for flight.

Ilex sat up. The cat opened one eye, decided nothing was worth its attention, and closed it again.

Through the gap in the shutters, the child saw them come: six riders in grey-and-crimson, the king's colors, breath steaming from their horses like small ghosts. They rode well. They rode like men who had ridden a long way through bad country and were tired of it, and their captain rode a little ahead, the way captains do, except —

Except his shadow was wrong.

The sun had come up thin and mean over the eastern ridge, laying all their shadows long across the snow, and five shadows stretched out honest and ordinary behind five horses. The sixth shadow — the captain's — leaned. It leaned east while the sun stood west of him. It leaned against the slope of things, the way a man leans against a doorframe, patient, and where the other shadows ended cleanly at their boots, his pooled and thickened, spreading into the trampled snow like ink into wet cloth, and the snow where it touched did not gleam. It drank the light instead.

Ilex knew that mark. Not from teaching — no one had taught the child anything — but from the same place the words came from. Some knowing older than knowing. *Keth-ra-iol,* whispered something behind the child's ribs. *The hungry year. The crown that eats.*

The knock, when it came, was courteous. That was somehow worse.

* * *

Tamsin answered the door the way they answered everything: unhurried, lamplit, unremarkable. But Ilex had already been moved — a hand between the shoulder blades, quick and gentle, steering the child behind the great loom in the corner, into the dark smelling of wool and dust, with a finger pressed briefly to lips. Through the warp threads Ilex could see the door open on six riders and a morning of hard blue light.

"Keeper Quintana." The captain's voice was pleasant. Sandpaper wrapped in velvet. "Riders of the Crown, on Crown business. We're asking after a runaway."

"A runaway." Tamsin's voice gave away nothing. "From the mines? From a master? This is a poor valley for runaways — nothing here worth running to."

"A child." A pause, weighted like a stone set carefully on a scale. "Perhaps ten years. Dark-haired. They say the child speaks strangely. An old dialect. You'd know it if you heard it — most folk cross themselves."

"I keep a gate," Tamsin said. "Folk pass it or they don't. I don't collect their tongues."

Behind the loom, Ilex's heart hammered so loudly it seemed impossible the riders couldn't hear it. The child pressed both hands flat against the cold stone floor and breathed the way the words had taught — without being taught — slow and deep, and felt the stones answer faintly, a hum below hearing, like a struck bell remembered rather than heard. *Be still,* the hum said. *Be stone. Be quiet as the Spire is quiet.*

The captain laughed — a real laugh, or something wearing a real laugh like a coat. "They said the Thornfell keeper was blunt. Good. Blunt folk waste less time." Leather creaked; something clinked. When he spoke again he held the words out like coins on a palm. "The Crown pays for silence, keeper. Fifty crowns of silver if the child passed your gate and you let it go. A hundred if you turn it over. No questions asked either way. The Crown doesn't want the child hurt." Another pause, thinner this time. "The Crown wants the child *remembered*."

And Ilex understood — the way one understands a nightmare while still inside it — that the captain had chosen his words badly, or truly, and that *remembered* was the cruelest verb in the language. Behind the loom the child's hands curled into fists. The stones hummed a half-tone sharper.

Tamsin didn't flinch. Ilex watched the keeper's broad back, the gray-streaked braid, the hands resting easy at the belt, and marveled at how still a living person could be. "No child passed my gate," Tamsin said. "Come spring the thaw brings shepherds, tinkers, the odd pilgrim. In winter? Nothing moves over Thornfell but wind and wolves. If a child crossed my threshold, I'd have fed it and sent it down-valley to the reeve at Millbrook — that's the law, and I keep the law. Ask the reeve."

"We will." Paper rustled. "Sign here, keeper. Acknowledgment of inquiry. Standard form."

"You can leave it. My writing hand's stiff in the cold."

A silence, longer than it should have been. Then the captain said, softly, almost kindly: "Your grandmother kept this gate before you. And her mother before her. Strange trade, keeping a gate in a valley nobody wants. Strange family, the Quintanas. As if someone wanted eyes here. Or a lock here." The words dropped one at a time, like stones into a well. "You'd know about locks, I expect."

"My grandmother's dead," Tamsin said. "She left me a gate and a roof that leaks. If the Crown has questions about the dead, it can ask them where the dead are."

Another laugh, shorter this time, with something underneath it that was not laughter at all. "Of course. Forgive me. Long roads make men talkative." Hoof-leather shifted; the visit was ending. "We'll ride up the valley, ask at the farms. If you think of anything, keeper — anything at all — a rider will pass within the day. We'll be in the valley some time yet. The Crown is thorough."

"The Crown always was," Tamsin said, and closed the door.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Tamsin crossed the room in three strides and pulled Ilex gently from behind the loom, and knelt, which clearly cost the keeper something, kneeling, and looked at the child eye to eye. Up close, Tamsin's face was older than it pretended to be, carved by weather and by waiting.

"Did you hear?"

Ilex nodded.

"They'll go farm to farm. Someone will have seen smoke from my chimney twice as thick these past days, or heard a second set of footsteps in the snow. Valleys talk." Tamsin exhaled slowly through the nose. "We have to move you. Tonight, before the thaw closes the high passes — no, before *they* think to watch them. There's a smuggler down at Coldharbor ford, Ferro, owes me a debt older than you. Mule train, no questions. He'll take you over the pass."

Ilex looked at the keeper and then, deliberately, at the door. At the place where the captain had stood. And Ilex spoke — not the common tongue, which sat in the mouth like borrowed clothing, but the other one, the true one, the words arriving whole and certain:

"*Keth-ra-iol. Vess-ah-lume.*"

Tamsin went white. "Don't—"

"He isn't a man," Ilex said, in the common tongue now, stumbling but sure. "The captain. His shadow leans the wrong way. Did you see? The snow wouldn't shine where he stood. He isn't hunting for the king. He *is* the king — or the thing the king wears. It eats what people remember. That's why he said *remembered*. That's why—" The child's voice cracked, steadied. "That's why my words woke up. It's getting closer to the last of them. To me. To the Spire-tongue. When everyone who remembers is eaten, there's only me left, and then it comes for me, and then it has everything."

Tamsin stared. Something moved behind the keeper's eyes — fear, yes, but beneath the fear a door swinging open onto a room long locked.

"How do you know that?" Tamsin asked quietly. "Who told you?"

"No one told me." Ilex touched the dragon-scale at their throat, warm as a held coin. "It's in the words. The words remember. That's what they're *for*."

Outside, far down the valley, the dogs began again — but differently now, ragged and frightened, and one by one they fell silent, farm by farm, as though something were walking the valley asking questions and the answers were being taken away.

Tamsin rose, and began, without another word, to pull the travel-chest from beneath the bed.

Ilex watched the keeper pack and understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that the quiet life behind this door had just ended — that the keeper had lied beautifully to a monster and it hadn't mattered, because monsters don't need proof, only time. Fear sat in the child's chest like swallowed river-stone.

But beneath the fear, something else was waking. The same thing that had woken in the words. Resolve, sharp and small and bright as the dragon-scale.

*Run,* the woman had said. But running was only half of it. The words had a destination. The scale grew warmer against Ilex's skin with every hour, tugging northward, upward, toward the mountains and whatever waited there — and the child understood that they were not fleeing toward safety at all.

They were carrying the fire to the flame.
