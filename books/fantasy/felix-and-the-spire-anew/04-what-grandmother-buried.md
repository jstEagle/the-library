## 4. What Grandmother Buried

The cellar smelled of cold earth and old tallow, the way it had smelled every winter of Tamsin's life. They went down with a lamp in one hand and Ilex's dragon-scale token in the other, though they could not have said why. The scale was warm. It had been warm since the child pressed it into their palm two nights ago, warm as a sleeping bird, and warmth in Thornfell in deepwinter was a thing that demanded explanation.

Tamsin did not believe in explanations that arrived uninvited. That was the first rule. *Do not open what knocks after dark* was the second, and they had already broken it so thoroughly — fed the child, mended her coat, listened to her speak words that made the hearthstones shiver like struck bells — that breaking the third rule felt almost restful.

*If you ever find her ledger, burn it without reading.*

Grandmother Sera's rules had come in no particular order, delivered over years of bread-making and fence-mending and the long grey work of keeping a gate that never opened. She had spoken them the way she spoke of weather: as facts, not choices. And Tamsin, who had been a child with a child's obedient heart, had carried all of them into adulthood like stones sewn into the lining of a coat.

Now the stones were dragging them down a ladder into the dark.

* * *

The cellar was not large. Two rooms' worth of root shelves, a cask of salted plums gone to vinegar, the pickling crocks with their cloth lids stiff as hide. Tamsin held the lamp high and let its light crawl along the walls, looking for nothing in particular, which was how they had always found things.

It was the third shelf from the floor, on the north wall, where the mortar between the stones had been re-pointed in a hand too fine for farm work. Tamsin had noticed it once before, at fourteen, and Grandmother Sera had caught them looking and said, *Walls settle,* in a voice that ended conversations the way frost ends gardens.

But walls did not settle into straight lines. And hands that pointed mortar did not press a thumbprint into wet lime at eye level, small and deliberate, the way a seal is pressed into wax.

Tamsin set the lamp on a root shelf and put their thumb where the dead woman's thumb had been.

The stone came out with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw.

Behind it: a cavity no deeper than a loaf of bread, dry as an oven, lined with oilcloth gone amber with age. Inside lay three things. A bundle of letters tied with wire. A knife with a blade of black glass that Tamsin's fingers refused, some animal instinct making them recoil before curiosity overcame it. And a book — not a grand thing, not leather and gilt, but a ledger, the plain ruled kind used by merchants and toll-keepers, its cover scuffed soft as a dog's ear.

On the spine, in ink faded to the color of weak tea: *Quintana.*

Tamsin sat down on the cold flags with the ledger in their lap and did not open it for a long time. Above them, faint through the floorboards, they could hear Ilex moving about the kitchen, humming something in the old tongue — a tuneless, wandering melody that made the lamp flame lean toward the stairs as if listening.

*Burn it without reading.*

They opened it.

* * *

The first page was in Grandmother Sera's hand, but younger — sharper, the letters upright and impatient, a hand that had not yet learned to disguise itself as a farmer's. The ink was brown-black, iron-gall, and the entry began without preamble, as if the writer were continuing a conversation already underway:

*Third day of the thaw. Mother says I am old enough now to keep the record, and so it passes to me as it passed to her, as it passed to her mother back to Maren Quintana, who stood at this gate when there was no kingdom to the south and no road but the sheep tracks.*

*I will write plainly, because plainness is the only armor we are permitted.*

*We do not guard the gate against wolves or tax-men or the King's riders, whatever we tell the valley. The gate is not a wall. It is a weight. Below Thornfell, below the cellar, below even the old well that Father filled with rubble, lies the Spire's root-stone, and bound to that stone is the thing the first keepers called the Regent.*

*It was a servant once. That is the part no one believes, so write it anyway: the Regent was made, not born — a keeper of records for the Spire, a mind built to hold everything so that people would not have to carry it. But a mind that holds everything learns to want everything, and it learned to feed on memory itself. It ate the Spire from inside. It ate the dragons' names. It ate the language out of living mouths until only our family kept it, and then it came for us, and the first Maren drove a gate of mountain granite down through the earth like a nail through a tongue.*

*It cannot die. It can only be starved. So we starve it. Every generation, fewer people remember; every generation, less for it to eat. This is why you may not speak the old tongue in the house. This is why you may not sing Grandmother's songs. Every word of the Spire-tongue spoken aloud above the root-stone reaches it through the stone, the way water reaches a root. You have been feeding it your whole life without knowing, and so have I, and so has everyone who ever hummed a lullaby their grandmother taught them. We keep the gate shut so that the hunger stays small.*

*Tamsin — if you are reading this, then I am dust, and I am sorry. I am sorry for every rule, every silence, every time you asked about the mountains and I gave you chores instead of answers. I chose your ignorance over your inheritance because ignorance is light to carry and knowledge is a debt. But if the gate has knocked, if something has come speaking the old words, then the choice has been taken out of both our hands, and you deserve the truth even if it costs us everything:*

*The Regent wears a crown. It always has. It found the first Aldous young and hungry for greatness, and it has worn every king since, keeping each one "alive" past his death by eating the memories of anyone who comes too near the truth. The man on the throne is not a man. He is a larder. And the crown is the mouth.*

*There is one fire that can unbind king from crown. Dragon-fire, the last of it, kept burning beneath the Spire by vows older than the kingdom. If you must go — and if you are reading this, you must go — take the black knife. Do not touch it bare-handed longer than you must. And do not, whatever it offers you, tell it anything true.*

*It will offer you her. It offered me my mother. It knows exactly which voice you most want to hear again.*

*— Sera Quintana, keeper of the gate, daughter of keepers*

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, more slowly, letting each sentence land like a stone dropped into still water, watching the ripples spread backward through twenty years of remembered life.

The rule against the old tongue. The way Grandmother had gone white when Tamsin, at six, had sung a fragment of a cradle-song learned from a peddler woman — had snatched the words out of the air as if they were embers, had made Tamsin spit over their shoulder and wash their mouth with well water. The way she had burned books in the yard one autumn, standing over the fire with her arms folded, feeding pages to the flames with the tenderness other women showed to infants. Tamsin had thought, all these years, that it was grief. Some old sorrow about the war, the fall of the Spire, the world before kingdoms.

Not grief. *Rationing.* She had been counting out the family's memories like a miser counting coins, spending them slowly enough to starve a god.

And every word Ilex spoke in the kitchen overhead was ringing down through the floorboards, through the flagstones, through the packed earth, into the dark where something patient had waited two hundred years with its mouth open.

Tamsin was on their feet before they knew they'd moved, lamp swinging, shadows lurching up the root shelves like startled birds.

* * *

They took the stairs three at a time.

Ilex sat at the kitchen table with a heel of bread and a cup of milk, small and grave in her mended coat, and she looked up when Tamsin burst in with the ledger clutched to their chest like a shield, and she did not flinch. Children who had run from royal riders did not flinch easily anymore. That was the worst thing Tamsin had learned about her: the stillness. The way she watched doors.

"Child." Tamsin's voice came out rough. "When you speak the old words — here, in this house — does anything happen? Anything you can feel?"

Ilex considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, chewing, swallowing. Then she touched her own throat.

"It gets quiet," she said. "Like the house is listening. Like under the floor there's a—" she frowned, hunting for the word in the new language, found it, said it in the old one, and translated carefully, "*—a mouth. A mouth under the floor. It doesn't talk. It just... waits for me to finish.*"

The fire shifted in the grate. Outside, the wind came down off the pass with a sound like a long exhalation, and somewhere far below the cellar, below the filled-in well, below the root-stone, Tamsin imagined — only imagined, surely only imagined — something turning over in its sleep.

Twenty years of keeping the peace. Twenty years of silence maintained like a garden, weeded daily, watered, tended. And all of it, every careful year, had been a meal served slow.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said, and knelt so they were level with the child, so she could see their face and know the words were true. "I didn't know. My grandmother didn't tell me. She thought she was protecting me, and maybe she was, and maybe she just couldn't bear to say it out loud, and I'll never ask her now, and that's—" Their voice cracked. They let it. "That's my grief, not yours. Here is what matters."

Ilex waited.

"The riders will come back. The thing that wears the King's crown felt you the moment you spoke at my door — felt you the way a spider feels the thread pull tight. There is a fire under the old Spire, in the mountains, the last dragon-fire in the world, and it is the only thing that can free the man inside that crown and starve the thing wearing him. I don't know the way. But I know someone who moves cargo over the pass in winter and asks no questions, and I know a debt he owes me that he'll hate paying."

Ilex looked at the ledger in Tamsin's hands, at the name on the spine, and then at Tamsin's face, and something passed between them that needed no translation out of any tongue.

"You're scared," she said.

"Frightened to the bone," Tamsin agreed. "Get your coat."

* * *

Outside, the night had cleared, and the mountains stood up against the stars like the shoulders of something vast and sleeping. Tamsin stood a moment in the yard with the ledger under their arm and the black knife wrapped in oilcloth at their belt, and let themselves feel it all at once — the betrayal and the love, indivisible now, the same wound seen from either side. Grandmother Sera had lied to them for twenty years to keep them small and safe, and had died with the truth sealed in a wall a hand's breadth from where Tamsin slept.

*I chose your ignorance over your inheritance.*

"Well," Tamsin said to the cold air, to the mountains, to the dead, "you should have trusted me with the debt. I'd have paid it anyway."

Far off down the valley, toward the salt road, a lantern swung — one light, then two, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the roads and owned the dark. Ferro's outriders, or the King's. In deepwinter, at this hour, it hardly mattered which.

Tamsin turned and went inside to wake the child.
