## 5. The Smuggler's Bargain

The thaw came early that year, and it came mean.

Tamsin had watched the mountain's moods for twenty years from the Thornfell gatehouse window, and they knew what this one meant: three days of warm rain peeling the snow off the high shoulders of the pass, then a hard freeze to seal it all into blue glass. After that, no road. No road until the melt proper, and by the melt proper, whoever hunted Ilex would have had a season to close every valley mouth like a fist.

So there was no choice in it, really. There was only the question Tamsin kept circling at night while the child slept in the loft above, breathing small and even as a banked fire: *who*.

There was exactly one answer, and his name was Ferro Vasq, and Tamsin owed him a debt twenty years old and ugly as a scar.

* * *

They found him where he always wintered — the low tavern at Marrowbridge, half inn, half warehouse, smelling of wet wool, tallow, and the particular sourness of men who made their living on roads that didn't officially exist. Ferro sat at his usual table with his back to the wall, a habit Tamsin recognized because it was their own habit too, and across from him an empty chair that everyone else in the room understood was not empty so much as reserved.

He hadn't changed much. Grey had come into his beard like frost into a hedgerow, and there were new lines around his eyes, but the eyes themselves were the same: brown, patient, and missing nothing. He'd been handsome once, in the way of weathered things.

"Gatekeeper," he said, without surprise. "Twenty years, and you still walk like someone counting exits."

"You taught me that."

"I taught you how to pack a mule so it wouldn't scream on scree." He nudged the reserved chair out with his boot. "Sit. You only come down from your rock when you want something, and you only want something when it's bad."

Tamsin sat. The tavern noise closed around them — dice, argument, a fiddle badly tuned — a curtain of ordinary sound behind which dangerous conversations could be conducted at reasonable volume. This, too, was Ferro's craft. He owned rooms the way other men owned knives.

"I need the train," Tamsin said. "Mules, guides, the high route over Ashen Bells. Before the freeze."

Ferro turned his cup slowly on the table, once, twice, a full rotation, watching it as if it might tell him something. "Ashen Bells is closed by any sane measure."

"I'm asking for the insane measure."

"The insane measure costs." He looked up. "And it costs more than coin, because coin I can get anywhere, and what you're carrying over Ashen Bells in the last week of safe passage is going to be worth killing for, or you wouldn't be sitting in my tavern looking like a woman who hasn't slept since the solstice."

Tamsin said nothing. That was the old game, and Ferro knew it, and let the silence stretch exactly long enough to be felt before he broke it himself.

"The debt," he said. "You're here to spend the debt."

"I'm here to ask about the debt."

"No, you're not. Asking is what you did with the letter you never sent, the one I know you wrote because you're the sort who writes letters and burns them instead of sending them. You're here to spend it. So let's talk plainly, gatekeeper, because plainness is the only currency I trust." He leaned forward, forearms on the table, and lowered his voice beneath even the fiddle. "I pulled you out of the Wrenwater flood when you were nineteen and stupid and carrying something in a satchel you wouldn't let me touch even to save it from sinking. You swore you'd owe me. I've kept that debt like a man keeps a knife — oiled, sheathed, waiting. I always figured the day you spent it, it would be for something that mattered. So before I put my people, my mules, and my own neck on a pass that eats caravans, you're going to tell me what matters this much."

"That wasn't the bargain."

"That was absolutely the bargain. You just hoped I'd forgotten." His smile was thin and genuine and entirely without mercy. "No questions, I promised you. But no questions isn't the same as no price. I don't ask what's in the packs. I do ask what I'm risking."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Around them the tavern roared on, oblivious, warm, lit yellow against the blue dark outside where the rain was already turning to sleet on the bridge stones. Somewhere up that dark, in a gatehouse under the mountains, a child slept with a dragon-scale token clutched in one fist and spoke in her sleep in a language older than the kingdom that wanted her dead.

Loyalty to the dead woman, Tamsin thought. Loyalty to Grandmother Sable, who had burned the books and barred the tongue and taught them that silence was safety, that the family survived by being nothing, by being a locked door in a landscape of locked doors. Twenty years of keeping that silence. And now the silence itself would kill the child unless it was traded away, piece by piece, to the only man with mules fit for the pass.

Grandmother would have called it betrayal. Grandmother had also lied, and Tamsin had read the ledger now, and knew what the gate was *for*, and knew that silence had never once been safety — only hunger, deferred.

"Ferro," Tamsin said quietly. "What do you remember about the Spire?"

His cup stopped turning.

It was a small thing, that stoppage, but Tamsin watched it land in him like a stone dropped down a well — the pause, the flicker, the way his gaze went briefly somewhere far away and long ago. Everyone in the borderlands remembered the Spire the way you remember a story you were told as a child to frighten you: the black needle on the far horizon, the king who never aged, the crown that ate memories. Children's tales. Except children's tales didn't usually make smugglers go quiet.

"I remember it's a story," Ferro said at last. "A good one. Mothers use it to keep kids off the moors."

"It's not a story."

"No?"

"No." Tamsin set both hands flat on the table, an old gesture, a keeper's gesture, though they hadn't known that's what it was until a week ago. "And I can prove it, but the proof is asleep in my gatehouse, and she is eight years old, and she speaks the Spire-tongue the way you speak Low Common — born to it, easy in it, humming the stones with it. And the king's riders are in the valley asking after her with silver in one hand and worse than steel in the other, and I have maybe four days before the pass freezes shut for a season, and you are the only man alive whose mules have crossed Ashen Bells in worse."

The fiddle scraped. Dice rattled. Ferro didn't move.

"A child," he said finally.

"A child."

"Speaking a dead language."

"Not dead. Just buried. There's a difference, and I've recently learned it the hard way." Tamsin held his gaze. "You want the truth about what we're carrying? Here it is, all of it, because you're right — you should know what you're risking. She's a key. Not metaphorically. Something old and hungry wears the king's crown, and it survives by eating the past out of anyone who learns too much, and that child is the one thing in the world that can unbind it. There's a fire under the Spire — the last dragon-fire — and it's the only flame hot enough to part a king from his crown. I'm taking her to it. That's the cargo. That's the whole of it."

Silence. Ferro looked at Tamsin the way he must once have looked at maps of routes nobody believed existed — measuring, weighing, deciding whether the thing drawn on the paper could possibly be real and, if it was, whether the reward justified the dying.

Then he laughed. It was a short, harsh sound, mostly breath, and there was no humor in it at all.

"Twenty years ago," he said, "you were carrying something in a satchel you wouldn't let me touch. I never asked. I've wondered about it ever since — through two marriages, one divorce, and more close calls than I've got fingers for. And now you sit down and tell me the world's true shape in the space of a cup of wine." He shook his head slowly. "Was it the same thing? Back then? In the satchel?"

"Books," Tamsin said. "Spire-books. My grandmother's. I was supposed to burn them. I couldn't. I hid them in the Wrenwater instead, and you fished me out of the flood with them, and I let you believe whatever you believed because the truth was worth less than your silence."

"And now?"

"Now the truth is the only thing worth anything at all."

Ferro sat back. He rubbed his jaw, and Tamsin watched the smuggler and the debt-holder and the young man he'd once been all argue behind his eyes, and waited, because waiting was also a skill the mountains taught.

"The debt covers the crossing," he said at last. "Mules, guides, my share of the road. That's spent — consider it spent the moment you walked in, because I know you, and you'd have died on that pass alone rather than come ask twice." He raised a hand before Tamsin could speak. "But hear the rest. The debt buys the road. It doesn't buy my people walking blind into a war with the crown. If Regent-bound soldiers hit us on the high route — and they will, because you don't move a living key over a mountain without the thing that wants it noticing — my people need to know what they're fighting. Not everything. Enough. Names, faces, what the wrongness looks like. You'll brief them yourself, at the trailhead, and you'll stand in front of them while you do it, because they'll take it from you and not from me."

"All right."

"And one more thing." Ferro's voice dropped further, almost gentle, which from Ferro was more frightening than anger. "The child. When it's done — whichever way it ends — the truth comes out. All of it. The Spire, the crown, the fire, the lot. Because I've spent twenty years running goods on roads that exist because everyone agreed to forget why they exist, and if what you just told me is real, then the forgetting has a *teeth*, gatekeeper, and I'd like to live in a world where it doesn't. So that's my price. Not silver. Not the debt. A promise: when this is over, whatever's left of over, you tell it. Loudly. To everyone."

Tamsin thought of the ledger in the cellar. Of the first entry, in Grandmother Sable's careful hand, naming the Regent. Of twenty years of a locked door.

"I promise," they said. "On the gate."

"On the gate," Ferro repeated, and something in his face eased, as if that oath meant more to him than any coin. Then he pushed back his chair and stood, and the smuggler reassembled over his face like armor going on. "We move tomorrow, first grey light. Three mules, six of my best, the north approach to Ashen Bells. Dress the child as a boy, dye the hair if it's marked, and teach her to keep her mouth shut in that language of hers — because if she hums the stones within a hundred miles of a bell-tower, every bound thing on this mountain will turn its head like a hound scenting blood."

"She understands danger better than either of us does."

"Good. She can understand frostbite too." He tossed a coin to the barkeep for the wine neither of them had drunk, and paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the tavern's yellow light. "Gatekeeper. One question I am allowed, as a friend and not a creditor."

"Ask."

"This fire under the Spire. The one that parts kings from crowns." His eyes were steady and very tired. "What's it cost to feed?"

Tamsin had read enough of the ledger by now to know the answer, and hated it, and had told no one yet, not even the child.

"Everything you love most," they said. "Freely given. Permanently."

Ferro nodded slowly, absorbing that the way a man absorbs a diagnosis. "Well," he said. "Then let's hope you love cheaply."

Outside, the sleet had thickened against the bridge stones, and far up the valley, faint beneath the wind, Tamsin heard — or imagined hearing — the first far bell of the pass tolling once, as if the mountain itself had felt them coming, and had begun, already, to count.
