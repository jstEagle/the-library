## 7. The Pass of Ashen Bells

The bells came first.

Ilex heard them before anyone else — thin, cracked notes drifting down from the heights, where a line of rusted iron bells hung from the pass's old waymarkers. Pilgrim-bells, Ferro called them, hung by people who had believed the mountains listened. The wind moved through them now, and they did not ring so much as *rattle*, like teeth in a dead man's jaw.

Ilex knew what the sound meant. Not the wind. Not the bells.

*Them.*

They had been three days in the high country, and Ilex had learned the shape of the mule train the way they'd once learned the shape of the cellar under Thornfell: by heart, by dark, by listening. Six mules. Ferro's cough, deep and wet, worse in the cold. Tamsin's boots on scree, always half a step behind Ilex, always there when Ilex turned around too fast and expected to be alone. And underneath it all, pressed against Ilex's chest inside their coat, the dragon-scale, warm as a sleeping bird.

It was warmer today. It had been getting warmer for two days, and last night it had been almost hot, and Ilex had not slept, because the scale only burned when *he* was thinking about them.

*I am coming for my ember.* The words had arrived in the night without sound, the way the Spire-tongue sometimes arrived, in that older grammar beneath language. Ilex had sat up in the frozen dark of the tent with their heart going like a trapped moth and had not told Tamsin, because what would be the use? There was nowhere left to run but up.

"Boy." Ferro reined his lead mule around, squinting back down the trail. "You've gone grey as the rock. What is it?"

"The bells," Ilex said. Their voice came out small. They hated how small. "They're not ringing right."

Ferro listened. His face did something complicated — the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Wind," he said, but he said it the way Tamsin lied to soldiers, smooth and hollow, and everyone on the mountain knew it was a lie.

Tamsin came up the line fast. They had their hood back and their hair plastered to their forehead with sweat despite the cold, and their eyes went first to Ilex's chest, where the scale's heat must have shown even through wool, a faint wrong glow like a coal breathing.

"How long?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"Since last night."

Tamsin closed their eyes for one breath. When they opened them, all the softness was gone out of them, packed away somewhere deep, and what remained was the gatekeeper, the one who had spent twenty years learning every stone between Thornfell and the sky. "Ferro. The narrows ahead — the place with the fallen archway. How far?"

"Half a mile. Maybe less."

"And past it?"

"Open ground to the saddle. No cover worth a damn."

"Then we don't go past it." Tamsin was already untying the mules' leads, hands moving quick and sure. "We hold the narrows. Rock at our backs, rock above them. If they come, they come in ones and twos or not at all."

"They'll come in numbers," Ferro said. "They always do."

"Then we make the numbers expensive."

Ilex stood very still while the adults became something else — something older than smuggler and gatekeeper, something from the time before the kingdom taught everyone to forget. They watched Tamsin pull a long knife from beneath their coat, a blade Ilex had never seen in all the weeks since the gate, and watched Ferro check a crossbow with the bored tenderness of a man greeting an old dog.

"You should hide me," Ilex said. "That's what you were going to do. Put me in a crack in the rock and fight, and if you lose, I run."

Tamsin crouched to their level. Up here, against the white sky, the gatekeeper looked ancient and tired and terribly awake. "I'm done hiding you," Tamsin said. "Hiding is how all this started. Someone hid something, and someone buried something, and twenty years later a child knocks on my door speaking the truth into a world that starves on lies. No more hiding. We stand where we stand."

The scale pulsed against Ilex's ribs, once, twice, like a second heart learning the rhythm of fear.

Then the bells above the narrows all rang at once — hard, struck, no wind could strike them — and the soldiers came over the ridge like water finding a crack.

* * *

There were nine of them. Ilex counted because counting was the only prayer Ilex knew. Nine shapes in grey-and-iron, moving too smoothly for men climbing scree, faces slack as unbaked clay, and above each brow a faint shine where skin met steel — the mark, the crown's fingerprint, the hum made visible. Ilex had seen the captain's shadow move wrong in the valley; these shadows didn't move wrong. These shadows didn't move at all. They pooled beneath the soldiers' feet like spilled ink refusing the sun.

"Hold the narrows!" Ferro roared, and his crossbow spoke, and the first soldier folded backward off the trail with a bolt through the throat — and did not cry out. That was the worst part, Ilex thought, pressing into a crevice with the scale burning through their coat. That none of them cried out. Men fell like furniture. Like things being put away.

A soldier reached Ferro. Then another. The old smuggler fought with a short hatchet and thirty years of dirty tricks, laughing — actually laughing — until a sword took him through the meat of the shoulder and spun him against the mules, and the mules screamed and broke their leads, and the whole narrow world came apart into noise and snow-dust and blood steaming on stone.

"TAMSIN!" Ilex screamed it in the Spire-tongue without meaning to, the old word for *help*, for *shelter*, for *the door that opens*, and the stones of the narrows *hummed*.

And Tamsin answered.

Later, Ilex would try to remember it clearly and fail, because it happened the way lightning happens — whole, blinding, already over. Tamsin came off the rocks like something the mountain itself had released. Twenty years of keeping peace, twenty years of opening gates and closing them, of lying gently and locking gently, and all of it broke at once. The long knife moved and a soldier dropped it and his hand with it. Another came on and Tamsin went *inside* his reach, low, and did something short and terrible, and the soldier sat down against the wall of the pass as if tired, and did not get up.

But there were nine, and two were down, and seven is a number that swallows two.

One of them got past everything. Got past Ferro bleeding and roaring, got past Tamsin turning, and its slack face found Ilex in the crevice, and its arm rose, and Ilex saw the shine above its brows brighten like a lamp being trimmed — the Regent looking through it, *seeing* through it, seeing the ember it had whispered to the mountains about—

The scale flared white-hot against Ilex's chest.

The soldier stopped. Mid-stride, mid-swing, stopped like a puppet whose string had been plucked, and its head tilted, and its mouth opened, and what came out was not a soldier's voice but something layered underneath it, vast and patient and horribly gentle:

"*Little ember.* You are carrying what belongs to the crown."

"Ilex!" Tamsin's voice, raw, from behind it. "*Down!*"

Ilex dropped. The long knife passed over the space where their head had been and buried itself in the soldier's eye to the hilt, and the thing wearing the soldier shuddered, and the light above its brows guttered like a candle in wind, and it fell, and the fall sounded like nothing, like a sack, like the end of a conversation.

Silence, then. Snow ticking on stone. A mule groaning somewhere below the trail. Ferro sitting propped against the archway's fallen pillar, one hand pressed to his shoulder, blood coming through his fingers slow and steady, his face grey and furious about being grey.

Tamsin stood among the bodies, chest heaving, the knife hanging loose in their hand. Seven soldiers. Some breathing still, some not. And Ilex watched the gatekeeper look down at their own hands — at the knife, at the red on it — with an expression Ilex had never seen on any adult's face and hoped never to understand fully: the look of a person watching the life they built burn down around them and finding, underneath the ashes, that they are still standing.

"Twenty years," Tamsin said softly. To no one. To the mountain. "Twenty years I kept the peace. Nobody died at my gate. Nobody—" Their voice caught and snapped. They knelt abruptly beside the nearest body, pressed two fingers to its throat though the slack face and the ink-pool shadow had already said everything, and stayed there a moment with their head bowed, as if in apology. As if asking forgiveness from a man who had none left inside him to give it.

"It wasn't you," Ilex said. Their voice shook. They made it stop shaking. "It was the crown. He wears them. He *eats* them. You set them down."

"I killed men, Ilex."

"You freed men." The words came out of Ilex in the old tongue first, and then, deliberately, in the common speech, so there could be no mistaking them. "You *freed* them. That's what the word means. I can show you. It's in the language. Everything true is in the language."

Tamsin looked up at them then — kneeling in the bloody snow, hood fallen back, older than the mountains and suddenly young — and something in their face shifted and settled, like a lock turning after decades.

"Ferro," Tamsin said hoarsely, rising. "How bad?"

"Shoulder's opened up. Missed the bone, mostly missed the important parts." Ferro grinned through the grey, which cost him visibly. "Mostly. But I'll tell you what's bad, gatekeeper. That thing talked through that soldier. Which means the Regent's close enough to spend its puppets' mouths. Which means the rest of the garrison isn't chasing us anymore." He jerked his chin toward the saddle beyond the narrows, toward the open ground and the far white shoulders of the Spire's mountain, faintly visible through the snow-haze like a tooth of the sky. "It's *waiting* for us. Every road from here goes through its front room."

Tamsin bound Ferro's shoulder with strips of tent-canvas and the last of the spirit-smell, jaw tight, hands steady now. Ilex gathered what the scattered mules had left — a little food, the fire-kit, the rope — and tried not to look at the bodies, and failed, and made themselves look anyway, because the grandmother's ledger had taught Tamsin that burying things was how the Regent won, and Ilex was beginning to understand that this was true of more than books.

They left the narrows as the light went copper behind the peaks. At the mouth of the pass, Ilex turned once. The ash-bells hung silent above the archway, crusted with frost, and beneath them the snow held nine dark stains that the wind was already patiently erasing.

"The bells," Ilex said. "Why are they called the Pass of Ashen Bells? Ferro never said."

Tamsin paused, one hand on the rock, and for a moment the gatekeeper's face was very far away.

"Because," Tamsin said, "a hundred years ago, pilgrims climbed this pass carrying fire from the Spire to relight the world. And the crown sent soldiers to meet them. And when it was over, the bells had all melted — except nobody could find any ash. The fire took the bells *and* the burning." They looked up at the silent iron. "My grandmother used to say the mountain kept the ash. Kept it for whoever came next, carrying fire again."

Ilex pressed their hand flat against their chest, against the warm, patient weight of the scale.

Ahead, through the thinning snow, the Spire's mountain stood waiting — and along its highest ridge, impossibly far away, a single point of light kindled and died, kindled and died, like something enormous blinking awake.

Or like a signal.

Or like a welcome.
