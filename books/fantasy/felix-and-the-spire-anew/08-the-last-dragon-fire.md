## 8. The Last Dragon-Fire

The mountain stopped being a mountain three days into the climb, or so it seemed to Tamsin. The paths narrowed to ledges where the wind came off the ice fields with the smell of old iron, and the rock itself changed — darker, veined with something that caught the light like cooling metal. Ilex walked ahead without fear, small boots finding purchase on stone that should have been treacherous, and Tamsin understood, watching, that the child was not climbing toward the Spire.

The child was being *remembered* by it.

"Does it hurt?" Tamsin asked on the second night, huddled against a shelf of granite while sleet hissed past. "The way it pulls at you."

Ilex considered this with the grave patience that had unsettled Tamsin from the first midnight knock. "It's like when you almost remember a dream," the child said. "But someone else is doing the remembering. And they're glad."

Tamsin did not sleep well after that.

* * *

They found the entrance on the morning of the fourth day, though no one could have called it a door. A seam in the mountainside, tall as a cathedral arch, half-drowned in a fall of frozen meltwater — and through the ice, faint as a coal under ash, a red glow.

"The drowned forge," Tamsin said, and their own voice sounded foreign to them, older, as if the words had been waiting in their mouth since childhood for exactly this threshold. Grandmother's ledger had named it. *Beneath the Spire, where the fire sleeps in its cradle. Fed by vows alone. Do not go unless the gate must open.*

Well. The gate must open.

The ice parted for them — not melted, but *unzipping*, seams of water running down the face like the mountain weeping — and they stepped through into a dark that was not empty. It was full. Tamsin felt it press against their skin like the inside of a bell that had just been struck: every sound they made came back to them layered with other sounds, other footsteps, other breaths, centuries of keepers walking this same passage toward the same light.

Ilex's hand found theirs. Small, cold, certain.

"It knows your name," the child whispered. "It's saying it wrong, though. Like the stones used to."

*Like the stones used to.* Tamsin's throat closed. In the house below Thornfell, the stones had hummed sometimes at night, and grandmother had thrown salt in the hearth and said it was settling. Twenty years of salt. Twenty years of telling themselves the humming was nothing.

They descended.

* * *

The forge opened beneath them like a held breath finally let go.

It was vast beyond sense — a cavern where the ceiling vanished upward into darkness and the floor was black glass, smooth as a frozen lake, and at its center, sunk in a cradle of stone carved with spirals within spirals within spirals, burned the last dragon-fire.

It was dying. That was the first thing Tamsin understood, and grief hit them like a physical blow, which made no sense because they had never seen it before, had never known it existed until a week ago — and still they grieved, the way one grieves a grandparent one never met but whose absence shaped the whole house. The flame stood barely knee-high, guttering, its color wrong: red shot through with gray, like embers breathing their last under ash. Around the cradle, rings of stone seats faced it, empty, worn smooth by generations of sitting. Vow-stones, the ledger had called them. Places where keepers had knelt and given.

And everywhere — everywhere — the walls were covered in writing. The Spire-tongue, spiraling outward from the cradle in bands, in columns, in rivers, tens of thousands of lines of it, and Tamsin realized with a lurch that they could read some of it now. Not learned. *Remembered.* Names. Dates. Promises. *We kept the fire. We fed it gladly. We remember so it may burn.*

"What is it eating?" Ilex asked softly, standing at the edge of the black glass floor, and their voice did not echo right; the cavern took the words and turned them over, examining them.

"Not eating." Tamsin stepped forward, and the glass rang faintly under their boot, a note too low to hear properly, felt in the teeth. "Being fed. There's a difference. Ferro would tell you that — everything on the smugglers' roads is either taken or given, and the price tells you which."

Ferro was two days behind them, fevered, left in the care of a shepherd who asked no questions because Tamsin had paid enough silver to buy his silence twice over. The wound in the smuggler's side had been Tamsin's fault, bought with the first life they had ever taken, and the guilt of it walked beside them up every mile of this mountain, heavier than their pack. They carried it down these last steps too. It would sit with them at the fire. Everything sat with you here. That seemed to be what the place was *for*.

They crossed the black glass together, and the fire lifted as they came — just slightly, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep — and Tamsin felt it look at them. Not see. *Look.* Into.

"Oh," Tamsin said quietly. "Oh, you're starving."

The flame bent toward them like a plant toward sun.

* * *

They made camp at the cradle's rim, because there was nowhere else to go and because leaving felt unthinkable, and while Ilex slept — truly slept, for the first time since the pass, curled against Tamsin's coat with the dragon-scale token clutched in one fist — Tamsin read the walls.

By the light of the failing fire they traced the spiral inscriptions, and the knowledge rose in them like water finding an old channel. This was the inheritance grandmother had burned books to bury: not power, not secrets worth killing for. Just *duty*, written out in beautiful, patient script by hundreds of hands across hundreds of years.

The fire had been great once. The walls told of it plainly: a flame that stood taller than the cradle, that roared up the mountain's spine and lit the snows red at dusk, that could unmake bindings laid by crowns and oaths and hungering things. The Regent could not have survived a single day of it. No memory-eater could; the fire burned only what was freely given, and it consumed falsehood the way ordinary flame consumes paper.

But kingdoms had come. Kingdoms always came. And a king — Aldous's grandfather, or perhaps Aldous himself in another wall's telling; the records blurred there, deliberately, Tamsin thought, blurred by hands that loved him — had feared a fire that could unbind crowns. So the vows had been forbidden. The keepers scattered. The feeding stopped.

And the fire, which could take nothing, began to starve.

Tamsin sat back on their heels before the cradle, and the truth assembled itself in their chest with terrible simplicity.

To feed it, someone must give it a true memory. Willingly. Permanently. Not a story told, not a coin dropped in an offering box — a piece of the self, surrendered whole, gone forever, the way a limb is gone. The walls were explicit about this. The vows carved nearest the cradle, the oldest ones, ended the same way every time:

*I give this and do not ask it back. I will not know what I have lost. Let it burn.*

There was no smaller version. No partial payment. The fire ran on truth, and truth does not come in installments.

Tamsin looked at Ilex sleeping, and understood why the child had been sent. Why the token, why the tongue, why the knock at midnight. The fire needed feeding, and the only ones who could feed it were those who remembered enough to know what they were giving — and the court had spent twenty years making sure no one remembered anything.

Except a keeper's grandchild. Except a child who *was* memory, living, walking, speaking the old tongue.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to the fire, to the walls, to the dead keepers whose patient script lined the dark. "Not the child."

The flame flickered. Waited. It had waited twenty years; it could wait out one stubborn keeper's refusal.

* * *

Dawn — if dawn could be said to exist this far under the mountain; the light simply changed, the dark going from black to a deep bruised blue — found Tamsin kneeling at the cradle's edge, turning their own life over like a peddler's tray, examining each piece.

What would they give?

Their mother's face — no. Too soon lost, too little held; surrendering it would leave them with almost nothing of her at all. The smell of the smugglers' roads in spring, wet pine and mule sweat and freedom — no; that was the self they'd built, the twenty quiet years, and to lose it might unravel all the rest. The taste of stolen plums behind the Thornfell gatehouse, summer they were nine —

— and there it was. Sitting in the middle of the tray, unmistakable, the way the truest things always are.

Grandmother's voice. Singing.

Not the stern voice that forbade the old tongue, not the voice that recited rules and threw salt on hearths and locked cellars. The other one. The one Tamsin had heard only a handful of times, late at night, half-dreaming, drifting up through the floorboards: a song in the Spire-tongue, low and looping, a lullaby that was also — Tamsin knew now, reading the walls — a keeping-vow, sung to a child too young to understand she was singing to the last keeper of the last fire. *Sleep, the stones are humming, sleep, the gate holds fast...*

Grandmother had sung the truth to them her whole life and forbidden them to repeat it. She had buried the inheritance alive in the one person strong enough to dig it back up. Perhaps that had been cruelty. Perhaps love wearing cruelty's coat. Tamsin would never ask her now.

But they could give the fire the one true thing they had of her. The voice, the song, the memory entire — the floorboards, the candle-smell, the ache of hearing something beautiful and forbidden and not knowing why. All of it, permanently. And live on without it, the way the walls promised the givers lived on: whole, but holed, like a tooth with the nerve drawn.

"I'll do it," Tamsin said. "But not yet. When the time comes. When it can reach the crown."

The fire bowed low, and the whole cavern sighed — a long exhalation through ten thousand carved throats, the mountain itself acknowledging the vow. Somewhere above them, far up the mountain's spine, Tamsin felt rather than heard a channel open: a chimney of stone running from this cradle to the crown of the world, waiting to carry flame.

Waiting to carry it to a king who did not yet know he was about to be freed.

Ilex stirred against Tamsin's coat and opened their eyes, and looked at the fire, and then at Tamsin's face, and whatever the child saw there made them frown.

"You've decided something sad," Ilex said.

"I've decided something necessary. There's a difference." Tamsin pulled the coat tighter around them both. "Sleep, little key. Tomorrow we learn how to light a fuse that runs up a mountain."

Above them, through miles of stone, something in the high dark shifted — a pressure, a presence, hungry and vast and suddenly attentive, like a wolf lifting its head at a scent on the wind.

The Regent had felt the fire wake.

And it was coming.
