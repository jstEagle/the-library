## 9. Unbinding

The forge was drowning when they found it.

Not in water — in silence. It lay beneath the Spire's roots, a vault of black stone where the air itself felt old, and at its center cradled in a basin carved like an open hand, the last dragon-fire guttered. A flame no larger than a candle. Blue at its heart, gold at its edges, trembling as though it had been holding its breath for three hundred years.

Tamsin stood at the threshold and could not make their feet move.

Twenty years they had kept the Thornfell gate. Twenty years of oiling hinges and sweeping snow and telling themselves the world behind them was ash, that the old tongue was dead, that grandmother's rules were simply rules and not walls built around a grave. Now the grave stood open before them, and inside it burned the only fire in the world that could unmake a crown.

"It's so small," Ilex whispered. The child's voice echoed strangely in the vault, and the stones answered — not words, but a hum, low and patient, the sound Tamsin had first heard on a winter midnight when a knock came at the door.

"Fires are always small," Tamsin said. "Before they aren't."

The ledger had told them what to do. Grandmother's handwriting, cramped and furious in the final pages: *It does not take wood. It does not take blood. It takes what you will not give back. Feed it truth, and it will burn until there is nothing left to feed it.* Below that, in ink gone brown with age: *I could not do it. I chose the gate instead. Forgive me.*

Tamsin had read those lines four times on the climb and hated her anew each time — and loved her too, which was worse, because love made the betrayal fit inside the grief instead of shattering it.

They descended the worn steps to the basin's edge. Up close, the flame made no heat. It made *recognition*. Standing before it was like standing before someone who had known you as a child and was waiting, kindly, for you to remember why you'd come.

"Ilex." Tamsin knelt. "When this begins, you must go up. Ferro's mule is waiting at the tree line, and Ferro is waiting on the mule, complaining, which means he's alive."

"I'm not leaving."

"You are. That's not a request. That's the last rule I have left, and I'm spending it on you."

Ilex's jaw set in a way that was far too familiar — the same stubborn architecture Tamsin saw in their own reflection. But the child reached into their coat and pressed the dragon-scale into Tamsin's palm instead. Warm. Alive-warm, like a sleeping bird.

"Then hold this," Ilex said. "So some part of me stays. And when it's done—" the child's voice cracked, steadied, "—when it's done, come teach me the word for *return*. You said there was one. In the old tongue. You hummed it once and didn't know you were doing it."

Tamsin's throat closed. They closed their hand around the scale and nodded, because speech had abandoned them.

Ilex hugged them once, fierce and brief, and then climbed the stairs without looking back, which took more courage than looking back would have.

Tamsin turned to the fire alone.

*What you will not give back.*

There was only one thing. There had only ever been one thing.

Grandmother's voice, singing. Not the hymns from the sanctioned book — the other songs, the ones she sang only when she thought the house was asleep, in the language she forbade by day. Low melodies about rivers that remembered their sources. About bells that rang underground. About a king who was kind, once, before the crown learned his name. Tamsin had been six years old, feigning sleep on the kitchen bench, and grandmother had sung with her eyes closed and her hands flour-white, and for those few minutes the forbidden language had not been a sin but a lullaby, and Tamsin had understood — even then, even at six — that they were hearing the truest thing in the house.

She had caught them listening. She had never sung again. And Tamsin had carried that voice through twenty years of silence like a coal cupped in bare hands, refusing to let it die, refusing even to think of it too often lest thinking wear it thin.

That was the memory. That was the price.

"You'll take her voice," Tamsin said to the flame. Their voice sounded very small under the mountain. "And I'll still have the words she taught me. Just not the sound of her saying them."

The fire leaned toward them. Patient. Honest. It would not trick them; it did not know how.

Tamsin thought of Aldous — the man inside the crown, if any of him remained. They thought of the captain whose shadow had moved wrong. Of the riders on the salt road. Of every person who had ever forgotten something important and called the forgetting peace. The Regent ate the past to keep one man breathing, and everyone else paid the bill.

*Feed it truth, and it will burn until there is nothing left to feed it.*

"All right," Tamsin whispered. "Grandmother. Forgive me twice."

They opened their hands over the basin — palms up, the way one offers bread, the way one surrenders a blade — and gave it.

The memory rose out of them like breath in winter. Tamsin felt it leave: the kitchen, the flour, the closed eyes, the melody climbing — and then the voice itself, the grain and warmth of it, the particular roughness at the bottom of each note, lifting away, and away, and gone. What remained was the song's shape without its sound. A footprint where music had been.

Tamsin wept without noise, the way keepers do.

And the fire drank.

* * *

In the capital, in the body of a king, something changed.

Aldous had learned to live small. That was the whole art of it now — to fold himself into the corners of his own skull, behind the eyes, beneath the tongue, while the Regent wore him out into the world. He watched through his own sight the way a man watches weather through glass. He had watched the Regent smile with his mouth at ambassadors. Watched it sign decrees with his hand. Watched it eat.

It ate memories the way he had once eaten plums, greedily, carelessly, spitting out the pits. It ate the faces of servants and replaced them with smooth blanks. It ate his mother — he had felt her go, a warm room going cold — and then it ate the fact that there had ever been a warm room, and he had grieved a woman he could no longer name, and then it ate the grief, and that was the worst of all. Grief was the last proof of love. When the Regent ate your grief, it unmade the loving.

But tonight — tonight the Regent was agitated. Aldous felt it pacing along the inside of his bones like a dog before a storm. Something was moving in the mountains. Something old. The scale — he could feel it the way you feel a splinter, distant but specific — the scale was climbing toward the drowned forge, and the Regent was afraid.

*It cannot be,* the Regent said, in the voice it used inside him, which was his own voice, younger. *The fire is a legend. The fire is ash. We let it gutter ourselves, centuries ago, we starved it of vows—*

And then the fire stopped being a legend.

It came up the mountain like a shout. Aldous felt it enter the world the way you feel dawn — not seen yet, but suddenly, unmistakably *coming*. The Regent shrieked, a sound with no throat, and clamped down on everything it held, hoarding its stolen years, and for one moment the pressure of all that hoarded past cracked something open in the king's mind, and—

Lucidity.

It hit him like cold water. One hour, perhaps less — he could feel the fire burning up through the stone of the Spire, burning through the Regent's hoard, freeing memories as it came, and each freed memory was a plank of himself returned. His mother's face. His coronation, and the terror underneath the joy. The taste of rain in a year of drought. The name of his first hound. Himself, whole, arriving all at once after three hundred years of being eaten by inches.

He stood in his chamber and wept, and the tears were his own, and the Regent could not stop them.

*Stop,* it begged. *You will die. Without me you are mortal. You have been alive nine hundred years—*

"No," Aldous said aloud, to the empty room, in a voice no one had heard in centuries. "I died a long time ago. You just kept the shape walking."

He could feel the fire coming for the crown. He could feel what it would cost. The Regent was fused to him — crown to skull, skull to soul — and there was no surgery in the world that could separate them. To kill the eater was to kill the eaten.

He had one hour. Perhaps less. He spent it well.

He walked to the window and looked at the city — really looked, memorizing, though he knew the fire might take the memorizing too. He forgave the guards in the courtyard who would not remember guarding him. He forgave the kingdom that had let a hungry thing wear its king rather than ask what sat upon the throne. Forgiveness was easier than he'd expected; he supposed three hundred years of captivity taught a man that most chains were forged by both parties.

*I can run,* the Regent whispered, small now, genuinely small. *There are other heads. Other crowns. Give me a horse and I will trouble you never again—*

"There is no other head," Aldous said gently. "There is no other crown. There is only this fire, and it has been fed something truer than either of us, and it is coming home."

He went to the center of the chamber, the place where kings stood to be crowned, and he planted his feet the way a keeper plants themselves at a gate.

He thought of Tamsin Quintana — he had felt them, felt the keeper's line reaching up through the stone, felt the memory they had fed the flame: a grandmother's voice, singing. It passed through him on its way to the fire, and for a moment Aldous heard it too, a melody about rivers remembering their sources, and he understood that this stranger had given up the dearest thing they owned to free a man they had never met.

He would not waste it.

"Come, then," he said to the fire, and to the Regent, and to the long dark century between them. "Let's finish honestly."

The flame arrived.

It did not hurt. That was the mercy no chronicle had recorded — it did not hurt at all. It came up through the floor like morning through a curtain, and it found the Regent coiled in the crown, and it began, patiently, thoroughly, to unbind. Every stolen memory it tore loose and set free, sending them home across the mountains like birds released. Every year of its hunger it burned as fuel. The Regent screamed and thinned and screamed again, and Aldous stood still, exactly as he had chosen to stand, and felt himself grow lighter with everything it lost.

Near the end — near the very end, when the Regent was almost nothing, when the crown was cooling on his brow like iron after rain — Aldous felt the last of his own hours running out, and he spent his final moment not on fear but on gratitude, aimed like an arrow at a gatekeeper in the mountains he would never see:

*You gave me back myself in time to choose. No one has ever given me anything truer.*

Then the fire took the rest, and the crown fell from a skull that was already dust, and in the capital a king who had been kept alive for nine hundred years finally, quietly, gratefully, died.

* * *

Far below the Spire, in the drowned forge, Tamsin Quintana knelt beside a basin where the last dragon-fire had burned down to a single blue point, then to nothing, then to a warmth in the stone that would fade within the hour.

The vault was silent. Truly silent, for the first time in three hundred years — no hum in the stones, no hunger in the dark. Somewhere far to the south, a crown had come off a head that no longer needed saving.

Tamsin pressed the dragon-scale to their chest and tried to remember a song.

The words were there. All the words — the whole forbidden language rising in them now like water finding its level, every syllable grandmother had spoken and sung and burned books to bury. But the voice that should have carried them was gone, given willingly, permanently, to a fire that had needed it more.

Tamsin opened their mouth anyway, alone under the mountain, and sang the word for *return* into the dark — pitchless, tuneless, imperfect, true.

Above, at the tree line, a child was waiting.
