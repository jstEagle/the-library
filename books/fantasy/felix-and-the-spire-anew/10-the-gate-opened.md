## 10. The Gate, Opened

The snow went all at once, the way it does in the high valleys — as if it had only been waiting for permission.

Ilex woke on the first morning of thaw to the sound of water everywhere: dripping from the eaves of Tamsin's cottage, running in bright threads down the mountain, gathering in the ruts of the salt road until the whole world seemed to be speaking in one long silver sentence. They lay still beneath the wool blanket and listened. All winter they had learned to sleep through silence, through the particular hush of a valley holding its breath. This was different. This was the sound of a place deciding to live again.

Downstairs, the smell of barley porridge and woodsmoke. And underneath it, fainter, stranger — the smell of iron and oil, of a lock that had not been turned in twenty years.

Ilex found Tamsin standing at the back door with a ring of keys in their hand. There were many keys on it, most of them small and dull, for cupboards and shutters and the chest where the winter apples slept in their straw. But one was long and black and heavy, its teeth worn almost smooth, and Tamsin held that one apart from the others the way you might hold a splinter you'd carried too long to remember it wasn't part of your body.

"You're going to open it," Ilex said. Not a question. In the Spire-tongue it would have been *sela-veth* — *the door remembers your hand* — but they said it in the common speech now, mostly, because Tamsin was teaching them the other way around too: how to be a person who spoke two worlds without bleeding between them.

Tamsin didn't answer right away. The morning light came through the doorway and lay across the floorboards in a long pale bar, and dust drifted through it, slow as snow falling upward.

"Twenty years," Tamsin said at last. "I used to tell myself I was keeping people out. That's what a gatekeeper does, isn't it? Keeps things out." Their thumb moved over the black key's worn teeth. "It took me longer than it should have to understand that a lock works both ways. All those years, I wasn't guarding the valley. I was guarding myself. And my grandmother before me, and her mother before her — a whole line of Quintanas locking themselves in with a secret, calling it duty."

"The ledger called it keeping," Ilex said quietly. "The old word. *Veth-ka.*"

"*Veth-ka,*" Tamsin repeated, and something crossed their face — grief and tenderness tangled together, the expression Ilex had learned to read all winter, the face Tamsin made whenever the grandmother appeared in memory, which was often now, since the fire had taken the singing and left everything else sharpened. "Keeping. She really believed that was what she was doing. Keeping the world safe from the past." A breath. "She never once asked whether the past might have wanted to come home."

Outside, the valley was undoing itself. The Thornfell gate stood at the end of the cottage garden, older than the cottage, older than the wall around it — a door of grey stone-bound timber set into the mountainside itself, banded with iron gone green with age, carved over every inch with letters that no one in the valley could read. Letters that Ilex could read. Had always been able to read, though they hadn't known until Tamsin knelt with them in the snow last autumn and traced the words and said, in a voice like a crack in ice: *You can read this. Child, do you know what that means?*

The carvings said: *Here the way is kept. Here the way is given.*

Not kept shut. Kept. The way a flame is kept. The way a promise is kept.

Tamsin walked down the garden path, and Ilex followed, and the mud came up soft and cold around their boots. At the gate, Tamsin stopped and stood for a long moment with their forehead nearly touching the ancient timber, the way you stand before a grave, or a sleeping thing you love.

"I don't know what's behind it anymore," Tamsin said. "That's the truth of it. Grandmother sealed the inner road when I was younger than you. Whatever's down there has been dark for thirty years." A pause. "Are you afraid?"

Ilex considered it honestly, because Tamsin had taught them that too — that fear was not a thing to swallow whole but to take apart and look at, piece by piece, like Ferro taking apart a mule's harness.

"Yes," they said. "But it's a good afraid. Like before the fire."

Tamsin laughed — short, surprised, real. "Like before the fire," they agreed. "All right then."

The black key slid into the lock as if the winter between had never happened. It turned with a sound like a word being spoken after a long silence — a deep, grinding, relieved sound — and the bolt drew back, and the gate swung inward onto darkness that smelled of stone and cold air and, very faintly, impossibly, of dragon-fire long burned down to embers.

Light spilled in. Somewhere inside, something that had been waiting began, very slowly, to wake.

* * *

They did not go far, that first day. Just to the threshold chamber, where the inner road began its descent toward the drowned forge, and where the walls were covered floor to ceiling in writing — thousands upon thousands of characters, some cut deep and old, some painted in pigments that had faded to ghosts. Ilex walked along the walls with a lantern, reading aloud, and Tamsin sat on an upturned crate with a slate and chalk like a schoolchild, writing down everything, asking questions, getting things wrong, laughing at themselves for getting things wrong.

"This character," Tamsin said, tapping the slate. "The one for *return*. I've seen it my whole life on the lintel above the kitchen door. Grandmother told me it meant *stay away*."

"It does mean stay away," Ilex said. "And return. It depends which side of the door you're standing on." They traced it in the air. "*Seth.* From outside, it warns. From inside, it welcomes home. Grandmother only ever taught you the outside meaning."

Tamsin looked at the slate for a long time. "She only ever knew the outside meaning herself, probably," they said finally. "By her generation, half the tongue was already lost. That's what hungering does. It doesn't just eat memories. It eats meanings. You forget what words are for." They set down the chalk. "We'll need more than the two of us. A language two people speak is a language dying. Three is a conversation. Ten is a village."

"So we find ten," Ilex said simply.

Tamsin looked at them — this child the Regent had hunted across half a kingdom, this living key who had knocked on a gatekeeper's door at midnight and made the stones hum — and something in their face eased, the way a fist eases.

"So we find ten," they agreed.

* * *

The valley heard about the opened gate within three days, because valleys hear everything.

They came first out of curiosity, in ones and twos. The miller's wife, who remembered her own grandmother humming a lullaby in a language nobody recognized. Two of Ferro's mule-drivers, who arrived with a cask of Ferro's best cider and Ferro's compliments, and stayed to copy characters onto slates with their big rope-scarred hands, tongues between their teeth. A boy from the salt road whose father had died in the pass fighting for the Regent's soldiers and who needed, Ilex understood without being told, somewhere to put his grief that wasn't shame.

The king's death had changed the shape of the world more quietly than anyone expected. There had been no grand funeral, no marble tomb, no myth. The court criers had spoken plainly, for the first time in living memory: *King Aldous is dead. He was taken by the crown when he was young. He chose, at the end, to die a man. Mourn him as a man.* And the kingdom, hungry for honesty the way the Regent had been hungry for memory, had swallowed the plainness and found it nourishing. In the taverns they argued about him now — whether he'd been weak or brave, foolish or kind — which was, Ilex thought, exactly what he would have wanted. Myths don't argue. Only the living do.

Some of the learners asked about the Regent. Was it truly gone? Starved into silence, the fire had eaten its hoard of stolen years, and with nothing left to feed on, it had shrunk and shrunk until it was less than a whisper, less than a shadow moving wrong, until even the crown — melted down, the metal distributed to smiths across the land to be beaten into plowshares and bells — held nothing at all.

"It's still there," Ilex told them once, when the miller's wife asked. "Somewhere. Very small. Very quiet. Hunger doesn't die; it just waits." They had thought about it a long time before saying the rest. "But hunger can't take what's given freely. That's the lesson of the forge. The fire only ate what was offered. So we offer our memories to each other instead. We teach. We give the past away, and giving it away is the one way of keeping it that hunger can't touch."

Tamsin had been silent after that, and later that night had said, at the door of Ilex's room: "When did you get wise?" And Ilex had said, "I had a good teacher," and Tamsin had made a rough sound that was half laugh and half something else, and closed the door softly, and Ilex had lain in the dark smiling at the ceiling.

* * *

By late spring there were eleven of them.

They met in the threshold chamber on evenings when the light through the open gate went gold and long, eleven voices stumbling through the old characters while the lanterns swayed and the mountain breathed its cold clean breath down the inner road. Ilex taught the words they had known in their bones before they could walk. Tamsin taught the discipline — the careful hands, the honest questions, the keeper's patience. Between them, the language began, letter by letter, to come back from the dead.

One evening, after the others had gone, Ilex stayed behind in the threshold chamber alone. The gate stood open behind them, letting in the smell of rain and green growing things. They walked the length of the written walls, lantern raised, and stopped at the oldest carving of all, low near the floor, half-worn, the one they had saved all winter to translate because they had been afraid of it.

They weren't afraid anymore.

*When the last keeper opens the last gate,* it read, *the way will not lead out or in. The way will lead onward. And those who walk it will carry the fire not in stone cradles but in themselves, and no darkness will be able to starve on them, for you cannot steal what is freely sung.*

Ilex stood a long time with their fingers resting on the worn letters.

Then they turned and walked back up the passage, out through the open gate, into the spring evening, where Tamsin was waiting on the garden path with two slates under one arm and the evening light catching the grey in their hair, and somewhere down the valley a bell — one of the new bells, cast from the crown's old metal — rang the hour, clear and unafraid.

"Ilex," Tamsin said. "There's a trader come up the salt road. Says there are other valleys. Other gates. Says some of them still have keepers — old ones, hiding, same as I was." They held out a slate. On it, in careful chalk, someone had copied a single character — one Ilex didn't recognize, angular and strange and alive. "He wants to know if we're teaching."

Ilex took the slate and looked at the unknown letter for a long moment, feeling it hum against their palms the way the gate-stones had hummed that first midnight, a year and a lifetime ago.

"Yes," they said. "Tell him yes."

And far below, in the dark of the inner road, something old and patient and no longer hungry stirred in its sleep — and, hearing the voices rise faintly through the stone, eleven strong and growing, settled again, and let them sing.
