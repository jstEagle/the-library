## 1. The City That Forgot

Greyhollow forgets beautifully. That's the trick of it — the city doesn't mourn its magic so much as misplace it, the way you misplace a key and then convince yourself you never owned a door.

I sell keys, actually. Among other things. Little brass charms shaped like keys, moons, fish, and one unfortunate owl that nobody has bought in three years because its eyes are slightly wrong and it watches people in a way owls should not. I sell them from a folding table at the edge of the night market on Candlewick Row, under a lantern I keep deliberately dim, because bright things attract attention and attention, in Greyhollow, is a slow-acting poison.

"Charms for luck," I tell the customers. "Charms for safe roads, sweet dreams, honest husbands." A pause. "The honest husbands are decorative only. No charm can perform miracles."

They laugh. They buy. And every single trinket I sell is exactly what it appears to be — except for the ones that aren't.

Here is what the city forgot, and what I am careful to remember: five years ago, enchantment was outlawed by royal decree, and the Spire went up on Harrow Hill like a needle through the sky's eye, and anyone born with the gift was given a choice between hiding and vanishing. The vanishing was dressed up politely at first. *Relocation. Assessment. Service to the crown.* Then the polite words wore thin, as polite words do, and now when someone is taken to the Spire for *testing*, their shutters stay closed a long time after, and their neighbors develop sudden interests in their own feet.

So I hide. I have gotten very good at it. I am, if I may flatter myself, an artist of it.

My name is Ulyana Amberdale, and I make maps of places that do not exist.

That's not quite true either. The places exist. It's the maps that shouldn't.

* * *

Every night after the market closes, I light one candle — beeswax, because tallow smokes and smoke stains ceilings and stained ceilings invite questions — and I unroll my mother's atlas across the kitchen table of my narrow rooms above the dye-shop. The atlas is the only inheritance she left me: a heavy book bound in green leather gone soft as moss, its corners worn round by forty years of her hands before they were worn by mine.

Most of it is ordinary. Coastlines. Mountain passes. Trade routes drawn in her neat, slanted script, annotated with things like *innkeeper cheats, but the stew is worth it* and *bridge out — use the ford, mind the eels.* My mother was a cartographer too, the honest kind, the kind who could walk into any guildhall in the old days and be paid in silver for her work.

But tucked between the honest pages are the others.

The Veilwood does not appear on any crown survey. Officially, there is nothing east of the Greywater but hills and old ruins and a great deal of nothing. Unofficially — unofficially, there is a forest that exists the way a note exists half a step below hearing, a realm folded against our own like a page pressed too close to its neighbor, and the only ink that will hold its shape is ink guided by a gift.

I have been mapping the Veilwood for eleven years. Five of those years, illegally. Before the outlawry, I'd told myself I was simply continuing my mother's work; afterward, I told myself I was simply being stubborn, which felt safer than saying I would rather lose everything than stop. Stubbornness is such a respectable vice. Nobody burns you for stubbornness.

The candle flame leaned as I opened to the current page. The Veilwood came up under my pen the way it always does — reluctantly at first, then all at once, like a deer stepping out of trees. The lines know where to go before I do. That's the part no one warns you about, the part that makes the gift feel less like a talent and more like a correspondence: the map is already there, waiting, and all I do is listen hard enough to trace it.

Tonight the forest had grown. That happens. The Veilwood shifts its borders the way a sleeper shifts under blankets, and last month a river I'd charted twice had wandered three miles north out of pure caprice. Tonight, though, something else. On the western margin, where I'd always drawn empty vellum, a faint pressure — a sense of edges, of architecture, of something vast and deliberate sitting just past the treeline.

I sat back. My pen hovered. The candle guttered in a draft that hadn't been there a moment before.

"Hm," I said, to the empty room, in the tone of a woman who has decided not to think about something.

This is a skill I recommend. Deciding not to think about things has kept me alive for five years. There is a whole discipline to it, nearly a science. You notice the strange thing, you name it *nothing*, and you put it away on the shelf next to all the other nothings, and you do not take it down at three in the morning when the house creaks.

I took it down at three in the morning, obviously. But by then I had other problems.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble in Greyhollow usually does, with a child doing something foolish near my stall.

It was market night, two days later, cold enough that my breath made small ghosts over the table. Business was slow. A fishmonger argued with his scale. Somewhere a fiddle played the same eight bars over and over because the fiddler was drunk or melancholy or both. I was rearranging my owl to face the wall when I saw the boy.

He couldn't have been more than six, wrapped in a coat built for someone twice his size, chasing a paper windmill that the wind had stolen off a peddler's cart. The windmill skittered along the cobbles, danced, and then — this is the part I still turn over in my head — skipped neatly off the edge of the Row and out over the open mouth of the culvert.

Greyhollow's storm drains are a legacy of some earlier, more ambitious century: wide stone throats that swallow the hill's runoff and deliver it to the Greywater. In dry weather they're a nuisance. It had rained two days before. The culvert was running fast and brown and deep, and the paper windmill landed on the current like a leaf, and the boy — laughing, reaching, six years old and entirely ignorant of physics — climbed over the low iron rail.

Time did that thing time does. It went syrup-slow and crystal-clear. I saw his heel leave the ground. I saw the rail, waist-high on me, chest-high on him. I saw that no one else had noticed, because everyone else was warm and facing away and minding their own carefully guarded lives, and why wouldn't they be? Looking away is the city's whole religion.

There were perhaps four seconds available to me. Let me tell you what a coward's arithmetic looks like in four seconds:

*If I shout, someone might catch him. If I run, I might reach him. If I do magic, in the open, on Candlewick Row, under forty witnesses, I die — slowly, eventually, at the Spire, but certainly.*

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, in my mother's voice: *A map is a promise that things are where you say they are. Be the kind of person who keeps promises.*

I did not shout. I did not run. I did the stupid thing, the beautiful thing, the thing I would spend the rest of the night alternately justifying and cursing.

I flicked two fingers — a gesture I disguised as snatching at my own scarf, which was indeed blowing in the wind, thank you, wind, for your cooperation — and pulled.

The gift answered the way it always answers: instantly, gladly, like a dog that has waited all day by the door. The air between me and the boy thickened, caught, became briefly a thing with texture and grip, and the windmill's flight checked mid-skitter as though it had hit glass. The boy, one hand outstretched, met the invisible wall of it and stopped — teetered — and fell backward onto the cobbles instead of forward into the water.

He sat there, blinking. The windmill spun gently against the unseen barrier, then dropped straight down, landing at his knee.

For one heartbeat, everything held. The fiddle played. The fishmonger shouted about scales. The boy picked up his windmill and looked at it with the profound suspicion children reserve for objects that have behaved interestingly.

Then he looked at me.

Children see everything; that's the problem with them. Adults have trained their eyes to lie, but a six-year-old looks directly at the world with no manners whatsoever, and this one looked at my fingers, still curled around my scarf, and then at the space above the culvert, where — I realized with a lurch — the rain-fog was swirling in a slow, wrong spiral, like water finding a drain that wasn't there.

"You," he said, with the gravity of a magistrate, "did a *thing*."

"I," I said, "handed you your windmill."

"It flew at you."

"The wind is generous tonight."

He considered this. He was deciding whether to believe me, and I want you to understand that my entire life hung on the decision-making process of a person whose previous observable interests included eating dirt. The fog spiraled. The fiddle played its eight bars. Somewhere behind me, a woman laughed at something a man said, ordinary as bread.

Then a voice called — a mother's voice, frayed with the particular panic of having lost sight of a child for ninety seconds — and the boy stood, clutched his windmill, and delivered his verdict.

"You're nice," he announced. "But you're weird."

He ran to his mother. She scooped him up, scolded him with her forehead pressed to his, and carried him off into the crowd without once looking back. Which was, I suppose, the best possible outcome: witnessed by a child, and children, mercifully, are not believed.

I packed up my stall early that night. My hands shook the entire time, and I dropped the owl twice, and I decided — firmly, decisively — that this was adrenaline and nothing more, and that I was fine, and that the woman who had watched me from across the Row, the one in the grey traveling cloak who had been looking at my stall and not buying anything for the better part of an hour, was also fine, also nothing, also a nothing to be shelved.

She had a hunter's stance. Weight even, hands loose, eyes doing that systematic sweep that isn't curiosity, it's inventory. I'd seen it before, five years ago, on the day the edicts went up and the first wagons rolled toward Harrow Hill.

I told myself she was probably just cold.

I am very good at telling myself things. It's practically a second gift.

* * *

Above the dye-shop, I did not light the candle. I sat in the dark with the atlas closed under my palms, listening to the building settle and the last vendors pack up below, and I conducted my usual nightly audit of fear.

Item: the child. Handled. Children aren't believed, and even if he babbled about a lady who did a *thing*, the city is full of ladies doing things; half of Greyhollow's fortune-tellers fake the gift for coin, which is either the saddest or bravest profession I know of.

Item: the woman in grey. Probably nothing. Probably. Hunters didn't linger in markets; they arrived in threes with writs and took people from their beds. Lurking was for informants, and informants were paid per name, and my name was clean — I paid my taxes, I charmed no one honestly, I was a trinket-seller of modest success and dull habits. Dullness was armor. I had spent five years forging it, plate by plate.

Item: the pressure on the western margin of the Veilwood. Something vast and deliberate, sitting just past the treeline of a realm that shouldn't be growing, shouldn't be pressing, shouldn't be *leaning* toward Greyhollow like a listener leaning toward a conversation.

I thought about my mother, then. I try not to; grief is a country I've mostly emigrated from, though I still pay taxes there. But sitting in the dark with her atlas under my hands, I remembered her at this same table, in a different city, a different life, drawing lines that only she could see while I did my sums beside her. She'd been afraid too, near the end. I hadn't understood it then. She'd hidden the atlas inside a false bottom of her travel trunk and taught me a nonsense rhyme to remember where, and I'd thought it was a game. *When the spire eats the moon, look beneath the wooden spoon.* I'd thought it was a game.

She died two winters before the outlawry. She never knew what was coming. Or — and here is the thought I shelved, the heaviest nothing on the shelf — maybe she did. Maybe that's what the fear was. Maybe she drew maps of a hidden realm and hid them in a false bottom because she already knew a day would come when maps like hers would be worth blood.

The atlas lay quiet under my hands. It always lies quiet. It's a book. Books are patient; that's their whole nature.

"Tomorrow," I told it, in the dark, the way you talk to graves and books and other things that don't answer. "Tomorrow I'll look at the western margin. Tonight I'm going to sleep like an innocent woman, because tomorrow I'll need the practice."

The atlas said nothing, in the way of books.

But as I blew out the stub of candle I'd finally allowed myself, I could have sworn — could have *sworn* — that the faintest warmth came up through the leather, brief as a hand squeezing yours, there and gone.

I told myself that was nothing too.

Below my window, on Candlewick Row, boots rang on the cobbles — several pairs, unhurried, in step. Not drunks. Drunks don't march. I stood very still at the window and watched four figures in grey cloaks pass beneath the lamp, moving east, toward the residential lanes, and the last of them turned his head as he walked, reading doorways the way I read coastlines.

They stopped three doors down. At the widow Sarelle's house. Sweet Sarelle, who brought me onion bread and complained about her knees and whose tea, I had sometimes suspected, steamed a little too fragrantly, a little too soothingly, for mere chamomile.

The knocking began. Soft, at first. Polite. Politeness, in Greyhollow, wears the same uniform as everything else.

I stood in the dark with my hand flat on my mother's atlas, and through the floorboards I heard Sarelle's door open, and voices — courteous, unhurried, terrible — and I understood that the city had finished forgetting, and had begun remembering instead, and that it remembers people like us the way a debt collector remembers names.
