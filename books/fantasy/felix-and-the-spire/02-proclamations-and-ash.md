## 2. Proclamations and Ash

They took Sarelle at dawn.

I know because I was awake to hear it — awake because of the knocking in the night, which had turned out not to be for me after all. The polite voices on my landing had passed my door without a pause and continued up one flight, where they knocked again, softer, more politely still, and a woman's voice answered them with the particular brightness people use when they are terrified and have decided to be dignified about it.

Sarelle Quill. My neighbor. A widow of sixty-odd years who grew moonflowers on her windowsill that bloomed out of season, though she'd swear before any tribunal that it was merely good soil and a southern exposure. She mended lace for the merchant wives and told fortunes at midwinter festivals using cards she'd painted herself — "only for amusement, dear, nobody believes it" — and once, three years ago, when I had burned my hand on a kettle and couldn't be seen visiting a physiker who might ask questions about why my scars healed wrong, she had sat with me all night with her hands hovering over mine, humming an old song, until the pain went somewhere else entirely.

She never called it healing. Neither of us ever called anything anything. That was the arrangement Greyhollow lived by: we did not name our gifts, and so, officially, they did not exist, and so, officially, no one could be guilty of them. It was a stupid system and it worked beautifully for five years, right up until it didn't.

Through the floorboards I heard her say, "Of course, sirs. Just let me fetch my shawl." As if she were going out to church. As if they'd come to walk her there.

Then boots on stairs, then the street door, then nothing.

I lay on my floor with my cheek against the boards like a child listening for the sea, and I heard the cart wheels, and I heard the wheels stop, and start again, and fade, and I thought: *she bloomed those flowers for the whole landing. She never charged anyone for a fortune. She hummed to my burned hand.*

And then I thought, with the cold practicality that is apparently what passes for my spine: *they walked past your door.*

*They will not walk past it twice.*

* * *

By midmorning the edicts were everywhere.

I saw the first one nailed to the customs post by the night market, where twelve hours earlier I had been selling luck-charms disguised as buttons and pretending my fingers didn't warm when I touched copper. The proclamation was a broadsheet the size of a door, heavy black type under the crown's seal, and a clerk stood beside it reading it aloud to a gathering crowd in the flat, bored voice of a man who has read terrible things so many times they've become weather to him.

*"By order of His Majesty, all persons possessing or suspected of possessing unlicensed enchantment shall present themselves at the Spire for testing within fourteen days. Testing is mandatory. Testing is free."*

Free. That was the detail that made the crowd murmur. Not the treason clause, not the confiscation of property, not even the word *testing*, which hung over the square like a blade over a bread loaf. It was *free*. The crown had learned something important about fear, which is that fear makes people stubborn, but generosity makes them suspicious, and suspicion keeps them talking.

*"Any person found harboring, concealing, or aiding the gifted,"* the clerk droned on, *"shall be guilty of high treason, punishable by forfeiture of goods and imprisonment at the crown's pleasure."*

At the crown's pleasure. Which, given the Spire's recent appetite, was presumably a short pleasure ending in a long fall.

I stood at the edge of the crowd with my hood up and my basket held against my chest like a shield, and I listened to the people around me react, and here is what I will tell you about Greyhollow, the city that forgot: it hadn't forgotten at all. It had been waiting. I could hear it in the way they talked — not horror, mostly, but arithmetic. A tanner wondering aloud if his journeyman was "one of them." A fishwife saying her sister-in-law's candles always burned blue and wasn't that worth reporting, just to be safe? An old man saying nothing at all, but copying down the date of the edict onto his cuff with a stub of pencil, the way you write down a debt.

Fourteen days. Then the doors would open on their own, neighbor informing on neighbor, because the crown understood the oldest truth of occupation: you don't need many hunters if you give everyone else a reason to hunt each other.

A boy was pasting a second broadsheet onto the fountain wall. I watched him smooth it flat with his palm, careful as a priest laying altar cloth, and I noticed his paste bucket bore the Spire's mark — a tower inside a circle — and I noticed, further, that the mark had been freshly repainted, the black lacquer bright as a beetle's back. Someone was proud of this work. Someone had budgeted for it.

I went home the long way, through streets I knew how to walk without being seen, which is a skill that sounds sinister and is really just the ordinary magic of a woman who has spent five years being no one.

* * *

My rooms above the chandler's shop smelled of beeswax and old paper and, beneath both, faintly, of the Veilwood — the green other-country only I could perceive, which clung to everything I owned the way woodsmoke clings to wool. I had never been able to wash it out. I had stopped trying.

I set my mother's atlas on the table first. Always the atlas first; it was superstition, probably, or maybe just the honest ranking of my possessions. Then I unpacked the basket, and laid out my stock, and looked at what five years of careful, cowardly craftsmanship amounted to.

Buttons that brought mild good fortune. Hairpins that kept headaches away. A brass locket that made the wearer slightly harder to notice in a crowd — my best seller, ironically, sold almost exclusively to people trying to avoid exactly the kind of attention that now came with a reward attached. A dozen knotted threads for safe travel. Three small stones that, if you held them and wished honestly, would show you a place you missed. I had sold two of those to homesick sailors. I had kept one for myself and used it, some nights, to look at the Veilwood paths my mother had walked, because grief is just homesickness for someone instead of somewhere, and I am nothing if not consistent.

Half of it, I decided, watching the light move across the table. Half of it had to go.

Here is the arithmetic of a fugitive's inventory: every charm I kept was evidence. Every charm was also, potentially, a life — a headache eased, a traveler protected, a frightened person made briefly invisible. Keeping them was mercy. Keeping them was suicide. Both things were true, and I stood there holding a hairpin that cost me four hours' work and might keep some girl from crying herself to sleep, and I hated the crown, and I hated the math, and I hated most of all that the math was right.

I built the fire up hot. Beeswax burns sweetly; enchanted beeswax, it turns out, burns green at the edges, with a smell like cut grass and thunderstorms, and I stood over the basin feeding it button by button, pin by pin, thread by thread, and watched five years of quiet kindness go up in colors no honest fire should make.

The locket last. I don't know why I saved it for last except that it felt like saving myself for last, and when it hit the coals the flames flared white and I heard — or imagined I heard, which amounts to the same thing — a sound like a small sigh, and I said, out loud, to a melting lump of brass, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Go be useful somewhere else."

That is the state I was in. Apologizing to jewelry. Five years of hiding does that to a person; it shrinks your world down to the size of the lie you're living, until the lie has furniture and opinions and a fire basin full of dying magic.

The stone I kept. The one that showed me the Veilwood. I told myself it was because it looked like an ordinary river pebble, which was true, and because no examiner would think twice about it, which was also true, and because I could not put my mother's country into a fire, which was truest of all.

Then I picked up the atlas.

* * *

You have to understand what the atlas is. Not what it contains — I'll get to that, or rather it got to me — but what it *is*. It is a book the size of a paving slab, bound in leather gone soft and dark as chestnuts, with brass corners worn round by a hundred years of hands. My mother carried it across three realms and back into this one. She carried it through the last years of the outlawry, when carrying it was already death with extra steps. She hid it in a false bottom of her map chest, under charts of honest coastlines, and she died with its location known to exactly one person, and that person was me, and I have spent five years treating that inheritance like a live coal wrapped in linen: necessary, beloved, and liable to burn the house down.

I opened it to the middle, where the pages are thickest, and I did what I had done perhaps fifty times since her funeral and never once allowed myself to do slowly.

I read my mother's handwriting.

Not the map-margins — those were hers too, little annotations in a script like bird tracks — but the frontispiece, where she had written, in ink faded to the color of weak tea:

*For Ulyana, who draws borders better than she believes them.*

Motherly, and cutting, and accurate. She'd known me exactly.

I turned pages. Coastlines I had memorized before I could write. The Veilwood rendered in greens that shifted, very slightly, depending on the hour — I had tested this obsessively at fifteen, convinced I was imagining it, and concluded eventually that either the maps moved or I did, and that the distinction mattered less than I'd assumed. And in the deep center of the book, behind a flap of vellum sewn in with stitches older than the crown itself, the pages that were not hers.

Those pages redrew themselves.

I had discovered it years ago by accident: left alone overnight, certain leaves of the atlas changed — roads rerouting themselves, a mountain range migrating half an inch westward, a spiral symbol near the center growing or shrinking like something breathing. I had told myself it was damp. I had told myself it was my eyes. I had told myself every lie available at retail, because the alternative was admitting that my mother had left me a map of something alive, and that the something alive was underneath Greyhollow, and that the Spire — the beautiful, terrible, needle-thin Spire that crowned the city like a exclamation point at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish — was drawn on those pages too, and drawn *wrong*, drawn as a wound, drawn with the spiral coiled tight around its foundations like a hand closed around a throat.

Tonight, with the fire still smelling of green smoke and my neighbor's shawl still hanging empty on its hook one floor below, I looked at those pages and stopped lying to myself.

The spiral had grown. Since autumn, it had grown. Whatever the Spire was doing down in its roots — whatever "testing" meant, whatever they needed a steady supply of gifted people *for* — the map knew about it before the proclamations did. The atlas had been trying to tell me the way animals know about earthquakes: quietly, persistently, and to an audience determined to sleep through it.

And then the pages moved again while I watched.

Not the slow breathing shift I knew. Something faster. The spiral contracted — sharply, like a flinch — and along the map's eastern edge, where the road ran down from the hills toward the city gate, a thin line of darkness appeared, moving. Moving at the speed of horses.

I put my palm flat on the page. The paper was warm. It had never been warm before.

Hunters. Elite riders, coming down the hill road, and the atlas showing them to me the way a spider feels a fly land on the far side of the web — and I understood, with a clarity that arrived all at once like cold water, that the book wasn't just a record. It was connected. To the wellspring. To the Spire. To whatever the crown was feeding into that spiral, and whatever the spiral was feeding back.

Which meant the atlas wasn't a keepsake.

It was a key. Or a weapon. Or bait.

And somewhere below the hills, men in the crown's black were riding toward a city where a widow's flowers would bloom tomorrow morning out of pure habit, and no one would be there to blame the soil.

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I did next, because later — when Corvin asks, and he will ask, he has a hunter's nose for omissions — I want to remember that I hesitated.

I stood at the hearth with the atlas open in both hands, and I held it over the fire, and I held it there for the length of ten heartbeats, which is longer than you'd think. Long enough to watch the firelight move across my mother's handwriting. Long enough to understand that burning it would be the sane choice, the survivable choice, the choice of a woman who wanted to grow old quietly selling buttons in a city that had finished forgetting.

Long enough to know I wasn't going to do it.

Because Sarelle had hummed to my burned hand. Because the boy in the market square had copied the edict's date onto his cuff like a debt. Because my mother had crossed three realms carrying this thing not to hide it but to keep it — there's a difference, I finally saw it, plain as the spiral on the page — and because the pages were warm under my palms and getting warmer, like something waking up, like something that had waited a hundred years and a generation and one very long night to be picked up by someone who would stop apologizing to the furniture and do something.

I closed the atlas. I wrapped it in oilcloth. I put it in the map chest, under the honest coastlines, exactly where she had kept it, because some inheritances you don't improve upon.

Then I packed. Fast, mean, efficient: the stone, dry rations, my good pens, coin, the knife I'd bought years ago and never explained to anyone. I left the rooms swept and the rent hidden under the chandler's prayer candle, because even fugitives have manners.

On my way down the stairs I paused at Sarelle's door. Her moonflowers had closed for the night, all except one, which had opened anyway — out of season, out of habit, out of whatever stubborn green faith lives in a plant whose gardener isn't coming home. I stood there a moment and let myself feel it, all of it, the whole weight of the morning, because I had a suspicion I wouldn't get another chance to feel anything safely for a long time.

Fear, I discovered, doesn't melt when you decide to act on it. It hardens. It sets around your resolve like wax around a wick, and maybe that's the point. Maybe that's what fear is *for*.

Fourteen days, the edict said. The map said the hunters were closer than that.

I blew out the lamp, and went down into the dark to find a horse I couldn't afford and a road that wasn't on any chart, carrying a book that was warmer than it should have been, and heavier than it looked, and — I was nearly sure — glad.
