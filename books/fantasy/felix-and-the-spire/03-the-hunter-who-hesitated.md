## 3. The Hunter Who Hesitated

There are two kinds of lies a man can tell with his face. The first is the lie you tell strangers, which is easy, because strangers want to believe you. The second is the lie you tell the people who trained you, who taught you how to spot a lie, and that one takes practice.

I had six years of practice, and on the morning of the Proclamation, I nearly wasted all of it.

* * *

The square outside the Greyhollow assizes had been dressed for the occasion. Banners of white and iron-grey hung from the customs house, damp with river fog, and a crier with a voice like a boot heel on gravel read the edict for the fourth time that hour. I stood at the edge of the crowd in my hunter's coat — dark wool, silver clasp, the crown's stag stamped on the shoulder — and listened to my own name in the law.

*All persons possessing or practicing enchantment shall present themselves at the Spire for testing, within the space of one moon. Any who harbor, conceal, or aid the gifted shall be judged guilty of treason against the crown.*

Treason. A fine word. It has a ring to it, like a bell, or a cell door.

The crowd didn't cheer. That was the thing the men in the assizes never understood about Greyhollow. The city didn't cheer, and it didn't weep either. It did what Greyhollow always did with bad weather and worse news: it pulled its collar up, hunched its shoulders, and waited to see who'd be soaked first. A fishmonger kept scaling his fish. Two old men finished a game of stones on a barrel head. Somewhere near the fountain a woman was selling candied ginger and calling out her prices in a voice that pretended nothing in the world had changed.

I knew that voice. I'd been hearing versions of it my whole life — the voice of a person performing ordinariness so well it becomes a kind of art.

The girl was eleven, maybe twelve. She stood near the fountain with her mother, and she was the only person in the square looking up at the crier with her mouth open, because she was the only person in the square who hadn't already known what the edict would say. Children are always the last to learn the rules, and the first to be held to them.

Her mother's hand closed around her shoulder. The girl flinched — and the flinch was wrong. It wasn't the flinch of a frightened child. It was the flinch of a child who'd been told, in the particular hushed voice parents use for particular hushed truths, *never do that where anyone can see.*

I'd spent six years learning to read that flinch. It's a hunter's trade, spotting the gifted: the way a charm-seller's hands move too smoothly, the way a hedge-witch's garden grows crooked and abundant, the way a child pulls back from a candle flame not with fear but with recognition. The crown called it the Sight and trained it into us at the Spire like a trick dog learns to point. What they never taught us — what they carefully, deliberately did not teach — was what to feel afterward.

"Sergeant Corvin." Captain Hale's voice at my shoulder, low and pleasant, which from Hale was always worse than shouting. "The crier's list. Third name."

I looked at the paper in his gloved hand. *Mira Callow, daughter of Edith Callow, weaver's lane.* Eleven years old. The charge was *unlicensed enchantment, witnessed,* which meant someone had reported her, which meant a neighbor had, which meant the edict had done its work before the ink was dry.

"Take Brannick," I said. "He's fresher."

"Hale wants you." Hale wasn't Hale; Hale was the captain, and I was making the mistake tired men make, thinking of him by the wrong name in my head, as if familiarity could soften him. "He says you've a gentle manner. Good with the young ones. Less weeping that way."

Gentle. Six years of arrests and he'd distilled my career down to *gentle,* the way you'd praise a knife for being clean.

* * *

The Callow house was on Weaver's Lane, which meant it leaned, which meant it was poor, which meant the arrest would be quick and quiet and nobody important would ask questions. Edith Callow opened the door before we knocked. She'd seen us coming — of course she had, we were two men in crown colors walking a street where crown colors hadn't walked in years — and her face had already done the terrible arithmetic mothers do.

"She's not here," she said.

"Madam." Brannick, bless his blockish heart, actually believed her.

I didn't. I looked past her shoulder, down the dim hallway, at the wash line strung through the back room, and I did the hunter's arithmetic instead. A girl of eleven, told all her life to hide, hearing boots on her street. Where does a child hide? Not the cellar — no second exit. Not the roof — she'd have gone over the back wall, and the back wall opened onto the tannery yards, and the tannery yards opened onto the river road, and the river road —

"Search the house," I said, and while Brannick clompered through the rooms upsetting everything that could be upset, I walked around back, unhurried, a man stretching his legs.

She was in the tannery yard, wedged between two drying racks, trying to make herself the size of a cat. She'd gone the wrong direction — deeper into the yards instead of out — because children always run toward the thing they know, and she knew her mother's back door better than the river road. Her eyes were wide and fixed on me with the flat animal attention of someone deciding whether to scream.

"Third name on the list," I said quietly. "Mira Callow."

She didn't answer. Smart.

"Your mother's inside. My partner's turning over her pantry." I crouched, which is a thing they teach you at the Spire never to do in front of a subject, because it lowers your eyes below theirs and makes you, briefly, a person. "There's a gate at the end of this yard. It sticks. Lift, then push. The tannery man takes his dinner at this hour; the lane will be empty."

She stared at me. I watched her do the sums — the ones children do faster than adults because they haven't yet been taught to doubt their own reading of a face. *This is a hunter. Hunters take you to the Spire. The Spire is where people go and don't come back the same, or don't come back.*

"I'm supposed to take you in," I said. "That's my duty. That's what the crown pays me for." I heard my own voice and was surprised by how steady it was, how reasonable, like a man explaining the weather. "And I'm telling you that gate sticks."

"Why?" she whispered. First word. Of course it was *why.* Children and philosophers, always the same question, and neither ever satisfied by the answer.

Because I've spent six years taking people like you through that gate and telling myself the Spire only *tests* them. Because last spring I escorted a charm-seller named Odell who sang to his canary the whole ride and I've never learned what they did with him. Because my hands have been steady for six years and I've just realized that's the most frightening thing about them.

"Because someone has to," is what I said, which was true and useless, the kind of truth you give a child when the real truth would take a year and a confession.

She went. Lift, then push. The gate didn't even stick. I'd remembered it sticking from three years ago, when I'd walked this same yard after a different name, and I'd stood at this same gate for the length of a long breath, and then I'd gone back inside and done my duty. The gate had stuck then. I remembered being grateful for it, the way you're grateful for any small obstacle between you and the thing you're about to do.

This time it swung open like it had been oiled for the occasion, and the girl slipped through, and that was that. A hunter's whole career can turn on the difference between a sticking gate and an oiled one. Nobody writes that down anywhere.

* * *

Brannick found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and Edith Callow wept with what I'm sure she believed was convincing grief for her vanished daughter, and I wrote in my report that the subject had fled prior to arrival, likely warned, likely gone to ground in the Warrens. All true. Reports are easy that way. The truth has so many rooms; you just choose which ones to show.

Hale read it standing in the assizes corridor with that pleasant flat expression he wears the way other men wear armor. "Warned," he said. "By whom?"

"Half the city heard the crier, Captain. The edict was posted at noon. The arrest was set for evening." I let a shrug carry the rest: *your planning, not my failure.*

"Mm." He handed the report back. He has a way of handing things back that makes you feel the paper is heavier than it was. "You know, Corvin, I've watched you work. Six years. You're the best I have at reading them — the gifted. You see it in them like it's written on their skin." A pause, measured out like medicine. "It's a rare thing, seeing that clearly. Makes a man wonder how you see it. Whether it's a window or a mirror."

The corridor was cold and smelled of ink and wet stone. I gave him the answer I'd been giving him for six years, the one I kept oiled like the gate: a laugh, a shrug, "If I could do what they do, Captain, I'd have better boots."

He laughed too. Hale's laugh is a professional instrument, tuned to the exact pitch that says *I have decided to find this amusing for now.* He clapped my shoulder with a gloved hand and walked away, and I stood there understanding, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching frost climb a window, that the gate had stuck for the last time.

He knew. Or he suspected, which in Greyhollow this season was the same thing with a shorter fuse. *A window or a mirror.* He'd said it lightly, the way you drop a stone in a well to hear how deep it goes. And I had given him the sound of a very deep well indeed, because I'd laughed a half-beat too late.

That's how it goes, in the end. Not the big mistakes. The half-beats.

* * *

I walked. It was what I did when the walls got close — walked the city like a patrol with no orders, letting my feet choose. Greyhollow at dusk in the season of edicts is a strange animal: shutters closing early, chimneys smoking like they're trying to signal something, the night market setting up in Tanner's Square with a defiant, desperate brightness, as if candlelight could be a form of argument.

The night market. Even the hunters left it mostly alone, because it was mostly honest — eels and tinware and secondhand coats — and because the crown had learned that a city allowed its small vices commits fewer large ones. So the stalls glowed, and the crowds came, and among the crowds came every hidden thing in Greyhollow, wearing its ordinariness like a coat.

I wasn't hunting. I want that on the record, whoever keeps it. I was a man buying eels.

That's when I saw her. The charm-seller.

I'd noticed her before — you notice everything in my trade, it's not a virtue, it's a symptom — a woman with ink-stained fingers at a stall of trinkets, the kind of pretty useless things tourists buy: brass compasses that don't point north, river-stones painted with eyes, little folded paper stars. She had the patter down, the laugh, the *no refunds, love, it's a decorative star, it doesn't grant wishes.* Good act. I'd catalogued her months ago as *probably nothing, possibly something,* and moved on, because half my job is deciding which somebodies are nobody.

But tonight she was doing something I'd only ever seen the gifted do.

A child had knocked over her stall. Not on purpose — a dog, a scuffle, the ordinary chaos of a market — and her stock was scattered, and a paper star had landed in a puddle, and the child was crying because children cry at the sight of any disaster they caused, and the woman knelt and gathered the star out of the water and — and here was the thing — she smoothed it, and it *uncreased.* Not gradually, not the way wet paper does when you're patient. All at once. The way a thing does when the world agrees to forget it was ever damaged.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then she was just a woman holding a soggy paper star, laughing at the dog, pressing the star into the crying child's hand like it was a treasure, and the moment folded itself away so neatly that if I hadn't spent six years training my eyes to catch exactly that kind of fold, I'd have missed it entirely.

She looked up. Across the square. At me.

And here is what I will never be able to explain properly, to Hale, to a tribunal, to myself at three in the morning: she didn't look away. A guilty person looks away — that's the first rule, the first lesson, the first thing they burn out of you at the Spire. A guilty person performs innocence, and performance has a seam, and I have spent my life finding seams.

She looked at me the way you look at someone you recognize. Not the face — the *situation.* The way one spy in a drawing room knows another, not by evidence but by the specific quality of the stillness. She looked at me and her expression said, as clearly as speech: *I know what you are. Do you know what I am? And what are we going to do about it?*

Then she smiled — a market smile, a trinket-seller's smile, *compasses, three coppers* — and turned back to her stall, and the seam closed, and she was nobody again.

I stood there with my eels getting cold and my heart doing something it hadn't done in six years, which was beating like it meant it.

Because that was the thing. She hadn't looked at me like a hunter. She'd looked at me like a colleague. And the worst part, the part I keep circling like a moth at a lamp, is that some exhausted, buried corner of me had felt *relief.* Six years of performing, six years of half-beat-late laughter and oiled gates and reports written in the grammar of cowardice — and one stranger across a square had looked at me and, for one unguarded instant, I had not been alone in the lie.

I didn't approach her. That would have been madness, and I was already carrying enough of it. I paid for my eels, which were terrible, and I walked back to my rooms in the hunters' quarter, and I sat down at my desk, and I took out the little notebook we're required to keep — *observations of suspected enchantment,* it says on the cover, in the crown's tidy script — and I turned to her page. I'd written one line about her, months ago: *Charm-seller, Tanner's Sq. Trinkets inert. Prob. nothing.*

I sat there a long time with the pen.

Then I wrote underneath: *Prob. nothing,* and struck through *nothing,* and wrote *something,* and then struck through the whole line, and then, because I am a coward and a careful man and both of those are just two names for the same animal, I tore the page out and burned it in the candle and watched the ash curl up like a paper star.

* * *

The summons came at dawn. Of course it did. Hale had let me sleep on it — let the frost climb the whole window — and then sent a runner with a note in his pleasant, terrible hand: *The Captain requests Sergeant Corvin at the assizes, ninth bell. Bring your observation book.*

Bring your observation book.

I stood in my cold room holding the note, and outside the window Greyhollow was waking up the way it always did, smoke and gulls and the river smell, a city pulling its collar up against weather it could see coming. Somewhere out there a girl named Mira Callow was running on legs that would carry her maybe as far as the next town, if the roads were kind, and they never are. Somewhere out there a charm-seller with ink-stained fingers was opening her stall and performing ordinariness for a crowd of people who might, any one of them, for a pardon or a grudge or a copper, report her by nightfall.

And I was going to walk into the assizes at ninth bell and lie to the best liar I knew, with my observation book in my hand and a burned page where the truth used to be, and the odds were — I did the arithmetic, the hunter's arithmetic, the kind I'm actually good at — the odds were I would not walk out again as the same man. Either I'd lie well enough to stay, in which case I'd stay and keep opening gates, or I'd lie badly, in which case I'd find out what the inside of the Spire looked like from the wrong side of the desk.

I put on my coat. I took the book. I checked that the ash was cold in the candle dish, because I am a careful man, and careful men die slowly instead of quickly, which is the only difference I've ever found between us and the reckless ones.

Ninth bell was going to ring in an hour. I picked up the book, and I went out into the grey morning, and every road in Greyhollow seemed to lead the same direction — toward the assizes, toward the Spire rising over the rooftops like a needle the city had swallowed and never digested.

What I didn't know — what no one knew yet, not Hale, not the charm-seller, not even the old ghosts who remembered — was that the atlas had already started to wake. That the map her mother had bled to hide was turning its pages in the dark. And that within the week, the three of us — the hunter, the cartographer, and a runaway the size of a scarecrow — would be running the same road with the crown's hounds behind us and the oldest magic in the world ahead.

But that morning, all I knew was this: I had one burned page, one hour, and one lie left to tell.

I meant to make it a good one.

I meant to.
