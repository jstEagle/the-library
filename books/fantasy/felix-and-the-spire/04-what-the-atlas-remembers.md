## 4. What the Atlas Remembers

My mother left me three things: a crooked nose I inherited along with her stubbornness, a recipe for plum cordial that could strip varnish off a table, and an atlas bound in green leather gone soft as old moss.

I'd spent five years pretending the atlas was just a book. That's harder than it sounds when you live in a city where owning the wrong book can get you a one-way trip to the Spire. But you can't burn your mother's handwriting. You can't burn the little marginal notes in her slanted script — *the river bends wrong here, ask Marta why* — or the pressed violet on page forty that still smells faintly of a summer before enchantment became a crime.

So I kept it at the bottom of my trunk, under a false floor I'd built myself, beneath a layer of perfectly legal maps of Greyhollow's sewer system, which no one has ever wanted to steal, read, or look at twice. It was the safest place in the city for it, and I told myself that every night like a prayer with poor theology.

Three nights after they took Master Ellery from the house across the lane — three nights after I stood at my window with the shutter cracked open and did nothing, absolutely nothing, while they put him in a wagon like a sack of turnips — I finally opened the trunk.

Not because of courage. Let's be honest about that. I opened it because I couldn't sleep, because every creak of the house sounded like boots on stairs, and because fear, given enough hours alone with itself, will always eventually reach for the thing it's most afraid of.

*If they're going to come for me anyway,* I thought, kneeling on the cold floorboards with the false panel lifted, *I should at least know what I'm dying over.*

The atlas was heavier than I remembered. That's not sentiment; I weighed it against a stack of charm-boxes once, back when I still had a scale, and it gained weight between seasons. Books don't do that. Maps don't do that. But then, my mother never drew ordinary maps, and I suppose I'd spent so long pretending she had that I'd half-convinced myself.

I carried it to the table, lit two candles instead of one — a extravagance, tallow being what it costs now — and untied the leather strap.

* * *

The first thing I noticed was that the pages were warm.

Not fever-warm. Hearthstone-warm. The particular warmth of something that has been sitting near a fire long after the fire's gone out. I pressed my palm flat against the cover and felt a slow pulse underneath, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, and I want to tell you I pulled my hand away like a sensible woman. I didn't. I'm my mother's daughter. I leaned closer.

The early pages were hers in the way I knew them: hand-inked coastlines, mountains rendered in fine cross-hatching, the Veilwood sketched in that loose, confident line she used for places only she could see. The Veilwood wasn't on any crown survey. It existed in the folds between Greyhollow and the next world over, reachable only by gift, and my mother had mapped it the way other people map their own back gardens — casually, lovingly, with annotations about where the fox-spirits liked to be left offerings.

I turned past those pages with a lump in my throat. Then I reached the middle of the book, and stopped.

The map there was of Greyhollow. Not the Greyhollow I knew — an older one, drawn before the Spire, when the hill at the city's heart had been bare. And in the margin, in my mother's hand, someone — she — had written:

*They built it on the well. They always build on the well.*

I stared at that until the candle guttered. Then I looked at the map again, and the map looked back.

I don't mean that charmingly. I mean the ink moved.

It was subtle at first — a shimmer across the paper like heat off summer cobbles, the kind of thing you'd blame on tired eyes. But then the streets of old Greyhollow began to shift, lines crawling like roots seeking water, and at the center of the map, where the Spire would someday stand, a shape surfaced through the parchment the way a fish rises through dark water: a circle, deep and black, ringed in silver, and around it lines converging from every direction like veins feeding a heart.

The wellspring. She'd drawn the wellspring. Under the Spire, under the city, under everything — a source of magic so old the pre-war mages had treated it the way you treat a sleeping bear: respectfully, quietly, and from a distance.

And the Spire hadn't been built to guard it. The Spire had been built to *drink* it.

I sat back. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because shaking hands are useless hands, and if anyone kicked down my door in the next hour I intended to need them.

"Right," I said aloud, to the empty room, because talking keeps you sane and also because no one was left in this neighborhood to hear me and think me mad. "You're a map that moves. Of course you are. Why would anything in my life be simple."

The ink-lines pulsed once, almost in answer, and settled.

I should have closed the book. I want that on the record, for whatever record survives: I knew I should close the book, put it back under the floor, and spend the rest of my life selling luck-charms to farmers and drawing sewers for the sanitation office. Instead I turned the page.

The next leaf showed the same city, but newer — the Spire rising from the hill, tall and thin and wrong, its shadow inked so heavily it bled through to the reverse side. Around the Spire's base, the vein-lines converged, but here they were thinner. Fainter. Being drawn *out*. And in the corner, small enough that I'd missed it on first reading years ago, my mother had written four words in her tightest hand:

*It is still hungry.*

* * *

I worked through the night the way I used to work through commissions — hunched, absorbed, forgetting to eat — except this time the commission was my mother's secret, delivered posthumously, with no fee and no client except the truth.

Page by page, the atlas told a story in cartography. The wellspring, whole and bright. The treaty-war that ended — she'd marked the battlefields with tiny violets, which I understood suddenly and horribly as her way of marking graves. The founding of the Spire, sold to the people as a bulwark, a shield, a promise that magic would never again burn the world. And beneath that public face, in the moving ink, the truth: the Spire as a tap driven into the land's deepest vein, siphoning magic year by year, storing it in its stone like a wine cellar hoarding vintages no one was allowed to taste.

That was why enchantment had been outlawed, I realized somewhere past midnight, with my chin in my hand and my eyes burning. Not because magic was dangerous. Because magic outside the Spire was magic the Spire couldn't tax. Every gifted child dragged in for "testing" wasn't being protected or punished. They were being *harvested*. Their gifts went up the Spire like water up a pump, and the crown called it safety, and everyone clapped.

Including, for five years, me. Clapping is easy when your hands are in your pockets.

I found her final entries near the end of the atlas. The last full map was dated eleven years ago — two years before she died — and it showed the wellspring's veins grown thin as spider silk across half the kingdom. Farmland going grey at the edges. Rivers running shy of fish. The slow, patient famine of a land being drained. In the margin, in handwriting shakier than any I'd seen from her:

*Ulyana, if you are reading this, then either I failed to hide it well enough or you failed to stay ignorant, and knowing us it is both. This book is not a keepsake. It is a key. The Spire's hunters will know it on sight, though they have never seen it — they will feel it the way dogs feel storms. Guard it. Or better, use it. Or best of all, my love, forgive yourself for whichever you choose.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. My mother had never once in her life said *I love you* without attaching it to an instruction, and somehow that made it worth more, not less.

I sat with the atlas open on my knees for a long time after that, watching the living map breathe its slow silver pulse, feeling like the last person awake at a funeral who's just realized the deceased left her the house, the debts, and the feuding relatives.

Then the atlas went cold.

Not cool. *Cold.* The warmth drained out of it in a single breath, the silver pulse stuttered, and every moving line on the page snapped rigid, all of them pointing — I checked twice, disbelieving — toward my front door.

I blew out the candles on instinct, which was stupid, since whoever was outside already knew exactly where I lived. I knelt by the window and eased the shutter apart a finger's width.

Two riders came up the lane at a walk. Royal hunters — I knew the silhouette even in the dark, the long coats, the iron-tipped staves they carried for "testing," which was a polite word for the same thing the harvest knife is a polite word for. They weren't hurrying. They paused at Master Ellery's dark doorway, spoke briefly to each other, and then — this is the part I'll remember on my deathbed — they turned their horses' heads, slowly, deliberately, toward my house.

The atlas against my chest pulsed hard, once, like a struck bell.

They stopped in the lane. One of them raised his stave, and the iron tip caught the moonlight, and I watched him frown at it — actually frown, turning the stave as if it had hummed a note he hadn't asked for. His companion said something. He shook his head. For a long moment they simply sat there, thirty feet from my door, and I stood in the dark holding a book that was shouting my name to the whole street, and I understood my mother's warning in my bones at last.

*They will feel it the way dogs feel storms.*

The hunters lingered another minute. Two minutes. Long enough for me to plan three escapes and abandon all of them. Then, for reasons I couldn't see and wouldn't understand until much later, the first hunter lowered his stave, said something short and sharp to his partner, and turned his horse away. They rode on up the lane, past my house, past the well, out toward the market square, and the atlas cooled against my ribs like a fire banking down to embers.

I stayed at the window until my legs cramped. Then I closed the shutter, lit one candle, and looked at the book.

"You," I said, "are going to get me killed."

The silver lines pulsed, gentle now, almost apologetic. Almost smug. If a map can be smug, this one managed it.

But here's the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, the splinter working under my skin: the hunter had hesitated. I'd seen it. He'd felt the atlas, felt *me*, and he'd chosen to ride on. Hunters didn't hesitate. Hesitation got hunters reassigned to border duty, or worse. Which meant either I'd imagined the whole thing in a sleepless fog —

— or somewhere in Greyhollow's iron coat of arms, a seam had just split, and something underneath was showing through.

I wrapped the atlas back in its leather strap and held it a moment longer than necessary. "All right, Mama," I murmured. "Guard it, use it, or forgive myself. You never did make the choices easy."

Outside, far off toward the Spire, a bell rang the second watch, and the sound rolled over the rooftops like a stone dropped into a very deep, very patient well.

I put the book under the floor. I did not sleep. And in the morning, when I opened my shutters to sell charms to nobody, I found a scrap of paper wedged into the doorframe, unsigned, in a blunt soldier's hand:

*Whatever you're hiding — hide it better. Next time it won't be me riding past.*

I read it three times. Then I burned it, which was the sensible thing to do, and spent the rest of the day wondering why the sensible thing felt so much like the beginning of something.
