## 5. A Bargain in the Rain

**CORVIN**

Rain in Greyhollow doesn't fall so much as seep — a fine grey misery that gets under collars and into bones and makes every rooftop drip like a leaky conscience. I'd been standing under the eave of a shuttered chandler's shop for two hours, watching the door across the lane, and by hour one my conscience was already leaking too.

The house belonged to Ulyana Amberdale.

I knew the name before I knew the face. Every hunter in the Spire's service knew it, though officially we weren't supposed to. Amberdale. Daughter of Maren Amberdale, cartographer, deceased, whose effects were catalogued and sealed nine years ago — except that somewhere between the catalogue and the vault, an atlas had gone missing. An atlas the High Inquisitor wanted found with a hunger that made me uneasy in the way a dog is uneasy before an earthquake. Vessal didn't want things. Vessal *acquired* them, and what Vessal acquired stopped belonging to anyone else, including itself.

And now his best tracker — that would be me, a title I'd have traded for a quiet posting anywhere it didn't rain — had been given a single instruction: *Follow the girl. Do not spook her. Bring me the book.*

Except I'd already failed at the first part of my career this week. The gifted girl on Tanner's Row — fourteen years old, magic so small it barely qualified, just enough warmth in her hands to keep her sick mother's fever down — I'd let her run. Looked the other way for exactly three seconds, which is all it takes to unmake a man. My commander, Dreegan, had watched me do it with eyes like two coins left out in the weather. He hadn't said anything. That was worse than saying something. Now there was a mark next to my name in a ledger I couldn't see, and marks like that don't fade; they accumulate interest.

So here I was, doing the arithmetic of a drowning man. Trade the Amberdale woman and her atlas for my reinstatement. One delivery, one ledger line scrubbed clean, one return to being a man who slept at night instead of a man who stood in doorways counting his sins.

Simple. Clean. A bargain.

I told myself that right up until she came out the door.

She moved like someone who'd practiced not being noticed, which is its own kind of tell. Hood up, satchel strapped crosswise, a wrapped bundle clutched against her ribs the way you hold something alive. She checked the street twice — once left, once right — and then did the thing that confirmed everything: she looked *up*, at the rain, as if reading it.

Gifted. Definitely gifted. Nobody reads rain without a reason.

I let her get half a block. Then another. Then I stepped out of the doorway and fell into step behind her, and the old training took over like a second spine: soft steps, matched rhythm, ghost-work. She turned down Cooper's Cut, past the tanneries where the smell could strip paint, toward the river district where the watch was thin and the alleys were honest about what they were.

That's when she cut into the covered market arcade, and I made my mistake.

Not a big mistake. A professional mistake. I followed her in too quickly, and somewhere between the fishmonger's stall and the shuttered fabric shop, the crowd thinned, and she stopped walking, and said, without turning around:

"You've been behind me since the chandler's. You breathe like a man trying not to."

I put my hand on the hilt of my blade — habit, not threat, or so I told myself — and stepped into the open. "Ulyana Amberdale."

"Corvin Ash." She turned. Up close she was thinner than her file suggested, with ink stains on her fingers and eyes the flat grey-green of a map's empty margins. "Yes, I know your name. Yes, I know what you are. No, I'm not going to run, because running is how people get caught, and I've spent five years not getting caught, thank you very much."

"I'm supposed to bring you in," I said. "You and the book."

"And yet." She tilted her head. "You're standing there talking to me instead of doing it. Which means either you're bad at your job, or you're negotiating."

*Both,* said the part of me I didn't listen to. "There's a version of tonight," I said carefully, "where you give me the atlas, and I report that I lost you in the Veilwood quarter. You keep your freedom. I keep my post. Everyone walks away."

"That's not a bargain," she said. "That's a ransom with better manners."

"It's the only offer you're going to get that ends with everyone breathing."

Something flickered across her face — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing their own arithmetic spoken aloud by a stranger. And then, because she was either braver or more foolish than her file indicated, she said:

"Come and see it first."

I should have refused. Every protocol, every instinct, every scarred lesson of ten years in the Spire's service said: take the book, take the woman, ask questions never. Instead I followed her through the arcade, out into the rain again, back the way we'd come, up the stairs to her rooms — because apparently I was the kind of traitor who got invited in first.

She unwrapped the bundle on her table with the care of a surgeon and the reluctance of someone opening a letter they already know is bad news.

It was an atlas. Worn leather, brass corners gone green, her mother's initials stamped on the cover. And when she opened it, I understood — finally, completely, with the full weight of ten years of willful blindness — why Vessal wanted it, and why the word *wants* wasn't strong enough.

The pages were moving.

Ink crawled across the parchment like living things. Coastlines breathed. A mountain range on the third page was, slowly, visibly, growing — and beyond the mountains, drawn in lines that shimmered like heat off summer stone, was a shape I knew. Every hunter knew it. We saluted it every morning.

The Spire.

Drawn from below. Drawn down into the earth, past its foundations, past the cellars and the sealed levels, to a place no hunter had ever been cleared to see: a vast chamber, and in it, rendered in ink that pulsed faintly gold, a wellspring. A sea of light, bound in chains of careful linework, feeding upward through channels drawn with terrible precision into the Spire's roots.

"They built it on top of it," Ulyana said quietly. "The Spire isn't a fortress. It's a cork."

"How long has it been drawing itself?"

"Since the night the hunters started knocking on doors." Her jaw tightened. "My mother mapped places that don't exist unless magic shows them to you. And this—" she touched the page, and the wellspring flared, and I swear the room got warmer "—this is the most real thing she ever found. It's awake, Corvin. It knows it's being drained. And I think the crown doesn't want the atlas because it's dangerous."

"No?"

"I think they want it because it's *instructions*."

The rain filled the silence. I stood in a fugitive's rented room, staring at the heart of everything I'd sworn to protect, and felt the last load-bearing wall of my career come down.

Because here was the thing I'd always known and never said: the testing at the Spire didn't add up. Gifted folk went in and didn't come out, and the official word was *relocation*, but relocation to where? The wagons went in empty and came out empty. And the land outside Greyhollow had been thinning for years — crops failing in soil that used to be rich, streams running sluggish, the Veilwood retreating year by year like a tide going out. We blamed weather. We blamed blight. We blamed anything except the tower we worked for.

A cork. Draining a wellspring. And the wellspring was the land, and the land was the magic, and the magic was the people in the wagons—

"Say it," Ulyana said, watching my face. "Whatever you just figured out. Say it out loud. It helps. Believe me."

"The crown isn't testing the gifted," I said. "They're harvesting them."

"There it is." She closed the atlas gently, like closing a coffin. "So. Your bargain, hunter. You hand me and this book to Vessal, he unlocks the wellspring, the crown becomes invincible, and every drop of magic in this country flows into one tower forever. Or you walk out that door and report that you lost me, and we both pretend tonight didn't happen."

"And option three?" I heard myself ask.

Her eyebrows rose. "You believe in option three?"

"I believe in knowing all the prices before I pay one."

"Option three," she said slowly, "is that you help me get this book somewhere Vessal can never reach it, and we figure out together what my mother was really guarding. Because she didn't hide this atlas, Corvin. She *armed* it. There are pages I haven't been able to open, seals I can't break alone, and I am one cartographer with a satchel and a talent for drawing places that don't exist. What I need—" and here her voice did something complicated, pride wrestling need to a draw "—is someone who knows how the Spire thinks. How it hunts. Where it will look."

"You need a hunter."

"I need *you*. Unfortunately."

I laughed. It surprised us both. Outside, the rain kept up its grey argument against the roof, and inside, a dead woman's map sat on a table between a traitor and a liar, waiting to see what we were.

"If I do this," I said, "and Vessal finds out — and he will find out, he always finds out — there's no coming back. Not for either of us. They'll burn our names off the rolls."

"Good," Ulyana Amberdale said. "Names are overrated. Mine's been a lie for five years anyway."

I looked at the atlas. At her. At the door behind me, which led to the life I'd had, and the window, which led to the rain and everything after.

"One condition," I said. "When this is done — whatever 'done' looks like — you draw me a map of somewhere warm. Somewhere with no towers in it."

"Deal," she said. "I'll even include roads."

**ULYANA**

Here is what nobody tells you about making a deal with a hunter: the hardest part isn't the moment you decide. It's the forty minutes afterward, when he's sitting at your table drinking your terrible tea, and you're both pretending you don't regret it, and every creak of the building sounds like boots on stairs.

He'd taken off his hood. Without it he looked younger and more tired, a man assembled from sharp angles and bad sleep, with a scar through one eyebrow that he probably thought made him look intimidating and actually just made him look like someone who'd survived something. He held his teacup wrong — like a weapon he'd been handed by mistake — and he kept glancing at the atlas on the shelf the way you glance at a sleeping animal you don't trust.

"You're staring at the book," I said.

"You're staring at me staring at the book."

"We're both very observant. It's why we'll make such a good team."

He almost smiled. It was a near thing, aborted halfway, like a man remembering smiling wasn't in his job description anymore. Except it wasn't his job description anymore, was it? That was rather the point.

"Tell me about the pages you can't open," he said.

I told him. The sealed leaves at the atlas's heart, bound shut with stitching that wasn't thread — hair-fine lines of silvered script that resisted everything I'd tried. Ink, heat, a knife. When I'd pressed my thumb to them, the script had drunk a bead of blood and shown me nothing but my own face, looking older.

"And you think your mother meant for someone else to open them."

"I think she meant for *me* to open them eventually, and I think she underestimated how much of her gift I didn't inherit." I turned my cup in my hands. "Or she meant for me to find someone. She wrote marginalia, Corvin. Little notes in the corners. *Two hands, one key.* I used to think it was a cartographer's joke about folding maps."

"And now?"

"Now I think my mother planned further ahead than I've ever planned anything, and I resent it deeply."

He did smile then, briefly, crookedly, and I noted with some irritation that it improved his face considerably, which was going to be inconvenient.

The plan we built that night was a bad plan. I want to be clear about that. It was a bad plan held together with geography and spite: leave Greyhollow before the week's end, follow the Veilwood's edge where my gift could read paths no warrant-chaser could follow, and make for the tollhouse road east, where my mother's notes mentioned names — old names, pre-outlaw names — of people who might know what the sealed pages needed. Two hands, one key. Maybe one of those hands was waiting at a tollhouse that hadn't collected a toll in nine years.

"A cartographer, a deserter, and a book," Corvin said, when we'd finished. "Vessal will send his whole elite guard after that."

"Then we'd better be worth chasing."

He gathered his hood, his weapons, his considerable capacity for trouble, and paused at the door with the rain silvering the air behind him. For a moment he looked like he was going to say something official — terms, conditions, a hunter's farewell. Instead he said:

"The tea really was terrible."

"I know. It's a disguise. Nobody suspects a woman who makes tea like that of harboring state secrets."

And then he was gone into the wet dark, and I stood alone in my little room with a living map and a decision that couldn't be untaken, and I felt — for the first time in five years of hiding — not safe, exactly. But less alone. Which is either the beginning of hope or the beginning of disaster, and history assures me they usually arrive holding hands.

I was blowing out the lamp when the atlas moved.

Not the pages. The *cover*. A faint shudder, like a sleeper turning over, and beneath my palm the leather warmed, and from somewhere deep in the sealed heart of the book came a sound I will hear until I die: a single clear note, like a struck bell underwater.

The book knew Corvin Ash had been in the room.

The book, I realized with a cold sliding feeling, had *approved*.

Outside, far off toward the Spire, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm. The third bell, the one hunters called the *summons*, the one that meant riders were moving. In this rain. At this hour.

Someone had already reported something.

I packed in four minutes flat.
