## 6. The Girl Called Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about running away: it is mostly hiding.

I had imagined running away as a great sweeping thing — wind in my hair, a road unrolling like ribbon, maybe a dramatic speech delivered over my shoulder to nobody. What I got instead was three days of crouching under hedges, eating a stolen apple so slowly I could taste every individual disappointment in it, and learning that my magic had opinions about my fear and expressed them by making things float.

Mostly spoons.

The convoy from the Spire had been taking us north along the river road when the wagon hit a rut and the lock on my cage-cart simply *gave up*. Not broke — gave up. Slid open like it had been waiting for permission. That was me. That was my magic doing the one useful thing it had ever done, and then immediately ruining it by lifting every iron nail out of the nearest fence post into a hovering cloud above my head while six hunters turned around.

So: running. Mostly hiding. Some floating cutlery.

By the fourth night I was in Greyhollow's outskirts, which everyone said was the worst place for someone like me because Greyhollow hated magic more than anywhere, which is exactly why I went there, because nobody hunts you where they've already decided you don't exist. I'd heard that from Tam, the older apprentice, before they took him up the Spire. He said it like a joke. It wasn't a joke anymore. Nothing Tam ever said was going to be a joke again, probably.

That was the night I found the cart.

* * *

It was parked behind a chandler's shop, tucked between rain barrels, a plain grey cart with a canvas top and a mule that looked at me with the deep personal offense of all mules everywhere. I was looking for somewhere warm. The cart looked warm. This is called logic.

I lifted the canvas flap and climbed in over a pile of crates, and I want it noted for history that I did not steal anything. I just lay down among somebody's inventory like a very small, very illegal blanket burrito, and told myself I'd leave before dawn.

I fell asleep in about nine seconds.

I woke to voices.

"—said the market, not the alley behind Marla's chandlery," a woman was saying, low and dry, the kind of voice that sounds like it has already thought of your argument and filed it under *no*. "If Vessal's hunters are sweeping the east quarter tonight, we go west."

"Then we go west," said a man's voice, deeper, careful. "But Ulyana. If they're sweeping the east quarter, they're looking for someone."

"They're always looking for someone. That's the whole job description now."

There was a pause, and then the woman said, in a completely different tone, flat and alert: "Corvin. The crates."

I held my breath. This is a known strategy of mine. Historically it has never once worked.

The canvas flap lifted and light poured in, and there was a woman silhouetted against it — dark hair coming loose from its braid, ink stains on her fingers, eyes that went from annoyed to alarmed in the space of one heartbeat. Behind her loomed a big man with a hunter's coat and a face like a closed door.

"It's a child," she said.

"It's a trap," he said.

"It's a *child*, Corvin."

"I heard you. It can be both."

I decided this was the moment for diplomacy. "I'm not a trap," I said. "Traps have better posture."

The woman made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The man did not move. His hand was resting on something at his belt in a way that suggested the something came out fast.

"Where are your parents?" she asked.

"Dead," I said, because it was true and because true things end conversations faster. "My master took me after. Master Ellery. He taught me letters and candle-dipping and how to keep my mouth shut about the other thing." My hands were doing the thing they do when I'm scared — prickling, itching, like bees under the skin — and I saw her notice, because her eyes flicked down and sharpened.

"The other thing," she repeated.

And here is where I should have lied. Tam would have lied. Tam was *good* at lying; that's why they took him second, after they took Master Ellery first, and I still don't know if those two facts are connected or if I just think about them until they look connected. But I was cold and hungry and tired of being a secret, and this woman had ink on her fingers like she used them for something besides dipping candles, and I was twelve years old and alone in a city that wanted people like me dead, and so instead of lying I said:

"Magic. I've got magic. Untrained. It does whatever it wants." I swallowed. "Please don't call the hunters. Please. They took everyone. They took Tam, and Tam didn't even do anything, his magic just — showed. Like mine shows. I ran from the convoy four days ago and I'm so tired and I'll work, I can dip candles, I can scrub anything—"

"Stop," the woman said.

I stopped. Bracing. This is the part where they turn you in. Everyone turns you in eventually; that's what outlawry means, it means the whole world is a door with a price on the other side of it.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into the cart, grabbed a crate, and pulled it out from under my head, which is honestly rude, and set it on the ground outside, and sat down on it, eye level with me, elbows on her knees.

"What's your name?" she said.

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren. Master Ellery said names are handles and handles get gripped."

Something moved through her face — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "He sounds like he was fun at parties."

"He's probably dead," I said. "Or up the Spire. Same thing, they say."

The man behind her shifted. "Ulyana," he said quietly, and it wasn't a warning exactly, but it had warning-shaped edges. "You know what harboring means now. It's in the edicts. It's treason."

"I can read, Corvin. I lettered half the forged edicts in this city before the crown started printing them properly." She rubbed her eyes. "Gods. Fine. Fine. Get in the cart, Wren. We're moving."

"That's—" Corvin started.

"You wanted to go west. West is a direction. Directions don't care who's in the cart." She stood up and looked at him, and something passed between them that I was too tired and too new to read, some whole conversation happening in eyebrow. He lost. I could tell he lost because he exhaled through his nose like a horse being saddled against its will.

"One rule," he said, pointing one thick finger at me. "No magic. None. Not a spark. Not a floating spoon."

"How did you know about the spoons?" I asked.

His jaw did something complicated. "Everyone floats spoons. It's always spoons."

"It's not always spoons," Ulyana muttered, climbing onto the driver's bench. "Sometimes it's knives. Get in, both of you. We're burning daylight we don't have."

* * *

Riding in the cart, I learned three things about my rescuers in the first hour.

One: Ulyana talked to her mule, whose name was Biscuit, with more tenderness than she talked to people, and Biscuit knew it and exploited it shamelessly, stopping every forty feet to smell something.

Two: Corvin sat facing backwards the entire time, watching the road behind us, and his hand never once left that spot near his belt, and when he caught me staring at it he didn't apologize, just said, "Habit," in a voice that made clear it was more than habit.

Three: neither of them had any idea what to do with a child.

This last one became obvious at the noon stop, when Ulyana handed me half a loaf of bread and an apple and then stood there watching me eat with the expression of someone who has rescued a stray cat and only now remembers cats need things. Ongoing things. Daily things.

"When did you eat last?" she asked.

"Yesterday. The apple."

"The apple you stole."

"The apple I *acquired aggressively*, yes."

Corvin snorted from his backwards perch. Ulyana gave him a look that could have curdled milk and then dug out more bread, and I understood, in the way you understand things sideways when you're twelve, that I had just been fed twice as much as planned and that this was going to be a pattern, and that neither of them had agreed to it out loud yet, and that it was happening anyway.

"Why did you take me?" I asked, around bread. Direct questions were another Tam lesson. Adults lie less when surprised.

Ulyana was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Because I spent five years pretending I wasn't what I am, and it kept me safe and it kept me nothing. And you're twelve." A shrug that didn't match her voice at all. "Someone should have done it sooner for other people. I can't fix that. I can fix Tuesday."

"Tuesday?"

"You. Today. Whatever day this is." She waved a hand. "I fix what's in front of me. It's a terrible strategy. Don't adopt it."

Too late, I thought. I'd been adopting it for four days without knowing it had a name.

* * *

The trouble started at dusk, because trouble keeps merchant hours and arrives punctually.

We were on the west road, past the last farms, when the bells started — the Spire's bells, carried on the wind from miles off, three notes repeating, and I felt them in my teeth before I understood what they meant. Corvin went rigid. "Sweep signal. They're closing the quarter gates."

"They're not closing *these* roads," Ulyana said, but her knuckles were white on the reins, and I could see her doing the same math I was: roads close, carts get searched, carts containing recently escaped apprentices get searched *thoroughly*—

And my magic, which has the survival instincts of a moth and the timing of a drunk uncle, chose that exact moment to panic.

It started small. The bread knife rose out of the supply box and hovered, quivering, at shoulder height. Then the tin cups. Then — gods help me — Biscuit's spare horseshoe, spinning slowly like it was auditioning.

"*Wren,*" Corvin said, in the voice of a man watching his own death arrive via cutlery.

"I'm sorry! I'm sorry, it does this when I'm scared, I can't—"

"Breathe," Ulyana said sharply, twisting around on the bench. "Wren. Look at me. Not the bells. Me. What's five things you can see?"

"What?"

"*Five things.*"

"Mule. Road. Tree. Your stupid hat. Knife."

"The knife is not helping. Four things you can hear?"

"Wheels. Bells. Wind. Corvin breathing like a bellows."

"I am *not*—" Corvin began, and the horseshoe dropped, and the knife dropped, and the cups clattered down into the straw, and my hands went cold and empty and quiet, and I realized I was laughing, a horrible hiccupping laugh, because the bells were still ringing and we were still probably doomed but the spoons — the *knife* — was down.

Ulyana watched me for a moment. Then she turned back around and said, to the road in general: "We're teaching her control. Starting tomorrow. Before she levitates us into a checkpoint."

"We're not teachers," Corvin said.

"No. We're worse. We're survivors." She flicked the reins. "Hold on, Biscuit. Faster, girl. The night's getting ideas."

* * *

They made camp in a hollow off the road, no fire, and I lay in the cart listening to them argue in whispers ten feet away, which they clearly believed was private, which it was not, because I have excellent hearing and no scruples.

"She stays with us," Ulyana said. "That's decided."

"It's decided because you decided it. That's not the same as deciding."

"She's a child, Corvin. Alone. With hunters sweeping every road."

"And when they catch us — and they will, eventually, because you drive a cart with the stealth of a parade — what happens to her then? You know what they do to gifted children at the Spire. I know better than you do." A silence. When he spoke again his voice had changed, gone rougher. "I've walked them down that corridor, Ulyana. I've stood outside the testing rooms. I know what the screaming sounds like through the door."

I stopped breathing.

Ulyana was quiet a long time. "Then we make sure they never catch us," she finally said. "All three of us. Because I am not handing her back to that place, and neither are you, or you'd have already turned us in, and you haven't. Why haven't you, Corvin?"

Another silence, longer. I heard him shift, leather creaking.

"Because I looked at her," he said at last, "and she looked at me like I was a door out. And I've been a door in too many times." A pause. "Go to sleep, Ulyana."

"You first."

"I don't sleep."

"You should try it. It's this thing where you fall over horizontally and stop being insufferable for eight hours."

Despite everything — the bells, the hunters, the corridor I now knew existed and would hear in my dreams forever — I smiled into the straw. And I felt it happen, that first small terrifying click, the one Master Ellery warned me about: the click of starting to belong to people. People you can lose. People whose sleeping-breathing you count.

I counted Corvin's anyway. Just to be sure he stayed.

* * *

Near midnight, Ulyana climbed quietly into the cart. I kept my eyes shut and my breathing even, professional-grade fake sleep, and she sat down beside me and didn't move for a while. Then she pulled the canvas higher over my shoulders, tucking it, clumsy about it, like someone who hadn't tucked anything in years.

"You're awake," she whispered.

"No I'm not."

A huff of breath that was almost a laugh. "Your mother teach you that trick?"

"My mother died when I was seven."

Silence. Then, softer: "Mine too. Recently." Her hand hovered — I felt it, the warmth of it, undecided — and then settled briefly, awkwardly, on top of my head, patting twice like she was testing a melon, and retreating. "Sleep, Wren. Tomorrow we start teaching you control. It's going to be awful. You're going to be terrible at it. I was terrible at it."

"Were you?"

"I once mapped a room so hard I fell out of it." She said it lightly, but underneath the lightness was something old and heavy, and I was too young and too tired to ask, though I would ask later, much later, when the asking cost more than either of us dreamed.

She climbed back down. I lay in the dark listening to the wind move through the trees, and the far-off bells had finally gone silent, and somewhere south of us a Spire full of stolen magic hummed like a struck string that wouldn't stop ringing.

I didn't know then what the atlas was. I didn't know what my new keeper carried folded in oilcloth beneath the false bottom of her supply chest, humming its own low note that only I, lying awake with my useless wonderful dangerous gift, could faintly feel — a pull, like a second heartbeat, patient and enormous.

But I felt it. Even then. Even that first night.

And it felt, faintly and terribly, like it was waking up.
