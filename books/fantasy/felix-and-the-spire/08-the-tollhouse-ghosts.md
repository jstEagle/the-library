## 8. The Tollhouse Ghosts

I had been a royal hunter for eleven years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things worth knowing: never trust a quiet road, never trust a full purse, and never, under any circumstances, trust a woman who says "it's just ahead" with the same confidence she'd used four miles ago.

"It's just ahead," Ulyana said.

The road ahead was a wall of grey mist and older trees, the kind that leaned over the path like gossips who'd heard something juicy about you and were deciding whether to share. The Veilwood's edge did that to a man. Made him feel discussed.

"Define 'ahead,'" I said.

Ulyana didn't look back at me from the cart bench. She'd stopped looking back at me somewhere around the second day, which I understood — I'd spent most of my career being the thing people didn't want behind them — but it still sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. "Half a league. There's an old tollhouse where the Amberdale Road crosses the mill stream."

"The Amberdale Road," I repeated.

"My mother named it." A pause. "Or it named her. The records were never clear, and she thought that was funny."

Wren, wedged between us on the bench like a cat who had decided this cart was hers now and we were merely staff, perked up. "Is your mother a ghost?"

"Wren."

"What? Ghosts are *useful*. Everyone knows ghosts guard treasure. If she's a ghost, do we get treasure?"

"She's dead," Ulyana said, in the flat way people say things they've practiced saying until the words wear smooth as river stones. "And no one guards anything anymore. It's outlawed."

"That's stupid," Wren announced, with the moral clarity of the twelve-year-old, which is to say, total and unburdened by consequence. I found I agreed with her, which was becoming a habit I'd need to break before it broke me.

The tollhouse appeared out of the mist the way things appear in stories: all at once, as if it had been waiting for us to be properly tired before showing itself. It was a low stone building straddling the road, its arch wide enough for carts, its upper floor long collapsed into a roof of moss and slate. Ivy owned the place outright; the crown had merely been permitted to visit, once, decades ago. The toll gate itself was gone, but the iron brackets that had held it still jutted from the stones like broken teeth.

"No toll," I observed.

"There hasn't been a toll since the Outlawry," Ulyana said. She reined the mule — a disagreeable animal named Duchess whom I had come to respect the way one respects weather — down to a stop under the arch. "My mother used to say the road remembers what it carried. That's why she liked tollhouses. They're where every journey has to stop and admit what it is."

"And what are we?"

She looked at me then, finally, and I wished she hadn't, because her eyes had that mapmaker quality — the sense of being measured, plotted, placed at coordinates I hadn't agreed to. "Fugitives," she said. "But you knew that when you chose it."

I had. That was the trouble with choices. They followed you around.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the arch, because Ulyana insisted the walls would hide our fire, and because I had learned over the past week that arguing with Ulyana Amberdale was like arguing with a river: technically possible, deeply unproductive, and you'd both end up wet.

Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, which was her gift, really — the girl could fall asleep talking, eating, standing. Her hands were still bandaged from the ford. Every time I looked at those bandages my old commander's voice played in my head: *collateral is acceptable, hunter*. And every time, some newer, quieter voice answered: *she is twelve*.

I was feeding the fire when the stones spoke.

Not metaphorically. Although with my week, I'd have believed metaphorical too. But no — a low hum came up through the flagstones, a vibration like a struck bell heard through the earth, and the ivy along the arch shivered though there was no wind. Ulyana went rigid beside me. Her hand went to the satchel where the atlas lived, the way other people touch a weapon or a prayer.

"Don't," she breathed. "Don't reach for your sword."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Your shoulder does a thing."

I lowered my hand from my shoulder, which apparently did *a thing*, and watched the darkness under the arch deepen. Not darken — *deepen*, the way water deepens, something moving beneath a surface that had seemed solid.

They came out of the walls the way smoke comes out of a doused fire: reluctantly, in pieces, and all at once when you least wanted it.

There were five of them. Old, mostly. Two women with hands like knotted roots, a man whose eyes had gone milk-pale but who moved without stumbling, a younger fellow with burn scars crawling up his neck, and one ancient creature folded into a chair that someone must have carried out here years ago, because chairs don't grow in doorways. Their clothes were patched grey-on-grey, road-colored, wall-colored. People who had learned to be architecture.

The pale-eyed man raised one hand, palm out, and the hum in the stones settled into silence.

"Amberdale," he said.

Every hair on my arms stood up. Because he wasn't looking at Ulyana. He was looking at the satchel.

"How—" Ulyana started.

"Child." The oldest one, in the chair, had a voice like dry leaves and the audacity of the very old. "We felt the atlas cross the stream before your mule's feet touched the water. Do you think a thing like that travels quietly? It sings. It has always sung. Your mother used to complain she couldn't sleep near it."

Ulyana made a sound I'd never heard from her — small, cracked, like a hinge unused for years. "You knew my mother."

"We *mourned* your mother," the pale-eyed man said. "There's a difference, and the difference took us years." He turned his blind gaze on me, and I had the distinct unpleasant sensation of being seen straight through, scars and all. "And this is the hunter."

"He's not—" Ulyana began.

"I'm the hunter," I said. No point lying to people who could hear a book singing across a stream. "Former. The crown and I recently parted ways over philosophical differences regarding the arrest of children."

The burned young man snorted. The old woman in the chair smiled with maybe six teeth, all of them pointed with intent.

"They always say that," she said. "The clever ones, anyway. Sit down, hunter. You look like a man who hasn't slept honestly in a year."

"I haven't slept honestly ever," I said, and sat.

* * *

Their names, offered grudgingly and in no particular order: Maren, the leaf-voiced elder. Oskar, the pale-eyed man, who had once been — I gathered — something called a wardwright, which sounded like a profession invented specifically to make me feel underqualified. Tam, the burned one, youngest at perhaps forty, who kept glancing at Wren sleeping against the cart wheel with an expression I recognized because I wore it myself lately. The two root-handed women were sisters, Pell and Sorrel, who communicated mostly in glances and finished each other's silences.

They had been the Tollkeepers, Oskar explained, passing me a tin cup of something herbal and punitive. Once, dozens of them. Keepers of the crossings, wardens of the roads where the Veilwood pressed close to the world of ordinary men. Before the Outlawry, their order had maintained the boundaries — not hiding magic, not hoarding it, but *tending* it, the way you tend a hedge so it doesn't swallow the lane.

"The Spire calls it chaos," Maren said. "Wild magic, unbound, dangerous. That's the sermon, anyway. Convenient, isn't it — the danger always seems to require gathering everything into one tower where careful men can mind it."

"I've heard the sermons," I said. "I gave some of them."

Nobody threw me out. I noted that with more gratitude than pride.

It was when Ulyana brought out the atlas that the evening changed shape.

She unwrapped it from its oilcloth with the care of a priest handling relics, and even I felt it this time — a faint pressure behind the eyes, a sense of vast attention turning toward us, like a lighthouse beam sweeping past. The Tollkeepers went very still. Then, one by one, they bowed their heads. Not to Ulyana. To the book.

"Stop that," Ulyana said sharply. "It's a book."

"It's a *keystone*," Oskar said. "Your mother didn't tell you?"

"She told me it was her life's work. She told me never to let the crown have it. She told me—" Ulyana's jaw tightened. "She told me a great many things in the language of *don't*, and almost nothing in the language of *why*. She died before she got around to explanations."

The old woman sighed, a sound like wind through a ruined house. "That was Sera. All lock, no key. She thought she was protecting you." A pause. "She was protecting everyone. There's a difference, and children aren't built to see it."

"Tell me," Ulyana said. Just that. Two words, stripped of everything, and I found my chest hurting on her behalf, which was becoming another habit I'd need to break.

Oskar turned his blind face toward the fire.

* * *

What he told us took the rest of the night, and I'll set it down here as straight as I can, because I've spent enough of my life delivering polished lies and I'm retired from the trade.

Before the Spire, there had been the Wellspring — a living source of magic rising through the land itself, threaded through roots and rivers and the deep bones of hills. Not owned by anyone. Tended by everyone, or near enough. The Tollkeepers were one strand of that tending; there were others, orders and circles and plain country hedge-witches, each keeping their piece.

Then came the war. Or rather — and here Oskar's voice went flat and bitter — came the *story* of the war. A border conflict, ugly but small, blown up by ambitious men into a tale of apocalyptic magical horror. Fear is the best mortar ever mixed. They built the Spire on top of the Wellspring itself, in the name of containing it, and wrote laws that pried magic out of ten thousand hands and funneled it, test by test, seizure by seizure, into that tower.

"The crown doesn't fear magic," Oskar said. "The crown *is* magic, now. All of it. One wellspring, one tower, one throne drinking from it. Your outlawed hedge-witch and your outlawed mapmaker weren't criminals, girl. You were *harvest*."

The atlas, he said, was different. Older than the Spire. Older than the war-story. A living map of the Wellspring itself — every vein, every thread, every hidden artery of it — drawn by hands that had understood what they were making. In the wrong grasp, it was a key to the whole hoard. Open it, bleed into it, speak to it, and a man could drink the Spire dry. Or worse: use it to bind the Wellspring tighter than ever, forever.

"In the right grasp," Maren said quietly, "it could do the opposite. Unbind it. Give it back."

"So Sera Amberdale didn't hide it," Tam said, watching Ulyana. "She *guarded* it. Twenty years in Greyhollow, sitting on the single most dangerous object in the kingdom, raising her daughter to be the next keeper, and never telling her — because a keeper who knows what she keeps is a target, and a keeper who doesn't know can pass any test, survive any interrogation. Ignorance was armor."

Ulyana stood up. Walked to the arch. Stood there a long while with her back to us, looking out at the dark road her mother had named.

I got up and went to stand beside her, because someone should, and because — I admitted it to the night, since I was admitting things — I wanted to.

"She lied to me," Ulyana said. "For twenty-five years. Every bedtime story, every 'someday I'll explain,' every time I asked why we couldn't go home to wherever home was. She lied."

"Yes," I said. Lying to her was apparently also a thing I did now.

"She was alone with it. All those years. She couldn't tell anyone, couldn't trust anyone, and she did it anyway, and she died doing it, and I thought—" Her breath caught. "I thought she was a coward. I thought she hid because she was afraid. I built my whole life on top of that. Five years in Greyhollow hating her a little for teaching me to hide."

"And now?"

"Now I find out she was a wall." Her voice broke on it, quietly, the way stone breaks — all at once after holding for centuries. "She wasn't hiding from the storm. She *was* the wall. And nobody ever thanked her, and nobody will ever thank her, and I spent five years ashamed of her."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything. Some griefs you can only stand next to.

After a while she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, like closing a ledger, and said, "The Spire's hunters are looking for a fugitive cartographer with a stolen book. They think it's evidence."

"It's not evidence," I said.

"No. It's a duty." She looked up at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "She guarded it alone. I don't intend to make the same mistake twice."

I want it recorded that I understood, in that moment, exactly what she meant, and that it terrified me considerably more than Vessal's elite hunters ever had.

* * *

We slept in shifts. I took first watch, sitting under the arch with my sword across my knees out of pure habit, watching the Tollkeepers bank the fire with gestures instead of tools.

Maren stayed awake too, wrapped in her chair-blankets, and after an hour she said, without preamble: "You loved someone the crown took from you. That's why you hunt the way you hunted. Badly. With apologies."

"That's not—" I started.

"Hunter. I'm ninety-one years old and half-blind and I read auras, badly, but yours is practically shouting." She poked the fire with a stick, unnecessarily. "Who was it?"

I hadn't spoken the name aloud in nine years. It came out easier than expected, worn smooth like Ulyana's grief. "My sister. Gifted. I was nineteen, newly sworn, and they tested her, and she didn't come back from the Spire, and they sent me a letter saying she'd been 'relocated for the realm's benefit.'" I turned the sword over in my lap. "So I became the best hunter they had. Figured if I was inside the machine, maybe I could find out where 'relocated' goes."

"And did you?"

"I found out it doesn't go anywhere. That's the answer nobody wants. There's no prison wing, no work camps. The testing either burns the gift out or burns the person out, and either way the paperwork says relocated." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eleven years. Eleven years of dragging people to that tower to keep a lie warm."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised, which was somehow the worst part. "And now you sit in a ruin with the last keepers of the old roads, guarding a book that could tear the tower down."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"Everything true sounds insane at first," she said. "That's how you know the lies have been in charge a long while."

Across the embers, Ulyana slept sitting up against the cart wheel, the atlas in her lap, one hand resting on its cover like a sentinel's. Wren had migrated in the night to curl against Ulyana's side, and Ulyana's arm had curved around the girl without her waking — instinct, already, the body learning family faster than the mind admits it.

Tam crouched beside me suddenly, quiet as ash falling. Up close, his burn scars mapped their own territory, ridge over ridge.

"The blind man didn't tell you everything," he murmured. "About the atlas. About what waking it costs."

"He told us enough."

"He told you what it *does*. Not what it takes." His eyes flicked to Ulyana, sleeping. "Every time it's opened, opened *properly*, it drinks from the one who holds it. Blood. Memory. Years, maybe. Sera knew. It's why she aged like a woman twice her age by forty. It's why she never let anyone else carry it — she wouldn't put that weight on another soul." He met my eyes. "The girl doesn't know. And when she finds out — because she will, she's her mother's daughter — she'll pay it gladly, and she won't tell you, and you'll watch her empty herself out page by page thinking she's the only one who has to be brave."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the dark wood, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Why tell me?" I said. "Why not her?"

"Because you're the one who'll notice," Tam said simply. "Keepers guard the book. Someone has to guard the keeper." He rose, brushed off his knees. "Watch her hands, hunter. When the ink starts costing more than it draws, you'll see it there first."

He walked back to the fire, and I sat under the broken arch of a road my — under a road *her* mother had named, holding a sword I no longer believed in, and watched Ulyana sleep with her hand on a book that was quietly eating her inheritance, her history, and — if Tam was right, and the sick certainty in my gut said he was — eventually herself.

Three days east, the Spire stood black against the sky, drinking.

And I understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever came next, I was no longer choosing between the crown and these two. I was choosing whether to watch Ulyana Amberdale spend herself alone, the way her mother had — or whether to stand in front of her and refuse, for the first time in my obedient life, to let duty mean standing aside.

Behind me, soft in her sleep, Wren whimpered and flung an arm out, and the embers flared green for half a heartbeat.

The Tollkeepers all turned to look at the child at the same moment.

"Well," said Maren, in the tone of a woman reading a headline she'd dreaded for ninety years. "That's new."
