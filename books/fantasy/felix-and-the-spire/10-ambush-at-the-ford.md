## 10. Ambush at the Ford

The river announced itself long before we saw it — a cold silver whisper threading through the pines, the kind of sound that makes a cartographer's fingers itch. I'd been dreaming about it for two days, honestly. Not the water itself, but the *mapping* of it: the way I could feel the ford ahead like a missing tooth, a gap in my mental atlas where the Veilwood's edge met the lowlands and every road worth the name converged.

Which was precisely the problem.

"Every road converges there," I said, reining the cart to a stop on the ridge. Below us, the Merrow ran wide and shallow over pale stones, glittering in the late afternoon light like spilled coins. "Every road, Corvin. Which means every hunter who can read a map knows exactly where we have to cross."

Corvin stood beside the cart with his arms crossed, wearing the expression he'd perfected over three weeks of travel — the one that said he agreed with me and hated that we were both right. "There's a bridge upstream. Two days north."

"A garrisoned bridge."

"There's a ferry downstream."

"Ferryman reports to the crown. You told me that yourself, back when you still thought honesty was a character flaw instead of a policy."

He exhaled through his nose. Behind him, Wren scrambled up the ridge from where she'd been crouched by the treeline, doing whatever it was she did out there — talking to plants, mostly, though she'd deny it under torture. Her hair had escaped its braid entirely and stood around her head like a startled hedge.

"So we swim?" she offered.

"It's autumn," Corvin said.

"So we swim *bravely*."

I almost smiled. Almost. But my eyes kept dragging back to the ford, and to the thing I hadn't told them yet, because telling them would make it real. The living map had been restless all morning. I'd felt it through the leather of my satchel like a cat pacing inside a box — pages shifting, ink crawling, redrawing itself in tight, urgent strokes. My mother's atlas only did that when something important was happening near one of its places.

And the ford was one of its places. Marked in her hand, in faded brown ink: *Merrow Crossing — where the old roads remember.*

"The atlas is agitated," I said quietly.

Both of them went still. They'd learned what that meant.

"How agitated?" Corvin asked.

"On a scale of 'mildly redrawn coastline' to 'the night the hunters came to my door'?" I pulled the satchel around and rested my hand flat on its cover. The leather was warm. It should not have been warm. "Closer to the second one."

Wren's face did the thing it always did when danger got mentioned — chin up, eyes too bright, twelve years old and determined to be brave in a way that made my chest hurt. "Maybe it's just excited about the river," she said. "Maps like rivers. You said so."

"I did say so." I had also, privately, noticed that the last time the atlas behaved this way, we'd found fresh hoofprints on a road that was supposed to be empty. "Let's look before we commit. Corvin — your eyes are better than mine for this sort of thing. What do you see?"

He was already moving, dropping low against the ridgeline, and I watched him become something else. It never stopped being unsettling, watching a man fold himself into a hunter. His breathing slowed. His gaze stopped being warm and started being *useful*, sweeping the far bank in methodical strips — trees, rocks, the reed beds, the dark seam of forest beyond.

I counted his breaths. At eleven, he stopped counting himself.

"Corvin?"

"Don't move," he said softly. "Any of you. Don't move."

My stomach dropped through the ridge and into the river below. "How many?"

"Six that I can find." A pause. His jaw worked. "Probably eight. They're holding position on the far bank, spread along the tree line. Waiting."

"For us to start crossing," Wren whispered.

"For us to be halfway across," Corvin corrected grimly. "Midstream. Nowhere to run, nowhere to hide, carrying everything we own in a cart that doesn't float. It's how I'd do it." He said it without pride, which somehow made it worse. "These aren't garrison men. Look at their spacing. That's Spire training. Elite."

"The ones from the Ford—" Wren started, then stopped, because we all flinched away from the word *ford* now.

"The ones who burned your hands," I finished for her, gently.

She looked down at her palms. The burns had healed into thin silver traceries that caught the light when she flexed her fingers — magic scalding its way out of a child who'd never been taught to hold it. She'd stopped complaining about the pain after the first week. That worried me more than the screaming had.

"So we go north," she said. "Two days. We can be quiet for two days."

"No," Corvin said. "We can't."

"Why not?"

"Because they're not waiting for us to cross *today*, Wren. They're waiting because they know we'll try the north road eventually, and someone's already riding to cut it off. This isn't an ambush." He finally turned back to face us, and I saw something in his face I'd only seen once before — the night he'd told me about the girl he'd let escape, back when he was still a hunter with a name and a uniform. "It's a funnel. They're herding us. Someone gave them our route."

Nobody said the obvious name. Nobody had to. The grandson of the tollhouse ghosts, the boy who'd taken Vessal's amnesty — his betrayal sat between us like a stone none of us could step over. I'd forgiven him in my head twice. Both times, the forgiveness had lasted about as long as it took to imagine Wren in a Spire cell.

"So what do we do?" Wren's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it — I watched her hate it, watched her jaw clench against the fear. "We can't fight eight elite hunters. Corvin, you said—"

"I know what I said."

"You said you'd never raise a hand against them again unless—"

"*I know what I said.*"

The silence afterward was awful. Wind moved through the pines. Down at the ford, the river kept whispering its silver whisper, indifferent to the fact that it was about to become either our road or our grave.

I put my hand on Corvin's arm. He didn't shake it off, but he didn't relax either. Every muscle under my palm was wound tight as bridge cable.

"They were your brothers," I said quietly.

"Some of them." His voice came out rough. "Dain was at my swearing-in. He held the rope when I climbed the Spire wall the first time, because I was green and shaking and he laughed at me until I stopped. Tamira taught me how to track in snow." He swallowed. "And every one of them knows exactly what happens to people we bring back to the Spire. And they're going to do it anyway, because duty is easier than doubt. I know, because I was them."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing on a ridge with a fugitive cartographer, a stolen apprentice, and a book that bleeds." He looked at me, and there it was again — that terrible, honest exhaustion. "Now I'm deciding which side of the river I live on."

* * *

We waited for dark. There wasn't really an alternative; crossing in daylight was suicide with extra steps, and the atlas had gone strangely quiet once the sun dropped, as if even my mother's ink understood the need for stealth. We ate cold bread and harder cheese. Wren fell asleep sitting upright against a wheel, mid-sentence, mid-argument about whether fish could be mapped. Corvin checked the straps on the cart four times. I pretended not to notice that he was checking them because his hands needed something to do besides shake.

At full dark, we went down.

The plan was simple, which should have been my first warning. Simple plans are for simple worlds, and ours had stopped being simple the moment my mother died holding a book that wanted things. Cross at the shallows, single file, wheels lifted and lashed, cart on Corvin's shoulders and my hands on the shafts, Wren between us. Quiet as drowned things. No magic — Wren especially, no magic, we'd drilled it, *nothing unless your life depends on it*.

The water hit my ankles like a knife. Then my knees. The Merrow in autumn was less a river than a very ambitious piece of ice, and by the time we reached the deep channel in the middle, my legs had gone from cold to theoretical. Corvin bore the cart above the waterline, jaw set, breath steaming, and I kept my eyes on the far bank — on the black line of trees where eight shapes might or might not be watching.

Halfway. We were halfway when the moon came out from behind the clouds.

And the whole world lit up silver, including the six figures stepping out of the treeline onto the bank we'd been aiming for.

"Halt!" The voice carried across the water, hard and official. A lantern flared — no, not a lantern. A witchlight, cold white and steady, held aloft by a broad-shouldered silhouette I recognized even at distance, because some voices carve themselves into you. Dain. Corvin's Dain, the rope-holder, the laugher. "In the crown's name, halt where you stand!"

Corvin set the cart down in the shallows with impossible gentleness, like he was laying down something sleeping. Then he stepped forward, water streaming off him, and raised empty hands.

"Dain Merrick," he called back. "It's Corvin."

"I know who it is." Another figure moved beside Dain — slighter, hooded, and I felt rather than saw the shape of a drawn crossbow. Tamira, probably. The tracker. "Traitor's name suits you fine."

"You don't have to do this. None of you do. What's in that Spire isn't testing, Dain. I've seen the records. I've seen what comes back—"

"What comes back is *order*," Dain said, and his voice cracked just slightly on the word, which told me he'd seen things too, and had chosen differently than Corvin. "You want to talk? Come out of the water. All of you. Hands empty."

Behind me, I heard Wren's breathing change.

I knew that breathing. Shallow, fast, catching on the inhale — the sound of a child whose body remembered fire before her mind could argue with it. I turned, slowly, keeping my hands visible, and found her eyes huge and fixed on the crossbow, on the witchlight, on the eight dark shapes closing along both banks now, and I thought, with the strange clarity of disaster: *she's going to break.*

"Wren." I kept my voice low and level, the voice I used on skittish horses. "Look at me. Just me. Breathe with me. In—"

The crossbow bolt left the string.

I'll never know if Tamira fired wild or if she aimed for Corvin and missed, or if some flicker of movement — mine, Wren's, the cart groaning in the current — pulled her shot. It didn't matter. The bolt hissed past my shoulder close enough to part my hair and buried itself in the cart's bed with a *thock* that sounded, in the frozen moment afterward, like the world's smallest door slamming shut.

Wren screamed.

Not with her throat. With everything else.

Light erupted out of her — not the wild orange flare of the ambush at the actual ford weeks ago, but something new, something that made my teeth ache and the hairs on my arms stand up and the *river itself* shudder. The water around her went rigid, silver-white, freezing outward from her small body in a blooming flower of ice — and then it didn't freeze, it *rose*, a wall of the Merrow hauling itself up into the air, gleaming and groaning, a wave standing ten feet tall between us and the hunters like a hand raised against a blow.

The witchlight guttered. Somewhere on the bank, a man shouted. The wave hung there, trembling, impossibly held, and at its center stood a twelve-year-old girl with her burned hands outstretched and tears freezing on her cheeks, holding back a river with nothing but terror.

"Wren," I breathed. "Wren, sweetheart, you can let it down now—"

"I can't," she sobbed. "I *can't*, it won't—"

The ice beneath our feet began to crack.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do. The wave collapsed — not toward the hunters, thank every merciful star, but sideways, dumping a hundred tons of river back into itself with a roar that swallowed all other sound. The shockwave of it knocked half the hunters off their feet. The ice shelf under the cart shattered, and the cart lurched, and Corvin made a choice — I saw him make it, saw the exact instant he chose, his old life on one bank and us on the other — and he threw himself under the axle and *heaved*, roaring, driving the cart up onto the far gravel bar as the current tried to take it.

A bolt sparked off a stone near his head. He didn't even look at the shooter.

"Dain!" he bellowed over the water. "Last chance! Walk away!"

For one heartbeat — one — the witchlight wavered. I saw Dain's face across the ruined ford, young and old at once, and I saw the doubt land in it like the bolt had landed in the cart.

Then Tamira shouted an order, and the moment drowned.

We ran. There's no dignity in how we ran — soaked, staggering, hauling a screaming child and a groaning cart up the far bank while bolts hissed past and the river, betrayed and boiling, tried to reclaim everything we'd taken across it. Corvin cut the harness loose and we dragged the cart by main strength into the trees, and behind us the witchlight swung and swung, hunting, while Wren's magic — still leaking out of her in spasms, frost feathering across every branch she touched — painted our trail silver for anyone with eyes to follow.

We ran until the trees thickened and the light died and my lungs filed a formal complaint. Then we ran more.

* * *

We stopped in the hollow of a granite outcrop sometime past midnight, when Wren simply folded, mid-stride, like a puppet with cut strings. Corvin caught her before she hit the ground. I built a fire small enough to hide in a teacup, because some rules matter less than others, and a freezing wet child outranks operational security.

Her hands were the worst of it. The old silver scars had gone angry red again, split and weeping, magic scalding its way out through the same wounds it had carved before. She cried while I cleaned them, silently, furiously, hating every tear.

"I broke the rule," she whispered. "No magic unless—"

"You lived," I said. "Rule upheld."

"But I couldn't stop it. Ulyana, I couldn't *stop* it, it just — it used me. Like I was the map and something else was drawing." Her voice pitched higher, cracking. "What if next time it's worse? What if I hurt *you*? Or Corvin? What if I'm not—"

"Stop." I took her scarred hands in mine, careful of the burns, and made her look at me. "Listen to me, Wren Amberdale—"

"You can't just *adopt* me into your surname whenever it's dramatic—"

"Wren *whatever-your-name-is*," I pressed on, because she was right and I refused to let her be right, "listen. That power saved three lives tonight. Yours, mine, and the man currently pretending he isn't listening from over there by the cart. Whatever it costs you, whatever it scares you with — it is not a monster. It's a storm. And storms can be learned." I tucked a strand of hair behind her ear. "I know, because I spent five years learning to hide mine, and I promise you, hiding is the lonelier road. You'll learn to steer. I'll teach you. Corvin will teach you. Even the atlas likes you, and the atlas has standards."

She stared at me. Then, slowly, she tipped sideways into my chest, and I wrapped my arms around her, and she shook apart and rebuilt herself against my shoulder while the tiny fire hissed and Corvin kept watch with his back to us, giving us privacy the only way he knew how — by standing guard against a world that had earned his suspicion.

Eventually her breathing slowed into sleep. I laid her down on the bedroll, and she grabbed my sleeve in her sleep, and I decided the sleeve was hers now. Possession is nine-tenths of family.

Corvin came and sat beside me at the fire's edge. For a long time neither of us spoke. Across the hollow, the cart dripped. Somewhere far behind us, faint as memory, the river went on.

"I saw him hesitate," Corvin said at last. "Dain. When the wave came up. One heartbeat where he wasn't sure." He turned his hands over, studying the blisters the cart's axle had left. "One heartbeat, Ulyana. Five years ago I'd have given anything for a commander who hesitated like that. Tonight I nearly died hoping for it."

"He didn't walk away."

"No. But he didn't push the attack, either. Tamira had to shout him into it." He looked at me, and the firelight did something unkind and honest to his face. "If it comes to it — if they corner us again and it's steel instead of words — I don't know yet whether I can raise a blade against them. And I don't know which answer frightens me more."

I reached over and closed my hand around his. His fingers were cold and blistered and they curled around mine like they'd been waiting for permission.

"Then don't decide tonight," I said. "Tonight you carried a cart up a riverbank. That's enough deciding for anyone."

He huffed — almost a laugh — and squeezed my hand, and for a moment the hollow felt almost safe.

Then the satchel warmed against my hip.

I froze. So did Corvin, reading my face. Slowly, I drew the atlas out and opened it on my knees, and my breath caught.

The living map had redrawn itself. The ford lay behind us now, marked with a smear of dark ink like a bruise. But ahead — two days north, exactly where Corvin had said the garrisoned bridge waited — new lines crawled across the page in my mother's handwriting, fresh and urgent, circling a point on the north road over and over, as if the map were trying to shout.

Beneath the circling lines, in letters that seemed to darken even as I read them:

*They are already there.*
