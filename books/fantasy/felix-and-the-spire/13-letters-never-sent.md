## 13. Letters Never Sent

The sanctum smelled like dust and old decisions.

That was my first thought, standing in the antechamber while Wren's magic — wild, untrained, and frankly alarming — finished unsealing the last door. Dust I understood. Every garrison barracks I'd ever slept in had it. But old decisions have a smell too, a kind of dry rot that gets into the stone when people stop coming back to check on what they left behind. I'd smelled it in the Spire's archive once, as a young hunter, and been told it was the scent of *order*.

It wasn't. It was the scent of things nobody wanted read.

"Corvin." Ulyana's voice came from somewhere past the doorway, hushed in the way she got around her mother's atlas — reverent, careful, like the whole room might bruise. "You should see this."

I should have said no. That was becoming a pattern with me: the smart answer was always no, and I kept saying yes anyway. Yes to letting the gifted girl go. Yes to cornering the cartographer instead of arresting her. Yes to running. Yes to all of it, one small treason at a time, stacked up like cordwood until the pile was tall enough to burn a career on.

"Yes," I said, and went through the door.

* * *

The chamber beyond was round, carved into the mountain's bones, and lit by lamps that had no oil and no flame — just a soft gold glow held in glass, still burning after who knew how many years. Shelves lined the walls floor to ceiling. Not books, exactly. Records. Ledgers. Bundles of letters tied with ribbon gone brittle as autumn leaves. A long table dominated the center, its surface scarred with ink stains and candle burns, and on it lay documents arranged with the tidy desperation of people who expected someone to come looking.

Nobody had come looking. Not for decades, maybe longer.

"They were archivists," Ulyana said. She stood at the table with the atlas open beside her, and there was something strange in her face — not wonder, exactly. More like recognition. "Before the outlawry. Mages who worked for the crown itself. They saw what was coming and hid everything down here."

"Hid what?" Wren asked. She'd perched herself on a stool too tall for her, feet swinging, trying very hard to look like she belonged in a secret vault full of forbidden history and not like a twelve-year-old whose hands were still bandaged from the ford. Her voice cracked on the question. She covered it with a cough.

Ulyana didn't answer right away. She slid a document across the table toward me — a heavy sheet, official seal, the kind of thing I'd spent ten years delivering without reading because reading was above my station.

"Read it," she said. "Out loud, if you can manage."

"I can read, cartographer."

"I know. I want to hear it in your voice. The voice they trained."

So I read it. And my voice, the voice they trained, betrayed me halfway through.

*By accord concluded this ninth day of Harvestmoon, in the four hundredth year of the Concord, the hostilities between the Crown of Greyhollow and the Free Conclaves are hereby ended. Neither party shall raise arms against the other. Neither party shall seek dominion over the wellsprings of the other. The magic of the land shall remain with the land, and the peoples thereof, in perpetuity.*

Four hundred years old. Signed by a king and, beneath his name, three signatures marked with sigils I recognized from my earliest training — the Conclave marks we'd been taught to draw a line through whenever we found them, the way you'd cross out a curse word in a child's primer.

"That's a treaty," I said. My voice came out flat. "A peace treaty."

"The war ended," Ulyana said quietly. "It didn't end the way the Spire teaches it. There was no great victory. No righteous purge. They *signed*, Corvin. Both sides sat down at a table and signed, and the magic stayed in the ground where it belonged, and everyone went home."

I set the page down. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Ten years of training will do that — teach your body to stay calm while the rest of you comes apart.

"There's more," she said.

Of course there was more. There is always more; that's the trap of truth. It doesn't hand you one clean knife. It hands you a drawer full of them.

* * *

We worked through the night. Wren fell asleep around midnight, curled on the stool with her head on a stack of sealed correspondence, and neither of us woke her. Some things a child shouldn't have to hold yet. She was already carrying enough — burned hands, a convoy behind her, a family she wouldn't speak about. Let her sleep on other people's secrets for one night.

Ulyana read. I sorted. That division suited us both: she had the gift for understanding what the pages meant, and I had the training to know what they *were*. Sealed orders. Supply requisitions. And, buried in a strongbox under the table, the thing I'll be turning over in my mind for the rest of my life, however much of it I have left.

Ledgers. Crown ledgers, spanning two centuries, tracking the flow of magic into the Spire.

Here is what I learned in that cold round room while the girl I was starting to love read numbers aloud in a voice gone hoarse:

The war ended in treaty. The treaty held for sixty years. And then a king — I won't grace him with the name; he doesn't deserve the memory — looked at the wellspring beneath what would become the Spire and decided that *in perpetuity* was a word for other people. He built the Spire on top of it. He called it a monument to peace. Then he passed the outlawry — not because magic had done anything, but because a kingdom that hoards the only magic has a monopoly, and monopolies need laws.

Every gifted citizen dragged to the Spire for testing. Every charm-burner, every hedge-witch, every child like Wren. They weren't being contained. They were being *harvested*. The testing wasn't a test. It was a tithe. The Spire drank what they carried and stored it in the wellspring, and the wellspring fed the crown, and the crown called it order.

And the land outside the walls — the failing crops, the rivers running thin, the winters coming earlier every year — that wasn't fate. That was *withdrawal*. The world bleeding slowly because someone had opened a vein and never stopped drinking.

I sat back from the table at some point near dawn and found Ulyana watching me.

"Well?" she said. "Say something. You've been quiet for an hour, and quiet hunters frighten me."

I laughed. It came out wrong — half breath, no humor. "I arrested a woman in Fallmere three years ago. Herb-healer. Old. She didn't even run." I turned a ledger page I wasn't reading. "She told me the land was sick because we were taking from it. I wrote it down in my report as *delusional sympathy for outlawed practices*. They commended me for thoroughness."

"Corvin—"

"There's a commendation plaque in the garrison hall with my name on it." I said it to the ledger, not to her. "For that."

She reached across the table and put her hand over mine. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold and I didn't move away, which by the accounting of my former life was its own confession.

"You couldn't have known," she said.

"No. But I could have *looked*. That's the difference, isn't it? They didn't blind us. They just made sure we never wanted to see." I closed the ledger. "I was good at not wanting to see. I was the best they had."

* * *

While Ulyana slept — she fought it, lost, and pretended afterward that she hadn't — I did the thing I'd been circling since midnight like a dog around a wound.

I found paper. Real paper, good stock, sitting in a drawer with a jar of ink that hadn't dried out, because preservation spells apparently don't discriminate between noble truths and petty comforts. And I sat at the long table with the ghosts of dead archivists all around me, and I wrote a letter.

To Commander Haleth.

My commander. My teacher. The man who pulled me out of the gutter at fourteen, taught me to hold a blade and a warrant and my tongue, and told me — the day I made full hunter, his hand on my shoulder in front of the assembled ranks — that I was proof the crown lifted up men who deserved lifting.

*Commander,* I wrote.

*By now you'll have my name on the traitor lists. I want you to know the lists are wrong, but not the way you think. I'm not a traitor. I'm the last honest man in a dishonest uniform, and it took me ten years and one stolen map to notice.*

*I've seen the treaties. The real ones, from before. The war ended, sir. It ended before your grandfather drew breath, and everything we've been told since is a story the crown tells so it can keep drinking. The outlawry isn't law. It's a lid on a well. And every person we ever dragged to the Spire — the healer in Fallmere, the boy in Ashford, all of them — we weren't keeping the realm safe. We were keeping the cellar stocked.*

*I don't write this to change your mind. I know better. You taught me too well for that — you taught me that evidence is just noise until command decides what it means. I write it because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a executioner's list.*

*You were the best thing the crown ever gave me. That's why leaving it feels like dying.*

*Corvin*

I read it four times. I thought about sending it — there were ways, once; a courier here, a sympathetic clerk there, though most of those bridges were ash now that my name was mud. I thought about how it would land on Haleth's desk. I could see him reading it, jaw working, eyes flat, and then I could see him feeding it to the stove and marking my file *unrepentant* in red ink, because that was the only ending his life had room for.

And then I thought about Wren, asleep on her stool with her bandaged hands curled shut like sleeping birds. About Ulyana, who hid for five years because the alternative was this exact fire. About the healer in Fallmere, whose name I never learned because names were above my station too.

Haleth had made his choice thirty years ago. He'd made it every morning since, cheerfully, with a straight back and a polished badge. A letter wasn't going to crack that. Nothing was going to crack that, except maybe the sound of the Spire coming down, and if I did my job right, he'd hear it soon enough.

I took the letter to one of the eternal lamps.

Paper burns fast when it's true. I watched Haleth's name curl black, then my own, then the words *you were the best thing the crown ever gave me*, which was the truest sentence in the whole letter and the reason it had to die. You can't hand the people who broke you the map of how they did it.

"What are you burning?"

Wren. Awake, of course. Children wake at the worst moments; it's practically their trade. She stood rubbing her eyes with the backs of her bandaged hands, hair flattened on one side, watching the last corner of the letter go up.

"A letter," I said.

"To who?"

"Someone who already knows what it says. He just doesn't believe it yet."

She considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to everything, then nodded once, satisfied, as if I'd given a correct answer. "My gran says you should never send letters when you're angry. Only burn them." A pause. "She also said never trust a man with a neat beard, so I don't know how much of it holds up."

"It holds up," I said, watching the ash drift. "More than you'd think."

* * *

Ulyana found me an hour later at the far shelves, where I'd gone to do the thing I was actually good at: inventory. If we were going to carry the truth out of this mountain, we'd need to carry it light. I'd set aside the treaty itself, the strongest ledgers, the requisitions proving the harvest quotas — a hunter's satchel worth of proof, enough to hang a kingdom.

She came up beside me and looked at the selected pile without touching it.

"You're planning something," she said. "You get a particular look. Like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"I always look like that."

"True." But she didn't smile, and neither did I, and the silence stretched until one of us had to break it. It was me.

"When we come out of this mountain," I said, "Vessal will already know where we've been. Sanctums like this leave traces — Wren's door-opening alone probably rang like a bell in whatever he uses for scrying. So we don't run anymore. Running is what they're built to catch." I tapped the satchel. "We go to the Spire. We put this in front of the one audience Vessal can't control."

"The people," Ulyana said slowly.

"The city. The ones whose neighbors disappeared. Whose farms are dying. Everyone's been told the Spire is the price of safety. Show them it's the cause of the sickness, and the price stops making sense." I shrugged, aiming for casual and knowing I missed. "It's a bad plan. It'll probably get us killed. But I've spent ten years enforcing a lie, and I find I'd rather die correcting the record than live extending it."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and straightened my collar — a small, absurd gesture, the kind you'd give a recruit — and left her hand resting flat over my heart like she was checking whether it still worked.

"It's a terrible plan," she agreed. "But it's ours, and I've had worse." Her eyes went past my shoulder, to the atlas lying open on the table, and something flickered across her face — quick, quickly hidden. Pain, maybe. Or fear. "Besides. I may have an idea about the wellspring that doesn't involve dying at all."

"Careful," I said. "Hope's heavier than it looks."

"I know." She picked up the satchel and slung it over her shoulder, and for just a moment, standing among the records of everyone who'd ever tried to tell the truth and failed, she smiled like someone who intended to succeed. "But I've carried heavier."

Above us, faint but unmistakable, the mountain hummed — the sanctum's wards waking, one by one, like watchmen lighting lamps.

Something was coming up the road.
