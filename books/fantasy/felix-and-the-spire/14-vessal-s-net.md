## 14. Vessal's Net

The atlas had started dreaming before I did.

I woke in the grey hour before dawn with my hand pressed flat against its cover, as if I'd been holding it down the way you hold down a lid on something that wants to boil over. The leather was warm. It was never warm. And beneath my palm I could feel a faint pulse, arrhythmic and wrong, like a heart remembering how to beat after years of pretending to be dead.

"Ulyana." Corvin's voice from across the dead fire, low, already awake. He'd taken the last watch without telling me again. "It's been doing that since moonset."

"You didn't wake me?"

"It stopped twice. I thought—" He rubbed his jaw, where three days of stubble had gone from scruffy to genuinely disreputable. "I thought maybe it was done."

Nothing about that atlas was ever *done*. It was my mother's life's work and her mother's before her, and lately it had developed opinions. Opinions with teeth.

Wren slept curled against the cart wheel with her bandaged hands tucked under her chin like a sleeping bird's folded wings. The burns from the ford were healing — healing too fast, if anything, which Corvin said wasn't normal even for gifted children, but Wren just shrugged and said everything about her wasn't normal, thanks very much for noticing. I envied her ability to say things like that out loud.

I opened the atlas by firelight that wasn't quite firelight anymore, because the coals had gone to ash an hour ago and yet I could still see the page clearly, which was its own quiet horror.

The living map had changed overnight.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about the tollhouse ghosts: they weren't ghosts at all, not properly. Old Maren and her people were what remained of the pre-outlaw magical communities — the ones who'd gone to ground when the crown outlawed enchantment, who'd hidden in ruined tollhouses and forgotten waystations along roads that no longer appeared on any honest map. They'd recognized the Amberdale name the moment I walked in with my mother's atlas slung across my back like a confession. Maren had held my face in her spotted, steady hands and said, *"So she finally sent her daughter,"* and I hadn't known what to do with that, so I'd cried into her shoulder like a child, which at least solved the problem of what to do with my mouth.

They'd fed us. Hidden us. Drawn us routes in chalk dust on the tollhouse floor. And Maren had told me the truth I still hadn't finished digesting: my mother hadn't been hiding the atlas all those years. She'd been *guarding* it. Standing watch over a weapon she hoped to die before using.

Well. She'd gotten half her wish.

Now, in the ash-grey light, the living map showed me the tollhouse — a small inked square that redrew itself whenever I looked away and looked back — and around it, moving in slow, deliberate strokes, new lines were appearing. Lines I recognized because I'd drawn their cousins myself, back when I still drew maps of realms that existed only through magic.

Hunter's marks. Converging. Four of them, then six, tightening around the little square like a noose being tested for weight.

"No," I said, out loud, stupidly, as if the map cared about my opinion.

Corvin was beside me instantly. He read maps the way he read terrain — fast, silent, and with his whole body going still in that particular hunter's way that meant every part of him was about to move at once. "That's the tollhouse."

"They went back." My voice came out flat and strange. "After we left, they went back. Maren said they'd scatter, she promised me—"

"Maren's eighty years old, Ulyana. Her knees gave out on the stairs while we were there. Where exactly was she scattering *to*?" He said it gently, which made it worse. Gentleness from Corvin always meant he'd already accepted a thing I was still fighting.

The lines on the page kept drawing themselves. One of them paused at the edge of the tollhouse square, hesitated — I swear the ink hesitated — and then a small symbol bloomed beside it. A circle with a vertical stroke. The mark the Spire's elite used for a *capture*, not a kill.

"They want someone alive," I whispered. "Someone who knows where we went."

* * *

We broke camp in silence, which is its own kind of conversation. Wren didn't ask questions; she saw our faces and simply climbed into the cart and started repacking the rope coils, tight and precise, the way she did everything when she was frightened. Corvin harnessed the mule. I sat in the cart bed with the atlas open on my knees and watched the ink finish its terrible work.

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, the map showed me a tollhouse with its doors standing open and no lights inside, and a line of small figures being marched westward, toward Greyhollow, toward the Spire, each one rendered in strokes so fine they might have been hair.

And one figure apart from the others. Larger. Slower. With a mark beside it I'd never seen before: a circle with a vertical stroke, and through the circle, a horizontal line.

"What's that one mean?" Wren asked. She'd come to stand at my shoulder without my hearing her, which should have been impossible in a cart bed full of dry leaves. Children learn silence young when silence keeps them safe.

"I don't know," I said.

"You do know," she said. "You're doing your face."

"My face?"

"The one where you look like you're reading bad news in a language you're hoping is wrong." She pointed at the horizontal line. "That one's different from the others. Different means important. Important means bad. That's how it always goes."

Out of the mouths of runaways. I closed the atlas.

"We need to know what happened at that tollhouse," Corvin said. He'd come around to the cart's side and stood with his arms crossed, looking east the way he did when he was calculating distances to things he wished were farther away. "There's a village two hours off our route. Harrow's Bend. Maren mentioned it — said her daughter's son kept a smithy there. Tam. If anyone heard word of the raid—"

"Then going there is walking into the exact place the hunters will also think to go," I said.

"Yes," Corvin agreed.

"And we're going anyway."

He looked at me then, and there it was — the thing that had been growing between us since the rain, since the bargain, since the night I held Wren through her burns and he stood watch and never once suggested, not even in the safety of his own eyes, that the girl was more trouble than she was worth. "You'd go if it were your blood kin," he said. "Maren held your face and called you her own. That makes Tam something adjacent to family. And you don't leave family to be questioned alone."

*I know,* I wanted to say. *That's rather the problem.*

* * *

Harrow's Bend smelled of coal smoke and turnips and had the particular hunched quality of villages that had learned, over five law-bound years, to keep their eyes on their own doorsteps. We left the cart in a copse outside town — Corvin's idea; a cart with a Veilwood-edge dust pattern on its wheels asks questions — and walked in separately, Wren between us wearing Corvin's spare cloak with the hood up, playing the role of a sick cousin being escorted to an herbalist. She played it beautifully right up until she sneezed with the force of a small cannon, but the street was empty and the gods, wherever they'd gone since the outlawry, were merciful about sneezes.

Tam's smithy was easy to find: the only building in Harrow's Bend with a sign freshly painted, which in a village this frightened meant either courage or stupidity, and often both. The man himself was built like his anvil and had Maren's same unbothered grey eyes, though his hands, when he took mine, were shaking.

"You're the Amberdale girl," he said. Not a question. "She described you. Said you'd come asking eventually, and that I was to tell you the truth even if it cost me, because lies had cost our family enough already." His jaw worked. "I'm guessing you've seen the map."

"The map showed me soldiers."

"Soldiers found them four nights ago." He said it the way men deliver news they've rehearsed saying, because rehearsal is the only way to get through it. "An Inquisitor himself led them. Vessal. Tall, grey-eyed, polite the way a knife is polite. He didn't burn the tollhouse. Didn't hurt the old ones. Just... asked. Politely. For hours. And then he took them west, all eleven, and posted a notice on my door before he left."

"A notice."

Tam reached under his workbench and brought out a paper, and I will give the Spire this much: their typography is impeccable. Even the cruelty is well-set.

**AMNESTY PROCLAMATION**, it began. *Any person providing information leading to the recovery of the Amberdale atlas shall receive full pardon for crimes of harboring, past and future, and protection under crown law for themselves and their household.*

"Past and future," Corvin read aloud behind me, in the voice he used for things that disgusted him professionally. "They're pardoning crimes that haven't been committed yet. That's optimistic, even for Vessal."

"There's more." Tam wouldn't meet my eyes now. He spoke to the anvil instead. "Vessal came here. To the forge. Sat right where you're standing and drank my tea and told me my grandmother would be treated gently if the atlas came home quietly. Told me the old woman's heart was weak and the Spire's cells are cold, and that cold and weak make short arithmetic." His fists clenched on the bench. "And then he thanked me for my time and left, and he didn't threaten me, that's the thing, he never once threatened me, he just — *left the arithmetic sitting there.*"

Wren made a small sound. When I glanced down, her hood had fallen back and she was staring at the proclamation with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing cold-and-weak arithmetic of their own.

"Did you tell him anything?" I asked Tam. "About us? About the route?"

"No." He said it fast, too fast, the way a man says a thing he needs to be true. "No. Nothing. I don't know your route. I don't want to know your route. But Ulyana—" and now he did look at me, and Maren's grey eyes were wet, "—he'll be back. Men like that are patient the way rivers are patient. And I've got a wife and two girls, and I love my grandmother, and I am telling you honestly, as one keeper-of-ugly-truths to another: I don't know which way I'll break when he comes back. So whatever your route is, make sure it stops being anywhere near Harrow's Bend."

* * *

We walked out of the village faster than we'd walked in, and nobody spoke until the copse swallowed the cart again, and then Wren exploded.

"He's going to tell them." She whirled on us, hood thrown back, eyes blazing with a fury far too large for her small frame. "That smith. He's going to tell them everything. I *saw* it — he couldn't even look at you! People who can't look at you are already halfway to betraying you, my master at the Spire said—"

"Wren." Corvin crouched to her height, which he'd learned to do somewhere in the last weeks without my noticing when. "What you saw was a man terrified for his children. That's not the same as treachery."

"It ends up in the same place!"

"It does," Corvin said quietly. "But it matters *why*."

I turned away from both of them and opened the atlas, because there was something I needed to check, and I needed to check it before the dread in my chest finished hardening into certainty.

The living map had updated itself again. The column of captured figures marching west had grown a tail: new lines, fresh-drawn, peeling off from the Spire road and bending north. Toward the mountains. Toward first frost country. Toward the sanctum of the unwritten, where the ghosts had sent us, where the sealed knowledge waited, where we were headed right now with every step.

And beside the northernmost of those new lines, small and precise as a signature, the map had drawn a figure I recognized with a lurch that dropped through me like a stone through deep water.

A tall figure. Grey-inked. Standing very still amid all the marching strokes, the way a spider stands still in the center of its web.

Vessal wasn't waiting in Greyhollow for the atlas to be delivered. He was coming for it himself. And he wasn't following our trail at all.

He was following the trail of everyone who'd ever known it.

"Corvin," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "How many people know the way to the sanctum?"

He counted silently, lips moving. "The ghosts. Maren's people. Tam, maybe, if Maren ever drew it for him. Us." He frowned. "Why?"

"Because the map says someone is leading them to us." I turned the atlas so he could see the tall still figure, the web of lines radiating outward from it, and watched his face do the arithmetic that Tam had warned us about. "And the only ones who could lead them..."

"—are the ones with the most to lose," Corvin finished.

Behind us, unnoticed, Wren had picked up the amnesty proclamation where I'd dropped it, and was reading it again, her burned fingers tracing the words *past and future* over and over, and I remember thinking, in the last calm moment I would have for a long time, that the Spire wrote very persuasive paperwork indeed — and that somewhere behind us, on the road we'd just left, a smith with his grandmother's grey eyes was standing in his doorway, watching the western road, holding a piece of chalk he hadn't yet decided whether to pick up.
