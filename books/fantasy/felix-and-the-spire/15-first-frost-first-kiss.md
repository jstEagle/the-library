## 15. First Frost, First Kiss

### Ulyana

The snow found us before we found the cave.

I want it noted, for the record of whatever future historian bothers to read my journals, that I did not choose the mountain pass. The mountain pass chose us, the way a debt collector chooses your door. We had been climbing since dawn through the Greyteeth with Wren chattering about whether snowflakes counted as magic — "they're water that decided to be something else, that's basically enchantment" — and by midday the sky had turned the color of old pewter and begun dropping winter on our heads like an accusation.

"There," Corvin said, pointing at a black seam in the rock face half a mile up. He said it in his hunter voice, the flat one that brooked no argument, and I opened my mouth to argue anyway on principle when my boot slid on ice and the argument died of natural causes.

The cave was deeper than it looked from outside — a throat of stone that swallowed us whole and kept its own weather, which was merely terrible instead of lethal. There was even an old fire ring inside, blackened stones arranged by some traveler years dead, and I chose not to think about what had happened to that traveler.

Corvin got a fire going with the grim competence he brought to everything, and Wren fell asleep almost before she finished her share of dried plums, curled against the cart horse's blanket like a comma at the end of a very long sentence.

Which left me alone with Corvin and the fire, and the fire was the less dangerous of the two.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you look at me like you're memorizing me." He didn't look up from the blade he was sharpening. "I've seen it for three days now. You did it to the ford, too, before we crossed. And to the tollhouse roof."

"I'm a cartographer. Looking at things is my entire profession."

"You looked at me the way you looked at the ford," he said, "and the last time you looked at the ford, you went pale as milk and couldn't tell me why."

The whetstone whispered along the steel. Outside, the wind worried at the cave mouth like a dog at a door.

"It's nothing," I said.

"Liar," he said, without heat, the way you'd correct a child who'd claimed not to have eaten the last honey cake while wearing the evidence on her chin. "Ulyana Amberdale. You've lied to hunters, to inquisitors, to an entire city's worth of customers buying your 'lucky river stones.' You're allowed one more lie if you need it. But not tonight. Not in here."

I should have lied. It would have been kinder, or at least easier, and I had built five years in Greyhollow out of easy kindnesses told to myself every morning in the mirror. But the fire was warm, and Wren was breathing in that deep boneless way children sleep, and Corvin was looking at me now — actually looking, the way no one had looked at me since my mother died, like I was a map worth reading all the way to the edges.

"Do you remember," I said, because my mouth apparently wanted to betray me, "the name of the inn where we stayed after the tollhouse?"

He frowned. "The Drowned Bell. In Harrowmere."

"I don't."

"What?"

"I don't remember it." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You told me the name. I wrote it down — I write everything down now, I have three notebooks of things I refuse to lose — but I don't *remember* it. I remember standing in its doorway. I remember rain. The name is just ink to me."

Corvin set down the knife. Slowly, the way he did everything when it mattered.

"How long?"

"Since the ford." Since Wren's magic had torn the air open and the elite hunters' shields had come apart like wet paper, and I had needed the atlas, needed it *badly*, and so I had read from it with blood on my thumb and asked it to show me the way out. "Every time I use it, it takes something back. A place. A memory of a place. The Veilwood grove where my mother taught me to see the paths — gone, mostly. The night market in Greyhollow — I can describe it to you, I could draw it perfectly, but if you asked me how the lantern light felt on my face, I'd be guessing. It eats the places I've mapped. And I've spent my whole life mapping places, so it turns out I've been carrying around quite a banquet."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark behind us, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"Why didn't you tell us?" Corvin asked, and there it was — not anger, worse, the hurt underneath the anger, the thing I'd been dodging for weeks.

"Because Wren looks at me like I hold the world together," I said. "And because you—" I stopped. Started again. "Because if I said it out loud, it would be real, and I'd have to decide what to do about it, and I already know what I'm going to do about it, and you'd try to stop me."

"And what are you going to do about it?"

"Keep using it." I hugged my knees. "Until we get Wren somewhere safe. Until this is over. It's only memories of places, Corvin. Not people. Not yet. I checked — I've been checking — the atlas takes geography first. So I'll spend the places and keep the people, and when this is done I'll be a woman who knows everyone she loves and nowhere she's been, and that's a trade I can live with."

"That's a trade that will kill you," he said quietly. "Not today. But you're hollowing yourself out place by place, and one day you'll reach for something and find the hole goes deeper than you thought."

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I watched my commander do arithmetic with people's lives for six years and call it duty, and I know exactly what it sounds like when someone decides they're the acceptable loss." He moved around the fire and sat beside me, close enough that his shoulder touched mine, deliberate as everything he did. "So here's my counteroffer. From tonight on, no more secrets about the cost. When you use it, you tell me. What you spent. What's left. I keep the ledger with you."

"That's a terrible idea. You'll worry constantly."

"I already worry constantly. At least this way I'll worry accurately."

I laughed — a real laugh, startled out of me, the first one in longer than I cared to count — and the sound seemed to surprise him too, tugging at the corner of his mouth. Up close, in the firelight, he looked younger than his scars. Tired. Human. A man who had walked away from everything he'd been built to be and hadn't yet figured out what he was instead.

"Ask me," I said suddenly.

"Ask you what?"

"Anything. One true thing about me, anything you want to know. While I still have all my pieces. Consider it —" my voice caught, and I pushed through it — "consider it collateral. For the ledger."

He thought about it. I watched him think, which was its own small pleasure; Corvin's face usually sat behind shutters, and watching them open one by one was better than any hidden realm my gift ever showed me.

"The night market," he said finally. "In Greyhollow. The first time I saw you — really saw you, not surveillance, I mean — you were selling charms to a fishwife and you made her laugh. She'd been crying. I could see it. And you made her laugh in under a minute." His jaw worked. "What were you feeling? Knowing what you were, hiding what you were, selling little lies to people who'd have burned you for the truth?"

I closed my eyes and went back there, carefully, the way you handle a page you fear is crumbling.

"Afraid," I said. "Always afraid. But also —" I searched for it, and found it, thank all the forgotten gods, still there — "useful. For one minute, my gift wasn't a crime or a curse or a secret. It was a joke that made a sad woman laugh. That was the whole reason I never ran away, all those years. Everyone told me to run. My mother's ghost told me to run. But running meant the gift belonged to the fear forever, and I kept thinking, if I stay, if I hide well enough, maybe once a week I can make one person laugh with it, and that's — that's a life, isn't it? A small one. But mine."

When I opened my eyes, he was closer than he'd been. Close enough that I could see the fire doubled in his eyes.

"It's a life," he said. "A better one than mine was. I had the truth and used it to drag girls out of their beds."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a cave with a fugitive cartographer and a child who talks to snowflakes," he said, "and for the first time since I took the hunter's oath, I know exactly whose side I'm on." His hand found mine in the dark between us. Calloused. Warm. Certain. "Yours. Whatever that costs. I'm done pretending I haven't chosen."

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way wind does, leaving a silence so complete I could hear the snow falling — thousands of small soft landings, the world quietly rearranging itself.

"Corvin," I said.

"Yes."

"If I spend every place I've ever loved, and at the end I'm standing somewhere I've never seen, with nothing in my head but people—"

"Then you'll be standing there with me," he said, "and I'll remember the places for both of us. That's what the ledger is for. That's what I'm for."

And because I am, at heart, a woman who cannot leave a map unfinished, I leaned across the last few inches between us and drew the line myself.

His kiss was careful at first, the way he handled everything precious — and then not careful at all, and I discovered that a person can be a place too, and that some places you don't so much visit as surrender to. The atlas sat in my pack in the shadows, patient and hungry, and for once I did not think about it at all.

When we broke apart, Wren's voice floated sleepily from her blanket: "Finally. I've been waiting *weeks*. Tam says it was obvious."

"Tam is your imaginary snowflake friend," Corvin said, sounding strangled.

"Tam is *very* perceptive," Wren said, and rolled over, and was asleep again before either of us could formulate a defense.

I lay back against Corvin's shoulder and watched the fire, and made myself a promise I wrote nowhere: whatever the atlas took from me, it would not take this. This cave. This warmth. Him. Some maps you redraw. Some you defend with everything you have left.

I just didn't yet know which kind tomorrow would demand.

### Corvin

I have kept watch a thousand nights. On walls, on roads, outside tents full of sleeping men who trusted me because the crown paid me to be trustworthy. I know how to sit motionless in the cold until the cold stops being a sensation and becomes a fact about the universe. I thought I knew everything there was to know about keeping watch.

I knew nothing. Keeping watch over something you love is a different profession entirely.

She slept against my shoulder with the atlas's strap wrapped around her wrist — she'd taken to sleeping holding it, like a soldier with a weapon, which I suppose is exactly what it was — and every hour or so she murmured in her sleep, and twice she said names I didn't recognize, and once she said "*Mama, wait, the path's moving,*" in a voice so young it cracked something in me I'm going to have to live without.

I did the arithmetic I'd promised her I'd do. Three uses since the ford. The Drowned Bell, gone. The Veilwood grove, mostly gone. The Greyhollow market, gutted of everything except its shape. Three places traded for three escapes, and the ledger balanced — for now. But I'd seen her face when she talked about the market lanterns. She'd been describing a painting of her own memory. And Vessal was still out there, and the pass ahead was four days of exposed ground, and the atlas was going to get hungry again long before we reached anywhere safe.

There had to be another way. There always had to be another way; I just hadn't found it yet, and finding ways was supposed to be my job now. I'd traded one master for another, crown for conscience, and conscience turned out to be the more demanding employer.

Wren stirred near the fire. For a moment her eyes were open and ancient, the way they got sometimes, twelve years old and carrying something that had been old before the Spire had foundations.

"She gave it up for you," Wren whispered. "The laughing. I heard."

"She gave it up for you too," I said. "For all of us."

"I know." Wren pulled the blanket to her chin. "That's what scares me. Grown-ups keep giving things up for me and nobody tells me the price until after." Her eyes drifted shut. "Don't let her spend the cave. I like the cave."

"I won't," I said, though we both knew the atlas didn't take orders from me.

She slept. The snow kept falling past the cave mouth in the firelight, endless and silent, each flake a small white decision to land somewhere new. And I sat my watch over my whole entire world — a cartographer slowly trading herself away, a child dreaming in the language of storms — and understood, with the clarity the cold gives you, two things at once.

The first was that I would burn down the Spire itself before I let that book finish eating her.

The second came an hour later, when Ulyana woke with a gasp, sat bolt upright, and stared at the cave wall with the expression of a woman reading something written in fire.

"Corvin," she breathed. "The atlas. It's redrawing itself. Right now. Something's coming — something's *waking* — and it's between us and the pass."
