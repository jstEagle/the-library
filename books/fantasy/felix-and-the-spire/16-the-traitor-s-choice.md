## 16. The Traitor's Choice

I want it on the record — my record, which is a scrap of birchbark I keep in my boot for exactly this kind of thing — that I knew something was wrong before anyone else did.

Not because I'm clever. Well. Partly because I'm clever. Mostly because I'm small and people forget to be quiet around me, and being forgotten is its own kind of magic if you're patient enough to use it.

The morning it happened, I was in the sanctum's outer gallery practicing the third seal pattern Corvin had taught me, drawing it in the dust with a stick because actual chalk was apparently too precious to waste on a twelve-year-old who'd once set her own eyebrows on fire. The pattern was supposed to hold steady for ten breaths. Mine held for four and then slumped sideways like a drunk spider.

"You're forcing it," Corvin said from the doorway, arms crossed, wearing that expression he had — the one that was half teacher, half man quietly amazed he'd ended up teaching anything at all.

"I'm *doing* it."

"You're strangling it. Magic's not a chicken, Wren."

"That is the single worst piece of advice anyone has ever given me, and Ulyana once told me not to lick the frost off the map pages."

"Did you lick them?"

"...They tasted like starlight and old pennies."

He sighed the sigh of a man who had once been a royal hunter with dignity and a uniform and was now babysitting. But there was warmth under it. There always was, by then. Somewhere between the ford and the mountain cave, between Wren-don't-touch-that and Wren-you-did-well, Corvin had stopped looking at me like a stray and started looking at me like — well. I didn't have a word for it yet. I was working on one.

Ulyana was deeper in the sanctum with the ghosts, bent over the atlas, doing whatever she did that made her go quiet and gray around the edges. She'd been going quiet and gray a lot lately. I'd noticed. I'd decided noticing wasn't the same as knowing what to do about it, and filed it away next to all the other things I couldn't fix.

That's when the hair on my arms stood up.

* * *

Here's a thing nobody tells you about raw magic: it doesn't come with instructions, but it does come with weather. You learn to feel pressure changes. A gathering storm has a taste — copper and static and the moment before lightning decides where to strike.

The sanctum's wards tasted wrong.

Not broken. *Bent.* Like someone had found the seam of the door and was peeling it back with careful fingers, the way you'd open a letter that isn't yours.

I dropped the stick. "Corvin."

He heard it in my voice. Twelve years old or not, I'd earned enough trust by then that he didn't ask how I knew. He just moved — fast, silent, hunter-quick — to the gallery's narrow window slit.

And then his face did something I'd never seen it do.

It went blank. Not calm-blank, the way he went when he was thinking. This was the blank of a man looking at something he'd hoped never to see again, and seeing it wearing his own colors.

"What," I said. "What is it."

"Hunters." His voice came out flat as slate. "Six of them. And Tam."

Tam. Old Tam, from the tollhouse, whose grandson Merric had brought us stew and gossip and bad jokes for three weeks. Merric, who'd gone to Greyhollow two days ago for salt and news and hadn't come back, and old Tam had said *he'll turn up, boy's got feet like a debt collector.*

Merric had turned up. He just hadn't turned up alone.

"He wouldn't," I said, and even as I said it I knew how young I sounded, how much I still believed the world worked the way stories promised. "His gran's a ghost of the sanctum, he grew up on these stories, he—"

"They offered amnesty." Corvin was already moving, pulling me away from the window, toward the inner door. "Vessal's been posting them since the ford. Food, pardon, a bed that isn't stone. For a hungry kid in winter, that's not a hard bargain. That's no bargain at all."

Behind us, deeper in the sanctum, I heard Ulyana's voice rise — sharp, urgent, the ghosts' voices answering in their whispery layered way, like wind through a keyhole choir. She'd felt it too. Of course she had. Her gift was stitched into every ward in this place; she'd feel hunters coming the way a spider feels the web shake.

We burst into the map chamber together, and the scene there I will carry until I die, so let me get it right:

Ulyana at the great stone table, atlas open, both hands flat on the living page, which was glowing faint gold and bleeding light through her fingers like she was holding a heartbeat. The ghosts circled her — seven of them, translucent, agitated, their forms flickering like candles in draft. And on Ulyana's face: terror. Not for herself. I want that understood too. Ulyana Amberdale's face, when it broke, broke outward. Every crack pointed away from her, toward us.

"They've found us," she said. "How—"

"Merric," Corvin said.

Something closed behind her eyes. Grief, quick and brutal, for a boy who'd brought her stew. Then it was gone, packed away, because there was no time for it and she was the sort of person who put grief in a drawer and sat on the drawer.

"The back passage," she said. "Through the sealed archive. It comes out in the ravine—"

"It takes twenty minutes to unseal," Corvin said. "The hunters will be through the front door in five."

"Then we run and we fight and we—"

"No." 

Both of them looked at me. I don't know whose voice it had been. Mine, I realized. My mouth had said it while the rest of me caught up.

Because here's what I knew, the thing that only I could know, the thing that being small and overlooked and magically untrained had taught me: the front door of the sanctum wasn't a door. It was a *ward*. A living one, grown out of the same old magic as everything else in this place, and it answered to intent, not strength. Six trained hunters could break it, sure. Eventually. With fire and iron and time.

But it would answer *me* faster. Because I was the only one in this room whose magic was loud enough to shout over it, and stupid enough not to know better.

"You can't hold a Spire ward," Corvin said. He'd read my face. Hunters do that. It's infuriating. "Wren, those are binding patterns. Trained inquisitors spend years learning them."

"And I spent six months being taught them *badly*, by people who assumed I'd never need them, which means I learned them the way you learn a song stuck in your head instead of the way you learn a lesson." I was talking fast now, hands moving, the words tumbling out ahead of my sense. "It doesn't have to be perfect. It just has to be *loud*. Confusion holds doors too. Ask any guard in any city ever."

"Wren—" Ulyana started.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "You said the passage takes twenty minutes to unseal. So give me twenty minutes of loud."

Nobody moved. The ghosts murmured among themselves, a sound like leaves deciding whether to fall.

Then Ulyana crossed the chamber and took my face in her cold ink-stained hands, and looked at me the way I'd spent my whole life wanting someone to look at me — like I was a map worth reading all the way to the edges.

"You come through that passage," she said. "You hear me? Twenty minutes, and then you run, and you don't stop running until you hit the ravine. If you're not there, I'm coming back for you, and I will burn down everything Vessal loves to do it. Swear it back to me."

"I swear," I said. And because I am, at core, an honest person: "Mostly."

"*Wren.*"

"Go. Your twenty minutes started forty seconds ago."

* * *

What followed was, I think, the finest performance of my career, and I include the time I convinced a Spire convoy quartermaster that I was the Inquisitor-General's niece and needed immediate release on grounds of family embarrassment.

I planted myself in the center of the entry hall, barefoot — shoes were for people with time — and pulled every scrap of magic I had up through my chest like hauling a bucket from a very deep well.

The trick to being loud, I discovered, is that you can't be precise. Precision is for people with training. I had panic and a head full of half-remembered binding patterns, and I mashed them together the way a child mashes paint, and what came out was a shrieking, strobing, contradictory mess of ward-light that made the sanctum's front door flicker through a dozen shapes at once — gate, archway, wall of thorns, waterfall, a brief and deeply confusing interlude as a giant mouth.

The first hunter through it screamed. I want that noted too. On the bark. In the record.

"STOP IN THE NAME OF—" someone shouted from outside, which is funny, because whose name? The crown's? The crown's name was built on a lie Corvin had burned in a letter. I told them so. Loudly. Possibly I also made the doorway flash the crown's sigil upside down, which felt symbolically appropriate.

They threw things at me. Light, mostly — binding nets, suppression charms, the dull gray chains of null-magic that the Spire used on people like me. I dodged some. I absorbed one in the shoulder that felt like being slapped by winter itself. I kept shouting. I made the door do the mouth thing again.

Somewhere behind me, deep in the stone, I felt the archive seals grinding open — slow, ancient, stubborn. Fifteen minutes left. Maybe fourteen. Time moves strangely when you're holding a door with your whole soul.

A hunter got close — big, armored, visor down, faceless as a coin. He swung a suppression rod at my head and I dropped under it and did the only thing my body knew how to do, which was grab his ankle and pour raw chaos straight up his leg. He went rigid, then sat down very suddenly, and I swear on the birchbark he said, in a small wounded voice, "*my toes*."

Twelve years old, and I was winning a fight against the most feared institution in the kingdom, one toe at a time.

Then Tam walked through the door.

* * *

Old Tam looked terrible. He'd been crying — I could see it in the red rims, the wet tracks in the grime of his face. Behind him, half-hidden in the doorway's shadow, stood Merric, sixteen and hollow-eyed, staring at the floor like it owed him money and he hoped it would pay up so he could leave.

"They have my grandson's pardon," Tam said hoarsely. "Wren. Girl. Stand aside. Please. I watched your mother work, once. I won't watch them take you in chains."

"My mother?" I said, because that was news, and even mid-battle my brain snagged on it like a sock on a nail. "You knew my—"

"Stand *aside*."

The hunters behind him were done playing. I saw it in how they moved — spreading out, flanking, rods leveled. The doorway flickered weakly around them. I was emptying. Everyone empties eventually; that's the first thing they teach you at the Spire, right before they teach you that what you empty yourself *into* belongs to them.

Ten minutes. Maybe eight. The archive seals were slower than Ulyana had guessed. Ancient things are always slower than you guess.

So I did the math, the way nobody expects the smallest person in the room to do math.

If I ran now, I might make the passage. Might. And six hunters would follow us into the ravine with fresh strength while we stumbled out exhausted, and Ulyana — gray-faced, memory-leaking, carrying an atlas that cost her pieces of herself — would have to outrun them or die, and Corvin would have to choose between fighting his brothers-in-arms and watching us fall, and either way, either way, either way—

Or I stayed. And they got me. And a captured apprentice was worth exactly one trade, and trades could be survived, and Ulyana had already proven — at the ford, in the cave, every single day — that she was the kind of person who came back for her people.

Being bait, I decided, was a job. Jobs could be done well.

"Sorry, Tam," I said. "Tell Merric his gran's ghost is going to haunt him *so* hard."

Then I stopped holding the door.

It snapped shut behind the hunters like a trap closing, sealing them in with me, cutting off the retreat I was pretty sure none of them wanted anyway. And I let the last of my magic flare up around me in one final useless gorgeous blaze — every color I'd ever stolen from the world, spinning — and then the suppression net came down over all of it, and the world went gray and heavy and mine no longer.

The last thing I saw clearly was the biggest hunter kneeling beside me, checking my pulse with rough gentleness, saying, "She's just a child, sir," and the commander — a thin man with a voice like dry paper — replying:

"She's leverage. Bag her."

* * *

They put me in a wagon. Iron cuffs, gray hood, the works. Very thorough. I appreciated the professionalism even as I hated everyone involved.

Through a gap in the hood I watched the trees slide past, and I did the bravest thing available to me, which was nothing. I stayed small. I stayed quiet. I thought about Ulyana's hands on my face and Corvin's terrible chicken advice and the taste of starlight and old pennies, and I repeated my oath to myself like a prayer with an asterisk:

*Mostly.*

Because here's the thing about being leverage: leverage only works if the other side cares. And I knew — with the fierce, furious certainty of the smallest member of a family that had chosen itself — that they cared.

Somewhere behind me, the sanctum's passage would be opening onto the ravine. Ulyana would count heads. One short. And I knew exactly what her face would do, because I'd seen it break outward before, every crack pointing away from herself.

I'm sorry, I thought at her, as the wagon rolled toward the Spire and the sky went the color of a blade. I'm sorry, but you'd have done the same, and we both know it, and that's why you picked me.

The hood slipped. Above the trees, black against the winter sky, I saw it for the first time with my own eyes: the Spire, vast and pale and drinking the horizon, its peak lost in cloud.

It was uglier than the maps made it look.

Good, I thought, as the gates opened ahead of us like a throat. Ugly things should be easier to hate.

I just didn't yet know how much I was going to wish I'd lied about the *mostly*.
