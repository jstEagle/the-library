## 17. Terms of Surrender

The raven came at dawn, which was rude of it. Ravens, in my experience, prefer dusk — all that theatrical shadow. This one landed on the windowsill of the safehouse with the bearing of a tax collector and a scroll case strapped to its leg like it had somewhere better to be.

I should have shot it. Corvin would have shot it. But Corvin was asleep on the cot by the door, one arm flung over his eyes, still wearing his boots because he'd fallen asleep mid-sentence while explaining, for the third time, why we couldn't go back for Wren yet. And I was awake, because I had been awake for three days, and awake people do stupid things like open windows for royal ravens.

The scroll inside smelled of ink and something colder underneath — the Spire's paper always did, like snow that had learned to hold a grudge.

*To the cartographer Ulyana Amberdale,* it began, in a hand so precise it looked typeset. *Regarding the girl Wren Ashvale, currently in the custody of the High Inquisitor's office.*

My hands went very still around the parchment. Stillness is a skill. You learn it young when your gift is illegal — you learn to stop your face, your fingers, your breathing, everything but the part of you that keeps working underneath, quiet as groundwater.

*She is unharmed. She will remain unharmed provided certain terms are met. The atlas of Maren Amberdale, delivered intact to the Spire Gate, three days hence, at the hour of Vespers. Come alone. Come unarmed. The girl walks free the moment the book changes hands.*

*You should know she has been asking for you. Children are resilient, but they are not patient, and neither am I.*

*In service to the crown,*
*High Inquisitor Vessal*

There was a postscript. Of course there was a postscript. Men like Vessal don't threaten; they *annotate.*

*P.S. — Your mother once stood where you stand now. Ask yourself what she would have done. Then ask yourself what she actually did. There is a difference, and I suspect you know it.*

I read the letter four times. On the fourth reading, my eyes snagged on the same detail they'd caught the first time and I'd refused to look at: *the girl walks free the moment the book changes hands.* Not "moments after." Not "once you've departed." The moment it changed hands. Which meant Wren would be standing right there at the gate, close enough to touch, close enough for me to see exactly how unharmed or otherwise she was, close enough for Vessal to watch my face while he decided whether to keep his word.

He wanted an audience. He wanted to see me choose.

Men like Vessal always want witnesses. It's not cruelty, exactly — it's worse. It's *certainty.* He believed I would come, because he believed everyone comes, eventually, when the price is small enough and the guilt large enough. A book for a child. Even the arithmetic was insulting.

Behind me, Corvin shifted in his sleep and murmured something — a name, half-swallowed. Not mine. One of his old squad, maybe, or his brother. The dead have a way of renting rooms in sleeping men's heads.

I looked at him for a long time.

Here is what I knew about love, as of that dawn: it makes liars of careful people. I had spent five years lying to an entire city, lying fluently, lying *professionally*, and it had never cost me anything but loneliness. But lying to Corvin — that was going to be different. That was going to be like cutting along a line I'd drawn myself.

Because here is what I also knew: if I told him, he would come. He would insist on coming, armed, in armor he no longer had any right to wear, and he would put himself between me and whatever Vessal had waiting, and Vessal knew Corvin's name and Corvin's face and Corvin's particular talent for turning hunters' training against hunters. Vessal's letter said *come alone* the way a trap says *free cheese.*

And if I refused to tell him and went anyway and died, well. That was survivable too, in the sense that dead people have very few problems.

So I did the cowardly, sensible thing. I folded the letter into my sleeve, fed the raven a strip of dried apple from my pack — bribery works on corvids and inquisitors alike — and began, quietly, to plan a forgery.

* * *

The atlas sat on the table between us that evening, and I lied with my whole face.

"We can't trade it," I said. "It's the only leverage we have. If we give up the living map, we give up everything — the wellspring, the truth about the outlawry, all of it. Vessal wins by lunchtime."

Corvin nodded slowly. He was cleaning a knife he didn't need to clean, which was what he did instead of pacing. "Agreed. So we don't answer. We move tonight, deeper into the hills, and we find another way to get her back."

"There isn't another way," I said, and watched him hear it. "You know there isn't. Three days, one gate, one book. He's not leaving us room to be clever."

"Then we get clever anyway." His jaw set in that way I'd learned meant the argument was already lost in his heart and being won in his pride. "Ulyana. We are not giving them Wren's ransom *and* the map. We're not handing Vessal the key to the wellspring because he asked nicely over a dead bird."

"I know," I said. "I know we're not."

And that much, at least, was true. We were not. *I* was. There's a difference, and I suspected he knew it, and I prayed nightly to gods I didn't believe in that he wouldn't.

I spent the next two days being the most exhausting version of myself. I argued routes. I insisted on scouting the eastern pass personally. I volunteered for the night watches and then complained about them convincingly. I let him see me sleep — actually arranged it, once, draping myself dramatically over a chair until he covered me with his cloak, because a woman planning to vanish at dawn does not get to look rested.

Meanwhile, in the stolen hours, I worked.

Forging the atlas was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I say that as someone who once drew a map of a realm that ate two of my memories just to hold still long enough to be surveyed. The living map wasn't ink. It was *attention* — my mother's attention, soaked into the pages over thirty years, the way a well-worn path is made of footsteps. You cannot fake attention.

But you can fake the *appearance* of it, if you are very good and very desperate.

I used the last clean leaves of vellum from my kit. I copied the Spire's page stroke by stroke — the tower rendered in lines that seemed to breathe if you didn't stare directly at them, the wellspring beneath it coiled like a sleeping animal. Then I bled on it. Just a little. My gift had been costing me blood since the ford, and I'd stopped asking why; some invoices you sign without reading. A living map wants a living hand, and mine was the only one available.

It wasn't perfect. Held beside the original, a trained eye would catch it in a heartbeat — the forged wellspring moved wrong, more like a fish than a sleeper, and the margins lacked my mother's tiny annotations, her observations in a hand like falling rain. But Vessal wouldn't be holding it beside anything. He'd be holding it in triumph, in front of witnesses, at an exchange he'd scripted himself. Triumph is the blindest emotion there is. I was counting on it the way drowning people count on driftwood.

The real atlas I wrapped in oilcloth and sewed into the lining of my coat, against my ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Then I wrote Corvin a letter. I sat up through the last night writing it, and this is the shameful part: I wrote four versions. In the first, I confessed everything and begged forgiveness. In the second, I confessed everything and didn't beg. In the third, I explained my reasoning so thoroughly and so reasonably that surely he'd understand, because Corvin respected reasoning the way other men respected swordwork.

The fourth version was two sentences long:

*I'm sorry. By the time you read this, it will either have worked or it won't matter.*

I left the first three versions in the fire and hated myself with great efficiency, the way you hate a headache — impersonally, continuously, while getting on with things.

* * *

Vespers of the third day came down grey and windless, the kind of sky that can't commit to rain. The Spire Gate road ran straight as a judgment through bare fields, and I walked it alone, exactly as instructed, with the forged atlas in a satchel against my hip and the real one against my ribs and my gift flickering under my skin like a lamp low on oil.

I could feel the Spire before I saw it. Everyone could, probably — it hummed in the teeth the way a storm hums in old bones. But I could feel it *specifically*, the way only a cartographer could: the wellspring beneath it pulling at the land like a wound pulls at a body, every stream within ten miles bending subtly toward it, every road, every root. Five years of stolen magic, drinking the world through a straw.

They brought Wren out when I was fifty paces from the gate.

That was the cruelty of it — not hidden, not held behind glass, but right there, walking between two hunters with her wrists bound in front of her and her chin up in that way she had, the way that said *I am twelve and terrified and if you acknowledge the terror I will bite you.* Her hands were bandaged. The burns from the ford had never healed right; I'd treated them myself and known, even then, that raw magic leaves scars the way lightning leaves trees — from the inside out.

She saw me and her whole face broke open, and then she slammed it shut again, because she was smart, smarter than any child should have to be, and she knew better than to show Vessal anything real.

Vessal stood at the gate itself. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, a narrow man in dove-grey robes with the kind of neat, forgettable face that must have taken decades of discipline to achieve. His eyes were the exception. His eyes were doing all the work his face refused to.

"You came alone," he said. It wasn't gratitude. It was confirmation. Somewhere behind those forgettable features, a ledger gained an entry.

"The letter was very clear," I said. "I try to follow instructions. It's how I've stayed alive."

"And yet." He extended one hand, palm up. Patient. A man who had never once needed to hurry. "The atlas."

I lifted the satchel off my shoulder. Fifty paces is a long way to walk when every step is a decision you've already made and are now pretending to survive. I made myself think about nothing — about weather, about the grain of the road stones, about absolutely nothing at all, because if I thought about Wren's bandaged hands or the weight against my ribs or the letter sitting in Corvin's coat pocket waiting to gut him, I would do something unforgivably honest.

Wren was watching me. I felt it like sunlight. Don't, I thought at her, as hard as I've ever thought anything. *Don't hope yet. Don't say anything. Let me finish the lie.*

"The girl first," I said. "Or at least together. I've read enough of your correspondence to know how you define 'the moment the book changes hands.'"

Something flickered across Vessal's face — not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "You've been studying me."

"I study everything," I said. "It's the gift."

He almost smiled. It was the most human thing he did that day, and it frightened me more than the hunters did.

"Together, then," he said, and gestured, and the hunters walked Wren forward until she stood an arm's length from me, close enough that I could see the burn scars creeping past her bandages, could see that someone had braided her hair — badly, clumsily, hunter's hands unused to the work — and I understood suddenly that they hadn't been cruel to her, that Vessal had *ordered* them not to be, because a hostage kept well is worth two kept broken, and I hated him for his competence even as some starving part of me was grateful.

"Count of three," Vessal said pleasantly. "On three, you release the book, they release the girl. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"One," said Vessal.

I looked at Wren. She looked at me. Twelve years old, and she mouthed one word at me, silent, fierce: *don't.*

Too late, little bird, I thought. The lie is already built. I'm standing inside it.

"Two," said Vessal.

And that was when the horn sounded from the eastern ridge.

* * *

Later — much later, in the wreckage of everything — I would replay that horn a thousand times and never decide whether it saved my life or ended it. But in the moment, there was only the sound itself, deep and bronze and wrong, rolling down off the hills where no horn had any business being, and watching Vessal's composure crack like lake ice.

"That's not yours," I said.

"No," Vessal said, and his voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet goes before violence. "It is not."

The hunters at the gate turned toward the sound. Wren turned too — and in that half-second of collective distraction, her eyes met mine again, and this time she didn't mouth *don't.* She mouthed *run.*

I didn't run. I did something worse. I opened the satchel, pulled out the forged atlas, and pressed it into Vessal's still-extended hand — because whatever was coming off that ridge, I wanted the trade *done*, wanted Wren's freedom purchased and sealed before chaos swallowed the arithmetic — and the instant his fingers closed on the cover, I grabbed Wren's arm and pulled her behind me.

For one heartbeat, nothing happened. Vessal looked down at the book in his hands with the expression of a man reading a letter written in a language he'd invented himself and finding a typo.

Then he smiled.

It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who has just confirmed something he'd suspected all along, and whose suspicion has just become the most valuable information in the kingdom.

"A forgery," he said softly. Almost tenderly. "Oh, Mistress Amberdale. Do you know what this tells me? It tells me the original is *close.* Close enough to copy. Close enough—" his eyes dropped to my coat, to the place where the oilcloth bundle shifted against my ribs with every breath, and my blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with being *seen*— "to carry. Against the body. Where a heartbeat might warm it."

He didn't reach for it. That was the worst part. He simply looked at me the way a cartographer looks at unfinished terrain, and said, "Take them both," and the hunters moved, and the horn sounded again, closer now, and somewhere down the road behind me — impossibly, absurdly, at a dead sprint with no armor and no plan — came the unmistakable sound of Corvin Brightwater shouting my name.

I had lied to him for three days to keep him away.

He had read my letter, burned it, apparently, and come anyway.

And as the hunters closed in and Wren's fingers found mine and gripped hard, I understood, with the terrible clarity of a map finally finished, that Vessal had never wanted the atlas at all.

He had wanted *me.*
