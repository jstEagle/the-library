## 22. The Cartographer of Ordinary Roads

The trouble with ordinary roads, I have discovered, is that they insist on existing whether or not you draw them.

This is a grievous insult to my profession. For five years in Greyhollow I mapped realms that no one else could see, and now that the whole world can see everything again — legally, loudly, with permits posted on every corner like wedding banns — the world has decided it doesn't need me to look at it. Roads simply *are*. Rivers run where rivers run. The Veilwood sits at the edge of town being smugly visible to anyone with functioning eyes and a free afternoon.

I am, for the first time in my life, a cartographer of things everyone can see.

And I love it more than I can say.

* * *

Spring came late to Greyhollow this year, which suited me fine, because spring means mud, and mud means the surveyors' guild pays extra for anyone willing to walk the lowland tracks and record where the frost heaves have swallowed the old markers. I walked them. All of them. My boots are ruined, my ink froze twice, and I got into a protracted argument with a farmer's goose that I maintain I lost only on points.

The map I made from it hangs now in the guildhall, drawn in plain iron-gall ink on honest paper, and not one line of it shifts when you're not looking. Not one road wanders off overnight because it had feelings about being charted. It is the most boring map I have ever produced and I show it to visitors the way other people show off grandchildren.

"You're insufferable," Wren told me last week, watching me straighten its frame for the fourth time.

"I'm *proud*."

"You're insufferable about being proud."

She's fifteen now, or she says she is; her birthday was somewhere in the fugitive months and we've agreed to count it as midwinter, mostly so Corvin can grumble that she made him stand in the snow holding a lopsided cake. She studies at the reopened Lyceum — openly, with her name on a roster and everything, which still makes me stop and stare sometimes when I pass the courtyard and see her arguing theory with a teacher who isn't trying to arrest her. Her magic settled over the winter the way a colt settles into its legs: all at once, then gradually, then all at once again. Her hands healed. She still flexes them sometimes when she thinks no one's watching, and I pretend not to see, because some scars want privacy.

She comes by my workshop on evenings when the Lyceum lets out early, puts her feet on my good chair, eats my bread, and tells me everything I'm doing wrong with my life. It's the most family-shaped thing I've ever had, and I guard it like a border stone.

Today she arrived with bread of her own and an expression I've learned to distrust.

"So," she said, setting it down. "Master Ellery asked about you again."

"Did he."

"He wants you to teach. Surveying. Or — well." She turned the loaf in her hands, suddenly very interested in its crust. "He said, and I quote, 'Miss Amberdale's maps have a certain something.' He wouldn't say what. He looked embarrassed."

"Your master is fishing," I said. "There's nothing in my maps but accurate distances and a goose."

"Uh-huh." She tore the bread, handed me half, and fixed me with the look she'd learned from Corvin — the one that says *I know you're lying and I've decided to let you, for now.* "The goose?"

"The goose is load-bearing."

* * *

Here is the thing I have not said aloud, not even to Wren, not even fully to myself: since the Spire fell, I have felt nothing. No shimmer at the edge of sight. No second country breathing under the first. When I close my eyes and reach for the Veilwood the way I used to reach for it — the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore — there is only dark, and the memory of dark, and the ache where the gift used to live.

I gave it away. Stroke by stroke, redrawing the living map, unbinding the wellspring's stolen magic back into the soil of the world. Every line cost me a place I'd mapped, and the last lines cost me the ability to map anything unseen ever again. That was the bargain. I knew it going in. I'd do it again before the ink dried on this sentence.

But knowing a thing and living in the quiet afterward are different countries, and I am still learning the roads in this one.

Some mornings I wake up reaching for my mother's atlas before I remember. It sits in the guildhall archive now, under glass, catalogued as a historical document — *Amberdale Atlas, provenance disputed, pages exhibit anomalous properties, do not expose to open flame (see also: incident report 4)*. Scholars come to look at it. Sometimes one of them swears the margins moved. The archivist has stopped writing it down.

I went to see it once, in the autumn. Stood there a long while with my breath fogging the glass. It didn't stir for me. Whatever lived in those pages, I spent it buying the world back, and the world was worth it, and I went home and drank tea and did not cry until Corvin came through the door and asked how my day was in exactly the wrong gentle voice.

That's another thing about ordinary roads. They don't tell you where the grief lives until you're standing on it.

* * *

Corvin keeps bees now.

I want that noted by history, because history will otherwise record him as the hunter who broke the Spire, and it will be dignified, and it will be a lie. What actually happened is that a man who spent ten years learning forty ways to kill people discovered he had no idea what to do with peace, got twitchy in a way that made furniture nervous, and needed something small and demanding and impossible to intimidate. Bees. He ordered a hive from a farmer two valleys over, installed it behind our cottage with the grim thoroughness of a man fortifying a position, and proceeded to lose three consecutive arguments with insects.

"They respect firmness," he insisted, after the second stinging.

"They're bees, Corvin."

"Firmness," he repeated, with the exact intonation he once used on recruits, and I laughed until I had to sit down on the step, and he pretended to be offended and then didn't, and that was the whole conversation.

We never made a declaration of anything. There was no moment, no speech, no scene worth painting. There was a cave in the mountains where I finally told him the truth about what the atlas was taking from me, and he held my hands and said, "Then let me remember for you," and that was — that was the whole of it, really. Everything since has been that sentence, said in smaller ways, daily. He remembers for me. When I lose a name or misplace a street I've known since childhood — and it happens less now, the healers say the damage settled rather than spread, but it happens — he doesn't make a ceremony of it. He just quietly knows the thing I've dropped, and picks it up, and hands it back as if it never left my pocket.

It should feel like charity. It feels like being carried home.

He still trains, mornings, in the yard behind the cottage — forms without weapons, the old hunter drills, though these days half the neighborhood children gather at the fence to watch and he pretends to be annoyed and then teaches them how to fall properly. Wren says he's softening. Wren says it with the smugness of someone who was personally softened first, back on the road, by a girl who couldn't control her own magic and a woman who lied beautifully and badly and always for the right reasons.

The city gave him a medal. He keeps it in a drawer with the beekeeping manual, which tells you everything.

* * *

So: the morning it happened, I was doing nothing remarkable. That should be stated plainly, for the record, because afterward everyone wanted the moment to have been remarkable, and it wasn't. It was a Tuesday. It was raining the thin, apologetic rain that Greyhollow does in April, and I was at the worktable finishing the commission for the millers' cooperative — a map of the mill race and the cart tracks along the Aldwater, four foot by three, due Friday, nothing in it stranger than a dispute over who owns the willow stump.

I was drawing the last stretch of track, the bit that runs along the water meadow toward the old ford — our ford, though I don't call it that out loud; the ford where Wren burned her hands saving us, where Corvin chose us over his brothers-in-arms, where the river ran red for an afternoon and then, like rivers do, forgot. I drew the meadow. I drew the alders. I drew the track curving down toward the water.

And then, at the very edge of the sheet — past the border rule, past the neat margin where a proper map simply stops — a line appeared.

Not inked by me. Not inked at all. A faint silver thread, like moonlight caught in wet thread, running off the edge of the paper into nowhere, the way roads do in dreams.

I put down my pen.

I sat very still, the way you do when a wild thing has landed near you and any movement might send it flying. Rain ticked against the window. Downstairs, Corvin was doing something to the hive that involved swearing. The silver line lay on my map and breathed.

I knew what it was. Of course I knew. I had spent five years drawing paths like that in a hidden atlas, in a hidden realm, with a hidden hand. The Veilwood's roads looked exactly like that, before the outlawry, before the Spire drank the world dry, before I poured everything back and paid with the seeing of it.

Except I wasn't drawing it. And I couldn't see it — not the way I used to, not with the second sight that let me walk realms no one else perceived. I could only see this because it was on paper, in front of me, faint as breath on glass. Which meant either I was imagining it —

— or the gift was coming back. Regrowing, slow as grass through rubble, in whatever scarred and partial shape it intended to take. Not the old wholeness. Maybe never that. But something. A path. One path, shimmering at the edge of an ordinary map of an ordinary road, asking nothing, promising nothing, just *there*, the way spring is there.

My hands were shaking. I noticed this with detached professional interest, the way I'd once noted that a bridge I was mapping was shaking, and reached for the same conclusion: observe first. Draw later. Panic on schedule.

I leaned close. The line didn't fade. If anything, brightened — barely, the way coals brighten when you stop poking them. It ran off the page's edge heading west, toward the hills, toward the tollhouse ruins where the ghosts had kept their fire, toward — I followed its angle with my eye, heart going like a drum — toward the valley where the wellspring had gone back into the earth. Where the land had greened last summer in ways the farmers called a fluke and the Lyceum called *a subject requiring further study.*

The magic hadn't just scattered. It was gathering. Finding its old channels. And maybe — maybe — finding its way back to the girl who'd opened the door for it, carrying what remained of her gift out through her own two hands.

Or maybe it was one trick of the rain and my tired eyes and a heart that wanted so badly to be mended it would mistake a smudge for a miracle.

There was one way to find out, and it was the same way I'd found out everything worth knowing: draw it. Follow it. Put it on paper and see if the paper agreed.

* * *

Wren took the news the way she takes everything now, which is loudly and then thoughtfully, in that order.

"You're going to follow it." We were in the workshop; she'd arrived with bread and stayed with accusations. "You're going to follow a mysterious glowing line off the edge of a map, alone, into the hills, because that worked out so well the last four times."

"I wasn't going to go alone."

"No," said Corvin, from the doorway, where he had materialized with the silent competence of a man who spent a decade tracking people for a living and now uses it mostly to know when dinner's ready. "She wasn't."

"I was going to ask," I said, "if the two of you wanted to come."

"We're coming," they said, together, and glared at each other for saying it together, and I sat there in my shabby workshop surrounded by honest ink and boring, beautiful maps, and felt something in my chest go loose and warm and wide.

Wren was already pulling the atlas references off my shelf, already talking route logistics, already arguing with herself about whether Master Ellery would excuse her from Thursday's seminar for *an expedition of significant thaumaturgical interest* or whether she'd have to forge a note, and Corvin was checking the weather with a hunter's squint at the window and muttering about provisions, and nobody — nobody — had said the obvious thing, so I said it:

"It might be nothing. It might be a smudge. It might be the beginning of something I can't predict and can't control, and I need you both to hear me say that I don't know what happens if I start drawing that kind of road again."

They stopped. Looked at me. And Wren — fifteen years old, scarred hands, fierce as a struck match — planted her fists on her hips and delivered the verdict of the entire new world:

"Yeah? Well. Nobody knows what happens next. That's the *point* of roads."

* * *

We leave tomorrow.

Tonight I finished the millers' map — willow stump and all, goose-free, due and delivered — and then I cleared the worktable, and pinned down a fresh sheet, and sat before it a long time with the lamp turned low.

At the edge of the old commission, the silver line still shimmered, patient as starlight. I laid the new sheet beside it. Dipped my pen. And at the margin of the blank page, where a proper map simply stops, I drew the first inch of a road that goes somewhere no surveyor's guild has ever commissioned and no crown has ever outlawed and no atlas has ever remembered.

The line took the ink. Held it. Brightened.

From the doorway, Corvin watched me work, and said nothing, and smiled the way he does — small, crooked, like a man who has finally stopped guarding a door and started standing in one.

I smiled back, and kept drawing.
