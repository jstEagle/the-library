## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first thing Xanthe Reyes learned about poison was that it had manners.

Not all of it, of course. There were the rude poisons—the ones that announced themselves with a scald in the throat or a numbness crawling up the gums, the kind any kitchen drudge with a week's training could catch. But the good poisons, the clever ones, the ones that had kept a taster employed and alive for nineteen years, those were polite. They arrived dressed as something else. They sat down at the table of your tongue like an invited guest and only later did you realize you'd never seen them come through the door.

So when Xanthe lifted the silver spoon to his lips that morning and tasted the king's breakfast porridge—barley, goat's milk, a whisper of honey, salt from the eastern pans—he was not merely eating. He was reading. And the porridge, as far as he could tell, told the truth.

He swallowed, set the spoon down on the white cloth, and waited.

Around him the royal kitchens of Vell went about their morning the way a river goes about its morning: without any particular interest in him. Forty cooks and scullions and turnspit boys moved through steam and clatter, and not one of them looked at the thin young man at the tasting table except, perhaps, the way one looks at a piece of furniture that might bite. That was fine. Furniture didn't have to eat supper alone in the corner by the flour bins. Furniture didn't have feelings about it either way.

"Reyes." The voice came from the doorway to the steward's office, and the temperature of the entire kitchen dropped by several degrees, which was impressive, because Steward Corvane managed it without any magic at all—and in Vell, that was saying something, since there wasn't any magic to be had.

Xanthe rose. One did not remain seated for Corvane; it was in the rules, right between *tasters eat last* and *tasters never leave*.

"You rang," Xanthe said.

"I don't ring. Ringing is for people whose time matters less than mine." Corvane was a long man, dry as a December stick, with a face like a folded letter nobody wanted to open. He held out a slate. "The king dines at seven with the Ambassador from Drossen. Nine courses. You'll taste everything, you'll report anything, and you'll do it without making faces at my table."

"I never make faces."

"You made a face at the partridge on Tuesday."

"The partridge deserved it. It had been dead somewhat longer than it believed."

Corvane's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, which was to become somehow more closed. "Nineteen years I've fed you, clothed you, housed you under the crown's roof, and still you talk like a boy who thinks wit is a skill anyone asked for."

"Nineteen years," Xanthe agreed pleasantly. "And yet here we both are."

For a moment Corvane studied him with those flat gray eyes, and Xanthe felt the familiar small coldness underneath his ribs, the one that lived there permanently now, the size and shape of a coin: the knowledge that his usefulness was the only wall between himself and whatever happened to tasters who stopped being useful. Nobody had ever told him what that was. Nobody had ever needed to. In Vell, things you weren't told were always the worst things.

"The wards hold," Corvane said, which was not a sentence so much as a sermon. "No magic crosses into this valley. No witchcraft, no hexwork, no cursed trinkets, no enchanted anything. Everything that can hurt the king must therefore be ordinary. Which means everything that can hurt the king can be caught by an ordinary tongue. Do your work, Reyes, and remember what you are."

"A furniture item with opinions," Xanthe said.

Corvane left. The kitchen exhaled and resumed being a river.

* * *

The rules of Vell were three, and every child in the valley could recite them before they could recite their own name.

One: no magic crosses the wards. The wards were old—older than the kingdom, older than the dynasty that ruled it, laid down by founders whose names had been worn smooth off every monument—and they ran along the rim of the valley like a ring of invisible mountains, keeping out everything that flickered, whispered, prophesied, or bit in ways that left no wound. Inside Vell, milk soured honestly. Bread rose honestly. People died honestly, of honest causes, and if that sounded grim, well, the valley folk had made a religion of it. *Honest bread*, they called their blessing. *Honest death*. Nothing in Vell was ever more than it appeared.

Two: tasters are crowned property. Bought, in Xanthe's case, at the age of six, from a dealer who had found him abandoned somewhere south with a tongue too valuable to waste on a family that couldn't afford him. He had no memory of the sale. He had no memory of anything before the kitchens—a fact he had made peace with so thoroughly that he'd built his whole personality on top of the gap, the way a tree will grow around a fence post.

Three: tasters never leave. Not to market day in the lower town, not to the orchards, not even to the valley road where you could stand and look north at the mountain—Greywether, the locals called it, though the old maps said something else, something Xanthe had once been whipped for copying out. Tasters stayed inside the palace grounds, inside the wards, inside the rules, until they stopped being useful, at which point rule three presumably resolved itself.

Xanthe had spent nineteen years telling himself this was safety. Walls, after all, keep things out. It had taken him until roughly age fourteen to understand that walls also keep things in, and until very recently—somewhere in the last year, in the slow way understanding arrives—to admit which side of the wall he actually stood on.

But admitting a thing and doing anything about it were different dishes entirely, and Xanthe had learned to serve the first without ever plating the second.

* * *

By late afternoon the nine courses for the ambassador's dinner were assembled in the preparation kitchen, arranged on a long table under linen cloths like a row of sleeping guests. Xanthe walked the line with his notebook—a small leather book, its pages soft as cloth, in which he catalogued everything he tasted. He'd kept it since he was ten. Corvane allowed it because it made Xanthe seem more like equipment and equipment was reassuring. Xanthe kept it because it was the only place in the world where his whole mind got to be visible.

*Course one,* he wrote. *Cold sorrel soup. Sorrel, cream, chive, a suspicion of white pepper. Honest.*

*Course two. Smoked trout, river-caught above the mill. Alder smoke, lemon, brown butter. The lemon is showing off. Honest.*

He worked down the line, tasting each dish with the small silver spoon that hung at his belt on a chain, describing flavors the way other men described horses or weather. It was, he had long ago decided, the closest thing to freedom available to him: for the length of each taste, his tongue traveled where his body never would. Lemon meant somewhere warm. Alder smoke meant rivers and hills. Saffron—rare, hoarded, appearing perhaps twice a year—meant a whole golden country he had invented for himself at age eleven and never revised, because revising would mean admitting he'd made it up.

Course five was the honey cakes.

They came from the pastry kitchen, twelve of them, glazed and gleaming, smelling of clover honey and warm butter, and Xanthe picked one up with the pleasant anticipation he permitted himself exactly twice a year, because honey cake was the single food in the world he genuinely loved. Not the flavor of it—though the flavor was lovely—but the memory-shaped hole it filled. He had no memories from before age six. But the first time he'd tasted honey cake, at seven, something in his chest had said *yes, this, again, you know this*, and he had built a small quiet superstition around it: that somewhere in the missing years there was a kitchen, and a woman, and honey cake, and that the taste was a letter from her that had arrived late.

He bit.

Clover honey. Butter. A warmth of ginger. Fine wheat crumb—

And underneath, so faint it was almost a courtesy, almost apologetic: *wrong*.

Xanthe did not move. He did not change his breathing. He stood at the tasting table holding half a honey cake and let his tongue finish its report while his face did absolutely nothing, because that was the first law of his trade: a taster who tastes trouble shows the trouble nothing.

There was a bitterness in the aftertaste. Not the bright bitterness of herbs or char—that was honest bitterness, bitterness with a job. This was a low, rooty, dusty bitterness, the kind of taste that sits at the back of the throat like a guest who has quietly moved in. He knew every legal bitter in the valley's larders. This wasn't one of them.

More than that—it shouldn't have been possible. Whatever this was, it tasted *grown*, in the way plants are grown, in dirt, in weather, in some specific somewhere. And things grown in magic were supposed to be unable to cross the wards. That was rule one. Rule one was the oldest rule, the foundation everything else stood on.

Xanthe set down the rest of the cake. He took a clean cloth from the stack, wrapped the remaining eleven cakes in it with the unhurried air of a man tidying, and then—when the nearest scullion had turned away—slipped the bitten half into the pocket of his coat.

His heart was doing something unprofessional. He told it to stop.

"Problem, Reyes?" Corvane, from across the room. Of course. The man had eyes in the back of his folded-letter face.

"No, Steward. The cakes are excellent. I'm saving the last bite for concentration."

A pause. "See that you concentrate."

Xanthe finished the line, wrote honest beside six more dishes, and carried his notebook out of the kitchen with the walk of a man who has nowhere in particular to be and is in no hurry whatsoever to get there.

Inside, under his ribs, next to the permanent little coin of cold, a new feeling had taken a seat: a thin, bright wire of curiosity, humming.

*Something crossed the wards,* he thought. *Something grew outside and got in.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, in the voice he tried never to listen to:

*Or the wards didn't hold.*

* * *

He should have reported it. He knew that. Rule of the trade, rule of the valley: a taster finds a flaw, the taster declares the flaw, the flaw is investigated, the culprit is found, and the culprit is—well. Vell was gentle with most crimes. It was not gentle with anything that smelled of magic, even secondhand, even by accident. Whoever had baked that bitterness into the honey had, knowingly or not, committed the one sin the valley had no honest word for.

And whoever was blamed might not be the person who'd done it. That was the arithmetic Xanthe had watched run for nineteen years: when something inexplicable entered the valley, the valley found someone to explain it, and the someone was usually the person least able to argue. A foreign-born cook. A servant with a limp and a reputation. Or—

Or a taster. Crowned property, conveniently already suspected of having a strange tongue, already halfway to being considered a tool rather than a man. *The taster let poison reach the king's plate.* It would take Corvane one afternoon to arrange that story, and the story would fit so neatly that everyone would be relieved to believe it.

So Xanthe did not report it. Instead he did what he always did with dangerous knowledge: he carried it carefully, like a full cup, and went looking for a private corner to set it down.

The cellar under the west wing was that corner. It was where the palace stored its wine, its preserves, its winter apples, and—one flight further down, behind a door the household pretended not to see—its prisoner.

Xanthe had known about her for three years. Everyone below stairs knew about the witch in the cellar, though knowing was technically against the rules, and so everyone expressed their knowledge by leaving bread outside her door and pretending the bread had always been there. She'd been locked away for smuggling—remedies, mostly. Feverfew for the laundry girls' headaches. Willow bark for the butcher's bad knee. Small mercies, moved hand to hand through the kitchens like contraband kindness, until the day Corvane had caught the chain of it and pulled, and the end of the chain had been a gray-cloaked woman with ink-stained fingers who hadn't denied anything, because denying was pointless in a valley where honesty was the state religion.

They called her Odile. They called her a hedge-witch, which in Vell was nearly an oxymoron—hedges grew wild, wild things were natural, natural things were honest—but the priests had examined her and pronounced her touched, which in Vell meant guilty of the only crime that mattered.

Xanthe had spoken to her exactly once, two years ago, through the door, when he'd brought her feverfew for a cough he could hear through the stone. She'd said, "You have a careful voice." He'd said, "It's the only part of me the crown lets be careful." She'd laughed—short, surprised, real—and he had thought about that laugh for longer than was sensible.

Now he stood outside her door with a wrapped half-honey-cake in his pocket and his heart hammering its unprofessional rhythm, and he knocked.

Silence. Then: "If you're Corvane, I've gotten worse-looking and you've gotten no smarter."

"It's the taster."

Another silence, different in texture. "The careful voice." A shuffle, the creak of a cot. "Come to gawk? The view's the same as last time. Stone, bucket, regret."

"I need to ask you about a plant."

"A plant." Her voice had gone flat and watchful. "That's a strange errand for a man whose whole job is food that isn't poisoned."

"That's rather the point." Xanthe glanced up the stairwell, listening. The palace noise was distant, muffled, safe. He unwrapped the honey cake and slid it under the door. "Tell me what this is."

Through the gap, her fingers—ink-stained, quick—drew the cake in. He heard her break it, smell it, and then a pause so long he counted his own heartbeats against it. Eleven. Twelve.

"Where," Odile said, and her voice had lost every ounce of its armor, "did you get thornroot?"

"I was hoping you'd tell me."

"Thornroot doesn't grow in valleys. It grows under mountains. It grows in the dark, in ground that's been—" She stopped. He heard her stand, heard her cross to the door, and when she spoke again her voice was pressed close to the gap, low and fast. "Boy. Listen to me. Thornroot is Undercourt work. Dead-court work. Mountain work. It does not travel. It cannot travel. Unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless something opened the road." A breath. "Unless the wards are thinner than anyone in this fat, comfortable valley knows."

Above them, faintly, the palace bell began to ring the hour. Six bells. Two hours until the king's dinner, until nine courses, until an ambassador, until a table where eleven untainted cakes sat waiting and one bitten half sat poisoning the inside of Xanthe's pocket like a secret with teeth.

"What do I do?" Xanthe asked. It was not a question he was used to asking. His whole life had been questions answered for him: what to eat, where to stand, when to speak, who to be. For the first time in nineteen years, the answer belonged to him alone, and it was terrifying, and it was—God help him—exhilarating.

Odile was quiet a moment. When she spoke, she sounded almost gentle, which frightened him more than the thornroot had.

"Now you decide who you are, taster," she said. "Report it, and spend the rest of your life as a footnote in someone else's story. Or find out who put it there—and why—and spend tonight becoming the main character of your own."

Xanthe looked down at the wrapped cake in his hands, at the small dark crumb of a world that wasn't supposed to exist sitting in the palm of his glove.

Somewhere overhead, the king's dinner bell rang, and the kitchens of Vell roared to life around him like a river that did not know it was about to freeze.
