## 2. What the Honey Held

The poison-proofed valley of Vell had exactly one rule that mattered, and it was painted on the kitchen door in letters so old the paint had gone the color of weak tea: *Nothing crosses the wards.* No magic, no curses, no charms, no hexes, no blessings. Nothing.

Xanthe Reyes had spent eleven years inside that rule and had come to think of it the way other people thought of weather — as a fact of the world, neither kind nor cruel, simply there. The wards kept out magic. The wards also kept in tasters. He'd learned early not to confuse the two protections, though the steward liked to imply they were the same gift.

"Grateful boys don't ask questions," Steward Hobb said that morning, handing Xanthe the day's ledger with two fingers, as if the paper might bite him.

"Grateful boys don't eat," Xanthe said pleasantly, and took the ledger before Hobb could decide to be offended.

It was Tuesday, which meant the king's supper would be pheasant, and pheasant meant the cook's wife would smuggle Xanthe the neck bones afterward because she believed, with the gentle unshakable faith of women who fed people for a living, that he was too thin. He was too thin. Everyone in Vell was either too thin or too frightened to notice.

He began his rounds the way he always did: slowly, and alone.

Tasting was not eating. That was the first thing they taught you, before they taught you anything else worth knowing. A taster's tongue was an instrument, and instruments were kept clean, rested, and honest. Xanthe took his work seriously in the way that lonely people take seriously the one thing they're good at — completely, and a little too much.

The morning's list ran long. Almond milk for the queen's aunt, who was convinced someone hated her (someone did; it was the queen's aunt). The brown bread. The butter from the low meadows, which tasted faintly of wild garlic this time of year and made him homesick for a home he'd never had. The soup — venison, barley, a whisper of juniper. The small beer. The honey cakes.

He saved the honey cakes for last, the way he saved all sweet things for last, because a man ought to have something to look forward to even if the something was four bites long.

There were six of them, set on a blue-glazed plate, golden as late afternoon. Cook Marla's recipe, unchanged since before Xanthe's time: honey, flour, eggs, a breath of rosemary, and — her secret, which everyone knew — a spoonful of burnt sugar stirred in at the end to give the crumb a smoky depth.

Xanthe broke the first cake open and let the steam rise past his face. He breathed it in. He touched a crumb to the center of his tongue. He waited.

Honey. Flour. Egg. Rosemary, shy. Burnt sugar, right on schedule, arriving like an old friend who always came through the same door.

And underneath it, thin as a hair laid across the tongue —

Something wrong.

He didn't spit. Spitting was for amateurs and for poisons you already knew. Instead he held the crumb against his palate and let the flavor finish telling him what it was, cataloguing it the way he catalogued everything: bitter, yes, but a particular bitterness — green and dry at once, like chewing the stem of a plant that had died angry. Beneath the bitterness, a ghost of pepper. And beneath *that*, something almost floral, almost lovely, the way certain beautiful things are lovely precisely because they'll kill you.

Thornroot.

He knew it only from books, from the battered *Physick of Common Harms* in the steward's office that he'd read cover to cover during three bored winters. Thornroot didn't grow within forty leagues of Vell. It certainly didn't grow inside the wards, where nothing magical survived long enough to put down roots — and thornroot was nothing *but* magic, a plant that drank enchantment the way ivy drank sun. Powdered fine, it killed slowly and politely, over weeks, mimicking a wasting fever so well that even a taster might miss it if he weren't paying attention.

Xanthe was paying attention. It was, in fact, the only thing he was ever doing.

He looked down at the blue-glazed plate. Six cakes. He broke the second one open. Same bitterness, fainter. The third — clean. The fourth — clean. The fifth and sixth, clean.

One poisoned cake, hidden among six. Not enough to kill the king. Enough to kill whoever bit into that one first.

*Or,* said a small cold voice in his head, *enough to kill whoever the king is angry at this week.*

He wrapped the bad cake in his handkerchief, tucked it into his sleeve — tasters' sleeves were cut deep for exactly this purpose, though usually for spitting — and finished tasting the rest of the supper with a steady hand and a face like a closed door.

Then he went looking for answers, which was, strictly speaking, against the rules.

* * *

The kitchens of Vell were built like a honeycomb, room stacked on room, and below the lowest room — below the wine cellar, below even the root cellar where turnips slept off the winter — there was a door that wasn't on any map.

Xanthe had found it at fifteen, following a draft. Cold air in a warm building has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere turned out to be a stair going down into the mountain's own bones, ending at an iron-banded door with no lock and no handle, only a slot at knee height, like a door built for feeding something.

Or for feeding someone.

He'd never opened it. For three years he'd told himself that was prudence. Tonight, standing in the dark with powdered thornroot folded in his sleeve, he admitted it had been cowardice wearing prudence's coat.

"Hello?" His voice sounded smaller than usual in the stone.

Silence. Then, dry as paper turning a page:

"If you're the steward, I've already told you where you can put your bread crusts."

Not the steward. A woman's voice, low and roughened, with an accent that had traveled — he could hear the far-north vowels under the court polish, worn smooth by years of moving.

"I'm not the steward," Xanthe said. "I'm the taster."

A pause. "The boy." He heard her shift closer; the slot at the bottom of the door went briefly darker, as if she'd crouched behind it. "They still keep you in a cage above stairs, do they? I can smell the honey on you."

"You can't possibly—"

"Burnt sugar and rosemary. You've been tasting cakes." A short laugh, without much humor in it. "How are the cakes?"

Xanthe unwrapped the handkerchief. In the gloom he couldn't see the cake, but he could smell it, and now that he knew what to smell *for*, the thornroot announced itself like a stranger in a quiet room.

"One of them's poisoned," he said. "Powdered thornroot."

The silence that followed was different from the first silence. The first had been boredom. This one had edges.

"Say that again," the woman said, very quietly.

"Thornroot. Bitter, green-dry, a pepper note under it, and something sweet underneath that I don't like. One cake out of six. Dosed light — a wasting dose, not a killing one. Weeks, not hours."

Another pause, longer. When she spoke again, her voice had changed entirely — the roughness smoothed away, replaced by something quick and precise, a scholar's voice, or a surgeon's.

"Describe the sweetness."

"Like violets. Or like the smell of a church after incense. Almost nice."

"*Almost nice,*" she repeated softly. "Yes. That's how it gets you. That's how it got half the villages north of the Sere, back when I—" She stopped herself. Started again. "Boy. Open the door."

"There's no handle."

"There's a bar on your side, top left, behind the bracket. There's *always* a bar. Whoever built these cells wanted to be able to get out again themselves — prison-builders are the most superstitious men alive."

His fingers found the bracket, found the bar, and lifted. The door swung inward on hinges that hadn't screamed in years, which meant someone oiled them, which meant someone used them.

She stood in the little cell beyond like a heron standing in shallow water: tall, thin, gray-streaked black hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual fishbone, dressed in a servant's smock gone soft with washing. Her eyes were the pale gray of frost on a window, and they went straight past his face to his sleeve.

"The cake," she said. "Give it here."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, no more — and handed it over. She broke it apart with her thumbs, sniffed, touched a crumb to her tongue with the same deliberate care he used himself, and something in her expression made him feel suddenly, absurdly, like a student being shown up by a master.

"Well?" he said.

"Well," she said, "you're not dead, which means your tongue is better than your sense of self-preservation. This is thornroot, ground fine and cut with ash to stretch it. And it should not exist within fifty miles of this valley, because—" she tapped the wall, and the tap rang oddly, flatly, as if the stone were listening — "the wards don't let things like this grow. Or live. Or *be carried in*, most ways."

"Most ways?"

"Most ways." She looked at him properly then, and he had the uncomfortable sensation of being read like a ledger. "You're the one they brought up from the south. The bought child. Reyes."

Heat climbed his neck. "That's not my name for sale."

"No," she agreed, surprising him. "Names shouldn't be. Souls especially." She brushed crumbs from her hands. "My name is Odile. I was the hedge-witch of Grayford parish until the king's men decided that curing a shepherd's fever was witchcraft and curing it *for free* was treason. I've been down here four years, smuggling willow bark and elderflower syrup to servants with bad lungs, and every year the walls get a little thinner and nobody notices but me."

"Why would nobody notice?"

"Because noticing costs something, and courts hate to pay." She held up the ruined cake between two fingers. "But *this* — this somebody paid for. Thornroot doesn't cross wards by accident, boy. It comes in old ways, deep ways. Ways that were paid for in advance."

"What ways?"

She was quiet a moment. Outside, far above, he could hear the kitchens carrying on their evening noise — pots, voices, Marla laughing at something — the whole safe machinery of Vell turning and turning. Down here the sound arrived thin, like news from another country.

"Sit down," Odile said. "No — don't sit, the floor's filthy. Crouch. Crouching feels less like surrender." She waited until he'd awkwardly crouched, then lowered her voice to nearly nothing.

"When I was a girl, my grandmother told me about the Undercourt. The court beneath the mountain, north of here, under the ice. A court of the dead, or of things older than dying. They keep ledgers. Debts. Every bargain anyone ever struck carelessly — a wish traded cheap, a life promised in desperation, a soul signed away — it goes in their books. And when the debt comes due, they collect. They don't send soldiers. They send *arrangements*. Doors left unlatched. Roads that lead where they want. And powders that find their way into kitchens through cracks no one knew existed."

"That's a story for frightening children."

"It is," Odile said. "It's also true, which is the trouble with the best frightening stories." She turned the cake fragment in the dim light. "This didn't come over the wall in a smuggler's cart. Wards like Vell's would burn it out of the air before it crossed the line. This came in the way debts come in. From beneath. From the mountain itself." Her frost-gray eyes fixed on his. "Which means somebody in the Undercourt has an account open with somebody in this palace. And tonight, somebody tried to make a payment."

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "The king eats these cakes."

"The king eats these cakes," Odile agreed. "But one cake in six, dosed to waste rather than kill? That's not regicide, boy. That's *selection*. Someone wanted a particular bite to reach a particular mouth." She tilted her head. "Who serves the king his plate?"

"A page draws lots for the serving. It changes nightly."

"Mm. And who tastes?"

"I taste."

"And if the taster dies of a mysterious wasting illness three weeks from now, who mourns him?"

The question landed harder than she could have known. Xanthe thought of his narrow room, his neat ledgers, the neck bones Marla saved him, the eleven years of careful, useful, invisible living. Nobody would mourn him. Somebody would file him.

"Nobody," he said.

Odile studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something for her. She sighed, and it was the sigh of a woman adding a weight to a pack she'd sworn was full.

"All right," she said. "Here's what happens now. You go back up those stairs, you say nothing, you taste tomorrow's supper, and you bring me a crumb of everything that tastes even one hair wrong. Can you do that?"

"Why should I trust you?"

"Because I'm locked in a hole and you're not, which makes me the least dangerous person in this valley." A ghost of a smile. "And because whoever sent that cake doesn't care whether it kills the king or the taster, and I'd very much like to know who I'm sharing a mountain with before they decide I'm next. Self-interest, boy. The only honest reason for anything."

It was, he had to admit, the most honest thing anyone had said to him in years.

He stood. At the door he paused, hand on the iron band, and asked the question he'd been carrying since the first bitter crumb touched his tongue.

"Odile. If it's really the Undercourt — if the stories are true — what would they want with anyone in Vell? We're nothing. We're a valley with good butter."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer.

"Debts don't care about importance," she said finally. "They care about *balance*. Somewhere in this palace, someone owes the mountain something large. And mountains," she added, tapping the wall again, that flat, listening ring, "are patient. Now go. And boy —"

He looked back.

"— if the stars ever go out," Odile said, "come straight down here. Don't pack. Don't tell the steward. Just come."

"That's a strange thing to say."

"It's a strange world," said the hedge-witch, settling back into the dark of her cell like water finding its level. "We've just been living in the padded part of it."

Xanthe climbed the stairs with the empty handkerchief in his sleeve and the taste of thornroot still ghosting his palate — that green, dry, almost-lovely bitterness, like a flower that had died angry.

Above him, through the kitchen's high windows, the evening sky over Vell was clear and crowded with stars, every one of them burning, every one of them accounted for.

He counted them anyway, out of habit.

All of them there.

For now.
