## 3. The Night Without Stars

The trouble with living in a valley that never changed was that you stopped believing anything could.

I had been in Vell for eleven years, and in all that time the wards had held the way a good roof holds—silently, thanklessly, and only noticed when they failed. Master Corvin, the under-steward, liked to remind us at every opportunity that nothing crossed them. No magic. No curses. No wild weather worth naming. The valley was a bowl of perfect safety, and we were the king's dishes, kept clean and covered until wanted.

So on the night the stars went out, I did what any sensible person does when the impossible happens: I assumed it was my fault.

Let me explain. I had been up late cataloguing the spice stores, which sounds duller than it is. There's a real pleasure in a well-ordered cupboard—cinnamon ranked by warmth, saffron locked away like a hostage, the pepper tins labeled in my own careful hand because the last boy who labeled them wrote *peper* and Master Corvin made him scrub the whole pantry floor with a toothbrush as punishment for typography. I had just finished the cumin when I noticed the light through the high kitchen window had gone strange.

Not dark. Strange. The kind of light that makes you look over your shoulder to see who's standing behind you, except nobody is.

I climbed onto the flour barrel and looked out.

The sky was empty.

Now, I want to be precise here, because precision was my trade. Not cloudy. Not overcast. Empty. I knew that stretch of sky the way I knew the king's supper table—the Shepherd's crook of stars above the eastern ridge, the three sisters that hung low over the mountain road, the one bright stubborn star the cooks called Old Marta because she refused to set before her time. Every night of my life they had been there, patient as salt.

They were gone. All of them. As if someone had reached down and picked the sky clean, the way I'd pick raisins out of a cake I didn't trust.

And beneath where the stars should have been, far to the north, the mountain glowed. Faintly. Greenly. The way pond water glows when something dead is in it.

"Right," I said aloud, to the flour barrel, because there was no one else. "That's not weather."

* * *

The kitchens woke the way a hive wakes when you poke it—first one confused hum, then a roar. Cook Brannick came down in his nightshirt with a cleaver he had clearly grabbed by instinct rather than plan, which meant he was prepared to defend the soup against an intruder. The scullery girls clung to each other and to the idea that it was an eclipse, though eclipses, as Odile would later tell me with some scorn, do not generally remove every star in the hemisphere while leaving the moon untouched. The moon was still there, hanging pale and wrong over the ridge like a coin someone had forgotten to take.

Master Corvin arrived last, fully dressed, which told me he hadn't been sleeping. He stood in the middle of the kitchen with his hands behind his back and his jaw doing that thing it did when he was deciding whom to blame.

"The wards are holding," he announced, in the voice of a man reading from a script no one had written yet. "Whatever this is, it is outside our concern. Return to your beds."

"Sir," said Brannick, "the stars are *gone*."

"Stars are outside the valley's jurisdiction," said Corvin. This was such a magnificently stupid sentence that for a moment the whole kitchen simply stared at him, and I understood that I was not the only one realizing the world was bigger than promised. Corvin believed, sincerely, in the little kingdom of his own authority. The stars going out was, to him, primarily an act of insubordination.

Then the cold came in.

It didn't creep. It poured—through the window seams, under the door, down the chimney like water finding its level. I felt it pass through the kitchen the way you feel a stranger walk past you in a market, and I felt something else too, something I'd never felt in Vell: a *looseness*. Eleven years I had lived inside those wards, and I had never once noticed their weight until it lifted. It was like spending your whole life wearing a coat and suddenly standing bare-armed in the wind. The air tasted different. Sharper. Older. It tasted like the world before the valley, if that makes sense, and it does make sense, because I tasted things for a living and I am telling you the air itself had changed flavor.

Brannick lit every candle in the pantry. The scullery girls started a fire in the great hearth big enough to roast an ox, though we had no ox. Somewhere upstairs, a bell began ringing—not the supper bell, not the alarm bell, but the old chapel bell that nobody rang because it was cracked and sounded like a spoon hitting a pot. Someone was ringing it anyway, over and over, as if noise could stitch the sky back together.

And in the middle of all of it, I remembered the cellar.

* * *

You have to understand: I wasn't thinking heroically. I was thinking practically, the way I always did. The cellar held the wine stores and the root vegetables and, since autumn, one imprisoned hedge-witch. If the wards were down—if the thing that kept magic out had failed—then the thing that kept her *in* had presumably failed with it, because they were the same walls. And if Odile got loose in a panicking castle, she would be blamed for everything. The stars, the cold, the cracked bell, probably the state of the pepper labels. Master Corvin needed a culprit the way a fire needs wood, and a smuggled witch was conveniently flammable.

Also—and I'll admit this part quietly—I wanted to know if she was still there. I wanted to know if the thornroot in the honey cake had come from her, or through her, or around her, and whether the green glow on the mountain had anything to do with the poison that shouldn't exist inside the wards. My tongue had found thornroot where no thornroot could be. Now the sky had emptied itself. I am a taster; I notice when flavors don't match their labels.

I took a candle and went down.

The cellar stairs were cold enough to hurt. My breath came out in ghosts. The door at the bottom—old oak, iron-banded, locked since the day I'd first heard her singing through it—stood open. Not broken. Open. Swung wide and abandoned, like the door of a house whose owner has stepped out to watch something.

Inside the cell, the cot was empty, the blanket folded. Folded! She had escaped from a dungeon and *made the bed*. I don't know why that detail undid me more than the vanished stars. There was something both absurd and deeply characteristic about it—a woman fleeing into the end of the world pausing to tidy up, because chaos is no excuse for slovenliness.

On the folded blanket sat a sprig of dried something—yarrow, I'd learn later—and underneath it, weighted by a pebble, a scrap of paper.

*Gone to see. Don't follow. —O.*

Below that, smaller, as if added after some debate:

*You will follow. Fine. North wall, the drain grate by the old ice house. Bring food. Witches run better fed.*

I laughed. Alone in a freezing cellar on the night the sky died, I laughed until my ribs ached, and I think it was mostly relief. The world had just torn open its hem, and the first message out of the tear was a joke with instructions attached. Whatever else was coming, at least I wouldn't face it alone, and at least it would be faced with provisions.

I went back up through the chaos—Corvin shouting about curfew, Brannick guarding the soup, the cracked bell hammering its spoon-on-pot grief—and raided the bread store. A heel of rye, half a wheel of hard cheese, a jar of pickled onions because I judged she'd appreciate the humor, and, after a moment's consideration, two honey cakes from the batch that had *not* been poisoned. It seemed important to reclaim honey cake somehow. To prove it could still be innocent.

Then I went out into the night.

* * *

I had never been outside after dark. That bears repeating: eleven years in Vell, and I had never once seen the valley at night, because tasters were locked in with the kitchens and the kitchens were locked in with the walls, and the walls were locked in with the wards, and the wards were locked in with the stars, apparently, though nobody had thought to mention that last lock to anyone.

The cold hit me like a hand. Not the polite cold of a Vell winter—I'd known that, the tidy frost that dusted the herb garden and made the well rope stiff. This was a cold with intent. It came down off the mountains and across the fields and it did not care about walls, because walls, I now understood, had only ever worked when the sky agreed to them.

The north wall of the castle backed onto the old ice house, a sunken stone building that hadn't stored ice since before I was born. The drain grate there was rusted but movable, and beyond it the ground fell away toward the river and the dark fields, and beyond *that*, filling a quarter of the empty sky, the mountain burned its slow green.

Up close, the glow was worse. Better. Worse and better. It wasn't firelight; it moved wrong for fire, breathing instead of flickering, swelling and dimming like something enormous asleep. And threaded through the green, if I tilted my head, were lines—thin, bright filaments rising off the peak and reaching up toward the place where the stars used to be. Reaching and finding nothing. Reaching anyway.

"It's drinking," said a voice beside me, and I nearly died of fright on the spot, which would have saved everyone a great deal of trouble.

Odile crouched on the wall's foundations with her hood back and her gray hair loose, looking younger than she had in the cell, or maybe just more awake. She had a knitted shawl and an expression of terrible recognition, the way you look at a letter from someone you hoped was dead.

"What's drinking?" I managed.

"The mountain. Or what lives under it." She took the bread and cheese from me without ceremony, broke the cheese in half, and handed half back, which I later understood was a witch's way of saying *we're in this together now, no refunds.* "Those threads—it's pulling on the sky. On whatever was left up there when the stars went out. Trying to drink the last of the light before winter closes."

"Why?"

"Because it's dying," she said simply, chewing. "Old things get frightened. Frightened old things with power do worse than steal. They collect." She looked at me then, really looked, candlelight and green mountain-light mixing on my face. "You tasted the thornroot. You're the one who pocketed the cake. I wondered which of the kitchen brats had the nerve."

"I'm nineteen."

"You're a brat with nerve. Congratulations. It's the only qualification that matters tonight." She nodded north. "Do you know what's under that mountain?"

"No."

"Neither do I, entirely. But I know what it takes." She swallowed the last of the cheese and stood, brushing frost off her knees. "Debts, boy. It takes debts. And when the wards fell tonight—when the strings got cut—" she snapped her fingers, and the sound cracked oddly in the frozen air, "—every debt this valley ever owed came due at once. Everything anybody ever bargained away in the dark. Everything somebody's grandmother promised to something hungry. All of it, called in, starting now."

The cold walked up my spine and sat down. "Called in from whom?"

Odile looked at me for a long moment. The green light made her eyes look like pond ice.

"That," she said, "is the right question. Which means you're smarter than the steward, which is a low bar, but I'll take it." She pulled the shawl tighter. "I don't know yet. But I watched the king's tower tonight, Xanthe Reyes, before I came up here. Every window lit. Guards doubled. And a rider went out the north gate at a gallop, alone, carrying a letter sealed in black wax."

"A letter to whom? There's nothing north but the mountain."

"Mm," said Odile.

Far off across the fields, the cracked chapel bell finally stopped ringing. In the silence that followed I could hear something new—very faint, very far, riding down the wind off the mountain. Not words. Not quite. More like the memory of words, the way you can almost recall a tune without recalling a single note.

Tam, the scullery boy, would later swear the same sound had been saying his name. But that night it was only me and the witch and the bread crumbs and the green, breathing dark.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something, and the sky certainly wasn't going to. "I brought pickled onions."

Odile took the jar, examined it, and smiled for the first time since I'd known her—a quick, crooked, dangerous smile, the smile of a woman deciding that if the world was ending, it might as well end interestingly.

"North, then," she said. "Before the mountain finishes its drink. And boy—walk fast, eat well, and whatever knocks on your door in the next few days asking what you owe—"

She paused. Down in the valley behind us, every bell in Vell began to ring at once.

"—don't answer."
