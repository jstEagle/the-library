## 4. A Debt Read Aloud

The bells rang for three hours, and then they stopped all at once, the way a crowd stops when someone important walks in.

I was in the kitchens when it happened. Of course I was. Tasters don't get summoned to councils or war rooms; tasters get summoned to tables. Master Corvin found me elbow-deep in a vat of barley soup, tasting for bitterness that might have been scorched grain and might have been something worse, and he said only two words:

"The king."

I dried my hands. I put on my good coat—the one with the silver thistle stitched at the collar, the mark of the Crown's Table—and I followed him up through the palace's warm innards, past the laundry steam and the guardrooms and the long gallery where the portraits of dead kings watched you with the patience of men who had never once worried about their supper.

Corvin didn't speak on the way. That was the first wrong thing. Corvin always spoke. He narrated his cruelty the way other men narrated their virtues—*the king requires discretion, the king requires speed, the king requires you not to be seen by the chambermaids*—as though cruelty were a craft he was perfecting. Tonight he walked in silence, and his silence had a texture to it, like the air before snow.

The second wrong thing was the route. We didn't go to the council chamber or the private dining room where I'd tasted the king's meals for eleven years, standing at the sideboard with my little silver spoon while great lords pretended I was furniture. We went *up*. Past the residential floors. Up the spiral stair behind the tapestry of the Founding Hunt, which I hadn't known opened, into a round room I had never seen, with one window and one table and one chair.

One chair.

"Sit," Corvin said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit," said the king.

He was standing at the window with his back to me, and I nearly didn't recognize him. In the dining room, King Aldous of Vell was a performance: the hearty laugh, the appetite, the way he'd wave off my verdicts with *"if Xanthe can eat it, I can eat it"* as if I were a lucky charm instead of a boy bought at auction. Alone, at night, without an audience, he looked like what he actually was—a tired man in a heavy robe, holding himself upright by the window frame.

I sat. The chair was warm, which meant someone had been sitting in it recently, waiting, and had left in a hurry when we came. I decided not to think about who.

"Do you know why the stars went out?" the king asked.

"No, Your Majesty."

"Do you know why the wards fell?"

"The wards are tied to the stars," I said. "Everyone knows that. It's the first thing they teach—" I stopped myself, because the second thing they teach is that tasters shouldn't finish sentences.

But the king turned around, and there was something in his face that made finishing feel permitted. "Go on."

"They're woven together," I said. "Star-thread and ward-thread. Same loom. When the stars die, the wards come down with them." I hesitated. "Except nobody ever explained who wove them. Or why the stars would die in the first place. Stars don't die all at once, sir. Even I know that, and I know about as much of the world as fits inside a kitchen."

Something crossed the king's face—grief, maybe, or its expensive cousin, guilt.

"No," he said. "They don't die all at once. They're *collected*. All at once, when a debt comes due that's large enough." He came away from the window and sat down across from me, and up close I could see that his hands weren't steady. Kings aren't supposed to have unsteady hands. I'd spent eleven years watching hands—that's most of a taster's work, watching the hands of everyone who comes near the king's plate—and I knew every species of tremor there was: fear, drink, palsy, poison. This was none of those. This was the tremor of a man holding a ledger he didn't want to read.

"You're going to tell me something," I said. "And you've had Corvin clear the room and bring me up the back stair and sit me in a warm chair, which means it's either a great honor or a great danger, and in my experience the honors and the dangers wear the same coat in this palace."

That almost got a smile out of him. Almost.

"When you were brought to Vell," he said, "you were six years old. Do you remember anything before that?"

"A woman singing," I said. "A door with a green light under it. Rain on a roof that leaked in the corner, over the bed." I kept my voice flat, because this was the game we played, kings and tasters: nothing touches you, nothing shows. "Why?"

"Her name was Maren Reyes." He said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "She came to the palace in the winter of your fifth year. Pregnant. Sick. Dying—though she didn't tell us that part until later. She'd walked a long way to reach the valley, because she'd heard the wards keep out magic, and she wanted her child born somewhere magic couldn't touch him."

My chest did something complicated. I kept my face still. Eleven years of practice.

"She was a hedge-touched woman herself," the king went on. "Small gifts. Weather-sense, mostly. Nothing the wards would have troubled. But she'd made enemies of a kind you can't walk away from, and she knew it, and so she ran here, to the one place in the world where nothing could follow her." He paused. "Including the thing she owed."

"And what did she owe?"

The king looked at me for a long moment. Then he reached into his robe and took out a folded paper—not parchment, something older, gray-green, soft as cloth—and unfolded it on the table between us.

It was covered in writing. Not ink. The letters were pressed into the stuff itself, like footprints in snow, and they moved very slightly when I wasn't looking directly at them, the way water moves when you're not looking directly at it.

"This appeared on my father's desk the winter she arrived," the king said. "It has appeared every year since, on the same night. Always the same words. My father never told me what it was. He died owing it, and now I owe it, and tonight—for the first time in forty years—it hasn't appeared. Because tonight the debt stopped being a letter and became a summons."

I read it. Or I tried to. The letters swam, but the meaning came through anyway, cold and clear, the way a taste comes through a mouthful of honey:

*One soul, held in trust against the debt of Maren Reyes. Term of loan: one life, hers. Collateral: the child unborn. Payable at midwinter following the fall of the wards.*

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, and started again. "That's not how debts work. You can't hold a person in trust. A person isn't a—"

"A jar of pickled onions?" the king said quietly.

I flinched. He noticed. Of course he noticed; he'd been buying children at auction since before I was born.

"Your mother was given a year," he said. "One year of warmth, of safety, of medicine my physicians could provide and no hedge-witch could match. In exchange, the Undercourt—the court beneath the mountain, the one your grandmother used to scare her grandchildren with—took a promise. Not her promise. She had nothing left to promise except herself, and they'd already priced her life at a single winter. So they took the only collateral she had left." His voice went very gentle, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "They took you. Unborn, unnamed, unasked. Her signature is at the bottom. She signed it to live long enough to give birth to you somewhere safe."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, faintly, the kitchens were still working—I could hear the ghost of the night ovens, the clatter of pans, the ordinary music of the only world I'd ever trusted.

"She signed it," I repeated.

"In her own hand."

"To save me."

"To save you *for a year*," the king said. "After that, you were crown property, like all the valley's bought children. And the debt—" he tapped the gray-green paper, and it shivered under his finger like a living thing, "—the debt stayed with the crown. Passed from my father to me. Forty years of paying the interest, Xanthe. Forty years of keeping the wards strong, keeping the stars fed, keeping the mountain asleep. Every ceremony, every tithe, every secret my father never wrote down and I never asked about—all of it was maintenance on a debt I inherited along with the throne."

"And now the wards are down."

"And now the wards are down," he agreed. "And the principal comes due. At midwinter. Which is—" he did the arithmetic aloud, and I hated him for doing it calmly, "—nineteen days from tonight."

I sat with it. That's what I'm good at, sitting with things. Bad flavors, bad news, bad silences. You hold them on your tongue until you understand their shape.

"Nineteen days," I said. "And then what? What does 'payable' mean? What do they do with a soul?"

"I don't know," the king said honestly. "No one alive knows. But I know what the mountain is, and I know what happens to kingdoms that default. There are ruins north of the Sere that used to be kingdoms. Whole dynasties, unwritten from history. The Undercourt doesn't rage, Xanthe. It doesn't burn. It *collects*, precisely and completely, and it takes whatever it decides the debt weighs."

"So pay it," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Pay it some other way. Gold. Land. Another soul—you're a king, you must have enemies whose souls nobody would miss."

"We tried substitutes once. Before your time. A condemned man, a willing priest, a purchased orphan." His eyes flicked away. "The mountain returned all three within a week. Returned them *changed*. The clerks beneath weigh souls like coin, and counterfeit coin insults them worse than default. No, Xanthe. There's only one payment that clears this ledger, and it has your name on it. It has had your name on it since before you had a name."

I looked down at the contract, at the moving letters, at the small cramped signature at the bottom that I would never learn to read as my mother's handwriting because I had never seen her handwriting, because I had never seen her, because she had traded a year of her life for my birth and signed me over like a cask of wine to cover the difference.

*Nineteen days.*

"Say it," I said. "Whatever you brought me up here to say. Say it plainly. I taste for a living; I can take the truth straight."

The king folded his hands on the table, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-odd years, and then some.

"At midwinter," he said, "you will be taken to the mountain. Formally. With honor—an escort, a written warrant, everything proper. The kingdom survives, the debt clears, and you will be remembered in the rolls as a servant of the crown who gave his life in service, which will be true, and honored, which will be more than most of us earn." He met my eyes. "I am not asking you to volunteer. I am telling you what will happen, because you deserve to hear it from a man's mouth rather than a clerk's pen. But I want you to understand that I have spent eleven years feeding you at my own table, and I take no pleasure—"

"*Feeding* me." Something in me snapped, clean, like a thread pulled too tight. "You fed me. Yes. You fed me the same food I tasted for you, night after night, in case someone had put thornroot in the honey cakes. You kept me in a valley with walls around it and rules that said tasters never leave, and you called it protection. And the whole time, the whole *time*, I was already sold. Already weighed. Already entered in some mountain clerk's ledger, accruing interest. You didn't raise me, Your Majesty. You *stored* me."

"Xanthe—"

"Did she know?" My voice cracked on it. "My mother. When she signed. Did she know what she was signing?"

The king was quiet for a moment. "The clause says 'payable in kind, or in the child.' I've read it ten thousand times. I believe she thought she was promising them a servant. A taster. Someone useful. I believe she told herself the mountain would get a loyal little functionary and she'd get a son who lived." He shook his head slowly. "Mothers are very good at telling themselves things. It's how they survive us."

I stood up. My legs were shaking, and I stood up anyway, because there are moments when sitting down feels like agreeing.

"Then here's what I tell myself," I said. "I tell myself that a woman who walked a hundred miles pregnant and dying, to a valley she'd heard might protect her, did not spend her last year buying me a cell. I tell myself that whatever she signed, she signed it the way I pocket a poisoned cake—quietly, quickly, hoping to fix it later before anyone swallowed the worst of it." I looked at the contract on the table, at the letters crawling like insects. "And I tell myself that nineteen days is not a sentence. It's a deadline. Deadlines can be met early."

The king rose too. For a moment we just looked at each other across the little table, the man who owned me and the boy he'd stored, and I saw something move behind his eyes that might have been shame, or might have been fear for his kingdom wearing shame's coat. With kings, it's usually both.

"You'll be guarded," he said softly. "I'm sorry. I can't leave this to chance—not with everything that falls if you default. Corvin will arrange it tonight. Comfortable rooms. Good food." A pause. "You'll want for nothing."

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me."

I bowed—the full bow, the one I gave before tasting, the one that means *I accept what's coming*—and I walked to the door. My hand was on the latch when he spoke again, quieter than before.

"For what it's worth," King Aldous said, "she sang. Your mother. The whole year she was here, you could hear her through the infirmary wall. The servants complained." He almost smiled. "I never did."

I stood there with my hand on the door and let that land, and it landed badly, the way kindness lands when it arrives nineteen days too late.

Then I opened the door, and Corvin was there, exactly where a jailer stands, and behind him two guards with kind faces and unsentimental hands, and I understood that the comfortable rooms had already been arranged.

* * *

They put me in the east guest wing, which tells you everything: not a cell, a *guest room*, with a fire and a featherbed and a window that locked from the outside. Supper came on real plates—quail in plum sauce, which I tasted out of habit and found flawless, and ate out of spite.

I counted exits the way I catalogued flavors. Window: barred. Door: guarded. Chimney: too narrow, and lit besides. Floorboards: old, but old boards sing. I lay on the featherbed in the dark and listened to the guards breathe outside my door, two men with families, probably, doing their jobs, guarding a boy the way you guard a treasury. Not a prisoner. An asset. Crown property, awaiting delivery.

Somewhere in the palace below, Odile was locked in a cellar, and the wards were down, and the mountain to the north was glowing green like a bruise, and in nineteen days I was due for delivery like a parcel of winter wool.

I thought about my mother singing through an infirmary wall. I thought about a woman who walked a hundred miles to buy her child one safe year, and what she'd have done—what *anyone* would have done—if she'd known the year was a loan and the child was the interest.

I thought about the honey cake. About powdered thornroot that shouldn't exist inside the wards, and a smuggled witch in a cellar who'd looked at me with sharp gray eyes and said the word *Undercourt* like a warning and a door at the same time.

Nineteen days was enough time to deliver me to the mountain.

It was also enough time to get to a cellar.

I got up quietly, took the quail bone from my plate, and went to work on the window lock, slow and patient, the way you work any stubborn flavor out of a dish—one scrape at a time, until what's underneath finally shows.

Outside, far to the north, the mountain glowed, and waited, and kept its books.
