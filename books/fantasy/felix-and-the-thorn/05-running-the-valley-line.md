## 5. Running the Valley Line

The wine cellars of Vell smelled of dust and old money, and Xanthe had never once been allowed inside them.

That was the thing about being a taster. You were precious enough to guard and cheap enough to lock away. He'd spent eleven years in the valley, and in all that time his world had been the kitchens, the sleeping corridor, the tasting room with its little lead-lined window, and the gravel path between them where the steward liked to walk beside him and remind him, conversationally, how replaceable he was.

Now he was running through the one part of the palace he'd never seen, carrying a stolen honey cake in his pocket like a talisman, following a woman who moved through the dark as if she'd been born in it.

"Left here," Odile whispered.

"There *is* no left here. There's a wall."

"There's a door behind the wall." She pressed her palm flat against the stone, fingers spread, and murmured something under her breath — not a spell, he realized, just a counting rhythm, like a child skipping rope. "Third keg from the corner. Help me shift it."

The keg weighed as much as a sleeping ox. Together they rolled it aside, and behind it, exactly as she'd promised, was a door — low, iron-banded, older than anything else in the palace, with a keyhole shaped like a thorn.

"You've been down here before," Xanthe said.

"I've been everywhere in this palace before," Odile said. "Eleven years is a long time to be a prisoner if you don't also become a locksmith." She worked something thin and hooked out of her sleeve. "Hold the light steady."

He held the candle steady. His hands were not entirely steady themselves, but the flame didn't seem to mind.

Above them, muffled by six feet of stone, the palace was screaming. He could hear it even down here — bells, boots, the steward's voice cracking across the yard as he tried to organize panic into columns. The wards had fallen at midnight, every star in the sky had gone out like snuffed candles, and the king's answer had been to summon a taster to a private audience and tell him he was going to be handed to the mountain like a coin tossed into a well.

*Midwinter,* the king had said, with that soft regretful voice he used when he wanted to sound kind. *You'll be treated honorably. You'll want for nothing on the road.*

Xanthe had smiled and bowed and said yes, Your Majesty, and then walked back to the kitchens with his heart hammering out a rhythm no cook would have approved of, and found Odile waiting in the dark of the cellar stairwell because of course she'd already picked her cell's lock; the guards had simply stopped checking after the first year.

"They'll notice I'm gone by morning," he said now, watching the hook turn in the old lock.

"They noticed you were gone an hour ago. That's why we're going through the wine cellars instead of the gate." The lock clicked. "Everyone runs toward the gates. Gates are for people who think the kingdom still wants to catch them gently."

"And the ward-roads?"

"The ward-roads are for people who know the valley better than the kingdom does." She shouldered the door open onto a passage so narrow they had to go single file, the ceiling low enough that Odile, who was not a tall woman, still had to duck. "The wards were built on roads, boy. Old ones. The masons laid the ward-stones along paths that were here before Vell was Vell, and the paths are still here underneath, like veins under skin. Magic can't cross the wards — but the wards can't cross the paths either. Dead ground. Blind spots."

"So we walk the blind spots."

"We walk the blind spots," she agreed, "and we do it quietly, and we do it *now*, before someone remembers the cellar maps exist."

The passage sloped downward, then turned, then turned again, and Xanthe lost all sense of direction within a hundred steps. The air grew cold and mineral-smelling. Once, water dripped somewhere ahead with the patience of a thing that had been dripping since before the kings. Odile counted turns under her breath — left at the drip, right at the crack in the wall, straight past the niche where somebody centuries ago had set a little clay lamp that still held a stub of tallow nobody had ever bothered to burn.

"How do you know all this?" he asked.

"Because when you're locked in a cellar for smuggling, you have a lot of time to talk to the people who bring your bread," Odile said. "And the people who bring bread know everything. The baker knows which walls are hollow. The scullery girl knows which guards sleep deepest. The boy who scrubs the wine cellar floor knows there's a door behind the third keg from the corner, because his grandfather helped build the false wall and told him so over supper, once, drunk on cooking sherry." She glanced back, and in the candlelight her face was sharp and tired and almost smiling. "Kingdoms run on secrets, Xanthe. Kitchens run on gossip. Guess which one lasts longer."

They climbed. The passage ended at a wooden hatch, and above the hatch, when Odile lifted it a hand's width, was night air and frost and the smell of the herb garden gone bitter with cold.

And above the garden — Xanthe's stomach dropped — the sky.

It was worse from outside. In the kitchen, with walls around him, the starless sky had been a rumor. Out here it was a fact, vast and total, a blackness so complete it felt less like night than like a held breath. No stars. No moon. Only the faint green glow far to the north, where the mountain sat, patient as a creditor.

"It's looking for us," he said, without meaning to say it aloud.

"The mountain doesn't look," Odile said. "Mountains wait. It's everything else we need to worry about." She pulled the hatch closed behind them and stood a moment in the herb rows, letting her eyes adjust. "Garden wall, then the drainage cut, then the orchard. After the orchard we're outside the palace grounds, and after that—" she patted the wall of the nearest shed, "—we're on the ward-road proper, and the king's riders can gallop over our heads all night without ever knowing we're beneath them."

They went. The frost crunched. Somewhere behind them a bell began ringing a pattern Xanthe didn't recognize, urgent and wrong, and Odile muttered, "Alarm for a missing taster. They'll search the valley line first. Everyone always searches the valley line."

"What's the valley line?"

"The ring of watchtowers around the valley floor. The only way in or out of Vell that anyone admits exists." She gave him a look over her shoulder. "Which is why we're not taking it. We're taking what's under it."

* * *

They might have made the orchard cleanly, if not for the boy.

He was huddled in the drainage cut where it passed beneath the garden wall — a small shape in an oversized coat, knees drawn up, hands blue with cold, and when Odile's candle swung toward him he flinched so hard he cracked his elbow against the stone and let out a yelp he immediately tried to swallow.

"Don't," he said. "Don't shout. Please don't shout. I'm not — I wasn't stealing, I swear, I was just—"

"Tam?" Xanthe said.

The boy looked up. It was Tam — the scullery boy, twelve years old maybe, all elbows and freckles, who scrubbed pots and stole ends of bread and had once, memorably, eaten an entire wheel of cheese that had been meant for the ambassador's table and blamed it, with a straight face, on rats of unusual size. Xanthe knew him the way kitchen people know each other: in passing, in the steam and noise, a nod across the boiling room.

Tam stared at him. Then at Odile. Then at the candle, as if it might explain things.

"You're running too," Tam said. It wasn't a question. His voice had a strange flat quality, like someone reciting something they'd rehearsed and then abandoned. "Of course you're running. Everyone who's marked is running tonight."

"Marked," Odile repeated carefully.

"By the mountain." Tam hugged his knees tighter. "Since the stars went out. It says my name." He said it the way you'd report the weather — flat, factual, terribly young. "Over and over. *Tam. Tam. Tam.* Like it's learning how to say it. Like it's practicing." He looked up at them, and his eyes were wet but his chin was stubborn. "I thought if I got out of the palace it would stop. It hasn't stopped. But it's quieter out here, and I thought — I heard the alarm, and I saw you two going over the wall, and I thought, well. If the mountain wants me, and the king wants him—" he jerked his chin at Xanthe, "—then maybe we're all going the same direction anyway."

There was a silence. The green glow pulsed faintly on the northern horizon, or perhaps that was only Xanthe's imagination.

"That's the worst plan I've ever heard," Odile said.

"Thank you," said Tam.

"It's not a plan at all. It's three frightened people walking north into the teeth of the thing that's calling you. Do you understand what's up there? Do you know what took the stars?"

"No," Tam admitted. "But it knows my name, and I'd rather hear it say it to my face than through a floor."

Odile opened her mouth. Xanthe watched her — watched the war move across her face, suspicion wrestling with something softer — and understood that she was doing the arithmetic of trust, the same arithmetic he was doing. Three days ago his whole world had been a tasting spoon and a gravel path. Now the world was this: a fugitive witch, a whispering boy, a starless sky, and midwinter coming like a debt collector.

His mother had sold him before he was born. The king had raised him like a pig for market. Every adult who had ever fed him had been fattening him.

But this boy had heard a mountain say his name and chosen to walk toward it rather than hide under it. That was either madness or courage, and Xanthe had learned, in the last three days, that they often wore the same coat.

"He comes," Xanthe said.

Odile exhaled through her nose. "You realize—"

"I realize. But the mountain's been whispering to him since the stars died, and the wards fell the same hour. Whatever's happening, he's part of it. And if we leave him here, the king's men find him by dawn, and the first question they ask a frightened twelve-year-old is *which way did they go?*" He crouched to Tam's level. "Can you keep quiet?"

"I can be quiet as a pot that's done boiling," Tam said solemnly.

"That's the quietest thing I've ever heard anyone promise," Odile muttered. "Fine. Fine. But rules." She held up fingers. "One: you do what I say on the road, first time, no arguing. Two: if the whispers get louder, you tell me, immediately, because a boy who hears voices is a boy who can be *used*, and I've seen what happens to people the mountain uses. Three—" she paused, and her voice gentled despite itself, "—when did you last eat?"

Tam blinked. "...Supper?"

"When did you last eat *properly*?"

"...Tuesday?"

Odile reached into her bag and produced half a loaf, wrapped in cloth, and handed it over with the gravity of a queen bestowing a duchy. Tam fell on it. Watching him eat, Xanthe felt something in his chest loosen slightly — the first loosening since the king's soft, terrible voice had said *midwinter.*

Found family, he thought. Or found something. A witch, a thief of a boy, and a taster with poison in his pocket. It wasn't much. But it was more than any of them had had an hour ago.

* * *

The ward-roads were nothing like roads.

They were gaps — places where the frost lay differently, where the wind didn't quite reach, where Odile would stop, press her hand to a fence post or a boundary stone, and murmur her counting-rhyme until some invisible seam admitted them. They traveled through the backs of things: behind hedgerows, along dry streambeds, through the hollow under a fallen barn where sheep had once sheltered and their warmth seemed somehow still stored in the wood.

Once, they heard horses on the ridge above them — the king's riders, torches swinging, calling to each other across the frost. The three of them pressed into the lee of a boundary stone while hooves churned the mud ten feet overhead, close enough that Xanthe could smell the horses' sweat.

"They can't see us?" Tam breathed.

"Not from the road," Odile whispered back. "The ward-stone blinds this whole hollow. Hold still."

A rider shouted something — *"the tower at Grayford will light the beacon"* — and then the hoofbeats faded, and the night swallowed the torchlight, and they were alone again with the black sky and the green pulse of the mountain.

"Grayford," Odile said softly, when they dared move. "That's on our line. We'll need to pass near it tomorrow." She looked at the sky, at the nothing where stars should have been, and for the first time since the cellar, Xanthe saw her look genuinely afraid. "We should rest. There's a charcoal burner's hut off this seam, half a mile. Burners are poor and polite and never ask questions."

In the hut — a low turf-roofed thing smelling of char and pine — they made a fire small enough to hide and ate the rest of the loaf and a hard sausage Odile produced from somewhere, and Tam, curled against the wall, said suddenly:

"It's saying it again."

Xanthe went cold. "Here? Inside?"

"Not my name." Tam's eyes were closed. His face, firelit, looked both younger and older than twelve. "It's... singing, now. Since we started walking together. Not words. More like — you know when the kettle's almost boiled, and it hums before it screams?" He opened his eyes. "It's humming. And I think — I think it's humming at *you*, Xanthe. Not me. It says my name, but it hums at you. Like it's pleased."

The fire popped. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the little window like a face.

"Well," said Odile, into the silence. "Then we'd best walk fast, and give it nothing to be pleased about."

She banked the fire. Tam slept almost instantly, the way children do, one hand still curled around the last crust. Odile took first watch, sitting with her back to the door and her hooks and picks spread out on her knee like a surgeon's tools, and Xanthe lay on the hard floor staring at the ceiling beams and listening to the boy breathe.

Somewhere north, a mountain was humming his name-adjacent name, holding his mother's soul in its ledgers, counting down to midwinter.

Let it count, he thought, surprising himself. Let it count. I'm coming to collect *first.*

He slept, and dreamed of honey, sweet and untainted, and woke before dawn to Odile's hand on his shoulder and the words no runner ever wants to hear:

"Smoke on the valley line. They've lit the beacons. Someone's told them which way we're headed — and, Xanthe? The beacon at Grayford isn't a warning beacon."

She held up the scrap of paper she'd taken from a rider's dropped satchel an hour before dawn, when she'd slipped out to scout. Her face in the grey light was grim.

"It's a tollhouse. And the toll," she said, "is memories."
