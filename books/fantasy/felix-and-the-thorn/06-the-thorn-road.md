## 6. The Thorn Road

The first thing you learn about frost-killed farmland is that it crunches. Not the pleasant crunch of snow underfoot — the ugly crunch of a whole year's harvest turned to glass in the fields. Odile walked through a barley field that had been golden in September and was now a field of brittle brown needles, and every step sounded like walking on the bones of somebody's bad decisions.

"Sorry," she muttered to the field. Old habit. You apologized to land when you took shortcuts across it; the land remembered, and hedge-witches lived by what the land remembered.

Behind her, Tam sneezed.

"That's the fourth one since Grayford," Odile said, not turning around. "If you're coming down with something, say so now, so I can walk upwind of you."

"I'm not sick." Tam's voice had gone thin and stubborn, the way it did whenever anyone suggested he might be cold, or hungry, or eleven years old. "It's the frost. It gets in your nose."

"Frost doesn't get in your nose."

"It does if the stars are gone."

Odile stopped walking. She turned. Tam stood in the ruined barley with his arms wrapped around himself, wearing a coat that had been cut down from an adult's and still hung off him like a tent, and he was looking north — not at the road, but past it, at the mountain that even from here made the horizon look like it had a bruise.

"It talks to you again?" she asked.

Tam's jaw worked. "It never stopped. It just got quieter while we were in the tollhouse. Like it didn't like the toll-keeper listening." He rubbed his nose. "It's louder out here. Open sky, I think. It likes open sky."

Xanthe caught up to them then, breath steaming, carrying all three bedrolls because he'd taken them without asking and Odile hadn't been fast enough to stop him. He had that look he got — the taster's look, cataloguing everything, filing it away somewhere behind his eyes where nobody could reach it.

"How much farther to the next village?" he asked.

"Thornbury, if it's still called that." Odile started walking again. "Two days, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. And given that winter came six weeks early and hasn't blinked since, I'd plan for three."

"And after Thornbury?"

"After Thornbury we leave the living roads." She said it lightly, the way you'd announce a change in the menu. "From there it's the thorn road the rest of the way."

Tam made a small sound. Xanthe said nothing at all, which from Xanthe was practically a scream.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen barn — fallen, Odile noted, in a way that had nothing to do with wind. The timbers were rotted through as though fifty years had passed over them in one season. Whatever had happened when the stars went out, it hadn't just taken the light. It had taken time's good manners too.

She got a fire going with a trick her grandmother had taught her, which involved no magic whatsoever, just knowing exactly which part of damp wood wants to burn if you flatter it correctly. Then she dug the last of the smoked fish out of her pack and divided it into three portions with the precision of a woman who had once fed nine siblings on less than this.

"So," Xanthe said, when they'd eaten. He sat with his knees drawn up, hands loose around them, watching the fire the way he watched everything — like it might be poisoned. "You've been putting off telling us. About the Undercourt."

"I haven't been *putting off*—"

"You changed the subject twice today. Once when I asked about the mountain, once when Tam asked why the villagers in that last hamlet had burned marks on their doorposts."

"They burned charms," Tam said. "I saw. Little bundles of thorns tied with red thread, nailed over the doors. And there was one house with a shoe nailed above the lintel, and Odile made a face at it."

"I made a face because it was done wrong," Odile said. "A left shoe. Everyone knows it has to be a right shoe or the dead just think you're waving at them." She sighed, and poked the fire, and admitted defeat the way she always did: gradually, then all at once. "Fine. Sit back. This is a long story and I'm only telling it once, so if either of you interrupts I'm starting over and adding an hour."

They sat back.

"The Undercourt," she began, "is older than the kingdom. Older than the wards, older than Vell, older than the idea of drawing lines on a map and calling it safety. When people first died — and I mean the very first ones, back when death was new and nobody knew the customs — they had to go *somewhere*. And the somewhere they went was down. Under the mountain in the north there's a cavern system that goes deeper than any mine, and at the bottom of it is a court. Candles instead of sun. Ledgers instead of laws. And a queen."

"The Queen Beneath the Mountain," Xanthe said quietly.

"You've heard of her."

"Everyone in Vell has heard whispers. The servants talk about her when they think the stewards aren't listening. She collects debts. That's all anyone says. She collects debts."

"She does." Odile pulled her cloak tighter. "Here's the thing about the living world, Xanthe. We have laws for everything. Who owes whom, what happens when a debt isn't paid, how much a man's labor is worth, what a widow gets when the lord takes the mill. We love our ledgers, the living. But there are debts the living law won't touch. Debts between the living and the dead. Promises made to things that don't have names. Souls traded before a child was born. The courts of men shrug at those. 'Not our jurisdiction,' they say, and go back to arguing about fence lines." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "The Undercourt says otherwise. The Undercourt says: a debt is a debt, and someone must collect it, and if the living won't do it honorably, we will do it *exactly*. To the letter. Forever."

"That sounds almost noble," Xanthe said.

"It would be, if it stopped there. But a court needs feeding, same as anything. Clerks need work. And a court that runs on collecting debts starts finding debts where none were signed. A villager who cuts a branch from a tree the dead liked. A child born on the wrong night. A witch—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—a witch who bargains carelessly. The Queen's clerks weigh souls like coins, and coins get spent."

Tam had gone very still. "The whispers call her the Pale Accountant," he offered, in the small voice he used for things the mountain told him. "Is that — is that a real title?"

"It's real enough." Odile stared into the fire. "And she's dying, if half of what I've heard is true. Dying things get desperate, and desperate queens with ledgers are the most dangerous creatures in the world. Which brings us to you, Xanthe Reyes, and why we're walking north in a winter that wants us dead."

* * *

The second day the frost climbed higher on the hedgerows, and the villages they passed grew meaner and more frightened. In one, a woman stood in her doorway and watched them pass with a bundle of burning thorns held out before her like a lantern, smoke streaming sideways in the wind.

"Burning charms against *us* specifically," Odile noted. "That's flattering. Usually it's general-purpose."

"Why thorns?" Tam asked. He'd been quieter all day, and kept glancing north, and kept pretending he wasn't.

"Because of the road we're taking. The old ward-roads north of Thornbury run through country the Undercourt claims, and the paths there grow blackthorn that doesn't belong to any season. The locals call it the thorn road. The thorn marks where the dead have walked — where their business leaks up through the ground like sap. Burn a charm of that thorn and the smell tells the clerks you know whose territory you're in. It's politeness, mostly. The shoe thing is politeness too. Half of hedge-craft is just good manners toward things that could kill you."

"And the other half?" Xanthe asked.

"Sleight-of-hand." She grinned at him. "Which reminds me. Before we hit the thorn road tomorrow, I'm teaching you both three tricks. One: never answer a question from below with a number. Two: if anything offers you food underground, you eat only what you brought. Three—" she tapped her own chest, "—if anyone asks what you're owed, and something in you wants to say *everything*, *justice*, *my mother back* — you bite your tongue until it bleeds. Down there, what you name is what you claim, and what you claim, you can be charged for."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. "What am I owed?" he finally asked, and it wasn't a question about the road. His voice had that raw edge it got when he thought about his mother, the mother whose face he couldn't remember, whose soul had been sold before he drew breath.

"Nothing," Odile said firmly. "You're owed nothing. That's the safest thing to be. Owed nothing, owning nothing, owing nothing. A blank page. The clerks hate blank pages."

"That sounds lonely."

"It sounds *alive*." She looked away, north, at the bruise-colored mountain. "Lonely keeps."

* * *

That night the whispers got loud enough that Tam screamed.

Odile woke with her knife already out — old reflexes — and found the boy sitting bolt upright against the barn wall, hands over his ears, tears freezing on his cheeks. Xanthe was already beside him, saying something low and steady that Odile couldn't catch, one hand hovering near Tam's shoulder the way you'd hover near a bird you didn't want to startle.

"It's counting," Tam gasped. "It's counting days. *Midwinter. Midwinter. Midwinter.* Like a bell that won't stop. And underneath it there's — there's singing, and the singing is sad, and I think it's her, I think it's the queen, and she sounds *scared*, and I don't want to hear a scared queen, I want to hear nothing, I want my head to be quiet—"

Odile crouched down in front of him. She took his hands off his ears gently, and held them, and did the thing her grandmother had done for her when the night got too full of voices: she hummed. Something tuneless and warm, a kitchen song, bread-rising rhythm. Tam's breathing slowed to match it.

"There," she said. "Listen to me instead. My humming's worse than any ghost. If you can stand this, you can stand anything."

A wet, hiccupping laugh. "It's really bad."

"I know. I've been told."

"He hears her being scared," Xanthe said quietly, over Tam's head. "Odile. A dying queen who stole the stars out of the sky because she's afraid. What does a court of exact debts do when its own queen breaks her own rules?"

Odile looked at the fire, and at the boy trembling under her hands, and at the man who'd been sold as a child and raised as a tool and was still — infuriatingly, stubbornly — kind.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I suspect we're going to find out. And I suspect it'll cost us something. It always costs something. That's the whole lesson of the thorn road, if you want one: everything down there is weighed, and nothing walks out unweighed." She squeezed Tam's fingers. "So we make sure what we carry is worth its weight. That's all any of us can do."

Beyond the fallen barn, the frost glittered under a sky with no stars in it at all, and far to the north — faint, green, patient — the mountain glowed like a candle seen through a keyhole.

Tam's whisper came small into the dark:

"It says we're expected."
