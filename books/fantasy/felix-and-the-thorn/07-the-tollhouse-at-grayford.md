## 7. The Tollhouse at Grayford

Tam had never been cold like this before. Cold in the kitchens was a thing you ran through — a dash to the ice house, a splash of well water on the wrists. This cold was a thing that followed you, patient as a debt collector, and sat down beside you when you stopped.

They stopped at dusk, because Odile said the road past Grayford was suicide in the dark, and because Xanthe's lips had gone the color of the frost on the fence posts, and because Tam had complained loudly enough that even he was embarrassed by it.

"There," Odile said, pointing with her chin. She pointed with her chin a lot. Tam suspected her hands were too busy for pointing, what with one holding a knife and the other holding the last of their bread.

The tollhouse crouched at the crossroads like an old toad: squat, stone, and ugly, with a single window glowing amber against the blue-black snow. A sign above its door read GRAYFORD TOLL — ALL TRAVELERS PAY — and beneath that, in smaller letters that someone had tried and failed to scrape away: *in kind.*

"Charming," said Xanthe.

"It's an old road," Odile said. "Old roads have old customs." She said it the way she said most things, which was as if she were daring the world to argue.

Tam looked at the little window glow and felt something twist in his chest that wasn't hunger for once. Warmth. Actual warmth, behind actual walls, with maybe a fire and definitely not another night of sleeping sitting up against a dead tree while Odile took first watch and Xanthe pretended he didn't shiver.

"I'll knock," Tam said, already moving.

"That's my line," Xanthe said. "I'm the one who tastes things for a living. Danger assessment is practically my trade."

"You taste soup."

"Poisoned soup," Xanthe said with dignity, "is still soup."

* * *

The toll-keeper was a woman made almost entirely of shawls. She had three of them at least, wrapped in layers, and beneath them a face so old and so smooth it looked less wrinkled than *erased*, like a page someone had written on and thought better of. She sat behind a desk in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, underneath both, something faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

"Well," she said. "Travelers." The word came out flat and pleased at once, the way you might say *mice* if you were a cat.

"Three beds," Odile said. "One meal. We pay fair."

"Oh, I'm sure you will." The toll-keeper unfolded herself from her chair with a sound like a deck of cards being shuffled. "Everyone pays fair here. That's the whole point of Grayford. It sits on the old ward-road, see, back when the valley kings wanted to know who came and went. And the crown couldn't post soldiers this far out — soldiers desert, soldiers sleep, soldiers want wages. So they posted me instead." She smiled. Her teeth were very white and very even, which somehow made everything worse. "I don't sleep. I don't desert. And my wage is paid by the travelers themselves."

Tam's stomach did something complicated. "What kind of toll?"

"Memories, dear." She said it the way a baker says *bread*. "Small ones preferred. The color of your grandmother's kitchen. The name of a boy you kissed once. A song you've forgotten you know until you hear it. Nothing you'd miss terribly." She tilted her erased face toward each of them in turn. "Though I confess, after a winter alone, I do enjoy a substantial payment now and then. A first love. A childhood summer. Something with *texture*."

The fire crackled. Outside, the wind found the gap under the door and played it like a bad flute.

"And if we don't pay?" Xanthe asked.

"Then you sleep in the snow, and the snow is honest company but a poor conversationalist." The toll-keeper settled back into her chair. "Supper's stew. There's bread. The beds are clean; I'm not a monster. Pay at dawn, before you go. That's the custom. Nobody travels Grayford owing anything — it's bad luck, and worse than bad luck further north." Her eyes flicked to Tam, just briefly, and Tam felt the whisper in the back of his skull stir like something rolling over in its sleep. "Especially for him."

"How do you—" Tam started.

"I collect memories, dear. I can tell when one of you is carrying luggage that isn't yours." She smiled again. "Stew's on the hob. Help yourselves."

* * *

The stew was good. That was the worst part. Tam had decided somewhere on the Thorn Road that anything offered by anyone with too many shawls and too-white teeth would be gruel at best and trickery at worst, but the stew was rich with barley and turnip and some dark meat he chose not to identify, and he ate two bowls and felt human for the first time in days.

Xanthe ate slowly, watching the toll-keeper over the rim of his bowl the way he watched everything — like a man cataloguing flavors. Tam had learned that about him on the road. Xanthe tasted the world. He'd told them once, around a fire, that he could taste lies in food — that a cook who hated the king salted his dishes differently than a cook who feared him, and Xanthe had spent ten years learning the difference without ever once being allowed to say it aloud.

"What are you going to pay?" Tam asked him, low, while Odile went to check the door bar for the third time. She checked doors the way other people breathed.

"I don't know yet." Xanthe turned his spoon over. "She said small ones. Things you wouldn't miss."

"Do you have any of those?"

Xanthe was quiet a moment. "Fewer than you'd think," he said, and smiled his kitchen smile, the one that meant *don't ask*, and Tam, who was learning, didn't ask.

Odile came back. "Bar's solid. Window's narrow. If she means to eat us in the night, we'll hear her coming."

"She doesn't mean to eat us," Tam said. "She means to *bill* us. Worse."

"Speak for yourself," Odile said. "I've been eaten by worse."

* * *

Tam woke once in the night, in a bed softer than any bed he'd ever owned, which was no beds, and heard voices from the main room. He should have gone back to sleep. Instead he crept to the top of the stairs, because Tam had never in his life met a staircase he didn't eavesdrop from, and listened.

"—and how long has the debt ridden you?" The toll-keeper's voice, smooth as poured cream.

A pause. Then Xanthe: "Since before I was born, apparently. I only found out last week."

"Mm. And the mountain's mark on the young one — that's recent. Since the stars went."

"Yes."

"And you, witch. You carry grief like a coin sewn into your hem. Old grief. Undercourt-shaped."

Another pause, longer. When Odile spoke, her voice was stripped of all its edges. "My sister's. What's left of it."

"Ah," said the toll-keeper, and there was something in the *ah* that wasn't cruelty. Almost tenderness. "Well. You're all owing, one way or another. Everyone who comes up this road is. That's why the road exists." A log shifted in the fire. "Dawn comes early. Think carefully about what you can spare. Memory's the only coin that spends itself — you pay it, and you never feel poorer, because you can't remember what you had. Most folk call that mercy."

"Some folk would call it theft," Odile said.

"Some folk," the toll-keeper agreed pleasantly, "would call a great many things theft if they could remember why they minded."

Tam crept back to bed and lay staring at the ceiling beams, and the whisper in his skull murmured something soft and green and patient, and he pressed his hands over his ears until morning.

* * *

At dawn the toll-keeper set three small wooden bowls on her desk, the kind used for measuring flour, and stood behind them with her hands folded like a woman about to say grace.

"One memory each," she said. "Into the bowl. You won't feel a thing. Well — you'll feel a little tug, like a hair pulled from your head. After that, nothing. The memory is mine, the road is yours, and everyone leaves Grayford square."

Tam went first, because he always went first at things like this, because waiting was worse. He cast about in his head for something small. The color of the scullery tiles — grey, boring, safe. The name of the dog he'd fed scraps to behind the bakery when he was seven. He reached for the dog —

— and found, with a lurch, that he couldn't quite remember its name. Or its face. Or whether it had been brown or yellow or just *dog-colored*. He remembered feeding it. He remembered loving it. But the shape of it had gone soft in his head, like a word on the tip of the tongue that never arrives.

He picked something else instead — the taste of burnt porridge from his first week in the kitchens, which was a terrible memory anyway — and leaned over the bowl. The toll-keeper touched his temple with one finger, cool as a key, and there was a little tug, like a hair pulled from his head, and then

then nothing. He blinked. The bowl sat empty. Somewhere in him was a small smooth hole where the porridge had been, and he couldn't even be sad about it, because he couldn't remember what he was supposed to be missing.

"Huh," he said. "That's — huh."

"Yes," said the toll-keeper. "Everyone says that."

Xanthe stepped up next. He stood over the bowl a long moment, hands loose at his sides, and Tam watched him reach inward the way you'd reach into a pocket you were afraid was empty.

"I'll pay the memory of my mother's face," Xanthe said.

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Dear," the toll-keeper said gently, "that's not a small one. That's the opposite of a small one."

"It's the only one I have left that matters," Xanthe said. "And I'd rather give it away than lose it to them." His jaw was set. Tam knew that jaw. That was the jaw Xanthe wore whenever the mountain came up, whenever midwinter came up, whenever the word *debt* came up. "Take it. Please."

The toll-keeper studied him for a moment with her erased face. Then she nodded, slow, and raised her hand—

—and stopped.

Her fingers hovered an inch from Xanthe's temple. Her head tilted. The way a taster tilts his head at a suspicious cup, Tam thought wildly. The way *Xanthe* tilts his head.

"Oh," the toll-keeper said softly. "Oh, you poor lamb."

"What?" Xanthe's voice cracked on it. "What's wrong?"

"She's not there, dear." The toll-keeper lowered her hand. "Your mother's face. It's already gone. Has been for years, I'd guess — taken in installments, quietly, the way the big debts feed. Every year on your birthday, I'd wager. A little here, a little there. The curve of her cheek first, probably. Then her voice. Then her laugh. Whatever's left is—" she made a small, apologetic gesture— "a shape with the middle cut out. I can't take what isn't there. It would be like billing a man for coins he's already been robbed of."

Xanthe didn't move. Tam watched him stand perfectly still, perfectly straight, the way he'd stood in the kitchens, the way he stood whenever the world hit him and he refused to let it see him stagger.

"I remember her," he said. "I remember — I remember that she hummed. While she worked. I remember that."

"You remember that she hummed," the toll-keeper agreed. "But not the tune?"

Silence.

"Not her hands? Not the color of her dress? Not—" 

"Stop," Xanthe said, very quietly, and the toll-keeper, to her credit, stopped.

Tam's chest hurt. He thought about the dog whose name he'd lost without noticing, and multiplied it, and multiplied it again, year after year after year, a birthday tax on the only person Xanthe had ever loved, collected by a court he'd never seen, for a bargain he'd never signed. Grief with no face to put it on. Just a humming with no tune. Just a hole shaped exactly like a mother.

"They've been eating it," Xanthe said. Not a question. "All this time. To make sure I arrive with nothing to bargain *with*. Nothing they owe me a claim on."

"Or simply because the debt feeds itself," the toll-keeper said. "Debts are hungry things, dear. They don't care who does the chewing." She drew her shawls tighter. "You'll pay me something else. Small. Go on."

Xanthe stared at the bowl. Then, in a voice carefully sanded flat, he said, "The smell of the palace kitchens on baking day. Cinnamon and browned butter. Take that."

The tug. The little pull behind the eye. And then Xanthe blinked and frowned faintly at the bowl and said, "Was I saying something?" and Tam had to look away, because that — that was worse somehow, watching the hole open and close and leave no scar.

* * *

Which left Odile.

Odile had watched the whole thing from the doorway with her arms crossed and her face doing absolutely nothing, which Tam had learned was how she looked when she was feeling the most. Now she stepped up to the desk and set both palms flat on the wood.

"My turn," she said. "And I'll tell you what I'm paying before I pay it, because I'm honest that way. I'm paying the memory of the smell of my sister's hair. Lavender and woodsmoke. That's the toll. Fair and square."

"A fine memory," the toll-keeper said, reaching out.

Except Odile's hand got there first.

It happened fast — faster than Tam could properly follow, and he'd watched her do card tricks for bored innkeeps on the road. One moment her right hand was flat on the desk; the next it had dipped into her sleeve, come out with something small and pale, and dropped it into the measuring bowl, all in the time it took the toll-keeper to blink. The toll-keeper's finger touched Odile's temple. The tug. The pull.

And then the toll-keeper looked down into the bowl, and her erased face did something Tam had never seen a face do. It *frowned*.

"This," she said slowly, lifting out the object between two fingers, "is a button."

"It's a lovely button," Odile said.

"It's a *wooden button*."

"From my sister's coat. Sentimental value beyond price."

"The toll is *memories*, you thieving little hedge—"

"And you got one!" Odile spread her hands, all injured innocence. "The memory of receiving a button. You have it now. You'll remember this morning forever — the strange travelers, the snow, the woman who gave you a button. That's a memory. Check your ledger. I bet it's in there."

The toll-keeper opened her mouth. Closed it. Tam could actually see her checking — see the small involuntary flicker of her eyes as she searched her own head and found, yes, a brand-new memory sitting there, bright and fresh and completely useless, of a witch handing her a button.

"That is not," the toll-keeper said, with the dignity of a queen addressing a rebellion, "how customs work."

"Isn't it?" Odile leaned in. "You said nobody travels Grayford owing anything. Do we owe you anything?"

A long pause. The wind worried the door. Somewhere in the walls, the house ticked and settled.

"...No," the toll-keeper admitted, sounding like the words cost her a tooth.

"Then we're square." Odile scooped the button back out of the bowl, blew on it, and tucked it away in her sleeve with the tenderness other people reserved for relics of saints. "Pleasure doing business. The stew was excellent. Tell your friends."

For a moment Tam genuinely believed the toll-keeper might turn them into something. Her hands flexed on the desk. Her too-white teeth showed. But then her gaze slid — past Odile, past Tam — to Xanthe, standing by the fire with his new smooth little hole where cinnamon used to be, staring at nothing, grieving a face he'd never been allowed to keep.

Something in the old woman's shoulders came down half an inch.

"Get out of my tollhouse," she said, not unkindly. "And boy." Her voice caught Xanthe like a hook. "When you get where you're going — and you're going *there*, all three of you, I can smell the mountain's road on your boots — ask to see the ledgers. All of them. The Undercourt keeps records of every installment it takes. Debts are hungry, but they're also *proud*. They write down everything they eat." Her erased face almost softened. "Whatever they took from you, dear, it's written down somewhere. And what's written down can be read back."

Xanthe turned. For the first time since the stars had died, Tam saw something in his face that wasn't fear or grief or that awful careful blankness.

It was hope, sharp as a fishhook.

"They kept receipts," Xanthe said.

"The Undercourt," said the toll-keeper, "has never once thrown anything away. Now go, before I change my mind about the button."

* * *

They walked out into a morning so bright and frozen it hurt to breathe. Behind them the tollhouse sat in the snow like a toad that had decided, on balance, to let them live.

"So," Tam said, after a mile. "We're going to break into hell and demand a refund."

"We're going to *read the contract*," Xanthe corrected. "In front of everyone. Out loud."

"That's much worse," Odile said. "I love it."

Ahead, the road climbed north through the frost-killed fields, and far off, where the land rose into mountains, the sky above the highest peak had a faint green tint to it, like a bruise healing wrong. The whisper in Tam's skull stirred and said his name, soft and patient, the way you'd say the name of something you were keeping warm for later.

Tam pulled his collar up and walked faster, and told himself the shiver was only the cold.
