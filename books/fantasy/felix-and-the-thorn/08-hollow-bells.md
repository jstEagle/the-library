## 8. Hollow Bells

Odile had been thrown out of better cities than Hollow Bells, but never one that mourned so beautifully.

They came over the last ridge at dusk, three travelers crusted in road-frost, and the city opened below them like a casket lined with candlelight. Every window burned amber. Every bridge was strung with lanterns shaped like teardrops. And everywhere—on the walls, on the rooftops, spilling through the gates in slow rivers of grey silk—the mourners.

"Is someone dead?" Tam asked.

"Everyone," Odile said. "Eventually. That's rather the point."

The Festival of Small Sorrows. She'd read about it years ago, in a smuggled book of northern customs she'd traded a love-charm to obtain. Once a year, the people of Hollow Bells donned masks and grieved publicly for whatever they'd lost—a parent, a sweetheart, a harvest, a dog—and because everyone wore masks, no one had to be ashamed of their grief. For one night, the whole city wept in company.

It was also, she thought grimly, the single best cover in the north for three runaways with a bounty on their heads. Which meant it would be crawling with the king's riders, who knew exactly how good a cover it was.

"We could go around," Xanthe said. He stood beside her at the ridge's edge, thin as a winter branch, watching the lanterns with an expression she couldn't read. "Circle east. Cross the Sere higher up."

"And freeze to death in the high passes before we ever see the mountain? No." Odile pulled her hood lower. "We go through. Grief is the one thing in this kingdom nobody questions. You can walk into any house in Hollow Bells tonight if you look sad enough."

"I'm very good at sad," Tam offered.

"You're very good at hungry," Odile said. "Which tonight is nearly the same thing."

* * *

Getting the masks was the easy part. A widow selling them from a barrow by the gate took Odile's last two coppers and pressed three into her hands without counting—one of white birch-bark with painted tears, one of plain grey felt, and one, for reasons the widow declined to explain, shaped like a fox.

"The fox is mine," Tam said immediately.

"You'll frighten the other mourners."

"Good. Keeps them at a distance."

Xanthe took the grey felt and held it a moment too long, turning it over, as though he expected it to bite. Odile watched him. In the week since Grayford she'd learned his silences the way you learn weather—there were tired silences and frightened ones and thinking ones, and this was a thinking one, which worried her more than either of the others.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing." He tied the mask on. Behind the blank grey, only his mouth showed, and even that went carefully neutral. "I used to know this city."

"You said you'd never left Vell."

"I hadn't. But the king's men came through twice a year. Tasters were shown off at court functions like—" he paused, choosing the word with a taster's precision, "—like spoons. Good silver. And there was a man who brought me here once, when I was eleven. Master Corvane. The king's spymaster." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He fed me dinner. Honey-glazed quail. He told me I had the finest palate in the valley and that if I worked hard, I might matter someday."

Odile felt the cold settle into her bones that had nothing to do with the season. "And this Corvane. Is he the sort of man who forgets a face?"

"He remembered my mother's name," Xanthe said quietly, "and she died before I could talk. So. No."

* * *

Inside the walls, Hollow Bells was drowning in its own tenderness.

They moved with the crowd down the Processional Way, and Odile kept her eyes moving—doorways, rooftops, the dark mouths of alleys—while around her the city performed its sorrow. A fat merchant wept copiously behind a mask of gilded tin, mourning, by the ribbon on his arm, a lost ship. Two young women in matching black walked arm in arm, each wearing half of a broken heart painted across their masks. A child no older than Tam dragged a small wooden coffin on a cart, and when Odile glanced inside she saw it held nothing but a collar and a folded blanket.

A bell tolled somewhere above them—deep, cracked, wrong-sounding—and the whole street sighed at once, a thousand breaths drawn and released together.

"That's where the name comes from," Tam said. He'd gone quiet under the fox mask; Odile could hear it in his voice. "The bells. Can't you hear it? They're not right. One of them's hollow. It rings and there's nothing inside it."

"There's nothing inside most bells," Odile said. "That's what makes them ring."

"No," Tam said, and stopped walking. "This one's different. It's like—" He pressed a mittened hand against his ear. "Like the mountain. Like the whispers, but sadder. It keeps saying *come home, come home,* but not to us. To somebody else."

Odile exchanged a look with Xanthe over the boy's head. Since the shrine, since the voice had screamed through Tam and marked Xanthe with whatever a mountain uses instead of a brand, they'd kept watch on him in turns—not because they distrusted him, but because the whispers didn't need his permission to use him. Tam hated being watched. He bore it anyway, which Odile privately considered braver than anything she'd done at his age, and she'd done some genuinely stupid things at his age.

"It's the festival," she said gently. "Old bells, old grief. Come on. We need food and a wall to sleep against, and I know where to find both."

She did, actually. That was the thing about being a hedge-witch: every city has a quarter where the desperate trade remedies for coppers, and every such quarter has a back room where a woman with a scarred reputation can buy soup, gossip, and six hours of floor. Odile led them off the Processional into the tanner's lanes, where the mourners thinned and the smells thickened, to a doorway she'd have bet her last charm still belonged to Mother Hessel.

It did. Mother Hessel had gotten older, wider, and considerably less pleased to see her.

"Odile Thornharrow," the old woman said, blocking her own doorway with a body built like a rain barrel. "I heard you were hanged."

"You heard wrong. Or possibly right, depending on which king's men you ask."

"I heard you were hanged for witchcraft in Vell, and I lit a candle, and now here you are standing on my step with two children in funeral masks, and I want you to know the candle was wasted."

"One bowl each," Odile said. "And a corner of your floor till dawn. And in exchange I'll tell you why the stars went out, which is worth more than the candle."

Mother Hessel's eyes narrowed above her own mask—black velvet, studded with jet, the mask of a woman professionally prepared for bad news. Then she stepped aside. "Two bowls. The children sleep by the stove. You sleep in the yard."

"Done," said Odile, who had slept in far worse yards.

* * *

The soup was barley and bone and honest, and Tam ate his like a man rescuing drowning kittens, one after another, faster than seemed anatomically possible. Xanthe ate slowly, tasting everything, because he was incapable of doing otherwise—Odile had watched him do it with road rations, with well water, once with snow, cataloguing flavors the way other men counted coins. Tonight, halfway through the bowl, he went still.

"What is it?" she asked quietly.

"Clove," he said. "And something under it. Burnt rosemary." He looked up, and even through the eye-holes of the grey mask she saw the color leave his face. "Mother Hessel cooks for the palace kitchens sometimes. She told me that herself, just now, making conversation while I waited. Which means someone from the palace has been in this room within the day."

Odile set down her spoon. Across the kitchen, Mother Hessel was ladling soup for a pair of latecomers, her back turned, humming.

"Riders?" Odile murmured.

"Worse. Riders ride past. This is—" He breathed in again, slow, professional, and Odile realized with a lurch that he was tasting the air the way he'd taste a supper. "Wax. Sealing wax, the good kind, red, with juniper oil. That's the spymaster's seal. I sealed letters with it for him, that summer. My hands smelled of it for a month."

"Your hands smell things?"

"My hands remember everything," Xanthe said, with the first flash of temper she'd ever seen from him. "That's what a tongue is for. Everything I ever tasted in that valley lives in me like a ledger, and right now this room is telling me that the man who fed me quail sat in that chair"—he pointed his spoon at a stool by the fire—"within the last day, asking questions."

Tam, mid-kitten-rescue, lowered his bowl. "Questions about what?"

There was a silence with three people in it and none of them wanted to live there.

"About us," Odile said.

* * *

They left by the yard, as agreed, though not to sleep in it. Odile paid Mother Hessel's suspicion the highest compliment available: she lied to her. Told her they were pilgrims bound for the coast, told her the masks were penance, watched the old woman nod along with the particular satisfaction of someone who intends to sell your description to the first rider she meets. It couldn't be helped. Sentiment was a luxury for people whose faces weren't on warrants.

Back into the festival, then, because the festival was still the safest place in Hollow Bells—a river of masked mourners in which three more masked mourners were drops of water. Odile kept them moving, kept them close, kept count of the lanterns and the alley-mouths and, increasingly, of the men who weren't grieving.

You learn to spot them. Real mourners weep toward each other; hunters weep toward the crowd. There was one by the fountain with a tear-streaked mask and dry shoulders in the rain. Another on the church steps whose boots were cavalry boots, however fine his mourning silks. And near the great bell tower, where the cracked bell tolled its hollow note over the square—

Near the bell tower stood a man in unrelieved black, wearing no mask at all.

"Oh no," Odile breathed.

He was older than she'd pictured—she'd pictured nothing, actually, she'd only had Xanthe's memory of honey-glazed quail and a boy's hero-worship to go on—but she knew him instantly all the same. He had the stillness of a man who has spent his life being mistaken for furniture. Grey at the temples, neat as a ledger-line, and standing in the middle of a festival of masks with his bare face lifted to the bell like a man listening to a letter read aloud.

Corvane. The king's spymaster. Hunting them, or worse—already having found them.

"We go left," Odile whispered. "Down the chandler's lane. Now, quietly, don't run, running is a confession—"

But Xanthe had stopped.

Of course he had. Of course. Because the man in black had turned, and his gaze had crossed the crowd the way a hand crosses a shelf of spoons, feeling for the good silver, and it had snagged—Odile saw it happen, saw the stillness sharpen into attention—on a thin figure in a plain grey felt mask.

"*No,*" she hissed, and grabbed Xanthe's sleeve, but he was already moving—not away. Toward. Walking straight into the lion's eye with the calm of a man who has decided, catastrophically, that there is something he needs to know.

Odile did the only sensible thing, which was to shove Tam behind a bread stall and follow.

* * *

The crowd carried them together, the way crowds do, until there were only six feet of festival between the spymaster and the runaway he'd fed dinner to eleven years ago. Corvane hadn't moved. He was looking directly at Xanthe now, and his face did something complicated—recognition arriving in stages, like frost climbing a window.

"Grey felt," Corvane said. His voice was exactly as Xanthe's stories must have built it: quiet, dry, kind in the way that knives are kind to whetstones. "Plain. Unadorned. Everyone else in this city is grieving loudly enough to wake the dead, and you're grieving like a man who means it." He tilted his head. "I've seen grief like yours before. On a boy who tasted my supper and told me the salt was from the eastern shore, and the cook was robbing the king by a pennyweight."

Around them the festival flowed on, oblivious, weeping its beautiful public tears. Odile slid half a step closer, hand finding the little knife in her sleeve, doing arithmetic she didn't like: one spymaster, presumably armed; two guards she couldn't see but could feel, the way you feel weather in a scar; one narrow lane behind them full of chandler's smoke; one frozen river beyond the walls; and between all of it, a boy in a grey mask who hadn't run.

"You're mistaken," Xanthe said.

"Almost certainly," Corvane agreed. "Mistakes are my profession. But indulge me. Take off the mask."

"Don't," Odile said softly, at his shoulder, for his ears alone. "Xanthe. Don't."

And here was the thing Odile would carry with her afterward, the thing she'd turn over on cold nights for the rest of her life: he hesitated. Not long. A heartbeat, maybe two. But she saw it—the pull of it, the old gravity, the man who had fed him quail and called him fine silver, the kingdom that had raised him in a poison-proofed valley and taught him that usefulness was the same as love. Eleven days ago Xanthe Reyes would have taken off that mask for Master Corvane before the man finished asking, and thanked him for the opportunity.

Eleven days of road, and a tollhouse, and a boy who heard mountains, and a woman who'd failed her sister twice—that was what stood in the balance of those two heartbeats.

Xanthe reached up. Odile's grip tightened on the knife.

And he adjusted the mask. Straightened it, slightly crooked, the way a nervous servant fidgets—nothing more. "Forgive me," he said, muffled through the felt, and pitched his voice deliberately wrong, younger, commoner-rough. "My lord. I'm grieving my father. I'd rather not show my face. It does ugly things when I cry."

Something passed over Corvane's face—disappointment, or relief, or the strange third thing that lives in the house where those two share a bed. He studied the boy a moment longer. Then he bowed, shallow and correct.

"Then keep your grief private," he said. "It's worth more that way. Most things are." He turned—and paused, half-turned, not quite looking back. "The quail was good, wasn't it? I've often wondered if anyone remembers."

"The quail was good," Xanthe said, in his own voice, just barely, just for him.

Corvane walked away into the festival, unhurried, a man crossing off an errand. The two shadows Odile had felt detached from doorways and followed him, and the crowd closed over all three like water.

* * *

They ran then. Properly ran, confession and all, down the chandler's lane and through the tanneries and out a postern gate whose lock surrendered to Odile's second-best charm, and didn't stop until the city was a smear of candlelight behind them and the frozen fields ahead were black under a sky with no stars and no moon and no mercy.

Tam collapsed first, wheezing, fox mask askew. Odile put her hands on her knees and breathed like a bellows and tried to locate her heartbeat, which appeared to have relocated to her ears.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said to Xanthe, without heat, because the heat had all been spent on the running. "You walked *toward* him. Toward! I have smuggled poisons past royal inspectors with steadier nerves than—"

"He let us go."

That stopped her. Xanthe stood in the frozen field with his mask pushed up on his forehead, and his face in the starless dark was wet, though whether from running or otherwise she couldn't say and didn't ask.

"He knew it was me," Xanthe said. "From the first word. Maybe from across the square. And he asked me to take off the mask knowing I'd refuse, and then he—he bowed, Odile. To a runaway. And walked away." He laughed, a small broken sound. "Why would he do that?"

Odile looked back at Hollow Bells, at its ten thousand candles grieving in the dark, and thought about a man who stood maskless among masked mourners, and about kings who paid debts with children, and about the particular loneliness of being the one person in a kingdom who knows where all the bodies are buried.

"Because not everyone who serves a monster is one," she said finally. "Come on. The river's two days north, and I promise you the riders will be less sentimental at dawn."

They walked. And behind them, faint across the frozen fields, the hollow bell of Hollow Bells tolled once more—long, cracked, empty—and Tam, without breaking stride, said quietly:

"It stopped saying *come home.*"

He wiped his nose. "Now it just says *run.*"
