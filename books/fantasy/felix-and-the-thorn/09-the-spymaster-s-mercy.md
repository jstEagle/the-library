## 9. The Spymaster's Mercy

The city of Hollow Bells had a hundred bridges, and Xanthe had managed to be cornered on the worst of them.

It was called the Weeper's Span, a narrow arch of wet black stone that crossed the canal behind the Mourner's Quarter, and it was the worst for three reasons. First, it was narrow enough that a grown man could bar it with his elbows. Second, its stones were slick with festival grease and old tears, because mourners came here to drop their grief-charms into the water, and grief-charms were mostly tallow. Third—and this was the reason that mattered—it was occupied.

The man standing at the far end wore a plain gray coat, unadorned, the kind of coat worn by men who wanted you to believe they had no rank at all. He held a paper lantern in one hand, though the festival lights behind him made it nearly useless. His face was half in shadow, but Xanthe would have known him anywhere, in any light, from any distance. There was only one man in the world who had ever cut his meat for him at supper.

"Master Corvane," Xanthe said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"Xanthe." Master Corvane's voice was exactly as Xanthe remembered it: dry, warm at the edges, like bread left near a fire. "You've grown into your shoulders."

Behind Xanthe, Odile went very still. He could feel her stillness the way you feel weather changing—a pressure against his back, a held breath. Tam he couldn't feel at all; the boy had gone ghost-quiet, which was either very wise or very bad.

There was nowhere to go. The canal on either side ran fast and black with snowmelt, and Xanthe could not swim. Odile probably could—she'd grown up on rivers, she'd said once, before catching herself—but the water would take Tam like a leaf. And even if they ran back the way they'd come, the Mourner's Quarter was full of the king's riders tonight, going door to door through the festival, lifting masks with polite gloved hands.

So Xanthe did the only thing left to him. He walked forward.

Odile's fingers caught his sleeve—"what are you *doing*" whispered sharp against his ear—and he squeezed her hand once and let go, and kept walking, out onto the middle of the bridge where the lantern-light and the festival-light met and canceled each other out, leaving him in a small island of honest dark.

Master Corvane watched him come. He didn't reach for a weapon. Master Corvane never reached for weapons; that was what other people were for. He simply set the paper lantern down on the bridge rail, where its little flame bent and shivered in the wind off the water, and folded his hands.

"You're wearing a mourner's mask," Xanthe said, when he got close enough. It was true: a white half-mask with a single painted tear, pushed up on Corvane's forehead like a pair of spectacles. "That's cheating. You're not mourning anyone."

"I'm mourning several people," Corvane said. "None of them are dead yet. That's the trouble with my profession—you do your grieving in advance." He looked Xanthe over, top to toe, with the unhurried attention of a man reading a ledger. "You're thin. Are they feeding you?"

"We feed ourselves," Xanthe said. "Badly."

"Hm." Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not quite a smile, not quite anything else. "You always catalogued flavors too honestly for court work. I told them so, years ago. 'The boy will tell you the truth about the soup,' I said. 'He's useless for flattery.' Nobody listened. Nobody ever listens to me until it's too late, which is why I'm still alive."

"You've come to arrest me."

"I've come," Corvane said, "to stand on a bridge in the cold, holding a lantern, talking to a boy who used to steal candied ginger from the high pantry." He paused. "Did you know I knew? About the ginger?"

Xanthe felt heat climb his neck. "I paid it back. Mostly."

"You paid it back *exactly*, which is worse. A thief steals. An accountant steals and then can't sleep." Corvane turned his head and looked out over the canal, where the grief-charms bobbed and guttered, hundreds of tiny flames riding the dark water toward the sea. "Do you know what those are, Xanthe? The charms?"

"Mourners' griefs. They burn them loose and let the river carry them off."

"That's the story the priests tell. The truth is more interesting and less comforting: most of those flames are lies. People buy their griefs pre-made, at the festival stalls. Little wax sorrows, mass-produced. It's easier than feeling the real thing." He glanced back at Xanthe. "I mention it because you strike me as a boy who has been carrying around someone else's version of events, and I thought an example might help."

"The king sent you."

"The king sent riders. I asked to come myself." Corvane's voice didn't change at all, and somehow that was the frightening part. "They think you'll run from soldiers. They were right—the last two parties you met, you ran beautifully. Very proud of you. But you won't run from me, because I fed you dinner for eleven years, and that is a debt neither of us knows how to default on."

* * *

The wind came up the canal and set all the little grief-flames dancing. Somewhere behind Xanthe, Odile shifted her weight, and he heard the soft particular sound of her hand moving inside her coat, toward whatever she kept there. He didn't turn around. Turning around would make it real.

"Then don't ask me to come back," Xanthe said. "Because I won't. Whatever he told you—I heard him, Master Corvane. In his own study, with his own mouth. He means to hand me to the mountain at midwinter. My mother sold my soul before I was born, and the king intends to pay her debt with me, and every rule I ever lived by in Vell—the wards, the valley, the whole safe little world—they were just the walls of the pen they raised me in."

"Yes," Corvane said.

Xanthe blinked. Of all the answers he had rehearsed for, all the denials and threats and cold official language, he had not rehearsed *yes*.

"You're not going to argue with me?"

"What would be the use? You heard it correctly. I was in the next room when he told you, if you want the full inventory of my failures." Corvane picked up the lantern again, absently, the way another man might pick up a cup. "Though you have the shape of it wrong. Everyone does. Even the king has the shape of it wrong, and it is *his* plan, which tells you everything about the quality of counsel he keeps these days."

"Then tell me the right shape."

Corvane was quiet for a moment. On the water below, a grief-charm snagged on a stone, spun twice, and came free. "Do you remember the winter you were nine? When you burned your hand on the oven-rail, and the king himself came down to the kitchens—not a messenger, the king—and sat with you while the salve was applied, and told you about the time he'd burned *his* hand learning to ride?"

"I remember." Xanthe's voice had gone flat. "I thought about it every day for years. I thought it meant something."

"It did mean something. That's the horror of it. It wasn't performance—he's not capable of performing kindness, he hasn't the imagination. He genuinely sat with you because he liked you, and genuinely told you a story because he pitied you, and then genuinely signed the order that will deliver you to the Undercourt, and none of these things contradict each other in his mind. That's what power does to men, Xanthe. It doesn't make them cruel. It makes them *sincere*." He shook his head slowly. "I have served that sincerity for thirty years, and I am tired down to the marrow of watching it eat people who trusted it."

"Then why are you here? If you're not arresting me—"

"I'm here because the plan changed." For the first time, Corvane's composure slipped—not much, just a hairline crack, a flicker at the corner of his mouth. "Three days ago the order was simple: retrieve the taster, deliver him north at midwinter. Clean. Terrible, but clean. And then the king's new counselor arrived from the capital with a document I was not supposed to see, and now the order is different, and I have spent two nights deciding whether to tell you, because telling you makes me a traitor, and *not* telling you makes me complicit, and I find at my age that I would rather be a traitor than a complicit man. Complicit men die comfortable. Traitors at least get to choose their regrets."

The words dropped into Xanthe's chest like stones into a well. He heard them fall and fall without reaching bottom.

"What document?"

"The Binding Charter." Corvane said it the way a physician says the name of a disease. "An old thing. Older than the wards, older than the valley. Do you know what the throne actually is, Xanthe? Not the chair—the *throne*. The thing beneath the chair, in the foundation stone of Vell. It's a vessel. It was built to hold a soul. One soul, bound into the stone, feeding the wards, keeping the magic out, keeping the valley pure and safe and dead. Every ward in that valley is downstream of a soul in a box."

The canal seemed suddenly very loud. "Whose soul is in it now?"

"No one's. It's been empty for sixty years. That's why the wards needed renewing every season—that's why the tasters existed at all, why the poison-proofing, why any of it. The empty throne has been running on ritual and habit, a lamp burning the memory of oil." Corvane's eyes came up and fixed on Xanthe's, and they were not unkind, which was the worst thing about them. "And now the wards have fallen, and the king's counselors have remembered what the throne was built for. And they have found, in their archives, a boy whose soul is already half-promised to the mountain. Already marked. Already *paid for*. Do you understand? You wouldn't even be wasted. The debt to the Undercourt could be renegotiated—the mountain gets its due eventually, they always do—but in the meantime, the crown gets sixty years of wards out of you. Sixty years of a safe, pure, magic-free valley, purchased with one taster's tongue."

"They'd bind me into the stone," Xanthe said. His own voice sounded far away. "Alive."

"Conscious, the archives say. Aware. Forever." Corvane set the lantern down again, gently, as if his hands had become unreliable. "The Undercourt at least has the decency to kill you first. The king's mercy is that you'd live. That's the difference between the two monsters, Xanthe, and I need you to hear it clearly, because everyone south of this canal keeps telling you the mountain is the danger. The mountain is a creditor. Creditors can be reasoned with—debts have edges, debts end. What the king wants has no edges. It's not a debt. It's a *wall*, and you'd be the mortar."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below the bridge the grief-charms went on floating past, all those little purchased sorrows, and Xanthe thought about how he had spent eleven years believing he understood the shape of his own life. Taster. Servant. Kept as long as useful. He had thought that was the worst truth available, and he had built a whole quiet, competent, lonely existence on top of it, the way you build a house on ground you haven't checked.

Now the ground was gone, and underneath it was more ground, and underneath *that* was a stone box with his name on it.

"Why tell me?" he asked finally. "Really. Not the traitor-versus-complicit speech. Why walk onto this bridge yourself, in a mourner's mask, and hand me the one piece of news that guarantees I'll never stop running?"

Corvane was silent long enough that the lantern flame between them gutters and recovered twice.

"Because twenty-two years ago," he said, "a woman came to me in Vell, before you were born. A clever woman, poor, pregnant, frightened. She had heard rumors about what happened to children sold to the crown, and she asked me—me, a young spymaster trying to make his name—whether the stories were true. And I lied to her. I gave her the official answer, the reassuring one, because the truth would have cost the crown a sale, and I was ambitious, and she was nobody." His jaw tightened. "She sold herself anyway, in the end. Sold a year of life for her child's warmth, and the child after that, and every piece of it traced back through a chain of small respectable decisions, and the first link in that chain was a lie told by a young man on a winter night who wanted to advance in his career."

Xanthe's breath had stopped somewhere. He made it start again.

"You're saying my mother talked to *you*."

"I'm saying I have owed her a truth for twenty-two years, and she is beyond collecting, and you are not." Corvane straightened, and for a moment the gray coat and the mourner's mask and the whole careful weightlessness of him fell away, and underneath was just an old tired man on a cold bridge. "This is not mercy, Xanthe. Don't mistake it for that. Mercy implies I have power over you and am choosing gentleness. This is arithmetic. A debt read aloud. I'm paying what I owe, late, to the wrong person, at ruinous interest—and I'd thank you not to forgive me, because forgiveness would let me off too easy."

"I don't forgive you," Xanthe said. It surprised him how easily it came. "But I believe you."

"Good. Belief is worth more." Corvane nodded once, briskly, business resumed. "Now listen, because I won't say this part twice. The riders sweep the Mourner's Quarter until dawn, then pull back to the gates. Your witch friend—the cellar smuggler, yes, I know about her too, I know about everything, it's my one skill and it has brought me nothing but sorrow—she should take you east along the canal road, not north through the pass. The pass has watchers. The canal road has tollhouses, and tollhouses have *tolls*, and I know what kind, so bring something to trade that you can bear to lose. Not memories you love. Memories you merely *like*. There's a difference, and you'll learn it."

"How do you know all this?" Xanthe asked. "The tolls, the roads—"

"Because I've read every report that ever came out of the north, for thirty years, hoping someday one of them would matter." Corvane smiled then, briefly, and it was a real smile, crooked and tired. "Someday is today. How's that for a career."

"And after Grayford? After the river? What's waiting under the mountain that I should know about?"

"Nothing I can tell you. That's the honest limit of my trade—I know what men report, and no living man has reported back from the Undercourt." He paused. "Except one thing. One rumor, secondhand, from a ferryman's widow, drunk, forty years ago. She said the court beneath the mountain honors its debts above all things—above mercy, above sense, above its own survival. She said if you ever stood before its queen, don't beg and don't bargain. *Read.* Find the paper. Read it aloud, word for word, and let the court's own law do the fighting." He shrugged. "Drunk widow. Forty years. Take it for what it cost you."

"Nothing," Xanthe said. "It cost me nothing."

"Then it may be the best advice you ever get." Corvane picked up the lantern and turned it in his hand, watching the flame lean. "The festival ends at dawn. After that, the masks come off, and I can't protect what I don't officially see. So." He looked up, and held out the lantern to Xanthe—an odd gesture, almost ceremonial, a man handing over a small fire. "Run faster."

Xanthe took it. The paper was warm against his palms.

"That's it? No warning about what happens to you, if anyone learns you warned me?"

"There will be consequences," Corvane agreed placidly. "There are always consequences. I've arranged to be inconveniently indispensable for at least another month, which should cover your escape and my retirement both, if I time it right." He stepped back, and pulled the white mask down over his face, and became once more a nameless gray man in a festival crowd. "One last thing, Xanthe. The king will tell the court you were stolen. Kidnapped by the witch, poor lamb, dragged north against his will. Let him tell it. It's a kinder story than the truth, and kinder stories keep kingdoms calm."

"And what's the truth?"

Corvane paused at the far end of the bridge, half-turned, the festival lights making a halo of the painted tear on his mask.

"The truth is that a boy tasted something wrong in his supper, and instead of swallowing it and forgetting—like every good servant in that valley was trained to do—he put it in his pocket." The painted tear gleamed. "That's the whole treason, right there. You kept the evidence. Everything since has just been the world catching up to you. Go on, now. Dawn's coming, and I hear the mountain whispering, and so, I suspect, does your kitchen boy."

Then he was gone, folded into the masked crowd like a coin into a purse, and Xanthe stood alone on the Weeper's Span with a warm lantern in his hands and the whole dark canal flowing past beneath him, carrying away its little borrowed sorrows toward the sea.

* * *

Odile materialized at his elbow the way she always did, all at once, as if she'd been assembled from shadows and grievance. Tam peered out from behind her, wide-eyed above his stolen mask.

"Well?" she demanded. "Are we arrested? Are we dead? Did he drug you? Check your tongue, you're a professional, check your—"

"He gave me a lantern," Xanthe said.

"A *lantern*."

"And directions. East along the canal road, avoid the pass, and—" Xanthe stopped. The rest of it was still settling in him, heavy and cold: the stone box, the sixty years, the king's sincere and terrible kindness. "And he told me the king isn't planning to give me to the mountain anymore. He's planning to keep me. Bind me into the throne, under the valley. Alive. Forever. To run the wards."

Odile went absolutely still. Then, quietly, with the flat calm she reserved for the very worst news: "That's worse."

"That's worse," Xanthe agreed.

Tam tugged Odile's sleeve. "The whispers," he said. His voice was small. "They've been saying it too. Since the stars died. They keep saying *they'll keep you, they'll keep you, warm and waking, forever and ever*—" He stopped, and swallowed. "I thought it was the mountain threatening us. I think—Xanthe, I think it was laughing."

The three of them stood on the bridge while the festival roared behind them, and the grief-charms floated under their feet, and somewhere across the city the king's riders knocked on doors with polite gloved fists.

"East," Xanthe said at last. He lifted the lantern. Its little flame leaned north, stubborn, toward the mountain, no matter which way he turned it. "Canal road. Before dawn."

"And the tollhouse at Grayford," Odile said grimly, already moving. "Bring something you can bear to lose."

Xanthe touched his pocket, where a honey cake's worth of evidence had once sat, and thought about memories, and about which ones he could spare.

He was afraid, suddenly and completely, that the answer was none of them—and more afraid of what that might cost.

They went east, into the dark, with the lantern leaning north.
