## 10. Winter Closes Its Hand

The snow began on the third day out of Hollow Bells and did not stop.

Tam had never seen snow before. In the kitchens of Vell he'd heard about it — the older cooks described it the way they described everything, in terms of food. Snow was like flour sifted from an enormous sieve held by God, or maybe it was more like the foam on bad ale, or the scum you skimmed off milk if you were too poor to be gentle with it. Tam had imagined something small and domestic. What actually fell out of the sky was a gray-white nothing that went on falling forever, as if the whole world above them had been tipped over and was emptying itself out.

"It's because there are no stars," Odile said, when he asked her about it. She said it the way she said most things: flatly, while doing something else, in this case trying to coax a damp fire into life under the leaning wall of a shepherd's hut they'd found two miles off the road. "Stars are holes the warmth leaks through. No stars, no leak. The cold just sits on us now."

"That's not how stars work," Xanthe said mildly, from the doorway, where he stood watching the white wall come down.

"You know how stars work, do you? The poison-taster?"

"I know how *honey* works," Xanthe said, "and I know that arguing with you is like arguing with a jar of pickled eels, so I'm going to stop." He came inside, shaking snow off his shoulders, and Tam noticed — the way he'd been noticing for two days and pretending not to — that his movements had gotten slower. Careful. A man rationing himself.

That was the other thing about the snow. They had left Hollow Bells with a sack of bread, half a wheel of hard cheese, a string of onions Odile had acquired through means she declined to specify, and a smoked ham that Tam had personally wept over acquiring. That had been nine days ago. The bread was gone. The cheese was down to a rind you could read a letter through. The onions had frozen into little yellow stones that Odile sucked on while walking, claiming it kept her teeth sharp, which Tam privately suspected was a lie she told for fun.

The ham was gone too. Tam tried not to think about the ham.

* * *

The shepherd's hut was the best shelter they'd found since the tollhouse: stone walls, a roof mostly attached, and a hearth that drew well enough once Odile got it going. There was even an old straw pallet in the corner that smelled of sheep and decades, but sheep-and-decades smelled like heaven compared to sleeping outside, where the snow came down all night with a sound like the world whispering *shhh, shhh, shhh*, and Tam lay awake listening to it and thinking about how the mountain had also whispered to him, once, and how the whispering had stopped three nights ago, and how he hadn't told anyone either thing.

He was thinking about it now, sitting close to the fire while Xanthe divided up the day's food — which today meant dividing one onion stone and the cheese rind into three portions with the solemn precision of a priest at an altar.

"Your turn to have the big piece," Xanthe said, handing Tam the portion with the rind still on it.

"They're all the same size."

"The big piece is whichever one I give you. Eat it before I change my mind."

Tam ate it. It tasted like cold and chalk and, faintly, of the cellar under the Vell kitchens where the cheese had lived its whole life, and he was so hungry it was one of the best things he'd ever eaten. Across the fire, Odile watched them both with her chin on her knees, and Tam saw her do the same arithmetic he'd been doing — the look down at the sack, the little pause, the way her eyes went to Xanthe's hands and stayed there a moment too long.

Xanthe's hands were getting thin. Tasters' hands, Tam thought, and then hated himself for thinking it, because it wasn't funny even in the privacy of his own head.

"We should talk about food," Odile said.

"I'd love to," said Xanthe. "Tell me about food. Describe it in detail."

"I'm serious. Grayford's two days back the way we came. There'll be a winter market. We could sell—" she patted her coat, where Tam knew she kept her charms and her sleight-of-hand tools and, he suspected, several things she'd stolen from people who deserved it less than she claimed, "—some of this. Buy dried meat. Flour, if the price hasn't gone mad."

"Prices will have gone mad," Xanthe said. "No stars, early winter, roads closing. Everyone with a cellar is sitting on it like a dragon."

"Then we go hungry and keep moving," Odile said. "We're four days from the river Sere if the pass stays open. Four days is nothing. I've gone longer."

"When?" Tam asked.

She looked at him. "What?"

"Gone longer without eating. When?"

There was a silence with a particular quality to it, the kind of silence that meant Odile was deciding whether to answer honestly or make a joke, and Tam had learned that with Odile the honest answers came out only when she was tired enough not to catch herself.

"When I was fourteen," she said finally, poking the fire harder than it needed poking. "My sister got sick in the autumn and there wasn't money for both medicine and supper, so I did the arithmetic every girl in a poor family does." She shrugged, as if she'd said something about the weather. "I ate every second day for a season. You get used to it. Your stomach stops shouting and starts just — sulking quietly. Like a mule."

Nobody laughed. Tam thought about saying something kind and couldn't find one that wouldn't sound like pity, and Odile would rather eat a pickled eel than be pitied, so instead he said:

"My mum used to say hunger's just your body telling you it trusts you to fix it."

"Your mum sounds like she was smarter than both of us," Odile said, and something in her voice made Tam glance at Xanthe, and Xanthe was looking at the fire with an expression Tam couldn't read at all, and didn't try to, because reading Xanthe's face when his mother came up was like trying to read a locked door.

* * *

It got worse on the second night.

The snow kept falling — Tam had begun to think of it as a creature now, a great gray animal lying down on top of the world and refusing to get up — and the pass did not stay open. They lost the road for half a day in the whiteout and found it again only because Odile had the sense to tie a cord between the three of them, and even then they walked into a drift chest-high and had to dig their way out sideways like crabs, laughing in a slightly unhinged way, because when you're that cold and that tired laughter is the only tool left in the kit.

They reached another hut — smaller, half-collapsed, but a roof — and there was nothing to eat at all. Not a rind, not a stone. Xanthe gave away his share without anyone noticing, which was the problem; Tam noticed, because Tam had grown up in a kitchen and knew what it looked like when someone was feeding everyone else out of their own bowl. He'd done it himself for his little cousins, back before.

So Tam sat by the fire with his stomach sulking like a mule, watching Xanthe pretend he'd eaten, watching Odile watch Xanthe pretend he'd eaten, and the whisper started up again.

It hadn't spoken in three days. Tam had almost convinced himself it was gone — that whatever lived under the mountain had gotten bored of him, or distracted, or dead. But now, in the quiet, with the snow going *shhh, shhh* on the broken roof, it came back like water finding a crack.

*tam.*

Not aloud. Never aloud. It was somewhere behind his ears, or under his tongue, or in the hollow of his throat — a voice like a very large person being very careful to speak gently, which was somehow worse than shouting.

*I'm not listening,* Tam thought, the way he'd been thinking it for weeks.

*you are always listening. that is what i like about you.*

*Go away.*

*i only want to help. the tall one is starving himself to feed the others. the witch would sell him tomorrow if it bought her sister back — oh yes, little kitchen boy, i know what she carries in her coat. and you. you are cold and hungry and afraid, and nobody asked you to come on this errand at all. you could be warm. you could be fed. bring him to me alone, and the mountain will hold you all winter like a coal in its hand.*

*He's my friend,* Tam thought.

*is he? you have known him one month. i have known you since the stars went out. i have been the only voice in your head every night since—*

*Shut up.* His hands had gone to fists in his lap. *Shut up shut up shut up.*

And the voice did something new. It laughed. Softly, kindly, the laugh of someone patting a child on the head, and it said:

*you needn't decide tonight. but the snow is patient, tam. i am patient. hunger is patient. everything on this road is patient except the boy who is dying of generosity, and he will not last the week.*

Then it was gone, and Tam sat there shaking, and across the fire Xanthe looked up and said, "You all right? You've gone gray."

"Tired," Tam said. Which was true. Everything else was true too, and he said none of it, and the lie sat in his mouth like the cheese rind had sat in his stomach: heavy, cold, and impossible to swallow properly.

* * *

On the third morning, Odile went out at first light and came back an hour later with two rabbits.

"How—" Tam started.

"Don't ask questions you don't want answered," Odile said, already skinning them with a small knife and total efficiency, and Tam decided he genuinely did not want the answer, because the snares had to have come from somewhere and the somewhere was probably a farmer's shed, and the farmer was probably having a worse morning than any of them.

But rabbit. Actual meat. Odile made stew with the last of the onions and a handful of something she called journeyman's herbs and Tam called weeds, and the smell filled the little hut until Tam's eyes watered, and for one hour the world was simple: fire, food, the two people who had become, without anyone signing anything, his family.

It was during that hour — warm, full, safe — that the guilt finally broke him.

Because that was the thing about the whisper. It was right. That was the horrible trick of it. Every promise it made was plausible. Every fear it named was real. It never lied to Tam; it just took the truth and bent it into a shape with a hook in it. And sitting there full of rabbit stew with his friends, Tam understood that the whisper had been waiting for exactly this — for him to feel safe enough to be honest with himself — because honesty was the hook.

"I have to tell you something," he said.

Odile looked up. Xanthe looked up. The fire popped.

Tam's mouth was dry. "Since the stars went out. Since the first night. The mountain's been — talking to me. Not out loud. Inside. And I told you it stopped, and it didn't stop, it just — went quiet for a bit, and it's been saying things, and I didn't tell you because—" His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "Because it promised me things. If I brought you to it. If I brought *you*—" he couldn't look at Xanthe, so he looked at the fire, "—alone."

The silence afterward was the longest of Tam's life.

He made himself look up. Xanthe's face had gone very still — not angry, worse than angry, the stillness of a man tasting something and waiting to find out what it is. And Odile —

Odile had gone pale, and then she had gone *dangerous*, which Tam had seen exactly once before, in Hollow Bells, when a drunk had grabbed Xanthe's arm on the bridge. She rose slowly, the way weather rises, and her hand was nowhere near her knife, which somehow made it worse.

"Say it again," she said quietly. "Slowly. All of it. What did it offer you?"

"Warmth. Food. Safety. It said the mountain would hold me all winter 'like a coal in its hand.'" Tam's voice shook. "It said you'd sell him anyway. For your sister. It knows about — it said it knows what you carry in your coat."

Something flickered across Odile's face — not guilt exactly, but the ghost of it, the place where guilt lived — and that flicker seemed to enrage her more, because it meant the mountain had been right about something, and she turned on Tam fully now.

"And you sat there. Night after night. Listening. Eating our food. Walking our road. And you said *nothing*."

"I was scared—"

"Of course you were scared! Do you think I'm not scared? Do you think *he's* not scared?" She jabbed a finger at Xanthe, who hadn't moved. "Scared isn't the crime, boy. The crime is that every night you chose the mountain's voice over ours, and we slept next to you, and we trusted you, and the whole time you were carrying its leash in your pocket wondering whether to use it!"

"I never would have—"

"You don't *know* that!" Her voice cracked open and underneath it, suddenly, was something raw and young and terrified. "You don't know what you'd have done at the end, when the hunger got bad enough, when the snow closed the pass, when it whispered *just leave him at the shrine, just walk away, just one small thing* — you don't know! I know what hungry scared people do, Tam. I've *been* the hungry scared person. I know exactly what's in us, and it isn't pretty, and the mountain knows it too, that's why it picks children—"

"Odile." Xanthe's voice. Quiet. Not loud at all, but it cut through the hut like a blade through cloth.

She stopped. Breathing hard. Hands shaking — Tam saw it now, her hands were shaking as much as his were.

Xanthe looked at Tam for a long moment. And Tam braced himself for the verdict, for the door closing, for the family dissolving back into three strangers in the snow — and what Xanthe said was:

"Why now?"

"What?"

"Why tell us now? You could have held it another week. Held it forever, maybe. The pass opens, we reach the Sere, you never have to say a word. So why now, Tam?"

Tam looked down at his hands. At the stew, half-finished, warm in his belly. At the fire.

"Because it was right about everything except one thing," he said. "It said nobody asked me to come on this errand. It said I was cold and hungry and afraid and alone. And I sat here tonight with rabbit stew and a fire and you two, and I realized — it's wrong. About the only thing that matters, it's wrong. And I'd rather tell you the worst thing about me while I'm warm and full and you can't throw me out into a blizzard—" his voice wobbled into something almost like a laugh, "—than keep its secret one more night. Because keeping it felt like holding its hand. And I don't want to hold its hand. I want to hold yours. Either of you. Both of you. Please."

The fire popped. Snow whispered on the roof.

Odile sat down slowly. She rubbed her face with both hands, and when she lowered them her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"If you ever carry anything like that again," she said, "without telling us — I will feed you to the river Sere myself, and I'll enjoy it, and then I'll feel terrible about it for the rest of my life, and you'll deserve both."

"Understood," Tam said.

"And you—" she rounded on Xanthe, clearly needing somewhere to put the rest of the fear, "—say something. You're the one it wants. Say something."

Xanthe was quiet a moment longer. Then he reached across the fire, took Tam's bowl, and ladled the last of the stew into it — the portion Xanthe had been pretending he'd already eaten his share of, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all the mountain.

"Eat," he said. "All of it. You can't fight a mountain's whispers on an empty stomach. Believe me, I know the arithmetic." He pushed the bowl into Tam's hands, and his eyes, when Tam dared meet them, were tired and clear and not closed at all. "You told us. That's the whole battle, Tam. The mountain wins by making people quiet. You just refused to be quiet. Whatever it promised you — it lied about the ending. It always lies about the ending."

"It didn't lie," Tam said miserably. "That's the worst part. Everything it said was true except—"

"Except us," Odile finished. She snorted, wiped her eyes roughly with her sleeve, and picked up her spoon. "Well. Let it chew on that."

They ate in silence for a while, the good kind, the kind that comes after a storm. Tam ate every bite of the stew, and it was the best meal of his life, though there was no rabbit in it worth mentioning.

It was near midnight, the fire banked low, all three of them wrapped together in the one blanket against the cold, when Tam felt it start again — faint, far under the ground, patient as snowfall.

*tam.*

His heart slammed. Odile stirred against his shoulder. Xanthe breathed slow and even, asleep or pretending.

*you told them. clever, cruel little boy. now they will watch you forever, and you will never be sure of them again, and neither will they. i can wait years. i have waited centuries. i only need one moment of weakness, and winter makes moments like that the way rivers make mud.*

Tam stared into the dark. And then, slowly, deliberately, he did the thing he'd been afraid to do since the first night — he answered it out loud. A whisper, barely a breath, so as not to wake the others.

"No," he said. "You don't understand what happened tonight. I'm not your spy anymore. I'm their *early warning*. Every word you say to me, I tell them. You haven't got a secret line into this family anymore. You've got a talking parrot that reports everything home." He smiled into the dark, and it felt dangerous and wonderful. "So go on. Whisper. Whisper all you like. Tell me your plans. I'll take dictation."

For a long time, the mountain said nothing.

Then, deeper than before, colder than the snow, it said:

*then i will stop whispering to you, little parrot — and begin whispering to them.*

And beneath Tam, beneath the hut, beneath the buried road and the roots of the petrified tree far to the north, something vast shifted its attention — and Tam felt it move past him, felt it probe outward into the dark like a hand feeling along a wall, searching for the next crack, the next hungry ear, the next quiet person to bend.

He sat up in the blackness, heart hammering, and looked at his sleeping friends, and wondered which of them the mountain would choose — and how he would ever explain, this time, that the whispering had moved on.

Outside, the snow went on falling, without stopping, like a message being delivered to the whole world at once.
