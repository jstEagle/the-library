## 11. The Witch's Confession

The cave Odile found them was less a cave than a wound in the hillside, a place where the rock had given up pretending to be solid and simply opened its mouth. It smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and it was the first roof they'd had in four days that wasn't made of snow.

"Home sweet home," she announced, dropping her pack against the wall with a thud that echoed longer than it should have.

Tam peered inside from the grey afternoon light, snow crusted in his eyebrows like a second set of lashes gone white. "It's very... honest," he said diplomatically.

"It's dry."

"It's also full of bones."

"Bones are just stones that used to be busy." Odile was already kneeling by the fire ring some previous traveler—some previous desperate traveler, she amended—had left behind, arranging kindling with the practiced economy of a woman who had built fires in worse places than this and been caught at most of them. "Xanthe, tell the boy bones are fine."

Xanthe crouched beside her without a word and began sorting her kindling pile into sizes, because that was what Xanthe did: he took whatever chaos she handed him and quietly organized it, the way he must once have organized the king's supper into *safe* and *not safe* and *poisoned with powdered thornroot, actually*. His hands were steady. His face was not. Four days of walking north through a winter that had arrived like an ambush, and his face had developed a look she recognized from mirrors—the look of someone carrying something heavy and refusing to say what it weighed.

She'd seen him looking at her, too. Not often. Just often enough.

That was the trouble with traveling with people. You ran out of road before you ran out of things they deserved to know.

* * *

The fire caught on the third try, which Tam declared lucky and Odile privately attributed to a charm she'd tied into the kindling, which was technically magic, which was technically forbidden in Vell but they were two days past the old ward-line now and the rules had died with the stars. She let Tam believe in luck. Luck was cheaper than charms and easier to explain to kitchen boys.

They ate barley porridge with dried plums stirred in, and Tam told a story about a cook named Marta who could throw a ladle forty feet with killing accuracy, and Xanthe laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a while the cave was almost warm in the way caves can be when you're young enough to mistake firelight for safety.

Then Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, the way fifteen-year-olds do, sliding sideways against his pack with his mouth open, one hand still curled around his bowl as if he meant to defend it in his dreams.

Odile looked at Xanthe across the fire.

He was watching her. Not accusingly. That was the worst part. He was watching her the way he'd watched the honey cake in chapter one of this whole disaster—with attention, with patience, waiting for the flavor to declare itself.

"You've been chewing on something for days," he said quietly. "I can taste it on you."

"That's a horrible sentence."

"I'm a taster. Everything I say is a horrible sentence eventually." He turned his bowl in his hands. "You knew about thornroot before I ever showed it to you. You knew what it was for. And when the wards fell, you weren't surprised—not really. Startled, yes. But it was like watching someone open a door you'd already knocked on."

Odile fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

There were perhaps a hundred ways to begin this confession and she'd rehearsed none of them, because rehearsal was for liars and she had decided, somewhere on the frozen road between Grayford and here, that she was finished lying to these two. The problem was that the truth had no good opening line. Truths never did. They just stood there in the doorway, dripping snow, waiting to be let in.

"My sister's name was Maren," she said.

Xanthe went still.

"She was older than me by three years and prettier by five, and she could do things with a needle and thread that I cannot describe to you because they'd sound like lies. She sewed charms into hems. Little ones. Wards against fever, against bad milk, against husbands who drank. Village work. Hedge work. Our mother called it *stitching*, dismissively, like it was darning." Odile heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for prices and warnings. "When the plague came through the Marches—it came fast, faster than the crown's physicians, who arrived afterward to count bodies and bill the survivors—Maren stitched until her fingers bled. And then she caught it herself, because that's the joke the world tells, isn't it? The healer drinks the well water last."

"I'm sorry," Xanthe said.

"Don't be sorry yet. Save it. There's a part where you'll want it back." She pulled off one glove and held her bare hands to the fire. Old burn scars across the knuckles, from a lesson learned badly at fourteen. "She didn't die of the plague. I need you to understand that. She died of the cure—or what passed for a cure out there. A cunning man, a real one, not hedge-trash like us, came through with a remedy that worked. Mostly. It worked if you could pay. And the price—" She laughed, short and ugly. "You'll appreciate this, taster. The price was never coin. Coin is nothing to the dead. The price was passage. A favor owed. He was a broker, Xanthe. For the Undercourt."

The fire popped. Tam slept on, blessedly, snoring like a small congested bear.

"Maren paid. Or rather, I paid on her behalf, because she was too sick to know what she was signing. I signed for her. My name, my blood, my mark—I gave her soul safe conduct down under the mountain, where they say nothing rots and nothing hurts, in exchange for the remedy." Her hands had stopped feeling the fire's warmth; they often did, in winter, in memory. "And then the broker took the payment and the remedy both and left, and Maren died anyway, because the man was a fraud or the plague was smarter than the medicine or the mountain simply changed its mind. Souls don't get refunds. That's the first rule of the Undercourt, and I learned it the hard way, standing over my sister's body holding a receipt."

Xanthe hadn't moved. His bowl sat forgotten. In the firelight his eyes were very dark and very steady, and Odile thought, absurdly, *he tastes everything twice*.

"So when I ended up in Vell," she said, "it wasn't an accident. Nothing about me ends up anywhere by accident. I got myself arrested on purpose. Do you know how hard it is to get imprisoned in a poison-proofed valley? You have to commit a crime that frightens them. Smuggling remedies to servants—that did it. They put me in the cellar under the kitchens, which is exactly where I wanted to be, because cellars connect to everything, and I had years to plan and nothing to lose." She met his eyes. "I brought the thornroot in. Before the wards fell. Years before. I smuggled it in piece by piece, hidden in remedy bundles, ground fine and packed in wax, because thornroot is Undercourt currency and I needed enough of it to buy Maren's soul back the moment I found a way below."

"And the honey cake," Xanthe said. His voice was very quiet. "The cake meant for the king."

"The cake was never meant for anyone. It was meant for the cellar floor. I was moving the last of my store that night—transferring it, hiding it deeper—and I spilled some into the batter trough. A few grains. Enough for your tongue to catch, because your tongue catches what no one else's would." She spread her hands. "I didn't poison anything. I've never poisoned anything. But I brought death into the valley, Xanthe. Ground up, wax-sealed, harmless as flour until it isn't. If the wards hadn't fallen, I'd have walked out of that cellar someday with enough thornroot to buy back my sister, and I'd never have told you any of this, and you'd have gone on thinking the worst thing in Vell was the steward's temper."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked against the cave mouth. Somewhere far off, wind moved through the dead trees with a sound like a crowd deciding whether to stay.

"Say something," Odile said finally, and hated how small her voice came out. "Curse me. Call me a fool. Tell me I nearly got you killed over a debt that was never mine to collect. All true. I'll nod along."

Xanthe looked into the fire for a long moment.

"You failed," he said.

"Yes."

"You spent years planning, got yourself jailed, smuggled poison grain by grain into the most heavily warded valley in the kingdom—and it all came to nothing. Your sister's still down there. Or wherever souls go."

"Yes."

"And instead of giving up, you're walking north. Through a starless winter. Toward the court that took her money and kept the goods." He shook his head slowly. "You're running the same errand twice."

"I'm aware of how it sounds."

"It sounds," Xanthe said, "like loyalty that doesn't know when to quit." He set his bowl down. "My mother sold me before I was born. One signature, one winter of warmth, and I was currency. I've spent my whole life being *worth* something to people who never loved me—my tongue, my health, my soul, all of it priced and catalogued. And here you are, having spent years and blood and freedom on someone you couldn't even save, and you're ashamed of it." He leaned forward. "Odile. Being willing to pay too much for someone you love isn't a crime. It's the only thing I've ever envied."

Odile discovered, somewhat to her fury, that her eyes were burning.

"Oh, don't," she managed. "I'm a hardened criminal. I have a reputation."

"You have a sister you'd walk into a mountain for. That's not a reputation, that's a spine." He reached across the fire and took her scarred hand, brief and matter-of-fact, the way he might steady a bowl. "You should have told me sooner. Not because I'd have judged you—but because you've been carrying it alone in the cold, and that's stupid, and I thought we were past stupid."

"We're never past stupid. Stupid is load-bearing."

That startled a laugh out of him, ragged at the edges, and she counted it a victory.

* * *

She woke in the black hours to the sound of Tam crying.

Not loudly—he was trying not to, she could hear the effort in it, the hitched breaths of a boy who'd learned that crying got you noticed and noticed got you hurt. Odile lay still a moment, listening, then rolled over to find him sitting up against the wall, knees drawn up, face silvered by the banked coals.

"Bad dream?" she asked.

"No." He wiped his nose with his sleeve. "Good dream. That's worse." He hesitated. "My mum was in it. Properly in it. Not the way I usually remember her—more like she was *there*. And she said—" His voice cracked. "She said the mountain keeps everyone who's owed. She said I shouldn't be scared of it. And then I woke up and I couldn't remember if that was really her or if it was the whispers wearing her voice, and I—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "How do you tell? How do you know what's real?"

Odile crawled over and sat beside him, and after a moment's consideration put an arm around his shoulders, because some questions aren't answered, they're just accompanied.

"You taste it," she said. "Like Xanthe does. Real things have weight. Lies are light—they blow around. Whatever promised you safety if you delivered Xanthe alone, that was light as ash. And your mum—" She thought of Maren, of stitched hems and bleeding fingers. "Love has weight, Tam. When you hear something that heavy, trust it. When you hear something that wants you to trade a friend, that's ash. Blow it away."

Tam leaned into her, sniffling. "Did you ever hear from someone you lost?"

"Every night since the stars went out," Odile said honestly. "And I check the weight every time."

Across the dying fire, Xanthe's eyes were open. He'd heard everything—the whole confession, probably, and the whole consolation—and he didn't pretend otherwise. He just nodded at her, once, slow, the way one professional acknowledges another across a crowded kitchen.

*Three of us now,* the nod said. *Bound tighter than blood, and twice as stubborn.*

Odile settled back against the stone, Tam's breathing evening out against her shoulder, and let herself drift toward sleep for the first time in days without the confession gnawing her ribs.

She was nearly under when she felt it—a faint warmth against her breastbone, where she kept the last twist of thornroot wrapped in waxed cloth. It had been cold and inert for years, dead stock, worthless.

It was warm now. Warm as a held coal. Warm as something answering a call.

And beneath the wind outside the cave, so faint she might have dreamed it, the mountain had begun to hum.
