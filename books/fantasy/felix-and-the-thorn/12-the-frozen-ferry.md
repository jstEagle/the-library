## 12. The Frozen Ferry

The river Sere announced itself long before we saw it, the way grief does.

First there was the sound—a low, grinding hush beneath the wind, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Then the cold changed. I had thought I knew cold by then; three weeks on the Thorn Road will teach you a person's whole vocabulary of shivering. But this was different. This cold came off the water in sheets, and it carried a smell I couldn't name at first, until Odile did.

"Old ice," she said, pulling her hood tighter. "Ice that's been frozen so long it's forgotten how to melt."

"And that smell under it?"

She glanced at me sideways. Under her hood her face was pale and chapped, her breath hanging in little ghosts. "That's the river," she said. "Or what the river carries."

Tam made a small noise and walked closer to me without seeming to decide to. He'd been doing that for days—drifting toward whichever of us was nearest when the whispers got loud. Lately they'd been quieter, which frightened me more than the loud ones. A voice that stops talking is either finished with you or saving up.

We crested a low hill of frost-killed gorse, and there it was: the Sere, wide as a lake and black as spilled ink, running slow between banks of white reeds. It wasn't frozen. That was the first wrong thing. Everything else along the road had frozen—the puddles, the streams, the mud in the wheel ruts—but the Sere moved, dark and unhurried, as if winter simply hadn't been able to get a grip on it.

"The ferryman keeps one crossing open," Odile said. "There's a stretch below the bend where the water runs too old and too deep to freeze. He rows the dead across, mostly. The living pay triple."

"How much is triple?"

"That depends on what you're carrying." Her eyes flicked to my coat pocket, where my last coin sat—the silver half-crown I'd stolen from the palace kitchens the night we ran, tucked into a fold of pastry dough where no one counted. It had survived three weeks of hunger because I kept telling myself we'd need it more later than now. Later had arrived wearing a ferryman's face, apparently.

* * *

He lived in a hut built from the ribs of some enormous boat, lashed together with rope gone gray as bone, and he was mending a net when we found him—or pretending to, since the net had no holes that I could see. He was older than old. His beard came down to his belt in two white ropes, and his hands were the color of someone who has spent a lifetime in water and never quite dried out. When he looked up at us, his eyes were the same black as the river.

"Three," he said. Not a question. He'd heard us coming, or smelled us, or the river had told him.

"We need to cross," Odile said.

"Everyone who comes here needs to cross." He set down the net. "That's not the same as being able to."

"We can pay."

"Can you." He rose, joints cracking like river ice, and looked each of us over the way a butcher looks at meat—not cruelly, just honestly. His gaze settled on Tam last and stayed there. Tam shifted. "This one's noisy," the ferryman said. "Full of voices. You should be careful carrying cargo like that. Cargo talks back."

"He's not cargo," I said, before I could stop myself. "He's our friend."

Something moved behind the ferryman's eyes—amusement, maybe, or its fossilized ancestor. "Friend. Well. That's a currency I don't take." He held out one broad, wrinkled palm. "Silver. Three pieces. Living fare."

I felt Odile go still beside me. We had, between the three of us, exactly one piece of silver, a handful of bent copper pins, a lucky button Tam refused to surrender, and Odile's sleight-of-hand, which had already failed once against a tollhouse ghost and she didn't trust it twice against anything that rowed the dead for a living.

"One," I said. "One silver. And—" I dug out the pins, the button. Tam pushed the button forward with a look of quiet heroism that would have broken my heart if my heart hadn't already been busy breaking over the coin.

The ferryman didn't even glance at the button. He was looking at me, and then past me, at the river, and when he spoke again his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from him was somehow worse.

"You're going to the mountain," he said. "All three. I can see it on you, the way you see frost on a window. Going down to the Undercourt to settle accounts." He folded his hands. "Then listen to me, boy, because I only say this once per customer, and most customers don't listen, and that's why most customers drown. The Undercourt honors debts. Every debt, to the letter, to the last farthing. It's the only law down there and it never bends. But hear the other half, because everyone forgets the other half." He leaned close. His breath smelled of river weed and candle smoke. "It despises beggars."

"Beggars?"

"A soul that arrives owing nothing and owning nothing—that's a soul the court must treat as an equal, because there's nothing to take and nothing to weigh. Do you understand? They can't bargain with an empty hand. An empty hand frightens them." He straightened up. "But a beggar—someone who arrives wanting, needing, grasping, holding out their cup—they'll strip him to the bone and thank him for the donation. So. Arrive owing nothing, or arrive owning everything. Those are your doors. Anything in between, you're begging, and beggars get eaten."

* * *

We stood on the bank while he readied the boat, and I turned the coin over in my fingers until it was warm.

Owing nothing or owning everything. I owed plenty—I owed Odile my life twice over, owed Tam the truth that his whispers had led us true even when they'd lied to him, owed my mother a debt I hadn't known existed until it was being collected from my chest. But those weren't the kind of debts the court weighed. The kind they weighed fit in pockets.

And what did I own? One silver half-crown. Stolen pastry money. My entire fortune, purchased at the cost of everything else I'd ever had: my kitchen, my trade, my name in the steward's ledger, the small warm box of a life I'd mistaken for safety.

I thought about what the coin was worth on the far side of the river. Nothing. Less than nothing—it would mark me as a man with property, a man with something to lose, a man who could be bargained with. The ferryman's words kept turning over with the coin: *an empty hand frightens them.*

"Xanthe." Odile's voice, low. She knew. Of course she knew; she always knew what I was doing before I did. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"It's not enough anyway," I said. "One coin for three fares. He'd leave one of us on the bank, and we both know which one he'd pick."

"I'd swim," Tam said loyally, and then looked at the water and went gray. "Actually. On reflection."

So when the ferryman called us down to the shingle, I went—and on the way, I passed the child.

She was sitting in the lee of an overturned skiff, half-buried in frost-dead reeds, and I nearly walked past her because she was so still. Small. Maybe seven, maybe eight, wrapped in sacking that had once been a grain sack, with the printed letters still visible across her shoulder. Her lips were the blue of the far side of dusk. She was watching the boat with the terrible patience of someone who has been waiting a very long time and expects to wait longer.

I stopped. I don't know why I stopped, except that I was raised to taste things, and something about the air around her tasted wrong—bitter, thin, like thornroot in honey.

"Are you waiting for the ferry?" I asked.

She looked up at me. Her eyes were enormous and dark and utterly calm. "Nobody pays for me," she said. Her voice was steady, which was worse than crying. "Dead people have coins sewn in their shrouds. I don't have a shroud. Nobody sewed anything."

The cold went through me then, all the way through, and it wasn't the river's cold. Somewhere behind me the ferryman was calling the fare, and Odile was arguing with him in her best market voice, and Tam was saying something anxious about the button, and I stood in the frost with a child who had died unshrouded and unmourned and unbought, and I understood—with the strange clarity that comes at the end of things—exactly what I was holding.

My last coin. Warm from my hand. Enough to buy one living passage across a river of the dead.

Not enough to buy three. Never going to be enough for what waited on the far bank either—the ferryman himself had told me so. A coin spent on myself was a coin wasted twice.

I crouched down to her level. Up close I could see she wasn't quite solid, the way frost isn't quite solid, the way a breath on glass isn't quite there if you look directly at it.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She considered this. "I had one," she said. "Before. It wore off."

"Well." I pressed the coin into her small cold hand and closed her fingers over it, and my own hand ached with the cold of hers, an ache I suspected I'd keep. "This buys your crossing. All of it. Tell the ferryman Xanthe sent you, and if he argues, tell him I know about the net with no holes."

Her fingers tightened around the silver. For the first time, her composure cracked—not into tears but into something smaller and more devastating: surprise. The expression of someone who has stopped expecting anything and just received something anyway.

"For me?" she whispered. "But it's your—"

"It's heavy," I said. "I've been carrying it for weeks and it's heavy. Take it."

* * *

The ferryman watched the whole exchange from the waterline, arms crossed, saying nothing, which I learned later was his version of applause.

When I came down the shingle empty-handed, Odile stared at my pocket, then at my face. "You gave it away."

"Yes."

"To the dead child."

"She needed it more than the Undercourt does."

Odile opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "You absolute—" She shook her head, and something in her expression broke loose from exasperation and landed somewhere warmer, somewhere she clearly didn't want it to land. "You know what he told us. Owning nothing or owing nothing. You've just handed away the only thing you owned."

"I know," I said.

"So now you're arriving with nothing. Empty-handed. At the court that eats beggars."

"No," said the ferryman, from behind us, and we all turned. He stood at the oar with the child already seated in the bow, wrapped now in his own oilskin cloak, looking impossibly small and impossibly safe. His black eyes rested on me with something that might, in a younger face, have been respect. "Now he's arriving the right way. There's a difference between having nothing and holding nothing back. The court can weigh a full pocket. It can't weigh an open hand." He nodded at the boat. "Get in, all three. Fare's paid."

"But we only had—"

"Fare's paid," he repeated, in a tone that ended the discussion the way winter ends autumn. "Dead girl's coin covers her. And the rest—" he shrugged, vast shoulders moving like a tide, "—the river covers friends of the generous. It doesn't happen often. Don't make a habit of it; I'll go out of business."

We climbed aboard. The boat was long and shallow and blacker than the water, and it moved before the oar touched the current, as if the river had been expecting us. Tam sat pressed against my side, whispering—not the mountain's whispers, I realized, just his own voice, naming things he could see, because the silence of the Sere was the loudest silence any of us had ever heard.

Halfway across, the child spoke. She had been watching me with her enormous dark eyes, and now she leaned close, and her voice was the sound frost makes growing.

"They'll try to weigh you," she said. "Down there. They weigh everything. But you're lighter than you think."

Then the far bank came up through the freezing mist—white reeds, black stone, and beyond them, filling half the sky, the mountain, green-veined and patient, breathing cold light like something asleep that dreams of waking.

And standing on the bank, waiting for us in a line as neat as clerks, were figures in gray robes, holding candles that burned without flickering in the wind.

One of them stepped forward as the keel kissed the stones, and its voice, when it came, was dry as ledger paper:

"Xanthe Reyes. Son of Maren. The court has been expecting you." A pause, and the candlelight tilted. "You are expected to bring nothing. We note, with interest, that you have complied."

Behind me, Odile exhaled slowly. Beside me, Tam's hand found mine, cold and gripping hard.

The clerk turned, candle first, and began to walk toward the mountain's root, and we had no choice but to follow—down, and down, and down, toward the place where every debt in the world came due.
