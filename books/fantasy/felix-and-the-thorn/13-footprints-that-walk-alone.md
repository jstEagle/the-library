## 13. Footprints That Walk Alone

The whispers had been getting louder all week, and Tam had been getting very good at pretending they hadn't.

It was a skill, pretending. He'd learned it young, back in the castle kitchens of Vell, where a boy who flinched at nothing got left alone and a boy who flinched at everything got teased about it until he either stopped or ran away. Tam had done neither. He'd perfected the middle path: the blank, pleasant face of a boy who heard nothing strange, saw nothing strange, and would absolutely not be interesting to anyone who liked making other people miserable.

But the mountain didn't tease. The mountain just *said things*.

*Tam,* it said, in a voice like frost forming on a window, like a coin dropped down a well that never hit bottom. *Tam, little listener. Tam, we know your name.*

He lay in the shelter of a fallen oak, wrapped in Odile's spare cloak, listening to Xanthe breathe on his left and Odile snore on his right — she denied snoring, loudly, whenever anyone raised the subject, which was itself evidence — and he stared up through the bare branches at a sky with no stars in it. Just black. Black like the inside of a closed pot.

*Tam. Come north. We have warm bread. We have a place for you. You are tired of being small.*

That last one was true, and that was the problem. The whispers never lied, exactly. They just told you true things in a voice that made them sound like the only true things in the world.

He thought about waking Xanthe. He'd been thinking about it for three days now, ever since the whispers had started saying things he couldn't unhear. But what would he say? *Excuse me, the mountain is talking to me again, and this time it's saying it will keep me safe if I bring it my best friend.* Xanthe had enough weight on his shoulders to sink a ferry. He carried his dead mother's soul in one hand and a kingdom's betrayal in the other, and he still made time every evening to ask Tam if his feet were warm enough, which nobody had asked him since he was six.

No. Tam would handle this himself. He was eleven years old and he had survived the kitchens of Vell under Steward Brannock, which Odile said was equivalent to surviving a war, only with more soup.

So when the whisper came that night — closer now, urgent, almost tender — *Tam, come now, come alone, come before they wake and fill your ears with doubt* — Tam did the bravest and stupidest thing he had ever done in his short, soup-stained life.

He got up, took the heel of bread he'd saved, tucked Odile's good knife into his belt because it seemed rude to go unarmed to meet a mountain, and walked north into the snow.

* * *

The snow had stopped falling for the first time in days, which somehow made everything worse. Without snowfall there was nothing to cover the sound of his own footsteps, and without wind there was nothing to drown out the whisper, which had stopped being a whisper and become something more like a voice speaking directly into the space behind his eyes.

*Good boy,* it said. *Good, faithful, clever boy.*

"I'm not clever," Tam muttered, because even walking to his own possible doom, some habits held. "Xanthe's the clever one. I'm the one who knows how many eggs a hundred men eat at breakfast."

*You know more than that. You hear. No one else hears. That makes you mine.*

"That doesn't make anybody yours," Tam said. "That's not how owning people works. I've seen how owning people works, and it's mostly paperwork."

The voice laughed. It was not a comforting laugh. It was the laugh of something very old and very large discovering that small things could be funny, and finding it slightly annoying.

The trees thinned as he climbed. The pines gave way to bare birch, white as bones in the starless dark, and then to granite, and then to a shelf of stone jutting out over the valley like the lip of a great stone bowl. And there, on the shelf, stood a shrine.

Tam had seen shrines before — little roadside boxes with candles, the kind travelers touched for luck. This was not that. This was a ring of standing stones taller than the tollhouse at Grayford, each one carved so deep with letters that the carvings looked less like writing than like wounds. Snow had drifted against their bases but refused to touch their tops, as if the stones were too hot for it, though when Tam put his hand near one, the air around it was cold enough to bite.

In the center of the ring, a fire burned. It burned green.

And in front of the fire, sitting cross-legged in the snow in his nightshirt with no boots on, was —

Well. It was Tam. Tam knew that, because he could see his own hands from the outside, hanging loose at his sides, and his own feet, blue with cold, and his own face, slack and peaceful as a sleeping child's.

"Oh," said the part of Tam that was still Tam, standing at the edge of the ring. "Oh, that's bad. That's — Odile says when things look like they're from a story, it's because they *are*, and the endings are always worse than the beginnings."

His body by the fire turned its head. Its eyes were open, and they were full of green light, and when it spoke, the voice came out of it like water out of a cracked jug — everywhere at once, spilling.

*XANTHE REYES.*

Not Tam's name. Not anymore. The mountain had used him as a door, and now it was calling through him to the person it actually wanted.

Tam tried to run. His legs wouldn't take instruction. He tried to shout, and his mouth made a small, thin sound, like a kettle far away. So he did the only thing left: he sat down hard in the snow at the edge of the shrine, hugged his knees, and waited to see whether his friends would come, and hated himself for hoping they would, and hated himself more for hoping they wouldn't, because if they came, the thing wearing him might hurt them.

Behind him, far below, he heard voices. Then footsteps. Then Odile swearing with an inventiveness that would have gotten her thrown out of three kingdoms.

They'd followed him. Of course they'd followed him. He'd left footprints in fresh snow, and Odile tracked things for a living, and Xanthe — Xanthe noticed everything, that was his whole gift, noticing what was wrong in a honey cake, what was wrong in a king's smile, what was wrong in a kitchen boy who'd gone quiet for three days.

*I'm sorry,* Tam thought, as hard as he could, at his own body, at the mountain, at the whole rotten starless sky. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.*

*You should have come alone,* the mountain said, and it sounded genuinely puzzled, the way a cat sounds puzzled when the mouse fights back. *We offered you safety. Safety is the only currency anything truly wants.*

"Shows what you know," Tam whispered. "Some of us want breakfast."

* * *

They came up over the shelf of stone together, Xanthe first with Odile half a step behind, both of them breathing hard, both of them in whatever clothes they'd grabbed in the dark. Xanthe had Odile's knife; Odile had a burning branch from their own fire, held like a sword, which would have been funny if Tam weren't currently being worn as a coat by a dying god.

"TAM," Xanthe shouted, and then he saw the figure by the green fire, and Tam watched his friend's face do the arithmetic — the face, the nightshirt, the missing boots — and arrive at a number he clearly hated.

"He called me," Tam's body said, in the mountain's voice, and Tam felt his own throat move and wanted to be sick. "He came when I asked. Did your other friends come when you asked? Did anyone? You are a boy bought and sold twice over, Xanthe Reyes. Once by your mother. Once by your king. No one has ever kept you. No one has ever *paid* for you."

Odile made a sound low in her chest, but Xanthe lifted a hand, and she stopped.

"You've got my mother's soul," Xanthe said. His voice was steady. Tam, listening, marveled at it. If a mountain spoke through *his* body and insulted his friends, he'd have cried, or run, or both. "That's why the wards fell. That's why the stars went out. You're collecting."

*I am owed,* the mountain said. *Debt is the bone of the world. Every court beneath every hill runs on it. Your mother signed. Your king agreed. The paper does not care whose body pays.*

"But here's what I don't understand." Xanthe came closer, stepping into the ring of stones, and Tam wanted to scream *don't, don't, it's a trap, everything is a trap, I read stories, I know how traps work* — but Xanthe kept walking, calm as a man crossing his own kitchen. "You could have taken me any time since the wards fell. I've slept in ditches. I've crossed a frozen river. I gave away my last coin to a child with blue lips. At any point on this road, you could have reached down and collected. Instead you spent a week whispering to an eleven-year-old. Why?"

The green fire flickered. For a moment — just a moment — the voice coming through Tam's body sounded tired. Old. Thin as ice at the edge of a thaw.

*Because the debt must be paid rightly,* it said. *Freely given, freely taken. A soul dragged screaming is worth half. A soul traded willingly is worth double. And a soul traded for another soul freely surrendered—*

"—is worth everything," Xanthe finished. "That's the offer, isn't it. That's what you told Tam."

*Yes.*

"You offered him safety if he brought me to you. And now you're going to offer me my mother — free, no bargain, no price — if I walk away from the two people standing behind me."

*Freely,* the mountain said, and the word came out almost gentle, almost loving, and that was the most frightening thing yet. *Her soul, whole, returned to you tonight. Her face, her voice, her name. Everything the ledger ate. Yours. All you must do is turn around, walk south, and let the boy finish what he began. He is willing. He has been willing for days. Ask him.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Tam would carry for the rest of his life: part of him *had* been willing. Not to betray Xanthe — never that, never — but the whispers had promised warmth, and safety, and an end to being small, and on the coldest nights, hungry, listening to Odile and Xanthe talk in low voices about things he didn't understand, some small cold corner of him had thought: *at least the mountain wants me. At least somebody wants me.*

He saw Xanthe glance at the body by the fire — at *him* — and he saw Xanthe understand all of it in one look. The offer. The temptation. The lonely corner. Because Xanthe had lived in that corner his whole life, and that was exactly why he knew its shape.

"No," Xanthe said.

The green fire went very still.

"I said no," Xanthe repeated, louder, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, which somehow made it stronger rather than weaker, the way a bell with a flaw in it rings truer. "You want to know what I've learned since the stars went out? I learned that everything I was promised in Vell was a lie. Warm beds, safe walls, a family — lies, all of it, built on top of a debt I never signed. So forgive me if I'm not eager to trade the one true thing I've found on this road for another promise from another power that eats children."

He turned and looked at Odile, disgraced hedge-witch, smuggler of remedies, keeper of a sister's ghost. He looked at the body by the fire, at Tam inside it.

"They're not currency," Xanthe said. "Neither am I. Neither was my mother. You can weigh souls like coins until your caverns crumble, and it won't make a soul worth anything except to the people who love it. My mother sold a year of warmth for my life. That wasn't a debt. That was love, and love isn't collectible. So keep your ledger. Keep your fire. Keep your whispers. I refuse — do you hear me, under there? — I *refuse* to bargain. Not today, not at midwinter, not ever. Take it up with the clerks."

For a long moment, nothing moved. The snow didn't fall. The fire didn't flicker. Even Odile seemed afraid to breathe.

Then the mountain screamed.

It screamed through Tam's body and out of the stones and up off the shelf of rock into the starless sky, a sound like a glacier calving, like a bell the size of a cathedral cracking down the middle, and Tam felt it tear through his chest and out of his mouth and it hurt, it hurt worse than anything, but underneath the pain he felt something else, something that made him want to laugh even while he screamed:

The mountain was *afraid*.

Nobody had ever said no to it before. Not like that. Not plainly, not kindly, not with the whole heart. Kings had bribed it. Witches had cursed it. Queens had bargained with it across a thousand years. But a boy with no boots had just stood in its shrine and told it that love wasn't collectible, and somewhere in its ancient, starving, hoarded heart, something had believed him.

The scream broke off. The green light drained out of Tam's eyes like water out of a basin, and his body folded forward into the snow, and the last thing the voice said — small now, and human-sounding, and lost — was:

*Marked. You are marked, Xanthe Reyes. The gate will know you now. God help you at the door.*

Then the fire went out, all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the dark rushed in.

* * *

Tam woke up with someone rubbing snow on his feet, which was undignified, and discovered it was Odile, which was worse, because she narrated.

"Frostbitten toes on a boy who owns one pair of boots, and those boots are sitting by our fire like a pair of smug little traitors," she was saying. "Honestly. I've smuggled contraband through three ward-lines and I have never seen anything so stupid as a child walking barefoot up a mountain at night to answer a *whisper*."

"I brought the knife," Tam croaked.

"You brought my knife, yes, which means you armed the enemy, because the enemy was *you*." She pressed a tin cup into his hands. Broth. Hot, salty, miraculous. "Drink. And then you're going to tell us everything it said, every word, because I need to know exactly how much trouble we're in."

Across the dead fire pit, Xanthe sat on a stone with his head in his hands. When Tam looked at him, he looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed, and he managed something that was almost a smile.

"You came after me," Tam said. "Even though it was obviously a trap."

"Obviously," Xanthe agreed.

"Even though it wanted you specifically, and walking into its shrine was the single most dangerous thing you could do."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Xanthe considered the question seriously, the way he considered everything, tasting it like a spoonful of soup before deciding what it was.

"Because you'd do it for me," he said finally. "You already did, sort of. You spent a week trying to protect us from it by yourself, and it nearly worked, and it nearly killed you. Nobody gets to carry that alone. Not you, not me, not anyone." He paused. "Also, Odile threatened to tie me to a tree if I didn't."

"I did," Odile confirmed. "I had rope ready. I'd picked the tree."

Tam drank his broth and laughed until his ribs hurt, and then, because the mountain had marked Xanthe and the gate would know him now, and because none of them knew what that meant except that it couldn't be good, he stopped laughing.

Below them, far to the south, the world lay black and starless. But to the north — close now, close enough that Tam could feel it in his teeth the way you feel thunder — the mountain rose against the sky, and along its root-line, faint as embers under ash, something was glowing.

Green.

"It's expecting us," Odile said quietly.

"No," said Xanthe, standing, and there was something new in his voice, something Tam had never heard there before — not fear, and not courage exactly, but the stubborn, unglamorous thing that lives underneath courage and keeps it fed. "It's waiting. There's a difference. Expecting means it thinks it knows what happens next."

He picked up his pack. He looked, for one moment, like a taster again — a boy trained to find the poison in the sweet, the lie in the loving, the clause in the contract that nobody was meant to read.

"And I," he said, "am done letting anyone decide that for me."
