## 14. The Gate Beneath the Root

Odile had always believed that if you walked far enough north in winter, you would eventually walk off the edge of the world. Her grandmother had said so, back when Odile was small enough to believe grandmothers knew things. *The north is where the map gives up, little bird. Past that, you're on your own.*

She was on her own now, all right. On her own with a runaway taster who catalogued flavors the way other men catalogued sins, and a kitchen boy who had spent three days walking with his head tilted like a hen listening for worms.

"Left," Tam said, for the fourth time that morning.

"You said left an hour ago," Odile said.

"It's still left. It's very left. The mountain is extremely insistent about left."

Xanthe, trudging ahead through the knee-deep snow, didn't turn around. "If the mountain is so insistent, maybe it can carry our packs."

"The mountain," Tam said solemnly, "does not discuss luggage."

Odile snorted, and her breath came out in a white flag of surrender. Three days since the shrine where Xanthe had shouted *no* at a god's voice and lived, and the boy still walked like someone who hadn't quite decided whether to be terrified or proud. She recognized the look. She'd worn it herself for eleven years, ever since the night she'd failed her sister.

The land had given up pretending to be farmland somewhere behind them. Now it was only frost-killed orchards and stone fences buried to their chins, and the mountain itself rising ahead like a judgment nobody wanted to read aloud. The snow here fell without wind, straight down, patient, as though the sky were sifting flour over a finished loaf.

"There," Xanthe said suddenly, and stopped.

Odile followed his pointing finger. Ahead, alone in a field of white, stood a tree.

It was dead — had been dead so long it wasn't even gray anymore but black, glossy black, like something carved from obsidian by a sculptor with more patience than kindness. Its branches spread bare against the pale sky, and not one flake of snow rested on them. Around its base, the snow lay smooth and unbroken, a perfect circle, as if the ground itself had decided this spot was none of its business.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Tam whispered.

"Trees don't move," Odile said.

"This one did." Tam's voice had gone strange and small. "It walked here. I heard it. Roots don't make much noise when they want to be quiet."

Xanthe looked at Odile. In the week since the cellar, she had learned to read his silences the way she'd once read weather: the set of his mouth meant *I don't trust this*, the lift of his chin meant *I don't care*. Right now both were happening at once, which was, she was coming to understand, simply his face.

"Well," he said. "We came to find a door under a root. There's a root."

"There's a door under *this* root?" Odile asked. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain the mountain has been shouting at Tam for a week and hasn't lied to him yet. That's more certainty than I've had about anything since the stars went out."

That was fair. Odile hitched her pack higher and led the way across the unbroken snow, because someone had to go first and she'd been going first since before either of these boys could walk. The cold deepened as they approached the tree — not the ordinary cold of winter, which at least had the decency to be honest about wanting to kill you, but a colder cold, an attentive cold, like walking into a room where someone had been discussing your name.

Up close, the petrified wood wasn't smooth. It was covered in fine carving, so old and so fine that at first glance it looked like grain, like bark, like nothing. But when Odile dragged off her mitten and touched it, her fingertips found letters. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Names, she realized. Names running up the trunk in columns, spiraling around the branches, names of every hand and age, some sharp as yesterday and some worn nearly to whispers.

"Debts," she breathed. "These are debts. Every name anyone ever owed the Undercourt."

"How do you know?" Xanthe asked.

"Because my sister's name is here." She found it without looking — she'd have known it blindfolded, in the dark, in her sleep. *Maren Yarrow*, third branch from the left, cut shallow, the way names got cut when the debt was never collected. Her throat closed around itself. "Cut but not paid. They keep the receipts."

Xanthe put his hand over hers on the bark. He didn't say anything. He'd learned, somewhere in the last hundred miles, that Odile didn't need comfort spoken aloud; she needed it witnessed. It was one of the thousand small ways he was nothing like the court that raised him.

Tam was circling the tree, head cocked. "It says we're expected," he reported. "It says—" he stopped, frowning. "It says the door doesn't like being kept waiting. It's been ready since midsummer."

"Midsummer," Odile repeated flatly. "The door has been sitting under a tree that didn't exist yet, waiting for us, since midsummer."

"I'm just telling you what it says."

Under the lowest root — a root thick as a church beam, arched up from the frozen ground like the rib of some buried giant — there was darkness. And in the darkness, a door.

It was smaller than Odile had expected. Smaller than any door had a right to be, really: barely shoulder-high, made of the same black glass-wood as the tree, with no handle and no hinge she could see. Set into its center at eye height was a round window of cloudy crystal, and behind the crystal something moved. Not light. Not shadow. Something patient.

Words surfaced on the door's surface the way names had surfaced on the trunk — rising slowly through the black wood like bubbles through tar:

***WHAT ARE YOU OWED?***

They all three read it. Then they all three looked at each other.

"It's asking what we're owed," Xanthe said unnecessarily.

"Yes, thank you, taster, my eyes work."

"It's a toll," Tam said. His voice had gone wobbly. "Like Grayford. Like the ferryman said — arrive owing nothing or owning everything. If we say we're owed something, we're beggars, and the court despises beggars."

"And if we lie?" Odile asked.

Tam tilted his head, listening. His expression did something complicated. "It'll know. It always knows. That's sort of the whole point of doors down there, apparently. They're not locks. They're questions."

Odile looked at the door a long moment. Behind the cloudy crystal, the patient thing moved again, and this time she could almost see it: a shape leaning close, the way a clerk leans over a ledger.

*What are you owed.* What a question to ask her. What a cruel, clever, perfectly shaped question. Because Odile Yarrow had a list. Oh, she had a list long enough to wrap around this tree twice. She was owed an apology from a king who let hedge-witches rot in cellars for selling feverfew to scullery maids. She was owed eleven years back. She was owed Maren — Maren's laugh, Maren's terrible singing, Maren's soul walking free instead of cut shallow into a dead tree like a debt half-forgiven.

And beneath all of it, older than all of it, she was owed an answer to the only question that had kept her walking north through a starless winter: *why couldn't I save her?*

The door waited. The crystal clouded and cleared, clouded and cleared, like an eye blinking.

*Owing nothing,* the ferryman had said. *Or owning everything.* And she'd thought she understood him. She'd thought it was a riddle about coins.

But standing here, she understood it differently. The door wasn't asking what she wanted. It was asking what she believed she was entitled to. And there was a difference — a wide, cold, door-shaped difference — between wanting a thing and believing the world owed it to you. Wanting Maren back was love. Demanding Maren back was a bargain, and bargains were how souls got sold in the first place. Her sister had died because someone, somewhere, had decided the world owed *them* something.

Odile had spent eleven years keeping a ledger of her own grievances. She knew exactly how heavy a ledger got. She'd watched the Queen Beneath the Mountain strangle on hers.

"No," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "Nothing."

The word came out steadier than she felt. She stepped up to the door, laid her palm flat against the black wood, and said it clearly, the way you'd swear an oath:

"I am owed nothing. Everything I want, I'll ask for. Everything I've lost, I'll grieve. But no one owes me. Not the world. Not the mountain. Not even you."

The crystal cleared. For one heartbeat Odile saw through it — saw warmth, gold light, a cavern hung with candles like a sky that had been taught to hold still — and then the door swung inward without a sound, opening onto a stairway of living root, spiraling down into amber dark.

Warm air rose up out of it and broke over her face like water. After weeks of cold, it nearly brought her to her knees. It smelled of beeswax and bread and something green and growing, impossibly, miles underground.

"One," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere, dry as paper turning. "Two to come."

* * *

Xanthe stood before the door longer than either of them had. Odile watched him from the threshold, watching him watch the question, and she thought: *he's reading it like a contract.* Of course he was. He'd been raised on contracts he couldn't see, fed by hands that signed things over his head. He would not answer until he understood every clause.

"What are you owed," he read aloud, slowly, tasting the words the way he tasted everything. "Hm. Trick question."

"How's it a trick?"

"Because everyone's owed something. That's what makes it a trick. Ask a man what he's owed and he'll start counting, and once a man starts counting he can't stop." He glanced back at her, and there was something almost playful in it, thin and tired but real. "The steward in Vell used to say I was owed my keep. The king used to say I was owed my place at table. Everyone who ever held me told me what I was owed, and funny thing — it was always exactly what they wanted to give me, and never anything more."

"So what's your answer?"

He turned back to the door. When he spoke, it wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. The whole tree leaned in to hear it; Odile felt the branches shift above them like a held breath.

"I was owed a mother. I've stopped collecting." A pause. "I'm owed nothing. I'm carrying everything I own, and half of it belongs to these two anyway."

The crystal cleared. Through it, gold light spilled across the snow, and Odile heard Tam make a small sound of wonder. The door opened wide — wider than it had for her, wide enough for three — and the warm air rolled out and out, and somewhere far below, faint as memory, bells began to ring.

"Two," said the dry voice. "Three to come."

* * *

Tam wouldn't go near it.

He stood ten feet back in the snow with his arms wrapped around himself, and when Odile went to him he flinched from her touch, which he had never done, not once, not even the night in the wine cellar when she'd pulled him out from behind the flour barrels by the collar.

"I can hear it better now," he whispered. "Now that the door's open. It's not whispering anymore. It's—" His face crumpled. "It's singing, Odile. It's been singing my name for a week and I thought it was the wind and it's *singing*, and it sounds kind. That's the worst part. It sounds so kind."

Odile crouched in the snow so their eyes were level. Tam was fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and flour dust, and he'd crossed a kingdom on whispers because the whispers promised him safety, and she had never once hated him for it. Fear made fools of everyone. It had made a smuggler of her, a liar, a cheat at tollhouses. The only difference between Tam and the rest of them was that his fear talked to him by name.

"Listen to me," she said. "That voice sang for me too, once. Different words, same tune. It promised me passage to my sister if I carried thornroot through the wards, and I did it, and you know what I got? A cellar and a chain and eleven years of walking. Kind voices that ask you to carry things are the most expensive voices in the world."

"But it knows my name."

"Maren's name is carved on that tree." She nodded at the black branches. "So is mine, probably, by now. Knowing your name isn't love, Tam. It's bookkeeping."

He laughed — a wet, surprised, broken little laugh — and wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Bookkeeping."

"Worst ledger in creation. Now." She stood and offered him her hand. "Xanthe shouted *no* at a god for you. The least you can do is walk down some stairs."

Tam looked at her hand. Then past her, at the open door, at the gold light lying warm on the snow like something spilled. Then at Xanthe, who had come back to stand beside them both, saying nothing, doing what he did — witnessing.

"If I go down there," Tam said slowly, "and the voice stops..."

"Then you'll finally get some sleep," Odile said.

"And if it gets louder?"

Xanthe answered that one. "Then it gets louder with witnesses. Nobody faces anything alone anymore. That's the rule now. We made it up about a hundred miles back and nobody objected."

Tam took her hand. Together the three of them crossed the last stretch of snow — and Odile noticed, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, that their footprints ended at the threshold. Not faded. Ended. As if the ground behind them had already begun forgetting they'd ever walked on it.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should.

They wound inside the root itself, the walls around them warm and faintly luminous, veined with gold like amber holding ancient light. Odile counted two hundred steps and gave up counting. The air grew warmer, sweeter, thick with beeswax and baking bread and, underneath it, something mineral and deep, the smell of the mountain's own heart. Their shadows stretched long and soft behind them, cast by a light source that never appeared.

"Is anyone else hungry?" Tam asked after a while, apologetic. "Like, desperately hungry? Is that a trap?"

"It's the bread smell," Xanthe said. "Professional opinion. Someone down there is baking, and they're good at it, and it's not a trap, it's just cruelty."

"Gentle cruelty," Odile said. "Everything down here is going to be gentle. That's what worries me."

The stair opened without warning into a gallery, and Odile stopped so fast Tam walked into her back.

It was a cavern, but *cavern* was too small a word, the way *candle* was too small a word for a star. The space soared up beyond sight, and it was lit — lit by thousands upon thousands of candles, floating or perched or dripping from ledges of black glass, each flame steady as a held breath, each one burning a different color: honey-gold, ember-red, one or two a blue so pale it hurt. And among the candles, filling the cavern like a congregation, stood shelves. Shelves and shelves and shelves, rising into the dark, and on the shelves—

"Ledgers," Xanthe breathed. "It's all ledgers."

Not just ledgers. Odile squinted, and her stomach turned over gently. Jars. Little glass jars, glowing softly from within, each with a paper label in a spidery hand. Souls, shelved like preserves. Somewhere on those endless shelves was a jar labeled *Maren Yarrow*. Somewhere was one labeled *Xanthe Reyes*, probably already prepared, waiting for midwinter like a cake waiting for a birthday.

A figure approached along the gallery floor — a clerk, robed in gray, with spectacles and a quill tucked behind one ear and the patient, tired face of someone who had worked the same desk for four hundred years. It looked at the three of them without surprise.

"Walk-ins," it said, in the dry paper voice from the door. "Unscheduled. Unbargained." It made a note on a slate. "Her Majesty will be *delighted*. She does so hate an appointment."

"We're here about a debt," Xanthe said. "One specific entry. Signed thirty years ago. I want to see the original contract."

The clerk's quill paused. Over its spectacles, its eyes — small, brown, unexpectedly human — moved from Xanthe to Odile to Tam, and something flickered across its face that Odile would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It took her a moment, and then she had it, and the having of it frightened her more than the jars ever could.

It was pity.

"Everyone who comes down here wants to see a contract," the clerk said mildly, turning to lead them deeper into the candlelight. "Do you know how many have ever read theirs all the way through? To the last line? To the signature at the bottom?" It glanced back, and the pity was still there, folded small, like a letter never sent. "No. Neither do I. But I've often wondered what would happen if someone did."

Ahead, the candles thinned, and the light turned the color of a dying queen's last afternoon, and the bells began to ring again — closer now, and lower, like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue.

"Come along," said the clerk. "And mind the third step. It remembers everyone who's ever hesitated on it, and it likes to mention names."

The third step, Odile noted, was already murmuring as they approached. Softly. Kindly.

Sounding, all the while, exactly like her sister.
