## 15. The Court of Small Debts

The door had swallowed them politely.

That was the only word Xanthe could find for it. The petrified tree's root-gate had opened onto a stair that went down and down and down, spiraling through stone so old it had stopped being rock and become something more like memory, and the whole time he'd had the distinct impression of being *received*—the way a good house receives guests, taking their coats, lighting their way—rather than merely admitted.

Now the stairs ended, and Xanthe understood why the warmth below had felt like a held breath.

The cavern opened around them like the inside of a bell. It was vast—he couldn't see the ceiling, only a darkness above the candlelight where the dark thickened—and yet it was not empty in the way mountains are empty. Every surface had been made useful. Shelves climbed the walls in tiers, hundreds of feet of them, crowded with ledgers bound in leather gone soft as cloth. Candles burned everywhere, thousands of them, stuck to iron sconces and wax-drip ledges and even, here and there, balanced on the spines of books themselves, and none of them guttered, and none of them melted, as if fire down here had agreed to behave.

And between the shelves, at desks of black wood, sat the clerks.

There were dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. They were roughly human-shaped, which was the kindest thing Xanthe could say about them, and they wore grey robes and small round spectacles that sat on faces like suggestions of faces—features present but uncommitted, as though each clerk had been issued a face long ago and hadn't seen much use for it since. Each one bent over a ledger with a quill that scratched in unison with all the others, so that the whole cavern sounded like rain on a roof, or like a thousand moths against a window.

"Souls," Odile said quietly. "They're weighing souls."

She was looking at the center of the cavern, where great brass scales stood on marble plinths, each one tall as a hay wagon. In one pan of the nearest scale sat something Xanthe's eyes kept sliding off—a shimmer, a weight, a grief-shaped nothing. In the other pan sat weights. Small ones, dull ones, stamped with numbers.

A clerk beside the scale turned a page, consulted it, moved a weight the size of a thumbnail from pan to pan, and nodded to itself with tremendous satisfaction.

"They're *coins*," Tam whispered. "They weigh them like coins." His voice cracked in the middle. Down here, without the mountain's whispers filling his skull, he looked younger and lost, a boy holding a lantern in a cathedral built by strangers. "Xanthe. What if my mother's down here? What if she's been sitting in a pan this whole time?"

"We'll look," Xanthe said. "We'll look for everyone we can. But first—" He touched his own chest, where his heart was doing something complicated and frightened. "First I need to find out what I'm worth."

* * *

Approaching a clerk turned out to have rules.

The first desk they approached belonged to a clerk who did not look up, so Odile tried the second, who also did not look up, and the third, who looked up with such suddenness that Tam yelped and dropped his lantern.

"You're living," said the third clerk. Its voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning. "Living persons queue."

"There's no line," Odile said.

"There is always a line. You simply cannot see it." The clerk gestured with its quill at a stretch of empty floor. "Stand there, please. Take a ticket."

"I don't want a ticket," Odile began, "I want—"

"Everyone wants something. That is rather the point of the establishment." The clerk produced, from somewhere in its robes, a small wooden token stamped with the number forty-one, and held it out. When nobody took it, it waggled the token impatiently. "Take the ticket. Argue while you wait. It passes the time, and time is the one thing we have too much of."

Xanthe took the ticket. The wood was warm, like something recently held by a living hand, and the number forty-one was branded into it with a little flourish, as though someone down here took pride in their numerals.

They stood in the invisible line. Around them the quills scratched. Somewhere far off, a bell rang once, softly, and three separate clerks sighed in unison.

"This is the most terrifying place I've ever been," Tam said, "and I grew up next to the scullery drain."

"It's a bureaucracy," Odile said. She sounded almost fond, in the way of someone recognizing an enemy. "My sister used to say you could get anything out of a bureaucracy if you had patience and the right paperwork. She got us out of a tithe dispute with the miller using a forged writ and two hours of complaining." Her voice caught on the word *sister*, just slightly, and smoothed itself over. "We find her ledger first. Then yours. Ledgers mean records, and records mean rules, and rules—" she smiled, thin and sharp, "—rules cut both ways."

Forty-one people ahead of them, Xanthe thought, watching the scales swing. Forty-one debts being settled, or argued, or mourned. A woman's shade stood at one desk arguing passionately with a clerk about a loom she insisted she'd been owed; the clerk kept tapping a ledger entry that said, apparently, otherwise. A man's shade stood at another desk saying nothing at all, hands folded, while the clerk read him a list. He nodded after each item. Whatever was on the list, he'd agreed to it long ago.

Nobody screamed down here. That was somehow worse than screaming. Everything was quiet, and orderly, and patient, the way a debt is patient.

"Next," said a voice, and it was theirs.

* * *

The clerk assigned to them introduced itself as Clerk Second Grade Vess, Records, and asked how it could be of service in the tone of someone who hoped very much that it couldn't.

"We're looking for a ledger entry," Xanthe said. "A soul sold before the person was born. Roughly twenty-two years ago. The seller would have been—" his throat tightened— "her name was Maren Reyes. Of Vell. Of the valley above."

Something happened to Vess's face then. Not an expression exactly—the face didn't seem built for expressions—but a kind of stilling, the way water stills when something large passes underneath.

"Reyes," the clerk repeated. "Maren Reyes." It opened a drawer, considered, closed it. Opened a different drawer. "You are aware, living person, that entries concerning the living are restricted?"

"I'm aware," Xanthe said, "that the entry concerns me."

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Down here, in the candlelight, Xanthe became aware that his shadow behaved oddly—it leaned toward the clerk instead of away from the candles, attentive, like a dog scenting kin.

"The taster," Vess said. "The valley child. Yes. We've been expecting your inquiry for some years." It rose from its desk with a rustle of grey robes. "Follow me. Do not touch the shelves. Do not blow out any candles, no matter how politely they ask."

"Do candles ask?" Tam whispered.

"Constantly," said Vess, and led them into the stacks.

* * *

The shelves went back farther than the cavern should have allowed. Xanthe counted his steps and lost count somewhere past four hundred, in an aisle where the candles burned blue instead of gold and the ledgers were bound in bark instead of leather. Vess walked with the certainty of something that had walked these aisles since before aisles were invented, trailing one grey finger along the shelf edges, reading spines by touch.

"Maren Reyes," it murmured. "Maren, Maren. Sold for warmth. One winter's warmth, contracted against—" The finger stopped. "Ah."

It drew out a ledger no bigger than a prayer book, bound in leather the color of dried blood, and carried it to a small reading table where a single candle waited, already lit, as though it had known they were coming. Which, Xanthe supposed, it probably had.

"Here." Vess set the book down but kept one grey hand flat upon the cover. "I will show you the entry. I will not show you more than the entry. There is a page following that you are not authorized to read, and I will thank you to keep your eyes on the authorized portion, because the unauthorized portion has a way of reading *itself* to people, and then we must file an incident report, and nobody wants that."

"What happens in an incident report?" Tam asked.

"Paperwork," said Vess, with genuine horror.

It opened the book.

The entry was written in brown-black ink in a fine, careful hand, and Xanthe knew the hand at once—not because he'd ever seen his mother write, but because he'd spent his life tasting things and knowing what went into them, and this handwriting had the same quality. Care. Attention. Someone had written this slowly, choosing every letter, the way a cook chooses every grain of salt.

**Entry 9,114. Maren Reyes, of the valley of Vell.**

**Debt received: one winter of warmth, rendered in full, per contract. The debtor was cold, and poor, and pregnant, and the winter was killing her. We warmed her hearth from first frost to thaw. She lived.**

**Payment due: one soul, hers, at death.**

**Paid: —**

Xanthe stared at that last line. *Paid: —*. A dash. An emptiness. His mother had died owing, and the debt had never been collected, and he felt hope rise in him like a bubble in deep water—

But Vess turned the page.

**Note, appended: the debtor died before payment. Per standing law, unpaid debts pass to issue. The debt of Maren Reyes transfers to her child, born and unborn at signing, payable in kind—**

"In kind," Odile read aloud, fast, hungry. "In kind means the same thing. Her soul's debt, paid with her soul. If her soul's gone—if it was never collected—then maybe there's nothing left to collect—"

"—or in the child," Vess finished, in its dry paper voice.

The words sat on the page in the same fine hand. The same care. The same attention. And beneath them, in smaller script, almost squeezed into the margin as if the writer had hoped the mountain wouldn't notice:

***Signed, Maren Reyes, who was warm when she wrote this, and asks whatever reads this someday to know she was warm, and that she would have signed colder terms than these to stay so, and that if her child comes asking, tell the child the winter was long and the soup was good and she loved him before she could name him.***

Xanthe did not cry. He was a taster; he had been trained since childhood to keep his face still while his mouth reported catastrophe. But his face was still the way a frozen lake is still, and everything under it was moving.

"She loved me," he said. It came out steady. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride, and mostly he just held the sentence like a coal.

"She loved you," Vess agreed. It had the grace, at least, not to make it sound like pity. "She also, and I say this as a neutral party of some centuries' standing, signed a truly dreadful contract. Warmth for a soul is the oldest bad bargain in the world. Half our shelves are full of it." The clerk tapped the margin note with one grey finger. "However."

"However," Odile breathed.

"However. You were shown this page because you asked after it, and the court honors inquiry. And you have now read the clause." Vess closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping person. "*Payable in kind, or in the child.* Two terms. One debt. A debt may be satisfied by either term—but it cannot be *redefined* once collection begins. That is not mercy. That is arithmetic. We are very good at arithmetic."

"So if the debt is collected in kind," Xanthe said slowly, working it through the way he'd work through an unfamiliar flavor, "the child term dies. And if it's collected in the child—"

"Then the Queen collects *you*," Vess said, "and your mother's soul remains ours regardless, because the dash in that ledger, living person, means what dashes always mean. Nothing was ever paid. Your mother's soul is still in our keeping. It has been on the shelf for twenty-two years." The clerk adjusted its spectacles, which did not need adjusting. "You should know that it is a well-kept soul. We do not let them spoil. It sits in the third tier, aisle nine, in a jar labeled with her name, and twice a year a clerk dusts the shelf."

Tam made a small sound. Odile put her hand on his shoulder without looking away from the ledger.

"And the Queen," Xanthe said. "Which term does she intend?"

Vess was quiet for a moment. Around them the blue candles burned, and somewhere in the deep of the stacks, faintly, a bell rang, and clerks sighed.

"The Queen," Vess said at last, "intends to collect in the child. She announced it this morning. She is dying, you understand—dying in a way queens are not permitted to die, slowly, with all her debts called in around her—and she has decided that your soul fits the weight she needs. The clerks were instructed to prepare the transfer." It paused. "Three of us declined. We are filing grievances."

"Grievances," Odile repeated.

"It is all we have," Vess said, "and it is not nothing. Down here, grievance is a weapon. Ask anyone on these shelves." It straightened, and gathered the ledger to its chest, and for a moment its suggested face seemed, very faintly, to suggest something like resolve. "The audience is granted. You will be brought before Her Majesty within the hour. I am required to warn you that she will offer you a bargain—that is what she does, it is what the whole court does, it is the air we breathe—and I am permitted, as a neutral party, to add one observation off the record."

"Say it," Xanthe said.

Vess leaned close. Up close it smelled of dust and candle smoke and, unexpectedly, of thornroot—faint, old, familiar, the smell of the honey cake that had started all of this.

"Her Majesty has never once, in ten thousand years, had a debtor refuse to bargain," the clerk whispered. "The court does not know what happens then. Neither do I. But the arithmetic, living person—" its spectacles glinted, "—the arithmetic says that a court built on honoring debts cannot survive the first debt honored *wrong*. Read everything. Sign nothing. And when you stand before her—"

Above them, far off through miles of stone, a bell began to ring. Not softly this time. All the candles in the stacks flared at once, gold and blue, and every clerk in the Undercourt rose from its desk in a rustle like wind through wheat.

"—be quick," Vess finished. "She's calling for you now."

* * *

They came for him with lanterns and grey robes and perfect courtesy, the way you come for a man who is already owned, and Xanthe walked between them with his mother's handwriting folded safe behind his ribs—*she loved him before she could name him*—while Odile gripped his sleeve and Tam pressed close on his other side, and the scales swung in the dark behind them, weighing, weighing, weighing.

At the far end of the cavern, doors of black root stood open on a light that was not candlelight, and from beyond them came a voice like the mountain learning to speak again.

"Bring me the taster," said the Queen Beneath the Mountain. "Let us discuss what he is owed."
