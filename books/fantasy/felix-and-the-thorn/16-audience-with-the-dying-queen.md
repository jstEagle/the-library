## 16. Audience with the Dying Queen

They came for him at what Xanthe guessed was morning, though morning had stopped meaning anything hours—or days—ago. Time beneath the mountain moved like honey in winter: slow, thick, and impossible to pour.

The clerks who fetched him were small gray creatures with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that sat crooked on faces like crumpled parchment. They did not speak. They simply appeared at the door of the candlelit room where he'd been kept—warm, comfortable, and unmistakably a cell, however kindly furnished—and gestured for him to follow.

Xanthe went. What else was there to do? He'd spent his whole life being led places by people who held power over him. At least these ones didn't pretend it was kindness.

The corridors of the Undercourt wound downward through stone that had been worn smooth by centuries of careful feet. Candles floated past them at shoulder height, unhurried, as if they too had business elsewhere. Once, they passed an open doorway where a clerk sat weighing something on brass scales—a small grey shimmer that might have been a memory or a Tuesday—and Xanthe's stomach tightened. Somewhere in this mountain was a ledger with his mother's name in it. He'd found the entry two days ago. *Sold for one winter of warmth. Payable in kind, or in the child.* He'd read the clause three times before his hands stopped shaking enough to fold the page closed.

*In kind,* meant her soul, already collected. *Or in the child* meant him.

He'd been turning the clause over ever since, looking for the gap in it the way he used to look for poison in a king's supper. A taster learned early that every dish had a seam somewhere—the place where the cook got lazy, where the ingredient didn't quite match its smell. Find the seam, and you knew whether to swallow or spit.

He hadn't found the seam yet. But he was looking.

The corridor ended at doors taller than any he'd seen above ground, black wood banded with silver, carved all over with names so old the letters had worn into suggestion. The clerks stopped. One of them produced a small bell from somewhere inside its robe and rang it once—a soundless ring, Xanthe felt rather than heard, a pressure behind his eyes.

The doors opened on their own.

Beyond them was a cavern vast enough to hold weather. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and between them, suspended in the air at the center of everything, burned a single point of light.

It took Xanthe a moment to understand what he was looking at, because he had spent twenty years learning flavors and only moments learning wonder. It was a star. One star, hanging alone in the dark, and around it, arranged on tiers of stone like courtiers around a throne, were hundreds more—dimmer, guttering, some no bigger than embers—but stars. The stolen stars. Every light that had vanished from the sky on the night the wards fell, gathered here like coins in a miser's vault.

And beneath them, on a throne of black root-wood grown up through the cavern floor, sat the Queen Beneath the Mountain.

She was dying.

Xanthe knew death when he saw it—not the dramatic kind, the battlefield kind, but the slow kind, the kitchen kind. He'd watched it take three tasters before him, one spoonful of arsenic at a time, each of them going grayer and quieter until one morning their porridge went uneaten. The Queen had that same look. Her skin was the color of candle-wax near its end, translucent, with a faint glow underneath that flickered when she breathed. Her hair had gone white not with age but with *thinness*, as though she were slowly becoming transparent all the way through. Where her hands rested on the arms of the throne, he could see the stone through her fingers.

She was holding herself together with stolen light. That was the thought that arrived whole, uninvited, the way the worst truths always do. All those stars—all that vanished sky—pulled down into the dark and hoarded here, and for what? Not power. Not conquest. To keep one dying woman lit a little longer, the way you might feed a fire stick by stick knowing the woodpile is finite.

"Xanthe Reyes," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and distant bells. "Taster of Vell. Son of Marisol Reyes, who signed."

"You have my mother's contract," Xanthe said. "I've read it."

"I know you've read it." Something moved across her face that might have been amusement, in a court where amusement had probably been rationed for centuries. "You read it the way your mother read things. Twice through, then once more slowly, hunting the lie. She never found hers either."

"My mother is dead."

"Your mother is *paid*. There is a difference, down here." The Queen lifted one translucent hand, and the nearest stars dimmed slightly, as if leaning toward her. "She bought a winter of warmth with what she had left to sell. The winter was delivered. The debt stands. Debts are the only law I keep, taster, and the only law that has ever kept me."

"Then let me pay it," Xanthe said. "That's why I came. Name the price."

The Queen studied him for a long moment. Up close, her eyes were unsettling—they had no whites at all, just deep grey going darker at the center, like wells. "Do you know what I expected? When the wards fell and the valley lay open, I expected a king's men. Chains, perhaps. A delivery, wrapped and weeping. Instead I get a taster who walks in the front door and asks for the bill." She smiled, and her teeth glowed faintly, which was somehow worse than fangs would have been. "Your mother would be proud. Or furious. With her it was usually both."

"Name the price," Xanthe repeated.

"The price is paid," the Queen said softly. "That is what you have never understood, child of the bargain. The clause reads *in kind, or in the child*, and your mother's soul was weighed the night she died—it came up light. Souls always come up light when they've been spent keeping someone else alive. So the ledger balances short, and the shortfall must be made up." She spread her hands, and through them the throne's carvings showed like bones through skin. "The ledger wants weight. Your soul, weighed against hers, carries the difference. That is arithmetic, not cruelty. I did not write the numbers. I only keep them."

"Then I'll stay. Weigh me. Keep me. Whatever the ledger needs."

"Generous," the Queen murmured. "Foolish, but generous. No." She rose from the throne, and the motion cost her—Xanthe saw the effort of it, the way a very old person rising from a chair shows you exactly how much time they have left. She descended the steps toward him, trailing faint light like a dress hem dragging through snow. "I am dying, taster. Do you understand what that means, for one such as me? Courts of the dead do not die. We fade. We thin. And when we go, everything we hold goes with us—every debt, every ledger, every soul in my keeping, scattered like ash. Including your mother's. Including, if I fall before midwinter, any chance you ever had of buying it back."

"So the stars—"

"Are medicine," she said simply. "Each one a year, more or less. I have been taking my medicine for a long time now, and the prescription is running out." She stopped three paces from him, close enough that he could feel the strange cold warmth of her, like standing near an oven door in winter. "Which brings me to my offer. You wanted a price. Here is one you can afford."

Xanthe waited. In the kitchens of Vell he had learned that the most dangerous words were never the loud ones. They were the reasonable ones, spoken quietly, by people who had already done the math.

"Tam," the Queen said.

The name landed in Xanthe's chest like a dropped knife.

"He is below even now. You know this. His soul fits the ledger's weight precisely—I have measured it, as I measure everything. A boy who hears mountains, born under a falling sky, unclaimed by any family, unblessed by any god. The clerks call such souls *clean stock*. They fit anywhere. Fill the shortfall with him, and the ledger closes, and your mother rests, and you walk out of my mountain free, owing nothing, owning everything, exactly as the ferryman told you to arrive." She tilted her head. "One kitchen boy. You've known him a season. Against a mother you never met, whose face you cannot remember, whose soul you crossed a kingdom in winter to save."

"That's not—" Xanthe started.

"A bargain?" The Queen smiled again. "It is the oldest bargain there is. The one your own king tried to make, in his way—spend the expendable boy, save the precious thing. I am offering you better terms than he did. He wanted to bind your soul to his throne forever. I am offering you freedom. Actual freedom. Walk away clean. Most people who stand where you are standing say yes, eventually. They simply need a moment to arrange their consciences first." She gestured, and a small table slid into being beside them—stone flowing upward like water deciding to be furniture. On it: a single sheet of paper, a quill, an inkpot of something dark and glittering. "Take your time. Read it twice, then once more slowly. Hunt the lie. I'm told that's how your family does things."

Xanthe looked at the paper. He didn't touch it.

His mind, traitorous and practical, was already doing what it always did: cataloguing. *Tam, sixteen, laughs with his whole body, hums while he works, was so frightened on the frozen river that he held Odile's sleeve the entire crossing and pretended afterward it hadn't happened. Tam, who heard the mountain whispering and walked toward it because he thought maybe, finally, something wanted him.* Tam, who had been taken in the night while Xanthe slept ten feet away, and who had probably called out for them, and—

No. Stop. Catalogue the *dish*, not the boy.

He made himself look at the offer the way he'd looked at the poisoned honey cake. What was actually on the plate?

A soul traded for a soul. Clean arithmetic. And underneath it, the seam—the place where the cook got lazy. Because the Queen had said *the ledger wants weight*, but she had also said *I did not write the numbers, I only keep them*. And a keeper who only keeps, who claims no authorship, who presents herself as servant to the arithmetic—that was a creature with an escape built into every sentence. Everything wrong with this room was somebody else's signature.

Also: she was dying. She had shown him her hands. She had let him see the stone through her fingers. A queen secure in her power did not show a stranger her weakness within the first hundred words. Unless the weakness was the argument. Unless the whole audience was staged to make him feel the clock ticking, to make *yes* feel like mercy instead of murder.

"You're frightened," Xanthe said quietly.

The well-deep eyes fixed on him. For a moment—one long, dangerous moment—the cavern seemed to lean in. The floating candles stilled. Even the stars seemed to stop their slow guttering.

"I am *dying*," the Queen said, and for the first time her voice cracked along its length, dry leaves tearing. "There is a difference. Fear is for creatures with futures."

"Everyone down here keeps saying that," Xanthe said. "The ferryman. The clerks. Debts, debts, debts, and nobody ever asks who set the prices." He nodded at the paper on the table. "You want me to sign Tam's name into my mother's ledger. But I've read enough contracts in the last few days to know that whoever signs second does the paying. My mother signed. Look how that ended. I'm not signing anything."

"Then the debt falls due at midwinter regardless," the Queen said, "and the ledger takes what it takes. Unweighted debts collect themselves, taster. It will not be gentle. It will not choose. It will simply *take*, the way winter takes, the way—" She stopped. Her translucent hand had drifted to her own chest, absently, the way people touch a pain they've stopped noticing.

"The way you're taking the stars," Xanthe finished.

Silence, then. Long silence, filled only with the soft sound of guttering light.

"You think I don't understand wanting to live," Xanthe said, softer now. "I was sold to the crown when I was six years old. Do you know what they told me? That being useful was the same as being safe. That if I was good enough, accurate enough, valuable enough, they'd keep me. I believed it for fourteen years. I tasted every supper like my life depended on it, because it did. And the whole time, the thing I was actually doing was paying someone else's debt without ever seeing the bill." He looked up at her, and found—to his surprise—that he wasn't afraid anymore. He was tired, and sad, and certain. "You're not evil. I don't think you're even cruel. You're just scared, and powerful enough that nobody's ever told you no. I know what that does to a person. I served one for fourteen years."

Something flickered across the Queen's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed.

"Careful," she said.

"I mean it as a compliment," Xanthe said. "Mostly."

The Queen turned away from him. She ascended the throne steps slowly, each one costing her, and settled back among the stolen stars like a woman sinking into a bath that wasn't warm enough. For a while she said nothing at all, and the clerks by the great doors shifted their weight, and somewhere far off in the caverns a bell tolled a note Xanthe felt in his back teeth.

"Midwinter," she said finally. "The debt comes due at midwinter, signed or unsigned. You have until then to find your loophole, taster. Hunt your seam. But know this—" and here she leaned forward, and the light of the nearest star caught the wells of her eyes, "—if midwinter arrives and the ledger stands short, it will collect. From whoever it chooses. Weight is weight. I will not—cannot—stop it. And the boy is still below, and the clerks are still measuring him, and arithmetic is patient."

"And if I find the seam?"

"Then you will be the first," the Queen said, "in nine hundred years, to beat the house." She almost smiled. Almost. "Go. The clerks will show you where you may wait. Perhaps bring your witch. She strikes me as someone who reads fine print for pleasure."

Xanthe bowed—because apparently his body still remembered court manners even when his mind had stopped respecting them—and turned for the doors.

"Taster."

He paused.

"Your mother," the Queen said, and her voice had gone quiet, almost private, "did not sign because she feared death. She signed because she had a child coming and no firewood. I have weighed ten thousand souls, and hers was the lightest I have ever held, and the cleanest." A pause. "Do not let them tell you she was foolish. She was *broke*, and she loved you. Those are different failures."

Xanthe stood very still for a moment, throat tight, tasting something he had no word for—grief and gratitude in the same mouthful, bitter and sweet at once, like thornroot hidden in honey.

"Thank you," he managed.

"Do not thank me," the Queen said, waving one fading hand. "I am about to steal your friend. Thank me after midwinter, if you beat the house."

The great doors closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The clerks led him back through corridors that seemed longer on the return, past the weighing rooms, past a chamber where a hundred candles burned in rows like a classroom awaiting students. They installed him in a small round room with a low ceiling, a brazier of coals that gave heat but no smoke, and a window of thick glassy stone looking out onto—nothing. Dark rock. The window was a courtesy, not a view.

Odile was waiting inside. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor with her boots off, feeding something small and invisible from her palm, and she sprang up the moment he entered, searching his face with the sharp, practiced eye of a woman who had spent years reading people to survive them.

"Well?" she demanded. "What did she want?"

"To make me an offer." Xanthe sat down heavily on the room's single bench. His legs had decided, somewhere during the audience, that they were done pretending to be steady. "My freedom for Tam."

Odile went very still. "And you said—"

"No. Obviously no."

"*Obviously*," Odile repeated, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, cracked, relieved sound—and sat down beside him, shoulder pressed to his shoulder. "Gods. You absolute idiot. You beautiful, stubborn idiot. Do you know what most people would have done?"

"Yes," Xanthe said. "That's the problem. I know exactly what most people would have done, because I could feel myself wanting to do it. Not the trading part. The *signing* part. She put a pen in front of me, Odile. A whole table. Ink that glittered. She wanted my name on something, anything, because whoever signs pays."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "Did you find the seam?"

"Not yet." He rubbed his eyes. "But I know it's there. She said the ledger isn't hers—she only *keeps* it. She said the debt collects itself. She showed me her hands, Odile. Deliberately. Everything wrong with that room is somebody else's signature, and she wanted me to believe there was no one to appeal to." He lowered his hands. "But contracts have authors. Ledgers have owners. Somebody wrote *payable in kind, or in the child*, and somebody taught the clerks to weigh, and somebody built a court where debts are the only law. If the Queen is just the keeper..." He trailed off, staring at the glassy dark of the window.

"Then who's the house?" Odile finished.

"Exactly."

They sat in silence for a while, shoulders touching, watching nothing out the courtesy window. The brazier ticked. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat, Xanthe thought he could hear the sound of scales—hundreds of them, thousands, the endless patient work of a court that weighed everything and valued nothing.

"She's dying," Xanthe said at last. "Really dying. The stars are medicine. She's been eating the sky to stay alive, and the prescription's running out, and she's terrified. That's the whole engine of this, Odile. Not malice. Terror with a treasury."

"Mm." Odile pulled her knees up. "Terrified things with treasuries are worse than wicked ones, in my experience. Wicked you can predict." She glanced sideways at him. "And Tam? Did she say where—"

"Below. Being measured." Xanthe's jaw tightened. "Clean stock, she called him. Like he's cloth by the ell."

"Right." Odile stood, and there was a new set to her shoulders, the one Xanthe had learned to recognize as *about to commit several crimes*. "Then we have until midwinter to find your seam, get Tam out of the measuring rooms, and beat a house that hasn't lost in nine hundred years. Any plan?"

"Working on it. Step one was refusing to sign. Step two is finding out who actually owns the ledger." He looked up at her. "Step three involves you reading fine print for pleasure. The Queen mentioned it. Apparently I strike her as someone who hunts lies, and you strike her as someone who enjoys contracts. I'm choosing to take both as compliments."

"Flatterer." But Odile was already moving to the door, testing the handle—unlocked, of course; the Undercourt didn't lock doors, it locked *futures*—and peering out into the corridor. "Come on, then. If we're going to rob the dead, I want to start with the archive. Every court keeps its founding documents somewhere, and founding documents are where houses bury the seams they hope nobody finds."

Xanthe followed her out into the candlelit dark. Behind them, deep in the mountain, the scales went on weighing, patient as winter.

And far below, in a room full of instruments and ink, a clerk bent over Tam's soul with a measuring chain, and noted, in a hand as neat as frost:

*Weight sufficient. Awaiting midwinter.*

* * *

The archive was three levels down and smelled of dust, cold iron, and something older underneath—paper, but paper that had been dead a very long time. The shelves rose into darkness overhead, and the aisles between them were narrow enough that walking them felt like moving through the ribs of something enormous.

"This is wrong," Odile whispered, half a shelf ahead of him, running her fingers along the spines. "This is *too easy*. Where are the guards?"

"There are no guards," said a voice from the end of the aisle.

Both of them spun. A clerk stood there—the same small gray figure, spectacles crooked, ink-stained fingers folded politely. But this one was looking directly at Xanthe, and its expression, insofar as clerks had expressions, was difficult to read. Something almost like hope.

"No guards," the clerk repeated. "The Queen believes the archive protects itself. She believes the *law* protects itself. She has believed this for nine hundred years." It stepped closer, and its voice dropped to something almost human. "You asked, in the throne room, who set the prices. No one has asked that question down here since the founding. The clerks have been waiting a very long time for someone to ask it."

Xanthe's heart was pounding. "Who wrote the ledger?"

The clerk reached into its robe and produced—not a document, but a single key, black iron, old beyond guessing.

"The founding contract," it said, "is on the top shelf. Aisle nine. And taster—" the crooked spectacles caught the candlelight, "—when you read it, read the *second* signature. Not the Queen's."

"The second signature," Xanthe repeated. "Whose is it?"

The clerk was already turning away, dissolving back into the shadows between shelves, and its last words came floating back like leaves on still water:

"The house's. The house always signs first."

* * *

Aisle nine. Top shelf. Xanthe stood on Odile's clasped hands and felt along the highest ledge until his fingers closed on a scroll-case of black iron, cold enough to burn, heavy as sin.

He climbed down. Odile took the case from him with trembling hands and turned it over once, twice, hunting for the catch the way she hunted everything—with a thief's patience. It opened.

Inside, rolled tight and tied with a cord of woven hair, was a contract. Older than the kingdom above. Older, possibly, than the idea of kingdoms.

Odile untied the cord with the care of a woman defusing something, and unrolled a foot of parchment onto the archive floor, weighting the corners with four loose books. Candlelight fell across the first lines. The writing was dense, archaic, and—Xanthe realized with a lurch—familiar. He had seen this hand before. Two nights ago, in the ledger room, on his mother's entry.

The same hand. The founding hand. The hand that had written *payable in kind, or in the child*.

"Read the signatures," Odile breathed.

Xanthe read. The first signature bloomed at the bottom of the founding terms in letters of dried silver: *the Queen Beneath the Mountain, keeper of the ledger, sworn to honor all debts until her ending.*

And beneath it, in ink so old it had gone brown, a second signature. Smaller. Careful. Almost humble.

Xanthe read the name twice. Then a third time. And when he finally understood what he was looking at, he had to sit down on the cold archive floor, because his legs had resigned again, and this time he let them.

"Odile," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "The second signature. It's not a person."

"What do you mean, it's not a—"

"It's the *ledger itself*." He traced the brown-inked characters with one finger, hardly daring to touch them. "The ledger co-signed its own founding contract. Look at the clause it added—here, at the bottom, in the smallest hand. *'All debts honored until the keeper's ending. Upon the keeper's ending, the ledger passes to the hand that holds it.'*" He looked up at Odile, and his mouth had gone dry. "*The hand that holds it.*"

Odile stared at him. Then at the contract. Then at him again, and he watched the same terrible understanding bloom across her face that was blooming across his.

"The Queen doesn't own the ledger," she whispered. "The ledger owns *her*. It's been keeping her alive—"

"—because a dead keeper can't hold it," Xanthe finished. "The stars aren't medicine. They're *wages*. The ledger pays her in light to keep her holding on, because the moment she ends, the ledger passes to whoever's holding it. And it wrote that clause itself. Nine hundred years ago. It's been arranging its own inheritance ever since."

Somewhere far above them, muffled through miles of stone, a bell began to toll. Not the soft administrative tolling of the clerks. This was deeper. Angrier. The sound of a house realizing, very late, that someone was reading its mail.

Odile stuffed the contract back into its case and shoved it inside her coat. "Archive's closing," she said grimly. "Run now, argue later."

They ran. Behind them the candle-shelves flickered and dimmed, aisle by aisle, as if the mountain were closing its eyes. Ahead, the corridor to their cell-room stretched long and empty, and at the end of it, silhouetted against the courtesy window's dark glass, stood a line of clerks. A dozen at least. Spectacles gleaming. Perfectly still.

Waiting.

The lead clerk stepped forward, and its voice, when it came, was not unkind. It was, in fact, almost gentle—which was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"Taster," it said. "The ledger has read what you carry. It knows you found the founding contract. And it wishes you to know—" the clerk inclined its head, courteous as a headsman, "—that midwinter has been moved up. It is now."

Behind the clerks, through the glassy window, the last of the hoarded stars guttered—once, twice—and went out.
