## 17. Tam's Choice

The chains were the worst part. Not because they were heavy—though they were heavy, iron links thick as Tam's thumb, cold enough to burn—but because they clinked when he walked, and Tam had spent eleven years in the royal kitchens learning to walk quietly. Cook had boxed his ears for less than a clink.

They brought him down through corridors that were not corridors. That was the only way Tam could think of it. The Undercourt didn't have hallways so much as it had *intentions*, and you walked along them. The walls were stone that remembered being alive: here a vein of quartz pulsed slow as a sleeping heart, there a seam of green moss glowed faintly in rock that hadn't seen sun since before the mountain had a name. The clerks who escorted him said nothing. They wore grey robes and carried ledgers chained to their belts, and their faces were kind in the way of people who have long ago stopped being able to help anyone but haven't yet stopped wanting to.

Tam counted his steps, because counting was what he did when he was frightened. He'd counted the night the stars went out (four hundred and six heartbeats of pure black). He'd counted the days on the road north (nineteen, though it felt like nineteen years). He was counting now, and he was at two hundred and thirty-one when the corridor opened into the courtroom, and he stopped counting altogether, because some things are too big for numbers.

It was a cavern the size of the whole palace back in Vell, if the palace had been hollowed out by patient water and hung with ten thousand candles. Candles everywhere—on ledges, in niches, floating in bowls of dark water—and every flame burning a different color, amber and blue and one or two a green so pale it looked like the memory of green. And beneath them, stretching away into shadow, desks. Hundreds of desks. Clerks at every one, weighing things on scales that held no coins.

They weighed souls down here. Odile had told them that on the Thorn Road, in her flat way, like she was describing weather. Tam hadn't quite believed it until he saw a clerk tip a small silver scale, and something invisible slid from one pan to the other, and every candle in the cavern guttered for half a heartbeat, all together, like the mountain itself had flinched.

And at the far end, on a throne of black root-wood grown up through the floor, sat the Queen Beneath the Mountain.

Tam had expected terror. He'd been rehearsing terror for days, ever since the chains went on. But what he felt instead, looking at her, was something that took him a long moment to name, because he'd never felt it about anyone important before.

He felt sorry for her.

She was dying. You could see it the way you could see frost on a window—not hidden, not pretending otherwise. She had been beautiful once; the shape of it was still there, like the shape of a face under snow. But she was thin as candle smoke, and her crown was made of starlight, actual starlight, stolen light wound into a circlet, and even that was dimming. It flickered around her brow the way a fire flickers when it's eaten everything it can reach and is starting on the hearthstones.

Xanthe stood before the throne. They'd taken Xanthe's coat and given him a plain grey smock, and without it he looked younger, and thinner, and Tam's chest did an angry squeeze, because Xanthe had shared his bread the whole road north even when there wasn't enough, and nobody put Xanthe in a smock. Nobody chained Tam's friends.

"Tam," the Queen said, and her voice was the voice. Of course it was. All those months of whispers in his skull, soft as snowfall, saying *come north, little listener, come north where you're wanted*—they came from her. He knew it the way you know your own name.

"You hear very well," she said. "That is rare. That is useful."

"I hear fine," Tam said. His voice cracked on *fine*, which was embarrassing, so he kept going fast. "Too fine, maybe. I heard you promising me things. Safety. A warm place. I heard you the whole way here, whispering in my ear like you were my friend."

"I could be your friend," the Queen said gently. "That is what I am offering."

*Here it comes,* Tam thought, and his stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

"The ledger requires a weight," the Queen continued, and she lifted one hand, and a clerk scurried forward with a great book bound in grey leather. "Your soul fits the scale. It is nothing against you—it is arithmetic. The boy Xanthe walks free, unclaimed, unowed. You remain below, in comfort, in honor. You will want for nothing." She smiled, and the smile was old and tired and almost real. "You will be safe, little listener. Is that not what you wanted? Is that not what I promised?"

Tam looked at Xanthe.

He'd been avoiding it, actually, because he'd known this was coming—the whispers had told him days ago, in their sly sideways way, that he would be the price, and he'd been carrying it like a stone in his boot, too ashamed to say it out loud. But now he looked, and Xanthe was looking back at him, and Xanthe's face was doing something terrible. It was doing *guilt*. Real guilt, the deep kind, the kind that has already decided something.

"No," Tam said.

The word came out smaller than he meant. He cleared his throat and tried again.

"No," he said, louder, and it echoed off the cavern roof and ten thousand candles shivered. "No, you don't get to do that. Neither of you."

"Tam—" Xanthe started.

"You don't get to trade me!" Tam's voice was climbing and he let it climb; the kitchens had taught him that a loud voice in the right moment was worth twenty quiet ones. "I'm not a coin! I'm not a *weight*! I'm Tam! I'm the one who found the loose grate in the wine cellar, and I'm the one who carried Odile's bag when her shoulder went bad, and I'm the one who—" His voice wobbled and he hated it and pushed through anyway. "I'm the one who heard you, Your Majesty. Every day. Every night. And you know what you sounded like?"

The Queen tilted her head. Around the courtroom, clerks had gone very still over their desks.

"You sounded *lonely*," Tam said.

The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere high above, water dripped onto stone, once, twice, three times, each drop ringing like a tiny bell.

"That's the truth of it," Tam went on, quieter now, because the loud part was done and the true part needed room. "You stole the stars because you were scared of dying. You whispered to a kitchen boy because you were lonely. You built all this—" he waved his chained hands at the candles, the desks, the endless grey order of it— "so that nothing would ever end, and now you're sitting on a throne trying to buy more time with somebody else's soul, and everybody here lets you, because they're scared. But they pity you. I've heard them. I hear *everything*, remember?"

A clerk near the front—a small one, round-faced, with ink stains on her fingers—made a sound that might have been a cough and might have been something else entirely.

"They pity you," Tam repeated, turning slowly, letting his voice carry the way Cook's had carried down the kitchen on feast days. "The ones who weigh souls all day, every day. Do you know what they say when they think no one's listening? I do. I heard a clerk on the way down here tell another one that the Queen used to be kind. That she used to forgive debts. That the court was better when she wasn't afraid." He turned back to the throne. "You're not scary, Your Majesty. You're just sad. And sad people who won't admit it end up hurting everyone around them. I know, because I lived with a cook like that for six years."

Someone in the ranks of desks made a noise that was definitely a laugh, quickly swallowed.

The Queen's expression didn't change. But the crown of stolen stars flickered—brighter, then dimmer, like a breath catching.

"You dare much," she said softly, "for a boy in chains."

"I dare exactly as much as anybody who's got nothing left to lose," Tam said. "Which is what you keep telling everybody else they have. Nothing to lose. So." He shrugged, and the chains clinked, and this time he didn't mind the sound. "Nothing to lose. Here I am, daring."

* * *

They gave him a bench, afterward. Not freedom—the chains stayed—but a bench near the wall, close enough to watch, and a cup of something hot that tasted like honey and pine and, faintly, of forgiveness. Tam drank it and watched the courtroom breathe.

Because that was what it did, he realized. Breathe. The whole cavern rose and fell in some slow rhythm, candle flames leaning all one direction and then all the other, and Tam understood that he was inside the mountain's chest, more or less, and that the mountain was holding its breath.

Xanthe was still standing before the throne. He hadn't moved during Tam's whole speech—he'd gone white, then red, then white again—and now the Queen was speaking to him in a low voice that Tam couldn't quite catch, which was a strange and awful feeling, being unable to hear. Like going deaf in one ear. Like losing a limb he hadn't known he had.

But he caught pieces. He caught *the ledger*, and he caught *your mother's hand*, and he caught the Queen saying, in a voice gone brittle as old ice:

"There is always a bargain. That is what a court *is*. Name your price, taster's son, and be done with it."

And he caught Xanthe's answer, clear across the whole cavern, in a voice that didn't shake at all:

"No."

Tam grinned into his cup. *Attaboy.*

"No bargains," Xanthe said, louder, and pulled something from the sleeve of his grey smock—a folded paper, creased and re-creased, soft as cloth from handling. "Just the truth. Read aloud. All of it."

The nearest clerks rose from their desks. Not summoned—rising on their own, craning, and Tam saw the round-faced one with ink-stained fingers push forward through her colleagues with an eagerness she wasn't bothering to hide.

"Every clause," Xanthe said. "Every signature. Including yours, Majesty. Including the part where you swore the debt would die with you."

The crown of stolen stars flared—and guttered—and in the sudden half-dark, Tam heard the smallest sound in the world: a clerk, somewhere in the third row, whispering to her neighbor,

"She swore *what*?"

The mountain drew its next breath very, very slowly.

And Tam, listening with everything he had, heard something else beneath it—far down, deeper than the courtroom, deeper than the roots—a sound like a lock turning over, considering, in a door that had been shut for a very long time.
