## 18. The Ledger Turned Backward

The clerks came for me at what I can only assume was morning, though beneath the mountain there was no way to tell. Time down there moved like honey off a cold spoon—slowly, and only when someone was watching.

They dressed me for the audience. That was the strangest part. Two of them, thin as candlesticks and gray as old paper, held up garments and debated my measurements in voices like pages turning.

"The black," said one.

"He's not in mourning."

"He's about to be."

I chose the brown myself, mostly out of spite, and they sighed together like a draft under a door.

Tam had been taken an hour before, or a day—I couldn't say—and Odile was kept apart from me, which told me everything about how the Queen meant to play this. Divide the family, weigh each piece separately. The Undercourt did its arithmetic on souls, and it never rounded in your favor.

But here is what they hadn't counted on, what no one in that candlelit cavern of ledgers and scales had bothered to consider: I had been raised in a royal kitchen, and if there is one thing a taster knows better than poison, it is *contract language*. Every delivery of spices, every shipment of wine, every sack of flour from the valley farms came with terms. I had spent ten years reading the fine print on barrels while the steward read it over my shoulder, waiting for me to miss something. I never missed anything. Missing things got tasters killed.

Somewhere in that court was my mother's contract. And somewhere in that contract, I was willing to bet my life—which, admittedly, was already spoken for—was a clause nobody down here wanted read aloud.

* * *

The Court of Small Debts assembled in the great cavern where I'd first seen the clerks at their weighing, but tonight—or whatever passed for tonight—the candles burned higher, and every scale in the room stood empty, waiting. The clerks sat in tiers along the walls like rooks on a chessboard, their pens poised over blank ledgers. In the center, upon a throne of pale stone shot through with veins of green light, sat the Queen Beneath the Mountain.

She was worse than when I'd seen her last. Fading was too gentle a word; she was *thinning*, like ink washed with water, her edges going soft, her crown sitting heavy on a head that seemed less solid than the crown itself. The stolen stars hung behind her in a net of woven dark, and they pulsed faintly, feeding her, keeping her, and even so she was losing.

She looked at me the way a starving woman looks at bread.

"Xanthe Reyes," she said. Her voice filled the cavern without effort, the way cold fills a cellar. "Son of Maren Reyes. Debtor's blood. You stand in the Undercourt owing—" she consulted nothing, because queens do not consult, "—everything."

"I stand here owning nothing," I said. "Your ferryman will vouch for me. He seemed very pleased about it."

A ripple went through the clerks' tiers. Somewhere, a pen scratched.

"You were offered terms," the Queen said. "Freedom for the boy Tam. A fair trade. His soul fits the ledger's weight almost exactly—it's a tidy little soul, quiet, unclaimed. You'd have walked out of here by now."

"And he'd have walked in wearing chains forever."

"He'd have been *comfortable*. We are very good to those we keep." She leaned forward, and the green light in the stone brightened around her like something leaning in too. "You mistake what this is, taster. This is not cruelty. This is *bookkeeping*. Your mother signed. One winter of warmth for one unborn soul. The debt has come due, and debts must be paid, or the whole architecture of obligation comes down. Do you understand what rests on that? Every vow ever sworn beneath this mountain. Every bargain. Every promise the living made and hoped to escape. If I let yours lapse, I let *all* of them lapse."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, "that I'm not asking you to let it lapse."

Her fading eyes narrowed. Around us, two hundred clerks went very still.

* * *

Here is what I had done with my hours in the guest cell—which was, I'll grant the Undercourt this, a very comfortable cell, with warm stone and a blanket that smelled of cedar and no bars at all, because the Undercourt had never once needed bars; who would run, in a place like this, and run where?

I had asked for my mother's ledger entry. Full record. And the clerk who brought it—young, or young the way these creatures measured youth, maybe fifty years at his post—had hesitated at the door.

"You're permitted the summary," he'd said. "Line item. Amount owed. Date due."

"I'm permitted the full contract," I'd said, "or I'm permitted nothing, and you know what happens then." I'd learned that much from watching Odile negotiate for bread in Hollow Bells: never ask for what you want. Ask for what you're owed, and make the wanting look like their idea. "The court honors debts. That's the whole of your power, isn't it? So honor this one. Show me the document. All of it. Signed and sealed."

He'd looked at me for a long moment with eyes like wet slate. Then he'd brought it. All of it. Because the law beneath the law—the one older than the Queen, older than the mountain, the one the whole court balanced upon like dancers on a wire—does not permit half a debt to be shown. A debt hidden is a debt denied, and a debt denied unmakes the denier.

And there, at the bottom of the third page, in handwriting that flowed like water finding its level, was the clause I'd been gambling on.

* * *

Now I stood in the great cavern with the folded contract inside my coat, against my heart, which was hammering hard enough that I worried the clerks could hear it and would enter it into the record. *One heartbeat, slightly elevated, property of the debtor.*

"Speak your request," the Queen said, "and be quick. My time is—" she gestured at herself, at the softness of her own hands, "—limited."

"I want the contract read aloud. In full. Before the court."

Murmurs. Pens paused mid-stroke.

"The terms are known to you," the Queen said.

"The *summary* is known to me. But your own ferryman taught me something on the river Sere, and your clerks confirmed it at the door: the Undercourt despises beggars and honors debts. It does not do business on summaries. So either the full contract exists, and it may be read, or it doesn't exist, and there is no debt at all—and I walk out of here free, because you cannot collect on a document you won't produce."

Silence, deep as the rock above us. The candles bent, all together, toward the Queen, like flowers toward a dying sun.

"Read it," she said at last, and her voice had gone quieter, and quieter was worse than loud. "Read it, taster. And understand that whatever you find in it, the debt stands. Words do not dissolve obligations. I should know. I have made a career of words that failed to."

I unfolded the pages. My hands were steadier than I deserved.

"To the court of small debts, kept beneath the root and the stone, in the year the frost came early"—my voice echoed off the vaulted dark—"this bond is entered by Maren Reyes, of the valley of Vell, of sound mind and failing body, who asks one winter of warmth against the cold in her lungs, and offers in payment that which she carries: the soul of her unborn child, payable at maturity, in kind."

"In kind," the Queen repeated softly. "Meaning the child himself, delivered willing or otherwise. You've just read your own death warrant aloud, boy. Was the acoustics worth it?"

"Keep listening," I said. "There's more. There's always more—that's the whole point of contracts, isn't it? Nobody signs the first page."

I turned to the second sheet. The clerks had gone from still to *statues*. Even the Queen's green light seemed to hold its breath.

"Conditions annexed, sworn by both parties. First: the debt shall not be transferred, sold, or assigned to any party other than the signatories and their lineal heirs." I looked up. "That's why you needed me specifically. Why the wards falling mattered. Why the king couldn't pay it with some other poor soul from his dungeons. It's also why your offer of Tam was never going to work—not cleanly, not lawfully. His soul might fit the ledger's weight, but weight isn't lineage. You weren't offering me a trade. You were offering me a crime."

"Contracts bend," the Queen said. "Courts interpret."

"Courts *interpret*," I agreed, "but courts do not break, not if they want to go on being courts. Which brings me to the second condition." I found the line. I had it memorized anyway, but I wanted the paper in my hand when I said it, because paper makes things true in a way memory doesn't. "Second: this debt shall endure no longer than the life of the creditor. Should the creditor die, or cease—in whatever manner ceasing comes to such as her—the debt dies with her, entire and unforgiven but *uncollectable*, and the debtor's line goes free."

I looked up at the Queen Beneath the Mountain.

"And you signed it."

* * *

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the fullest silence I have ever stood inside—two hundred clerks not breathing, a thousand candles not flickering, a netful of stolen stars pulsing overhead like hearts in a box.

"That clause," the Queen said finally, "was boilerplate. Standard form. No one reads the standard forms."

"My mother did," I said. "Dying women read everything. They haven't got time to trust anybody." I held the page up, turning it so the tiers of clerks could see the signature at the bottom—a long, flowing name in letters that seemed to move slightly, like water. "There's your hand, Majesty. Sworn before witnesses, sealed in the old way, binding under the law beneath the law. The debt dies when you do. And you—" I made myself say it gently, because cruelty wasn't the point, and besides, I'd watched enough cruelty in Vell to know I didn't want any of mine, "—you are dying. Right now, in front of all of us. Those stars you stole are a bandage on a wound that doesn't close. You've been trying to hold back midnight with a handful of noon, and it hasn't worked, and it was never going to."

"Do not," she said, rising, and the cavern groaned, "presume to—"

"Let me finish. Please." And something in the *please* stopped her, because it wasn't a beggar's please. It was a taster's. The please of someone who has swallowed a hundred poisons and lived, and knows exactly how much time everyone at the table has left. "You offered me Tam's soul for my freedom. You offered me my mother's soul for my friends. Every offer you've made has been a way to keep this debt alive past its term, because the debt is the last thing anchoring you to existence. As long as it's collectable, you have a reason to keep breathing. I understand that. I *grieve* that. But here is what I've come to tell you, and I want every clerk in this room to write it down:

"If you collect this debt—if you take me, or Tam, or anyone, by force or fraud or clever interpretation—you break the second condition, sworn in your own hand. And the Undercourt's entire power rests on honoring debts. Break this one, and every debt in every ledger in this cavern becomes negotiable. Every vow becomes a suggestion. Every signature becomes a smudge. The clerks know it. Look at them."

They looked. I looked too, and what I saw in those slate-gray faces was not pity for me.

It was pity for *her*.

"They've been weighing souls down here for longer than kingdoms have had names," I said, "and they know arithmetic better than anyone alive or dead. And the arithmetic says: a court that breaks its own oldest contract isn't a court anymore. It's just a cave with candles."

The youngest clerk—my slate-eyed friend from the cell door—set down his pen. Deliberately. The sound of it was very small and very enormous.

Then the clerk beside him set down his pen.

Then the next, and the next, down the tiers, two hundred pens coming to rest on two hundred ledgers, until the only sound in the Court of Small Debts was the Queen's breathing, which had gone ragged, and the stars overhead, pulsing faster now, like something panicking.

"You would unmake us," she whispered, "over a clause."

"No," I said. "You would unmake yourselves over a collection. I'm offering you the way out you wrote yourself, back when you were honest enough to put it in writing. Honor the debt. Let it die with you, as sworn. Free my mother's soul, free her son, and go out—" and here my voice did crack, just a little, and I let it, "—go out having kept faith with the only thing that ever made you mighty. Not the stealing. Not the hoarding. The *keeping of promises*. That's the law beneath the law. It's the only one either of our worlds ever really had."

The Queen looked at her empty court. At the pens lying still. At the scales with nothing in them, waiting to see what she would weigh next: a boy, or her own word.

And the Queen Beneath the Mountain began, very slowly, to laugh—a dry, rustling sound, like autumn deciding to be finished.

"You are your mother's son," she said. "She read the standard forms too."

* * *

What happened next belongs partly to her, and partly to the stars, and partly to a girl named Odile who was at that moment picking the lock on a door that had never actually been locked, because the Undercourt had never imagined anyone would want to leave a warm room—but that is her story to tell, and she tells it better than I do, with more swearing.

For me, there was only this: the Queen raised one thinning hand, and the great ledger of the court opened itself in the air before her, pages turning back and back and back, decades of entries streaming past like snow in reverse, until it found the one it was looking for.

*Maren Reyes. One winter of warmth. Paid in full.*

The words settled onto the page in ink that glowed briefly gold, and somewhere far above us—so far I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it—a star came home to the sky.

The Queen slumped in her throne of green-veined stone, lighter by one debt, dimmer by one century, and smiled at me with something that was almost peace and almost mischief.

"There is one more thing I kept, taster," she said. "Something of hers. I had planned to die holding it, out of spite. But you've rather ruined spite for me."

Behind her, at the edge of the cavern, a side door opened—and Odile stepped through it with a stolen ring of keys in her fist and an expression that promised the Undercourt a lecture it would remember for a thousand years.

"Xanthe," she said, "we have a problem, and it's about four feet tall and currently chained in the antechamber—"

"Yes," I said, folding my mother's contract closed and putting it away, not as evidence anymore, but as inheritance. "Let's go get our boy."

The Queen watched us go, and behind us, softly, she began to sing—the first song the mountain had sung in a thousand years, and every star in her net leaned toward the sound like children called home to supper.
