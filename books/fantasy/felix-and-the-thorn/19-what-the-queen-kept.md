## 19. What the Queen Kept

Odile had spent her whole life learning what dying looked like.

She'd sat with her mother through the long gray autumn of the fever. She'd held her sister's hand in a rented room that smelled of tallow and rosemary, watching the light go out of Wren by inches, like a candle guttering in a draft no one could find. She'd seen soldiers die badly and beggars die gently and one toll-collector die so politely he apologized for the inconvenience. Dying, she had thought, was a thing you learned to read the way Xanthe read flavors: a slight wrongness, a bitterness at the edge, a sweetness that had turned.

The Queen Beneath the Mountain was dying the way a cathedral dies—slowly, enormously, and with everyone pretending not to notice until the roof came down.

She sat upon her throne of black root-wood in the great candlelit cavern, and now that Odile knew what to look for, she could not stop seeing it. The Queen's skin was the color of old paper. Her crown—a circlet of what Odile had taken for silver—was actually frost, renewed each morning by clerks with little brushes, like servants dusting a shrine. And around her, stacked in towers that reached toward the cavern ceiling, were the stars.

They didn't look like stars down here. They looked like eggs. Pale, warm, faintly pulsing things, each one cradled in a niche of stone, each one humming a note so low Odile felt it in her back teeth rather than heard it. Thousands of them. Every star that had vanished from the sky on the night the wards fell, carried down into the dark and hoarded like a miser's gold.

*She stole the sky to keep herself alive,* Odile thought. *And it isn't working.*

The courtroom was silent. Tam stood between two clerks, still in his chains, his chin up in a way that made Odile's chest ache—he was fourteen, he smelled of kitchen grease and river mud, and he had just told the oldest power under the earth, to her face, that even her own clerks pitied her. The clerks themselves stood frozen at their weighing-tables, quills hovering, souls-in-waiting balanced on their brass scales, all of them watching their queen with an expression Odile recognized from every sickroom she'd ever sat in: the careful blankness of people waiting for the end.

And Xanthe stood in the middle of the floor with his mother's contract open in his hands, having just done the single most Odile thing Odile had ever witnessed another human being do: he had refused to bargain.

"You swore it," he said again, quietly, because apparently once hadn't been enough. He held the contract up—not thrust, not brandished, just *held*, the way you'd hold out a hand to show there was nothing in it. "Your signature. 'This debt ends with me.' You wrote it yourself, before my mother ever signed. You never wanted to collect."

"I wrote many things," said the Queen. Her voice was vast and tired, like wind through a canyon. "Courts write what courts require."

"Then unrequire it." Xanthe folded the contract closed with exaggerated care, the way he did everything, as if even defiance were a task requiring clean hands. "I'm not trading anyone. Not Tam. Not myself. Not anything. So either you break your own law—and I've seen your law, madam, it's the only thing down here that still works—or you honor it. Those are your choices. I don't have any more."

Silence. Somewhere in the dark of the cavern, a candle guttered.

It was Tam who moved first. Odile saw it happen—the boy stepped forward, chains clinking, and put his small dirty hand on Xanthe's sleeve, and said, not to the Queen but to the whole room:

"She's scared."

Everyone stared at him. The Queen stared at him. Odile would have paid good money—well, she'd have paid someone else's money—for a portrait of the clerks' faces.

"Look at her," Tam said. His voice wobbled but held. "You're all looking at the crown and the throne and the stars, and I'm telling you, I *heard* her. For weeks. All that whispering in my head, and underneath it, the whole time, she was—" his breath caught— "she was humming. Like my gran did. When she was frightened and didn't want anyone to know." He turned to face the throne, and Odile saw his knees shaking and his spine straight anyway. "You took the sky because you're afraid of the dark. That's all. That's the whole terrible secret. You're just afraid."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the Queen began, very slowly, to laugh.

It was not a kind sound, exactly, but it wasn't cruel either. It was the laugh of someone who has been caught out after years of successful hiding—the laugh of a child found behind a curtain. It shook frost from her crown. It made the nearest tower of stars shiver on its shelf, and their hum changed pitch, rising, warming, and Odile felt tears startle hot down her own cheeks before she understood why. The sound of those stars, warming—it was the sound of a night sky in summer. It was the sound of being seven years old and looking up.

"Oh," said the Queen. "Oh, you dreadful, wonderful children." She rose from the throne. Up close, without the distance of ceremony, she was smaller than Odile expected—tall, but stooped, the way trees stoop under snow. She looked at Xanthe. "You are right, taster's son. I never wanted this debt. Your mother came to me weeping, begging one winter of warmth for a child already dying inside her, and I told her the price, and I hoped—I *hoped*—that she would refuse. Living people usually do, when the price is honest. But she signed. And a court that does not honor its signatures is not a court. It is only a hole in the ground full of candles." Her mouth twisted. "So I honored it. Badly. Slowly. I let the debt sit for sixteen years because every year I delayed was a year I did not have to look at what I'd agreed to hold."

"The child," Odile said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "The clause. *Payable in kind, or in the child.*"

"In kind," the Queen said, "meant the soul itself, returned to me at her death, freely given. That was the bargain. The clause about the child was mine—an escape hatch, written in shame. If she would not give her own soul, then the court could take her son's instead. I told myself it made the contract *fair*. Two doors instead of one." She closed her eyes. "I have been a very old creature for a very long time, and I have learned that when you give yourself two doors, you will always take the worse one, because it is easier to walk through a door that opens away from you."

Xanthe hadn't moved. Odile watched him the way she watched everything about him—with a wariness that had long ago curdled into something fonder and much more inconvenient. His face was doing the thing it did in the kitchens, she realized. The tasting face. Weighing something invisible, cataloguing, deciding whether it was poison.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now," said the Queen, "you have shamed me before my own clerks, broken my court's oldest habit, and taught a kitchen boy to read me like a grandmother." She opened her eyes, and they were bright—too bright, the brightness of the last coal. "There is one thing left to decide, and it is not yours to decide. It is mine. It was always mine. That is what none of us remembered—not the king above, not the clerks, not even me." She lifted one hand, paper-pale, and gestured at the towers of stars. "I kept them. All these years, I kept them, telling myself I needed their light to live. And perhaps I did. But a hoard is a choice, taster's son. Every day I chose to keep them rather than give them back. And today—" her voice caught, actually caught, and Odile thought, *there it is, there's the person under the crown* — "today I am going to make a different choice. My last one. I find I want it to be a good one."

* * *

What happened next, Odile would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly.

The Queen walked down from her dais, past the clerks—who bowed, all of them, and several of them were openly weeping, which Odile suspected had not happened in the Undercourt since its founding—and began, with her own two hands, to open the niches.

Each star, freed, drifted upward. Not fast. Gently, the way soap bubbles rise, the way sparks rise off a bonfire. And as they rose they sang—that low warm hum climbing note by note into registers Odile's body understood better than her ears—and the cavern, which had been lit by ten thousand candles for ten thousand years, filled with a light that was nothing like candlelight. It was moving light. It was *weathered* light. It flickered and shimmered and threw colors across the root-wood pillars, blue and gold and one green so deep it made Odile think of her sister's garden, of the summer before everything went wrong.

"Help me," the Queen said. Not commanded. Asked.

Tam got there first, of course. He scrambled up onto the second tier of shelving, chains and all, and started prying niches open with his fingers, laughing and crying at once. Xanthe worked beside him, steady and quick, passing star after star up into the air. And Odile—

Odile stood at the bottom of the towers with her arms full of sky and thought about Wren.

Because there, on the third tier, in a niche marked with a clerk's neat brass tag, was a soul she recognized. Not by sight—souls down here didn't look like people—but by feel. She'd have known it anywhere. It hummed slightly sharp, slightly impatient, the way Wren had done everything: talked too fast, laughed too loud, loved too hard, died at nineteen because a fever came through the fen-country and the hedge-witch's remedies arrived one day late.

"Wren," Odile whispered.

The star pulsed against her palms, warm as a bird.

"Go on," said the nearest clerk, an ancient creature whose spectacles had fogged with crying. "That one's ledger closed an hour ago, when the queen released the old contracts. She's free to go. She's been free to go for an hour. Someone simply has to—" his voice cracked— "someone has to let her out. We're not permitted to touch them ourselves. Conflict of interest. Terrible rule. Never liked it."

Odile lifted her sister out of the wall of the world.

The star rose from her hands slowly—so slowly, turning once as it went, and Odile would swear until her dying day that in that turn it paused, just for a heartbeat, level with her face. Warmth brushed her cheek like a hand. Like fingers pushing hair back from a forehead, the way Wren used to when Odile fell asleep studying herb-lore over the table.

"You absolute menace," Odile said, wrecked, laughing. "You waited for me. You cheeky, patient, impossible girl, you *waited*."

The star rose. It joined the others streaming up through the cavern's high darkness, hundreds of them now, thousands, a river of light flowing home, and Odile stood beneath it with her hands still cupped around the shape of her sister's absence and sobbed like a child, and found, to her enormous surprise, that it was the good kind of crying. The kind that empties rather than fills. Somewhere behind her, Tam was cheering every star like a regatta. Somewhere beside her, Xanthe had gone very still.

* * *

The last star the Queen opened was not on the shelves.

It was in her chest. Odile saw it happen—saw the Queen press both hands flat below her collarbone, and close her eyes, and breathe out, long and slow, and the light that left her was brighter than all the rest, brighter than the cavern could hold, and when Odile's vision cleared the throne was empty and the frost crown lay on the dais, melting into ordinary water.

The clerks bowed to the empty throne. Then, one by one, they began putting out the candles—which, Odile gathered, was the Undercourt's way of applause.

Xanthe was standing where the Queen had last stood, and something was happening to him. Odile saw it from across the chamber: a light settling on him, sinking into him, sinking *in*, the way rain sinks into ground that has been dry too long. He made a small sound. His hand came up to his face, to his temple, to the place behind his eyes where a person keeps the pictures of the people they love.

He'd told her once, on the frozen ferry, in the flat voice he used for the worst things: *I can't remember her face. I remember that I should remember it. There's just a hole shaped like her.* The debt had eaten it, piece by piece, the way debts do—traded away in installments neither of them had ever signed for.

Now the Queen's last act of grace came home.

"It's her," Xanthe said. His voice was strange, half-strangled, wondering. "Odile. Odile, I can see her. She's—" He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "She's laughing. She's laughing at something, she's got flour on her hands, she's laughing and she's wiping her hands on her apron and she's—she's looking at me. She's looking at *me* and she's happy. Why is she happy? I'm not—I wasn't even born yet—"

"Because you were coming," Odile said. She crossed the floor and took his face in her hands, the way you'd hold something newly hatched. "That's why, you great fool. Mothers laugh like that when they're expecting you. I've seen it. Flour and all."

He cried. Odile held him while he cried, this competent, careful, stubborn man who had faced down kings and courts and a dying god with nothing but a contract and clean hands, and who now wept like the orphan he had always technically been and never once allowed himself to be. Tam piled in too, because Tam piled into everything, and for a while the three of them stood in the middle of the emptying Undercourt, in the rising river of returning light, and grieved and rejoiced in the same breath, which Odile had long suspected was the only honest way to do either.

Above them, far above, through miles of stone, the sky was filling back up.

* * *

They climbed out near dawn.

The passage the clerks showed them—the clerks, who pressed honey-cakes on Tam and argued over who got to carry Odile's pack, because gratitude in the Undercourt apparently expressed itself as aggressive hospitality—came up through the roots of the petrified tree, past the door that asked what they were owed. This time, when it asked, Odile answered "nothing" and meant it more completely than she had ever meant anything. The door opened like a sigh.

Outside, the world was blue-black and blazing.

Stars beyond counting. Stars in rivers and clusters and careless scatterings, stars Odile had names for and thousands she didn't, the whole outrageous inventory of the heavens restored overnight while they'd been underground. The snowfields of the high mountain caught the starlight and threw it back, and the cold air tasted like iron and pine and beginning.

Tam dropped to his knees in the snow. For a horrible moment Odile thought something was wrong—then she realized he was listening, head cocked, eyes shut, listening to the silence where the whispers had lived for months.

"Gone," he whispered. "It's all gone quiet. It's just—" his breath hitched— "it's just *quiet*. Like a house at night. A safe house." He opened his eyes, wet and wide. "I'd forgotten what my own head sounded like."

Odile crouched beside him and pulled his hood up over his ears, because someone had to, and because his gran wasn't here to do it and Odile had decided, somewhere between a tollhouse and a frozen ferry, that she was.

Xanthe stood a little apart, face tipped up to the sky, and Odile knew—he was finding her. Somewhere in all that glittering wreckage of night, he was picking out the ones that felt like flour on hands and laughter, and he was memorizing them, because he was Xanthe, and he would never lose that memory again. Not to debt, not to death, not to anything.

"Well," Odile said at last, when the cold had gotten into all three of them equally, which seemed fair. "We owe the dead a funeral, I believe. She did ask."

They built the cairn themselves, halfway down the mountain where the treeline began, stacking stones over the spot where Odile had buried the melted frost-crown in its little wooden box. No priest. No rite. Just Tam laying a honey-cake on top ("she seemed like she'd have wanted one"), and Xanthe standing with his hat off, and Odile saying the only words she knew for the occasion, the ones fen-folk said over their dead:

"What you kept, keep it well. What you gave, come home by."

The wind moved through the pines. The stars wheeled overhead, all of them present, all of them accounted for.

And it was Xanthe, squinting south along the valley road where the first gray hint of dawn was gathering, who said quietly:

"There are lights coming up the valley. A lot of them. Torches." He paused. "And banners. Those are the king's colors, Odile. Somebody's been busy while we were underground."

Odile sighed, resettled her pack, and counted her remaining tricks on her fingers.

"Right," she said. "Nobody panics. Tam, hide the honey-cakes. Let's go see who's come to meet us."
