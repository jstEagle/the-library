## 20. Ascent

The last stair was not a stair at all.

It was a seam in the mountain's throat where the stone had grown tired of being stone and split itself open to the sky — a crack no wider than a coffin lid, breathing cold air down at us like the first honest breath I'd felt in what might have been days or might have been years. Time below the mountain moved like honey off a spoon. You couldn't trust it. You could only climb through it.

Odile went first, because Odile always went first when there was a chance of something biting. She wedged her shoulders into the crack, swore once in a language I suspected she'd invented specifically for swearing, and hauled herself up and out of sight. Then her hand came back down into the dark, palm up, fingers spread.

"Come on, then," her voice said, thin and strange with distance. "The sky's doing something."

"The sky does lots of things," Tam said behind me. His voice had a raw edge to it. He hadn't spoken much since the courtroom — since the Queen had looked at him with those fading coal-eyes and *unmade* whatever it was she'd built inside his head. "It rains. It snows. It snowed for a month."

"This is different." A pause. "Hurry up. Please."

Please. Odile didn't say please. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed coal, warm and frightening all at once.

I took her hand. It was smaller than I remembered, or maybe I'd gotten bigger — months on the road will do that, feed you frost and fear until your bones come in thicker. She pulled, and the stone scraped past my back, and then the world opened.

I have tasted a thousand things. Bitter things, sweet things, things that were poison wearing sugar's face. But nothing I ever put on my tongue prepared me for the taste of that first breath above the mountain: pine and snowmelt and cold iron and, underneath it all, so faint you'd miss it if you weren't trained to catch the faintest note — starlight. Starlight has a taste. It tastes like the smell of rain before it falls, like the first sip of water after fever, like every good thing you've ever been promised and never delivered. It tastes like *almost*.

Because the stars were back. All of them. Every one the Queen had hoarded in her dying hands, poured back into the black cloth of the sky, and they hung so thick and close over the peak that it seemed if I stood on my toes I could stir them with a finger. The Milky Way ran from horizon to horizon like spilled milk, like a road, like a river someone had finally un-dammed.

Tam climbed out last. He got one knee onto the snow, looked up —

— and sat down hard, and began to weep.

Not quietly. Not the careful, rationed crying of a boy who'd learned in the kitchens that tears cost you bread. This was the ugly, honking, whole-body weeping of someone who has put down a weight he forgot he was carrying, and Odile and I just stood there in the starlight while he sobbed at the sky, because there was nothing else to do. There is never anything else to do. You stand witness. That's most of love, I've come to think. Standing witness while someone puts their weight down.

"They're gone," he managed, between great shuddering breaths. "The whispers. They're *gone*, Xanthe. There's nothing in my head but me. There's only *me* in here." He pressed both palms against his temples as if checking the walls of a house he'd never lived in alone. "I didn't know how loud it was. I didn't know how loud it was until it stopped."

"Is it quiet?" Odile asked gently. "Or is it empty? Those are different things."

Tam thought about that. I watched him do it — actually think, turning the question over like a coin he wasn't sure was real. Snow gathered on his lashes. Somewhere below us, the mountain breathed, vast and slow, but it did not speak. It would never speak to him again. The Queen was dead, and her voice had died with her, and whatever small piece of herself she'd threaded through a kitchen boy's ear had gone out like a pinched wick.

"It's quiet," he said at last. Wonderingly. "It's quiet like the kitchen at night. Like when everyone's asleep and the ovens are still warm." And then he started crying again, but differently this time, easier, the way you cry when the worst thing has already happened and it turned out to be the best thing instead.

I crouched beside him and did the only useful thing I knew how to do. I'd carried a heel of bread up from the Undercourt's threshold — stolen honestly, which is to say a clerk had given it to me with a look that said *you'll need this more than the ledger needs the entry*. I broke it three ways. Warmth still lived in it somehow, or maybe that was just my hands pretending.

"Eat," I said. "Grieving burns more than you'd think. I've read about it."

"You haven't read anything," Odile said. "You can barely read."

"I read an entire contract aloud to a court of the dead. I'll thank you to remember."

"You *recited* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I held the candle."

Tam laughed through his tears, wet and cracked, and took the bread, and that was how we began the ascent: three people eating stolen bread under ten thousand returned stars, too tired to stand properly, too full of something unnamed to sit still either.

* * *

We climbed through the night, because none of us could bear to sleep under a roof of stone again, even a roof made of open sky and pine boughs. The mountain's southern face gave us switchbacks and frozen streams and one memorable stretch where Odile walked across a snowdrift that turned out to be a frozen waterfall, and we followed her because following Odile across obviously fatal surfaces had become, somewhere on the Thorn Road, simply what we did.

She talked as she walked. Odile always talked when her hands were busy and her heart was busier.

"She could have kept them, you know." Her breath plumed white. "The stars. Another century, maybe two. She'd have faded slower, hoarding them, feeding on them. The clerks told me — after, when they were unbinding everything — that the stars were the oldest magic left in the world. Older than wards. Older than debts. She stole them out of the sky the way a drowning woman steals a plank, and who among us wouldn't?" She kicked a stone off the path and watched it clatter away into darkness. "But she let go. In the end, dying, she opened her hands."

"She didn't want to collect," I said. "That's what I keep coming back to. Her signature. The clause swearing the debt would die with her. She wrote it herself, centuries ago, hoping she'd be dead before anyone called it in. And then her own court dragged it out of the archive because a debt unpaid is a court undone." I shook my head. "She spent her whole reign trying to lose a piece of paper."

"And you found it."

"I read it aloud." I smiled despite myself. My voice was wrecked — days of speaking over clerks and candles and a queen's unraveling silence had left me rasping like a rusted hinge. "Turns out that's the whole trick. The law beneath the law. They *have* to honor what's written. Even when it ruins them. Especially then."

Tam, trudging behind us with his arms wrapped around himself, said quietly: "She liked him. At the end. The boy who wouldn't bargain."

"She pitied him," Odile corrected.

"No." Tam shook his head. He'd known her longer than any of us, in the way you know a voice that lives in your skull. "Pity looks down. What she felt looked *across*. Like recognizing someone. Two people who never wanted the thing they were given." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own eloquence, and stuffed his hands in his armpits. "Anyway. She gave you the memory, Xanthe. That wasn't pity. Pity doesn't pay its debts."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. Because folded against my ribs, wrapped in the scrap of silk the clerks had provided with bureaucratic solemnity — *Returnable Upon Grief, Form 9, in triplicate* — was a memory. One memory. My mother's face, laughing.

I hadn't opened it yet. I hadn't dared. Some part of me feared that grief, given a face, would stop being a shapeless companion I'd learned to walk with and become something specific, something with eyes, something I'd have to look at every day for the rest of my life. Another part of me — the larger part, the part that had refused a queen — knew that was exactly why I had to.

Not yet, though. Not on the mountain. Some gifts you unwrap in private, or not at all.

* * *

Dawn caught us above the treeline, on a shoulder of bare granite furred with old snow, and we stopped because stopping was no longer optional. Our legs had unionized without our knowledge. We collapsed against a shelf of rock that some ancient glacier had polished smooth, and the sun came up over the eastern ridges and set every fresh snowfield burning rose and gold, and the stars faded out one by one like candles being snuffed by a considerate hand.

It was Tam who noticed the candle.

He'd been carrying it since the courtroom. Of course he had — he was the one the Queen had spoken through; it figured that he'd be the one she trusted with the last of her light. It was a stub of grey wax no bigger than a thumb, ordinary as anything, except that it burned without flame and without heat and without consuming itself, and its light was the exact color of the green glow we'd seen on the mountain's flank the night the stars died. She'd pressed it into his hands as the court dissolved around her, as her clerks bowed and scattered into the dark like startled moths, and she'd said — Tam repeated it now, in a fair imitation of that vast, tired voice:

"*Burn it where the living can see it. Not where I ruled. Where I wished I'd lived.*"

"So," Odile said, looking around at the bare granite, the wind-scoured snow, the enormous clean silence. "Above the treeline. Where nothing grows. Where nobody lives." She snorted. "Queens. Even the good ones give you impossible errands."

"There's a hollow." Tam was already moving, brushing snow from a shallow basin in the rock where a stunted pine had tried and failed to grow a hundred years ago and left behind a cup of stone. "Here. See?"

We dug together. It should have been grim work — burying the last light of a dead queen — but it wasn't. It was like planting. Odile broke the frozen crust with her knife-hilt, and I scooped, and Tam knelt and fitted the candle into the stone cup with the concentration of a boy arranging flowers on an altar, and when it was seated he looked up at us.

"Should we say something?"

"You say something," Odile said. "You knew her best."

"That's horrible."

"That's true."

He thought. The wind moved his hair. Far below us, the world was waking — a thread of smoke from some far valley hearth, a bird making its idiot morning argument, the long light climbing.

"She wanted to be a person," Tam said finally. "Not a queen. Not a collector. Just — a person, who got to choose what happened to her own soul. She never got to. So." He struck a match against the stone, though the candle needed no striking, and touched it to the wick anyway, because some gestures are for the living. "This one's yours. Choose wherever you want to go."

The candle flared green, then gold, then a color I don't have a name for — the color of the space between stars, maybe, the color of a debt forgiven — and sank slowly into the stone hollow like a coin into deep water, and the light stayed. It stays there still, I'm told. Travelers on the high passes see it on winter nights and call it the Queen's Lantern, and leave bread beside it, and no one remembers anymore that it was ever anything but a kindness.

We stood there a moment with our heads bent. Then Odile straightened, cracked her back like a dry branch, and said:

"Right. I don't know about either of you, but I have not bathed since Hollow Bells, I have lice I could name individually, and the nearest hot spring is — " she squinted southward — "two days that way, near Grayford, and the tollhouse keeper owes me a favor and a barrel of cider."

"You cheated him," I said.

"I *negotiated creatively*. He respects that. He told me so while chasing us."

Tam laughed — really laughed, belly-deep, the laugh of a boy whose head contained only himself and was delighted by the tenant — and slung his arm around my shoulders, and Odile took my other side, and the three of us started down the mountain the way we'd done everything else: badly, loudly, leaning on each other, arguing about whose turn it was to carry the bread.

Halfway down the first slope, I stopped.

Below us, the world lay open. Frost-killed farmland going green again at the edges — the long winter breaking early, now that the stars were home. The river Sere a bright thread. Beyond it, faint with distance, the walls of Vell, and beyond Vell the whole kingdom, waking up to find its wards dead and its king's secrets showing like bones through skin. We had no plan. We had no money. We had one memory I hadn't opened, a candle already planted, and each other.

"Xanthe?" Tam said. "What is it?"

I looked at my mother's face laughing — no. Not yet. I looked at the road instead, and found I was smiling so hard my ruined voice hurt.

"Nothing," I said. "Everything. Come on — I know a soup pot in Hollow Bells with our names on it."

They didn't ask what I meant. They never did. They just came, one on each side, and we went down together into the morning.
