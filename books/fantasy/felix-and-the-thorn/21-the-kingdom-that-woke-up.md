## 21. The Kingdom That Woke Up

**Xanthe**

The road south smelled different.

Xanthe noticed it before anything else—his tongue had always been the first part of him to know things. On the way north the air had tasted of frost and fear and the iron tang of a world with its wards torn open. Now it tasted of woodsmoke and wet wool and, faintly, absurdly, of bread. Someone in the valley below was baking, and the smell of it climbed the hillside to meet them like a dog that remembered being fed.

"You're smiling," Odile said, not looking up from where she was rewrapping her blistered feet. "You look ridiculous. You've been walking for three days and you look like a man who's been handed a present."

"I am," Xanthe said. "Bread. Real bread, from a real oven, made by someone who doesn't know yet that her whole kingdom changed while she slept." He breathed in again, deliberately, cataloguing it the way he'd been trained to catalogue everything: *woodsmoke, birch, wheat, salt, no thornroot, no nightshade, nothing wrong.* "I spent eleven years tasting other people's food for poison. I'd forgotten food could just be food."

Tam sat on a fallen log with his head tipped back, eyes closed, listening—to nothing, Xanthe knew, and weeping quietly about how beautiful nothing was. The mountain's whispers had left him at the treeline like a hand letting go. He kept reaching for them out of habit, the way an old man reaches for a wife of fifty years, and finding only morning birdsong.

"They'll have questions for us down there," Tam said without opening his eyes. "The valley people. About the stars. About the king."

"Yes," Xanthe said.

"And what are we going to tell them?"

Xanthe considered. Below them, Vell lay in its bowl like a dropped coin—the kitchens, the taster's quarters with their narrow beds and narrower rules, the great house where he had eaten poison so that a king could sleep soundly. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys. Somewhere a bell was ringing, but not the alarm bell; it had the loose, cheerful rhythm of a bell rung by someone who simply felt like ringing it.

"The truth," Xanthe said finally. "Or as much of it as they can stand before breakfast."

* * *

**Odile**

Odile had expected the valley to be in flames, or under martial law, or at minimum in a state of productive panic. What she found instead was a kingdom doing its laundry.

That was the thing about wards, she thought, watching a woman beat a rug against a fence post with the violence of the truly unbothered: most people never knew they were living inside one until it fell, and most people, once it fell, discovered they didn't much miss it. Oh, there were charms burning in doorways—she counted nine on the market street alone, crude little bundles of rowan and nail clippings, half of them tied wrong—but they were the charms of habit, not terror. Nobody had burned an effigy. Nobody had fled. A boy was selling roasted chestnuts *outside* the ward-line, which would have been unthinkable a season ago, and doing brisk trade in it.

"Your king's criers have been busy," she murmured to Xanthe. "Listen."

They listened. In the square, a herald with a voice like a cracked pot was reading from a paper, and the paper was not the usual litany of grain prices and curfew hours. It was, of all things, an apology.

*"...and His Majesty regrets to inform the people of Vell that the valley's protection, long maintained by means the crown is not at liberty to describe, has failed, and cannot be restored. His Majesty further regrets—"*

"Regrets," Odile repeated softly. "There's a word I never thought to hear out of that office."

She knew why, of course. She'd known since Grayford, when the toll-collector's ledger had shown her more than she'd let on. Kings did not apologize. Kings issued decrees and called their mistakes weather. An apology meant either a new king or a very frightened old one, and she intended to find out which before she let these two boys anywhere near the great house.

What she found, asking careful questions of a fishwife who talked the way rivers run, was neither.

"He's still up there," the fishwife said, jerking her chin toward the great house. "But he doesn't sit the throne anymore, if you follow me. Sits in his rooms. Has his meals sent up. The Lord Spymaster reads the petitions now—him and that council of theirs—and the strange thing is, nobody minds. Man's been running the country for years anyway, far as anyone can tell. King just signed things."

"And the debts?" Odile asked, casual as a knife going into a sheath. "The crown owed money, I've heard said. Old money. To old creditors."

The fishwife leaned in, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman who had waited all winter for exactly this conversation. "Paid off," she whispered. "Every last one. There was a clerk came down from the mountain itself—not dead-looking at all, well, mostly not—a fortnight past, with a book this big"—she held her hands a loaf-width apart—"and read out every debt the crown ever carried, and struck them through, one by one, in front of the whole council. Said the court beneath had closed its books. Said no more would be collected, from anyone, forever. Then he paid for his supper with real silver and left."

Odile stood very still among the fish stalls.

Closed its books. She thought of candlelit caverns and clerks weighing souls like coins; of a dying queen who had hoarded the stars because she could not bear to be extinguished alone; of a ledger turned backward by a taster with flour still, somehow, under his fingernails. She thought of her sister, whose soul had walked free into whatever comes next, carrying no debt at all.

"Well," she said, when she could speak steadily. "Imagine that."

* * *

**Tam**

Tam heard the spymaster before he saw him, which was a new experience, because for two months he had heard only the mountain and now he heard everything—cart wheels, gulls, the particular squeak of a boot trying to be quiet on gravel—and all of it was his, none of it belonged to something else.

The boot squeaked behind the woodshed where Tam had gone, ostensibly to fetch kindling, actually to be alone with the enormous private fact that he was home and home didn't want him chained to a kitchen anymore and he had no idea who he was if not a kitchen boy who heard voices.

"You've gotten slower," said Master Corvane.

Tam spun. The spymaster stood at the edge of the yard in a plain gray coat, no badge, no guards, holding a paper-wrapped parcel that smelled of meat pies. Up close he looked older than Tam remembered, and tired in a way that went all the way through, like a wall with water behind it.

"You shouldn't sneak up on people," Tam said. "People round here startle easy. We've had a winter."

"We've had a revelation," Corvane corrected. "It's worse." He held out the parcel. Tam took it after a moment, because refusing meat pies was against his religion. "Where are the others?"

"Why do you want to know?"

Corvane smiled, and it was the same smile he'd worn feeding Xanthe dinner in the great hall years ago, patient and a little sad, a smile that had done a great deal of work and believed in almost none of it. "Because the council will ask within the hour, and my answer determines whether they spend spring hunting three heroes or toasting them. I've already told them the taster died on the mountain. Grief is useful that way—it closes ledgers. But I find I'd rather tell the truth, if the truth is willing to stand up straight."

Tam chewed. He was aware this was not a dignified pause but he was seventeen and starving and the pie was excellent. "They're at the inn by the south gate," he said finally. "Xanthe wanted to see the valley first. From the outside. He said—" Tam swallowed. "He said he wanted to know if it missed him."

"And does it?"

"The valley?" Tam shrugged. "Valleys don't miss people. Kitchens do, though. Cook Marta's been leaving a plate out every night since the stars came back. Nobody eats it. She won't stop."

Something moved behind the spymaster's eyes—quick, buried, real. "No," he agreed quietly. "She wouldn't."

He turned to go, then stopped. "Boy. When the mountain spoke to you. Did it ever say what it wanted with him? Truly?"

Tam thought about the whispers, the cold silk of them, promising safety, promising warmth, promising that a small life could be spared if a smaller one was given away. He thought about a queen alone in the dark, buying time with stolen light.

"It wanted to not die by itself," Tam said. "That's all any of it ever wanted, I think. The mountain. The king. Maybe even you."

Corvane took that the way Tam had seen him take poison reports: without flinching, and filing it somewhere permanent. "Eat your pie," he said, and was gone between one boot-squeak and the next.

* * *

**Xanthe**

They came for him at dusk, which Xanthe appreciated; he had always done his best thinking at dusk, over the cooling remains of the king's supper, tongue cataloguing, mind elsewhere. Now there was no supper to taste and the thinking was all his own, and it had brought him to the kitchen garden behind the great house, standing among the frozen stubble of last year's herbs, looking up at a window where a lamp burned behind closed shutters.

The king's window. The king had once waved from it, Xanthe remembered, a small ceremonial wave at some festival, and the whole courtyard had cheered, and twelve-year-old Xanthe had cheered too, because he hadn't yet known that the man above him had been counting the days until he could hand a child to a mountain like settling a butcher's bill.

"You could throw a stone," Odile said beside him. "I'm just saying. I have stones."

"You always have stones."

"A hedge-witch travels prepared."

The door opened before Xanthe could answer, and Master Corvane stepped out into the blue evening with his hands visible and empty, an old negotiator's courtesy. Behind him, in the doorway, stood a woman in cook's whites with flour to her elbows, and Xanthe's heart did something complicated and painful, because it was Marta, Marta who had slipped him extra bread for eleven years and never once asked why a boy should live his whole life inside a valley, and she was crying and pretending very hard that she wasn't.

"You're supposed to be dead," Marta said thickly. "I've been setting out a plate for a dead boy for a month. Do you know what that does to a person? Get in here. Both of you. All three of you. There's stew."

"There's always stew," Xanthe said, and his voice cracked on it, and he decided to let it.

* * *

Later—stew eaten, tears shed and denied, Tam asleep with his cheek on the table like a child at a feast—Corvane laid it all out for them by lamplight, in the flat voice of a man reading a report he wished he didn't have to write.

"The wards cannot be renewed. The stone that anchored them was Undercourt work, a loan, and loans are recalled. So the valley is simply... land now. Like everywhere else. Cold winters, honest borders, no magic at all unless someone carries it in." He glanced at Odile. "Which some people consider an improvement."

"Some people," Odile agreed, "have excellent taste."

"The king—" Corvane paused, and for a moment Xanthe saw the cost of the sentence before the sentence came. "The king signed his abdication nine days ago. Quietly. No trial, no scaffold—he asked for that, and the council granted it, partly from mercy and partly because a public trial would have required explaining what he'd bargained, and there are families in this valley who sold things too, small things, desperate things, and don't need their names read aloud. He lives in the east wing. He takes his meals alone. He has asked, once, whether the taster survived."

Xanthe set down his cup. "And what did you tell him?"

"That you did. And that you declined to visit."

"Was he angry?"

"No," Corvane said. "He was relieved. That's the worst of it, isn't it. He was never cruel the way monsters are cruel. He was cruel the way ledgers are cruel—everything balanced, nothing forgiven. I served him thirty years and I never once saw him enjoy a meal." He looked at Xanthen across the table, and the lamplight caught the gray in his beard. "You could have a place here. You understand that. The council knows your name now—they know whose signature unmade the Undercourt's books. They'll offer you a title, a stipend, probably a chair with your name carved on the back. They're drafting the letter tonight."

"I know," Xanthe said. "A herald started reading it in the square this afternoon. I heard the first line and left."

"And your answer?"

Xanthe looked around the kitchen—at Marta pretending to scrub a pot that was already clean, at Odile stealing the last onion out of the basket with the unbothered grace of a lifelong thief, at Tam drooling gently onto the table he'd once been beaten for resting his elbows on. He thought about chairs with names carved on the back, and about a soup pot big enough for anyone who owed nothing, and about a windowsill in Hollow Bells that was waiting for a plate.

"My mother sold one year of warmth," he said, "and it bought a kingdom's worth of trouble. I think I'd rather give warmth away free and see what that buys." He stood, and offered the spymaster his hand. "Tell the council thank you. Tell them the taster is dead—let them keep that. And tell them if they ever want to find me, they know where the hungry people are."

Corvane took the hand and held it a moment longer than necessary. "Thirty years I've tasted power," he said. "Yours is the first that didn't leave a bitterness."

"That," said Xanthe, "is the nicest thing anyone's ever said about my tongue."

Marta threw a dish towel at him. Outside, the stars—every single one of them, returned and burning—came out over a kingdom learning, slowly, clumsily, gently, to govern itself awake.
