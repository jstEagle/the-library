## 1. The Smuggler's Latitude

The pass did not exist yet. Wren Nystrom could feel that the way other people felt weather coming — a pressure behind the eyes, a hollowness in the teeth, the particular silence of a mountain holding its breath before it decided what shape to be.

They stood at the mouth of it, at the place where the smugglers' trail gave out against a wall of grey granite, and behind them waited eleven wagons, forty-one mules, and a caravan master named Oskell who had paid them more silver than Wren had ever held at once and trusted them with less than they'd ever been trusted with. The Thornfells rose ahead, folded and refolded like cloth shaken out by a giant's hands, and somewhere inside those folds was a road that would carry them over the spine of the range without touching a single crown road, a single toll gate, a single pair of watching eyes.

There was only one problem, and it was the same problem there always was: no such road existed. Not yet.

Wren unrolled the leather case across a flat stone — the good case, oiled calfskin, buckled twice against the wind — and drew out the tools of a trade that had no name anyone would speak aloud. A pen of hollow reed. Three bottles of ink: oak gall for ordinary work, iron-and-ash for stubborn places, and the third bottle, small as a thumb joint, wrapped in waxed linen, whose contents Wren had never shown another living soul. They called it nightwater when they called it anything at all, and it was the color of a starless sky seen from very far away, and it moved on the page if you let it sit too long, curling at its edges like something dreaming.

Wren dipped the reed pen into the ordinary ink first. That was the discipline of it, the ritual, the thing Halvard had drilled into them across nine years of cold mornings and colder lessons: *the map comes first, and the world comes second, and never confuse which one is begging.*

They began to draw.

It was not drawing the way a portraitist draws, or even the way a surveyor draws, careful and true. It was closer to prayer, if prayer were done with a steady hand and a bad temper. Wren sketched the line of the ridge as they *wanted* it to be — a shoulder of stone sloping down toward a notch between two peaks, a shelf wide enough for wagons, a grade gentle enough that the mules wouldn't balk. Then, below it, the road itself: two parallel strokes, close together, with the small hatch-marks that meant *firm footing* in the private shorthand Halvard had invented and sworn Wren never to teach.

And then Wren looked up, and the mountain had begun to listen.

It always started small. A scree slope that should have slid shifted instead, stones settling into the exact contour of the drawn line, finding their seats with a sound like a long exhale. A seam of shadow along the granite deepened where the ink said a crevice ran. The notch between the peaks — which, an hour ago, had been solid rock all the way through — now showed a thread of pale sky through it, thin as a wire, widening as Wren watched.

"Gods below," breathed Tam, the outrider, a wiry woman with a face like a closed fist. She'd seen it a dozen times and never gotten used to it. "It's doing it again."

"It's not an *it*," Wren said, capping the ink. "It's a pass. It's just agreeing with me."

"That's worse," Tam said, "if you think about it."

Oskell came up the trail at his heavy rolling walk, a man built like a wine barrel with opinions, and squinted at the notch, then at Wren, then back at the notch. He had the particular expression of a man who has decided not to ask questions because the answers might cost him money.

"How long?" he asked.

"By midmorning we ride through."

"And it holds? The road?"

"It holds as long as I keep it drawn." Wren tapped the leather case. "As long as this stays dry, stays whole, and stays mine."

Oskell grunted, which among caravan masters was a hymn of praise. He spat, wiped his mouth, and went back down to shout at the muleteers, and Wren stayed alone at the head of the trail with the wind combing through the coarse grass and the mountain slowly, patiently becoming the thing they had promised it would be.

This was the gift, though *gift* was a generous word for it. Halvard had preferred *affliction*. Wren could chart realms that existed only in memory — and more than that, could make memory into ground. Draw a bridge where no bridge was, and if the drawing was true enough, if the hand was steady enough and the will behind it hard enough, boots would one day cross it. The world, it seemed, was a manuscript forever awaiting revision, and someone, somewhere, had given Wren the editor's pen without asking whether they wanted the job.

They had not wanted the job. Nobody wanted the job. But you did not get to choose what you were; you only got to choose what you did about it, and Wren had chosen the mountains, and the smugglers, and a life spent drawing roads that officially had never been built by anyone, anywhere, ever.

For nine years it had worked. For nine years, the crown's surveyors had ridden past these valleys shaking their heads at maps that refused to match the ground, and the magistrates had shrugged, and the toll collectors had gone unpaid, and Wren had grown from a half-starved runaway into something the smugglers called *our cartographer* with a possessiveness that was almost tender.

Uneasy freedom. Borrowed time. Halvard's words, always, whenever Wren got comfortable. *Nothing drawn is safe, child. Anything that can be made can be unmade, and anything unmade leaves a scar.*

Wren hadn't understood him yet. That was the trouble with being young and gifted: you thought the lesson was for later.

* * *

The caravan moved through the notch at midmorning exactly as promised, and it was, Wren allowed themselves to feel for perhaps a quarter of an hour, magnificent.

The pass wound down between walls of stone streaked white with old snowmelt, the light falling in long slanted bars, the mules' bells ringing off the rock in bright scattered echoes. Below, a valley opened — green-gold, threaded with a stream that caught the sun and threw it back in pieces. There were no roads here on any crown survey. There were no roads here at all, except the one under the wagon wheels, which had not existed three hours ago and now bore ruts, and dung, and the particular trampled look of a road that had always been there and always would be.

That was the strange part, the part Wren still hadn't stopped marveling at after all these years: how quickly the world forgot it had been otherwise. By afternoon, the muleteers were complaining about the pass as though it were an old enemy — *that cursed narrow bit above the falls, always iced* — inventing grievances against a road that had been born before lunch. Memory was soft. The world wanted to be mapped. Give it a line and it would believe the line had always run there.

Wren rode at the front with the case strapped across their chest, redrawing as they went — a culvert here where meltwater needed somewhere to go, a switchback eased there where the grade turned cruel. Small corrections. Maintenance. The way a shepherd mends a fence. Each stroke of the pen settled the world a little further into agreement, and each settlement cost a little something Wren couldn't name — a faint fatigue behind the sternum, a sense of having spent rather than earned.

Halvard had a theory about that. Everything drawn was borrowed from somewhere. Nothing was free. Wren had nodded and privately filed the theory under *old man worries*, the vast category of things Halvard said that Wren would only understand in retrospect, probably too late, probably painfully.

They camped that night in a hanging meadow the map insisted on, and it was there, just past dusk, that the world offered its first objection.

Tam found it. She came into the firelight walking fast, face unreadable, and dropped something onto the ground between Wren's boots: a stone, flat, the size of a plate. On its surface, worn nearly smooth by weather but still legible, was carved a single line of text in the old high script of the range:

**NO ROAD WAS EVER DRAWN HERE.**

Wren stared at it for a long time.

"Where," they said finally.

"Half a mile back, set upright beside the trail. Like a marker." Tam crouched, turning the stone with her boot. "Old. Older than the smuggler routes. Someone put it there a long time ago and meant it to last."

"Meant what to last?"

"The warning." Tam looked up, firelight catching the flat challenge in her face. "You tell me, mapper. You're the one who knows what lives up here besides us."

Wren didn't answer. They picked up the stone, weighed it, and set it carefully at the edge of the firelight, face-up, as though it might be offended by neglect. Around them the meadow lay quiet, the wagons drawn into their defensive ring, sentries posted, everything ordinary. But Wren lay awake most of the night listening to the mountain breathe, and thinking about scars, and about how every act of making was also, somewhere, an act of trespass — and wondering, for the first time in nine comfortable years, who else had ever held a pen like theirs, and what they had written, and why they had stopped.

* * *

They came down out of the high country on the fourth day, into the long pine-shadowed descent toward the lowland waystation at Coldharrow Ford, and it was there, nailed to the customs house door with four fresh iron nails, that the borrowed time ran out.

Wren saw the crowd before they saw the paper. Waystations in the Thornfell lowlands were quiet places, half empty even on market days, and this one had gathered every soul within six miles: drovers, charcoal burners, a knot of pilgrims, two crown post-riders whose presence suddenly explained everything. At the center of the crowd, a town clerk with a voice like a cracked bell was reading aloud, and the crowd was making the sound crowds make when they are frightened and don't want to admit it — a low shuffling murmur, laughter that isn't laughter.

Wren left Oskell with the wagons and pushed forward through the press of bodies, and read the proclamation over a charcoal-burner's shoulder.

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN, it began, in letters tall enough to read from horseback, and Wren's eye went down the lines the way water goes downhill, fast and helpless.

*Let it be known throughout the realm that certain persons exist who possess the forbidden art of shaping place by mark and measure — who make roads where none were laid, walls where none were raised, and waters flow where nature intended drought. These persons are declared enemies of the realm. They are to be seized wherever found. Their works are to be destroyed. Those who shelter them share their guilt.*

And beneath that, smaller, in the clerk's own hand as he transcribed it onto the posting copy, the sentence that took the air out of the world:

*Reward of five hundred crowns for each such person delivered alive to the magistrate at Greywater. Two hundred crowns for information leading to capture.*

Five hundred crowns. Wren did the arithmetic without wanting to. Five hundred crowns was what Oskell had paid for the entire crossing. Five hundred crowns was a farm, or a boat, or ten years of a family's bread. Five hundred crowns was what any soul in this crowd could earn by saying, *that one, the one standing right there with the leather case.*

The clerk droned on. Somewhere behind Wren, a woman whispered to her neighbor, and the neighbor said, "Shh," and both of them looked — briefly, involuntarily — at everyone around them, doing sums of their own.

Wren stepped back out of the crowd the way you step back from a well you've realized is deeper than your rope. The morning was bright. The pines smelled of resin and cold. Nothing had changed, and everything had changed, and the difference lived entirely inside a piece of paper nailed to a door.

They found Oskell loading the last of the cargo, and told him nothing, and watched him read the proclamation himself ten minutes later with the expression of a man recalculating every contract he'd ever signed. To his credit — and Wren would remember this, would hold onto it in the dark days coming — Oskell said nothing either. He simply finished lashing the load, walked over, and stood close enough that his shoulder touched Wren's, which in the language of caravan masters was a speech.

"You'll want to travel separate from us now," he said quietly. "Safer for both."

"I know."

"There's a place up-valley. Old man, keeps goats, owes me money. Halvard." He pronounced the name carefully, like a coin he wasn't sure was real. "You know him?"

"He taught me everything I know."

"Then you know more than the crown does." Oskell spat, resettled his hat, and became brisk again, the moment of tenderness folded away like a map. "Go at dusk. Take the north track. And Nystrom—" he paused, not looking at them, "—whatever it is you do with that case, do it quietly for a while. Mountains are patient. Crowns aren't."

Wren went at dusk, as instructed, taking the north track with the case strapped tight and the third bottle riding cold against their ribs like a second heart. Behind them, the waystation lights dwindled, and the proclamation hung on its door in the dark, five hundred crowns glowing in every window of every farmhouse between there and the horizon.

They walked all night. Toward dawn, climbing into the high pine country where the smuggler trails thinned to deer paths, Wren stopped on a ridge to catch breath and looked back the way they had come — and saw, far below, a thread of smoke rising from the direction of Coldharrow Ford. Too much smoke for cookfires. Too black for wood.

They stood there a long moment, chest heaving, telling themselves it was nothing, telling themselves the crown burned proclamations all the time, telling themselves whatever a frightened person tells themselves at dawn on a ridge with a price on their head.

Then they turned north, toward Halvard, toward the deep folds of the Thornfells where the maps gave out entirely — and began, for the first time in nine years, to run.
