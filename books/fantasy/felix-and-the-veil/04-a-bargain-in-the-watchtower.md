## 4. A Bargain in the Watchtower

They came for Wren at dusk, when the light went the color of old pewter and the pines along the ridge stood black as ink spilled down a page.

Wren had chosen the hollow beneath the fallen fir deliberately — a place that did not exist on any map, because until three days ago it had not existed at all. They had drawn it into being with cold fingers and a stub of graphite, sketching the overhang, the seep of groundwater, the screen of bracken, and the mountain had obliged, folding itself around the drawing like a hand closing over a coin. It was the oldest trick they knew and the truest: *a thing drawn is a thing made.* For eleven years in the Thornfells it had kept them fed, dry, invisible. Tonight it failed them.

The failure announced itself as a wrongness in the paper.

Wren was mending their boots by lamplight, the little oil flame shuttered to a bead, when the map spread across their knees — the working copy of the pass, soft as cloth from handling — shivered. Not wind. Wind moved the whole sheet. This was a single tremor, localized, like a pulse under skin, and it came from the western margin where Wren had sketched the ruined watchtower at Grey Hollow two seasons past, back when it had been nothing but a crown garrison abandoned to crows.

Someone was standing inside the drawing.

Not physically — the tower was half a day's hard walk away, across the scree and the frozen stream. But the ink knew what the ink knew, and the figure Wren had never bothered to render now stood rendered anyway: a tall shape in the doorway of the tower's ground floor, drawn in a hand that was not Wren's, in lines so confident they seemed less added than *revealed*. The figure held something flat and rectangular. A ledger. A journal.

Halvard's journal.

Wren's hands went still around the boot needle. The fire-bead hissed. Somewhere above the hollow, gravel clicked under a deliberate boot heel, once, twice — the sound of someone who wanted to be heard.

"You draw well," said a voice from the dark at the rim of the overhang. "Better than the reports suggested. The pass you opened last spring — the one the smugglers call the Needle's Eye — I walked it in June. Halfway through, I realized the road only existed where your ink had been." A pause. "I've served the crown's survey office for fifteen years, and I have never seen anything like it."

Wren did not reach for the knife. Reaching would tell the voice everything. Instead they closed the map slowly, keeping Halvard's name behind their teeth, and said, "If you're a witch-finder, you're wasting your breath. I'm just a guide."

"Guides don't leave roads behind them like snail-trails of silver. Guides don't make mountains agree with them." The voice was low, unhurried, and carried an accent Wren placed after a moment — the capital, the old quarter near the cartographers' hall, vowels rounded by expensive schooling now worn rough by exile. "My name is Felix Arden. You may remember the name. It was struck from the rolls six years ago."

*Felix Arden.* Even in the mountains, even among smugglers who could not read, the name had weight. The royal cartographer who had gone mad — that was the court's word — who had stood in the map hall and accused the king's own shadow of treason before the assembled ministers, who had been stripped of office and very nearly of his right hand, and who had vanished into the hill country with a handful of malcontents and a grievance the size of a kingdom.

"They say you're dead," Wren said.

"The crown says a great many things." Gravel shifted; a shape resolved against the pewter sky — a lean man in a grey traveling cloak, hood back, face all planes and shadow except for eyes that caught the last light and held it like a lens. He did not step into the hollow. He understood, then, what the hollow was. That he understood frightened Wren more than the knife at his belt would have. "There are four of us on this ridge, Nystrom. Two above you, one below, one standing in your drawing of my tower, which — I'll confess — took me some effort to enter. I'd like to talk. But if you run, we will follow, and I'd rather not do anything either of us regrets."

"And if I refuse to talk?"

"Then I'll talk, and you can listen. It's how most of history gets made."

He crouched at the rim of the overhang, forearms on his knees, close enough for Wren to see the burn-scar along his jaw — old, deliberate-looking, the kind of mark left by a seal pressed hot against flesh. Exile's brand. He wore it uncovered, unhidden, the way other men wore medals.

"I know what happened on the Salt Road," he said. "Crown soldiers took a caravan two days ago. Took an old man named Halvard Voss, a map-merchant, sixty-eight years old, bad lungs, worse temper. The soldiers were looking for someone else. Someone whose drawings keep appearing in places no road was ever surveyed."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Wren watched the ripples spread and could do nothing about any of them. "You've been following me."

"Since the Needle's Eye. Serah Voss picked up the trail first — she's crown, a finder, she smells drawn places the way dogs smell blood — but we picked it up from *her*, which makes us slower but better company." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and gave up halfway. "She lost you at the fold. Walking through a ridge you folded out of the world — that was well done. She nearly broke her own senses trying to follow. But she doesn't quit, and neither do I, and there are more of her coming."

"Why?" The word came out harder than Wren intended. "Why does a disgraced cartographer with four rebels on a ridge care whether I live?"

"Because I need you to help me prove the king is innocent."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren heard the seep-water ticking into its pool, the pines working against the wind, their own heartbeat loud and stupid in their ears.

"Innocent," Wren repeated.

"Of everything," Felix said. "Every levy, every burning, every village emptied on his order, every gifted child taken from its parents' arms. None of it is his. The king is not mad. He is *bound* — his mind stitched to something ancient that wears him the way a hand wears a glove. I have spent six years gathering proof, and every proof has a hole in it, and every hole is shaped exactly like the one person alive who can map what cannot be mapped." He leaned forward. "The Veil, Nystrom. The borderland where stolen memories are kept. I know it exists. I know what it holds. What I don't know is the way in — and you are the only cartographer in this kingdom who draws roads into existence instead of merely recording them."

Wren laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You want me to map a fairy story."

"I want you to save an old man."

Everything stopped. The water, the wind, the heart.

"What did you say?"

"Halvard Voss is alive. He's in the dungeons at Grey Hollow — the garrison below the watchtower, not the tower itself. He hasn't been transported to the capital yet; the convoy waits for the thaw. Which means there is time. Which means—" Felix paused, and Wren saw him choose his next words with the care of a man laying bridge-stones over a gorge, "—which means there is a bargain available. My people shelter you. We move you beyond Serah Voss's reach. And when the time comes, you help me reach the king. In exchange, when we take Grey Hollow — and we will take it, within the month — Halvard walks free."

"You can't promise that."

"I can promise I'll die trying, and I have a reputation for meaning it."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then: "No."

"No?"

"No. I don't know you. I don't know your rebels or your cause or your king-in-a-glove story. I know smugglers and passes and how to stay alive, and staying alive means staying alone. Take your bargain to someone foolish enough to believe it." They began rolling the map, hands steady through long practice, though nothing inside them was steady at all. "And stay out of my drawings. It's rude."

Felix didn't move. "May I show you one thing before you go? One page. Then I'll walk away, and you'll never see me again unless you want to."

"Nothing you have—"

"It's Halvard's journal."

The roll of the map stopped. Outside, the light was going from pewter to ash, and in that failing brightness Wren watched Felix Arden reach inside his cloak and draw out a small book bound in oiled leather, swollen with damp, its corners worn round by years of a coat pocket. Wren knew that book. Had seen it a thousand mornings, tucked beside the camp kettle while Halvard drank his bitter tea and wrote in it with a carpenter's pencil, shielding the pages with his forearm like a schoolboy. Had teased him about it. *What's in there, old man, your sins?* And Halvard had grinned with his three remaining teeth and said, *Only the ones that matter.*

"He had it on him when they took him," Felix said quietly. "A soldier sold it to one of mine for a bottle of rotgut. Soldiers sell everything. It's the one honest economy left in this kingdom." He held it out across the rim of the hollow, arm extended, and did not come closer, and somehow that restraint — that understanding of exactly how far he could reach before Wren bolted — was the most persuasive thing he'd done all evening. "Read the entry dated the ninth of Frostmoon. Twenty-two years ago."

Wren took the journal.

It weighed what a life weighs. They opened it with a care that surprised them, thumbs finding the date by the ragged edge of the pages, and read:

*Ninth of Frostmoon. Cold enough tonight that the ink froze in the pot and I had to warm it over the candle like a granny. The boy asked me again why the king never comes to the winter fair, and I told him the truth, which is that I don't know, and he looked at me with those sharp eyes of his and said, "Then find out." So I am writing here what I would never say aloud: I think the king is not absent but kept. I have seen the palace surveys — the old foundations, the rooms that appear on no plan, the corridor beneath the throne that every surveyor measures differently and no two ever measure the same. There is a door down there older than the dynasty. Old Marta in the kitchens says her grandmother called it the Veil, and that the founders put things behind it that the world was safer without. Memories, she said. The founders stored memories there like grain against famine. And every king since has been set to guard the storehouse — no, worse than guard. Stitched to it. Bound, the way you bind a dog to a door so it cannot follow you into the street.*

*I told no one. I am telling this book. If I am ever taken, let whoever reads this understand: the old man was not mad either. He was afraid.*

Below, in a different ink, newer, the pencil pressed so hard it had torn the paper: *I found the boy. The cartographer's orphan from the fire. I erased what needed erasing to keep the Veil quiet, God forgive me, and I have watched over the child ever since, and the child does not know what they are, and someday the Veil will want it back.*

Wren read the last line four times. The words did not change. The mountain did not change. Only something in the center of Wren's chest changed, quietly and completely, the way a landscape changes when a river cuts a new channel overnight.

*The cartographer's orphan from the fire.* A burning house. A woman calling a name that wasn't Wren. Fragments that surfaced sometimes in dreams and sank again before they could be charted, soundings in water too deep to fathom. Halvard had found them wandering the Salt Road at nine years old with no name they could recall and no grief they could locate, and he had said, *You're mine now,* and that had been the whole of it, and Wren had never thought to ask why an aging map-merchant with no family had been walking a burned stretch of borderland in the first place.

He had known. All those years, all that bitter tea, all those evenings with the journal shielded under his forearm — he had known what Wren was, and what had been taken from them, and he had carried it alone rather than break the peace of the only family either of them had.

"Wren." Felix's voice, gentle now, the schooling showing through. "I didn't bring you that page to wound you. I brought it because it proves two things. First, that the Veil is real — your mentor staked his soul on it twenty years ago. Second, that the binding is real, because Halvard helped make it." He let that sit. "Your old man has blood on his hands, Nystrom. So do I. So, I suspect, do you, though yours may be someone else's choosing. That's what the crown does. It takes ordinary people and makes them instruments. I mean to unmake the instrument. I need a cartographer to do it. And I think — I *think* — you need to know what was taken from you, and the only road to that knowledge runs through the Veil."

Wren closed the journal. Held it a moment longer than necessary. Then held it out.

"Keep it," Felix said. "It was never mine. And it was never truly theirs, whatever the soldier thinks he bought." He rose, gravel whispering under his boots, and the two shapes on the ridge above melted back from the skyline with a discipline that spoke of long practice. "The offer stands. Shelter, passage, Halvard's freedom when Grey Hollow falls. In return, your maps, and eventually — only when you choose it — the Veil. Think on it. We'll be at the watchtower. You already drew the road; it seems rude not to use it."

He turned to go.

"Felix." The name came out of Wren before they had decided to spend it. He stopped. "The convoy for the capital. When does the thaw come?"

"Eighteen days. Nineteen if the wind stays north."

Wren looked down at the journal in their hands, at the torn page, at the sentence that had rewritten them: *the child does not know what they are.* Eighteen days. Enough time to tell themselves a hundred times that this was only about rescue — about an old man in a cell with bad lungs, about a debt between the last two people who had ever been family. Enough time to keep the other reason locked in the same dark room where Halvard had kept his twenty years of secrets, the reason with a woman's voice calling a name that wasn't Wren's, waiting behind a door older than the dynasty.

"Draw me into your tower," Wren said. "In your map. Properly, this time — I'll dictate the lines. And put a bed in it. And if any of your four friends tries to take this journal from me while I sleep, I will fold your entire watchtower into a piece of paper small enough to lose."

For the first time, Felix Arden smiled all the way.

"Welcome to the rebellion, Cartographer."

* * *

The watchtower at Grey Hollow, as rendered in Felix's field-book, was a humble thing: two floors of good stone, a stair, a hearth, arrow-slits that framed the darkening peaks like slanted eyes. But when Wren walked up the switchback trail an hour later and saw the actual tower rising against the last bruised band of sunset, they understood what kind of man they had bargained with. The real tower matched the drawing line for line — except that the drawing had shown a second floor, and the real one had only ever had one, and as Wren crossed the threshold, the stone overhead groaned softly, like an old ship taking new cargo, and became two floors.

"You drew it into being," Wren said, staring up the new stairwell. "You can't. No one can. That's not how the gift works — it's supposed to be mine alone, it's supposed to be—"

"Yours works on places," Felix said, climbing ahead of them, his voice echoing off stone that had not existed that morning. "Mine works on plans. I cannot make a road exist. But give me a drawing of a room, and the world will furnish it. They cut the gift out of the rolls when they exiled me — called it forgery, called it madness, called it treason." He glanced back, and the burn-scar caught the lamplight. "They were right about one thing. It is treason. Everything worth doing is, now."

At the top of the new stairs, in a room that smelled of fresh-cut timber and lamp oil, a bed stood made up with a grey blanket, and on the blanket lay the journal, returned to its place, and beside it a cup of tea already poured, already cooling, bitter in the way Halvard had always brewed it.

Wren stood in the doorway a long time.

It was only about rescue, they told themselves, setting the cup untouched on the sill. An old man in a cell. Eighteen days. Nothing more.

But that night, sleeping in a room that had not existed a week ago, Wren dreamed of fire, and of a woman's voice calling a name they could not quite hear — and woke with the terrible, certain knowledge that somewhere beneath a palace in the capital, behind a door older than the dynasty, that name was waiting. And that they had just agreed to go and find it.
