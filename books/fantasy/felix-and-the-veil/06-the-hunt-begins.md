## 6. The Hunt Begins

The road to the capital did not exist, and so Wren made it.

They traveled as a survey crew — nine of them in dust-gray coats with brass buttons polished dull, driving two carts heaped with tripods, measuring chains, and crates stenciled with the Crown Survey Office's seal, which Felix had forged so well that Wren had to look twice to see the lie. The disguise was elegant in its audacity: no one questioned a survey crew, because no one wanted to be the fool who delayed the king's roads. Soldiers waved them through checkpoints with bored eyes. Farmers pulled their carts aside and touched their caps. A tax collector at the Ferrow ford actually apologized for the delay his toll bridge caused His Majesty's surveyors, and Felix had accepted the apology with such grave courtesy that Wren had to turn away to hide a smile.

But the road itself — that was Wren's work, done quietly, hour by hour, from the back of the second cart.

It was not drawing in the way most people understood drawing. It was more like remembering something that had never happened. Wren would close their eyes, feel for the shape of the land ahead — the true land, the bones of it beneath whatever the crown's mapmakers had decided was there — and then lay a road over those bones like a hand smoothing a wrinkle from cloth. Where the pass narrowed, the road narrowed. Where a river cut too deep to ford, the road climbed and found a ridge that no one had surveyed but which had always, in some patient geological sense, been waiting to be walked. The ink went down on the page in Wren's lap, and the world rearranged itself around the ink, politely, as if it had only been waiting to be asked.

The others felt it too, though they didn't speak of it. They simply noticed that the journey was easier than it should have been, that the miles folded up like a fan, that they reached the Ferrow ford a day and a half before any honest traveler could have. Tam Ossa, the broad-shouldered woman who drove the lead cart, kept glancing back at Wren with an expression caught between gratitude and unease, the way one looks at a fire that is burning exactly where you need it and nowhere else.

"You could make us a road straight into the palace," Tam said on the fourth morning, only half joking. "Save us all this trudging."

"Roads go where roads are wanted," Wren said. "That's the trick of it. I can't draw a door where nobody means to open one."

It wasn't entirely true, and they both knew it. But it was a comfortable lie, the kind you tell about your own gift so you can sleep at night.

Felix rode at the rear, playing the part of the senior surveyor with a precision that sometimes frightened Wren — the way he adjusted his spectacles when soldiers approached, the way he complained about gravel and drainage in a voice pitched exactly between authority and tedium. He had been born to this court, whatever he claimed now. Exile had not burned it out of him; it had only taught him to wear it like a costume.

On the fifth day, Felix drew his horse alongside Wren's cart and said, without preamble: "We're being followed."

Wren didn't look up from the page. "How long?"

"Since the ford. Maybe since before." He passed a hand over his eyes. "There's a rider who keeps appearing at the edge of my sight and then not being there. Gray cloak. No livery. And yesterday, at the toll bridge, I saw her talking to the collector — not asking directions. Asking *questions*. About us."

"About the crew?"

"About the road." Felix's jaw tightened. "She asked how many bridges we'd crossed, how many fords, whether any of them seemed *new*."

The word sat between them like a stone dropped into still water. Wren looked down at the map spread across their knees — the living map, the one whose lines breathed faintly when they weren't looking, the one that had carried them three days through country that should have taken six.

Someone had noticed that the miles were wrong.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below an old quarry, and Felix doubled the watch, and Wren sat apart with the map and tried to think.

Here was the problem, laid out as plainly as a surveyor's chain: every mile of road Wren drew left a residue. Not on the land — the land forgot quickly enough, sliding back into its old shapes within a season or two. No, the residue was in the *drawing*, in the ink itself, which never fully dried, which stayed faintly warm, faintly alive, faintly *aware* of the place it had made. Any gifted cartographer could smell another's work the way a hound smells blood. Wren had always assumed they were the only one.

But the crown had witch-finders. Everyone knew the stories, though the stories were old — from the last hunt, two generations back, when the gifted had been dragged from their lofts and their studies and their smugglers' dens and made to kneel in the market squares. The stories said the finders could taste magic in the air like frost coming. Wren had always filed those stories under legend, the way you file things you hope will stay legends.

Now a gray-cloaked rider was asking questions about bridges that seemed new.

"Wren." Felix crouched beside them, voice low. "If she's what I think she is, then our road is a trail of breadcrumbs leading straight to the capital. Every mile we've drawn is a mile she can follow. We have to stop drawing."

"And travel honestly?" Wren gestured at the dark country ahead. "Through the King's Reach, with checkpoints every ten miles and proclamation posts on every corner? With *this*?" They tapped the forged seal on the nearest crate. "The survey cover is the only reason we're alive. And the survey cover only works if the survey is real. If we stop making the road real, we're just nine rebels in stolen coats."

"Then we find another way."

"There is no other way. There's me, or there's nothing." Wren heard the bitterness in their own voice and hated it. "You recruited a cartographer, Felix. This is what a cartographer is. I draw, and the world pretends I'm right, and everything I draw stays mine until I unmake it. You don't get the gift without the smell."

Felix was quiet for a moment. In the firelight his face looked older than it had in the watchtower, the conviction worn down to something rawer. "Then tell me," he said. "Can you erase? Unmake what you've drawn?"

"Why?"

"Because if she's following our ink," Felix said slowly, "then maybe the answer isn't to stop leaving a trail. Maybe it's to leave one that ends."

Wren hadn't considered it. Erasure was the other half of the gift, the half they used least, the half that felt less like creation and more like theft. To draw a place was to argue with the world and win. To erase one was to take something the world had agreed to and pretend it had never been argued for at all. Bridges fell out of maps all the time — bad surveys, floods, wars. The world was used to losing them. That was what made erasure possible. That was also what made it monstrous.

"I can do it," Wren said. "I've never done it to anything anyone was standing on."

"Let's hope we keep it that way."

* * *

Serah Voss found them on the seventh day, at a place called Marrow Bridge.

Later, Wren would replay those hours again and again, searching for the moment things turned, and would never quite find it. There was no single moment. There was only the gray cloak appearing on the southern ridge at midmorning, closer than she had ever allowed herself to be, and Tam's whistle cutting across the quarry wind, and then the world becoming very simple and very fast.

"She's not alone," Tam shouted. "Riders — six, eight — coming up the river road!"

Not soldiers. Wren saw that immediately. The riders wore no uniform, but they moved like a single animal, fanning out along the high ground with the unhurried confidence of people who already knew where their prey would run. And at their center, dismounting now, walking to the edge of the ridge as calmly as a woman walking to her own garden gate, stood the gray-cloaked figure.

Even at four hundred yards, Wren felt her.

It was like standing near an open door in winter. Something cold came off her in waves — not cold exactly, but *absence*, a hunger shaped like attention. She stood on the ridge with her head slightly tilted, and Wren understood with sick certainty that she was reading them. Reading the camp. Reading the carts. Reading the road beneath their feet, the hundred miles of gently falsified country Wren had smoothed into existence, and finding, in all of it, a signature. *Wren Nystrom*, written across the landscape in ink that never dried.

"She knows," Wren said. Their mouth had gone dry. "Felix, she already knows. This is just the collection."

"Then we don't be collected." Felix was already moving, snapping orders — Tam to the carts, Brenn and the twins to the horses, everyone else into the trees. "Survey crew disperses under attack, that's our story if any of us live to tell it. Move!"

They almost made it.

The hollow had two exits: the river road south, wide and open and watched, and a deer track north that climbed toward the old quarry works and the high passes beyond. Wren had drawn the deer track wider that morning — a small kindness, a few feet of sure footing where there had been scree — and it was toward this track that they fled, carts abandoned, the forged crates left gaping open in the grass like evidence.

The riders came down the ridge like water finding a crack.

What followed was not a battle; battles have shapes. This was a scattering. Wren ran with the map case strapped tight against their spine, branches whipping their face, and behind them the morning filled with shouting and the crack of a crossbow and someone screaming a name — *Davin*, it was Davin, one of the twins, down somewhere in the bracken with a bolt through him — and Wren ran because running was the only thing their body remembered how to do.

At the top of the track, where the deer path crossed the shoulder of the quarry, the ground opened onto the old works: a shelf of broken stone above the river gorge, and spanning the gorge, the reason the place was called Marrow Bridge.

It was an old thing, older than the crown's roads — a span of fitted stone on twin piers, built by hands no one remembered, sagging in the middle like a tired back. It appeared on no official map. It existed only because the smugglers used it, and the smugglers used it because Halvard had once told Wren about it over a fire, years ago, in the easy voice he saved for things that mattered: *Marrow Bridge, girl. The crown doesn't know it's there. That makes it the most valuable road in the Thornfells.*

Halvard. In a cell somewhere, or worse. The thought went through Wren like a hook.

Below, the last of the rebels were crossing — Tam hauling Brenn, who had taken a slash across the thigh and couldn't put weight on it. Felix stood at the far end, waving the stragglers through, and beyond him, up the river road, the gray-cloaked figure walked at the head of her riders, unhurried, closing the distance with the terrible patience of someone who has done this many times and never once lost.

And Wren understood, standing on the quarry shelf with the wind pulling at their coat, what the ending of a trail meant.

The bridge was not theirs. They hadn't drawn it. It belonged to the world, to the smugglers, to Halvard's memory, to every foot that had ever crossed it. Erasing it would not be tidying their own ink off a page. It would be reaching into the world and taking away something real — something people needed, something that had stood longer than any living memory — and doing it in a heartbeat, casually, the way you snap a thread.

Serah Voss raised her hand, and her riders broke into a gallop.

Wren knelt, tore the map from the case, and found the gorge by touch — they had charted it that morning, a habit, a completeness, the cartographer's disease of never leaving blank space. The bridge was there in fine brown line, two piers, one sagging span. Wren wet their thumb.

*I'm sorry,* they thought, though they weren't sure to whom. To Halvard, maybe. To the bridge itself. To whoever would come this way someday and find a gorge with no way across and no reason why.

They pressed the thumb to the span and drew it sideways, once, the way you strike through a word you've written wrong.

The world flinched.

There was no thunder, no light. That was the worst of it — how quiet it was. The bridge simply stopped having happened. One heartbeat it stood, gray and ancient and load-bearing, with Tam and Brenn stumbling off its far end; the next, the gorge was empty, a clean stone gap, and the air where the bridge had been rang with a silence like a struck bell fading. Dust rose from the piers' foundations — the piers were gone too, all of it, unwritten — and the river below ran on as if nothing had ever interrupted it, because nothing ever had.

The lead riders reined up so hard that two horses went down screaming. Serah Voss stopped at the lip of the gorge, and even across the distance, Wren saw her head tilt — that same hungry attention — and saw her look up, directly, precisely, at the quarry shelf where Wren knelt with a thumb still wet with erased stone.

She didn't shout. She didn't gesture. She simply looked, for three full seconds, the way a scholar looks at a text she has finally learned to read.

Then she turned, and the riders wheeled, and they were gone up the river road as if the gorge had swallowed them.

* * *

No one spoke for a long time.

They regrouped two miles north in a fold of hills, and Felix counted them twice with the grim arithmetic of a man afraid of his own totals. Davin was dead — left in the bracken below the quarry, because there had been no time and no way, and because the alternative was all of them. His brother Sorrel sat apart with his back against a boulder, staring at nothing, his hands still curled as if around a crossbow he'd dropped somewhere and couldn't remember dropping. Brenn's thigh had been bound with a belt and a torn shirt, and he lay gray-faced by the cold fire pit, whispering thanks to no one in particular.

Two rebels. One dead, one bleeding. Bought with a bridge.

"It was the right call," Felix said quietly, crouching beside Wren at the edge of the camp. "Another minute and she'd have had all of us. She'd have had *you*, and everything you are would be in crown hands by nightfall."

"I know."

"You don't look like you know."

Wren looked at their hands. They were steady. That was the thing they couldn't stop looking at — the steadiness. Somewhere in the last hour they had reached into the world and deleted a piece of it, a piece that smugglers would look for next month and not find, a piece that Halvard had prized above nearly any road in the mountains, and their hands hadn't even shaken. The gift had gone through them like water through a channel that had always been there, waiting. As if erasure were not a monstrous thing at all, but merely a skill. As if they'd been built for it.

"The bridge was real," Wren said. "I didn't draw it. I took it anyway. Do you understand what that means? It means the gift doesn't care whose things they are. It means I can—" They stopped. Started again. "It took less effort than drawing. Less than *sketching a bird*. Felix, I erased eighty tons of fitted stone the way you'd wipe crumbs off a table, and it didn't cost me anything, and I don't know what that makes me."

Felix was silent. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he suspected was thin. "It makes you the only person alive who can do what needs doing in the Veil."

"That's not comfort. That's a job description."

"Maybe it's both." He glanced back toward the camp, toward Sorrel's motionless silhouette against the boulder. "Grieve the bridge. Grieve Davin. You should — the day you stop grieving is the day you become the thing Voss thinks you are. But grieve on the move, Wren. Because she read you today. Three seconds, face to face across a gorge, and whatever she learned in those three seconds, she's carrying back to the capital right now."

Wren followed his gaze south. The hills were empty. The river was empty. The whole wounded landscape lay quiet, holding its new gap in its teeth.

"She'll report that the trail ends at Marrow Bridge," Wren said slowly. "A bridge that no longer exists. What does a witch-finder write in her ledger about a cartographer who can delete the world?"

"Nothing good," Felix said. "But here's what I know. She'll also report that we're heading for the capital — because where else would we be going? And she'll ask for whatever the crown keeps locked deepest, because she's just seen proof that the stories about the gifted weren't stories after all." He rose, brushing dirt from his knees, and there was something almost like grim satisfaction in his face. "Which means, Wren, that Serah Voss is about to go looking for records of the Veil. She's going to walk into the same archive we're trying to reach, asking the same questions, hunting the same truth — and she doesn't know yet that the truth is bigger than her vengeance."

Wren pulled the map back into their lap. The gorge stared up at them from the page, a clean white absence where the brown line used to be — the first blank space they had ever made on purpose.

"Then we'd better get there first," they said, and began, with a hand that still did not shake, to draw the road north.
