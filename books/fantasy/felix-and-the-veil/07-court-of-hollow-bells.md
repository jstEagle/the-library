## 7. Court of Hollow Bells

The bells of Vael Amaran began to ring at dusk, and Felix counted them the way a man counts his own heartbeats — out of habit, out of dread, and because he had once known them better than his own name.

Seven towers. Seven bells. In his childhood they had rung in rounds, bright and overlapping, so that the whole city seemed to be arguing joyfully with itself. Now they tolled in strict alternation, one voice at a time, each waiting for the last to die before the next was permitted to speak. Someone had decided, years ago, that harmony was disorder. Someone had decided that a city should listen.

Felix stood in the shadow of the Saltgate customs house with a forged survey commission folded inside his coat and let the sound wash over him. Beside him, Wren shifted their weight from foot to foot, hood up, eyes moving over the gate guards with the flat attention of someone memorizing exits. Behind them both, six rebels dressed as road-surveyors milled among the cart traffic, handling their instruments with convincing boredom. Tam had even acquired the calluses to match. The disguise would hold unless someone asked questions, and no one in Vael Amaran asked questions anymore. That was the first thing the architecture told you, if you knew how to read it: this was a city built by people who did not want to be heard.

"Your city," Wren said quietly. Not unkindly. Which was worse.

"It was never mine," Felix said. "I only worked here."

He felt Wren's glance land on the side of his face and deliberately did not return it. There were things he had not told them. There were things he had not told anyone, including himself — a thought that arrived now with an odd, cold clarity, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there. He set it aside. The gate was ahead, and the gate required a version of him that had never been exiled, never disgraced, never dragged from these halls by the collar while the court watched with its beautiful blank faces.

"Names," the guard sergeant said, when they reached the checkpoint. He was young. His armor fit badly, as though it had been issued to a larger man, or as though the man inside it had grown smaller.

Felix handed over the commission. "Master Corvel, surveyor of record, Crown Works Division. We're here to verify the foundation lines beneath the old quarter. Subsidence concerns."

"Subsidence." The sergeant said the word as if tasting it. "In the palace?"

"The palace sits on the oldest foundations in the kingdom," Felix said. "Older than the palace. Older than the dynasty, if the records are honest, and I've read enough of them to know they aren't." He smiled the smile of a man who complained about paperwork the way all men complain about paperwork: as a shield against being asked anything real. "You'd be amazed what shifts under a building that size. You'd be more amazed who gets blamed for it."

The sergeant looked at the seal. It was a good forgery; Tam's forger had wept over it, which Felix understood completely. He had spent eleven years of his life making seals exactly like it, back when forgery had been called cartography and the thing being faked had been the king's confidence in his own kingdom. The sergeant looked at the seal, and then at Felix's hands — ink-stained, scarred at the knuckles where a drafting knife had slipped twice in one winter — and something flickered across his young face. Recognition, maybe. Or the memory of recognition. Everyone in the capital over thirty had seen Felix Reyes's face once, on the day of his exile, when the heralds had made sure of it.

But the flicker passed, or was buried, or was never there at all. The sergeant waved them through, and the bells tolled again, one voice at a time, and Felix walked into the palace grounds of Vael Amaran for the first time in nine years.

* * *

They entered through the service corridors, because that was how you entered a body if you meant to examine its organs.

Felix had expected the palace to have changed. Palaces changed; that was their nature, that was their whole argument with time. What he had not expected was for it to have changed *this way* — not upward into grandeur but inward into silence. The corridors of his memory had been loud places. Servants ran in them. Ministers argued in them. Somewhere below, the kitchens roared like a river in spring, and above, the royal apartments filled with music three nights out of seven, because the old king — the current king's father — had believed a silent palace was a dying one.

Now the walls were padded.

Not visibly. That was the horror of it. The tapestries were magnificent, the paneling was oak, the sconces burned with the same steady flame he remembered. But walk ten paces down the Long Gallery and clap your hands, and the sound died like a bird hitting glass. No echo. None. A corridor that long should have sung back at you for four full seconds. This one swallowed everything within half a breath, and it did it so smoothly, so expensively, that you only noticed if you had spent your youth learning what rooms were supposed to do.

"They've deadened it," Felix murmured. He pressed his palm flat against the wall and felt nothing — no draft, no vibration from the hall beyond, nothing. "Wren. Feel this."

Wren laid their hand beside his. Their fingers were ink-stained too, now; the habit was spreading to them like a fever. They frowned, tapped the paneling once, sharply, and the tap vanished into the wood as if the wall had eaten it.

"That's not construction," Wren said. "That's *mapping*. Someone has drawn silence onto this place. Layer after layer of it. Like... like hushing a room the way you'd shade a hillside."

Felix looked at them. "Can you tell how many layers?"

"No." Wren's jaw tightened. "And that frightens me more than the number would."

They moved on. And everywhere Felix looked, the confession continued, written in plaster and sightlines and the angle of doorways. The great stair had been rebuilt with a landing that blocked the view from the entrance hall to the throne room doors — you could no longer see the king's door from the street side of the palace, which meant no petitioner ever again saw the king's door and wondered about it. The windows along the east range had been reglazed with glass so thick and faintly smoked that the gardens beyond looked like memories of gardens. The servants' passages, which in his day had been a chaotic lattice every page boy knew by heart, had been walled shut in at least four places he could identify, collapsing the palace's circulatory system into a single set of approved routes. Nothing looped anymore. Nothing surprised. A visitor could walk from the gates to the throne room and back and see exactly what the crown wished seen, in exactly the order it wished it seen.

It was, Felix thought, the most honest document in the kingdom. Every tyrant writes memoirs; most just use stone.

"Corridor," Wren said suddenly, stopping.

"What?"

"This one loops." Wren turned in a slow circle, and Felix saw their hand drift toward the satchel where they kept their papers, the reflexive reach of a mapper confronting a space that lied. "I've counted the arches. Eleven since the last junction. On the way in, it was nine."

Felix felt the hair rise on his arms. "You're certain?"

"I'm always certain. That's my curse." Wren stared down the corridor, and Felix watched them do the thing they did — the thing Halvard must have seen in them, years ago, that made an old man hide a child in a smuggler caravan. Their eyes went slightly unfocused, seeing the space behind the space. "It's not shifting. It's *breathing*. The palace inhales, and the corridor is two arches longer. Exhales, and—" They stopped. Went pale. "Felix. The palace isn't built around the Veil. The palace is built *into* it. These walls are holding a shape the way a dam holds water, and the shape wants to move."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Above them, muffled to almost nothing, the evening bells finished their lonely round.

"Then we know why the walls are thick," Felix said finally. "Come on. The survey office still keeps the foundation charts, and I want to see them before midnight, when the watch changes."

* * *

The survey office was exactly as he had left it, which was its own small cruelty.

His old desk stood under the north window, third from the hearth, with the burn mark on its corner where he had set a candle too close during the winter of the border revisions. Someone else sat at it now — a round, gray-templed man named Ostrec whom Felix vaguely remembered as a junior clerk, promoted into the vacancy Felix's disgrace had opened. Ostrec was asleep over his charts, snoring gently, and Felix stood in the doorway of the room where he had spent the best years of his life and felt nothing so much as a burglar's contempt for the man sleeping in his bed.

They worked fast. Wren kept watch at the door while Felix lifted the foundation charts from the locked cabinet — the lock was the same lock he had picked as a bored apprentice, and his fingers remembered it without consulting him. He spread the great vellum sheets across Ostrec's abandoned work and held the lamp low, and there it was: the palace, sectioned, the bones of it laid bare in brown ink and faded red.

The foundations went down nine levels. Everyone knew that. What everyone did not know — what only a royal cartographer would have been in a position to notice, all those years ago, before noticing it ruined him — was that levels eight and nine were not drawn to scale.

They were drawn *larger* than the floors above them. Considerably larger. As though the palace were a fist closing over something that needed room to move.

"Look at the load-bearing lines," Felix whispered, tracing them with a dry fingertip. "Every pillar above level seven lands on a pier below that doesn't align with anything. They're not holding the building up. They're pinning something down. Wren — these aren't foundations. This is a *cage diagram*. Someone drew a prison and built a palace on top of it to hide the drawing."

"And the king," Wren said slowly, "has lived his whole life directly above it."

"Directly above it, and bound to it, if I'm right. Which is why the walls are deadened. Why the corridors breathe. Why the bells—" Felix stopped. The bells. He had been counting them since the gate, out of habit, and only now did the habit yield its meaning. Seven towers, ringing alone, never together. "Why the bells are forbidden to harmonize. Sound carries. Sound *travels*. If the binding is a kind of map — and everything in this building says it is — then resonance is a threat. A chord might shake something loose. So they tuned the entire city to silence."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Halvard knew. Didn't he. That's what's in his journal — the part he wouldn't show me yet. He helped build this."

"I think," Felix said carefully, "that Halvard helped build a great many things, and has spent twenty years deciding which of them to confess. But yes. I believe he knew."

He rolled the charts, and that was when the lamp guttered — not from any draft, because the room had no drafts, the whole palace had no drafts, the air moved nowhere it wasn't sent — and in the stutter of light Felix glanced up through the dark window at the east range opposite, at the tall smoked glass of the royal apartments, three stories above the garden wall.

A figure stood at one of the windows.

Even at this distance, even through glass the color of deep water, Felix knew the silhouette. He had drawn that silhouette a hundred times in his official capacity — the broad shoulders slightly stooped, the head carried forward as if perpetually leaning toward something only he could hear. King Aldric IV. The man whose mind Felix had spent nine years insisting was not broken but *taken*, and whom half the court called the Hollow King behind cupped hands, and whom Felix had been exiled for defending.

The figure raised one hand and pressed it flat against the window.

Felix went very still. Across the dark garden, through the smoked glass, the King of Cindralon stood looking down at the survey office window with the absolute focus of a man who has found the one lit room in a house of sleepers. And his mouth was moving.

No sound crossed the distance. Of course no sound crossed it; the palace had been engineered against exactly that. But the mouth moved, steadily, rhythmically, and Felix found himself reading the shapes the way he read maps — pattern first, meaning after — and the shapes repeated. Over and over. Three words, maybe four, formed and reformed with the patience of water wearing stone.

"He can see us," Wren breathed at his shoulder. "Felix, he's—"

"I know."

"What is he saying?"

Felix didn't answer. Because he had caught the rhythm now, and the rhythm was wrong for speech — too regular, too cyclical, the cadence not of a message but of a *counting*, or a prayer, or a name said so many thousands of times it had worn smooth as a river pebble. And beneath the regularity, barely, something else: the smallest fracture in the pattern, a hitch on every seventh repetition, like a bell that couldn't quite keep time with itself.

The lamp steadied. The figure at the window lowered its hand, stepped back, and was gone into the smoked dark of the royal apartments, leaving only the reflection of the lamp burning in the glass like a small captive star.

"Felix." Wren's voice was very quiet. "What was he saying?"

Felix rolled the foundation charts closed with hands that were not entirely steady, and told the truth, because he had exiled himself from lying to himself a long time ago and the habit was hard to break tonight.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'm going to find out. Tomorrow night, we go up. Through the servants' routes that haven't been walled shut — there are two, I checked them on the way in, the masons missed them because they don't appear on any plan drawn after the binding. The palace forgot its own secret roads. But I didn't forget. I *drew* some of them."

He slung his satchel over his shoulder and took one last look at the dark window where the king had stood, mouthing his patient, fractured words into a room built to make sure no one ever heard them.

Tomorrow, Felix thought, someone would finally listen.
