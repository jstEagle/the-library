## 8. The King's Empty Room

The palace breathed at night. Wren had learned that much in three days of listening from the servant corridors — the way the stone expanded as the fires died, ticking and settling like a great animal curling into sleep; the way the guards' boots kept their own rhythm, a heartbeat of iron on marble, four beats and a pause, four beats and a pause, until you could set your own pulse against it and move inside its gaps.

They moved now through one of those gaps, pressed into an alcove behind a tapestry of some dead hunt, breath shallow, counting.

Four beats. Pause. Four beats. Pause.

*Go.*

Wren slipped out and crossed the landing like a shadow given errands, up the narrow stair that Felix had described — *the servants' spine, it runs behind the state rooms, they built it so no one would ever have to see the king being carried to bed* — and felt the old resentment of the place settle over them like dust. Everything in this palace was built to keep someone from seeing something. Corridors that looped. Walls packed with sand to muffle sound. A whole architecture of not-looking, and at the center of it, the man everyone was paid not to see.

The king's private apartments occupied the eastern face of the palace, three floors above the Hall of Bells where the court performed its hollow ceremonies each morning. Wren had expected locks. They had picked two doors already tonight, clumsy with borrowed tools and nerves, and both times the fear had been less of capture than of *him* — the mad king, the hollow king, the thing wearing the crown, whatever he was. Felix spoke of him like a prisoner. The rebels who wanted him dead spoke of him like a storm front. Serah Voss, somewhere below the city with her witch-finders, spoke of people like Wren like kindling.

Wren didn't know what they believed yet. That was why they had come. Not for Felix's crusade, not for the rebellion's politics — but because Halvard had died with a confession half-swallowed, because the journal in Wren's pack had pages about the Veil written in a hand that shook, and because Wren had spent their whole life mapping places that shouldn't exist and needed to know if this one did.

The door to the royal bedchamber was unlocked.

That stopped them cold. Every other door in this wing had been locked, barred, warded with little lead seals that hummed faintly when Wren passed — protections against everything except, apparently, entry. Wren stood before it a long moment, hand hovering, and thought: *either there is nothing in there worth stealing, or nothing in there worth protecting.*

Or, said a quieter voice, *whatever is in there has nowhere else to go.*

They pushed the door open.

Dust. That was the first thing — not the thick felted dust of abandonment but a fine, deliberate grey, laid down evenly across every surface like a sheet drawn over furniture. No one had cleaned this room in years, and no one had disturbed it either. The air tasted of closed stone and old candle smoke and, beneath that, something faintly sweet and wrong, like flowers left too long in water.

The bedchamber of the King of Vael was empty.

Not unfurnished — *empty*. The great bed stood made, its coverlets smooth as pond ice, corners tucked with a precision that looked less like care than like surrender. A wardrobe gaped open, bare rods shining. A writing desk held nothing but a ring-shaped stain where an inkwell had once sat, and a single quill, its nib worn to a stub, laid parallel to the desk's edge as if placed by someone who could no longer remember what desks were for. A washstand. A mirror draped in cloth. A cold hearth swept clean.

No books. No letters. No sign that a man lived here, or had ever lived here. It was a room shaped exactly like a life, with the life poured out of it.

Wren stood in the doorway and felt the hair rise along their arms. They had mapped haunted places before — places memory had worn thin, places the gift slid off like rain off glass. This room had that same wrongness, but inverted. It wasn't that memory had failed here. It was that memory had been *removed*, scooped out with a spoon, and the room had been left to heal around the absence like flesh around a wound.

*He doesn't live here,* Wren thought. *He is kept here.*

They crossed to the far wall almost without deciding to. Their feet knew. Cartographer's instinct, Halvard used to call it — the sense a mapmaker develops for the true shape of a space, the way water knows downhill. And the true shape of this room was wrong. The eastern wall stood too far from the windows. Wren had studied the palace plans Felix had stolen; they had memorized them in an hour, the way they memorized everything, ink sinking into the mind like water into chalk. By those plans, this wall should have been six feet closer. There should have been a dressing room beyond it.

Instead there was wall. Twenty feet of blank plaster, pale as bone.

Except it wasn't blank.

Wren lifted the lamp they'd brought — a shuttered thing, its light a knife-slit — and let the beam climb.

Drawings covered the wall from floor to ceiling. Hundreds of them, thousands, rendered in charcoal and crayon and what looked at first glance like mud but was not, Wren realized with a lurch, mud at all. It was ink mixed with ash. Someone had ground their own burnt drawings to powder and redrawn them, again and again, layer over layer, until the wall itself had become a palimpsest of a child's obsession.

Houses with crooked chimneys. Stick figures holding hands under scribbled suns. And maps — maps everywhere, spilling off the edges of each drawing into the next, coastlines crawling up the wallpaper border, rivers running through the stick figures' joined hands, mountains rising out of a child's scrawled sun. Drawn in the wobbling, earnest line of small hands. Drawn, Wren saw as they stepped closer, heart beginning to hammer, by someone learning to draw the way cartographers learn: repetition, correction, repetition. The same coastline attempted forty times, each version steadier than the last. The same mountain range redrawn until the ridgelines ran true.

And always, at the center of every map, the same shape.

A door.

It appeared in the oldest drawings as a simple rectangle, crude as a child draws a door — brown rectangle, black knob. In later ones it gained detail: an arch, hinges, a keyhole rendered with furious little stabs of charcoal. In the most recent — these lowest, nearest the floor, where a grown man would have had to kneel — the door had become something else entirely. It stood alone now, detached from any house, floating in white space, and around it the artist had drawn the door's shadow falling the wrong way, toward the light, and had written beneath it, in a careful adult hand gone ragged at the edges:

*I put it here. I know I put it here.*

Wren sank slowly to their knees in the dust.

*Oh,* they thought. *Oh, you poor man.*

Because they recognized this. Not the drawings — the *act*. This was what Wren's own sketchbooks looked like after a bad year, after the nights when the gift pulled memories loose and Wren drew and redrew a house they couldn't name, a burning roofline, a woman's mouth shaping a word that never arrived. Drawing was how mappers remembered. Drawing was how mappers mourned. And this wall was decades of a man trying to hold onto something that had been taken out of his head by force — trying to reconstruct, from nothing, the shape of his own prison.

The king had been bound as a boy.

The realization arrived whole, terrible, undeniable. These weren't the ravings of a madman. They were the earliest strata of a mind fighting an excavation. Somewhere in this palace twenty-odd years ago, a child prince had been walked into a room like this one and had something carved out of him, and the child had done the only thing he could: he had drawn it. Before the binding settled, before the memories finished dissolving, he had committed the shape of the Veil to plaster and charcoal, because even then — even at eight years old, ten, twelve — some part of him had understood that the only safe place for a memory was outside the head that was being emptied.

And then he had grown up, and been crowned, and forgotten why the wall mattered. But the wall remained. Servants must have been ordered never to touch it — or perhaps he reissued the order each time the forgetting came back around, a man circling the same wound, finding it fresh.

Wren reached out and touched the lowest drawing, fingertips brushing the ash-ink. Under their skin, faint as a pulse in the wrist, the paper in their pack seemed to warm. Their gift stirred the way it always did near strong drawn things — a low tide-pull behind the eyes, the sense of lines wanting to complete themselves.

And the wall answered.

It was very slight. Wren might have imagined it. But one of the rivers in the newest drawing — a blue-grey crayon line running from the door toward the wall's edge — seemed to shift as Wren watched, the way ink moves when water touches it. Not flowing. *Adjusting.* As though the drawing were checking Wren's face against something. As though it recognized the gift the way a lock recognizes a key's silhouette through a door.

Wren snatched their hand back.

"Careful," they whispered to themselves, and the whisper sounded enormous in the empty room. "Careful, careful."

But their heart wasn't pounding with fear anymore. That was the strange thing. Standing in the stripped cell of the most feared man in Vael, kneeling in his dust among his desperate beautiful childish maps, Wren found that the fear had simply — gone. Poured out of them the way the life had been poured out of this room, and something else had run in behind it, something hot and grieving and certain.

Pity. Terrible, particular pity.

Felix had told them, that first night in the watchtower, with the fervor of a man defending a heresy: *he is not a tyrant, he is a hostage.* Wren had nodded the way you nod at grief you haven't earned. Now they knelt in the evidence of it. Whatever wore the crown, whatever the court feared and the rebels cursed and Serah Voss burned people for — it had begun here, as a child with a stub of charcoal, trying to draw the door of his own cage before he forgot there had ever been a cage.

*I put it here. I know I put it here.*

"Yes," Wren murmured to the wall, to the boy who had written it. "You did. And I think I'm the only person alive who can read your map."

They unslung their pack and took out their sketchbook, and began, carefully, reverently, to copy the wall — not all of it, not tonight, but the newest drawings, the door with its backward shadow, the river, the annotations in that ragged adult hand. Their pencil moved fast and sure. This was the work they had been born for, the work Halvard had died for, the work that had cost them their own past: to take a thing that existed only in someone's breaking memory and give it lines that would last.

They were deep in it, lost in the transcription, when the light changed.

Not the lamp. The room. A grey luminescence crept up the walls like fog through a window seam, sourceless, and the temperature dropped so fast Wren's breath bloomed silver. The dust on the floor stirred, lifting in slow spirals, and every drawing on the wall — every stick figure, every crooked chimney, every one of the thousand small doors — turned, gently, all at once, to face them.

Wren went absolutely still.

From the corridor beyond the unlocked door came footsteps. Not the guards' four-beat rhythm. Slower. Heavier. A walk with weight behind it, and a sound underneath the footsteps like the dragging of something vast across stone — or like a chain being paid out, link by link, from a great depth.

And beneath that, faint, muffled, coming through the wall itself — through the wall that should have had a dressing room behind it — a voice. A man's voice, cracked and rusted from disuse, repeating the same words over and over with the patience of someone who has been saying them for years:

"I know you're in there. I know you're in there. I know you're in there."

Wren closed the sketchbook with hands that did not shake, which surprised them, and rose, and turned to face the door — and the darkness gathering behind it — knowing, with a mapper's certainty, that whatever came through it next, they had just become the second person in twenty years to see the king's wall.

And the first person the wall had ever answered.
