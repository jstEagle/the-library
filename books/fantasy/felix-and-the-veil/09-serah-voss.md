## 9. Serah Voss

The dungeons beneath the Citadel of Ashes were older than the crown that claimed them, and Serah Voss liked them for exactly that reason. Stone did not lie. Stone remembered nothing and forgot nothing, and it held the cold the way a held breath holds a scream. She had spent eleven years in rooms like this one — low-ceilinged, torch-lit, smelling of wet lime and old iron — and in all that time she had learned one truth above all others: everyone talked eventually. Not because they broke. Because they wanted to.

The old man chained to the wall had not yet learned to want to talk. That was fine. She was patient in a way that frightened people who mistook patience for softness.

Halvard sat slumped against the damp stone, his wrists manacled above him at an angle that would ache by the second hour and scream by the sixth. He was seventy if he was a day, with a white beard gone yellow at the mouth and hands that trembled — though she noted, with professional interest, that the trembling stopped whenever he looked directly at her. Cartographer's hands. A draftsman's steadiness buried under age. He had been someone once; the confiscated journal in her satchel said so in a cramped, beautiful hand that drew little compass roses in its margins as if the man could not help decorating even his own ruin.

"You've been down here two days," she said, "and you haven't asked me a single question. Most prisoners ask questions. *Where am I. What will happen to me. Will it hurt.* You just sit there like a fence post."

"Questions cost strength," Halvard said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "I'm rationing."

Serah smiled despite herself. She pulled the three-legged stool closer and sat, arranging her grey coat so the sigil of the Office of Finding faced him plainly: a closed eye stitched in silver thread. "Rationing. I like that. My mother used to say something similar about tears."

"Did she?"

"She said crying was spending what you'd need later." Serah let the smile fade on its own schedule. "She was right more often than she was wrong."

* * *

They had taken Halvard at the mountain waystation, half-dead from the cold, clutching a satchel of maps he refused to surrender even when the soldier holding him broke two of his fingers. The maps were extraordinary. Serah had examined them through the night after the capture, by lamplight, and had felt the particular sickness rise in her throat that she always felt near drawn places — that faint wrongness, like a word repeated until it lost meaning. These weren't records of roads. They were roads. She could smell the ink working, could feel the paper straining against the world like a seed against soil.

And every map bore the same marginal signature, small and looping: *W.N.*

"Wren Nystrom," she said now, watching the old man's face.

Nothing moved in it. Good composure. But his breathing changed — a hitch no deeper than a thread pulled taut — and Serah had built a career on hitches.

"You know the name," she said.

"I know many names."

"You knew this one first." She opened the satchel and drew out the journal, letting him see the compass roses. His eyes went to it the way a starving man's eyes go to bread, and there it was again, that tell, that terrible human honesty of the body. "This is yours. Confiscated at your arrest. It's a remarkable document, Master Halvard. Do you know what I see when I read it?"

"A lot of wasted ink."

"I see a teacher's hand correcting a student's. Here—" she turned pages until she found the one she wanted, "—this passage on folding ridges. The ink changes weight halfway through. Someone else finished the thought. Someone younger. Someone whose lines are still learning confidence." She looked up. "You taught Wren Nystrom. And whatever they are, whatever they can do — you've known since the beginning."

The torch guttered. Somewhere far above, water dripped its idiot metronome into a basin.

Halvard exhaled through his nose, long and deliberate, and Serah recognized the sound. It was the sound of a man deciding how much of himself to spend.

"You have sisters?" he asked.

The question landed harder than any blow she'd received in years, and she hated him a little for finding it, the way a hunter hates the deer that steps on the snare before she's ready. Her face, she knew, gave away almost nothing. Almost. "One," she said. "Had."

"Had," Halvard repeated softly. "There it is. I wondered what drove the famous Serah Voss. Zealots burn out, girl. Vengeance keeps the lamp trimmed."

"Don't call me girl."

"Forgive me. Witch-finder." He said the title without mockery, which was somehow worse. "Ask your questions. I'll answer what doesn't endanger anyone but me."

"Everyone says that. No one means it."

"I mean it." His eyes, pale as skimmed milk, held hers. "I've been old a long time, witch-finder. Old men make excellent liars about everything except their own deaths. I know precisely how much of me is left to lose."

* * *

She began, as she always began, with the small things. Where had he trained. Who else shared the gift. How many students. He answered some, deflected others with a craftsman's skill, and she let him deflect, because interrogation was not a hammer but a tide — you came back and back and back, and each return took a little more of the shore.

It was on the fourth pass, somewhere past midnight by her reckoning, that she set the sister between them.

"My sister's name was Ilsabet," Serah said. She kept her voice level, conversational, the voice of a woman discussing grain prices. "She was nineteen. We lived in Lowmarsh, on the fen edge, where the houses lean on each other like drunkards. There was a cartographer came through our district that autumn. Licensed. Crown-stamped. One of the surveyors mapping the drainage cuts." She paused. "Except he wasn't mapping drainage. He was practicing. Drawing places out of his own head and walking into them, and when he walked out, things came back wrong. Doors that led to rooms that didn't exist. A lane behind our house that hadn't been there before, lined with trees that dropped leaves in the shape of letters."

Halvard had gone very still.

"Ilsabet followed him into one of his places. I don't know why. Curiosity, maybe. Or maybe he lured her — the inquiry never settled it, because here is the thing about drawn places, Master Halvard." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the torchlight made her shadow loom up the wall behind him like a second prisoner. "When they're erased, they take what's inside them along. The lane vanished. The trees vanished. And my sister vanished with them, and the inquiry wrote *missing, presumed fled*, and my mother spent thirty years leaving a lamp in the window for a daughter who was never coming home, because there was no body, don't you understand? No grave. Nothing to bury. Nothing to finish grieving. Just a door in the world that closed, and everyone telling us to move on."

"And so you hunt the gifted," Halvard said quietly.

"So I hunt the gifted." She sat back. "You think I enjoy this room? I hate this room. I hate the cold and the chains and the smell. I'd rather be anywhere on earth. But every gifted cartographer loose in the world is another man drawing lanes behind houses, and I have sworn on my sister's lamp that no family will stand where mine stood, staring at empty air, waiting for a door to reopen." Her jaw tightened. "So yes. Call it vengeance. Call it zealotry. Call it whatever helps you sleep against the stone. It changes nothing about what you're going to tell me."

For a long moment Halvard said nothing. Then, slowly, painfully, he laughed — a dry, rusted sound with no joy in it whatsoever.

"Oh, you poor, magnificent fool," he said.

"Careful."

"No, listen to me. Listen." He strained forward against the manacles, and his milk-pale eyes burned. "You think the gift makes lanes behind houses. You think we draw places for sport, for trespass, for cruelty. Child — I have spent forty years teaching gifted children to *hide*. To bury the talent so deep they pass for deaf-mutes, for simpletons, for anything but what they are. Do you know why? Not because of witch-finders. Witch-finders are weather; we've survived weather before. Because of what the crown does with them when it finds them." His voice dropped. "Because of what's sitting on the throne right now, and what sits under it."

"The king," Serah said flatly. "You'll have to do better than mad-king stories. Half the realm whispers them."

"The realm whispers that the king is mad. I'm telling you the king is *bound*." The words came out of him like stones pulled from a wound. "Stitched. His mind tied to something old, something that wears him, and the binding was done by gifted hands — coerced ones — twenty years ago. And the thing the binding guards, the place all those stolen memories are kept—" He stopped himself, jaw clamping shut, and Serah saw with frustration that he had reached the edge of what he'd decided to spend.

But she had heard enough. More than enough. Bound kings. Stolen memories. Places beneath palaces. It was sedition dressed as prophecy, and yet —

And yet.

She thought of the confiscated maps, the ink that seemed to breathe. She thought of the report from the capital, six months old, that she had dismissed as hysteria: a royal shadow that moved a half-second behind its owner. She thought of the proclamation nailed to every waystation in the Thornfells, naming the gifted traitors, and how strange it had struck her even then — why now? Why, after decades of quiet tolerance, did the crown suddenly need every gifted soul flushed into the open?

Unless the crown needed them for something. Unless something under the throne was hungry.

"You expect me to believe fairy stories," she said, but her voice had lost its footing and she heard it herself.

"I expect you to believe nothing," Halvard said. "I expect you to *check*. That's the difference between a finder and a zealot, isn't it? You find. So find, Serah Voss. Find out why the proclamation came down the mountain the same week my student's name went on it. Find out what the Office of Finding reports to, and whether it reports to a man or to the thing wearing him." He sagged back against the wall, spent, breathing hard. "And while you're finding — find my student before your soldiers do. Because if the crown takes Wren Nystrom alive, they won't hang them. They'll use them. And then the last person alive who can undo what was done to the king will be in chains beside me, and this realm will rot from the crown down for another twenty years."

* * *

She left him then. It was procedure — sessions ended, food was brought, the lamp outside his cell was trimmed — but her legs carried her up the stair faster than procedure required, and she caught herself gripping the rail, and made herself stop.

In the wardroom above, she sat alone with the journal and did not read it. She looked instead at her own hands, steady as always, and thought about lamps in windows.

*Ilsabet,* she thought. *Nineteen years old. Fled, presumed.*

What if she hadn't fled?

The thought arrived uninvited and refused to leave, the way the worst thoughts do. What if the inquiry had been wrong — not lazy, not corrupt, simply wrong, because no one investigating had understood what a drawn place was, and so had written the only ending they had words for? What if her sister was not dead and not gone but *kept* — folded into some borderland of stolen things, along with wedding days and first snowfalls and whatever else the crown's founders had judged too dangerous to leave lying around in living heads?

Halvard had watched her tell the story of her sister, and he had called her a fool, and he had been gentle about it, which meant he pitied her, which meant he believed — gods, he actually believed — that the thing she'd spent her whole life hunting might be the only thing that could tell her what happened to Ilsabet.

"Infuriating," she said aloud to the empty wardroom. "Infuriating old man."

Because that was the worst of it, the part she would never say in the light: she recognized his loyalty. She knew exactly what it was to chain yourself to a single person and call it duty. She had done it with a memory for eleven years. Halvard was doing it with a student, and both of them were fools, and both of them would burn for it gladly, and she could not decide whether she despised him or was looking into a mirror.

A knock at the door. A young lieutenant, rain-wet, holding a dispatch case stamped with the triple crown.

"Finder Voss. Word from the capital. Priority seal."

She broke the wax. Read it once. Read it again, slower, feeling the floor tilt beneath a sentence.

The Office of Finding was to be placed under direct palace authority, effective immediately. All prisoners with knowledge of the Veil — the word leapt off the page, *the Veil*, written as casually as a place-name, as if everyone should already know it — were to be transferred to palace custody within the fortnight. Including, the final line specified, the old cartographer, Halvard, whose transfer was requested *by name*.

By name. Someone in the palace knew exactly what Halvard knew, and wanted him close enough to silence.

Serah folded the dispatch very carefully, along its original creases, and found that her hands were not quite steady anymore.

Outside, the rain came down over the Citadel of Ashes, and in the window of the wardroom her own reflection held a lamp's glow, and for one unguarded moment she did not see a witch-finder at all — only a woman from Lowmarsh, standing at a lit window, waiting for a door to open.
