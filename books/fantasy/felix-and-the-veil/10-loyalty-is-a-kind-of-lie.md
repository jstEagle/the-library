## 10. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The safehouse beneath the ruined watchtower had once been a wine cellar, and it still smelled of it — old oak and spilled vintage gone to vinegar — though for three years it had held nothing stronger than gunpowder, forged writs, and the particular bitterness of men arguing in whispers. Felix stood at the head of the long table with the court map unrolled before him, weighted at its corners with candlesticks, a dagger, a stone from the tower above, and Wren's hand.

That last weight was the one he felt most keenly. Wren's fingers rested on the edge of the parchment where the king's shadow was inked, and they had not moved in an hour. Around them, the council of Thornfell sat in a rough crescent: Maren Ostrow with her arms crossed like a drawbridge raised; Tam Vey picking at the wax seal of a letter he'd already read four times; the twins, Ilsa and Corren Ashvale, who never spoke first but always spoke together; and old Brannic, who had fought in the border wars and carried his opinions the way he carried his scars, visibly and without apology.

The candles guttered when the door opened below, letting in a draft that smelled of pine and cold iron. No one had knocked. No one ever did, down here; the watchtower's ruins were patrolled by two boys who whistled a smuggler's tune when strangers came, and the absence of the tune was itself the warning.

"Word from the capital," said Odile Ferran, shaking rain from her grey cloak. She was the cell's eyes in the city, a woman who had spent so many years pretending to be a laundress that she now folded everything she touched — maps, letters, arguments — into neat, damp squares. She set a packet on the table. "Voss has been given a warrant. Not just for the cartographer. For all of us. The witch-finders are calling it the Quiet Purge."

"Quiet," Brannic snorted. "They burned the Hollis family's mill with them inside it. That wasn't quiet. That was a sermon."

"The quiet is what comes after," Odile said. "No trials. No proclamations after this week. Names go on a list, and the list goes into a furnace, and by spring no one remembers there were gifted at all." She looked at Felix, and her look was careful, which meant it was pitying. "And the king signed it."

"There it is," Maren said, unfolding her arms only to plant both fists on the table. "There's your prisoner, Felix. Your poor mad king in his gilded cage. He signs warrants with his own hand. He dines while children burn. And you want us to bleed ourselves hollow to *free* him."

Felix had rehearsed this argument for years. He had rehearsed it in exile, in prison, in the years of small rooms and smaller reputations after the court cast him out. He knew every counter, every proof, every scrap of evidence he'd spent a decade assembling. But he also knew — had known since the night Wren's ink crawled across the parchment of its own accord — that the thing they were arguing about was larger than any of them, and that certainty, in a room like this, was worth less than a copper coin with a hole through it.

"He signs what is put in front of him," Felix said. "You've read my reports. The hand that signs is steady. The eyes above it are empty. I watched him for six years from the map room, Maren. A king who wanted the gifted dead would have done it loudly, with speeches and glory, because that is what kings are. This king does nothing loudly. This king does nothing at all except what the crown requires of him."

"And you know what the crown requires because—"

"Because I've seen his shadow move against him." Felix turned the court map so they could all see it: the palace rendered in the old survey style, every room measured, and beside the figure of the king at his devotions, a second figure, inked in a darker hand, standing where the light fell wrong. "I documented it forty-one times over six years. I brought it to the Lord Cartographer, and three days later I was exiled for falsifying records. Ask yourself why a court would ruin its royal cartographer rather than simply laugh at him. You don't burn a man's life to bury a joke."

"You don't burn a man's life to bury treason either," Tam said quietly. "But they do that all the time."

The silence that followed had teeth. Felix let it sit. He had learned in the court that silence was a blade you handed to your enemies and hoped they cut themselves.

It was Ilsa Ashvale who broke it, in her flat twin-voice, with Corren nodding along as if the words were being drawn out of him too. "Say we believe you. Say the king is bound, stitched to something old, wearing him the way a hand wears a glove. Then answer the question that matters. What happens when you pull the glove off? Does the hand come with it? Does the *arm*?"

"We don't know," Felix said.

Honesty, at least, cost nothing here.

"What we know," he went on, "is that the binding is a made thing. Made things can be unmade. The Veil keeps stolen memories — Halvard's journal confirms it, and Wren has—" he glanced sideways, and stopped himself. He had been about to say *Wren has already mapped a fragment of it*, about to spend their discovery like coin in front of people who might spend Wren differently. "Wren believes the same structure that binds can be redrawn. A knot untied instead of cut."

"A knot," Brannic said, "tied around a kingdom's throat. And your plan is to untie it in the cellar of the palace itself, with Voss hunting us, with half the cells in the east already ash." He shook his head slowly. "I've followed you three years, Felix, because you were right about the shadow. But right isn't the same as wise. There's another way to end this. A shorter way."

"No," Maren said, and Felix heard it — heard the moment the room turned. Not toward him. "Brannic's right. Gods forgive me, but he's right. If the king dies, the binding dies with its anchor. Whatever wears the crown loses its vessel. We don't need to understand the Veil. We don't need rituals and red ink and a smuggler's cartographer gambling with forces nobody's survived. We need one arrow, one window, one morning. Grief ends. Tyranny ends. The realm gets a regency and time to breathe."

"And the memories?" It was Wren who spoke, the first words they'd offered all night, and the whole table turned like iron filings to a lodestone. Their voice was quiet, roughened from days of drawing. "The Veil doesn't keep the king's memories alone. It keeps everyone's. Every name on Voss's list, every mind she means to open and empty — the founders built that place to hold *stolen things*, and it holds them still. Kill the anchor and you don't free what's inside. You orphan it. You leave ten thousand selves locked in a dark with no keeper and no key." They looked down at their own hand on the map. "I've drawn a piece of it. I know what it feels like. It doesn't want to be burned. It wants to be *returned*."

"Pretty words from someone who's been among us a fortnight," Maren said, but without heat; even she had learned that Wren's quiet was not weakness but a kind of weather, and you did not argue with weather.

"They're not pretty words," Felix said. "They're the only words backed by evidence anyone in this room has actually touched." He put his palm flat on the map, over the dark second figure. "I'm asking you for one thing. Let us reach the palace. Let Wren find the way down. If I'm wrong — if the king is exactly what he appears — then Maren, you'll have your arrow, and I'll hand it to you myself."

"That's a strange promise from a loyalist," Tam murmured.

"I'm not a loyalist. I'm a cartographer. I want the territory to match the map." He straightened. "And I'll tell you what I told the Lord Cartographer six years ago, the day before he ruined me: I would rather be exiled for the truth than promoted for a comfortable lie."

It was a good line. He'd used it before. He watched it land the way good lines land, softening Brannic, stalling Maren, giving the twins something noble to nod at. And under the table, out of sight, his other hand curled into a fist, because the line was itself a lie — or near enough. He had not told the council how much of the rite he understood. He had not told them that the fragments in Halvard's journal matched pages Felix had copied, years ago, from a book in the restricted archive whose title he had never spoken aloud. He had not told them that he knew the unbinding required a vessel, that the vessel was consumed, that he had known this since before Wren had ever drawn their first moving line — and that he intended, when the moment came, to stand in that place himself, and that every argument he made tonight was built on top of that buried intention like a road built over a sinkhole.

He told himself it was strategy. A council that knew its leader planned to die would fracture sooner than one that didn't. He told himself the lie was a kindness, and then he told himself it was a necessity, and the two stories fit together so neatly that he almost believed neither was a story at all.

The vote, such as it was, went his way — narrowly, grudgingly, Maren abstaining with a look that promised she would revisit the arithmetic of arrows. The council dissolved upward into the rain. Odile took her packet and left for the capital before dawn. And Felix stayed behind to roll the map, alone with the candles, doing what he always did after councils: rebuilding the argument, shoring the road, pretending the ground beneath it was solid.

"You lied tonight."

Wren hadn't left. They were sitting in the corner where the cellar's old racks still stood, half-shadowed, a stub of candle burning beside the leather roll of their instruments. Felix hadn't seen them move, hadn't heard them breathe, and that frightened him more than the accusation did.

"I argued a case," he said carefully. "Every case leaves things out."

"This wasn't omission." Wren rose and crossed the floor, and set something on the table between them: a sheet of paper covered in Felix's own handwriting — no, not quite his own. His handwriting, but written in a hand that pressed too hard, too fast, the way a man writes when he is copying something he must not be caught copying. Felix recognized it with a lurch: notes on the binding rite, taken years ago, hidden in the lining of a coat he had burned. Except this page was in front of him, and his coat was ash, and the paper smelled faintly of the Veil — of snowfall and wedding days and something underneath like turned earth.

"Where did you get this?"

"It came back with me," Wren said. "From the fragment. When I mapped it, some things mapped *me*. Ink remembers, Felix. It remembered you touching these words. It remembered how hard your heart was beating when you wrote them." They tapped the page, on a line near the bottom, and their finger did not tremble, which somehow made it worse. "*'The vessel that holds the severed memory is spent. There is no rite of return. Choose the vessel before the work begins, and tell no one who cannot bear to stop you.'*" They looked up. "Those are your words. In your hand. Written years ago. Which means you've known since before you ever found me what the unbinding costs. You've known there's a price, and you've known who means to pay it, and you stood in front of that council tonight and let them vote on a plan you've already decided to die for — without telling them, without telling *me*."

The candles had burned low. Felix found he could not look away from his own handwriting, from the young man he had been, pressing so hard the pen had nearly torn the page.

"If I'd told them," he said finally, "Maren would have won the vote for the arrow. Brannic would have agreed with her. A plan that requires my death sounds like madness in a council chamber, Wren. It sounds like martyrdom, and martyrdom makes men reach for shorter solutions." He made himself meet their eyes. "The lie kept the longer road open. That's all it was. Strategy."

"Loyalty is a kind of lie," Wren said softly. "Halvard used to say that. He said a man who lies for you will lie to you, and you'll never get to choose which day." They began rolling their instruments back into their leather case, each motion precise, unhurried, terrible in its calm. "I'm not angry that you mean to sacrifice yourself. I'd have guessed it anyway; you have the face of a man who's been writing his own eulogy for years. I'm angry that you decided *for* me. Because here's what you didn't copy out of that book, Felix. Here's what your page doesn't say." They slung the case across their shoulder and walked to the stairs, pausing at the bottom step, candlelight catching the ink stains on their fingers. "The vessel doesn't have to be chosen by the one who lies. It can be chosen by the one who draws. And I've been drawing roads my whole life that other people didn't know they needed to walk."

They climbed the stairs. The door closed above with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Felix stood alone in the wine-dark dark, surrounded by the ghosts of arguments, and looked down at the map, at the dark second figure standing in the king's shadow, and understood — with the cold clarity of a man watching a bridge crack beneath him — that he had lied to the council, lied to Wren, and lied longest and best to himself. And that somewhere above him, in the rain, Wren was already drawing.

* * *

He did not sleep. Toward the fourth hour he climbed to the broken crown of the watchtower, where the wind came off the peaks carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet, and stood where the masonry gave a view south: the black spine of the Thornfells running down and down toward the distant smudge of the capital's lights, a scatter of embers that never died.

Somewhere down there, a king mouthed words at a window. Somewhere down there, Serah Voss sharpened her warrants. And somewhere down there, in the oldest foundations, under stone laid by founders who had built a prison and called it a treasury, the Veil waited with its shelves of stolen lives — including, he suspected, a shelf with his own name on it, holding whatever the court had cut out of him the day they exiled him. Sometimes, in the space between waking and sleep, he felt the shape of the missing thing. It felt like conviction. It felt like a fire lit in an empty hearth by hands he couldn't remember.

He heard Wren come up the stair behind him — heard it this time, because they let him hear it — and did not turn.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you. You'll need your hands steady." Wren came to stand beside him at the broken parapet, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and for a long moment they both watched the far lights. "I'm not going to fight you for it tonight," Wren said at last. "The council's voted. The road's drawn. We go south tomorrow. But understand me, Felix Reyes: between here and that throne room, you're going to tell me everything that book said. Every word. Even the ones that break the plan. *Especially* those. Because if you make me choose between your secret and your life—"

They stopped. Below them, far down the mountain road, a light appeared where no light should be: a single white flame, moving without a lantern's sway, keeping pace with nothing, and even at this distance Felix felt the hair rise on his arms, because he had seen drawings of that light in the margins of forbidden maps, and Wren had gone rigid beside him.

"She's found the trail," Wren breathed. "Voss is in the mountains."

The white flame paused on the road, as if tasting the wind. Then it turned — unmistakably, deliberately — toward the watchtower.

"Get everyone below," Felix said. "Now."
