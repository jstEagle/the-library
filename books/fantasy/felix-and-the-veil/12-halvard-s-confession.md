## 12. Halvard's Confession

The diversion went up at the third bell after midnight — a bloom of orange light over the eastern garrison, followed by the deep-throated cough of a powder store catching. Wren felt it through the soles of their boots before they heard it, the ground shuddering like something turning over in its sleep.

"Go," Felix said. "Now. We won't get a second window."

They went.

The dungeons beneath the Hall of Records were older than the palace above them, older than the dynasty that had built the palace to hide them. Wren knew this because they had drawn the way down — three nights of secret work by candlelight, sketching corridors that predated every map in the royal archive, roads that existed only in the foundations' memory and now, thanks to Wren's ink, in fact. The walls here were black stone veined with something pale, like frost caught mid-bloom, and the air tasted of iron and old water.

Two rebels flanked Wren as they descended: Brenna, broad-shouldered and silent, and a young man called Tam whose hands shook around his lantern. Neither spoke. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said on the road — *get him out, don't be seen, if it goes wrong leave him.* The last instruction had been Felix's, and Wren had looked at him for a long moment before nodding, because they both knew it was a lie. Neither of them would leave Halvard. That was the whole reason any of this had become possible.

The cell block was nearly empty. The hunt for the gifted had filled these rooms once, Wren understood, but Serah Voss preferred her interrogations elsewhere, closer to the instruments, and most of the cages stood open and rusting like mouths with the teeth knocked out.

Except one.

Halvard sat against the far wall of the last cell, and Wren's heart stuttered at the sight of him. In the caravan years he had been a mountain of a man — grey-bearded, thick-fisted, forever mending harness by firelight with the patience of someone who believed all things could be repaired given time and good leather. The man in the cell was folded small, one arm cradled against his chest at an angle that made Wren's stomach clench, his face gone the colour of tallow. His eyes found the lantern light first, then Wren's face, and something moved through them — relief, then grief, then a strange, terrible calm.

"No," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "No, you stupid, brave child. You shouldn't have come."

"You're welcome," Wren said, because the alternative was to let their voice break, and there would be time for breaking later. They pressed their palm to the lock and drew — a quick, vicious sketch of the mechanism opened, its tumblers falling like tired soldiers. The ink sank into the iron and the lock obeyed, groaning apart.

Brenna took the corridor watch. Tam hovered. Wren knelt in the straw and put their hands under Halvard's good arm.

"Can you walk?"

"I can walk." He proved it, lurching upright, swaying, one hand braced on Wren's shoulder with a weight that frightened them. Up close he smelled of sickness, of the sweet rot that Wren had learned to dread in the mountains when fever went through a camp. "But not far, girl. Not far at all."

*Boy,* Wren thought automatically. They had never corrected him. It had seemed, once, like a small thing to keep.

They made it forty paces down the corridor before his legs gave out.

It happened without drama — one step, then none, his knees simply deciding the argument was over. Wren caught him as best they could, lowering him against the black stone wall, and Tam crouched beside them with the lantern held high, its light trembling across Halvard's face. Beneath the beard, along the line of his jaw, the skin had gone wrong. Greyed. Translucent, almost, as though the man were becoming a drawing of himself, an outline waiting to be filled or erased.

"The diversion," Halvard said. "That was yours. All this — the roads, the noise, the risk." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing bent him double. When he straightened, there was blood on his lips, thin and bright as sealing wax. "You always did spend your ink too freely."

"We're getting you out," Wren said. "There's a road. I drew it. It comes up behind the tanneries, outside the wall—"

"Wren."

Just the name. Just that. And Wren heard what lived underneath it, and stopped talking.

Halvard reached out with his good hand and took theirs. His grip was still strong; that was somehow the worst part, the strength of a dying man's fingers, as though his body were spending everything it had left on this one act of holding on.

"There's no road long enough," he said, "for what I have to tell you. So we'll do it here, and you'll listen, and then you'll hate me, and then — if there's any mercy in you, and there is, I've watched it for twenty years — you'll forgive me. Not because I deserve it. Because carrying this alone has already killed me, and I'd rather it finished the job honest."

Tam shifted. "I can give you the corridor," the young man said quietly, and withdrew to the mouth of the passage, lantern raised, a thin silhouette against the dark.

Wren did not let go of Halvard's hand. "Whatever it is," they said, "it can wait until—"

"It waited twenty years. It can't wait another hour." He closed his eyes. Drew breath. When he opened them again, they were clear in a way Wren had never seen — the look of a man setting down a weight so heavy that setting it down is itself a kind of wound.

"I bound the king," Halvard said.

The words fell into the silence between them and lay there, and Wren stared at him and felt the world tilt gently, like a deck shifting underfoot.

"What?"

"Twenty-two years ago. Before you were born — before you could walk, anyway. I was younger than you are now, and I believed — gods help me, I *believed* — that I was saving him." Halvard's thumb moved across Wren's knuckles, an old habit, the gesture he'd used to soothe them after nightmares on the road. "You've heard Felix's theory. The Veil. The stolen memories. The crown as vessel. He's right, Wren. Every word of it is true, and I know because I helped build the binding. Not the first one — the first bindings are older than the kingdom, older than writing, maybe. But bindings fray. They need mending. And every generation, the crown finds cartographers with the gift, and gives them a choice, and calls the choice mercy."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "A choice."

"Bind the king and keep the Veil anchored, or refuse and be unmade. Those were the options. I was told the king was mad — truly mad, raving, dangerous, that the binding would quiet him and let him reign in peace. And I looked at him, this poor shaking boy of nineteen on the throne, and I thought: yes. Yes, this is kindness. This is the kindest thing anyone will ever do for him." Halvard's voice cracked. "He begged me, Wren. Do you understand? He *begged*. He said the thing behind his eyes was eating his mother's face out of his memory, eating his childhood, eating his name, and he couldn't hold it back much longer, and would I please, please help him. So I drew the lines. I stitched his mind to the Veil the way you'd stitch a wound, and he smiled at me afterward, calm as still water, and I went home and slept better than I had in years."

"And the price," Wren said. Their voice came out flat, distant, someone else's voice. "Felix said the bindings take memories. From the binder."

Halvard nodded slowly. "From everyone touched by the working. The Custodian takes what it needs to balance the ledger. For me it took—" he paused, and something ancient and ashamed crossed his ruined face, "—it took my wife's voice. I remember that I loved her. I remember her hands, her laugh's shape but not its sound. Twenty-two years and I cannot hear her anymore. That was my tithe, and I paid it, and I told myself it was fair."

The lantern flame leaned. Somewhere above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the diversion was still burning itself out.

"But me," Wren said. "What did it take from *me*? I wasn't there. I wasn't born. I wasn't—"

And then they stopped, because Halvard was looking at them with an expression of such naked sorrow that the question died in their throat, and the answer arrived ahead of his words, cold, arriving the way floodwater arrives — not gradually but all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

"A burning house," Wren whispered. "In the foundations, when I drew the road. I saw a burning house. A woman calling a name that isn't mine."

"Is yours," Halvard said. "It was yours. You had another name before Wren. You had a mother who sang off-key and a father who mended nets and a house above the harbour in Callowmere, and you had six years of a life before the Veil needed stabilizing." His grip tightened. "The binding I made frayed faster than expected. The king fought it — he was stronger than they'd estimated, his gift ran deeper. The Custodian demanded more anchor. More memory. More *weight*, to pin the whole architecture down. And the crown's cartographers went looking for the heaviest thing available, and they found a gifted child, six years old, whose own talent was already showing — a child whose memories, properly taken, would hold the Veil steady for a generation."

"No," Wren said.

"They didn't kill your parents, Wren. I want you to know that, because you'll wonder, you'll have wondered your whole life in the shape of that hole inside you. The fire was real, but it was set to cover the taking. Your parents survived. They were made to forget you ever existed — that was part of the price, the symmetry of it, a memory removed must be removed from both ends or the ledger won't balance. Somewhere in Callowmere there may still be two old people who feel, on winter evenings, a sadness they can't explain, and they'll die never knowing why." Halvard's eyes were wet now, streaming freely into the grey of his beard. "And you were carried into the mountains and left where I would find you, because I was the only binder left alive with a conscience, and the men who arranged it knew I'd take you in. They counted on it. They used my guilt as your cradle."

The black stone walls seemed to breathe. Wren became aware, distantly, that they were shaking — that their whole body had become a tremor around the still point of Halvard's hand in theirs.

"You raised me," they said. "You taught me to control it. To hide it. You spent *twenty years* hiding me from the very people you—"

"Yes."

"Why?" The word tore out of them. "Why raise me at all? Why not finish it? Why not leave me in some village and wash your hands?"

"Because I owed you a life," Halvard said simply. "Because I took one and the least I could do was guard the remainder. Because every map lesson, every night I taught you to fold a ridge or blur a border, was a coin against a debt I could never repay. And because—" his breath hitched, wet and rattling, "—because I loved you, girl. Boy. Child of mine in every way that matters and every way that doesn't. I loved you before I knew what you'd pay, and I loved you after, and loving you never once balanced the ledger. Nothing does. That's the confession. That's all of it. I bound a king believing it was mercy, and the mercy cost a child everything, and the child was you, and I let it happen, and then I spent two decades pretending that teaching you to draw was the same thing as giving back what I stole."

Silence filled the corridor like water filling a hull.

Wren knelt there and felt all of it at once — the fury, white-hot and enormous, big enough to burn down kingdoms; and underneath it, unbearable, the love, which had not diminished at all, which had apparently been waiting all along beneath the erased places like bedrock beneath topsoil. They wanted to strike him. They wanted to hold him. They did neither. They bowed their head until their forehead nearly touched his knuckles, and breathed, and the shaking slowed.

"The woman," they said finally, hoarsely. "In the vision. Calling my name. Was she—"

"Your mother. What you saw was an echo. The memories live in the Veil, Wren — they aren't destroyed, nothing there is destroyed, it's all kept, catalogued, shelved. Which means—" and here Halvard's voice lifted, and for a moment the dying man sounded like the teacher on the mountain, urgent with discovery, "—which means nothing is truly lost. If the binding breaks, if the ledger is overturned, everything the Custodian holds comes home. Every stolen wedding day. Every first snowfall. Your name. Your mother's voice. My wife's laugh. *All of it.*"

"That's what Felix believes."

"That's what I *know*, because I helped write the terms." Halvard coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed he slumped against the wall, and Wren saw with a lurch how much the effort had cost him. The translucent quality had spread to his neck. He was fading even as they knelt there, ink running in rain.

"Halvard. We have to move. The road—"

"Listen." His hand found Wren's collar and pulled, weakly, commandingly, the old caravan-master's authority flickering through the ruin of him. "Listen to me. The Custodian will offer you a trade. It does that — it's in its nature, it's the ledger-keeper, it always offers. It will offer you your past. Your name, your mother, the burning house unburned, six years of yourself returned whole. And all it will ask is that you walk away. Leave the king bound. Leave Felix to his crusade. Take your memories and go, and the Veil stands, and no one ever has to know."

"How do you know it will offer me that?"

"Because it offered it to me." Halvard smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had ever seen. "Twenty-two years ago, after the binding, it came to me in a dream wearing my wife's voice. It said: *give us more, and have her back.* One more binding. One more child. There was a family in the next valley, a gifted daughter, eight years old. All I had to do was draw the lines and my wife's laugh would return to me." His hand tightened on Wren's collar. "And I said no. That's the only clean thing in me, Wren, the single thread of honour in the whole rotten cloth — I said no, and I ran, and I hid, and I raised you instead. Remember that when you hate me. Hate me for the binding. Hate me for your stolen name. But remember that when it offers you everything, I am the man who refused everything, and I did it so that you would exist to sit here and curse me."

Wren's vision blurred. They wiped their face with their free hand, angry at the tears, furious at them, and found Halvard watching with an expression of profound, exhausted tenderness.

"Do you forgive me?" he asked. "Don't answer quick. Answer honest."

Wren thought about the burning house. About the woman's voice calling a name like a bell across water. About twenty years of campfires and map lessons and rough hands mending harness, of being taught, patiently, secretly, lovingly, how to survive with a dangerous gift in a world that hunted it — by the very man whose guilt had made the danger necessary.

"I don't know yet," they said at last. "Maybe that's the honest answer. Maybe forgiveness is a road too, and I have to draw it as I walk it."

Halvard laughed — a real laugh, brief and rusty and glad. "That'll do. That'll do fine." He sagged. "Now get me up. Point me at your impossible road. I want to die in the mountains, not under a palace, and I want the last thing I see to be a horizon you invented."

They got him up. It took everything Wren had and both of Tam's arms besides, and even then Halvard could only shuffle, one foot dragging, his weight a slow collapse that they merely delayed. But his eyes had cleared, and he kept muttering directions — *left here, the old fool, mind the low stone* — correcting Wren's own map from memory, as though even now he could not bear to let them travel unguided.

At the mouth of the hidden stair, where Wren's drawn road began — a passage of Wren's own making, walls sketched into solidity, torchlight inked warm and gold — Halvard stopped. Leaned against the frame. Looked back down the dark corridor toward the cells.

"When you reach the Veil," he said, "and it speaks to you in my voice — because it will, it keeps me, some part of me is on a shelf in there right now — you listen close. And if the voice says *run, child, save yourself*, then you'll know it's really me, and you'll know I'm sorry, and you'll know—" his breath caught, held, released, "—you'll know I was proud. Every single day. Proud as any father ever was."

Then his legs went, and this time they did not come back, and Wren lowered him onto the threshold of the drawn road with Tam shouting for Brenna, with the diversion's fires dying overhead, with Halvard's hand gripping theirs one final time.

"Tell Felix," the old man whispered. "The ritual needs a vessel. Tell him I know which one. Tell him—"

The sentence never ended. It simply stopped, the way a pen stops when the hand holding it goes still, and Wren knelt there in the doorway of a road they had invented, holding a man who had unmade them and remade them and loved them in the same breath, while somewhere far below the palace, in a borderland of shelved and catalogued hearts, something ancient stirred on its shelf and began, very softly, to speak in a dead man's voice:

*Run, child.*

*Save yourself.*

*And bring my map home.*
