## 13. Into the Borderland

**WREN**

The king's drawings were drawn in a child's hand, but they were not childish.

Wren had understood that the moment they spread the stolen pages across the floor of the rebel safehouse beneath the ruined watchtower — pages Felix had lifted from the royal apartments two nights before, folded into a surveyor's satchel between false charts of the eastern provinces. Crayon and charcoal and one page done in what looked disturbingly like dried blood. A child's hand, yes: wobbly lines, suns with faces, houses drawn as boxes with smoke curling from chimneys like question marks. But the geography was consistent. Page after page after page, across what must have been years of a boy's life, the same river bent at the same angle, the same mountain wore the same crown of trees, the same road ran to the same door.

A child drawing from imagination forgets. A child drawing from memory does not.

"He was mapping it," Wren said quietly, tracing one wobbling line with a fingertip. "All those years. He wasn't playing. He was trying to draw his way home."

Felix stood at the window slit, arms crossed, watching the dark. He had not slept. Wren could see it in him the way you can see weather coming in a coastline — the gray at his temples looked grayer, the lines around his mouth deeper, his stillness too deliberate, the stillness of a man holding himself together by force of will.

"The entrance is beneath the palace," he said. "The old foundations. The founders built the Veil's threshold under the throne itself so no one would ever look for it. Who digs beneath their own seat?"

"Someone who wants to make sure it's never found," Wren said. "Or someone who wants to make sure it's never *left*."

Felix turned. There was something raw in his face tonight, something Wren hadn't seen before — not the crusader's certainty, not the exile's bitterness, but fear. Real fear, worn openly for the first time since the watchtower.

"When we go through," he said, "we may not come back the same. Or at all."

"You've said that every night for a week."

"And I'll say it until it stops being true or until it kills me." He crossed the room and crouched over the drawings, gathering them with hands that were not quite steady. "The boy-king drew these as maps out. Do you understand? Every stroke was an escape attempt. Every chimney smoke, every crooked door — he was charting the prison they put him in before he even knew it was a prison. And they let him keep the crayons." His laugh came out wrong. "Because a prisoner who draws his cell is easier to watch than one who digs."

Wren said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a lie or a promise, and they had sworn off both.

They went down at moonset, when the palace above slept its drugged, muffled sleep — a palace built to be quiet, Felix had told them, walls packed with ash and wool so no scream would ever reach the street. The old foundations opened beneath the wine cellars through a door that had been bricked and unbricked so many times the mortar never matched. Below it, stairs descended past the reach of any torch, and Wren did what they had been born to do: they drew light into being, a small gold circle on the map they carried, and the darkness obeyed.

At the bottom was a wall.

Not stone. Wren knew stone the way other people know their own heartbeat. This was something else — a surface that gave slightly under the palm, warm, faintly pulsing, like pressing a hand to the flank of a sleeping animal. And across it, unmistakable, the same wobbly lines. The same crooked doors. The boy-king's drawings, repeated at vast scale, grown into the fabric of the border itself like ivy grown into brick.

"He drew on the walls," Wren breathed. "Down here. They locked him away with his crayons and he came down here and he *drew*—"

"Forty years of escape attempts," Felix whispered. "And every one of them still here. Still waiting."

Wren unslung their satchel. Their hands were shaking, and they were honest enough with themselves to admit it was not entirely fear. It was the shaking that came before a first line — the same shaking they'd felt at seven years old, drawing a road into a hillside that had no road, feeling the world lean forward to see what they would make of it.

They pressed the king's drawings flat against the living wall. And then they drew.

The line went down from the last charcoal door like a thread pulled from a hem. Where it touched, the wall remembered how to open. Ink sank into that warm pulsing surface and the surface drank it the way dry earth drinks rain, greedily, hungrily, and where it drank, color bloomed — impossible color, colors Wren had no names for, bleeding outward from the line like dawn bleeding across a frozen lake.

The wall became a doorway. The doorway became a road.

And the road smelled like someone else's childhood.

* * *

**FELIX**

He had prepared for terror. He had prepared for wonder. He had not prepared for the smell of bread.

That was the first thing through the breach: warmth, yeast, woodsmoke — a bakery that did not exist, had perhaps never existed anywhere except inside a memory some founder had torn out of someone's head and paved into the ground. Felix stood on a street built of mornings. Not stones. Mornings. Under his boots the cobbles were golden and soft-edged, each one a different kitchen, a different window, a different pair of hands kneading dough, and if he looked too long at any single cobble he began to remember it — a woman humming, flour on her forearms, a boy reaching up to steal the heel of a loaf — and none of it was his. None of these griefs and joys belonged to him, and yet they pressed against his skin like heat from a hearth, intimate as breath.

"Wren." His voice came out strange, flattened, as though the air here were made of cotton. "Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it." Wren stood a few paces behind him, and Felix heard them inhale — a sharp, involuntary sound, the sound of a cartographer meeting a country that outranked every country they had ever charted. "I'm seeing all of it."

The street ran ahead of them farther than streets should run, curving gently upward toward a horizon that glowed without a sun. Buildings lined it, but they were wrong in the way dreams are wrong — a house made entirely of a single summer afternoon, its windows lit with the specific amber of light at half past six in July; a chapel whose walls were the sound of bells; a market square roofed with umbrellas that were actually the feeling of being small and safe while rain fell somewhere you weren't. Nothing had edges. Everything had *weight*. Felix reached out and touched the wall of the bakery-that-was-only-a-smell, and his fingers came away dusted with flour, and he felt, absurdly, unbearably, like crying.

"This is what they took," he said. "This is what the crown has been harvesting for four hundred years. Not secrets. Not treason." He turned in a slow circle, and his voice cracked on the scale of it. "*This.* First days. Last days. All of it, paved down here so the ones upstairs could rule with clean hands and empty hearts."

Wren had stopped walking. They were staring at the ground, and when Felix followed their gaze he understood why.

The cobbles were moving.

Not shifting — moving. Flowing. Ahead, the street widened into a square, and the square was fed by a river, and the river was made of snowfall. Not water carrying snow — snowfall itself, millions of individual first snows braided together into a current, each flake a different winter, a different child's upturned face, a different gasp of cold wonder. It moved without wind. It made no sound. It simply fell sideways forever, a river of beginnings, and along its banks people stood — or shapes like people, translucent, unfinished, sketches of the souls whose memories had been taken to build this place — and watched it fall.

"They're still here," Wren whispered. "The people. The memories didn't just get stored. They got *worn*."

"By whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

Felix looked up then, following the river back toward its source, and that was when he saw the sky.

It was not a sky. It was a ceiling painted with a sky, except no painter had made it — it assembled itself as he watched, cloud-forms drifting into place with the slow deliberation of ink spreading through wet paper. And scattered through the clouds, catching the glowless light, were shapes. Round shapes. Wet shapes. Shapes with depth, with pupils, with the particular glisten of eyes that have been crying or are about to.

Dozens of them. Then hundreds. Then more than he could count, opening across the whole vault of the false heaven like stars being born in reverse.

None of them blinked.

* * *

**WREN**

Later — if there was later; time moved strangely here, pooling in the low places like water finding its level — Wren would try to explain to Halvard's ghost, or to no one, what it felt like when the sky noticed them.

It did not happen all at once. That was the horror of it. The eyes arrived gradually, the way frost arrives on a window, one crystal deciding where the next should grow. At first there were only a few, high up, half-hidden in cloud, and Wren might have taken them for moons. But moons do not track you. These tracked. When Wren walked three steps east, the eyes drifted three steps east, all of them, in perfect silent concert, a congregation of attention following them across the square of wedding days.

Because that was what the square was. Wren knelt and laid their palm against it and felt it immediately: vows. Hundreds of thousands of vows, compressed into paving, each stone a different voice saying *I do* in a different language, in different centuries, to different beloveds. Some of the stones were warm with joy. Some were cold — oh, some were very cold — and those, Wren realized with a lurch, were the weddings that had ended badly, the memories kept anyway, because the Custodian kept everything, because grief was apparently as valuable as joy when you were building a prison out of the human heart.

"Felix." They stood. Their voice sounded thin even to themselves. "The sky."

"I see it."

"It's not weather. It's—" Wren groped for the word and hated the word when they found it. "It's *attention*. We're being attended to."

Felix had gone very still beside them, and Wren saw his hand drift toward the knife at his belt and then away again, deliberately, the way a man moves his hand away from a thing he knows will not help. Good, Wren thought. At least one of us remembers that you cannot stab a sky.

The eyes kept opening. There was a rhythm to it now, a terrible patience, like something enormous turning over in bed and opening one eye, then another, taking its time, utterly certain that nothing in its bedroom could possibly threaten it. And threaded through the clouds, beneath the eyes, Wren began to hear — not sound exactly, but pressure, the way you feel thunder in your sternum before you hear it. A voice assembling itself out of fragments. Out of *everyone's* fragments.

It spoke in Halvard's voice first.

*"Little cartographer."*

Wren's knees nearly buckled. It was his voice exactly — the gravel of it, the dry Vellish accent, the particular way he'd always said their name like a word he was testing for cracks. Two days dead, and here was his voice wearing the sky like a coat.

"That's not him," Wren said aloud, and their voice shook, and they hated that it shook. "You don't get to wear him. You don't get to *use* him."

The voice changed. It became Felix's mother, then a girl Wren had never known, then — obscenely, casually — Wren's own voice, played back at them from the clouds, saying words Wren had no memory of ever saying: *"I want to go home. Please. I want to go home."*

Wren went cold all over. Because those words fit. Those words fit inside the hole where their childhood used to be, the burning house, the woman calling a name that wasn't Wren — the fragments the road through the castle foundations had shaken loose. Somewhere in this place, on some shelf or street or riverbank, was the rest of their life. And the sky knew it. The sky was holding it up like a lantern.

*"You came looking for a king,"* the many-voiced thing said, and now it had settled into a register that was no one and everyone, a chord sustained beyond endurance. *"But the king is only the lock. I am the door. And you have walked through me into my house."*

Felix stepped forward — stepped *between*, putting his body in front of Wren's as if flesh meant anything here, and Wren loved him so much in that moment it felt like injury.

"We've come for the king," Felix said to the sky, in the voice he used on lieutenants and enemies, the voice that did not shake. "The man you bound. The boy who drew these walls. We're taking him back."

Silence. The eyes all closed at once — every one, simultaneously, like a held breath —

and then, in the heart of the false heaven, directly overhead, a single eye opened larger than all the others combined, an iris wide as a cathedral rose window, and looked down at them with an expression Wren could only describe, in the privacy of their own skull, as *interest*.

Beneath their feet, every wedding day in the square shuddered. The river of first snowfalls stopped falling sideways and hung, suspended, millions of flakes frozen mid-beginning. And far away — impossibly far, down avenues of stolen years they had not yet walked — something began to move toward them with the unhurried weight of a keeper who has finally, after forty years, been noticed by the prisoners' friends.

Wren took Felix's hand. His fingers were ice.

"Run," they said. "Or draw. I genuinely don't know which one saves us."

The great eye dilated.

Somewhere ahead, past the frozen river, a door made of one boy's forty years of crayon lines began, very softly, to open.
