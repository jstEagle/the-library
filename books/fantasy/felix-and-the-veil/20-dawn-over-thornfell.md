## 20. Dawn Over Thornfell

The morning the king burned the proclamation, Wren stood in the courtyard of the palace they had spent a month trying to break into, and felt nothing at all.

That was not entirely true. They felt the cold — Thornfell cold, though this was the capital's own grey dawn, and the wind coming off the mountains carried that particular bite that meant winter was settling in for a long argument. They felt the ache in their drawing hand, the fingers still stiff and ink-stained from the unbinding, the skin of the palm cracked where the Veil's threshold had tried to close over them like a mouth. And beneath all of it, faint as a watermark, they felt the absence. The shape where Felix had been.

But grief, Wren was learning, was not a feeling so much as a room. You walked into it and stood there, and the room did nothing to you, and you did nothing to it, and eventually someone called your name and you walked back out because there was work to do.

Someone was calling their name now.

"Wren Nystrom." The herald's voice rang off the stone. "Come forward."

The courtyard had been cleared for the occasion. Not cleared in the way the crown used to clear things — no lines of soldiers, no scaffold, no crowd held back by pikes — but swept, and washed, and hung with banners whose crests Wren did not recognize, which apparently belonged to provinces that had spent twenty years pretending the capital did not exist and were now, cautiously, sending representatives again. They lined the edges of the yard like birds on a wire, watching to see whether the new age would hold its shape or collapse by noon.

King Aldric stood at the center of it, and he was thin. That was the first thing. Twenty years of binding had left him like a candle burned down to the wick, and even restored — even *lucid*, his eyes clear and present in a way that made servants weep when they brought his breakfast — he moved as if the world were slightly heavier for him than for everyone else. He wore simple clothes. He had refused the ceremonial armor; Wren had heard the argument through a door, the old marshal insisting that a king must look like a king, and Aldric answering, quietly, that he had spent two decades looking like whatever something else wanted him to look like, and he intended never to do it again.

In his hands was the proclamation. Wren knew it on sight — the heavy vellum, the crowned seal, the list of names that had grown longer every week of the hunt. Their own name was on it. *Wren Nystrom, cartographer, gift unregistered, dangerous.* Somewhere below that line, in smaller script, was Halvard's name too, recorded as an accomplice taken alive. Halvard, who had died in a smuggler's safehouse with Wren's hand in his and a confession in his mouth that Wren was still learning how to carry.

Aldric read the proclamation aloud. All of it. Every word of the charges, every clause of the hunt, every name. His voice did not waver, but it did not perform either. When he reached the final line — *let all who harbor the gifted be counted among them* — he stopped, and looked up, and found Wren in the crowd without seeming to search for them.

"This document," the king said, "was written by a man who could not remember why he was afraid." A murmur went through the courtyard. "I have spent some weeks now remembering. I remember signing it. I remember being proud of it. I remember the thing wearing my face telling the council it was a matter of security, and I remember believing, with my whole heart, that the thought was mine." He turned the vellum over, once, as if checking the back for instructions. "There is no punishment I could devise for what was done to me that would not also be a punishment for what I allowed to be done in my name. So I will do the only honest thing available to me."

He handed the proclamation to a page, who carried it to a brazier already burning at the courtyard's edge. The vellum caught slowly — crown-sealed paper, treated against exactly this — and then all at once, the names going up in a bloom of orange light, the seal cracking with a sound like a knuckle.

"The hunt is ended," Aldric said. "Every gifted person in custody is freed. Every order of seizure is rescinded. And every record the witch-finders kept" — here his voice did change, hardening around something old and cold — "will be opened to the people named in them, so that they may know what was written, and burn what they choose."

The courtyard did not cheer. Wren had expected cheering, or feared it, and got instead something stranger: a long collective exhale, hundreds of people letting go of a breath they had been holding for years, some of them sobbing quietly, some of them simply standing very still, as if movement might undo it. An old woman near the front took off her hat and held it over her heart. A soldier — one of Voss's, Wren noted, still wearing the grey — sat down on the cobblestones and put his face in his hands.

Wren watched the fire eat the last of the names and thought: *Halvard should have seen this.* Then thought: *No. Halvard helped build the cage.* Then thought, finally, in the small flat voice of truth: *Both of those are true, and I will spend the rest of my life holding them both, and that is the inheritance he left me.*

* * *

They came for Wren afterward, of course. They came in relays.

First the chancellor, a round careful man who smelled of ink and diplomacy, who proposed a title — *Cartographer Royal*, the very office Felix had once held and been exiled from — with lands, and a stipend, and a place at court. Wren declined before he finished the second sentence.

Then the marshal, who wanted them to draw the border fortresses anew, since half the old maps were compromised by roads that no longer existed or had never existed. Wren told her she did not want a map drawn by someone whose gift bent reality, and she, to her credit, considered this seriously for a full ten seconds before agreeing.

Then a delegation from the University, three scholars with hungry eyes, who wished only — they swore it — to *study* the gift, under the gentlest possible conditions, purely for the advancement of knowledge. Wren looked at the youngest of them, a girl who could not have been more than nineteen, whose right hand was wrapped in bandages that ink had stained through, and said, "Ask her what she wants, not what you want," and walked away. To their mild surprise, the girl followed. Her name was Tamsin. She had mapped her village so thoroughly in her head that she could walk it blindfolded, and the witch-finders had burned the village looking for her, and she wanted — she said, staring at her boots — to learn to draw places that were *safe*. Wren told her to report at dawn. It was the closest thing to an honor they accepted all week.

And then, on the fourth day, Serah Voss came.

Wren had been dreading it and longing for it in equal measure, which was, they were beginning to understand, the signature of everything that mattered. She arrived without escort, without her grey coat, wearing plain travel clothes that fit her badly, as if she had dressed in someone else's life. She found Wren in the map room — the real one, deep in the palace, where Felix had once worked, where the walls still bore faint ghost-lines of charts he had pinned up twenty years ago. Wren had been spending hours there. They told themselves it was research.

"You're leaving," Wren said. It was not a question. Voss had the look of a woman who had already said her goodbyes to a version of herself.

"I was given access to the records," Voss said. Her voice was level, but Wren heard the cost of the levelness, the way you hear the strain in a bridge. "The king kept his word. Everything the witch-finders' archive held, opened to the named. I went looking for—" She stopped. Started again. "My sister. Lira Voss. Taken nineteen years ago. The record says a gifted cartographer erased her — drew her out of the world. That was the file I built my life on. I could recite it."

"And?"

"And the archive keeps drafts." Something moved behind her eyes, some tectonic shift far down. "It keeps the *originals*. The erasure was ordered by the crown itself. Lira had mapped a road into the Veil — accidentally, a child playing at her gift — and the Custodian's anchor required maintenance, and the court fed her to it. The 'gifted cartographer' in the official file was the scapegoat. A woman named Maren Ostwick, who was hanged for it. Who was *innocent*." Voss's hands were very still at her sides, the stillness of someone gripping something invisible to keep from shaking. "I hunted her kind for nineteen years to avenge a murder my own order committed."

Wren said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not be either cruelty or a lie.

"I stood down," Voss said. "Formally. Before the king. I gave him the archive keys myself." A pause. "He offered me a pardon. A commission. Rehabilitation." Her mouth twisted around the word. "I told him there are things service cannot rehabilitate you out of. He understood. He's the only one who could."

"Where will you go?"

Voss looked around the map room, at the ghost-lines on the walls, and Wren watched her realize whose room it had been, and watched her decide not to say so. "North, I think. There are villages in the high passes the crown forgot decades ago. Some of them have children in them." Her jaw worked. "Children who draw. Someone should get there before someone like me does."

She turned to go, then stopped at the door, her back to Wren.

"Nystrom. The man who stayed behind." Wren's chest closed like a fist. "In the Veil. I saw the records of the breach. I saw what the Custodian was, and what it took to starve it, and I saw that someone chose to become its anchor rather than let it take the memories loose on the world." She still did not turn around. "I spent nineteen years believing people like you were the disease. I was wrong about the disease. But I wasn't wrong that power always costs someone. Make sure it costs *you* something. That's the only tax I ever believed in."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the corridor, and Wren stood alone in Felix's old map room with the dust motes turning in the light, and understood that they had just been given, by the last person they'd have expected, permission to grieve.

* * *

The honors kept coming anyway. The king, patient as bedrock, found new ones.

There was a medal struck — Wren never learned what the design was, having declined to attend the ceremony. There was a street to be renamed. There was a proposal to carve the story of the unbinding into the palace gate, and Wren wrote back a single line: *Carve instead the names of everyone the Veil ate, including the ones nobody missed.* To their surprise, the king agreed, and stonemasons began the work in the autumn, and Wren avoided that gate thereafter.

Aldric himself came to see them off, on the grey morning in late autumn when Wren finally packed to leave the capital. He came alone, no guard, no herald, wearing the same plain clothes, and he stood in the courtyard while Wren loaded a single pack onto a borrowed mule.

"You could stay," the king said. "You know I mean it. There is no one in these kingdoms whose counsel I would trust more."

"Your Majesty trusts me because I don't want anything from you," Wren said. "If I stay, that stops being true within a year. Court does that to everyone. It did it to you."

Aldric laughed — a rusty sound, a door opening after decades — and then sobered. "The mountains," he said. "You're going back to the Thornfells."

"Someone has to redraw the smuggler roads properly. Half those passes exist only because I drew them, and I was drawing scared. Scared maps are bad maps." Wren tightened a strap. "And there are others like me up there. Hiding. There have always been others. The hunt is over on paper, but paper doesn't reach into the high valleys. People need to see a gifted cartographer walking openly, drawing openly, and nothing burning down."

"And the other reason?"

Wren's hands went still on the strap.

Because there was another reason, and the king, who had spent twenty years as a vessel and come out the other side knowing exactly what it was to be inhabited by an absence, saw it as easily as reading a legend on a chart.

"There's a corridor," Wren said, after a while. "In the Veil. Deep in it. The records call it the Anchor's Walk. Whatever holds the binding lives there — aware, they think. Sane. Sealed behind a door that opens from neither side." They looked at the mountains, north and grey and waiting. "I keep dreaming of it. Not nightmares. Just... the corridor. And sometimes, lately, the dreams aren't quite dreams. Sometimes there's a line of ink in them that I didn't put there."

Aldric was quiet for a long moment. "You believe he can reach you."

"I believe the binding is a map," Wren said. "And every map can be redrawn. I don't know yet what that means, or whether it means anything, or whether I'm a grieving fool talking to a door. But I'm a cartographer. My whole life has been drawing roads toward places that didn't exist until I drew them." They swung up onto the mule. "So I'm going to go draw."

The king bowed to them — actually bowed, a king to a smuggler's brat from the Thornfells, and Wren, mortified, returned it as best they could from muleback — and then Aldric said the thing Wren would carry north like a coal in the chest:

"He chose it, you know. In the throne room. I was lucid by then, barely, but I saw his face. No one forced Felix Corvane into that corridor. He walked in the way people walk into rivers they've decided to swim." The king's clear eyes held Wren's. "Grief lies to us that it was theft. Sometimes it's a gift. Your work is deciding which."

* * *

The road north took eleven days, and Wren drew none of it, and that was its own strange discipline — walking ordinary roads, on ordinary maps, that existed whether or not anyone believed in them. Tamsin rode part of the way with them as far as the foothills, chattering about contour lines and asking questions Wren answered honestly, which startled her every time. At the crossroads where she turned east toward her own valley, she hesitated, then said, "You look like someone carrying a letter they can't deliver."

"That's the most accurate map of me anyone's drawn yet," Wren said, and sent her home.

Thornfell received them the way the mountains receive everything: indifferently, then completely. The pass where the caravan had once moved contraband by night was empty now, the waystation rebuilt, the proclamation's nail-hole still visible in the doorframe like a scar. Wren stood in it a long time on the first evening, snow beginning to fall, and touched the hole, and thought about how a kingdom could change its mind in a season and a mountain couldn't change its mind at all, and found, unexpectedly, that they preferred the mountain.

They began to draw again on the third night, by lamplight, in the rebuilt waystation. Not roads. Not yet. They drew the corridor.

It came out of their hand almost faster than they could guide it — the long pale passage from the Anchor's Walk, the door at its end, the quality of light that exists nowhere in the waking world. Wren worked until the candle guttered, and when it was done they sat looking at it, heart hammering, because along the corridor's floor, in ink that was definitely not theirs — a heavier hand, a surer line, the confident stroke of a royal cartographer who had once drawn the king's own shadow — there was a mark. Small. Deliberate. Placed precisely where a figure would stand if a figure were pacing that corridor, waiting.

Not words. Not yet. Just a mark, like a man tapping his foot on the far side of a wall to tell you he's there.

Wren pressed their palm flat against the page, over the mark, and the mountain wind screamed past the eaves, and somewhere impossibly far away — or perhaps not far at all, perhaps closer than any place had ever been — something on the other side of the drawing pressed back.

They fell asleep at the table with their hand still on the paper, and dreamed, for the first time since the throne room, not of loss, but of a road.
