## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The valley had a smell, and the smell was nothing.

Sebastian had learned to read it years ago, the way other people learned weather. Crossing the boundary line was like walking into a house where someone had just died—every scent scrubbed away, the air scoured down to its bare mineral bones. Cold stone, wet moss, iron in the water. Nothing else. No incense clinging to a traveler's cloak, no ghost of a god's attention, no whisper of the small magics that followed most people around like lint. Just the world, stripped and honest, the way it had been before anyone thought to ask it for favors.

They liked it here. That was the truth Sebastian never said aloud, because the truth made people look at you strangely. Twelve years in the valley, and they had come to love the silence the way a deaf man loves a quiet room: fiercely, defensively, with the knowledge that the love itself was a wound.

"You're doing the face," said Wren.

"I don't have a face."

"You have one face. It's the one where you're deciding whether to kill someone, and you're doing it right now, at a perfectly nice goat."

Sebastian looked at the goat. The goat looked back with the flat amber insolence of its kind, chewing on the strap of Sebastian's pack as if the strap had been placed in the world solely for its amusement. It belonged to the merchant they were escorting—a fat, sweating wool-factor named Aldous Cray who had paid too well for a two-day crossing and asked too many questions about why the valley's guards never came this far north. Behind them, Aldous's cart creaked under bolts of dyed cloth that would sell for triple in the towns past the pass, and Aldous himself was singing something tuneless and contented, because men with full purses always sang.

The goat was fine. The face was not about the goat.

They were three hours from the boundary stones.

The stones were old work—older than the town, older than the road, older than any charter anyone had bothered to keep. Twelve of them in a ragged arc across the mouth of the valley, each one taller than a man, each one carved with spirals that no living stonemason would claim. They marked the line where magic ended. Step past them and a witch's charms went dead in her pockets, a blessing became so many pretty words, a god's attention slid off you like rain off oiled leather. The valley was a hole in the world's hearing. People had fought wars over less, and mostly the valley's founders had simply been lucky that no one wanted the hole badly enough to bleed for it.

Sebastian crossed that line four times a year, sometimes more, and every crossing felt the same: like stepping out of a burning house into snow. The relief was so total it hurt.

Except today the stones were wrong.

They saw it from a hundred yards out, and at first Sebastian thought it was frost. The morning had come down gray and close, the kind of cold that made the breath hang in front of you like a confession, and the low light did strange things to wet stone. But frost didn't pool like that. Frost didn't gather at the base of the fourth stone in a thin, silvered film that moved—actually moved, a slow crawl like breath on a window—when the wind shifted.

"Hold here," Sebastian said.

Wren, who was nineteen and had the survival instincts of a startled hare, held. Aldous did not.

"Is that silver?" the merchant called, already hauling his cart forward. "Is there silver at the stones? Because I'll tell you, in the low country they pay—"

"Touch it and you'll lose the hand," Sebastian said, without heat, the way you'd tell a child the stove was hot, and Aldous stopped so fast his ox nearly sat down.

Sebastian went alone.

Up close, the film on the stone resolved into something worse than frost and better than silver. It looked like wax, if wax could remember being water. It sheathed the lower three feet of the boundary stone in a smooth, pale coat, and where it thinned toward the top, Sebastian could see the spirals underneath, the old carvings, and—this was the part that made their hands go cold inside their gloves—the spirals were wet. Beaded with moisture. As though the stone had been weeping.

Or as though something had been pressed against it, hard, for a long time.

Sebastian circled the stone. On the far side, sheltered from the wind, the ground had been worn bare in a patch no bigger than a hearth-rug. Knees had pressed there. A body had knelt in that spot so many times that the grass had given up and the dirt had gone smooth and dark, and scattered through that dirt were things that made Sebastian's oath—the old one, the buried one, the one they had spent twelve years pretending was a scar instead of a living thing—twitch.

Wax stubs. Dozens of them, burned down to nothing, their wicks like burnt eyelashes. A ribbon, rotted almost to thread, once blue. And scratched into the stone itself, low, at kneeling height, in a hand that shook with either cold or fervor:

*hear me. hear me. HEAR ME.*

Someone had been praying at the boundary stones.

That was not, in itself, impossible. Pilgrims came sometimes—fools and romantics who'd heard that the valley's silence could cure a noisy soul, who walked the line for a day and went home disappointed. But pilgrims prayed *at* the silence. They prayed to feel it swallow their words. This—this was different. This was someone praying *into* the valley, over and over, for months, maybe years, kneeling in all weathers at the exact seam where the world's hearing stopped.

And something had answered.

Sebastian knew it the way they knew a blade was sharp: not by looking, but by the memory of the edge in their own skin. The film on the stone was not frost, not wax, not silver. It was *attention*. It was the residue of something vast turning its face toward a very small voice, and the valley—dead valley, silent valley, the hole in the world's hearing—had caught that attention the way a spiderweb catches a moth, and held it, and the holding had left a mark.

The oath under Sebastian's skin twitched again. Deeper this time. A scar remembering it had once been a mouth.

They had a brand, Sebastian Reyes. Low on the left ribs, old work, the kind of mark you told people was a war-tattoo and they politely believed. It had been silent for twelve years—silent the way everything was silent in the valley, because the valley killed magic and the brand was nothing if not magic, a knot of someone else's will tied into their flesh. In the low country it ached before storms. In the valley it was just a scar, smooth and dumb, and Sebastian had learned to love its dumbness like a mother's face.

Now it itched.

Not ached. *Itched*, the way a limb itches when it's waking up, when the blood comes flooding back and every nerve screams its own name. Sebastian pressed their palm flat against it through three layers of wool and felt, unmistakably, a pulse. Slow. Vast. Patient in the way that mountains are patient.

Something on the other side of that stone had heard the prayers. And something inside Sebastian had heard the something.

"Seb?" Wren's voice, pitched carefully casual, the way you talk to a dog that's gone stiff over a scent. "You've been standing there a while."

"Checking the stones." Their voice came out level. They were proud of that. "Road's clear. Let's move."

They moved. Aldous's cart groaned back onto the track, the goat trotting after with a final insolent flick of its ears, and the little caravan wound north along the valley floor with the stones dropping behind them and the mountains rising ahead, white-shouldered under the gray sky. Sebastian rode rear guard and did not look back, and looked back anyway, four times, and hated themselves for each one.

* * *

The town was called Ferrow's Rest, because everything in the valley was called after some dead man's convenience, and it had two streets, a shrine with no god in it, and a tavern where the beer was bad and the fire was good and nobody asked questions, which was the real reason Sebastian had stayed twelve years.

They took a room at the back of the tavern, the one with the window that faced the mountain instead of the street, and they sat on the bed with the brand bared to the cold air and a whetstone in their hand, not sharpening anything, just turning the stone over and over, and they tried to think like a sensible person.

Sensible person's account: someone had prayed at the stones. Prayer left marks. The mark on the stone was strange, but strange things happened at the edge of the silence; the boundary was a seam, and seams frayed. The itch under their ribs was an old wound and old wounds itched in cold weather. There was no god waking up. There was no god at all, not for them, not anymore. They had paid their price. Their mother had paid it for them, which was not the same thing and was also somehow worse, and the court beneath the mountain had taken what it was owed, and the Wolf—

The Wolf had gone silent, and Sebastian had gone silent back, and that was the whole of the arrangement, twelve years of it, and it had worked. It had *worked*.

They set the whetstone down.

Here was the thing they never said aloud, the thing they kept below even the brand, in the cellar under the cellar: the Wolf had never spoken. Not once, not in all the years of the service Sebastian barely remembered giving. Other gods spoke to their people. Other gods sent dreams, omens, the occasional burning bush with opinions. The Wolf gave Sebastian nothing but the protection—unbreakable, unasked-for, absolute—and silence. Sebastian had spent years furious about it, then years grateful, then years in the gray country between, where most love actually lives.

And now something had answered a prayer at the stones, and the brand was waking up, and Sebastian's hands would not stop being cold.

A knock at the door. Wren's knock—two soft, one hard, the pattern they'd agreed on years ago for *nothing's wrong but come anyway*.

Sebastian pulled their shirt on and opened the door.

Wren stood in the hall with the lamp from the stair behind them, and their face was doing something complicated. Wren was a bad liar and a worse hider, and right now every line of them was shouting *I found something and I don't know if it's good*.

"There's a woman," Wren said. "Downstairs. Came in an hour ago, half-frozen, on foot, no pack. Aldous bought her a bowl of stew because she looks like a scarecrow that lost its field." They paused. "She's asking after you."

"No one knows me here."

"She's not asking by name. She's asking—" Wren's throat moved. "She's asking for the one who walks the silence. That's what she said. *The one who walks the silence.* She said she was told there'd be such a person. She said—"

"Who told her?"

Wren looked, for a moment, very young. "She wouldn't say. But Seb, she's got wax on her fingers. All over her hands. Old wax, burned-in, like she's been—" They stopped, because Sebastian had gone past them, down the stairs, three at a time, the brand singing under their ribs like a struck bell that only they could hear.

The common room was low-ceilinged and amber-lit and smelled of tallow and bad beer, and the woman sat at the corner table with a bowl of stew untouched in front of her, and she was praying. Sebastian saw it from the stairs—saw her lips moving, saw her hands folded on the table with their coating of pale wax like a saint's gloves, saw the stillness of her, the terrible focused stillness of a person who has been talking to something for a very long time and has finally, *finally*, begun to be heard.

The room's noise bent around her. Not magic—the valley saw to that. Just the ordinary human instinct to leave the devout alone.

She looked up. She had a pilgrim's face, wind-scoured and chapped, and eyes that were doing the thing Sebastian's own eyes had done in the mirror for twelve years: watching the world for a sign, and half-afraid of getting one.

"You," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough from cold and from use. "You feel it too. I knew you would. I *prayed* you would."

Sebastian stopped at the bottom of the stairs, one hand on the rail, the brand pulsing against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and every sensible thought they owned—every careful, hard-won, twelve-years-in-the-building wall of sensible thought—cracked straight down the middle.

"What," they said, "did you pray for?"

The woman smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone standing at a door they had knocked on for years, watching it finally begin to open.

"I prayed for the Wolf," she said. "And something answered."
