## 2. Prayers Answered Out of Order

The silence of the valley was not an absence. Odile Vane had spent three days learning that, and it had cost her a blistered heel, two frost-nipped fingers, and the last of her certainty.

Silence in the cloister had been a made thing—a hush maintained by rule and ritual, by sisters moving on soft soles and speaking only when speech served the Quiet Mouth. It sat upon the world like a hand laid gently over a mouth. You could feel its edges. You could feel, always, the will beneath it.

This silence was different. This silence was a wound in the world, and the world had healed around it without ever quite closing.

She felt it first at the boundary stones: a gray ring of standing rocks furred with lichen, each one carved with spirals worn nearly smooth, and as she stepped between them the prayer she had been murmuring under her breath—the small constant prayer she had carried since the night everything broke, *keep me, keep me hidden, keep me*—simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Like a note cut with a knife. The words were still in her mouth; they simply went nowhere. She stood among the stones and prayed at the top of her voice for the first time since she was a novice, and heard nothing but her own breath steaming in the cold, and felt nothing at all where the listening used to be.

It should have been a relief. It was the whole reason she had come: the Valley That Forgot, where magic could not follow, where gods could not reach, where a woman through whom a god had spoken could go to be no one's vessel, no one's oracle, no one's scandal. Every sister in the Order of the Quiet Mouth knew the doctrine. *The valley is the deaf ear of the world.* They taught it to novices as a caution—do not grow attached to what cannot hear you—and Odile had repeated it a thousand times in a thousand sermons without once understanding what it meant to stand inside that deafness herself.

Now she understood, and it was not relief. It was like going deaf. It was like having a limb amputated that she had never known she used.

Three days walking down the valley road, and she had not stopped reaching for the listening. That was the humiliating truth of it. Every morning she woke and prayed out of habit, and every morning the prayer fell dead at her feet like a bird dropped from a height, and every morning she grieved it a little, and hated herself for grieving, because wasn't this what she wanted? Wasn't this the famous mercy? No god could find her here. No god could open her mouth and pour itself through her voice in front of four hundred faithful and one horrified Abbess. Here she was only a woman with cold feet and a stolen horse she had already sold, walking toward a town whose name she kept forgetting because names mattered less here, everything mattered less here, everything was *quieter*—

And yet.

And yet at night, lying in ditches and haylofts with her cloak pulled to her chin, she caught herself praying anyway. Silently. Uselessly. Into the deafness. *Keep me. Keep me hidden. Keep me.*

Hope, she thought, is the one sin we never name.

* * *

The town was called Ferrow's Rest, or perhaps just Ferrow; the signs disagreed. It sat low in the valley's throat where two grey rivers braided together, a scatter of slate roofs and smoking chimneys and one stone bridge old enough to have been built by people who remembered why the valley forgot. Odile came into it at dusk on the third day, hungry enough to be honest with herself about needing work, and did what she had done in six towns before this one: found the inn, found the keeper, and offered the only trade she had left.

"I can read," she said. "I can write a fair hand. I know numbers, herb-lore, midwifery, and how to keep a ledger that would satisfy a tax-collector." She did not say: I know liturgy in four tongues, I can recite the Ladder of Silence from memory, I once held four hundred souls in the palm of my voice. "I'll work for bread and a corner near the hearth."

The keeper—a broad woman with flour to her elbows and eyes that measured strangers the way a butcher measures meat—looked her over twice.

"Mercenary company comes through regular," the keeper said. "They need letters written home. Wills, sometimes." A pause, weighted. "You'd be writing wills more often than letters."

"I've written worse things," Odile said, and meant it more deeply than the keeper could know.

So it was arranged, and that was how Odile Vane, formerly First Voice of the Order of the Quiet Mouth, came to be sitting at a scarred table in the common room of the Drowned Bell, copying a mercenary's farewell to his mother in her best book-hand while around her the room filled with the particular noise of armed people relaxing.

There were eight of them, escorting something in a locked wagon up-valley—salt, someone said, or silver, or nothing at all, because mercenary cargo was a rumor the cargo paid to spread. They drank steadily and argued amiably and called each other by names like Hobb and Weir and a big gentle giant of a man everyone called Mutton for reasons never explained. And apart from them, at the end of the bar, apart from all of them the way a held breath is apart from breathing, sat the one the others didn't talk to.

Sebastian Reyes.

She knew the name before she saw the face. Everyone in the order knew the name, though most of them thought it apocryphal: the mercenary who walked into the valley twelve years ago and never walked out, who took contracts no one else would take inside the silence, who had survived things in the valley's deep places that killed whole companies. The sisters told it as a ghost story. *There is a soul in the valley that owes nothing to heaven,* the novices whispered, *and it carries knives.*

Odile had assumed the stories were embroidered. Then the man himself—or woman herself, or person themselves; the years and the road had planed Sebastian Reyes down to something past easy naming—had come down the stairs that evening, and Odile had felt the brand before she saw it.

Not a brand on Sebastian. A brand on *her*. Low on her sternum, where the Quiet Mouth marked its First Voices with a sigil of closed lips, there was a heat like a coal wrapped in wool. It had been cold for three days—everything in her had been cold, everything had been *silent*—and then Sebastian Reyes walked down those stairs and the coal woke, and Odile's hands shook so badly she nearly ruined the will she was writing.

Because the mark did not burn the way heresy burned. It burned the way recognition burns. It burned the way a struck bell hums after the clapper has stilled, humming because something nearby was *tuned to it*.

She watched Sebastian cross the room. Watched the way the mercenaries' voices dipped—not fear exactly, but the careful courtesy people give to weather. Watched the flat gray eyes take the room in one pass and dismiss it, and snag—just barely, just for a heartbeat—on her. On her hands. On the ink stains. On whatever it was that Sebastian Reyes saw when looking at a stranger, which Odile suspected was threat, and distance, and exits.

And then Sebastian had gone still, halfway across the floor, like a deer scenting smoke, and Odile realized her lips were moving. Three days of dead prayers, and now, near this person, her mouth had started moving on its own.

*Keep me. Keep me hidden. Keep me—*

The coal against her sternum flared white-hot. Across the room, Sebastian Reyes pressed one hand flat against their own ribs, as if something under their skin had answered.

Odile stood up so fast her stool went over.

"You," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough from cold and from use. "You feel it too. I knew you would. I *prayed* you would."

Sebastian stopped at the bottom of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and looked at her with an expression Odile would spend the rest of her life learning to read: a wall with a crack in it, and light coming through the crack, and terror of the light.

"What," Sebastian said, "did you pray for?"

"The Wolf," Odile said. "I prayed for the Wolf. And something answered."

* * *

Later—much later, after the common room had emptied and the fire had been banked and the keeper had pointedly gone to bed, leaving them alone with a candle and a jug of small beer neither of them drank—Odile told it all. She had not planned to. She had planned, insofar as she had planned anything in three desperate days, to lie: to be a traveling scribe, a widow, a woman with no history worth the telling. But Sebastian Reyes listened the way stone listens, giving back nothing, offering nothing, and lies need an audience that pushes back, and against that silence her truth came out of her like blood from a opened vein.

"A month ago," she said, "I was preaching the Feast of the Closed Lips. Four hundred faithful. The whole chapter house. And I was speaking the homily I have spoken eleven years running—*the gods are loud and we are quiet, and quietness is holy, and the loudest thing in creation is a mouth that stays shut*—" Her hands curled around the mug. "And somewhere in the middle of it, I stopped being the one speaking."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"It was like drowning in reverse. Like being pulled up from underwater by the throat. And the voice that came out of me—it used my voice, my music, my cadence, but it was *vast*, Sebastian, it was vast the way a winter sky is vast, and it said—" She had never said it aloud. Saying it now, in this deaf valley where no god could hear, was the bravest thing she had done since she ran. "*I am awake. Who buried me? Who buried me, and where?*"

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled.

"Four hundred people heard it. The Abbess heard it. And the Abbess—" Odile laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what my order actually does? All those lovely sermons about silence? We don't worship quiet. We *enforce* it. There are gods older than the pantheon, gods the bright ones buried when the world was young, and my order keeps them buried. We are gravediggers with hymnals. And when one of them spoke through the First Voice of the Quiet Mouth, mid-sermon, in front of the whole chapter—" She spread her hands. "There is a rite for that. I was raised on the texts. I know the words that would close my mouth forever. They gave me until dawn to submit to it willingly."

"And instead you ran."

"Instead I ran." She looked at him—at them; she still hadn't settled it and suspected she never would. "Twelve years you've lived in this valley. Twelve years of the silence. Tell me I'm mad. Tell me the deafness is a mercy and I should have let them close my mouth and gone back to being a good gravedigger. Because I have been telling myself that for three days and I cannot make myself believe it, and I am so tired, and you—" the coal flared again, and she pressed her fist to her sternum, furious at it, at herself, at the hope she couldn't kill— "you *burn* when I pray. Do you understand what that means? Nothing burns in this valley. Nothing answers here. Except you."

Sebastian Reyes had not moved in a long time. The candlelight lay along the planes of their face like snowfall on rock. When they finally spoke, their voice was low and even and utterly without warmth, the voice of someone reading terms.

"Nothing answered your prayer," they said. "Whatever you felt, whatever you think you felt—I'm a mercenary. I carry a knife and a bad reputation and a contract or two. There's no god in me. There's nothing in me at all. That's the whole point of this valley." They stood, pushing back the bench. "You want a miracle, walk back over the stones and pray till your knees bleed. In here, you're just a tired woman talking to a tired soldier. Get some sleep. The wills won't write themselves."

They climbed the stairs. The door above closed with a click soft as a knuckle tap.

Odile sat alone in the banked firelight, and the coal under her ribs pulsed once, twice, slow as a sleeping heart, and she found she was smiling, and could not stop.

*Stubborn,* she thought. *Liar. Frightened. Twelve years building a wall with a god on the other side of it.* She knew the architecture intimately. She had been raised inside one.

She blew out the candle, and in the dark she did the forbidden thing, the thing she had sworn off at the boundary stones, the thing that had gotten her exiled and hunted and very possibly doomed.

She folded her hands, and silently, into the deafness of the valley, into the space between herself and the sleeper upstairs, she prayed:

*Wolf. If you can hear me through them—if you're using them the way it used me—then listen. They will never ask for themselves. They will die polite and invisible rather than ask. So I'm asking for them. Wake up. Come and get what's yours.*

The coal burned steady, like a lamp set in a window.

And far above the valley, far above the deafness, something with the patience of winter turned over in its long sleep, and began, at last, to dream of hunting.
