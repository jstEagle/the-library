## 3. What the Wolf Left

Sleep in the valley was a small country, and Sebastian had learned its borders well.

Twelve years of practice. You learned to sleep light when magic couldn't reach you—learned that dreams here were only dreams, dim and doorless, no gods leaning through the walls of them, no debts collecting interest in the dark. Other people found the valley's silence restful. Sebastian had found it survivable. There was a difference, and they had built a life inside the difference: escort work along the boundary roads, coin enough for bread and lamp oil, a room above the cooper's shop where the barrels kept the wind honest. Wary contentment. A scar you stopped touching.

But lately the scar itched.

It had started at the boundary stones—the western row, the ones older than the town's charter, granite shoulders worn smooth by centuries of weather and one century of deliberate forgetting. Someone had been leaving offerings there. Not the usual things: not coins, not bread, not the little knotted charms mothers tied for safe births. Someone had been leaving *prayers*. Sebastian had found them on the last escort—strips of linen, inked in a careful hand, folded into triangles and wedged into the cracks between stones like seeds into furrows. And the stones had been *warm*. Warm in a valley where nothing answered, warm like a hearthstone hours after the fire died, warm in a way that made the old oath under Sebastian's skin twitch and roll over like a dog hearing its name after years of silence.

They'd burned the linens. They'd told themselves it was caution, not fear. They'd slept badly anyway, three nights running, and on the fourth night the Wolf came.

* * *

The dream did not begin so much as arrive, the way winter arrives—not at the door but already in the room.

Sebastian stood in snow that fell upward. That was the first wrongness, and they catalogued it the way they catalogued everything: upward-falling snow, a sky the color of iron gone soft, a forest of black pines whose branches held no snow at all because the snow refused them. The air smelled of cold stone and wet fur and something older underneath, something like the inside of a mountain before anyone had ever dug into it. Under their feet the ground was not ground but the memory of ground, springy and silent, and every breath drew a taste across their tongue like blood taken from a cupped palm.

And the brand—the old oath-mark, the faded thing behind their left ear that had been quiet as ash for twelve years—*burned*.

Not pain. Worse than pain. Recognition.

Sebastian turned in a slow circle, hand finding the knife at their hip out of habit, though even in the dream they knew knives were beside the point. The pines stood in ranks around them, patient as soldiers. Somewhere among those ranks, something was moving without disturbing a single needle.

"You're late," Sebastian said aloud, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them, because inside they were shaking like a struck bell.

The forest answered with silence—but it was a different silence now. The valley's silence was an empty room. This was a held breath.

Then the trees ended all at once, the way scenery ends in dreams, and Sebastian stood at the edge of a clearing where the upward snow gathered into a spiral, turning, turning, and in the center of the spiral sat the Wolf.

Not a wolf. Sebastian had hunted wolves in the years before the valley, had tracked them through the high passes for bounty and for meat, and this was no wolf the way a cathedral is no house. It was vast and it was lean and its coat was the gray of storm-light over water, silvered at the edges as if the moon had been worked into it thread by thread. Its eyes were the color of the space between stars, and they were fixed on Sebastian with an attention so total it felt like being flayed gently, layer by layer, down to whatever was truest.

Sebastian had prayed to this thing for eleven years and been answered with nothing. They had made their offerings in secret—blood from a pricked thumb on the boundary stones, back when the stones still carried a whisper of the god's scent—and received silence. They had concluded, reasonably, that the Wolf was dead, or distant, or had never wanted a mercenary's devotion in the first place. They had grieved it the way you grieve anything unfinished: badly, privately, and then not at all.

Now it sat in the snow looking at them like a father who has crossed a country to find a child who stopped writing.

"You could have come sooner," Sebastian said. The steadiness cracked on the last word. "Eleven years. I prayed until praying felt like talking to a grave."

The Wolf's ears tilted. When it spoke, its voice was not sound exactly—it arrived the way cold arrives, through everything at once, through skin and bone and the spaces between thoughts. It was a voice like a river under ice: deep, moving, muffled, immense.

*I heard every word.*

"Then why—"

*Because I could not cross what your mother bought.* The great head lowered, and the star-colored eyes did not blink. *Sit, little oath. What I have to tell you is long, and you will want to be sitting.*

"I don't sit when strange gods tell me to."

Something moved across the Wolf's face that on a lesser creature would have been a smile. *You never did. Even at eight years old, face down in the shrine mud, you argued with me about the proper way to ask for things.*

The words went through Sebastian like a spear through water—no resistance, just sudden cold everywhere. Eight years old. Shrine mud. The little chapel above the river bend where their mother had taken them every feast day of their childhood, where Sebastian had talked to the god the way other children talked to imaginary friends, except the friend had sometimes talked back, in feelings rather than words, in warmth rather than voice.

No one knew that. No one living knew that.

"How do you know that?"

*You gave me those years,* the Wolf said. *Freely, before you knew giving had a price. They are mine to remember even if they are yours to forget.* The Wolf rose, and rising seemed to take a long time, the way a mountain range seems to rise if you watch it long enough. Snow spiraled outward from its flanks. *Walk with me. It is easier shown than said.*

* * *

They walked, and the forest walked with them, and the dream taught Sebastian things the way dreams do—by simply knowing them, suddenly, completely, without the courtesy of explanation.

They walked through a winter twelve years deep.

The first scene assembled itself out of the falling snow like frost forming on glass: a kitchen. Their mother's kitchen, the low-beamed room with the blackened hearth and the table scarred by twenty years of knife-work and the window that looked out on the river bend. But the kitchen was wrong—too dark, too still, the fire burned down to a red eye, and the quality of the darkness was wrong, thick, expectant, the darkness of a room where something enormous has just finished speaking.

Their mother stood at the table. Younger than Sebastian remembered her—younger than Sebastian was now—and she was crying, but she was not stopping. That was the detail that would stay. She cried the way people do arithmetic: steadily, without pause, tears tracking down a face set hard with purpose. On the table before her lay a scrap of parchment, and her finger moved along it, reading terms, and her lips moved with it, and the shadow in the corner of the room—the shadow that was deeper than the room's own darkness, the shadow that had edges like a doorway—leaned in to watch her read.

"Mama," said a child's voice, and Sebastian's whole body flinched, because it was Mateo's voice, Mateo at six years old, standing in the kitchen doorway in his nightshirt with his feet blue with cold. "Mama, why are the shadows talking?"

"Go back to bed, love."

"The big one says my name."

"*Go back to bed.*"

Mateo went. He always obeyed her; he was the obedient one, the sweet one, the one who stayed. And their mother turned back to the parchment, and Sebastian watched her own hands—younger hands, unscarred—reach across the years and fail to stop what happened next, because this was a memory, or worse than a memory, and nothing in it could be touched.

She signed. The shadow took the parchment, and where the ink had been there was now a mark on *her*, and then—Sebastian watched it happen with the helpless clarity of dreams—a second mark bloomed, fainter, smaller, behind the ear of a child sleeping down the hall. Two children. One signature. The shadow in the corner made a sound like coins counted slowly, and the kitchen went ordinary again, and outside the window the first snow of the season began to fall on the valley that would keep them.

"No," Sebastian said. Their voice came out of them torn. "No, that's not—that was protection. Everyone knows. The valley's protected, that's why we came, that's why everyone—"

*Everyone knows the story,* the Wolf said, gentle as an avalanche. *Now look at what the story cost.*

The snow swirled, and the second scene formed.

A ledger. Vast as a hall, pages tall as sails, bound in something dark and veined like marble, and across the open page ran columns of script in an alphabet that hurt to parse—each character a small contract, each line a life reduced to terms. And there, mid-page, in their mother's careful hand, the entry:

*One soul, given freely by the undersigned, as surety and payment entire, together with the souls of issue, two (2), against the protection of the Valley of the Silent Ford, term perpetual, condition: the ledger remain open, the court remain fed, the debt remain unread.*

Two souls of issue. Two.

*Mateo's was collected first,* the Wolf said. *The fever that took him was no fever. The court called in its collateral early—it does that, when it wants to remind a debtor who holds the paper. Your mother was told the boy's soul would buy the family's continued good standing. She was told a great many things. Courts beneath mountains are generous with words and miserly with truth.*

The clearing spun. Sebastian was on their knees in the upward snow and did not remember falling, and the grief that came up through them was not new grief—it was twelve years old, it was the oldest thing in them, it was the night they had held their brother's cooling hand and been told God's ways were mysterious, and now the mystery had a name and a ledger and a column of figures, and it was so much worse than mystery. Mystery you could survive. Arithmetic you had to live inside.

"She sold us." The words tasted like copper. "To protect the valley. To protect—" A laugh came out of them, jagged, awful. "She didn't even protect *us*. Mateo died anyway. I got the brand and the silence and twelve years of hiding, and the valley got its protection, and she got to be the woman who saved everyone, and nobody—"

*Nobody asked what it cost,* the Wolf finished. *That is how courts prefer it. Debts unread are debts unpaid forever. A read debt can be contested. An unread one only collects.*

Sebastian looked up. The Wolf stood over them, immense and still, and there was something in the star-eyes that Sebastian had spent a lifetime refusing to name because naming it would mean hoping, and hope was a currency they had sworn off years ago.

"Why now?" they rasped. "Why come back now? Why answer?"

*Because the court stirs,* the Wolf said. *Because the old gods are waking, one by one, and the courts are sealing their ledgers before we can read them. Because solstice comes, and at solstice the gates beneath the mountain close until spring, and whatever is written in that book when the gates close is written for a season of dark.* The Wolf's head swung toward the north, toward the mountains Sebastian had spent twelve years not looking at. *And because someone has been praying at your boundary stones, little oath, and prayers are doors, and I am done staying shut out.*

"The linens," Sebastian whispered. "I burned them."

*You burned the paper. The prayer remains. It was a good prayer. Desperate, badly worded, sincere.* Something like warmth moved through the god's voice. *Those are the kind I answer best.*

"Whose prayer?"

*That,* said the Wolf, turning back to them, *is a question for waking. I have shown you what was taken. Now choose what you will do about it, because the choosing cannot be done in dreams. Dreams are mine. Choices are yours. That was always our bargain, and I have kept my half of it every day of your life, even the days you cursed me for silence.*

Sebastian knelt in the snow with their brother's death rewritten and their mother's love reweighed and their whole careful, survivable, wary-contented life lying around them in pieces like a dropped lantern, and they found—to their own astonishment—that the first coherent thought in the wreckage was not grief.

It was fury. Clean, clarifying, directional fury.

"The ledger," they said. "You said a read debt can be contested."

The Wolf's ears came forward.

"Then I'm going to read it," Sebastian said. "Out loud. Every line. Hers, mine, Mateo's—all of it. And then I'm going to take my soul back."

For a long moment the god said nothing, and the upward snow hung suspended around them both, thousands of small white decisions paused mid-air. Then the Wolf bowed its head—an inch, no more, but from that neck an inch was a landslide—and the star-eyes closed, and opened.

*Then wake,* the Wolf said, *and count your allies. You will need more than fury to walk into a mountain. You will need roads, and names, and someone who owes the court favors he has not yet paid—*

The dream tore like cloth.

* * *

Sebastian woke in the grey hour with the brand behind their ear burning like a coal and the taste of blood in their mouth where they'd bitten their tongue, and lay listening to the cooper's barrels settling downstairs, and the wind working at the shutters, and—beneath it, faint, rhythmic, wrong—

Footsteps. Crossing the yard below. Stopping.

A knock at the street door, three times, apologetic. And a voice they knew from the ferry landing, Tomas Bell's voice, pitched low and urgent through the wood:

"Reyes. Reyes, I know you're awake—I saw your lamp. Open the door. There are collectors on the valley road asking after you by name, and I've got till dawn to decide what I tell them."
