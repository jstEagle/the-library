## 4. A Ferryman in Debt

The river came down out of the mountains the color of old pewter, and Tomas Bell had spent thirty years learning its moods the way other men learned their wives': which bends silted up after a hard rain, where the current ran lazy and deep enough to drown a horse, and which stones you could trust to hold a rope in flood season. He knew the ferry crossing at Graywater better than he knew his own face. His own face had changed too much to bother with.

He was mending net in the gray hour before dawn when the collectors came, and he knew them before he saw them, because the river told him first. The water went still. Not calm—still, the way a held breath is still, every ripple lying down flat as if something vast had put its hand on the surface and said *quiet now*. Thirty years on that water and Tomas had never once seen it lie flat. It moved even in ice, under the ice, a slow black muscle. Now it stopped, all along the shingle below his hut, and the mist that came off it hung in ropes instead of drifting.

Then he heard their boots on the frost-stiffened gravel, and he understood what the river was doing. It wasn't calming itself. It was flinching.

There were three of them. They came down the north path from the mountain road, walking in step without appearing to try, and they were dressed like respectable travelers—good wool cloaks, travel-stained but well cut, soft boots, the kind of clothes that said *I have money and no need to tell you where it came from*. That was the first wrongness. The second was that they cast shadows, though the sun hadn't cleared the eastern ridge yet, and the shadows fell toward the light instead of away from it. The third wrongness was small and it was this: the elder of the three, a woman with a face like a folded letter, smiled at him as she approached, and the smile reached her eyes last, if at all, and by then it had already died of the journey.

"Tomas Bell," she said. Her voice was pleasant. It was the pleasantness of a well-tuned instrument being played by someone who did not care for music.

"Ferry's not running till full light," Tomas said. He kept his hands busy with the net, because hands that stop working look like hands that are afraid. "And I don't carry passengers before I've had my tea. House rule."

"We're not passengers." The woman stopped at the edge of his work-light, just outside the circle the lantern threw, and stood there with her hands folded like a woman waiting at a counter. "We're here about your account."

Tomas's hands kept mending. His mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it did when the river rose faster than expected. *Your account.* Three favors. Everyone who crossed his ferry carrying something the law would want to look at too closely paid the same toll: passage, silence, and a marker against the court beneath the mountain. Three favors, called in whenever the court liked, however the court liked, and everyone in the valley knew it and nobody spoke of it, because speaking of it felt like knocking on a door you hoped never to see opened.

He'd taken the bargain eleven years ago, the winter his daughter Ilsa got sick and the valley's healers shrugged and the mountain's medicine worked miracles—for a price. He'd signed the paper with a hand that shook so badly the ink blotted twice. He'd told himself, every year since, that favors owed were better than debts unpaid, that the court was patient, that patience meant safety. He had told himself so many comfortable things over the years that he sometimes wondered if there was anything left underneath them but river water.

"You've come a long way to collect a debt," he said. "Most folk wait for spring."

"The court does not observe seasons," the woman said. "It observes accounts." She unfolded one hand and turned it palm-up, and something lay in it: a scrap of parchment no bigger than a thumbnail, and on the parchment, written in ink that seemed to drink the lantern light rather than reflect it, a single line of script. "This is your first favor. We've brought it written down, so there can be no confusion later about terms."

"I can't read court-hand," Tomas said, which was true, and also a lie, because he could read enough to know that whatever was written there, he didn't want to read it.

"Of course you can't." The woman's smile again, brief as a fish flashing under water. "That's why we read it aloud. Listen carefully, ferryman, because we will say it only once." She lifted the scrap to her lips, and the two behind her leaned in slightly, the way men lean toward a fire, and Tomas realized with a cold sliding feeling that they weren't leaning toward the parchment at all. They were leaning toward her voice. Toward whatever rode inside it.

"'The first favor is called,'" she read. "'Bring us the branded one. The mercenary who dwells in the valley of forgetting, marked by our seal, whose soul stands surety in our ledger. Deliver them living, undamaged, and unprayed-over, to the gate of the court beneath the mountain, before the closing of the year.'"

The words settled into the morning air and stayed there. The river didn't move. Even the wind seemed to be holding its opinion.

Tomas set down the net. Slowly. He looked at the woman, and then at the two behind her, and then out at the flat dead water, and he thought about Ilsa, who was somewhere beneath that mountain right now, alive and breathing and serving at the pleasure of the thing that owned a piece of his soul's worth. He thought about how many crossings he'd made in eleven years, and how many of those crossings had been people running from exactly this kind of thing, and how he'd always told himself the favors would never actually come due, or if they did, they'd be small ones. A night's labor. A message carried. A blind eye turned.

Not this. Never this.

"Branded one," he repeated. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "You mean the mercenary. Reyes."

"Sebastian Reyes," the woman agreed. "Currently employed escorting a wool merchant's caravan through the valley of forgetting. Currently unaware that anything has changed. Currently—" and here she paused, and the pause had weight to it, like a stone placed deliberately on a scale, "—unaware of us. You will keep it that way until delivery."

*Undamaged, and unprayed-over.* The phrase snagged in Tomas's mind like a burr in wool. Unprayed-over. As if prayer could damage the merchandise. As if someone, somewhere, was worried about exactly that.

"And if I refuse?" he asked.

The woman tilted her head, considering him with genuine curiosity, the way a scholar considers an interesting error. "Refusal isn't a term of your account, ferryman. Your account offers compliance, delay, and default. Compliance is rewarded. Delay is tolerated briefly. Default—" she glanced out at the flat water, almost fondly, "—default is creative. Shall I describe default?"

"No," Tomas said.

"Compliance, then." From within her cloak she produced a second scrap of parchment, blank except for a single line of court-hand near the bottom, and a thin stylus of dark metal. "Sign acknowledgment of receipt. The favor is fulfilled upon delivery. Until then, we remain nearby. We do not sleep. We do not eat. We do not leave. And ferryman—" the smile again, dying on arrival, "—we will know if you speak of this to anyone. The valley forgets many things. We are not among them."

He signed. The stylus was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature; it drank warmth from his fingers the way the parchment drank light. When he finished, the woman took back both scraps, folded them away into wherever such things lived when they weren't being terrible, and inclined her head with the courtesy of one merchant to another.

"A pleasure doing business," she said.

They left the way they'd come, up the north path, walking in step, casting their shadows the wrong way, and the moment they passed out of sight around the bend the river exhaled. Water sound came back all at once—the lap and gurgle of it, the tick of ice forming at the margins—and Tomas sat down hard on his own doorstep, in the frost, in the gray light, and discovered that his hands were shaking worse than they had the day he'd signed the original paper.

*Deliver them living, undamaged, and unprayed-over.*

He knew Sebastian Reyes. Everybody in the valley knew Reyes, the way you know a dog that keeps to itself: the escort who took jobs nobody else wanted, who walked the boundary roads alone, who paid in honest coin and asked nothing of anybody and spoke to no god and no neighbor more than necessary. Twelve years in the valley. Twelve years keeping their head down so hard you'd think they were trying to grow roots through their own skull. Whatever brand they carried, whatever the court wanted them for, they'd done nothing to earn collection except exist. Except survive.

Except be sold. Because that was what the ledger language meant, once you turned it over in your head the way you turn a stone to see what lives under it. *Whose soul stands surety in our ledger.* Somebody had sold that soul. Somebody had signed a paper with a shaking hand, maybe, or a steady one, and gotten something for it, and the price of that something had been walking around the valley for twelve years doing escort work and minding their own business.

Tomas knew what that was like from the inside. He'd sold something too. Not a soul—he didn't think—but a daughter's health bought on credit against three unnamed favors, and now the bill had come, and the bill was a person. A specific person, with a specific face, who'd probably ridden his ferry a dozen times and paid honest coin and maybe even helped pull a rope in high water once, because that was the kind of thing Reyes did when nobody was watching.

* * *

He didn't decide anything that day. That was the trick of it, he understood later—there was no moment of choice, no clean fork in the road. There was just the day happening around him while the decision made itself somewhere underneath his thinking, the way a river cuts a new channel: not by force but by persistence, grain by grain, until one morning the water is going somewhere else entirely.

He ran the ferry. Full light came and went; he carried a tinker across with a cart of dented pots, and a young couple going the other way with all their belongings in sacks, emigrating to some cousin's farm, and he took their coin and wished them luck and watched them go and thought, *that's what running looks like*, and thought, *that's what staying looks like*, and couldn't tell anymore which one he was doing.

The collectors were visible twice. Once at midday, standing on the ridge above the north road, three figures in good wool, motionless, watching the valley floor with the patience of herons. Once at dusk, closer, at the tree line near the boundary stones, and this time when Tomas looked at them one of the younger ones—had that one been a woman before? it was hard to remember—turned its head and looked back at him across half a mile of open ground, and even at that distance he felt the look land on his skin like a brand of his own. *We do not sleep,* she'd said. They didn't blink, either, apparently.

So he couldn't warn Reyes openly. Couldn't walk up to the escort camp with the truth in his mouth; they'd know before he got within shouting distance. Couldn't send a letter; letters traveled through hands, and hands could be read. Couldn't tell the wool merchant, or the caravan guards, or the priestess—because there was a priestess now, word came upriver of a runaway holy woman asking after the silence of the valley, and Tomas filed that away in the part of his mind where he kept things that might matter later, next to tide tables and the memory of his daughter's laugh.

What he could do was what he'd always done. Work the river. Work the crossing. Be the ferryman, thoroughly and completely, until being the ferryman was a wall between himself and everything else.

Near evening a boy came down from the valley town with a message: the wool merchant's caravan, delayed by weather on the high road, would reach the crossing in two days' time and needed passage for twelve souls and their beasts. Standard work. Tomas sent back word that the ferry would run, and sat looking at the flat stretch of water where the river had flinched, and began, grain by grain, to cut his channel.

Two days. Two days to figure out how to warn a person you barely knew that three things in good wool cloaks were waiting to collect them, without telling anyone you'd warned them, while three things in good wool cloaks watched you not sleeping, not eating, not leaving.

He thought about Ilsa. He thought about her constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. She'd been nine when he'd signed. Nine years old, burning with fever, and the mountain's medicine had pulled her back from the edge like a fisherman hauling in a line, and he'd have signed away ten times three favors for it, and he knew that, and knowing it didn't help, because the court counted on exactly that. That was the whole architecture of the place, he understood suddenly, sitting in the dark with his cold tea: they didn't take. They let you give. They waited until love or fear or desperation put the pen in your hand, and then they simply held you to what you'd written, forever, in ink that drank light. Every soul in that ledger had walked in on their own feet. That was the horror of it. That was how they slept at night, if they slept at all—they didn't take anything anyone hadn't already handed over.

And now they wanted him to hand over a person. Living, undamaged, unprayed-over.

*Unprayed-over.* He kept coming back to that phrase. Why would the court care who prayed over the merchandise? Unless prayer did something. Unless someone's prayers had been doing something for twelve years, unnoticed, accumulating like silt against a pilings—unless somebody up there had finally noticed that the branded one had a patron, or a protector, or a scar that twitched, and wanted the goods delivered before whatever answered those prayers woke all the way up.

Old gods stirring. He'd heard the talk, same as everyone. Weird lights on the peaks. Weather coming early and wrong. The priestess running from her order because a god had used her mouth mid-sermon like a puppeteer grabbing strings. The world was shifting the way a river shifts before it changes course, and the court beneath the mountain was moving fast to secure its assets before the current changed, and Tomas Bell, ferryman, debt-holder, father, was the asset-securing instrument of the week.

He laughed, alone in his hut, and it came out wrong, and he put his head in his hands.

* * *

On the second night he dreamed of Ilsa.

She was nine in the dream, fever-bright, and she was standing on the far bank of the river in a place that wasn't any place he knew, and the water between them was flat and silent and wrong, and she was saying something he couldn't hear. Her mouth moved. No sound crossed. He shouted to her—I'm coming, sweetheart, hold on, Papa's coming—and the river ate his voice the way it had eaten everything else, and she kept talking, patient, urgent, her little hands making shapes in the air, and he woke with his heart slamming and the taste of iron in his mouth and the absolute conviction, fading even as he grasped at it, that she had been trying to tell him something important.

Something about the water. Something about listening.

He got up in the dark and went down to the bank, because there was nothing else to do with a conviction like that except bring it to the river, the way you'd bring a question to an old teacher. The collectors were nowhere in sight—or everywhere, invisible, which amounted to the same thing. The moon was a thin nail-paring low in the west. Frost furred the rope rails of the ferry.

"Right," he said to the water, quietly, feeling foolish and past caring. "If you've got something to say, say it. I've carried your dead and your living both, thirty years, and I've never asked you for anything. So."

The river said nothing. Rivers generally didn't. But the current came back—that slow black muscle under the skin of the world—and it pushed against the hull of the moored ferry, gently, insistently, the way a dog pushes its nose against a hand, and then it swung the boat's head around, against the wind, against the logic of mooring lines, until the bow pointed upstream.

Upstream. Toward the high road. Toward the pass where the wool merchant's caravan would come down tomorrow with twelve souls and their beasts, including one branded mercenary who thought they were safe inside the valley's forgetting.

Tomas stood on the bank a long time, breath smoking, heart doing something complicated in his chest.

"That's not a warning," he said finally. "That's a delivery route. You're telling me where they'll be."

The current pushed the bow a little harder, patient, insistent, and then—this was the part he'd remember for the rest of his life, in the long years after, when the household of strays sat around fires and told the story—the current eased, and the river spoke. Not in words. In pressure, in movement, in the grammar of water: it swirled once around the ferry's anchor post, twice, and then flowed off downstream toward the boundary stones, and there it pooled, and eddied, and held, a slow turning knot of darker water in the moonlight, marking a place.

Marking the place where, three days ago, someone had knelt in the frost and prayed at the boundary stones, and something had answered.

Tomas Bell had lived on that river for thirty years and had never once believed it was anything but water. Standing there in the dark with his dead wife's daughter burning in his heart and three unblinking creditors on his land, he revised thirty years of certainty in about four seconds.

"All right," he said. "All right. But I'm telling you now—if this goes wrong, if they take them anyway, if Ilsa pays for my spine—you'd best be prepared to do something about it. Because I can't fight the mountain. Nobody can fight the mountain."

The river turned its slow knot of dark water, and said nothing, and somehow the nothing sounded like: *we'll see.*

* * *

The caravan came down off the high road at midmorning, strung out along the switchbacks like beads on a greasy cord, and Tomas watched it from the ridge above the crossing with a spyglass older than his marriage had been. Twelve souls. Wool merchant Vassek in his furred hood, riding fat and complaining. Six hired guards, bored, competent-looking. Pack animals. And at the rear, riding drag position with the easy slouch of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, wrapped in a road cloak with the hood back, a face he recognized from a dozen crossings: sharp-planed, watchful, tired around the eyes in a way that had nothing to do with the road.

Sebastian Reyes. The branded one. Living, currently undamaged, currently unprayed-over—at least as far as Tomas knew, though given the talk of prayers at the boundary stones, maybe not for long.

The collectors were gone from the ridge. That frightened him more than their presence had. Things that watch you are predictable. Things that stop watching you have moved somewhere better.

He went down and ran the crossing. It was ordinary work, blessedly ordinary: loading beasts, taking coin, cursing Vassek's lead mule with real feeling. Reyes helped with the loading, as Tomas had known they would, hauling on a rope in silence, economical, strong, and when the last animal was aboard and the pole was pushed off, the two of them stood eight feet apart on the deck of the ferry with the whole flat gray river sliding underneath them, and Tomas had perhaps ninety seconds of crossing in which to do the most dangerous thing he had ever done.

He couldn't say it. He'd rehearsed a hundred ways to say it and every one of them died in his throat, because the collectors might be anywhere, might be hearing through anything, and a spoken warning was a death sentence—for Reyes, for himself, for Ilsa beneath the mountain. Words were out. Ink was out. Everything that carried meaning legibly was out.

Which left the illegible. Which left the river.

"So," Tomas said, loud enough for the guards to hear, playing the garrulous ferryman, playing it hard. "Wool prices up or down this season, then? Vassek looks pleased with himself, and that man's never pleased unless he's robbed somebody."

"Priced up," Reyes said. Their voice was low and rough, a voice used to being listened through rather than to. Their eyes kept moving—shores, sky, the guards, the water. A professional's eyes. "Somebody's buying for the northern courts. War cloth, maybe."

"War cloth. Hm." Tomas hauled the pole, shifted it, hauled again. Under the deck plates, under the cargo, under the feet of twelve oblivious souls, the river pushed. He'd rigged nothing. He'd had nothing to rig. He'd simply asked, last night, with his forehead nearly touching the water, and the river had said *yes* in its wordless way, and now he was gambling twelve strangers' safety and one stranger's life on a current's promise. "Rough crossing today. Water's behaving strange."

"It's always strange here," Reyes said. "That's the point of the place."

"That it is." Tomas spat over the side, theatrical, and in the spitting bent close to the rail, and let his voice drop to almost nothing, a murmur pitched under the creak of the deck and the slap of water, shaped to be lost in it, shaped to be heard by one pair of ears only—a murmur with no words in it at all, only rhythm. Three knocks of the tongue against teeth. Pause. Three more. The rhythm of the collectors' boots on the frost gravel, the rhythm he'd heard them walking in step, the rhythm that had haunted two nights of dreams. *Someone is coming. Someone is coming.* Then, softer still, barely breath: "Boundary stones. Ask the water."

Reyes's eyes flicked to him. Just once. Sharp-planed face giving away nothing, professional eyes giving away less than nothing—but something moved behind them, some old wariness waking up, some instinct that had kept that body alive through whatever had carved those planes in the first place. They didn't nod. Didn't ask. Didn't do anything at all except shift their weight, casually, so that they stood facing the north shore instead of the south, facing the direction of the boundary stones, a small change of posture that meant everything.

The ferry ground against the south shore shingle. Loading out began, beasts and bundles, Vassek complaining, guards joking. Ordinary work. Tomas threw the rope to Reyes out of habit, and their hands met on the hemp for a moment, and Reyes's grip was brief and hard and deliberate, and their eyes said, in the only language left that the mountain couldn't read: *received. understood. unknown thanks.*

Then the caravan moved off up the south road, toward the valley town, toward the boundary stones, toward whatever was waiting there in good wool cloaks with folded-letter faces, and Tomas Bell stood on his empty ferry deck in the middle of his river and shook like a man with fever.

He'd chosen. Grain by grain, the channel had cut, and now the water was going somewhere else, and there was no unmaking it. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a girl with her mother's stubborn chin was keeping records or scrubbing floors or whatever they had her doing, and her fate was now tied to a mercenary's escape, and Tomas had traded his daughter's safety for a stranger's freedom without ever speaking a word aloud, and the worst part—the part that would keep him up nights for the rest of his life—was that it hadn't even been a hard choice. That was the thing the court had never understood about people like him, people who lived on rivers, who ferried the running and the fleeing and the desperate: some debts you pay, and some debts you *refuse to collect*, and the difference between those two things is the whole difference between a man and an instrument.

He poled back across. The river ran smooth and strong beneath him, carrying him, and he could have sworn—it was fancy, he knew it was fancy, but he could have sworn—the current leaned into the pole like a shoulder under a burden, helping.

Behind him, on the north bank, half-hidden in the reeds, something watched him go with eyes the color of the water, and did not blink, and was not a collector, and was gone before he turned.
