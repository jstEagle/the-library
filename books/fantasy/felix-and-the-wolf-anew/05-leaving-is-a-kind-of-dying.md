## 5. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

The boundary stones did not look like the edge of the world. They looked like what they were: seven leaning granite teeth in a frost-browned meadow, lichened gray, half-swallowed by gorse, each one carved with a spiral so old the wind had worn it to a suggestion. Children from Hollowmere came out here to dare each other to touch them. Goats grazed between them without ceremony.

Sebastian had spent twelve years pretending they were nothing at all.

They stood now at the edge of the meadow with their horse's reins wrapped twice around one fist and the morning cold working through every seam of their coat, and they let themselves look—really look—for the first time since the night they'd been carried through, feverish and fourteen and already sold.

Nothing moved. Nothing glowed. The stones were just stones, the way a door is just wood until you know what's behind it.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said behind them.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at something ordinary like it owes you money."

Sebastian didn't turn around. "It does owe me money."

Behind them, further back, Tomas was doing something elaborate and unnecessary with the ferry cart's axle, whistling a tune that kept losing its nerve and starting over. He'd been doing that since dawn. Since the plan had stopped being talk and started being distance—since the miles between here and the mountain court had begun counting themselves down whether anyone liked it or not.

Twelve years. Sebastian turned the number over the way you'd turn a stone in your hand, feeling for the soft underside. Twelve years inside the valley where magic went to die, where the Wolf's silence had been indistinguishable from absence, where a mercenary could be just a mercenary and a brand on the ribs could be just a scar. The valley had been a wound and a bandage both. They had hated it and needed it in exactly equal measure, which is its own kind of love, though no one tells you that.

And now the Wolf was awake. Now the dream-hunt had come, all teeth and green light and a voice like a landslide remembering how to speak, and it had named what was missing: *your soul, little wolf, sold before you could spend it.* Now there was a court beneath the mountain holding a ledger with Sebastian Reyes written in it somewhere, in ink older than grief, and solstice coming, and gates that would close.

Which meant leaving. Which meant this meadow. Which meant the stones.

"Odile." Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "Tell me again what it feels like. Crossing."

She came up beside them, small and sharp-eyed under her hood, her stolen priestess's hands folded like she was about to deliver a sermon she'd rather not. She'd crossed into the valley weeks ago, fleeing her order; she'd crossed out once since, briefly, to scout the road north, and had come back looking like someone who'd been shown the machinery behind the sky.

"Like being born," she said. "If being born hurt more and took longer. Everything comes back at once—the world's whole weight of it. Every prayer ever said near you, every god's attention you've ever brushed against, all of it arriving in the same heartbeat. Most people faint." A pause. Her eyes flicked to the brand hidden under Sebastian's shirt. "You're not most people. You're also carrying more than most people. So I'd sit down first, if I were you."

"I'm not sitting down."

"No," she said softly. "I don't suppose you are."

* * *

They made their preparations with the grim economy of people who have done hard things before. Tomas checked the cart's false bottom where the winter stores rode; Odile burned a pinch of something bitter-smelling in a tin dish and muttered over the smoke, not magic—not in the valley, there was no such thing as not-in-the-valley—but habit, the muscle-memory of faith, which apparently survived even where its object couldn't follow. Sebastian ate. It seemed important to eat. The body was going to be asked for something today, and it was rude to make demands of an empty vessel.

The ghost did not appear. Mateo never appeared when Sebastian wanted him to, only in the margins, only sideways—a cold spot by the fire, a door swinging when there was no draft, once, unbearably, a hummed fragment of a lullaby in a voice twelve years dead. Sebastian told themselves they weren't waiting for him. They waited anyway, scanning the treeline, the shadows under the gorse, the places where the light pooled wrong.

Nothing. Just the meadow, and the stones, and the long white shoulder of the mountain rising beyond the valley's rim like a verdict nobody had read aloud yet.

Tomas finished his fussing with the axle and came to stand at Sebastian's shoulder. Up close he smelled of river water and pipe smoke and fear sweated out overnight. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a ferryman's forearms and a debtor's eyes, and he had chosen—three days ago, in a lamplit kitchen, with collectors' footprints still in his snow—to warn instead of deliver. Sebastian hadn't thanked him. Thanking felt premature. Trusting felt premature. But he was here, loading a cart he might never bring home, and that counted for something in a currency Sebastian was only learning to count.

"My mother crossed here," Sebastian said. They hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, the way blood comes out of cloth.

Tomas went still. Even Odile's muttering stopped.

"That night," Sebastian said. "The bargain. She carried me through. I don't remember the crossing—I remember the fever, and the road, and her singing, and then I remember waking up in Hollowmere and the world being quiet in a way I couldn't name for years." They laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I thought the quiet was grief. Everyone said it was grief. Grieve your brother, grieve your father, give it time. Nobody said: no, child, that's the shape of a hole where your soul used to be. Nobody said the quiet was purchased."

"Sebastian—" Odile began.

"She sang," Sebastian said. "That's the part I keep. Not the argument I heard through the floor, not the stranger in our kitchen with the mountain's smell on him. She sang to me the whole way across, and her voice broke in the middle, and I thought—fourteen years old, burning up, I thought *Mama's sad too, we'll be sad together, it will be all right.*" They looked at the stones. "It wasn't grief. It was guilt. She knew exactly what she was buying with me, and she sang anyway, because the alternative was letting the town go unprotected, and she chose, and I have spent twelve years calling her choice my wound."

The meadow held the words the way the valley held everything: without echo, without judgment, without answer.

Tomas cleared his throat. When he spoke, his voice was rough as rope. "My daughter's down there," he said. "In the court. Has been six years. And every night I tell myself a story about why I haven't gone after her, and every morning the story's a little thinner, and I keep telling it anyway, because the alternative is—" He gestured at the stones, at the mountain, at all of it. "This. Standing at a door you can't unopen."

"Yes," Sebastian said.

"So." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand, a gesture suddenly boyish. "Do we open it?"

Sebastian looked at Odile. Odile looked at the sky, reading weather or gods or both. Then she nodded once, small, like a pin dropping.

"We open it," Sebastian said.

* * *

They left the cart. There was no arguing with the terrain beyond the line—Tomas had been eloquent and profane on the subject—and so they went light: bedrolls, food, the iron and rope of Sebastian's trade, Odile's satchel of relics she claimed were worthless and guarded like children. Tomas led a spare horse for the first stretch of road. After that, feet.

Sebastian walked to the stones alone.

Not because the others hung back out of courtesy—they didn't; Odile hovered at the meadow's edge like a crow deciding whether something was edible—but because some distances you have to cover by yourself or they don't count. That was a thing Mateo used to say, about rivers, about fences, about the gap between saying you'll do a thing and doing it. *Come on, Felix, it's three steps, jump it.* He'd been the brave one. Everyone always assumed it was Sebastian, all scars and swagger, but it had been Mateo who jumped first, Mateo who dared the deep water, Mateo who—

Mateo who died the night the bargain was made, and whose death Sebastian had carried like a stone in the chest for twelve years, and who was, if the Wolf and the chapel-cold and the humming were to be believed, not entirely done following them.

"Felix," Sebastian said to the stones, very quietly, using the name only the dead and the dreaming used anymore. "I'm jumping."

The spiral carving under their palm was cold. Colder than granite had any right to be in weak sun. Cold like the far side of a door.

They stepped through.

* * *

Later, they would try to describe it to Tomas, who had crossed a hundred times and swore it was nothing, just a shiver, just a change in the light—and they would fail, because there was no describing what it was to have a limb grow back in a single instant, to have a sense return that you hadn't known you lacked. What they said was: *it's like color to someone who's been colorblind.* What it actually was, was this:

The world arrived.

All of it, at once, the way a wave arrives—not water but weight, not sound but pressure, the entire humming machinery of creation slamming home into a body that had forgotten it was a body. Sebastian's knees hit frozen grass. Their vision went white, then gold, then split open into layers: the meadow as it was, and beneath it the meadow as it flowed, every root and worm and thread of frost threaded through with light, with current, with the vast slow breathing of things that had power in them. The stones roared—not audibly, but in the bones, in the teeth, in the fluid of the eyes. Twelve years of silence ended in a heartbeat and the silence had been damming up a river.

Magic flooded back. And with it—

With it, everything else.

Sebastian felt the Wolf first, because the Wolf was loudest: a presence vast and green and patient, suddenly *there*, the way a mountain is there, the way weather is there, an attention the size of winter turning toward them with something that was not quite words and not quite warmth but was unmistakably recognition. *There you are,* said the presence, or the memory of the presence, or the scar tissue where the presence lived. *There you are, little wolf.*

Then the rest. Prayers, old ones, soaked into their skin like smoke into wool—the desperate arithmetic of everyone who'd ever begged anything of anything near them. The residue of Odile's order, holy and terrible, clinging to her like burrs. The mountain itself, distant and enormous, aware. Aware of them. Turning its attention south and down and finding, with the mild satisfaction of a creditor consulting a ledger, exactly what it was looking for.

Because the mark was back.

Sebastian knew it before they saw it—felt it kindle along their ribs like a coal slipped under the skin, hot and precise and *addressed*, a signature written in a hand that was not theirs. They clawed their shirt up with shaking hands and looked down at their own body and found the brand no longer a brand.

It was a seal. A sigil in silver-black lines, intricate as frost on glass, spread across their ribs and sternum where the old scar had been: the mark of debt, of ownership, of a soul entered into a ledger beneath the mountain. And it *shone*. In the valley it had been ink; out here it was a lamp. Any tracker in the province with a drop of the sight, any hound bred on the court's coin, any priest with a scrying bowl would see it from miles off, a bonfire of belonging burning in the shape of a person.

"There you are," Odile whispered. She had dropped to her knees in the grass beside them, and her face was wet, and she was looking at Sebastian the way people look at cathedrals. "Oh. Oh, you're *loud*. You're—Sebastian, you're the loudest soul-touched thing I've ever stood next to, and I grew up in a house of bells."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No," she said honestly. "It's supposed to be true."

Tomas had backed away three full steps and was holding his horse's bridle like a railing. "Every tracker," he said. "You said the mark would show. You didn't say it would *shine*."

"It doesn't shine," Sebastian managed. Talking cost effort; their tongue was learning to move inside a skull full of ocean. "It's just visible. To the right eyes."

"How many right eyes are there?"

"All of them that matter."

* * *

They gave it an hour, because there was no choice. Sebastian sat against the boundary stone with the new-old roar of the world pouring through them and let the vertigo crest and break, crest and break, each wave a little smaller. Odile sat with them and talked—not about anything, about the weather, about the horse's opinion of gorse, a steady drizzle of ordinary words that gave the drowning something to hold. It was, Sebastian suspected, a skill from her order. Tending the overwhelmed. She did it without seeming to notice she was doing it, which was either humility or habit, and Sebastian decided not to ask which.

Grief came with the third wave.

It came because the magic brought memory with it, memory with the volume turned up, and suddenly Sebastian was fourteen again and whole-souled and standing in their mother's kitchen hearing the argument through the floorboards—*he's a child, he's my child*—and then the stranger's voice, smooth as ledger paper: *the valley asks a price, woman. Protection isn't charity. One soul, freely given, and the snow stays kind and the wolves stay wild and your town sees spring for a hundred years.*

Freely given. Sebastian's hands curled in the frozen grass. Freely given by whom? The mother had signed. The mother had sung. And the court had taken a child's soul as collateral and called it commerce, and the town had slept warm for twelve years on the interest, and every single person in Hollowmere who'd ever pressed a coin into young Sebastian's hand for escort work, who'd smiled and called them *our Felix*, had been spending a dead child's soul without knowing it.

Or knowing. The thought arrived like ice water. Or suspecting. Small towns know things. Small towns keep quiet.

"Don't," Odile said quietly.

"Don't what."

"Go down that road. The one where everyone's guilty. I've walked it. It goes in a circle and the scenery never changes." She pulled her hood back. Her hair was cropped short and uneven, self-cut, fugitive's hair. "Your mother made a monstrous choice. Probably the only choice she had. Both things are true, and neither one gets to erase the other, and you're allowed to hate it and mourn her in the same breath. I do it with my order every day."

"Do you?" Sebastian looked at her. "Hate and mourn?"

"Hourly." Her mouth twitched. "It's very efficient."

The laugh that got out of Sebastian surprised them both—cracked and wet and real. Above the meadow, clouds the color of pewter were sliding in off the mountain, and the air had begun to taste the way it tasted before the long winters: metallic, expectant, like the sky clearing its throat. Weeks early. Everyone said so. Winter was coming down like a verdict this year, and the courts were sealing their doors, and somewhere beneath the mountain a Queen was sharpening her ledgers for solstice.

Sebastian got up. The world tilted, righted, tilted less. They pulled their shirt down over the shining mark—which changed nothing, hid nothing, was like pulling a curtain over a window with the sun behind it—and looked north, where the road ran thin and white toward the dark mass of the mountain.

Freedom. This was what it tasted like. Cold air, returned magic, a soul-mark blazing on their ribs like a signal fire, and every power in the province now able to see exactly what they were and where they stood. The first breath of freedom, and it cost visibility, and visibility would cost blood.

Worth it. They made themselves think it plainly, the way you set a bone. Worth it, because on the other side of this was a ledger, and in that ledger was their name, and in the margin of that entry, if the Wolf was right, were other names—other souls sold alongside theirs, other children carried sleeping through boundary stones by singing mothers. They were not the only entry. That fact had rearranged the architecture of their grief, and it was not done rearranging it yet.

"Tomas," Sebastian called. "How far to the first waystation?"

He squinted up the road, chewing the inside of his cheek. "Two days, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't. Four if the weather holds and we're unlucky." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "There's a tollhouse at the ford. Court men staff it, this time of year. Collectors' road, north of the line."

"Collectors," Odile repeated. "As in the ones who visited your kitchen."

"As in exactly those ones." Tomas wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. His hand had gone, without his noticing, to the little wooden bird he wore on a cord around his neck—Ilsa's whittling, he'd said, the one thing she'd left him. "They'll have seen the mark by nightfall, if they've got anyone on the walls with the sight. Which they will."

Sebastian rolled their shoulders. Under the shirt, the seal pulsed like a second heart, patient and luminous, broadcasting their location to every ear tuned to hear it. Let it broadcast. Let them come. Every hunter drawn to the mark was a hunter not watching the mountain's back gate, and Sebastian had spent twelve years learning how to be overlooked, and those lessons were not nothing.

But the first test came sooner than any of them guessed.

They were barely a mile up the road, the boundary stones sinking behind them into the gray afternoon, when Odile stopped walking. She didn't say anything. She simply went rigid, head tilting, the way a deer goes still—not at a sound, but at the *absence* of one.

"What," Sebastian said.

"Behind us," she breathed. "On the road. Something's been following since the stones. Not men." Her hand found the relic-satchel. "Men leave tracks. This leaves *cold*."

Sebastian turned. The road behind them was empty—empty of everything except the falling light and the first fat, deliberate flakes of the early winter, drifting down out of the pewter sky.

And in the middle of the road, printed neat as punctuation in the fresh snow, three sets of paw prints that no wolf alive could have made, each pad the size of a dinner plate, glowing faintly green.

Heading the same direction they were.

"Well," said Tomas faintly. "Either that's death coming for us—"

"It's not death," Sebastian said. Their chest was tight in a way that had nothing to do with the mark. The Wolf's presence leaned against their back like a great warm wall, like a hand between the shoulders, and beneath the terror and the grief and the shining brand, something in them that had been silent for twelve years lifted its head and *howled*. "It's an escort. We've got an escort."

The paw prints led north. Toward the mountain. Toward the tollhouse at the ford, and whatever waited there with the sight and the court's coin in its pocket.

Sebastian pulled their collar up against the snow and followed their god into the gathering dark.
