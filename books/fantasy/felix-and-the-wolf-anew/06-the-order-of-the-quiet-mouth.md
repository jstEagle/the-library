## 6. The Order of the Quiet Mouth

Odile Vane had been running for nine days when she finally understood that her sisters were not chasing her.

They were herding her.

She realized it at dusk on the ninth day, standing in the lee of a granite outcrop while snow needled down through the pines, watching three figures in grey move along the ridgeline above her with the unhurried patience of women who knew exactly where she must go. They did not run. They did not call out. They simply walked the high ground, spaced like stitches in a seam, closing the passes one by one behind her.

Of course, she thought. Of course they would do it this way. The Order of the Quiet Mouth had never needed to shout. Silence was their sacrament; why would pursuit be any different? You did not hunt a stray thought. You narrowed the mind around it until it had nowhere left to be.

The valley lay below her, a long green-grey trough of dark pines and pale fields, and Odile felt its pull the way a drowning woman feels the surface. Everyone in the province knew the stories. The valley where magic died. The valley where prayers went unanswered and gods could not follow and the branded and the blessed alike came to be ordinary. She had preached against it once, from a pulpit of white stone, warning her congregation that the silence of that place was not peace but amputation — that a soul which cut itself off from the divine was a soul slowly bleeding out and calling the numbness health.

That had been before a god spoke through her throat in front of four hundred people.

She pressed her hand to her neck, where the words still lived. Not a scar exactly. More like a splinter of someone else's voice lodged under the skin. *Run,* it had said, in a voice like wind through a bone flute, using her mouth, her tongue, her breath, while Sister Casserly's face went white as tallow in the front pew. *Run north. He is waking.*

The Wolf. She had read about him in the forbidden folios, the ones chained shut in the undercroft: the god who ran ahead of the others, the first to sleep and the last to wake, whose name had been struck from every liturgy so thoroughly that even the order's oldest sisters no longer remembered what they were striking out. And now he was stirring, and the Quiet Mouth — whose entire holiness rested on the premise that gods slept best when no one spoke to them — wanted him lulled back down by any means necessary.

Including, if the whispers in the refectory had been true, the means of a knife.

*He is waking,* the splinter-voice said again, faintly, as it had said every day for nine days. *Run north.*

"I'm going," Odile muttered. "Stop nagging. You're supposed to be a god, not my mother."

Below her, somewhere in the darkening valley, was the mercenary. She had heard the stories in the last tavern that would serve her — the one with the brand, the one who'd taken work escorting wool carts through the dead valley for twelve years because nothing in there could touch them, no curse, no geas, no god. The townsfolk called them Felix, though whether that was a name or an irony nobody seemed to know. What mattered was simpler: the mercenary carried something under their skin that burned when divinity came near. Odile had felt it herself, two nights ago, praying alone in a birch grove near the boundary stones — a flare of heat in the dark like a banked coal suddenly breathed upon, a presence that flinched away from her words the way a horse flinches from a whip.

Someone in that valley was marked by the Wolf. Someone who had prayed to him, once, hard enough to leave a scar.

And gods waking needed mouths. That was the whole terrible architecture of it. A god could not walk into the world on his own legs anymore — the old roads were closed, the old names unspoken. He needed hands. He needed voices. He needed, perhaps, exactly the kind of desperate, excommunicated, heretic priestess currently freezing on a mountainside with three silent women closing the passes behind her.

"Fine," Odile told the dark. "But I'm negotiating."

* * *

She crossed the boundary stone at dawn, and the silence took her like cold water takes a swimmer.

It was not absence. That was the thing no sermon had ever gotten right. It was not the quiet of an empty room. It was the quiet of a room where someone has just stopped speaking — where the shape of the voice is still hanging in the air, where your ears strain after it, where you understand suddenly how much noise the world had been making all along. Odile stood ten paces inside the valley line and felt every prayer she had ever spoken go out like candles in a draft. The splinter-voice in her neck dimmed to an ember. The constant low hum of the divine — the thing she had heard since childhood, the thing the Order trained girls to hear and then taught them to fear — cut off as cleanly as a lute string snipped with scissors.

She sat down in the snow and wept, briefly and furiously, and hated herself for both.

Then she got up, because grief was a luxury and her sisters were patient, and walked down into the valley toward the town with the wool market, asking after a mercenary named Felix.

* * *

She found them repairing a fence.

Not fighting bandits, not brooding on a crag, not doing anything remotely befitting a figure of legend. Sebastian Reyes — and it was Reyes, she confirmed it later, the surname surfacing through the town gossip like a coin from a well — was crouched in a muddy field with a mallet and a bundle of hazel stakes, arguing amiably with a farmer about the proper depth for post-holes while sleet came down sideways. Up close, the legend was unimpressive: medium height, weathered brown skin gone grey-pale from twelve years of northern winters, hair cropped short and practical, hands scarred in the specific pattern of someone who had held weapons for a living but used tools more often. A brand showed at the collar of their shirt when they bent — a spiral, or maybe a paw print worn smooth by time, she couldn't tell.

They noticed her before she was ready. Of course they did. Mercenaries noticed things; it was the whole profession.

"You're not from here," they said, straightening, mallet resting easy against their shoulder. Not hostile. Just factual, the way you'd note the weather.

"Sister Odile Vane." She used the title deliberately, watched for the flinch. It came, small but real. Good. They knew what the orders were. "Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Mouth. Currently of nowhere."

"Felix," they said, which told her nothing, and then, after a pause that had a decision inside it: "Sebastian Reyes."

"Reyes." She let the word sit. "That's a southern name."

"That's a southern name," they agreed, and waited. They were good at waiting. She could see it in the way they stood, weight settled, eyes doing that mercenary arithmetic — exits, distances, the mallet as a weapon of last resort. Twelve years in a valley where nothing magical could reach you, and still they counted doors. People only did that when they believed, deep in the marrow, that something was coming for them eventually.

"I need passage," Odile said. "Out of the valley, north, toward the mountain. And I can't take the open roads, because—"

"—because there are three women in grey on the ridge behind you who've been walking a pattern for two days, and patterns like that mean a cordon, and cordons mean an order with money." Sebastian said it mildly, the way you'd recite a shopping list. "I saw them yesterday. I assumed they were lost pilgrims." Something moved behind their eyes — not quite a smile. "Pilgrims don't pace."

"So you'll help me."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You've been holding that mallet wrong for a conversation this long, which means part of you is already planning the route." Odile stepped closer, boots sinking in the churned mud, and made herself smile the smile she'd used on wealthy donors and frightened novices alike — warm, rueful, a little self-deprecating, utterly calculated. "Look. I know what I look like. I know what my order looks like. But I'm not here to drag you anywhere, Sebastian Reyes. I'm here because a god spoke through me nine days ago, and I think it was your god, and I think we're both in considerably more trouble than either of us has admitted yet."

The change was instant and total. The amiability didn't vanish so much as freeze — a pond locking over in a single night. The mallet came off the shoulder. The farmer, wisely, found urgent business at the far end of his field.

"Walk with me," Sebastian said quietly. "Away from the road."

* * *

They talked in the birch wood above the farm, where the sleet couldn't reach and the silence of the valley pressed close as a hand over the mouth. Odile told it all — the sermon, the voice, the splinter under her skin, the folios in the undercroft, the whispered council where Sister Casserly had said the word *lull* in a tone that meant *kill*. She watched Sebastian's face the whole time, looking for disbelief, and found instead a horrible, dawning recognition, like a man watching his own house burn in a stranger's story.

When she finished, Sebastian was very still.

"You felt me," they said at last. "At the boundary stones. Two nights ago. When you prayed."

"The brand burned. Or whatever it is you carry. Like a coal breathing."

"It wasn't a brand. It was—" They stopped. Their jaw worked. For a moment Odile saw twelve years of careful silence straining against a door that had already begun, inexorably, to open. "It doesn't matter what it was. What matters is you're telling me my god is waking up, and your sisters want to put him back to sleep with a knife, and somehow I'm standing in the middle of it."

"Yes."

"And you came to me because?"

"Because you're marked. Because a waking god needs hands and mouths, and yours are the closest ones to him. Because—" she hesitated, and then, because lying to this person suddenly felt like lying to a mirror, "—because I'm frightened, and you're the only other person in the world who knows what it sounds like when a god says your name."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead a crow complained and fell silent, embarrassed, as if even the birds respected the valley's rules.

"I don't want him," Sebastian said finally, and their voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "Whatever he wants from me. Whatever he thinks I owe him. I spent twelve years building a life where nothing owed me anything and I owed nothing back. Do you understand that? There are people in that town who think I'm dull. *Dull.* Do you know what a gift that is? To be dull? To be safe?" They laughed, and it was the worst sound Odile had heard in nine days. "And now you come walking out of the snow with a god in your throat telling me the bill has come due."

"I'm not telling you the bill has come due. I'm telling you the collectors exist." Odile crouched, slowly, bringing herself below their eye line — an old trick, a novice's trick, de-escalation posture, but also honest, because she was tired and her knees hurt. "Here's what I'm offering, and I'll offer it plainly because I suspect you've had enough of plain lies dressed as piety. I know the mountain roads. All of them. The pilgrim ways, the smugglers' cuts, the approaches to the court beneath the mountain that don't appear on any map the Queen's own cartographers admit to. I spent eleven years memorizing them because my order believed the paths to the divine should belong to the order alone." She shrugged. "Arrogance makes excellent maps."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange you get me out of my sisters' reach. Passage, protection, whatever arrangement you make with whoever carries us. I'm not asking you to fight them. I'm asking you to outwalk them. You've done it for twelve years. Teach me."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. Then they laughed again — differently this time, shorter, sharper, almost surprised.

"Teach you to run," they said. "A priestess. From her own church."

"Ex-priestess. Heretic. God-touched abomination, if you prefer the formal charge." Odile spread her hands. "I've been called worse in the last nine days than I was in the whole rest of my life. It's remarkably freeing. You should try it."

Something flickered across Sebastian's face — pain, quickly mastered, the ghost of a joke someone else used to make. Odile filed it away. There was history here, deep history, the kind that left craters.

"You lie well," Sebastian said at last. "I want that noted. You lie beautifully. The humble smile, the kneeling, the 'honest offer.' I watched you do it to that farmer before you ever saw me watching." They crouched too, until they were level with her, close enough that she could see the exhaustion under their eyes, the twelve years of vigilance worn into them like water wears stone. "So here's my counter-offer, priestess. We travel together. You share your maps. I share my paranoia. And neither of us believes a single word the other says until we've bled together at least once. Deal?"

It was, Odile reflected, the most honest contract anyone had ever offered her.

"Deal," she said.

They shook on it, mud and all, and if each of them kept a blade loose in their sleeve during the handshake, well — that was just good manners between liars.

* * *

They were halfway back down through the birches when Odile felt it: a coldness sliding up her spine that had nothing to do with the sleet. She stopped. Sebastian stopped a half-step later, reading her body before her face, and Odile understood in that instant that they would make a fine teacher.

"What," Sebastian said.

"My neck." She touched the splinter-place. It had woken — not burning, not speaking, but *straining*, the way a dog strains at a leash, the way a compass needle strains north. "It's pulling. North. Toward the mountain." She swallowed. "Sebastian. My sisters aren't herding me toward the valley anymore."

"They're not?"

"No." Odile turned, slowly, and looked back the way she had come — at the boundary stones, at the ridge, at the grey figures who were no longer pacing a cordon but standing in a row, still as fence posts, all three of them facing not toward her but toward the peaks beyond, where the mountain stood wrapped in its first true snows of the season. "They've stopped chasing me. Which means they've decided I'm not worth catching."

"Or," Sebastian said quietly, "they've decided you're leading them somewhere."

The splinter in Odile's neck pulled north, faithful as a heartbeat, and far away beneath the snows the mountain waited with its gates and its ledgers and its sleeping, waking god, and for the first time since the sermon Odile Vane felt less like a fugitive than like a key — carried, turned, used.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better get ahead of them."
