## 7. Winter Comes Down Like a Verdict

### Sebastian

The winter came down like a verdict three weeks before it had any right to.

Sebastian felt it first in the valley's teeth—the old ache in their jaw that meant the air was turning mean—and then in the birds, which left all at once, in one vast silent river of wings that passed over the escort road at dusk and did not stop for anything, not water, not rest, as though something behind the mountains had told them *run*. The farmers along the low fields brought in a harvest that wasn't ripe and didn't care. Green wheat, cut green, because the alternative was losing it under snow. Sebastian watched a woman weep over her own scythe like it was a knife she'd been forced to use on family.

"Early," said the carter, who was paying them to guard his grain.

"Winter doesn't come early," Sebastian said. "It comes when it's called."

They hadn't meant to say it aloud. The carter looked at them the way people had started looking at them lately—since the boundary stones, since the brand beneath their collar began to warm at odd hours like a banked coal—half fear, half hope, both of them worse than indifference.

Three days later the snow came, and it came wrong. Not the polite first snows of Sebastian's childhood, tentative as a knock at a door, but a wall of it, a white avalanche standing on its hind legs, and behind it the dark—not night, which ends, but the long dark, the weeks of dim blue noon and black afternoon that the old songs called *the wolf-months*, when courts sealed their doors and debts went dormant and anything owed had to be collected before the closing or wait until thaw.

Sebastian stood in the doorway of the carter's barn and watched the road disappear. Not drift across. *Disappear*—as if the world were being written over, line by line, in a hand too large to argue with.

Odile came to stand beside them, hood back, letting the cold bite her bare face like penance. "The gates," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been saying it for days, quietly, the way you'd repeat a diagnosis to make yourself believe it.

"The gates." Sebastian turned the word over. Beneath the mountain, the court of the Queen of the Long Winter kept its doors shut from solstice to thaw, and everything inside—every ledger, every soul held as collateral, every debt including the one with Sebastian's name inked into it—would sit sealed and untouchable until spring. And if they reached the court before solstice, they might have a chance. A thief's chance. A gambler's chance. If they missed it—

If they missed it, they waited out the wolf-months in some frozen town while every tracker in the province closed in on the soul-mark now blazing on Sebastian's skin, visible to anyone with the sight, since the day they'd crossed the boundary line and let magic flood back into them like water into a cracked hull.

"We can't outrun this weather," Tomas said from the barn's warmth, where he'd been pretending not to listen. He had Ilsa's letter folded into his coat pocket still, after all these months, soft as cloth from handling. "No one travels in the long dark. Even the collectors go home."

"Then we don't travel," Sebastian said. "We shelter. We wait for a break in it, and when it breaks, we move like the hells themselves are open behind us."

"And where do we shelter?" Odile asked. "Every inn between here and the mountain will have heard by now. A branded mercenary, an apostate priestess, a ferryman who owes the court favors. We're a ballad waiting for a tune."

Sebastian almost smiled. Almost. "There's a chapel," they said. "Half-drowned. On the old pilgrim road before the pass. My mother—" The name caught, as it always did, like a fishhook in the throat. "People used to say the flood took it. It only took the floor. The rest is above the waterline, and the water keeps it honest."

"What does that mean?" Tomas asked.

"It means no one lies well in a drowned church."

### Odile

The chapel had been dedicated to a god whose name even Odile's order had lost, which was, she thought as they waded through the knee-deep water of its nave, perhaps the most honest dedication of all.

Pillars rose out of the flooded floor like drowned men standing at attention, their capitals furred with white mineral bloom. The water was so clear it seemed ashamed of itself, and so cold it burned, and somewhere beneath it lay the original tiles, and beneath those, presumably, whatever bones the flood had been sent to collect. Light came down through a broken roof-slit in a single slanted column, and dust moved through it slowly, like ash that had forgotten which fire it belonged to.

"It's beautiful," she said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it. Beauty had been her order's first lesson and its first trap: *the gods speak through what is lovely, sister, therefore guard the lovely places.* As if divinity were a door that could be locked.

She set about making camp on the raised chancel, the one dry island in the drowned nave, hauling their gear up out of the water while Tomas saw to the boat—he'd insisted on buying a leaky skiff from a dying village two days back, and had spent the whole journey bailing it with a hat and swearing at it with real affection. Sebastian stood in the shallows, motionless, looking at the far wall.

Odile followed their gaze. Above the waterline, ringed in mineral stain like the iris of an enormous eye, was a carved frieze: figures processing toward a mountain, and above the mountain, worn nearly smooth by centuries of damp, the shape of a wolf.

Not a wolf as her order carved wolves—snarling, muzzled, chained in stone as a warning. This one sat. Its head was lifted. It was listening to something the sculptor had not thought to include, or had thought no one would need to see.

"You knew this place," she said.

"I grew up forty miles from it." Sebastian's voice had gone flat, the way it did when feeling pressed against the underside of their words like a hand against a drumskin. "Pilgrims used to come here to ask for silence. Real silence. The valley kind. They'd wade in and pray until the water went cold enough that they stopped being able to tell their heartbeat from the drip off the roof." A pause. "My mother brought me once. I don't remember what she prayed for."

Odile had learned, in the months since the sermon that broke her life open, to hear the thing underneath a person's words the way her sisters had taught her to hear the thing underneath a god's. Underneath Sebastian's flatness was grief. Underneath the grief was anger. Underneath the anger, deeper than all of it, was a question asked so many times it had gone hoarse: *why me, why sold, why her.*

She did not say any of this. She built the fire instead, small and disciplined on the stone altar—they had argued about that, the first night, and Sebastian had won by pointing out that the god this altar served had no name left to offend—and she made tea, and she prayed.

She prayed the way she'd prayed since the day the Wolf spoke through her mid-sermon: badly, briefly, without ceremony. *If you're there. If you're listening. They're tired, and scared, and won't say so. Do something.*

The flame bent sideways. There was no wind.

And the water in the nave went still—all of it, at once, every ripple flattening as if a great palm had smoothed it—and in the column of light the dust stopped moving, hung suspended like stars, and the cold changed quality. Not colder. *Attentive.*

Odile had felt gods before. Her whole life had been spent among gods the way a kennel-keeper spends his life among dogs: fed, managed, muzzled, useful. She had felt their attention as pressure, as heat, as the itch of being *handled*.

This was not that. This was the feeling of being *seen*—thoroughly, patiently, the way winter sees a field, holding nothing back and demanding nothing either.

Then Sebastian made a sound like a man taking a blade out of his own body, and dropped to one knee in the freezing water, and the brand on their neck lit through their collar like a coal breathed upon.

"Oh," Odile whispered, into the silence that followed, which was not empty at all. "Oh, you're actually here."

### Tomas

Tomas Bell had spent fifty-one years learning exactly one skill: knowing when to be elsewhere. He'd dodged press-gangs, tax collectors, two wives' funerals, and every favor the mountain court had ever hinted at, by the simple method of being on the water when trouble came ashore. It was a good skill. It had kept him alive and it had cost him his daughter, and he was too old now to pretend those two facts weren't the same fact.

So when the god arrived in the drowned chapel, Tomas did what he always did with things bigger than him.

He picked up the bail-hat and started working on the boat.

It was stupid. The boat was already dry; he'd bailed it twice that evening. But his hands wanted employment, and the skiff was the only thing in the room that couldn't look back at him. Around him the water lay flat as hammered tin, and the light stood straight as a pillar in a temple, and the mercenary was on their knees in the shallows with their eyes rolled white, and the priestess had gone rigid as a struck bell, and the cold coming off the flooded nave had begun to taste of pine and blood and high places.

Tomas bailed. His hat scraped the boards. *Scrape, scrape, scrape,* the sound of a man minding his own business at the end of the world.

Then the voice came, and even the boat couldn't protect him from that.

It did not come through the ears. That was the horror of it, and the mercy—it bypassed the ears entirely and spoke somewhere behind the breastbone, in the place where a man keeps the names of his dead. It was not loud. It was not deep. It sounded, Tomas thought wildly, like a father reading aloud in the next room: unhurried, a little rough, patient past all reason.

**I have been silent a long time,** it said. **Silence was my covenant. Your valley bought it. Now the covenant is spent, and I have debts of my own to collect, and little time to collect them in.**

The fire on the altar leaned hard sideways, though there was no wind, and righted itself.

**Listen, then, all three of you, because I will not say it twice, and I will not say it gently.**

Tomas put down the hat. There was no dignity left in pretending.

**The gates of the court beneath the mountain close at solstice. Not at dusk, not at the turning of the year—at the exact hour the longest night begins. When they close, they close on everything inside: the ledgers, the souls, the debts. Nothing moves in or out until thaw. Nothing *is*—in or out—until thaw. The Queen built her court to outlast gods, and it will outlast your courage, your strength, and your lives, if you let the doors shut with what you want still inside.**

In the shallows, Sebastian lifted their head. Their eyes had cleared, and they were wet-faced, and they were glaring upward at nothing with an expression Tomas recognized from ferry landings: the look of a passenger arguing with the river.

"Then we go now," Sebastian said. "Tonight. Through *that*—" they flung a hand at the broken roof, the roaring dark beyond it, the snow falling thick as feathers from a slaughtered sky.

**You cannot.** For the first time the voice roughened, and Tomas heard, unmistakably, grief in it. **The wolf-months are mine as much as the Queen's. In the long dark I am strong—but the passes are death, and the trackers hunting your mark do not sleep, and if you die in the snow, everything dies with you: your soul, the others bound beside it, the town your mother sold. No. You shelter. You endure. You let the winter erase the roads behind you and the trackers with them, and you move the moment it breaks—and it will break, once, briefly, near the turn of the month. A false thaw. A liar's spring. It will last two days, maybe three.**

"Two days," Odile whispered. "To cross the high country and reach the gates before solstice."

**To reach the gates before they close.** A pause, and when the voice resumed it was quieter, and somehow that was worse. **I will not lie to you, little thief. The odds are a knife's width wide. Some of you will not come home. Perhaps none of you will. I could carry you—I have the strength, in these months, to carry a legion—but the moment I shield you openly, every power that hunts the waking divine will know precisely where you are, and the Queen's collectors will stop hunting and start *waiting*, at the gates, in numbers you cannot imagine. So I give you what I gave your valley for twelve years: silence. My silence. Walk in it. Hide in it. And when the hour comes—when you stand in that glittering hell and the ledger is read aloud—I will be there. Not beside you. *Behind* you. Closer than your own pulse.**

The light in the roof-slit trembled. The dust-stars shivered.

**One more thing.** The voice gentled further, and turned, and Tomas felt it turn like weather turning, and settle—not on him, thank every drowned god, not on him. **There is one who travels with you whom you have not counted. He has been counting you. Ask him why he stayed.**

And the cold lifted, all at once, like a hand withdrawn, and the water in the nave remembered how to ripple, and the fire stood up straight, and the ordinary dark came flooding back in, huge and blind and safe.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Tomas sat down slowly on the gunwale of his beloved leaky skiff and put his face in his hands. "I've ferried smugglers," he said, to no one. "Deserters. A plague-doctor. Once, a bride running from her own wedding with the dowry in a flour sack. In thirty years on the water I never once carried a *god's errand*."

"You carried us," Odile said. "Across the line. That was the errand. Everything else is just the fare."

"Fare." Tomas laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "You know what the fare is? The second favor. Still sitting there in the court's book with my name on it. They haven't called it yet, but they will, and when they do—" He stopped. Looked at his hands, which had rowed honestly for fifty years and were trembling now. "When they call it, one of you may have to decide what kind of man I am. I'd rather not know myself."

Sebastian rose out of the shallows, streaming, shivering, alive. They looked older than the winter. They looked, Tomas thought, like someone who had just been handed a verdict and found, buried in the fine print, a single clause of mercy.

"Then we'll decide together," Sebastian said. "That's the whole trick of it. Nobody decides alone anymore. That's how my mother damned a town."

### Odile

Later, when Tomas slept by the skiff and Sebastian kept watch at the broken eastern wall, staring north toward the mountain with the brand glowing faintly at their throat like a second pulse, Odile waded out alone into the middle of the nave.

The water reached her thighs. She didn't care. She had knelt in worse—in incense smoke thick enough to drown in, in the Order of the Quiet Mouth's marble halls where the floors were polished with prayer and the prayers were polished with fear. At least here the drowning was literal. Honest architecture.

She cupped her hands, lifted the chapel water, and let it run through her fingers.

"He said *ask him why he stayed*," she murmured. "Wolf. Patron. Whatever you are to them." The words fogged white in the cold. "You know I'm not asking for myself. I burned my vows. I don't get to petition anyone anymore—that was the price, and I paid it gladly, and I'd pay it again. I'm asking for *them*. Sebastian. They've got a ghost riding their shoulder and a soul in a vault and a mother's sin around their neck like a millstone, and they will walk into that mountain and trade themselves away the moment they think no one's watching, because that's who they are. So watch closer than their pulse. Hear me?"

The water dripped. Somewhere in the walls, ice ticked and settled.

And then—small, so small she might have dreamed it—a sound outside, at the edge of the snowfield beyond the broken wall. Not a wolf's cry. Nothing so simple. Just the faint crunch of footsteps in fresh snow, pacing the chapel's perimeter, patient as a sentry, keeping a distance that was almost respectful.

Odile went very still.

Because the party had made no tracks coming in—the snow had seen to that, erasing the road behind them within the hour. Because nothing living should be walking that perimeter in the teeth of the long dark. And because, when she strained her senses the way her order had trained her, straining toward whatever walked out there, she felt nothing at all.

No warmth. No breath. No soul.

Only a presence like a held breath, and a patience like a vigil, and—faintly, unbearably—the impression of someone standing at a grave they cannot leave, watching through a window at the last person they love.

"Sebastian," Odile called softly, across the drowned nave, not turning from the wall. "Your brother's outside again."

At the eastern wall, the watchful silhouette went rigid.

And out in the snow, the footsteps stopped—right at the edge of the firelight, close enough now that the dark took on a shape, a young man's shape, shoulders hunched against a cold he could no longer feel—and waited, as he had waited for twelve years, to be invited in.
