## 8. The Brother Who Stayed

The drowned chapel had no roof worth the name. Snow came through the broken vault in long slow spirals, settling on the altar like a shroud laid by careful hands, and where the flood had reached years ago it had left a tidemark of silt across the walls at shoulder height, so that everything holy in the room seemed to stand waist-deep in a sea that had gone away and forgotten to come back.

Sebastian sat on the steps of the altar because it was the only dry stone left, and fed twigs to a fire small enough to hide behind a shield. Outside, the winter ground on like a verdict being read clause by clause. Inside, the Wolf's presence was a warmth along their spine, a great weight of attention that did not need words. It had spoken plainly enough three nights ago—*reach the court before solstice*—and since then it had simply stayed, close as a second heartbeat, watching the dark with them.

Odile slept against the far wall with her prayer-beads still wound through her fingers, as if even unconscious she expected someone to demand proof of her faith. Tomas was out in the snow checking the sledge runners for the fourth time, though there was nothing left to check; he needed his hands busy or his guilt got loud. And Sebastian kept watch, because keeping watch was the one prayer they still knew how to say.

That was when they saw him.

He stood in the doorway where the door had been, framed in falling snow, and the snow did not land on him. It fell past him, through him, each flake continuing its patient descent as though the shape of a boy were only a rumor the air had agreed to entertain. He was fourteen years old. He would be fourteen years old forever. He wore the coat their mother had made him the autumn before she sold them both, brown wool gone gray at the elbows, and he had his hands pushed deep in its pockets the way he always had, shoulders hunched against cold that could not touch him anymore.

"Mateo," Sebastian said. The name came out of them like something coughed up from a wound—twelve years of not saying it arriving all at once, too big for the throat.

"Hi, Seb." His voice was exactly as they remembered it and that was the cruelest thing, that grief had worn smooth every other memory of that night but had left his voice untouched, bright and ordinary, a boy's voice complaining about chores. "You look terrible."

"You're dead," Sebastian said, because of all the things waiting to be said, that one seemed load-bearing. Everything else would have to be built on top of it.

"Yeah." He looked down at his feet, at the snowflakes passing through his boots. "Yeah, I am. Sorry about that. I know you've had—" He gestured vaguely at Sebastian, at the brand hidden under their collar, at the whole ruined architecture of their life. "I know you've had a lot going on."

Sebastian stood up too fast. The fire spat. Somewhere behind them Odile stirred but did not wake, and some distant part of Sebastian noted that the Wolf had gone very still—not alarmed, but attentive, the way it attended to anything that touched the debt.

"You've been *here*?" Their voice cracked down the middle. "All this time? Twelve years, Mateo, I—I burned offerings for you. I said your name into the valley silence every year on the anniversary because it was the only place sound went anywhere. I thought—" They had thought he was gone. Truly gone, past even gods, past even regret. They had built their whole exile on top of that grave.

"I couldn't cross the boundary stones," Mateo said. "Not properly. I'd sort of... pool, near them. Like water finding a low place. You'd walk by on escort jobs and I'd try to say something and it just—" He made a sound like wind through a keyhole. "You never heard me. Once you stopped, right at the north stone, and looked straight at me, and asked your client if she smelled smoke. That was a bad day."

"That was the fourteenth of Thawmonth," Sebastian said. "You were wearing the blue shirt."

Mateo's face did something complicated. "You remember the shirt."

"I remember everything about that night except the part that matters." Sebastian crossed the chapel in five strides and then stopped, because there was no way to do this, no etiquette for embracing a ghost, no way to know if their arms would pass through him and close on cold air and make this worse instead of better. "Why now? Why can I see you now?"

"Because you left the valley." He said it gently, the way you tell someone a diagnosis. "The debt-mark is on your skin again, out here. I'm bound to the debt, Seb. Wherever the ledger's claim reaches, I reach. In the valley the claim went quiet, so I went quiet. Out here—" He spread his hands. Snow fell through his palms. "Out here I'm loud as a bell."

*Bound to the debt.* Sebastian felt the words settle into them like stones dropped into a well, one after another after another. Their mother had sold their soul to buy the valley's protection. The Wolf had told them that much, three nights ago, in the dream-hunt: *your mother paid with what was yours.* But debts were not single threads. A debt was a net. And a boy who died the same night the bargain was struck—

"The bargain killed you," Sebastian said. Not a question. They had always known it, in the wordless way you know the shape of a thing you refuse to look at. "Whatever she called down that night, whatever came to collect—it took you instead. Or besides. Or—"

"It came for both of us," Mateo said. "The collectors. Two of them, dressed like respectable men, with a ledger between them like a table. Mama signed, and signing meant delivery, and delivery meant—" He shrugged, a boy's shrug, helpless. "They couldn't take my soul because mine wasn't the collateral. Yours was. So they took the rest of me. The part that isn't filed anywhere. And the leftover of me stuck to the debt like burrs to a blanket. I've been following the collateral ever since."

Twelve years. Sebastian put a hand against the altar to keep the floor from tilting. Twelve years of walking through the valley feeling watched on bad nights and telling themselves it was the god, it was paranoia, it was the brand aching. Every anniversary offering spoken aloud into the silence—and him right there, pooling against the boundary stone, trying to answer.

"What did you try to say?" they asked. "At the stones. All those times."

Mateo considered. "Mostly *don't take that job*, honestly. The one with the bandits, the year before last—you got the scar from that one. I screamed myself hoarse and ghosts don't even get hoarse, which tells you something about how the afterlife works." He tried a grin. It wobbled. "And once I said happy birthday. You were twenty-five. You bought yourself a meat pie and ate it sitting on the boundary stone like a gravestone, which, rude, by the way, that's basically my headstone."

A laugh tore out of Sebastian, sudden and awful, half sob. They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "You were *there*. You've been there the whole—"

"Every step," Mateo said softly. "Every single one. You were never alone, Seb. You just couldn't hear me. Which, granted, is most of being alone."

Outside, Tomas's footsteps crunched past the doorway and away again, a man giving privacy to a conversation he could not see. The snow thickened. The fire leaned and recovered.

"Why are you telling me now?" Sebastian lowered their hand. "What changed?"

"The ledger closes at solstice." Mateo said it the way Odile might recite a psalm—something learned, heavy, true. "When it closes, everything in it gets collected. Called in. Filed. And I'm attached to your entry, so when they file you—" He looked at his own translucent hands. "I don't know what happens to leftovers. Maybe nothing. Maybe that's the problem. Maybe I stop being even this much." He lifted his eyes, and for a moment the boyishness fell away and Sebastian saw how old he was underneath, how twelve years of watching without being seen had aged something in him that had no business aging. "But mostly I'm telling you now because you finally left. Because you're actually going to do it. Go to the court. And I wanted—" His voice broke, reformed, broke again. "I wanted to say it while you could hear me. I'm not angry, Seb. I was never angry. You were sixteen. She signed *your* name and you didn't even know what a soul-ledger was. There's nothing to forgive. I checked. For twelve years I checked."

Sebastian crossed the last distance and embraced their brother, and their arms did not pass through him. He was cold the way river stones are cold, cold the way morning is cold, and solid enough, and real enough, and when he wrapped his arms around them in return the pressure was slight as a hand laid on a sleeping person—but it was pressure. It was there.

"I missed you," Sebastian said into the space where his shoulder should have had warmth. "Every day. Even the days I didn't let myself think about you. Especially those."

"I know," Mateo said. "I watched you not think about me. You're very loud when you're not thinking about something."

The laugh-sob again. They held on until holding on became letting go, and stepped back, and wiped their face with a sleeve already stiff with old weather.

Behind them, soft as snowfall, Odile spoke from the wall where she sat. Her eyes were open. How long they had been open, Sebastian could not say.

"The debt-bound dead," she said quietly. "There's a rite for them. In my order we called it *unbinding the mouth*—a soul that's been silenced gets named aloud by someone who loved it, in front of witnesses, and the naming goes into the record. It doesn't free them. But it makes them *counted*." She rose, beads whispering. "Counted souls can contest. At the closing ceremony, when every debt is read—if his name is in the record, he has standing. He can speak. He can even—" She hesitated, glancing at Mateo with an expression that held awe and fear in equal measure. "He can stand surety. Or witness one who does."

Mateo went very still. Snow fell through him, patient, unhurried, and for the first time since he'd appeared in the doorway, he looked less like a memory and more like a man being handed a weapon.

"Say my name, then," he said to Sebastian, and his voice shook, and it was the bravest shaking Sebastian had ever heard. "Say it loud. Let's give the Queen below something to read."
