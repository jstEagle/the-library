## 9. Roads That Owe Us Nothing

The collectors came at dusk, the way debts always do.

Tomas Bell was mending a net when they stepped out of the tree line—not walking so much as arriving, the way cold arrives, the way regret arrives. Three of them. They wore the gray of the mountain court, which was not really a color but an absence, wool that seemed to drink the light around it. Their faces were smooth and patient as river stones, and each of them wore a small brass token on a cord around the neck: a circle with a line through it. The mark of a favor owed.

Tomas's hands went still on the netting. His heart did not race. He had known this moment was coming for eleven years, since the night he'd signed his name three times in the Queen's ledger for a boat that didn't leak and a daughter who would eat. He'd spent eleven years telling himself that favors like these slept forever, that the court beneath the mountain collected interest only in stories told to frighten ferrymen's children.

The tallest collector stopped at the edge of Tomas's dooryard and did not cross it. There were rules even for them, apparently, or perhaps they simply enjoyed making men come to them.

"Tomas Bell," the tall one said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It sounded like a man you'd trust with your purse.

"I know why you're here."

"Of course you do." The collector smiled without moving its mouth. "You've been expecting us. Guilt is a kind of preparation, isn't it? You've rehearsed this conversation every night since the branded one crossed your water. We only came to collect what you already owe."

* * *

They sat at his table because he asked them to, because refusing felt more dangerous than hosting them. He put out bread and salted fish because his mother had raised him to feed guests, even guests with brass tokens and light-drinking wool, and because his hands needed something to do or they would shake where the collectors could see.

The tall one—its name, if it had one, was never offered—took a piece of bread, held it, and set it down again untouched. A courtesy performed backward.

"The first favor," it said, "was the warning. You gave it. Sloppily, but a warning given is a warning given, and the court honors its ledgers." It folded its smooth hands. "We are here for the second."

Tomas poured himself a cup of cider he did not want. "Name it."

"The branded one. Sebastian Reyes. And the priestess who travels with them, and the ghost that follows them like a dog that won't be called home. You will take them across the river at the shallows below Widow's Bend—the crossing they're already planning, we know their plans the way you know your daughter's handwriting—and there you will see to it that they do not survive the water."

The words landed in the room and just stayed there, filling it like smoke.

"You want me to drown them," Tomas said.

"We want you to fail to save them. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of your soul's worth, so attend to it. Rivers are honest things. People drown in rivers all the time, especially in the long winter, especially people carrying secrets and gods. No one will look at a ferryman whose passengers slipped under. Grief makes everyone nearsighted." The collector leaned forward, and up close its eyes were the color of old ice, blue gone gray gone green. "Or you may forfeit. Forfeit the second favor, and the third comes due immediately. And the third favor, Tomas Bell, is Ilsa."

The name hit him like a pole driven into riverbed silt—everything above it swaying, everything below it holding.

Ilsa. Sixteen years old when she'd taken service in the court, because the wages were impossible and the food was real and her father's debts had made her practical far too young. Four years now since he'd seen her face except in dreams, and in the dreams she was always standing in a doorway made of lamplight, and she was always about to say something he never got to hear.

"She serves willingly," the collector said, watching him. It had the tone of a man offering comfort and enjoying the fact that none was possible. "She keeps records. She is good at it. The Queen values her. But valued things can be revalued. Debts can be transferred. There are deeper floors beneath our floor, ferryman, and work down there that no one volunteers for twice."

Tomas looked at his own hands. Big hands, scarred, rope-burned, hands that had pulled forty strangers out of cold water over thirty years on the river and buried nine who'd been past pulling. Hands his daughter used to hold up to the light and call *oars*, when she was small enough to think her father could row anywhere.

"If I refuse outright," he said slowly, "you take her tonight."

"If you refuse outright, the ledger closes the account and opens another. Yes."

"And if I do what you ask—if I drown three travelers under my own crossing—you'll leave her be. Until the third favor comes due. Which it will."

"Which it will," the collector agreed pleasantly. "But that is a problem for a future Tomas Bell, and future men are famously optimistic." It rose, and the other two rose with it, and the room got lighter by degrees as they moved toward the door, as though they carried the dusk away with them in their pockets. At the threshold the tall one paused.

"A word of professional advice, since you've been courteous. Do it badly or don't do it at all—but know that we will know the difference. We have watched rivers a long time. We know what drowning looks like, and we know what pretending looks like, and only one of them fills a ledger." The door opened onto blue snow-light. "Solstice comes early this year. So does judgment. Good evening, ferryman."

Then they were gone, and the net lay half-mended on the table between the bread and the untouched cup, and Tomas Bell sat alone in his house listening to the river talk to itself in the dark, saying nothing useful at all.

* * *

He did not sleep. He walked down to the water instead, because the water was where his thinking happened, had been since he was a boy poling his father's barge through fog. The river ran black under a skin of new ice at the edges, talking its endless low talk, and Tomas stood on the bank and let it say all the things he couldn't.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as stones in a ford:

Three travelers. One of them branded, sold, hunted by a god's return; one of them a runaway priestess with a voice that cracked open heaven; one of them dead already and following anyway. Strangers. Kind ones, mostly—the priestess had mended his coat sleeve without being asked, sewing stitches so small they were nearly invisible, and the branded one had paid for passage with coin that was probably stolen but had said *thank you* like it cost them something, like gratitude was a muscle gone stiff from disuse.

And on the other side of the scale: Ilsa. Not a stranger. Not kind, exactly—Ilsa had inherited her mother's sharpness along with her mother's mouth—but *his*. The only thing left of a marriage the river hadn't taken and a life he'd mostly failed at. She'd gone down the mountain with her chin up and her bag packed herself, sixteen years old, saying, *Somebody in this family has to be solvent, Da.* He could still hear it. He heard it most nights.

Love against duty. Everyone in the valley sang songs about that choice, and in every song love won, because songs are written by people who have never stood at the actual scale.

But here was the thing the songs left out: the collectors had told him to do it badly or not at all, and they'd told him they'd know the difference, and they were right, they would, they knew rivers better than any man alive. But they did not know Tomas Bell. They knew his ledger entry. They knew his debt. They did not know that he had spent thirty years learning every mood of that water, every lie it told, every place it kept its strength hidden—and that a man who knows a river that well can make it do almost anything.

Almost anything.

Except, perhaps, the thing he was about to ask of it.

* * *

He found them at dawn, camped in the lee of a fallen oak two miles short of Widow's Bend, and he saw at once that they'd been expecting trouble—just not his kind. Sebastian sat awake, back to the trunk, sword across their knees, watching the treeline with the flat patience of a person who has been hunted before and expects to be hunted again. Odile slept sitting up, prayer beads wound through her fingers like a rosary of knots. The ghost—Mateo, they called him, though only Sebastian seemed able to hear him—stood a little apart, translucent as breath on glass, looking east toward the mountain with an expression Tomas was grateful he couldn't read.

"Ferryman," Sebastian said, without surprise. "Change of heart? Or change of price?"

"Neither." Tomas crouched by the cold fire ring, warming his hands over ash out of habit. "There were collectors at my house last night. Mountain court. Gray wool, brass tokens."

Sebastian went very still. Even the ghost turned.

"They bought my first favor off me months ago—your warning, the night you crossed. Last night they called the second." He made himself say it plainly, because these people had been lied to enough by everyone else in the world. "They want you drowned at Widow's Bend. All three of you. And if I refuse, they take my daughter down under the mountain and put her somewhere the word *willingly* doesn't reach."

Silence. The river talked faintly in the distance. Odile had woken without seeming to wake, her eyes open, her hand resting on her beads.

"So," Sebastian said at last. Their voice was level. Their eyes were not; there was something moving behind them, some old machinery grinding through new gears. "Why are you telling us?"

"Because I'm going to do it badly," Tomas said. "On purpose. And badly done, done right, might still keep everyone breathing—including Ilsa, if the river cooperates and the collectors are half as clever as they think and half as stupid as I need them to be." He looked at each of them in turn: the mercenary, the priestess, the ghost. "It's a bad plan. I've had two days' less time than they think, and no good options, and I'm asking three people I barely know to trust a drowning."

Odile spoke first, softly. "What did the collectors tell you about doing it badly?"

"That they'd know the difference." Tomas smiled, and it felt like a crack running through old wood. "They will. That's the point. I need them to know."

* * *

Widow's Bend at midday, under a sky the color of dishwater, was exactly as ugly as its name promised. The river narrowed there and quickened, sliding between shelves of black rock, and the crossing was a ferryman's trick and no mistake—a hard pull upstream against the current's shoulder, then a swing wide, then a drop into the slack water below the bend where the boat could come alongside the far bank. Done well, it looked easy. Done badly, it looked like murder.

Tomas loaded them aboard with the flat efficiency of a man doing his job, and if his hands were rougher than usual, well, winter made every ferryman rough. Sebastian watched him the whole time with those unreadable eyes, and Mateo drifted at the stern like a figurehead carved from fog, and Odile murmured something under her breath that might have been a prayer and might have been a curse, and Tomas pushed off into the current with his heart going like a drum in a procession.

Halfway across, at the exact place they had agreed on, he began to kill them.

That was how it had to look, anyway. He shipped the stroke oar wrong—visibly wrong, a novice's error, the blade catching flat—and the boat slewed. He shouted, cursed, fought the oar as though it were alive and winning. The current took the bow and swung it toward the fast water below the bend, and Odile screamed, a real scream because he'd asked her to scream and she was better at it than he'd expected, and Sebastian grabbed the gunwale with a soldier's grip and played terror with a straight face while their eyes bored into Tomas's like drills.

And then the boat struck the shelf rock, and everything became true at once.

He had planned for the strike—he had chosen the rock, measured the angle a hundred times in his head—but planning and cold black water are different countries, and the river did not care about anyone's plan. The hull cracked along a seam he'd sworn was sound. Water came in like an opinion nobody asked for. The current grabbed the wreck and rolled it, and suddenly Odile was under, and Sebastian was under, and Tomas went in after them because thirty years of habit does not consult the ledger.

Cold like a fist. Cold like a verdict. He found Odile by her beads, of all things, the little wooden spheres snagged on his sleeve, and hauled her up into air and shoved her at the slack-water eddy where the bank shelved gentle. Found Sebastian next, tangled in the swamped boat's painter, and cut them free with the knife he kept strapped inside his wrist, and drove them toward the eddy with a shoulder in their back. The whole time some cold clerk in the back of his skull was taking notes: *visible struggle, genuine peril, plausible failure, plausible rescue attempt.* Let the collectors watch. Let them write it all down. Let them see a ferryman fail honestly.

He dragged himself out last, coughing river, and collapsed on the shingle beside the others. For a moment the four of them—five, counting the ghost, who stood wringing water from a sleeve that couldn't hold any—lay gasping like landed fish, alive, soaked, half-frozen, and utterly convincing.

Then Sebastian started to laugh. It was a terrible sound, rusty, like a gate opening after years, and it shook them until they had to press their face against the wet stones.

"What," Tomas managed, "is funny."

"He said—" Sebastian wheezed, gesturing vaguely at the ghost, at the sky, at the whole ruined arrangement of the world—"he says you faked that *badly*."

"I told you I would."

"No." Sebastian lifted their head, water streaming from their hair, and grinned at him with something wild underneath it. "Badly means *convincingly*. Badly means the collectors believe it. What you did was so bad it was *good*, you mad old man—you sank your own boat. Your boat is at the bottom of the river. They'll believe it because no one would choose this."

Tomas looked at the wreckage of his livelihood bobbing in the eddy, at the seam-split hull that had carried him thirty years, and felt something between grief and freedom move through him like the current itself.

"Well," he said. "Debts should cost something. Otherwise they're just promises."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the far bank, out of the wind, and it was there, drying their clothes over a fire built small and careful, that the cost of half-measures came due—because it always does, because the river gives nothing back for free.

Sebastian found him at the fire's edge, alone, and sat down without asking, the way soldiers do.

"The collectors," they said quietly. "When they realize we're alive—and they will, ghosts talk, gods talk, the mountain has ears in every crow—they'll know you chose us. The third favor comes due. Ilsa."

"I know."

"And you spent your boat to buy us a day's head start."

"I spent a boat," Tomas corrected. "Boats can be rebuilt. Daughters can't." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Besides. I've been thinking, since last night, about what the collector said. *Valued things can be revalued.* That's all Ilsa is to them—a value in a ledger. And I've spent my whole life ferrying other people's valuables across water, keeping them dry, delivering them safe." He looked up, and for the first time in eleven years the fear in him had a shape, and a shape can be fought. "Maybe it's time I ferried something *out*."

Downriver, far off, a horn sounded—once, low, from the direction of the mountain road. Then again. Then a third time, closer, and Odile was on her feet with her beads clenched white, and Mateo had turned toward the sound with his face full of something no living man should have to wear.

"They know," Sebastian said, rising, hand finding their sword. "They already know."

The horn sounded a fourth time, and this time it did not sound alone.
