## 10. The Market of Small Mercies

The waterfall had been frozen for so long that the town inside it had forgotten it was ever anything else.

Odile felt that forgetting like a hand pressed flat against her sternum as they descended the ice stair cut into the falls' outer face. Above them, the river hung mid-plunge, a hundred feet of white water turned to white stone, and light came through it the way light comes through a saint's window—green-gold, wavering, holy in a way that made her teeth ache with old training. She had spent eleven years teaching novices that holiness was a discipline, a posture of the soul held straight through long hours. She had never once told them the truth: that real holiness was this, an accident of cold water and winter sun, asking nothing, forgiving nothing, simply *there*.

"Mind the third step," Tomas said behind her, breath fogging. "It's a liar."

She stepped over it. The ice beneath her boots groaned but held, and then they were through the curtain wall of frozen spray and into the market proper, and Odile forgot to breathe for a moment out of something that was not quite awe and not quite grief.

The interior of the falls was a cathedral no one had built. The cavern was ribbed with columns of ice where the water had frozen in sheets, and between those columns the traders of the high valleys had strung their lanterns—wax candles in tin cups, oil lamps in blown glass, one extravagant thing that burned blue and cast everyone's shadow twice. Stalls clung to ledges like swallows' nests. A woman three tiers up was selling dried fish by shouting prices down the ice in a voice trained by generations to carry across a gorge. Somewhere a fiddle played, badly, cheerfully, and the sound bounced off the frozen walls until the whole cavern seemed to hum with it, a note held under everything, the way a held breath sits under speech.

"It's called the Fair of Small Mercies," Tomas said. He had gone quiet on the stairs, and now he went quieter still, which for Tomas Bell meant his guilt had arrived ahead of him. "Everyone trades here under truce. Even people who'd kill each other on the open road. Even—" He stopped.

"Even your collectors," Odile said.

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They had been three days out from the drowned chapel when the roads began to die. The long winter had come down early and hard, exactly as the Wolf had promised—or warned; with gods, Odile was learning, the two were often the same word—and the passes north of the valley had filled with snow faster than any of them could walk through it. The fair was the only door left open before solstice. Everyone knew it. Which meant everyone who needed to move before the world sealed itself shut would be here: pilgrims, smugglers, debt-fleers, and the agents of courts both above and below the mountain, all of them pretending, in the lantern light, to be ordinary travelers buying ordinary things.

Sebastian moved through the crowd like someone reading a page written in a language only assassins learned. They wore their hood up and their hands visible, which Odile had come to understand was a mercenary's courtesy—a way of saying *I am not reaching for anything*. Their brand, hidden under wool and linen at the base of the throat, did not burn here. Odile checked without meaning to, the way she still crossed herself before meals though she no longer believed the gesture meant anything to anyone. She had spent two weeks telling herself the burning was coincidence, that proximity to a god's chosen would inflame any sensitive skin, that her own prayers had simply grown too loud since the day one had answered her back in front of four hundred congregants. She was a liar by profession now, or by necessity, and she knew the difference between a lie she told others and the ones she told herself at night.

The truth was simple and unbearable: when she prayed near Sebastian Reyes, something *listened*.

Not the Wolf. Something older and more patient, something that had been following her since the sermon, since the moment the god's voice had poured through her mouth like floodwater through a broken door and left her standing in the wreckage of her own vocation. Her order called it heresy. Her order was, at this moment, very likely calling it something worse.

"Split up?" Sebastian asked, low, at the mouth of the central aisle.

"Tomas finds us passage," Odile said. "You find us food that isn't fish. I'll—"

"You'll stay where I can see you," Sebastian said, "because you've been rubbing the back of your neck for an hour, and I know what that means."

Odile's hand stilled. "And what does it mean?"

"That you're about to do something religious." Sebastian's eyes, gray as slate under the green-gold light, held hers without cruelty. "I grew up around priests. My mother sold me to a god's creditors, but before that she sold candles to their houses. I know the fidgets."

"I'm not going to pray," Odile said. It came out sharper than she intended. "I'm not going to do *anything*. I just want to look at the reliquary stalls. Old habit. Priests collect relics the way magpies collect—"

"Bright things that belonged to the dead," Sebastian said. "Go. But keep the aisle in sight."

She went, and hated how much steadier she felt with their gaze between her shoulder blades, and hated hating it, and kept walking.

* * *

The reliquary stalls were exactly what she needed and exactly what she feared.

A man with frost in his beard sold finger-bones of minor saints in matchboxes. A woman whose stall was nothing but a plank laid across two barrels offered rosaries carved from the antlers of deer that had grazed on consecrated ground. And at the end of the row, half-hidden behind a hanging sheet of amber ice, a stall that made Odile stop so suddenly that a pilgrim behind her walked into her back and swore.

The goods were prayer-scraps. Strips of paper and vellum, thousands of them, pinned in drifts to corkboard, each one a prayer someone had written down because speaking it aloud had been too dangerous or too shameful or too heavy. *For my son, that he comes home before the gates close.* *Forgive me, I took bread.* *Let her live, take my eyes instead.* The stall-keeper sold them by weight, Odile realized. Prayers by the ounce, to be burned by buyers who wanted their wishes carried on the smoke of other people's desperation.

She picked one up. The ink had run where a tear—or meltwater, she told herself firmly, meltwater—had touched it. *I did not mean to hear You,* it said. *Please stop answering.*

Her hands shook. She put it down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a sleeping animal, and when she looked up—

Sister Maurelle was standing at the next stall over, examining rosaries.

She had not changed. That was the first obscenity of it. Eleven years of shared cells and shared silences, and Maurelle stood there in her gray traveling habit with her hands folded and her face composed into its usual expression of gentle, patient disappointment, as if they had parted yesterday over some trivial quarrel about the proper storage of incense. As if Odile had not fled the Motherhouse at dawn with nothing but a satchel and a scream still caught in her throat.

"Sister Vane," Maurelle said. Not Odile. Never Odile. The name was a leash.

"Sister Maurelle." Odile's voice came out level. Some part of her, the part trained through a decade of hearing confessions, detached itself and observed with clinical interest: *your heart rate has doubled, your palms are wet, and yet your voice is perfectly steady. This is what they built you for.* "What a small world."

"The world is always small," Maurelle said, "when God is in it." She set down the rosary she had been holding and turned fully, and Odile saw the second sister in the shadows beyond the ice curtain, and the third, and understood with a lurch that this was not chance. This was a net, drawn closed. "We have been looking for you since the Feast of the Quiet Mouth. Four months, Odile. Do you know what you cost us? Do you know what the faithful said, when word spread that a god had spoken through one of ours and she had *run*?"

"They said I was blessed," Odile said. "Some of them. The ones who wrote to me afterward. There were letters, Maurelle. Forty-one letters from people whose prayers got answered for the first time in their lives, and every one of them begging me not to let the Order silence it."

"And the Order's answer is mercy." Maurelle smiled, and it was the smile Odile had loved once, the smile that had greeted her on her first terrible night in the novitiate, warm as a stove in a cold room. That was the thing about Maurelle: she had never once lied to Odile. She had only ever believed things that were monstrous, and believed them gently. "That is why I am here and not the Inquisitors. The Mother Superior remembers you at seventeen, weeping through your first vigil. She does not want you dragged home in chains. She wants to offer you *absolution*, Odile. Full erasure of the heresy. The record struck. Your vows restored. All of it—gone, as if the sermon never happened, as if the god never used your mouth."

"And the price."

Maurelle's smile deepened by a single degree. "You know the price."

* * *

Later—much later, hours later, after the fiddle had stopped and the lanterns had burned low and the party had regrouped in a rented alcove behind a chandler's stall, wrapped in blankets that smelled of tallow—Odile would try to reconstruct exactly what she had said. She would fail. Memory refused the task, the way a tongue refuses a rotten tooth. What remained were fragments:

Herself saying: *He is not a thing to be delivered. He is a person, and he is under my protection.*

Maurelle saying, softly: *Under your protection? Odile. Sweet girl. You cannot even protect yourself. You are a runaway with a stolen psalter and a heresy in your mouth, and the only reason the Inquisitors have not found you is that we found you first.*

Herself saying: *If I refuse?*

Maurelle saying: *Then the offer expires at solstice, and what follows will not be gentle. The Order has hunted louder heresies than yours across worse winters. And Odile—the god-touched man. Whatever he is carrying, whatever mark he bears, it shines through our scrying like a bonfire. We would have found him eventually. You have merely decided whether it happens kindly.*

And herself, at the end, saying the only true thing in the whole conversation, the thing she would carry like a stone in her shoe for days: *You call it forgiveness. Forgiveness doesn't come with a bill of sale.*

* * *

The alcove was lit by one candle. Sebastian sat with their back to the ice wall, arms loose, watching her with the particular patience of someone who had already guessed.

"Well?" they said.

"She wants you." Odile pulled the blanket tighter. The cold in the falls-market was a living thing; it found the gaps in everything. "My order. They've been tracking your brand since we crossed the boundary line. The Mother Superior is offering me full absolution—my heresy erased, my vows restored, my name clean—in exchange for delivering you to them at solstice. Before the gates close. Before—" She stopped. "Before whatever happens below the mountain happens. They don't want you saved or damned. They want you *silenced*. A god woke up, Sebastian. Woke gods say things. Say things through people like me. The Order of the Quiet Mouth exists to make sure the world stays quiet enough to be governed."

Tomas, curled in the corner with his cap over his eyes as if sleep might be performed into being, said without moving: "And what did you tell her?"

"What do you think I told her?"

"I think," Tomas said, "that you're telling us now because you didn't tell her no loudly enough. I think you're telling us because some starving piece of you wanted to say yes, and you need witnesses to the fact that you didn't." He lifted the cap. His eyes were kind and tired. "I know the shape of that, priestess. I owe the mountain court three favors. You think I haven't lain awake doing arithmetic with my daughter's name in one column?"

"Tomas," Sebastian said quietly.

"He should say it," Tomas said. "Someone should. We're all carrying knives pointed at each other, and pretending otherwise is how people get stabbed."

Silence. The candle guttered. Through the ice wall, muffled and distant, the fair went on singing and selling, small mercies by the ounce.

Odile looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked back, and there was no accusation in their face, which was somehow worse than accusation, and then they said, in a voice stripped of everything except honesty:

"If delivering me gets you your life back—if it gets you your *god* back, the quiet one, the safe one, the one that never answers—you should consider it. I mean that. I've been sold before. It's survivable. I'd rather be sold to nuns than to the court below." A pause. "But I'll ask you one question first, and I want you to actually sit with it. When Maurelle offered to erase your heresy—what exactly was she offering to erase?"

Odile opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because she knew. She had known since the sermon, since the floodwater voice, since the forty-one letters. The Order had framed it as sickness, as possession, as error—but the thing they wanted scrubbed from her record was the single moment in her entire life when prayer had *worked*. When the words had gone somewhere. When the silence she had devoted herself to maintaining had cracked open and something enormous had looked through it and said her name.

"They're not offering forgiveness," she said slowly. "They're offering amputation."

"Yeah," Sebastian said. "That's what I figured." They pulled the blanket off their shoulders and stood, and for a moment the candlelight caught the edge of the brand at their throat, the old scar-tissue shine of it, and Odile thought: *that is what I would be giving them. Another scar to add to their collection.* "Sleep on it. We trade for passage tomorrow at dawn, and then we move, and whatever you choose, you choose walking, not kneeling. That's the only rule in this company. Nobody chooses on their knees."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the dark of the alcove while the others breathed, and she listened to the fair dying down tier by tier, lantern by lantern, until only the river's frozen roar remained, that vast held silence inside the ice.

And in that silence, as it had every night since the sermon, the presence stirred. Not words. Never words, not anymore—it had spent its one great utterance and left her with only pressure, attention, the sense of something vast turning its face toward her like a sunflower toward a window. Waiting. It had been waiting four months. It could wait until spring.

*I did not mean to hear You,* the stranger's prayer-scraps had said. *Please stop answering.*

Odile understood the writer completely. And she understood, finally, sitting in the frozen dark with absolution priced and waiting for her at solstice, that she was never going to write that prayer herself. Because the Order was right about one thing, the only thing: a god had spoken through her, and it had ruined her life, and it was going to go on ruining her life, and the ruin felt—for the first time, terrifyingly—like being *used for something*.

At dawn, the trading began. And at dawn, Odile saw, at the far end of the central aisle where the light fell greenest through the ice, Sister Maurelle waiting beside a stall that sold passage north—with three more sisters she had not seen the night before, and a fourth figure in gray whose face was turned away, whose hands were folded, whose stillness Odile recognized the way one recognizes a parent's footsteps.

The Mother Superior had not sent an offer after all.

She had come to collect in person.
