## 11. What We Steal From Ourselves

The fire in the drowned chapel burned green for the first hour of the night, fed by something in the old pews that had soaked up a century of river water and blessing both. Sebastian had stopped asking why. The valley was behind them now, weeks behind them, and everything since the boundary line had been a lesson in not asking why: the snow that fell upward near the mines, the birds with too many joints, the way Odile's prayers left frost on glass even when she only whispered them into her collar.

Sebastian sat sharpening a knife they did not need to sharpen, because their hands wanted work and their heart wanted silence, and only one of those things could be given.

Across the chapel, Odile was not sleeping.

She had taken the corner nearest the broken window—the one she always took, the one with the sightline to the road—and she had arranged herself beneath her cloak in the posture of a woman asleep, which was itself a kind of prayer, because the Order of the Quiet Mouth taught its daughters to lie beautifully even to the dark. Sebastian knew the posture. They had worn it themselves in a hundred mercenary camps, listening for the step that meant the contract had changed.

Tomas slept truly, snoring against the font like a man who had made his peace with whatever came. Mateo—Mateo was wherever ghosts went when they were not needed, though lately he lingered closer, a cold spot at Sebastian's shoulder like a hand that had forgotten how to weigh anything.

And the Wolf—

The Wolf was a warmth along the north wall, a pressure of attention, the sense of amber eyes in a dark that did not otherwise have eyes. It had not spoken since the chapel. Gods, Sebastian was learning, spoke rarely and meant it.

So it was just the two of them awake, the mercenary and the runaway priestess, and the fire ticking, and the snow coming down outside like a verdict being read slowly.

"Sebastian." Odile's voice, low. Not the sermon-voice. The other one, the one she used maybe six times a day, when she forgot to perform.

"Mm."

"I have to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you decide what I am."

The knife stilled on the whetstone. *There it is,* thought some old part of Sebastian, the part that had survived twelve years in a valley where no god could reach them by learning exactly this grammar: the confession-shaped sentence, the pause before it, the way a person's breathing changes when they have been carrying something heavy and have finally set it down where you can see it.

"Say it," Sebastian said.

* * *

She told it plainly, which was the first mercy. She had been raised to dress truth in vestments, and she stripped them off as she spoke, kneeling by the fire with her hands open on her knees like a woman showing she held no weapon.

At the Market of Small Mercies, while Sebastian had haggled over passage and Tomas had drunk exactly enough to be brave and not enough to be stupid, three women in grey had found Odile among the ice-stalls. Sisters of her order. Mother Casserly herself, her old teacher, her *first* teacher, the woman who had taught her letters out of a book of silences.

They had not come to drag her back. That was the horror of it, Odile said—they had come with open hands. The order would erase the heresy. The mid-sermon moment when a god had poured itself through her throat like floodwater through a cracked bell would be struck from every record, every memory the order kept, every ledger of sins. She would be Odile Vane again, daughter of the Quiet Mouth, whole and forgiven and *home*. All it would cost was the name of the god-touched mercenary, and the direction they were traveling, and perhaps—Mother Casserly had smiled when she said it, that gentle smile Odile had loved her for once—a small delay. A door locked. A bridge "washed out." Nothing that would stain her hands. The order did not kill; the order simply *silenced*, and winter did the rest.

"And I said I would think about it," Odile finished. Her voice did not shake, which meant she had spent the last three days making sure it wouldn't. "I said yes to thinking about it. Which is a kind of yes. And then I walked back to you and ate stew and laughed at Tomas's joke about the ferry tolls, and I let you make camp beside me, and I didn't say anything for three days. So. Now you know what I am."

The fire ticked. Snow hissed faintly against the broken window, melting on stones still warm from some drowned summer.

Sebastian set down the knife.

They should have been angry. They knew that; some distant part of them catalogued it the way they catalogued exits and weapon racks, automatically, professionally. Anger was the correct response. Betrayal was the correct response. Three days. Three days of roads and rations and Odile's frost-whispered prayers at night, all of it sitting inside an open question that could have killed them, and Sebastian had *slept* beside that question, had handed her watch shifts, had—gods help them—begun, without deciding to, to think of her as theirs. Household. The word had crept up on them somewhere in the last week and they had not examined it closely because examining it felt like standing on ice and hearing it sing.

But anger required surprise, and Sebastian found, searching themselves honestly, that there wasn't any.

Because here was the thing about being sold. Here was the thing Sebastian had learned in chapter after chapter of their own long life, in the dream-hunt where the Wolf had finally spoken, in the ledger-entry they had read with their own eyes in the court below: people did not betray you out of malice, mostly. They betrayed you out of love, misdirected. Their mother had sold them out of fear—for the town, for Mateo, for the shape of a future she could not hold any other way. Every terrible bargain Sebastian had ever traced came back to someone loving the wrong thing in the right amount.

And Odile loved her order. You could hear it under the confession like a second heartbeat. Twelve years of bells and silence and sisters' voices in the dark of the novitiate; Mother Casserly's hand on her hair; the whole architecture of a self built before she ever knew it could be built differently. Forgiveness offered by the people who taught you what forgiveness was—that was not a temptation you refused with virtue. That was a wound you refused with grief, and grief was slower than virtue, and sometimes it lost.

So instead of rage, Sebastian heard themselves say, in a voice quiet enough not to wake Tomas:

"Take it."

Odile's head came up. In the green firelight her face went through several weathers very fast—shock, hurt, the beginning of fury, disbelief. "What?"

"The offer. Take it." Sebastian turned the knife flat across their knees, an old habit, something for the hands to mean. "Go back to your sisters. Give them my name, my road, my description—I'll draw you a likeness if it helps. Tell them I sleep on my left side and pray to nobody and trust too easily, starting with priestesses."

"That's not—" Odile stood, and the cloak fell from her shoulders, and she looked suddenly younger and much more dangerous, the way she always did when the performance burned off entirely. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to sit there and *offer* me absolution like I'm some—some penitent in your parish. I confessed. I confessed because I chose you over them three days ago and I wanted you to know it was a choice, not an accident of cowardice—"

"You chose nothing yet," Sebastian said. "That's why I'm telling you to take it."

"Why?" And there it was, cracking through the fury: the real question, the frightened one underneath. "Why would you—after everything, after the valley, after your *mother*, after all of it, how can you look at me offering to sell you and tell me to go through with it?"

Sebastian looked at the fire for a while. The Wolf's attention had shifted; they felt it settle on them like a hand between the shoulder blades, patient, weighing. Let it watch, then. Let it see what its chosen soulless creature did with a moment like this.

"Because I know what it is to be given away," Sebastian said. "I know it from the inside. My mother gave me away because she loved a town, and I have spent twelve years understanding that, and understanding it never once made it hurt less, and I would not wish the understanding on anyone." They looked up. "If I rage at you, you'll refuse her out of loyalty to me. And loyalty to me is a debt, Odile, and I am so tired of debts. I've been collateral since before I could walk. I will not be the coin you pay your order with, and I will not be the coin you pay your *conscience* with either. If you stay, you stay because staying is yours. So take the offer. Hold it. Weigh it against what you actually believe. And if forgiveness from the Quiet Mouth is worth more to you than this ragged freezing mess of a road—then take it, and I will genuinely, truly hope it keeps you warm."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You're serious," Odile said. "You absolute—you're *serious*."

"I'm the most serious person in this province."

Something broke in her face then—not the way faces break in stories, all at once and tragically, but the way ice breaks on a river in thaw: a crack, a groan, a long grinding shift, and then movement, water underneath, the whole surface reorganizing itself around the fact that spring exists. She laughed, or sobbed, or both, one harsh sound, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"She said she'd light a candle for me," Odile said, muffled. "Mother Casserly. After I said I'd think about it. She lit a candle for my *return*, in the alcove where they keep the vows of the dead sisters, and she let me see her do it. Do you understand? She showed me the flame. That's how they mark a sister who has died to the world. She was showing me the shape of my own funeral, and calling it welcome home."

"Yes," Sebastian said quietly. "That's how it works. The kindest ones hold the knife best."

* * *

Odile knelt a long time by the fire after that, saying nothing, and Sebastian did not watch her openly but watched her the way you watch weather—peripherally, constantly, with respect.

What she did next, she did without asking permission, which Sebastian would later recognize as the moment she stopped being anyone's daughter and started being her own.

She reached into the breast of her habit and drew out her vow-cord.

Every sister of the Quiet Mouth wore one: a braided length of grey silk, knot upon knot upon knot, each knot a vow, each vow a silence sworn. Odile had told him once, on a night road, counting them absently with her fingers like a rosary gone feral—*the knot of obedience, the knot of secrecy, the knot of the unmoving tongue, the knot of the closed ear, the knot of the sealed throat*—forty-one knots, tied over eleven years, each one blessed, each one binding. The cord was the order in miniature. Cut it and you were excommunicate; burn it and you were worse. Burn it and the order's records wrote *apostate* beside your name in ink that never dried, ink that other orders could read, ink that followed a woman through every holy house in the province for the rest of her life.

She held it over the fire.

"Tomas," she said, conversationally, terribly calm.

Tomas surfaced from sleep with the speed of a man who'd spent twenty years on a river—no gasp, no flailing, just open eyes and a hand already finding his belt-knife. He took in the scene in one sweep: the priestess, the fire, the cord, the mercenary sitting very still. "Don't stop on my account," he said, and stayed exactly where he was, watching, which was its own kind of witness.

"These knots," Odile said, to the fire, or to Sebastian, or possibly to the forty-one years of silence braided in her hands, "say that gods must not be heard. That the heard god must be silenced. That a sister who carries a god's voice is a broken vessel, and broken vessels are mended by breaking further." Her fingers found the first knot, the oldest one, the obedience-knot tied when she was nine years old and believed heaven was a quiet room. "I carried a god's voice. And it was the most holy thing that has ever happened to me, and my order calls it heresy, and I have spent four months thinking they must be right, because they taught me everything I know about what holiness is."

The firelight moved on her face. Outside, the snow kept falling, erasing the road behind them and the road ahead alike, the great impartial eraser of the long winter.

"But I met a wolf," she said.

Sebastian's breath caught. Across the chapel, the warmth along the north wall deepened, gathered, became unmistakably *present*—not manifesting, not speaking, but attending, the way a father attends a child's first steps from across a room, refusing to hover, refusing to look away.

"And the wolf does not ask for silence," Odile said. "The wolf asks for *running*. For teeth and weather and loyalty that costs something. And I have watched a soulless mercenary offer to spend their last protection buying strangers out of a ledger, and I have watched a ferryman lie badly for love, and I have watched a ghost carry a lantern for a sibling who couldn't see him for twelve years, and none of it—not one word of it—has ever been quiet." She lifted the cord higher. The green firelight ran along the grey silk like water along a blade. "So the vows are wrong. Not strained. Not bent. *Wrong.* And a wrong vow isn't a sin to break. It's a theft to return."

"Burn it, then," Sebastian said softly. "Steal yourself back."

"I intend to," said Odile Vane, "and I intend to enjoy it."

She fed the cord to the fire.

It did not go quickly. Silk resists; blessings resist harder. The knots blackened and swelled and split one by one, and with each one Odile spoke its vow aloud—*the knot of obedience, released; the knot of secrecy, released*—her voice steady as a cantor's, tears running freely down her face and her voice *steady anyway*, which Sebastian understood was the whole secret of courage and no one had ever told them. Forty-one vows. Forty-one small detonations of smoke and ash. When the last knot burst, the fire flared green-white, and for half a heartbeat every shadow in the chapel stood up straight—pews, pillars, the three of them, and a fourth shape, vast and four-legged, thrown enormous against the far wall, ears forward, tail still.

Then the light settled. The shadows lay back down. The smell of burnt silk and old incense filled the drowned chapel like a new kind of hymn.

Tomas cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he said, "the order's loss is—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the chapel, at the general catastrophe of their lives— "well. It's our gain, presumably, though I'd want to check the toll schedule first."

Odile laughed—wet, cracked, real—and came and sat down beside Sebastian, close, shoulder to shoulder, the way soldiers sit who have decided to be something else.

"You asked me to take the offer," she said.

"I did."

"So that I'd stay *choosing*." She shook her head slowly. "You cruel, careful man. Woman. Whatever you are before the gods, who apparently also don't know." She bumped her shoulder against theirs. "It worked. I hate that it worked. I want it noted that I hate it."

"Noted," Sebastian said, and found, to their considerable surprise, that they were smiling.

* * *

Later—much later, when Tomas had gone back under and Odile slept the boneless sleep of the newly unbound—Sebastian sat by the fire alone with the knife across their knees and the warmth of the Wolf at their shoulder.

Mateo came then. He had a way of arriving that was less appearing than *being noticed*: one moment the cold spot was empty, the next it was shaped like a brother. He settled against the pillar opposite, translucent in the firelight, wearing the face he'd died with at nineteen, forever, and he looked at the little drift of ash where the vow-cord had been.

"She'll do," he said. Ghosts' voices sounded like wind through a keyhole, but Sebastian had gotten fluent. "The priestess. She'll do."

"You approve?"

"I approve of people who burn things that deserve burning." His grin was the old grin, the one from before, from the valley, from summers that had cost more than anyone knew. Then it gentled. "You did a hard thing tonight, Felix."

"Don't call me that."

"It means lucky. It means happy. It means free." He tilted his head, that maddening older-brother tilt. "You gave her the door out instead of the chain to stay. Ma couldn't do that. Ma loved us and chained us anyway. You loved her and opened your hand." He shrugged, insubstantial, luminous. "That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it took you thirty years and a wolf to learn it, but you learned it."

Sebastian looked into the fire and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the ash wasn't already saying.

Then Mateo's gaze slid past them, toward the chapel's broken eastern door, and the temperature of his presence changed—cold sharpening into something older and more urgent, the ghost going taut as a wire.

"Felix," he said. "There are riders on the north road. Six of them. Grey cloaks, and they're not moving like sisters come to forgive."

Sebastian was already reaching for the knife.

Behind them, unasked, the Wolf rose—all of it at once, the warmth becoming weight becoming *presence*, filling the drowned chapel the way dawn fills a well—and in the last instant before the knocking began at the door, Sebastian heard it, not in words, never in words, but plain as a bell:

*Run.*
