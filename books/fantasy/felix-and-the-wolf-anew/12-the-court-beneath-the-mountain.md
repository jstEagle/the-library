## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

### Sebastian

The mine mouth breathed cold air against them like a held breath let go.

Sebastian had expected a hole in the mountain, a seam of black rock and rotting timber, something honest in its ugliness. What they got instead was a throat. The entrance arched high enough for three men on horseback, and the stone around it had been carved—no, *grown*—into ribs that curved inward as the passage descended, so that walking into it felt like being swallowed by something patient. Lantern light slid along walls veined with silver and something paler than silver, something that caught the flame and gave it back wrong, tinged blue at the edges.

"Prayer-scraps," Odile said quietly, an hour in. She had stopped walking. Her hand was raised to the wall, not touching, hovering over a small pale shape embedded in the stone.

Sebastian came back up the line to look. At first they thought it was a fossil, some pressed fern or fish from before the mountain had a name. Then they saw the letters. Tiny, cramped, scratched into a scrap of vellum no bigger than a thumbnail, rolled tight as a seed and set into the rock like a jewel: *please let my daughter live through the winter please please please.*

"They're everywhere," Tomas said. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was counting debt. He turned his lantern along the wall, and the wall answered—a thousand scraps, ten thousand, each one a folded whisper of desperation sealed into the mountain's flesh. Some were ancient, browned to lace. Some looked fresh. One still had a thumbprint of wax on it, still soft-looking, as if the hand that pressed it there had only just pulled away.

"Is this what the court does with them?" Sebastian asked. "Eats them?"

"No." Odile's voice had changed. It was her sermon voice, but stripped of all its performance, down to the raw wood underneath. "It *keeps* them. That's worse."

They walked on. The passage spiraled down in long slow turns, and the prayer-scraps thickened until the walls seemed furred with them, pale flecks in the dark like snowfall frozen mid-air. Sebastian found themselves reading without meaning to. *Let him come home from the war.* *Forgive me, I didn't mean to sell her horse, I'll buy another.* *I am so tired. I am so tired. I am so tired.* The last one repeated itself down half a yard of stone, the same scrap pressed again and again by someone who could not stop praying even after the mountain stopped listening—or perhaps because it hadn't.

Mateo walked beside them. He had grown quieter as they descended, which Sebastian had not thought possible for a ghost who never stopped talking when he was nervous. Now he drifted close, his translucent shoulder almost brushing theirs, and stared at the walls with an expression Sebastian had last seen on his living face the night their mother sat them down at the table and told them the valley would be safe now, safe forever, and wouldn't that be worth anything, *anything*, my darlings?

"You remember," Sebastian said, too low for the others to hear.

"I remember everything," Mateo said. "That's the whole problem with being dead."

The air warmed as they went down. That was the first wrong thing, though it took Sebastian a while to name it. Mines should be cold. This warmth rose from below like breath, like the heat off a sleeping animal, carrying smells that did not belong underground: cut grass, woodsmoke, bread, rain on dust. The smells of the world above, of everything the mountain had taken and kept. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, they could hear singing—not words, just voices, hundreds of them, layered and patient, holding one long note between them the way a congregation holds its breath before the amen.

Tomas stopped first. "Do you hear—"

"Yes," said Odile.

"Don't answer it," said Mateo, and both of them flinched; they kept forgetting he was there, forgetting that the dead could offer advice. "It's not singing *to* you. It's singing to itself. But if you hum along—even in your head—it starts a ledger page. A blank one. With your name at the top."

Sebastian looked at their brother. "How do you know that?"

"Because I have a page," Mateo said simply. "I've had one for twelve years. It's very short. It mostly says *waiting*."

The spiral tightened. The ribs of the passage drew closer together, and the prayer-scraps gave way to something else: names. Carved directly into the stone now, thousands upon thousands, in every size and style of lettering, some elegant, some childlike, some gouged deep by hands that must have been bleeding as they wrote. Sebastian ran their fingers over one—*MIRELLE OF THE ASH FORDS*—and felt the groove still sharp-edged, unfinished, as if the name had been interrupted mid-carving and never completed.

Below the names, the walls turned to mirror.

Not glass. Not metal. Something like polished ice, dark and deep, and in it Sebastian saw themselves reflected—except the reflection wore the soul-debt mark openly, a brand of interlocking rings across their throat and collarbone, glowing faint gold, and the reflection smiled when Sebastian did not.

"That's you," Odile whispered behind them. "That's what you look like to the mountain."

"Then the mountain can go to hell," Sebastian said, and walked faster.

### Odile

Odile had spent her life in buildings built to make her feel small—the great chapel of the Order of the Quiet Mouth, with its ceiling painted to resemble a sky no god had ever lived in—and she had believed herself immune to architecture. She had believed awe was a tool, and she held the handle.

The Court Beneath the Mountain took the tool out of her hand and showed her what it was for.

They came out of the mines onto a ledge, and the ledge opened onto a cavern so vast that lantern-light died before it found the far side. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ, miles of them, and every surface glittered—but the glitter was not mineral. As their eyes adjusted, Odile understood, and wished she hadn't, that the sparkle was *light trapped in souls*. Each point of brightness was a fragment of someone: a laugh, a talent for whistling, the memory of a mother's face, the ability to feel hope on spring mornings. The court had broken its currency into small denominations and spent them decorating its halls.

"It's beautiful," Tomas said, and then, immediately, "gods forgive me, it's beautiful," because it was, and that was the horror of it. Beauty built from stolen things is still beauty. That was how it got away with it.

A road of white stone switchbacked down from the ledge toward the cavern floor, and the cavern floor was a city. Odile had seen the capital once, from a pilgrim's window, all spires and banners. This made the capital look like a child's drawing. Towers of black glass rose from terraces lit by soul-light, and bridges arced between them like frozen waterfalls, and everywhere moved figures—some human, some almost-human, some wearing shapes that hurt to look at directly, as if the eye kept trying to fold them into something sensible. And above it all, at the center, stood the palace: not built but *grown*, a single massive formation of pale crystal like a thorn the size of a cathedral, and at its crown a light burned cold and steady.

"The Queen's lamp," Mateo said. He had come to stand at Odile's elbow, and she was long past being startled by him. Ghosts, she was learning, were simply people with nothing left to hide. "It burns on the oldest debts. The ones nobody remembers making. There are kingdoms down here that exist only because someone's grandmother sighed at the wrong moment."

Down the switchbacks they went, and the city received them the way water receives a dropped stone: without comment. No guards stopped them. No gates barred them. That frightened Odile more than any checkpoint would have. In her order, the places you could walk freely were the places you were already owned.

The streets smelled of hot metal and honey. Market stalls lined the lower avenues, and Odile made herself look, made herself see what was sold here. Not bodies—nothing so crude. Vendors sold *qualities*. A stall of little drawers labeled in neat script: *the courage to leave / the courage to stay / the memory of your first love / the memory of your worst day / patience / the sound of your own name spoken kindly.* Buyers leaned over the drawers and breathed them in, and Odile watched a woman purchase what appeared to be her own grief, paying for it with a small vial of golden light that Odile realized, with a lurch, was the woman's capacity for joy. The transaction took less time than buying bread.

"This is what silencing looks like," Odile said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "My order thinks holiness is quiet. We spend our lives trying to make the world quieter. And this—" she gestured at the glittering dark, the drawers, the patient crowds, "—this is where quiet ends up. Everything muffled. Everything filed."

Sebastian was staring at a shop window. Inside, on a cushion of grey velvet, under a bell jar, floated a single point of light like a firefly in winter. The label beneath read: *ONE SOUL, WHOLE. UNSPENT. ANTIQUE.*

"That could be mine," Sebastian said. Their voice was very level, which Odile had learned was the most dangerous register they owned. "Somewhere in this city is a drawer with my name on it, and everything I am is inside it, and someone might be breathing me in right now to get through a hard week."

"No," said Mateo, softly. "Yours isn't in a drawer. Yours is collateral. Collateral stays in the vault." He paused. "I've seen the vault, LITTLE WOLF. In dreams. It's the warmest room in the palace."

"Why warm?"

"Because it's full of everything people ever loved about themselves. All that light, packed together. It has nowhere to go, so it just—" he spread his ghost-hands, "—shines."

Odile watched Sebastian absorb that, watched their jaw set, watched the brand at their throat flicker gold in the soul-light like something answering a call it couldn't refuse. She thought about her own vows, sewn into her skin in ink and habit, and wondered which of them she'd be able to burn when the moment came, and which would turn out to be load-bearing.

An usher found them then—a slim figure in grey whose face was smooth as an egg, featureless except for a small courteous smile—and bowed.

"The Ledgerkeeper's office will receive the debtor Sebastian Reyes at the morning bell," it said, in a voice like pages turning. "The others are welcome to wait. The waiting rooms are comfortable. Most guests find them unforgettable."

It turned and glided away, and none of them followed it, and none of them slept either, though beds were offered. They sat together in a chamber of black glass overlooking the city, four of them—three living, one dead—and watched the soul-light rise and fall in slow tides, like the breathing of something enormous and content.

At the morning bell, a servant came for Sebastian alone.

### Tomas

Tomas Bell had crossed the valley line two hundred and eleven times in his life, and he had a rule for fear: name it, price it, carry it. Fear was freight. You loaded it, you hauled it, you delivered it or you dumped it in the river, but you never let it drive the boat.

He was failing at his rule.

The problem was Ilsa. Every corridor of the palace—they had been given rooms in an outer wing, guest quarters of unreasonable luxury, as if comfort were also a form of debt—every corridor smelled faintly of the soap she'd used as a girl, tallow and lavender, and Tomas knew it was impossible, knew the court was doing it deliberately, seasoning his guilt the way a cook seasons a pot. It worked anyway. He kept catching himself turning toward doorways, listening for a fourteen-year-old's footsteps, hearing instead the soft endless shuffle of servants whose faces were all the same courteous blank.

He found her on the second day, or she found him—he was never sure afterward which. He had slipped away from the others, following a hunch and a memory, down a stair that smelled of paper and candle-wax, into a gallery lined floor to ceiling with ledgers. Thousands of them. Millions of pages. And at a high desk in the middle of the room, quill moving with terrible fluency, sat a young woman with her mother's stubborn chin and his own crooked left eyebrow.

She looked up. She did not drop the quill.

"You're shorter than I remembered," Ilsa said.

"You're taller than I feared," Tomas said, and then neither of them could speak for a while, and the quill waited, and the ledgers waited, and somewhere above them the Queen's lamp burned on debts older than grief.

When he could speak again, he said the thing he had carried up the whole mountain: "I came to buy you out. Whatever it costs. I've got one favor left and I'll spend it on this, I swear it on the ferry, on the river, on—"

"Daddy." She said it gently, the way you'd correct a child reaching for a hot stove. "Look at the ledger you're standing next to."

He looked. The spine read: *FERRYMEN, VALLEY LINE, OBLIGATIONS OUTSTANDING.* He opened it with hands that shook. Found his own name. Read the entry twice, three times, the letters swimming.

Beside his name, in the court's immaculate script, was written: *PAID IN FULL — DAUGHTER RETAINED AT HER OWN REQUEST.*

"I asked to stay," Ilsa said. "Six years ago. I signed. Nobody tricked me. The collectors didn't lie to you, Papa—they just never mentioned that part, because it wasn't useful to them." She set down her quill and folded her hands, and she had the Queen's own stillness, and it broke his heart in a new shape. "Down here, a promise means something. You know that better than anyone. You've spent your whole life watching promises up there dissolve in the rain. The court keeps its word. Always. Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones." She hesitated, and for one moment she was fourteen again, asking if the river would hold. "Your friends are going to try something at solstice. I keep the records, Papa. I will know. And I am asking you—before I have to choose what to do about it—tell me why I should help you anyway."

Tomas Bell, who had named and priced and carried every fear of his life, stood in the hall of ledgers with his daughter's question hanging over him like the Queen's cold lamp, and understood that the heaviest freight he had ever hauled was this: the truth, delivered honestly, to someone he had failed.

"Because," he said slowly, "your brother—" he stopped. Started again. "Because the people I'm traveling with gave up everything safe to come down here for strangers. Because I watched a god let go of the person it loved most rather than watch them be owned. And because I owe you six years of answers, and this is the first one I've ever been able to give you in person: I was wrong to sign you away, whatever you asked for, whatever it bought. Love isn't a favor. It doesn't come due. It just stays."

Ilsa looked at him for a long time. Behind her, the ledgers rustled as if turning their own pages, listening.

Then she reached beneath her desk and brought out a key of black iron, and set it on the open ledger beside his name, and said, in the voice of a record-keeper who knew exactly what she was risking:

"The vault where they keep the collateral souls opens at the solstice ceremony. Every debt gets read aloud. And a read debt, Papa—" her eyes were bright and terrified and certain, "—can be contested by anyone willing to stand surety. Go tell your friends. Tell them the Queen already knows they're here. Tell them she's *letting* them plan. And tell them to ask themselves why."

### Sebastian

That evening, as the soul-light dimmed to its night-tide and the city below settled into its glittering sleep, a servant in grey came to Sebastian's door with an invitation written on vellum pressed thin as a moth's wing.

*Her Majesty, the Queen of the Long Winter, requests the pleasure of the branded guest's company. Alone.*

Mateo hovered at the window, agitated, his edges fraying like smoke. "Don't go. Don't go alone. Don't go at all."

"If I don't," Sebastian said, fastening their collar over the brand that pulsed like a second heartbeat, "she'll come to me. I'd rather meet her on my feet."

They followed the servant down corridors of black glass, past the drawers and the bell jars and the patient, muffled crowds, deeper than the ledgers, deeper than the vault's warm glow seeping through the walls, down to a door of pale crystal that opened without being touched.

Inside, a fire burned in a hearth of silver, and beside it sat a woman in winter-white, sewing. She did not look up.

"Sit, child," said the Queen of the Long Winter. "We have much to discuss, you and I. Your mother and I nearly finished our conversation, twelve years ago. I do hate leaving a bargain half-read."

And on the table between them, open to a single page, lay a ledger with Sebastian's name at the top—and beside the name, in fresh ink, a second entry, dated *this very night*, that had not existed an hour before.

Sebastian looked at the new entry, and the floor of their certainty tilted, and the chapter of everything they believed closed like a door.
