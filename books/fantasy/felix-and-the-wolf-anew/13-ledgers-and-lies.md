## 13. Ledgers and Lies

The court beneath the mountain smelled of ink.

That was the thing no one warned you about, Sebastian thought, standing in the queue of debtors with their hood up and their hands folded like a penitent's. You expected a place where souls were bought and sold to smell of blood, of smoke, of something burnt. Instead it smelled of a counting-house: tallow candles, cold stone, and the iron-gall bite of ink laid down in layers so deep that the whole cavern had gone black-veined with it. The walls themselves were ledger pages, grown over with writing, line after line after line, names in a hundred dead scripts. Somewhere below them, deeper than the mines they'd descended through, water dripped onto paper. Sebastian could hear it. *Drip.* A name being written somewhere, or erased.

"Next," said the clerk.

The clerk was not human. It had the shape of one—a grey robe, grey hands, a face like a thumbprint pressed into dough—but its eyes were two holes through which you could see the ledger behind it, columns receding into dark. Odile had told them what to expect. *Don't look at its eyes,* she'd said on the road down, when the party huddled in a mine gallery among prayer-scraps—little knots of paper and cloth, wishes wedged into cracks by miners who'd sold small things first, an hour of sleep, the memory of a song, and wanted receipts. *Look at its hands. Everything in this court is done with hands.*

So Sebastian looked at the hands. Grey, patient, ink-stained to the second knuckle. Hands that had written Sebastian Reyes into existence as a line item before they were old enough to walk.

"I'm here about a debt," Sebastian said. Their voice came out steady. Twelve years of mercenary work taught you that much: how to sound bored while your heart tried to climb out through your ribs. "An old one. Family business."

"All debts are family business," the clerk said. Its voice was dry as a page turning. "Name."

"Felix," Sebastian said.

It was the name they'd chosen for this, three nights ago in the drowned chapel, with the Wolf's dream-hunt still ringing in their skull and Mateo's ghost sitting at the edge of the fire like a man warming hands he no longer had. *Felix,* Odile had suggested. *Lucky. No one suspects a lucky man of owing anything.* Tomas had snorted and said luck was just debt you hadn't been billed for yet. But the name had held. Felix, son of Marisol Reyes, of the valley town of Aldermoor. Born under a boundary stone. Nothing to see here.

The clerk's fingers moved across a surface that wasn't quite there, and the sound it made was the sound of the drip from far below, magnified. *Drip.*

"Debtor Felix," it said. "Collateral account, opened thirty-one years ago. What is your inquiry?"

Thirty-one years. Sebastian did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt gently beneath them. Thirty-one years ago they had been four years old, and Mateo had been six, and their mother had walked up the mountain in the snow with two children and come back down with one child and a receipt.

"I want to read my entry," Sebastian said. "In full. I'm told debtors have that right."

"They do." The clerk tilted its thumbprint head. "Reading is free. Understanding costs." A pause, and then, with something that might have been humor in a creature made of bookkeeping: "Understanding will be deducted from your account."

"I'll pay it," Sebastian said. *Everything in this court is done with hands,* Odile had said. And everything in this court was paid for, too. That was the whole architecture of the place. Nothing given, nothing taken, only transferred. Even grief had a price column.

The clerk turned. It did not walk so much as file itself away, robe whispering over stone, and Sebastian followed it down a corridor where the walls grew closer together and the writing on them grew larger, until the words were big enough to read and Sebastian wished they weren't. *SOLD: ONE SUMMER, BELONGING TO HALLORAN, T. — PRICE: TWO WINTERS' WARMTH.* *PURCHASED: THE NAME OF A FIRSTBORN, ANONYMOUS BUYER.* *FORFEIT: THE COLOR BLUE, ONE HOUSEHOLD'S ENTITLEMENT, PERPETUAL.* Whole lives broken into clauses. A market street of other people's catastrophes, and every catastrophe neatly ruled and totaled.

*They don't know,* Sebastian thought, walking faster to keep the grey robe in sight. *The people back home. They don't know any of this exists, or that they're in it.*

But that wasn't true either, was it? Some of them must have known. Some of them must have signed. His mother had signed. And the valley had stayed safe for thirty-one years—no plague, no war, no magic running wild through it, nothing but the long ordinary silence that Sebastian had mistaken, all their life, for peace.

The corridor opened into the Reading Room.

It was round, and lit from above by a shaft of pale light that fell from some impossible distance—the mountain's throat, maybe, open to a winter sky—and in the middle of the floor stood a single lectern of black wood, and on the lectern lay a single book, open, its pages blank.

"Your entry," the clerk said, "will present itself to you alone. Others reading over your shoulder will see only blankness. This is courtesy. The court extends courtesy to all its clients." It stepped back against the wall and became, if possible, even more still. "You may read for as long as you wish. Time spent reading is also deducted."

Sebastian approached the lectern the way you approach a body you've been sent to identify.

Up close, the book was worse than the walls. The pages weren't paper; they were skin, fine and pale and faintly warm, and the writing rose off them in relief, scar-tissue letters that shifted when Sebastian breathed. At the top of the left-hand page, in a hand that was almost beautiful, almost loving:

**REYES, FELIX (born REYES, SEBASTIAN).**

Below it, the entry unspooled like a thread pulled from a wound.

*Asset: one soul, entire, undivided. Condition: unblemished at time of deposit. Depositor: MARISOL REYES, of Aldermoor, the Valley Province, acting with authority over dependents in her household.*

*Consideration received: PROTECTION OF THE VALLEY OF ALDERMOOR, ENTIRE, IN PERPETUITY—*

And there the line stopped. There was a smudge there, a place where the scar-tissue letters had been worked and reworked, and Sebastian leaned close, close enough to feel the warmth of the page against their face, and read the amendment in smaller script beneath:

*—IN PERPETUITY, SUBJECT TO CLOSURE OF LEDGER AT SOLSTICE.*

*Subject to closure of ledger at solstice.*

The words sat there. Sebastian read them again. And again. The way you read a sentence in a letter from home that can't possibly say what it says, and so you read it until it agrees to mean something else, and it never does.

"Not in perpetuity," Sebastian heard themselves say aloud. Their voice sounded very young in the round stone room. "Not forever. She didn't buy us forever."

"The protection," the clerk said from the wall, in the tone of a man reciting weather, "was contracted for the life of the ledger. The ledger closes at solstice. All accounts settle. All collateral is called in." A pause. "This is standard."

*Standard.* Sebastian's hands had gone numb on the lectern's edge. Below the consideration clause, the entry went on, itemizing, itemizing, the way the court itemized everything, because everything was transferable, because everything had a price column:

*Note: depositor acted under duress of circumstance (famine year, third winter of the gray sickness). Note: depositor wept. Note: weeping is not deductible.*

*Note: collateral includes one (1) additional dependent soul, REYES, MATEO, deceased age six, held in suspension pending settlement.*

Mateo. Sebastian's eyes snagged on the line and could not move past it. Held in suspension. Not at rest, not gone, not eaten—all those things Sebastian had feared for twelve years, all the shapes of guilt they'd built in the dark of the valley, the brother who died the night the bargain was struck, the fever taking him while their mother climbed the mountain with her surviving child and her terrible arithmetic. Held. Waiting. Filed. A note in someone else's margin, pending settlement, and all this time Sebastian had thought their ghost was a haunting, when really it had been a *holding pattern*. Mateo hadn't clung to the world. The world's paperwork had refused to release him.

Something in Sebastian's chest cracked along a seam they hadn't known was load-bearing.

They kept reading, because the entry demanded it, because the page kept giving, and near the bottom, in the same almost-loving hand, came the clause that finished the work the rest had begun:

*Amendment, appended year 12 of contract: PROTECTION EXTENDED TO ALL BOUNDARY SETTLEMENTS OF THE VALLEY PROVINCE, per renegotiation, depositor deceased, consideration unchanged.*

Depositor deceased. Their mother was dead. Had been dead for nineteen years, and Sebastian had not known, had not been able to know, because knowing would have meant leaving the valley, and leaving the valley meant the mark, and the mark meant the trackers, and so Sebastian had hidden twelve years inside the protection their mother bought with their soul, inside a wall paid for with the coin of their own self, and now—

Now the wall was coming down. Now the protection expired at solstice, weeks away, and every settlement on the boundary—Aldermoor, Millbrook, the ferry crossing, the little farms under the ridge, every place that had slept easy for thirty-one years believing itself blessed—would wake on the far side of solstice with no wall at all, in a province where the old gods were waking too, where magic pooled and hunted, where every power that had eyed the valley's strange silence for three decades would find the door standing open.

Their mother hadn't sold a soul. She'd sold a *town*. A whole valley full of people who had never signed anything, who had never been asked, whose safety had been mortgaged against a child's self without their knowledge or consent—and the interest had come due quietly, year after year, and the principal came due at solstice, and nobody knew. Nobody but the Queen of the Long Winter, keeping ledgers older than gods, waiting for the gates to close with everything squared away and the valley delivered naked into the dark.

Sebastian became aware, distantly, that they were shaking. The lectern hummed faintly under their palms, warm as a sleeping animal.

"You may lodge a contest," the clerk said. It had drifted nearer without moving; that was the trick of clerks, Odile had warned. "Contested debts are read aloud at the closing ceremony. Any soul present may stand surety against a contested line." Another pause, longer. "Few do. Surety is expensive."

"How expensive?"

"That," said the clerk, "depends on the debt."

Sebastian looked down at the page. At their own name, wearing another name like a coat. At Mateo's line, *pending settlement*, and thought: *I came here to steal back one soul. One. Mine.* And the ledger had just shown them the true size of the theft required—not one soul but a valley's worth of borrowed safety, not one wound but a province's, and the gates closing in seventeen days whether anyone stood surety or not.

Somewhere above them, muffled by a mountain's weight of stone, the wind changed key. Winter settling in for the long dark. The solstice clock already running.

"I want a copy of my entry," Sebastian said. Their voice had stopped shaking. It had gone somewhere past shaking, into a flat cold country where the mercenary lived, the one who planned ambushes and counted exits. "All of it. Every clause, every amendment, every note."

"Copies are not free."

"Bill me."

The clerk's grey hands moved, and the sound they made was the drip from far below, and Sebastian understood suddenly, completely, what the drip was. Not water. Ink. Somewhere in this court, always, a pen was touching paper, writing someone's life into a column. It had been writing theirs since before they could talk.

As the copy took form—a slip of skin-pale parchment, curling like a living thing—Sebastian asked the question they'd carried down the mountain, the one they'd sworn in the chapel they wouldn't ask, because asking told the court you cared, and caring was leverage:

"My mother," they said. "Marisol Reyes. Is there an entry for her?"

The clerk considered. The holes of its eyes gave nothing back.

"There is," it said. "Would you like to read it?"

Every instinct Sebastian owned said no. Say no. Walk away. You came for facts, not wounds; you've got the wound that matters; don't hand them another knife.

"Yes," said Felix, debtor, unlucky, liar.

The clerk filed forward and turned the great book's pages backward, backward, backward—thirty-one years of other people's bargains flickering past like birds—and stopped, and presented the page, and stepped away with the courtesy of a thing that has done this ten thousand times and knows exactly what it is handing people.

The entry was short. Debts of the dead usually are; the court doesn't waste ink on settled accounts.

**REYES, MARISOL. Asset: none remaining. Consideration rendered in full. Status: PAID.**

Paid. Two words, and beneath them, in smaller script, the last line of her life as the court recorded it:

*Final payment tendered: one (1) memory, self-selected. Selected memory: the face of her children.*

She had paid the last of herself to forget their faces. To make the ending clean, maybe. To make it bearable. Or perhaps the court had required it—collateral surrendered whole, no sentimental attachments outstanding—and perhaps she had chosen which memory to give the way you choose which limb the surgeon takes, and she had walked back down the mountain light by one soul and one memory, and she had looked at her surviving child afterward and seen a stranger, and Sebastian had spent their whole childhood wondering why their mother's eyes went distant when they laughed.

*Weeping is not deductible.*

Sebastian closed their eyes. Stood in the Reading Room with the pale light falling and the ink-smell thick as incense and let the grief come through, all of it, the twelve years of it and the thirty-one years of it, and did not make a sound, because the clerk was watching, and in this court even tears were transactions, and Sebastian Reyes was done paying this place anything they didn't owe.

When they opened their eyes, the plan was already forming, cold and complete, the way plans do in the country past shaking.

Not a theft of one soul. A theft of the ledger. A contest, read aloud, at the closing ceremony, with the whole court watching—and someone willing to stand surety against every line. Seventeen days. Odile would know the ceremony law; Tomas would know the gates; Mateo—Mateo was *pending*, and pending meant reachable, and reachable meant useful, and Sebastian hated themselves a little for thinking in those terms and kept thinking anyway, because the alternative was kneeling in this room forever.

They took the copy of their entry. It curled in their palm like something newborn.

"One more question," Sebastian said at the door. "The protection clause. When the ledger closes—who collects? Who walks into the valley?"

The clerk's thumbprint face inclined.

"The Queen," it said, "keeps what she is owed. And the Queen, debtor Felix, is owed a valley."

* * *

Odile was waiting in the mine gallery where they'd left her, hood back, prayer-scraps glowing faintly around her like moths. She read Sebastian's face in one glance, the way she read everything—one glance, total recall, a mind trained since childhood to catch the exact word where a lie begins.

"It's worse than we thought," she said. Not a question.

"It's worse than anyone thought." Sebastian held out the parchment copy, watched her unroll it, watched her lips move through the clauses, watched her go still at the amendment—the whole valley, the whole province, protection expiring—and then go stiller at Mateo's line. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious, which was Odile's way of being kind.

"They never knew," she said. "Your townsfolk. Your ferryman's passengers. None of them ever signed."

"None of them were ever asked."

"And the Queen means to collect at solstice with the gates shutting behind her." Odile rolled the copy tight, handed it back, and pressed Sebastian's fingers closed around it, both her hands around their fist, a priestess's blessing shaped like a debt collector's grip. "Then we don't steal your soul, Felix. We steal the *book*."

From the shadows at the gallery's edge, where the firelight didn't reach, a voice like wind through a doorway: Mateo, patient, six years old and older than the mountain, saying the thing he'd been waiting thirty-one years to say.

"Told you," his ghost said. "Told you she loved us. Read it again."

And below them, far below, the drip of the pen went on.
