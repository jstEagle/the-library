## 14. The Daughter Below

The ledgers of the court beneath the mountain were kept in a hall that had no ceiling, or rather, whose ceiling was the mountain itself, so far overhead that the lamplight died before it reached the stone, and the dark up there was not empty but *full*—full of the weight of everything pressed down upon it, all the years and all the debts, leaning close to listen to the scratch of Ilsa Bell's pen.

Tomas knew this because he stood in the doorway of that hall for the better part of an hour without being seen, and in that hour he learned more about his daughter than he had learned in the eleven years since he'd last held her hand crossing the ferry dock.

She sat at a desk of black wood that seemed grown rather than built, its legs rooted into the floor like the roots of some pale tree. She had grown too. She'd been nine when her mother took her below—nine years old, with a gap where her front tooth had come out and a habit of counting things under her breath when she was nervous, fence posts, river stones, the strokes of her oar. She was twenty now, and there was no gap in her smile because she wasn't smiling, and she still counted under her breath, though what she counted now, as her pen moved down column after column, was names.

"Vess Marrow," she said softly, to no one, to the ledger. "Sold for two casks of spring wine and a promise of fair winds. Term: ninety-nine years." The pen scratched. "Debt current. Interest accruing."

There were hundreds of them. Thousands. The hall stretched back farther than any hall had a right to stretch, desk after desk after desk, each one lit by a lamp that burned without oil, and at every desk a figure bent over its work—some of them living, like Ilsa, pale as mushrooms from their years without sun; some of them not living at all, but not dead either, souls on loan to themselves, working off their own entries with hands gone translucent at the edges.

Tomas's own name was in one of those books. He knew it the way you know a scar is there without looking: three favors owed to the court beneath the mountain, incurred the winter his wife had been dying and the ferry wouldn't run and a gentleman in grey had appeared out of the snowfall offering passage for a price that didn't have numbers in it. He'd signed. He'd carried people across the valley line ever since, smuggling them out of the silence, and told himself it balanced something, though nothing had ever balanced, and now the first favor had come due and he'd spent it warning Sebastian Reyes instead of delivering them, and the second was coming due like weather.

But none of that was why he stood shaking in the doorway.

He stood shaking because his daughter was alive, and he had spent eleven years believing otherwise.

* * *

They had told him she was dead. That was the shape of it—the court did not lie, exactly; the court simply let you believe whatever cost you least to believe. When the collectors came the first time, five years after Marta had taken Ilsa below "for her health," they had brought him a small item wrapped in oilcloth: a child's boot, leather, worn at the heel. No body. No explanation. Only the boot and a collector's flat voice saying, *The account is settled,* and Tomas had buried the boot on the island in the middle of the river because there was nowhere else to put a grief that size, and he had grieved her every winter since, and every winter the grief had frozen a little deeper and thawed a little less.

And here she was. Counting names. Alive, whole, her hair braided back in the severe style of the court's servants, ink to the second knuckle of her right hand, a lamp burning beside her that threw no shadow—or that threw a shadow, but the shadow belonged to someone older, someone he didn't know, and it moved a half-second behind her movements like a debt following its debtor.

He must have made a sound. He didn't remember making a sound. But her pen stopped, and she said, without turning, in a voice gone cool and precise as well-water:

"If you're a petitioner, petitions are heard at the third bell. If you're a collector, the Queen's office is through the eastern arch. And if you're neither—" now she turned, and the lamp caught her face, and Tomas watched eleven years hit her the way the river hits a piling, all at once and then forever— "then you shouldn't be here, and you should go, and you should go *now*, Papa, before someone sees you."

*Ilsa.* He said it out loud. It came out broken, half a word, the rest of it drowned. He crossed the hall—he didn't remember deciding to cross it—and she rose from her desk and met him halfway, and for a moment, just a moment, she was nine again, her arms around his ribs, her face against his coat, and he could feel her counting under his breath, his heartbeat, his ribs, the buttons of his coat, one two three four, the way she'd always done when the world got too big.

Then she stepped back, and she was twenty, and a record-keeper of the Long Winter, and her eyes went past his shoulder to the doorway, to the shadows, checking, always checking.

"How," he said. His voice didn't work right. "Marta said—I buried your boot, Ilsa. I buried your *boot*. On the island. I've been—eleven years, I've been lighting a candle for you every—"

"I know about the candle." Her voice cracked on it, just barely, and then smoothed over. "One of the collectors mentioned it once. They mention things. It's how they work." She looked at her hands. "The boot was mine. They took it when Mama made her arrangement. They let you think what you'd think because a grieving man is a pliable man, Papa, and they needed the ferry running."

"Marta." He couldn't say the name properly either. "Where is your mother?"

"Gone." Flat. Final. A door closing. "Her term ended four years ago. The court keeps its promises, Papa. That's the thing nobody above ground understands. Her ninety-nine years were served—no. Wait." She closed her eyes. "I'm doing it wrong. Let me say it correctly, the way I say everything here. Mama sold *her* service, not her soul. Ninety-nine years of labor in the records office, in exchange for my care and keeping below, in perpetuity, no interest accrued against the child. She served four years of it and then her heart stopped, and the court does not extend terms for dead women, and it does not cheat widowers' daughters either. Everything she contracted for me was delivered. Every clause. To the letter."

"And you stayed."

"And I stayed." She opened her eyes, and they were Marta's eyes, brown and level and unflinching, and they dared him to say the next thing, because she already knew what the next thing was.

He said it anyway. "You could have come home. When her term—when she died, you could have walked out. The gates open for released servants. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a sound with no childhood left in it. "Papa. Sit down. You're going to want to sit down, and there isn't time for you to fall over."

* * *

She poured him something from a clay jug beneath her desk—tea, or what passed for tea below, hot and bitter and faintly sweet, tasting of iron the way everything below tasted of iron—and she talked while he drank, and her voice took on the cadence of the ledgers themselves, measured, itemized, each grief entered in its proper column.

"The gates don't open for released servants. Released servants are escorted to the boundary and handed to whoever holds their next obligation, and if no one holds one, they're given a choice: sign a new term, or walk out into the province with no papers, no name on any roll, no protection. Do you know what happens to the undocumented in this province in winter? Nothing good. Nothing quick, either." She wrapped both hands around her own cup. "I was ten. I'd just buried my mother in a drawer, because that's what they do here, they put you in a drawer until your term fully expires and then they file you. And a clerk—a kind one, if such a thing exists—told me the truth, which was: outside, you are nobody's. Nobody's daughter, nobody's debtor, nobody's problem. In here, I am on the rolls. My bread is accounted for. My bed is written down somewhere in a fair hand. When I am sick, there is a clause for it. When I die, there will be a drawer with my name on it, spelled correctly."

"That's not freedom, Ilsa. That's a cage with tidy bookkeeping."

"Yes," she said. "It is. And it's the only cage in the world that has never once lied to me." She set the cup down hard enough to slop. "You want me to say I stayed because I was frightened, or fooled, or broken. Some of that's true. But hear the rest of it, because you owe me the rest of it. You owe me eleven years of listening, and we both know it."

He bowed his head. The tea trembled in his cup.

"When Mama was dying up top—before, I mean, the first dying, the slow one—you remember what the winter was like. You remember the ferry frozen in, the doctor who wouldn't cross, the church that prayed for us and sent nothing. I was six. I remember a man from the valley council coming to our door to collect the mooring fee while Mama coughed blood into a rag, and I remember you paying it, because you always paid it, because that's what men like you do, they pay, and pay, and pay, and the world takes it and takes it and never writes anything down." Her voice shook now, properly shook, and she let it. "The court wrote it down. That's all. That's the whole of it, Papa. The court *wrote it down*. Every promise anyone ever made me below, it's in a book, in a fair hand, and it has been kept. Kept to the letter. Kept past the point where keeping it hurt the court's own interests. Do you know how many promises anyone topside ever kept for me? I can count them. I count everything. There were none."

The lamps burned. Somewhere far back in the hall, a translucent soul turned a page with fingers you could see the lamplight through.

"So yes," Ilsa said, quieter. "I stayed willingly. I stay willingly. I keep the Queen's records because the Queen's records are the only honest thing I have ever known, and I hate that, and I hate that I hate it, and I will hate it until the day they file me in my drawer. But do not stand there with your candle-oil and your island grave and tell me I was stolen. I was *kept*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole world, and nobody up there has ever once understood it."

Tomas set down his cup. His hands were not steady. Eleven years of grief turned inside out does not leave a man steady; it leaves him hollowed, like a boat with the keel cut out, and everything he'd believed about his own sorrow was pouring out through the hole.

"I thought you were dead," he said. "Every day. Every day for eleven years, I thought my daughter was dead, and it was a *lie of convenience*, and they let me—"

"They let all of us," she said. "That's the other thing you need to understand, and you need to understand it fast, because I saw the manifest this morning and your name was on it, and not in the favors column." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to the register she must have used a thousand times, guiding petitioners, soothing collectors, surviving. "You came down with the mercenary. Reyes. Branded, god-touched, the one the whole mountain is humming about. And you came down owing, which means the Queen knows exactly what you are and exactly what you'll do, because the Queen has read your entry, Papa, and your entry says you warn people instead of delivering them. It's written down. In a fair hand. She's known since the ferry."

The cold that went through Tomas then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"She let us in?"

"She let you in." Ilsa's eyes flicked to the doorway again, to the dark, to the listening weight of the stone overhead. "Which means she wants something only you can carry. Or someone." Her jaw tightened. "Or she wants the mercenary to get all the way to the solstice ceremony believing they've slipped the leash. There's a reading of the debts at solstice. Every entry, aloud, before the gates close. It hasn't been contested in four hundred years, because contesting requires standing surety, and surety costs—" she stopped herself. Recounted, under her breath, one two three four. Then, very carefully: "It costs what it costs. And the Queen believes no one alive would pay it."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe the Queen is usually right about people." Ilsa looked at him then, full on, and for a moment the clerk fell away and there was only his daughter, six years old at a door with a fee collector on the other side of it, learning early what the world wrote down and what it didn't. "But she's never been right about me. And she might not be right about you."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." She was already turning back to her desk, already squaring her pages, becoming the record-keeper again, armor going on piece by piece. "Don't ask me yet. Don't ask me to choose in a hallway with the walls listening. Come back at the third bell tomorrow, when the changeover comes and the hall clears. Come alone. And Papa—" her pen hovered, and her voice, for one unguarded instant, was small, "—the candle. Keep lighting it. Not for the dead. Just—keep it. I like knowing it's there."

Then the pen scratched, and she was counting again, names and names and names, and Tomas Bell walked out of the hall of ledgers with his heart in pieces and one of them, at last, beating.

* * *

He found Odile waiting in the alcove where they'd agreed to split up, the priestess tucked into the shadow of a prayer-niche with her hood up, looking like a woman trying to be furniture. Behind her, Sebastian leaned against the wall with their arms crossed, the brand on their wrist wrapped in linen, watching the corridor with the flat patience of a person who had spent twelve years expecting the worst and budgeting for it.

"Well?" Sebastian said.

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Eleven years of words crowded there—*she's alive, she hates me, she's right, she stayed, she chose it, she chose it, God help me, she chose it*—and what came out, finally, was the thing that mattered most, the thing that would turn the whole plan on its head if it was true, and the look on Ilsa's face said it was true.

"She's coming with us," he said. "At the third bell tomorrow. And Sebastian—she says the Queen's known we were here since the ferry. Since the *ferry*. This whole descent, every step we've thought we stole—" he laughed, and it came out ragged, "—we've been walking down a page she already wrote. The reading of the debts at solstice. The Queen doesn't think anyone will stand surety."

Odile went very still. "And does your daughter think someone will?"

Tomas thought of the candle on the island, still burning in his mind's eye, eleven winters tall. Thought of a girl counting his heartbeat through his coat. Thought of a drawer with her name on it, spelled correctly, waiting.

"She thinks," he said, "that the Queen's never been right about a Bell yet."

Sebastian pushed off the wall, and somewhere far above them, deep in the mountain's throat, the first bell of the changeover began to toll—slow, iron, final, one stroke closer to solstice.
