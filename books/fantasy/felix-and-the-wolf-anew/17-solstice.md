## 17. Solstice

### Odile

The gates of the court beneath the mountain were not doors. They were a wound in the world, stitched shut with light.

Odile stood among the assembled debtors and servants and soul-bearers on the great stair of the under-hall, and she watched the solstice begin. Above the Queen's dais, two slabs of white stone—each taller than a chapel tower—had begun to grind together, slow as glaciers, patient as winter. Between them, the last open seam of sky showed a thread of grey: the memory of the surface, the last daylight the deep would see until spring.

The sound they made was almost gentle. A long exhalation. Stone remembering it could move.

Around her, thousands bowed their heads as the ceremony opened. Odile did not bow. She had spent eleven years learning to keep her mouth quiet, her eyes downcast, her hands folded in the posture of a woman who had surrendered her will to something larger. She knew exactly how obedience looked from the inside. She also knew, now, what it cost—and that the price was never paid only once.

*The ledger must be read aloud,* Sebastian had said, three nights ago, in the drowned chapel's echo. *Every debt. That's the law even here—the Queen keeps her ledgers honest because honesty is her armor. And a read debt can be contested.*

*By anyone willing to stand surety,* Ilsa had added, and had not looked at her father when she said it.

*Anyone,* Sebastian had said. *Even me.*

Odile had prayed, then, for the first time since the sermon that broke her life open. Not to the Wolf. Not to any god. She had prayed the way she imagined a soldier prays before a wall must be climbed: briefly, without decoration, with everything she had.

*Let my voice hold.*

The herald's bell rang—a sound like a coin dropped down a well—and the reading began.

A clerk in grey mounted the lower step and unrolled the first scroll. His voice was flat, beautiful, terrible: the voice of arithmetic made flesh.

*"Debt the first. Held against the estate of Marisol Reyes, deceased. Collateral: one soul, branded at birth, value assessed—"*

Odile stopped breathing.

Sebastian stood three rows ahead of her, hood back, brand bare to the hall. Every eye would find them soon enough. But the clerk read on, name after name, debt after debt, and Odile understood with a lurch that this was the horror none of them had fully imagined: there were *hundreds*. Whole families sold in fragments. Children entered as line items. A village's protection purchased with forty-one souls, itemized like sacks of grain.

She found Tomas in the crowd. He was white as the stone above them, his lips moving—not prayer, she realized, but counting. Counting names.

The gates groaned another handspan closed. The seam of sky narrowed to a wire.

*Not yet,* Odile told herself. *Wait for the moment when the whole ledger lies open. Wait until every debt is named and nothing can be quietly amended. Then stand up.*

Her former sisters were somewhere in this hall. She could feel them the way one feels weather coming—three presences of trained silence, the Order of the Quiet Mouth, come to collect their heretic or their mercenary, whichever proved easier. Let them come. Let them watch. If silencing gods was holy work, then today she intended to commit the loudest blasphemy of her life.

*"Debt the thirty-ninth,"* the clerk intoned. *"Held against the ferryman Bell, Tomas. Three favors owed. Two expended. One remaining."*

Tomas flinched as though struck.

And the Queen, on her throne of black ice and older bone, turned her head and smiled at him.

* * *

### Tomas

He had spent his final favor getting them through the third gate, and he had known—he had *known*, signing the writ with a hand that shook—that the moment the ledger read his name aloud, the Queen would call it due in front of everyone, where refusal meant ruin and acceptance meant betrayal, and either way he would be spent.

"Come forward, ferryman," the Queen said. Her voice did not carry; it simply arrived, already inside your skull, warm as a hearth you'd been invited into and couldn't leave. "Your last favor. You may spend it as you choose."

The whole hall turned to look at him. Thousands of faces. Somewhere among them, Ilsa, at her writing-desk beside the ledger-stand, quill poised, face composed into the smooth serviceable blankness she wore like armor. She wouldn't look at him. She hadn't looked at him once in nine days, and he deserved it.

Tomas walked forward. The stones were warm under his boots, which was wrong, which was the worst thing about this place—everything below was warm, cozy, lit gold, while the world above froze. Hell should not feel like home. That was the trap of it.

"I choose," Tomas said, and his voice cracked, and he hated himself for it, and kept going anyway, because Ilsa was watching now, he could feel her watching, and by every god dead and living he would not be small in front of her twice. "I choose to spend it on myself."

Murmurs. The Queen's smile widened by a degree.

"I ask the court," Tomas said, louder, "to release me from all further obligation. My debt is paid. I am free of the mountain, forever, and no favor of mine can ever be called again."

Silence.

Then the Queen laughed—a low, delighted sound, like ice settling on a lake. "Oh, *clever*. Spend the favor buying freedom from favors. Yes, ferryman, that is within the letter of the law." She rose from her throne, tall as a doorway, crowned in frost that did not melt. "You are free. And so very foolish. Because your daughter's contract is not yours to buy. Her seven years of service are her own."

Ilsa's quill stopped moving.

"And," the Queen continued, turning, letting her gaze sweep the hall like a lantern, "since the ferryman is now beyond obligation, he may do nothing—*nothing*—when I call the next name. Debt the fortieth. The branded mercenary. Come forward, little wolf-chosen, and let us conclude our business before the gates close."

The seam of sky above was a knife-cut now. Thin. Closing.

And Sebastian Reyes stepped out of the crowd with their chin up and their brand burning visible gold, and Odile Vane stood up beside them, and the hall went quiet in a way Tomas had never heard a hall go quiet—as if the mountain itself were holding its breath.

* * *

### Ilsa

Nine days ago, her father had looked at her across a desk of black ice and said, "Come home," and she had said, "This *is* home," and meant it, mostly, and hated how much of it was true.

The court kept its promises. That was the whole of her heresy, the thing she could never make him understand. Up on the surface, promises melted like snow in rain. Down here, a contract was a contract was a contract, and no one had ever gone hungry in the Queen's service, and no one had ever lied to her about coming back.

But she had read the ledgers. All of them. It was her work, and she was good at her work, and being good at your work means seeing what the work is *for*.

Forty-one souls bought a valley's protection. Forty-one souls, and the ledger showed the protection expiring at solstice regardless—because the Queen had never promised the valley anything permanent. The town's elders thought they held a covenant. They held a lease. And at solstice, with the gates sealed and the debtors locked below until spring, the Queen would renegotiate from a position of perfect strength, and the forty-one souls would become forty-one *leverages*, and the valley would learn what its safety had always cost.

Ilsa had decided, somewhere around the ninth day, sitting in the gold-warm dark with her father's face haunting her, that she would rather be her father's daughter than her Queen's record-keeper. She had just needed to be sure. Sure took time. Sure looked, from the outside, exactly like betrayal.

Now Sebastian stood before the throne, and Odile stood beside them, and the gates were a hand's width from sealing, and the clerk unrolled the final scroll.

"The full ledger is read," the clerk announced. "All debts named. The ceremony concludes—"

"No," Ilsa said.

It came out small. She made it larger. She stood, and her chair scraped against the stone, and four thousand heads turned, and she felt the sound of it in her chest like a struck bell.

"No," she said again. "The ledger is not fully read. There is an entry omitted. There has *always* been an entry omitted."

The Queen's warmth turned, very slightly, cold. "Record-keeper."

"Article Nine of the Founding Contracts," Ilsa said, and her voice did not shake, because she had rehearsed this in the dark of her cell every night for nine nights, alone, whispering it to the walls. "Any entry in the ledger may be annotated by the keeper of records. Any annotation becomes part of the public reading. I am the keeper of records. And I have annotated."

She lifted the master ledger—it weighed nothing in her arms, it had never weighed anything, that was the trick of souls, they were lighter than paper—and she opened it to the page she had marked, and she read her own handwriting aloud in a clear voice that carried to the highest gallery:

*"Annotation to Debt the First: the collateral of Marisol Reyes was accepted not as payment but as surety, renewable annually, at the sole discretion of the Crown. The valley's protection was never purchased. It was rented. The lessor reserved the right of recall. This reservation appears nowhere in the covenant copy held by the town of—"*

"*Enough.*"

The Queen's voice hit the hall like a door slamming. Frost bloomed across the floor between them, racing toward the dais where Ilsa stood. But the law of the court was older than the Queen—everyone below knew it, whispered it, trusted it—and the law said a read thing could not be unread.

In the sudden ringing silence, Odile Vane stepped forward, lifted her head, and said, in the carrying voice of a woman who had once preached to a thousand:

"I contest the ledger."

### Sebastian

Later, Sebastian would remember the contest itself strangely—as if seen through water. Odile's voice naming the law. The Queen's lawyers rising like crows off a fence. The gates grinding down to a slit of grey light, and the whole glittering underworld balanced on the edge of its own oldest rule: *a read debt can be contested by anyone willing to stand surety.*

What Sebastian remembered clearly—what they would carry out of the dark and into the rest of their life—was Mateo.

Their brother stood at the edge of the crowd, half-transparent in the gold light, the way he had stood at the edge of everything for twelve years. Bound still to the soul-debt, not yet freed, flickering like a candle in a draft. And when Odile spoke the words of contest, Mateo smiled. Not sadly. Proudly. The way he used to smile when Sebastian came home with a scraped knee and a story that made the scrape worth it.

*Do it,* his ghost-mouth said. No sound. There never was. *Whatever it costs. Do it, little sister. I'll hold the door.*

"You cannot both contest and stand surety," the Queen said, and her warmth was entirely gone now, and the hall was growing colder by the breath, frost climbing the pillars, the crowd pressing backward. "The law requires a surety willing to pledge something of equal weight to the debts named. Forty-one souls, mercenary. What could you possibly hold—"

"The Wolf's claim on me," Sebastian said.

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere far above, through the closing seam, a wolf's voice—or something shaped like one—began, very softly, to sing.

"My god's protection. Their mark upon me. Their promise that no power in this world may claim what they have chosen." Sebastian's voice was steady. Inside, they were twelve years old and kneeling at their mother's bedside, hearing her say *it's for the best, mija, it's for all of us.* "That is what I hold. It is the only thing I've ever held that was truly mine. And I pledge it—all of it—for every soul in this ledger. Void the debts. Free the names. Take the protection instead."

"You would be naked," the Queen said softly. "Unclaimed. Unprotected. Every hunter, every collector, every hungry thing that walks will smell you on the wind, and no god will answer you again."

"Yes," Sebastian said.

Mateo was nodding. Odile had her hand over her mouth. Tomas stood with tears freezing in his beard and didn't wipe them away.

The Wolf's song grew louder. It did not sound like grief. It sounded—Sebastian searched for the word and found it, and it undid them more than any threat the Queen had made—it sounded like *permission*.

"Then let it be entered," the Queen said, and for the first time in her long reign beneath the mountain, the Queen of the Long Winter sounded afraid. "Strike the ledger. Transfer the surety. Open the—"

The gates chose that moment to finish closing.

They did not slam. They sighed shut, the way winter sighs over a valley, and the last thread of daylight went out, and the great hall plunged into a darkness broken only by the gold of Sebastian's burning brand and the blue-white flare of the Queen's crown.

And in that darkness, from the direction of the sealed gates, came the sound of claws on stone—

—and a voice that was not quite Mateo's, and not quite the Wolf's, saying cheerfully into the black:

"Well. Someone has to hold the door."
