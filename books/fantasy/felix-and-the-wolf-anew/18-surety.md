## 18. Surety

The hall of ledgers had gone quiet in the way a forest goes quiet before something large moves through it.

Sebastian stood at the center of the reading floor with the great book open before them on its lectern of black stone, and the silence pressed against their ears like deep water. Above, the cavern ceiling glittered with veins of ore that caught the light of ten thousand prayer-candles and threw it back broken, so that the whole court beneath the mountain seemed to be made of frost and gold held together by nothing at all. The Queen of the Long Winter sat upon her throne of salt-white bone at the far end of the floor, and she was not looking at Sebastian. She was looking at Odile.

Odile stood with her burned vows still smoking faintly at her feet—a curl of grey ash where her order's covenant had been, sacrificed to the contest-stone not an hour past. Her voice had carried. That was the miracle of it; Sebastian kept returning to it, turning it over like a coin they could not believe was real. In a hall built to swallow sound, in front of a Queen whose patience was older than rivers, Odile Vane had lifted her chin and read the ledger's own law aloud: *A debt read publicly may be contested by any who stand surety.*

And then she had said, clear as a bell over ice: "I contest it."

The Queen had smiled the way winter smiles—without warmth, without malice, only vastness—and had turned the page.

"Then let us see," she had said, "who among you can pay for what you wish to unmake."

* * *

The ledger did not want to be read. Sebastian felt it even now, standing close enough to touch the vellum. The book breathed. Its pages moved like the flanks of a sleeping animal, and every entry written there was a soul, and every soul was warm, and the warmth pulled at Sebastian's chest like a hook set behind the sternum.

Their own entry lay open under their hands.

*Reyes, S.—taken in trade, twelfth year of the long count. Collateral against the protection of the valley called Hollowmere. Term: until the ledger closes.*

Below it, in the same patient hand, the other names. Forty-one of them. Not just Sebastian. The whole valley, sold in one stroke by a desperate woman kneeling in the snow with two children—one already dead, one dying of a fever no herb in Hollowmere could touch—and a god who would not answer. Their mother had gone down into the mountain with Mateo's body wrapped in her shawl and had traded everything that could be traded, and the court had accepted, because the court always accepted, because acceptance was what it was *for*.

Twelve years Sebastian had believed themselves the price. One soul for one life saved. It had been almost bearable, framed that way. A fair bargain. A wound with clean edges.

It had never been one soul. It had been forty-two, and a valley, and a term of years that ended this very night, at solstice, when the gates closed and the ledger closed and everything the Queen held became hers without appeal, forever, sealed until spring—and spring, everyone in this hall knew, was a word the Queen used the way a fisherman uses bait.

Forty-one names besides their own. Sebastian read them again, slowly, letting each one land. The miller's daughter who had drowned the year after the bargain and never known why her luck went bad. The ferryman's father. A child born the same winter Sebastian had nearly died, marked from birth with a stillness that made dogs uneasy. Names without faces, mostly. It didn't matter. They were warm, all of them, and the warmth was being counted down to nothing.

"The contest stands," the Queen said now, and her voice filled the cavern the way cold fills a valley, from the top down. "But a contest is not a voiding. Law is older than I am, little thief, and the law is simple." She rose from the throne of bone, and the light bent around her the way light bends around deep water. "Every debt in this ledger must have a surety. Someone must promise payment—not coin, not favor, not prayer. Payment of self. For each name. All at once. Or the reading is a noise, and the gates close, and we are done here."

She descended the steps. Each footfall made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than thunder.

"Do you understand what you have done, priestess?" the Queen asked, stopping before Odile, looking down at her with something that might have been curiosity in a smaller creature. "You have invited someone to buy forty-two souls. Do you know the going rate?"

"I know what faith costs," Odile said. Her voice did not shake. Her hands, at her sides, were white-knuckled, but her voice did not shake. "I paid it once. I'd know the sum again anywhere."

The Queen's gaze slid past her, found Tomas at the edge of the crowd of courtiers—courtiers, Sebastian thought, things wearing the shapes of people, things that had been people once perhaps—and then found Ilsa beside him, the record-keeper with her ink-stained fingers and her father's stubborn jaw, holding the secondary rolls against her chest like a shield. And then, last, the Queen looked at Sebastian.

And smiled again.

"You," she said. "Of course. You came all this way. You crossed my winter, you spent the ferryman's favors, you burned the priestess's vows, you read your mother's shame aloud in my hall. You want your soul back." She drifted closer, and up close she smelled of snowmelt and old paper and, faintly, unbearably, of the fever-smell of that long-ago sickroom. "Here is my mercy, mercenary. Take it. Walk out tonight with your own name cleared, and I will let the valley keep its protection through spring after all. One soul returned, one town spared, and all these others—" she gestured at the breathing ledger, gently, fondly, "—were lost before you ever knew their names. No one can call you cruel. Your mother made the same arithmetic and history will call her a saint."

Behind Sebastian, somewhere in the press of bodies, Mateo made a small sound. Not a word. He hadn't managed words since the chapel, not in front of others. But Sebastian knew that sound. It was the sound he'd made at nine years old when their mother had told them there wasn't enough bread for both of them and had put the larger half on Sebastian's plate.

*Take it,* the sound said. *Please. Just this once, take the larger half.*

Sebastian looked at the ledger.

They had come down the mountain ready to steal. That had been the whole shape of it in their head for months—thieves' work, mercenaries' work, get in, take the thing, get out. They had imagined the moment of taking their soul back the way you imagine drinking cold water after a long march: simple, physical, a matter of reaching.

But souls were not taken in this place. Souls were *paid for*. And the Queen had just shown them the scale, and the scale did not balance with one name on one side.

There was only one thing Sebastian Reyes held that was worth forty-one souls.

They had known it since the frozen chapel, if they were honest, since the Wolf had spoken plainly through the dark and the drowning water and said *reach the court before the gates close*, and Sebastian had understood, in some cellar of themselves too honest for daylight, that the god's protection was the only currency they carried. Every tracker in the province had been able to find them since they crossed the boundary line because the mark of the soul-debt showed on their skin—but underneath the mark, underneath everything, there was the other claim. The older one. The god's claim. Twelve years of silence, twelve years of prayers thrown into a well that gave back nothing, and then the Wolf had come, and the coming had changed them, marked them, *kept* them. Nothing in the world could collect on Sebastian while the Wolf's claim stood. Every power hunting the newly woken divine had learned that much and learned it bitterly.

It was the only thing they held. And it was not theirs to spend.

That was the thought that stopped them, hand hovering over the vellum. The protection was a gift. You did not sell gifts. You did not spend your father's name to settle your debts—

But that was exactly what a household was, said another voice, quieter, older, wearing Mateo's cadence though it came from nowhere outside their own ribs. That's what family means. What's held in common covers what's owed in common.

* * *

"Before I answer," Sebastian said, and their voice came out rougher than they intended, so they started again. "Before I answer, I want to ask the ledger a question."

The Queen tilted her head. Around the hall, the courtiers stirred like reeds. "The ledger does not answer questions."

"It answers readings," Sebastian said. "You said so yourself. A read debt may be contested. So read me this: what constitutes surety? Name the terms precisely. Ilsa—" and they saw the girl straighten, quick as a hawk, "—the secondary rolls. Read the definition."

Ilsa was already moving, unrolling the second scroll across a side table, her finger racing down columns of cramped script. "Surety," she read, and her voice rang off the ore-veined dark, "is the pledge of a thing of equal weight to the debt contested, freely given, freely accepted, and *freely released*—" she paused, frowned, read it again, "—freely released by the pledger's patron, should the pledger hold claims not their own."

The hall went very still.

"Patron," the Queen repeated softly.

"I hold a claim on this mortal," said a voice, and the whole cavern changed.

Sebastian had felt the Wolf before as pressure, as presence, as the sense of yellow eyes at the edge of the firelight. This was different. This was the god arriving *in* the world, in the one place magic had been locked out for twelve years, because a ledger-hall was a place of contract, and contract was its own kind of magic, older than the valley's silence, older than the Queen. The candles did not gutter. They leaned. Ten thousand flames bowed toward the center of the floor like grass under wind, and the shadows of every courtier stretched long and wrong, and out of the gathered dark at the edge of the reading floor came the smell of pine and blood-warm fur and the first snow of a season that meant to stay.

The Wolf did not take full shape. It didn't need to. There was a suggestion of shoulders higher than a man's, eyes like the space between stars, breath that fogged the air though the air had been warm as a hearth a moment before. The courtiers drew back. Even the Queen, Sebastian noted—even the Queen took half a step toward her throne.

"You," she said. "You're supposed to be sleeping. You've slept for an age. All of you."

"One of us woke," the Wolf said. Its voice was not loud. It was simply true, the way stone is hard. "One of us heard a prayer that had been falling down a dry well for twelve years, and climbed down the well to answer it. Late." The great head turned, and those star-gap eyes settled on Sebastian, and Sebastian's whole body wanted to kneel and their whole life refused to, and the tension between those two facts was, they realized, the truest feeling they had ever known. "Late," the Wolf said again, softer. "Forgive me. I was asleep, and the world sold you while I dreamed."

"I'm not angry," Sebastian said, and found it was true. "I stopped being angry in the chapel. I'm just—" They looked down at the ledger. At the forty-one warm names. "I need to spend your claim on me. All of it. As surety. For every soul in this book."

"No," Mateo said.

Everyone turned. The ghost stood at the edge of the candlelight, more solid than he had ever let himself be, his dead boy's face fierce, his hands fisted at his sides. "No," he said again, and this time the word came out whole, his first whole word in twelve years, spent on this. "Seb. Don't. Find another way. There's always—"

"There isn't," Sebastian said gently. "You know there isn't. You've read the rolls. You've been following me for years, Mateo—you know how these accounts work better than I do." They turned back to the Wolf. To the eyes like winter stars. "It has to be given freely, and it has to be released freely. By my patron. So I'm asking. Not commanding—I couldn't command you if I tried. Asking. Let go of me. Spend me. Buy them."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, ore ticked and settled in the warming rock. The Queen watched with her head slightly inclined, and Sebastian understood suddenly that she had offered the private deal—their soul for the others, the valley protected forever—because she had expected exactly this, and she had wanted to watch it happen. Cruelty, at her age, was probably mostly curation.

The Wolf came closer. Close enough now that Sebastian could feel the heat of it, absurd in this cold country, a heat like a body slept against in a blizzard. The great head lowered until its breath stirred Sebastian's hair.

"You understand what you are asking," the god said quietly. "Not death. Worse than death, sometimes. You will walk out of here unprotected. Every hunger, every hunt, every old grudge of the waking world will be able to reach you, and none of them will find me in the way. You will be only a person. Mortal, breakable, owed to no one and claimed by no one. Some souls cannot bear being only a person."

"I've been only a person for twelve years," Sebastian said. "It's the being *claimed* that's been killing me. Everyone's claimed me. My mother, your court down here, your order—" a glance at Odile, who had the grace to wince, "—everyone but me. Let this be the one claim I give away instead of lose."

The Wolf was silent a long moment. Then it did something no one in the hall expected, least of all Sebastian. It laughed—a low, rolling sound like distant thunder over a summer range, and there was grief in it, and pride, and something so tired and so tender that Sebastian's throat closed.

"My mother did this," Sebastian whispered. "Sold a town to save her children. And you're about to—"

"Yes," the Wolf said. "It is the oldest story there is, little thief. The strong giving away their strength for the weak, and calling it love, and being right to call it love." The star-gap eyes blinked, slow. "I accept the loss. Hear me, ledger. Hear me, Queen. The claim of the Wolf upon the mortal called Sebastian Reyes is released—freely, wholly, and gladly—into surety for the forty-one souls named in this book, and for the valley their lives were pledged against. Weigh it. You will find it heavy enough."

The ledger *shuddered*. Sebastian felt it through their palms, a great convulsion like a beast turning in its sleep, and the pages began to turn themselves, faster and faster, forty-one entries flickering past, and on each page, as it passed, the warm name dimmed and faded and the ink went grey and then blank, and the breathing of the book slowed, slowed, slowed—

"Stop this," the Queen said, and for the first time in twelve years, or a hundred, or an age, her voice was not vast. It was sharp. It was *hurried*. "Ledger, I forbid—"

"The ledger hears its law," Ilsa said clearly, and slammed the secondary roll down flat on the reading table. "Freely given. Freely released. *Weighed and entered.*"

The last page turned. The book closed itself with a sound like a door.

And the mark on Sebastian's skin—the soul-debt brand they had carried since crossing the boundary, the thing every tracker in the province could see—cooled, and faded, and was gone. What remained underneath was nothing at all. No claim. No protection. Just skin, and a heartbeat, and the enormous vertiginous lightness of a person belonging to no one.

They swayed. A hand caught their elbow—Odile, steady-eyed, smelling of woodsmoke and burnt vows—and on their other side, impossibly, solidly, Mateo gripped their sleeve, and for one moment the ghost had enough weight to hold his brother upright.

The Queen stood alone on the reading floor amid ten thousand leaning candles, and the gates of her court, far above, had begun to grind open instead of shut.

"This is not finished," she said.

"No," Sebastian agreed. Their voice was hoarse and their legs were shaking and they had never in their life felt so poor or so rich. "But it's not yours anymore."

The Wolf was already fading, drawn back out of the contract-country by the same law that had let it in, and as it thinned into shadow and pine-smell it touched its muzzle, brief as a blessing, to the crown of Sebastian's head.

"Live small," the god said. "Live free. Grow strange. I will find you in the spring, if you'll have me—not as a claim. As a guest."

Then it was gone, and the candles stood upright, and somewhere overhead, iron doors that had been closing for an age groaned against their hinges—and beyond them, in the dark of the mines, something began to run.
