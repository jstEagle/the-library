## 19. When Gods Grieve

I have been dead for twelve years, and in all that time I never once managed to say my sister's name.

Not for lack of trying. Ghosts are made of wanting, and wanting is a kind of speech, but it is a speech with no mouth behind it. For twelve years I pressed against the walls of the world like breath against a window, and everything I meant came out as frost. A candle guttering when Sebastian walked past. A door swinging wide on a night they needed shelter. The smell of our mother's bread in an empty room, once, in a bad winter, because I wanted so badly to give them something warm that the world bent a little to let me try.

They never knew it was me. How could they? They thought I was gone—gone the way the dead are supposed to go, cleanly, into whatever quiet waits past the edge of things. They carried me like a stone in the chest and never once looked down at their own shadow and saw me walking in it.

But I was there.

I was there the night they crossed the valley line and the magic came flooding back into them, and I felt the soul-debt wake on their skin like a brand being read aloud. I was there in the drowned chapel when the Wolf spoke plainly, and I was there—I am still not sure how—in the moment beneath the mountain when Sebastian stood before the Queen of the Long Winter and gave away the only thing they had left: their god's protection, surrendered, so that every soul in that glittering ledger could walk out free.

Including mine.

That is the part I keep turning over, here at the end of all things, standing in the dark of the closing gate with the Queen's cold coming up through the floor like water rising in a well. My little sibling gave up heaven for me. Gave up the Wolf's teeth at their throat and the Wolf's warmth at their back, gave up the one protection in all the world that had ever held, and did it without hesitation, without even the small cruelty of pausing first.

And now the gate is closing, and the Queen is coming, and someone has to hold the door.

* * *

Let me tell you what dying felt like, since I have time now, and no one living to trouble with it.

It felt like cold. That's the truth nobody puts in the elegies. Our mother sold us both that night—her son outright, her other child's soul as collateral—and she did it in the parlor while I slept, and the court's collectors came up through the floorboards like smoke given manners. I woke because the cold woke me. It was a cold with intention. It knew my name.

Sebastian was eleven. They were sleeping in the next room, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity of the last minute of a life: *they're next, they're next unless something stands between.* So I did the only thing a boy of fifteen can do against the court beneath the mountain. I got in the way.

It didn't work. Of course it didn't work. But it bought seconds, and in those seconds our mother—gods forgive her, gods understand her, I have had twelve years to try and I mostly do—grabbed Sebastian and ran, and the collectors took me whole instead of taking us both, and the ledger closed around my name like a fist.

I don't remember pain. I remember the door of the world staying open just long enough for me to see her running with Sebastian in her arms, and I remember thinking: *good, good, run, live,* and then the dark took me down under the mountain where souls are kept like coins in a vault, stacked and silent and spending nothing.

Twelve years in the dark. And then, tonight, a hand reached into the dark and turned the ledger open, and a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat said, *them too. All of them. Take me instead.*

* * *

The gate is iron older than iron, and it is closing the way a jaw closes—slowly enough to seem merciful, quickly enough that mercy is a lie. Behind me, the freed souls are streaming up the mineshaft toward the winter roads: hundreds of them, thousands, a river of the un-bought pouring out of the court beneath the mountain into a night full of snow. Some of them are barely more than light. Some of them still wear the shapes they died in. One old woman pauses at the threshold of the shaft and looks back at me—at me specifically, though she has never seen me before—and says, "You'll come too, lad."

"Not yet," I say.

My voice works now. That's the strangest gift of being freed. Twelve years of frost-speech and now I have a voice like anyone's, rough and young and fifteen forever, and the first real sentence I ever spoke aloud as a free soul was *not yet.*

The old woman nods like this is sensible. Maybe to the dead it is. Then she goes, up and out, and I turn back to the gate.

The Queen is coming. I can feel her the way you feel a storm change its mind—the pressure dropping, the air going thin and mineral, the taste of deep places. She does not run. Queens do not run. But her cold is fast, faster than the living, and the living are slow tonight: Odile half-carrying a soul-child who keeps flickering like a bad lamp, Tomas hauling Ilsa along by the hand while she looks back and back and back at the ledgers she is leaving burning behind her, and Sebastian—

Sebastian is last, because Sebastian is always last, because Sebastian has spent their whole life putting everyone else through doors first.

They're limping. Did you know that? Twelve years of watching from the shadow-side and I know every hurt they've ever hidden. They're limping because they gave the Wolf's protection away and the giving-away took something physical out of them, some strut of the spirit, and now they move like a person who has set down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was heavy. Free, and unsteady as a foal. Alive in a way that costs.

They will make it. They have to make it. But the Queen's cold is coming up the shaft behind them like floodwater finding a cellar, and if it touches the freed souls before they clear the boundary of the court's claim, the ledger can reach back and take them again—a debt recalled, a coin repossessed. I heard the clerks say it. Ilsa told them, gasping, on the stairs: *the recall clause, the recall clause, debts are valid within the court's demesne until formally voided at the boundary.*

So. Someone holds the door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about ghosts: we do not haunt houses. We haunt unfinished sentences.

Mine was always the same sentence. *Get them out.* Every candle I guttered, every door I opened, every loaf-smell I conjured in a bad winter—it was all one word, said over and over for twelve years into ears that couldn't hear: *out, out, get them out.* I haunted my sibling the way a lighthouse haunts a coast. Not because I wanted anything from them. Because I wanted everything *for* them.

And now the sentence is nearly finished. The souls are out—or pouring out—and Sebastian is coming up the shaft with their borrowed light and their brand-new freedom, and the Queen's cold is three turns of the stair below and rising, and I am standing in the gate with my dead hands flat against dead iron, and I find, to my enormous surprise, that I am not afraid.

Grief, yes. Grief is the water I swim in; I was born into it and I'll die—again, properly, finally—inside it. But not fear. Fear is for people who might lose something. I already lost everything once, and tonight I got it back for one hour: a voice, a name, a body made of borrowed dark, a chance to stand between my sister and the thing that took me.

One hour. It's more than most brothers get.

* * *

The cold arrives before she does.

It comes up the stairwell like a tide finding a channel, and where it passes, the torchlight bends away, and the stone wears frost in patterns like fern-leaves, like feathers, like the fine white filigree the court puts on everything it owns. I plant myself in the gateway and I feel my ghost-stuff stiffen against it, and I learn something in that moment that no one taught me: a freed soul has weight. Not flesh-weight. Debt-weight. Claim-weight. The Queen's power is the power of ownership, and I am not owned anymore, and somewhere in the arithmetic of the universe that makes me a wall.

"Step aside," says the Queen of the Long Winter.

She comes up the last stair like winter itself climbing out of a cellar—tall, gray-robed, crowned in something that isn't metal but remembers being metal, her face the kind of beautiful that a glacier is beautiful, which is to say: from far away, and briefly. Her eyes are ledger-eyes. They total you at a glance.

"No," I say.

"You are a freed asset," she says. "Technically voided. Technically outside my claim." Something moves at the corner of her mouth that in a human face would be called a smile. "But gates, little ghost, are not souls. Gates are mine. And you are standing in my gate."

"I know," I say. "That's why I picked it."

She studies me the way a clerk studies an entry that won't balance. Around us the gate grinds on, slower now, as if the mountain itself is listening. Far above, faint as a memory of sound, I hear the living: Odile's voice raised in something that is either a prayer or a curse, Tomas shouting at someone to *run, damn you, run*, and underneath it all, closer than any of it, the particular rhythm of my sibling's footsteps, limping, limping, limping toward the door I'm holding.

"Your brother died for nothing," the Queen says. "You know that. The bargain your mother made went through anyway. The valley was protected. The child was marked. Nothing you did that night changed a single entry in any book."

"That's true," I say.

Something flickers in the glacier-eyes. She expected grief to be a lever. Everyone does. They think the dead are made of regret, and regret is a handle, and handles are for pulling.

"But here's what your books don't have a column for," I say, and I press my hands flatter against the iron, and I let her see—not the boy, not the ghost, but the twelve years of frost-speech, the candles and the doors and the bread-smell, the whole long sentence of *get them out* finally reaching its final word—"nothing I did that night changed the ledger. But this isn't the ledger. This is a door. And doors are different. Doors are *chosen*."

The cold presses against me like a thumb testing fruit.

"You will scatter," she says. "Do you understand? You are newly loosed. You are barely strung together. Hold a gate against me and you will come apart like smoke in wind, and there is no ledger entry for you anymore, which means there is no return. No vault. No keeping. You will simply stop, and this time no one will have sold you or saved you or remembered you into anything. You will be *unwritten*."

"Yeah," I say. "I figured."

And here is the thing I want written down somewhere, in whatever book keeps the accounts of the dead: I mean it. All of it. There is no bitterness in me. I searched for it while the cold climbed the stairs, the way you check your pockets for a ticket, and it isn't there. What happened to me was terrible and it was done by people who loved me, which is the worst kind of terrible, and I have had twelve years in the dark to chew on it, and what I swallowed in the end was this: our mother was afraid, and fear makes small gods of big monsters, and Sebastian lived, and Sebastian lived *good*—mean and kind and stubborn and soft in the wrong places, exactly the shape of a person worth haunting for twelve years.

I don't want revenge. I want my sibling to reach the snow.

* * *

The cold pushes. I push back.

It is not a battle like the songs describe battles. It is more like two weathers arguing over one valley. The Queen spends ownership the way other beings spend gold, and I spend the only currency I have ever had, which is refusal. *No. No. No.* Twelve years of practice saying it silently; now I say it with everything I am.

And I am more than I was. That's the joke of it, the beautiful stupid joke: the court made me strong by letting me go. A claimed soul is a coin—small, portable, spendable. A freed soul is a *story*, and stories are heavy, and mine has twelve years of open doors and guttered candles and bread-warmth in it, and every one of those small mercies is a stone in my foundation. The cold breaks against me and finds no purchase, because you cannot repossess a gift.

The Queen understands this before I do. I see it land in her face—the first new thing to land there in a thousand years, maybe. Not anger. Something worse for her: *recalculation.*

"You would unwritten yourself," she says slowly, "for a sibling who does not know you are here. Who has never known. Whose whole life you have haunted invisibly. What is the return on that investment?"

And I laugh. First laugh of my second life, and it comes out cracked and young and echoing down the shaft like a bell dropped in a well.

"Lady," I say, "you really do keep everything in ledgers."

Then the footsteps arrive.

* * *

Sebastian comes around the last turn of the stair and stops dead, and I watch my sibling see a ghost standing in a closing gate with his hands flat on the iron, and I watch them know me.

Not guess. *Know.* Twelve years of frost and candles and doors, and some part of them—the part that never stopped listening even when listening seemed like madness—recognizes me the way you recognize your own name in a crowd. Their face does something complicated. Their hand comes up, halfway, the way you reach for someone across a table.

"Mateo," they say.

Fifteen years I waited to hear that. Eleven of them dead. And it sounds exactly like it sounded when we were children and they'd lost me in the market and found me again: furious and relieved and about four years old.

"Hey, Felix," I say.

Because I know. I know about the charter, or I know about the name, or I simply know my sibling, and either way the word lands like a key turning. Sebastian—Felix—makes a sound that is not quite a laugh and not quite anything else, and the Queen's cold is pressing at my back like a hand the size of the sky, and above us the gate is a hand's width from shut.

"Go," I say. "Through. Now."

"The recall clause—" they start.

"Is voided at the boundary, and the boundary is *up*, and you are *slow*, and I am *heavy*," I say. "Go. Please. I have been telling you to go places for twelve years. Do it once with your ears working."

They hesitate. Of course they hesitate. They are Sebastian Reyes, and hesitation in the face of self-sacrifice is practically the family crest. And then Odile's voice comes down the shaft, ragged: "*The cold's in the water! It's in the water!*"—and my sibling looks at me one more time, and I give them the thing I have been saving all night, the whole hoard of it:

"It doesn't hurt," I lie, gently. "Holding a door. It's the best thing I've ever done. Both times."

Felix's face breaks and remakes itself into the mercenary mask, which I know is love wearing armor. They step through the gap. They turn, on the far side, and put their hand against the iron from the outside, palm to palm with mine through six inches of ancient metal, and for one moment I can almost feel it—warm, alive, stubborn, *there*.

"Come after," they say. "Find us. However you do it. Find us."

"I will," I say, which is the second lie I tell tonight, and the kinder one, because we both deserve to leave each other with a door still open.

Then they run, limping, up toward the snow, and I turn back to the Queen of the Long Winter, and I set my shoulder against the gate, and I begin, in earnest, to hold.

* * *

What does it cost? Everything. What did you expect?

The cold comes through me like winter through a shuttered house, finding every crack. I feel myself thinning, fraying, going from boy-shaped to smoke-shaped to something with less shape than smoke. The Queen does not fight me so much as *wait* for me, patient as ice, because she knows what I know: stories burn. Mine is burning fast now, all twelve years of it lighting the gate like a bonfire, and the fire is doing its work—the gate slows, shudders, catches—and the last of the freed souls pour past me, up and out, and somewhere above I hear Tomas bellow something joyful and profane, and I hear Odile singing, actually singing, some hymn the Quiet Mouth forbade, and I hear the snow.

Mostly I hear the snow. It sounds like applause. It sounds like a whole valley whispering *yes.*

The Queen watches me come apart with her ledger-eyes, and at the very end—when I am mostly memory, mostly the idea of a boy holding a door—she says, quietly, almost respectfully:

"What was it for?"

And I think about it. Honestly. With the last of me.

"For nothing," I say. "For free. That's the point. That's the whole—"

The gate shuts. The mountain seals. The cold takes me like sleep takes a child mid-sentence.

And then—

Light.

Not the vault-light, not the torch-light, not the thin gray nothing I expected. This is green-gold and moving, like sun through leaves, like the inside of spring, and there is ground under me that isn't stone, and there is a smell I know from before I knew anything else: pine, and thaw, and woodsmoke off in the distance.

Someone is walking beside me. Big, at my shoulder, easy, the way an older brother walks. When I look over—and I have eyes again, or the memory of eyes, and they work—all I see is gray fur and a suggestion of teeth and an amusement that feels, impossibly, like family.

"Well held," says the Wolf.

"I'm dead," I say. "Properly. Unwritten. You shouldn't be able to—"

"I grieve too," the Wolf says. "Did you think the living invented it?" We walk. The green-gold light moves with us. Somewhere ahead there is a road, and the road smells like home. "A god lost a servant tonight. A brother gained a brother. The arithmetic is terrible on both sides." The great head swings toward me, and one amber eye considers me with what I swear is fondness. "But I have found, over a very long time, that the best souls are the ones who held doors. And I keep, little gate-keeper, a country where such souls are not unwritten. Only early."

"Early," I say. My voice cracks on it, fifteen forever, and I don't mind.

"Early," the Wolf agrees. "They will build walls in the valley without magic. They will raise wards by hand. Your sibling will sign their name *Felix* on a charter, lucky and free, and every spring when the ash in the shrine is swept, there will be paw prints in it that no one owns." The Wolf's tail brushes my shoulder like a hand. "And sometimes, on the first warm night, when the household of strays sits up late and tells stories, a door will open that no one opened. A candle will steady that was guttering. Bread will smell warm in a cold kitchen."

"That's not much," I whisper.

"It is everything," says the Wolf. "It is exactly what you did for twelve years. You are very good at it. Come. Walk with me. The road home is long, and you have earned the walking of it."

So I go.

I am Mateo Reyes. I died twice, once for nothing and once for free, and both times it was the best thing I ever did. I held the door. They got out. And somewhere above the snow, my sibling is limping into the light with a priestess and a ferryman and a daughter and a hundred freed souls, heading home to build something no law and no god can repossess—

—and if you listen, on the first warm nights, in a valley that forgot its magic and remembered its people, you can hear a boy laughing in an open doorway, glad, at last, to be let go.
