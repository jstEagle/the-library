## 20. The Long Way Home

The mountain began to scream before they reached the first gate.

Tomas Bell had spent his whole life listening to water — the river's moods, the ice groaning in late winter, the particular hush a ferry makes when it takes on too much weight. He knew the sound of things giving way. The court beneath the mountain was making that sound now, deep in its bones, a long grinding note like a millstone turning on nothing, and every soul in the corridor felt it through their boots.

"Keep moving," Sebastian said, and their voice was raw as rope. They still held the ledger — or what was left of it, a sheaf of pages gone soft as moth wings since Odile read the debts aloud and Ilsa turned them against their Queen. "Don't look at the walls."

Tomas looked at the walls.

He couldn't help it. They were made of prayer-scraps — he'd seen that on the way down, millions of folded slips of paper and vellum and bark, each one a wish somebody had sold to buy something else. Now the scraps were coming loose. They drifted down the corridor like grey snow, catching in his hair, sticking to the sweat on his neck, and when he brushed one away he saw the writing on it: *let her live, let her live, let her live.* Someone's whole heart, folded into a square and spent like a coin, fluttering loose now that the ledger that held it was void.

Behind him, the freed souls were walking. That was the only word for it. There were more than forty of them — men and women and a few children who had been entries in the Queen's books for years, some for generations, kept as collateral in the dark with no memory of having been anything else. They walked because Sebastian walked, and Sebastian walked because there was no other direction. Their eyes had the shine of people newly woken from a fever, seeing everything twice.

One of them, an old woman with a miner's crooked back, caught Tomas's sleeve. "Is it true?" she asked. "Are we — is there anywhere we're going?"

"There's a valley," Tomas said. It was all he had. "There's a valley where the magic died, so nobody thought to guard the road out of it. We know the way."

"And after?"

"After," Tomas said, and found he was smiling, though his face felt like someone else's, "after is the part nobody's written yet."

* * *

They came up out of the mines into the cold and the light hit them like a blow.

Not sunlight — there was no sun under the solstice dark, only the pale bruised glow of the long winter sky — but after the glittering underworld, where everything shone because everything was bought, even that thin grey light seemed honest. Snow fell in fat slow flakes. The road up from the mountain's root was a switchback cut into black stone, and along its edge stood the boundary markers of the court's territory: iron posts crowned with little cages, each cage holding a flame that burned without heat.

As the party passed, the flames went out. One by one, like lamps being snuffed down a street by a lamplighter walking ahead of them.

"The gates are closing," Odile said. She had Ilsa by one hand and did not seem to know it; Ilsa had not pulled free. "Sebastian — how long do we have?"

"However long it takes the Queen to stop screaming." Sebastian didn't turn around. They had Mateo's ghost at their shoulder — visible now to everyone, since the ledger broke, a young man of nineteen forever, translucent as breath on glass — and they spoke partly to him, partly to the air. "She'll seal what's left and count what she lost. Counting takes her a while. She's old."

"She's furious," said a new voice, dry and small, and everyone flinched except Sebastian.

It was one of the freed souls — a boy, maybe fifteen, who had been in the ledgers so long his name had worn off him like paint. He looked embarrassed to have spoken. "Begging pardon. But I was a record-adjacent. I filed. You learn to hear the building. She isn't grieving yet. Grieving comes later. Right now she's doing arithmetic, and arithmetic means she thinks she can still win something back."

"Then we run," Sebastian said.

They ran. Forty-odd souls and four living people and one ghost, stumbling up a frozen switchback in the dark, snow filling their footprints almost as fast as they made them. Tomas carried a child on his back whose mother had fallen two hundred years ago in a mine collapse and whose debt had outlived her. The boy weighed nothing. That frightened him worse than the screaming mountain did.

* * *

At the top of the switchback the road opened onto the high plateau, and the plateau was white to every horizon, featureless, the kind of country where you could walk in circles until spring found your bones. The wind came across it flat and hard, carrying ice grains that stung like thrown sand.

Tomas stopped, hands on knees, lungs burning, and laughed.

It startled him out of his own mouth. He hadn't decided to laugh; it simply came, a wheezing, ridiculous bark of a laugh, and then he couldn't stop, and then the old woman with the crooked back started laughing too, and then three or four others, helpless, gasping, half-hysterical laughter rolling across the plateau while the mountain behind them ground its teeth.

"What," Sebastian said, "in the name of any god still taking calls—"

"We're alive!" Tomas managed. "We stole from a queen and we're *alive* and I haven't been alive since—" He waved a hand at himself, at the ferry, at thirty years of favors accruing like silt. "Since before my daughter could walk! Do you understand? I owe *nothing!* The ledger's void! My favors died with it! I am the freest man in this province and I'm standing in a blizzard with no idea which way south is!"

"North-east," said Ilsa.

Everyone turned. She stood with snow gathering on her shoulders, in her court-grey servant's dress, her record-keeper's ink still staining her fingers, and she pointed with a hand that did not quite shake. "The valley's north-east. Three days' walk if the weather holds, five if it doesn't. I memorized the province maps when I was nine because there was nothing else to read down there." A pause. Her chin came up, and Tomas saw his own stubbornness looking back at him out of a face he'd last seen when it was small enough to fit inside a ferry cabin. "I also memorized where the Queen's outriders patrol. If anyone asks, I've served the court eleven years and I know exactly how it hunts."

"Ilsa," Tomas said. His voice cracked in the middle of her name.

"You don't get to say it like that." She wasn't crying, but her eyes were bright and furious and wet all at once. "You don't get to say it like you didn't leave me. Eleven years, Da. I learned to love a place that keeps its promises because the place that was supposed to keep them didn't."

"No," Tomas said. "No, you're right. I didn't keep them. I sold you to keep the ferry, and the ferry to keep us fed, and I told myself it was the same as keeping you." He crossed the snow between them — three steps, it was only three steps, it took him a year — and stopped just short of touching her, because she hadn't said he could. "I can't give you those eleven years back. Nobody can, not even a god, and believe me, I've seen what gods can do lately. All I can give you is the rest of it. Every day I've got left. The boat, if you want it. Or not the boat. Anything. Nothing. Whatever you choose, I'll stand surety for it."

Ilsa stared at him. Behind her, the whole ragged procession of freed souls had gone quiet, watching, forty-some people who knew precisely what it meant to be bargained for.

Then she stepped forward and put her forehead against his chest, hard, like a door slamming shut, and grabbed two fistfuls of his coat, and said into the wool, muffled and furious and small: "You're going to be insufferable about this for the rest of your life, aren't you."

"Completely unbearable," Tomas agreed, holding her like something he'd fished out of a flood.

* * *

They walked north-east through the falling winter, and the strangest thing, Tomas thought later, was how ordinary it became.

Ordinary misery, anyway: cold that got into the joints and stayed, blisters, the endless white monotony, rationing bread that had frozen into something you had to break against a knee. But underneath the misery ran a current none of them had ever felt before, and it showed itself in small ways. The old woman with the crooked back — her name was Marta, it turned out, Marta Ostrow, missing since before Tomas was born — sang work songs in a language the valley had forgotten, and taught them to the children as they walked. Two brothers who'd been sold together argued cheerfully about whether the stars over the plateau were the same stars they remembered. The boy who'd heard the Queen's arithmetic walked beside Sebastian and narrated, quietly, everything the wind told him, because it turned out that freedom had given him back whatever small gift had gotten him sold in the first place.

And sometimes, in the snow around their camp at night, there were paw prints in the morning. Big ones. Coming close to the sleepers and going away again, never touching anyone, never leaving tracks that led anywhere but out into the white.

Odile knelt over them once, on the second morning, and touched the ash of their dead fire to the prints the way she'd touched ash to boundary stones a lifetime ago, it seemed. "He's counting us," she said. "Like a shepherd."

"He's not my shepherd anymore," Sebastian said. They said it steadily, but Tomas, who had spent a life reading faces across a ferry rail, saw the cost of the steadiness. "I spent his claim on me. That was the price. He doesn't — I don't belong to him now."

"No," Odile agreed, rising, brushing snow from her knees. "But belonging was never really what he offered, was it? Look at the prints again. They circle the camp. They don't lead to you. They lead around all of us." She smiled, tired and crooked. "That's not ownership, mercenary. That's a father walking the fence line."

Sebastian didn't answer. But that night, Tomas noticed, they slept facing the open snow instead of the fire, the way a man sleeps when he's listening for footsteps he swears he doesn't expect.

* * *

On the fourth day the weather broke them, briefly.

A white wind came down off the heights with no warning at all — one moment the plateau lay quiet, the next the world was a spinning sheet and you couldn't see the person holding your hand. Sebastian shouted for them to link arms and dig in, and they did, forty souls kneeling in a ring around the children with their backs to the storm, and for six hours Tomas existed nowhere but in the grip of Marta Ostrow's iron fingers and the sound of his own heartbeat.

When it passed, the world had been redrawn. Drifts ten feet high. The road gone. And at the edge of camp, half-buried, a shape that made the freed souls cry out — a waystone, an old one, pre-dating the court, carved with a wolf's head worn nearly smooth.

"That's the valley border," Sebastian breathed. "That's — we made better time than I thought. We're *here.*"

They dug the waystone free with bare hands, laughing and swearing, and when it stood clear against the pale sky, Marta Ostrow put her palm flat against the wolf's head and wept without any noise at all, the way you weep when you've forgotten how.

Beyond the stone, the land changed. Tomas felt it before he saw it — a flattening, a muting, the sensation of stepping into a room where a bell has just stopped ringing. Magic couldn't follow them here. Whatever trackers the Queen sent, whatever her arithmetic told her, they would cross into the valley deaf and blind and mortal.

Home, he thought. The word arrived uninvited and stayed.

* * *

They came down out of the passes at dusk on the sixth day, and the valley received them the way valleys receive the defeated: silently, with its lights already lit.

Townsfolk stood in the lanes as the procession wound through — forty strangers in rags, a disgraced priestess, a ferryman, the record-keeper who had betrayed a queen, a mercenary with a brand that no longer burned, and a ghost that half the children could see and point at. Faces at windows. Doors opening. An old man on the well-step who recognized Marta Ostrow and made a sound like a hinge and had to be helped to sit down.

Nobody cheered. It wasn't that kind of arrival. But nobody barred a door either, and when the freed souls faltered in the square outside the chapel — the drowned chapel, the one where the Wolf had spoken — it was the townsfolk who brought blankets, and broth, and armfuls of firewood, without being asked and without asking why.

Sebastian stood in the middle of it all, gray-faced with exhaustion, and looked at Tomas across the fire that was being built.

"They're going to want answers tomorrow," Sebastian said. "About the protection. About what we broke to get it broken. Some of them aren't going to forgive it."

"Some of them will want to hang us," Tomas agreed comfortably. He'd found a bench. He intended, if the gods permitted, never to stand up again. "Some of them wanted to hang me before I ever did anything interesting. That's towns." He nodded toward the chapel, where Ilsa was showing Odile where the dry pews were, arguing amiably about which wall leaked least. "But look at it, Reyes. Look at who's carrying the wood. Nobody ordered that. Nobody paid it. That's the whole trick, isn't it? That's what you figured out down there with a knife at the world's throat."

Sebastian followed his gaze. For a moment their face did something complicated and young.

Then the church bell — cracked, half-drowned, absurd — began to ring, rung by one of the freed children purely because it could be rung, and the sound rolled out over the valley, ragged and off-key and entirely human, and somewhere out past the boundary stones, in the deepening snow, Tomas would have sworn he heard something answer it.

Something with four feet, moving north.

Sebastian heard it too. They went very still, head lifted, listening, and the firelight caught the old brand on their skin — faded now, a scar where a claim used to be.

"It's early," Sebastian said quietly. "Spring's weeks off. What's a wolf doing out of the high country in a winter like this?"

Tomas looked north, at the dark coming down between the mountains, and felt the strange lightness in his chest shift — not fade, never fade, but make room.

"Maybe," he said, "it's coming home too."
