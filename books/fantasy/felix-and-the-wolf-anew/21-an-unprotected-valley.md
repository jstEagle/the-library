## 21. An Unprotected Valley

We came home through the pass at dusk, and the valley did not know us.

That is a strange thing to write, but it is the truest thing I know how to say. For twelve years the boundary stones had hummed against my skin like a mother's hand on a fevered brow—*you are kept, you are kept, you are kept*—and now, as Tomas's mule cart rattled down the frozen switchbacks with nine freed souls huddled under oilcloth in the bed of it, the stones said nothing at all. They stood where they had always stood, gray and lichen-eaten, and they were only stones.

I put my palm against the nearest one. Cold. Ordinary cold, the kind that belongs to weather and not to covenant.

"It's gone," Odile said behind me. She had her hood back for the first time since the court, her shaved head bare to the wind, and she looked younger without it and more tired too. "I can feel it. The whole valley's gone quiet in the wrong way."

"Quiet was what we wanted," I said. "Twelve years I prayed for this kind of quiet."

"You prayed for silence," Odile said. "This is an empty room."

I hated her a little for saying it, which is how I knew it was true.

The others came up around me: Tomas with his hands still cracked from the Queen's ledgers, Ilsa walking beside him as if she couldn't quite believe the ground would hold her weight outside the mountain, and behind them the returned—the souls we'd pulled out from under the solstice closing, blinking in their borrowed bodies or their half-mended ones, squinting at a sky none of them had seen since they were sold. One of them, a woman named Marta who had been collateral on a miller's loan for forty years, asked me if the stars here were always so many. I told her yes. She cried about it, quietly, the way people cry when they've forgotten they're allowed.

And Mateo—

Mateo was gone. He had stayed behind the gate, holding it shut with nothing but his stubbornness and whatever love keeps a ghost upright, and he had bought us our head start, and by the time we reached daylight there was nothing left of him to carry home but the shape of his absence, which I have learned fits in the chest like a second heart. I will write about him later. Or I won't. Some griefs you spend slowly.

The point is this: we came down into the valley at dusk, and the valley was dark.

Not winter-dark. Winter-dark has lamps in it, has hearthlight bleeding through shutters, has the orange smudge over Millbrook where the forge never fully sleeps. This was a darkness with no one tending it. The town sat at the bottom of the valley like a thing dropped there, and no light moved in it, and no smoke rose, and the only sound was the wind coming off the high snowfields with nothing left to slow it down.

"They felt it go," Ilsa said. She had spent years reading the Queen's ledgers; she knew what the death of a contract looked like from the inside. "When the ledger closed, every soul in this valley felt the protection end. Like a floor going out."

"Then they'll be frightened," Tomas said.

"Frightened people are honest," Odile said. "It's the comfortable ones who lie."

I looked at the town a long time. My town—not mine, I had never belonged to it, that had been the whole arrangement—but the place where I had hidden twelve years, escorting caravans through a valley where magic could not follow, because magic could not follow, because my mother had sold a whole town's safety along with her daughter's soul and called it providence. Every soul in Millbrook had slept sound on the interest of that bargain without knowing they paid it. And now the note had come due, and the collector was the sky itself, and the first hard frost of unprotected night was already sliding down the valley walls toward them.

"Well," I said, and got back on the cart. "Let's go be the bad news."

* * *

They met us at the bridge with torches and pitchforks, which I suppose is the traditional greeting for strangers arriving out of the long winter with a god's brand fading on their face.

I knew some of them. Garrick the reeve, whose caravan I had escorted eleven times without losing so much as a wheel. The Widow Anselm, who had paid me in bread once when her coin ran short. Young Piet, who used to follow me around asking about the wars until his mother made him stop. They stood across the bridge in a ragged line, and behind them the whole town was spilling out of its houses into the cold, because when the protection died everyone had come outdoors at once, the way people do when the house itself stops feeling safe.

Garrick held up a hand. "Stop there. Stop the cart there."

"Tomas Bell," Tomas said mildly. "Ferryman. Born on this river."

"I know who you are." Garrick's torch wavered. His eyes went past Tomas to the cart bed, to the faces under the oilcloth, to me. And I watched him find the brand—or where the brand had been, because the Wolf's mark had gone pale as an old scar the moment I gave up its protection, though it still ached in cold weather like any honest wound. "You're the mercenary. Reyes."

"I am."

"The valley's dead." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've been saying all day to anyone who'd listen. "The wards are dead. The stones don't answer. Old Marta Kessel tried the blessing her grandmother taught her and nothing happened, nothing, and then these—" he gestured at the cart with the torch, and his voice cracked down the middle, "—these *strangers* come out of the mountains in the middle of the long winter, and you want to cross my bridge."

"Yes," I said.

"Why?"

It was a fair question. I made myself answer it fairly.

"Because the protection you lost was never yours," I said. "It was bought. Before most of you were born. My mother sold my soul to the court beneath the mountain, and the court sold your valley its safety, and the ledger closed at solstice and everything came due at once. I'm sorry. That's the whole of it. I'm sorry, and I'm home, and I don't have anywhere else to be."

Silence. Wind. Somewhere behind the crowd a child started crying and was hushed.

Then the Widow Anselm pushed forward, small and square and seventy if she was a day, and looked up at me with the expression of a woman who has buried two husbands and a mortgage and does not frighten.

"That girl," she said, pointing one knuckled finger at Ilsa, "came down from the mountain with the ferryman. I saw her at the solstice fair before the roads closed. She's the Queen's own record-keeper. Everyone knows it."

"I was," Ilsa said. Her voice didn't shake. "I kept her ledgers for six years. Then I read them closely enough to understand what this valley was paying, and I turned the records against her, and I burned my service-vow in her own hearth. So now I'm nobody's record-keeper. If you want someone to check my work, check it. I'll show you every page."

Another silence, longer. And then—and I will remember this until I die—Piet, the boy who used to ask about the wars, stepped out of the line and planted his pitchfork butt-down in the snow like a man planting a standard.

"My da says the wards kept out the wolf-pack three winters running," he said. "My da says without them we're meat. But my da also says the wards never answered him *once*, not once, not even when my little sister was sick, and maybe—" he faltered, looked back at the crowd, found no help, and finished anyway, louder, "maybe something that never answers isn't worth grieving."

Out of the mouths of children. Out of the mouth of this child especially, standing in the snow with his ears red, having just said aloud the thing the whole valley had been afraid to think.

Garrick lowered his torch a hand's width. Not surrender. Consideration.

"You," he said to me. "You were bound to a god. Everyone heard the stories after the fair. You're the one the Wolf woke for."

"Woke for, and then unbound," I said. "I gave up its protection to void the ledger. It's gone. All of it. There is no god in this valley anymore, and no court, and no ward, and no law above the ones we make ourselves. That's the inheritance. I won't dress it up."

The Widow Anselm snorted. It was, absurdly, the sound that turned the night.

"Good," she said. "About time somebody cleared the rubbish out."

* * *

Change does not come like a wave. It comes like thaw—slow, uneven, with ice still clinging in the shadows long after the ditches run.

The first week, they barely let us inside the walls. We camped in the tithe barn, all eleven of us plus the mule, and the townsfolk brought food the way besieged people bring food to other besieged people: grudgingly, generously, watching us the whole time. Marta Kessel came on the third day. She was ninety-one and had been collateral for forty years, and she walked through the barn door, looked at the freed souls sitting among the hay, and said, "Which of you is from Harrow's Bend? Anyone? I had a cousin sold out of Harrow's Bend in the famine year." A man named Aldric raised his hand, shaking. They talked until midnight. By morning half the town had come to look, not at the strangers, but at the *returned*—at proof, living and breathing and eating porridge, that the thing under the mountain was real and had been robbing them blind for generations, and that it could be beaten.

Fear curdles or it clarifies. Ours clarified.

It was the smith, Brannoc, who started it—not with words but with iron. He came to the barn on the ninth day carrying a horseshoe, an ordinary horseshoe, except he'd worked a thread of silver into it in a pattern old as any forge-fire, and he wouldn't meet my eye while he explained it.

"No wards," he said. "No gods. Just iron, and silver, and the shape of a door. My grandfather hung these before the valley ever had its bargain. Said a threshold is a promise you make with your own hands." He set it on the straw between us. "Doesn't need anything to answer it. Only needs hanging."

I picked it up. It was warm from his forge and heavy as honesty.

"Brannoc," I said, "this is the wisest thing anyone's said to me in twelve years, and I include the god."

He almost smiled. "Aye, well. Don't tell the priestess. She'll want to preach about it."

Odile preached about it anyway. Of course she did. But not the way her order would have—no silencing, no quiet mouth, no holy hush. She stood in the market square on the first clear day in a month, in front of perhaps sixty skeptical farmers, and said: "Your valley was protected the way a debtor is protected—by someone else's collateral. Now you are free the way a debtor is free, which is to say, exposed. Here is what exposure is for." And then she taught them. That was the miracle of it, if I use the word at all anymore: she *taught*. Threshold-iron and salt-lines and the naming of corners, the old household crafts that predate every god, the small domestic magics that belong to no liturgy because they belong to the kitchen and the doorpost and the hands. She taught the Widow Anselm's granddaughters to braid rowan over lintels. She taught Piet to read the wind for what it carried. She stood in the cold for four hours with her bare head shining like a lamp and never once invoked anything higher than the people in front of her, and when she finished, old Marta Kessel got up and said, "Right. Who's got a hammer?"

They raised the walls themselves. That's the part I keep returning to, the part I suspect I'll be turning over in my mind when I'm as old as Marta: they raised the palisade *themselves*, every post felled from the north slope and every joint cut by hand, because Brannoc said a wall you buy is a wall someone else owns, and the town took that sentence into itself like a seed takes water. Garrick organized the labor rolls. Tomas ferried timber down the river on a barge he rebuilt with his own frostbitten hands, singing the obscene ferryman's songs he swore were traditional, and Ilsa kept the accounts in a ledger she ruled herself—first page, first line, in her careful record-keeper's script: *Millbrook Freehold. Owed to no one.*

Owed to no one. I read that line a hundred times that winter. I read it the way you press a tongue to a missing tooth.

And me? I hammered posts. I hauled stone. I stood surety for nothing, owed nothing, commanded nothing, and discovered—slowly, the way you discover a limb has healed—that I did not know how to live like that and was learning. Some nights the absence of the Wolf ached like the phantom weight of an arm. Some nights I dreamed of the hunt, of running beside something vast and warm in the snow, and woke with wet eyes and no memory of why. But the paw prints still appeared sometimes, in the ash of the shrine we hadn't built yet, in the snow outside the barn door, single and deliberate and going nowhere, and I chose—I choose—to believe they meant what I needed them to mean: *not gone. Only letting go.*

* * *

On the last day of the long winter, we hung the final horseshoe.

It went over the gate of the new palisade, the biggest one Brannoc had ever forged, silver-threaded and blackened at the edges, and the whole town turned out to watch Brannoc lift it and the Widow Anselm drive the last nail, because she had insisted and no one was fool enough to argue. When it was done she stepped back, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at the gate for a long moment.

"There," she said. "Now we're protected by something we can take down and rehang ourselves. That's the difference, girl." She jabbed a thumb at me. "Between being kept and being *kept*."

I laughed. It startled me, the sound of it, rusty as the old bell in the chapel tower. Behind me I heard Odile laughing too, and Tomas, and Piet shouting something about supper, and the noise of it rose up into the thinning evening the way smoke rises from a chimney that someone is tending.

Later—much later, when the household had gathered in the big room of the house we were slowly building at the edge of town, and Ilsa was arguing with Odile about whether mercy counted as a debt, and Tomas was pretending to sleep in his chair—I stepped outside to check the sky, an old soldier's habit I saw no reason to break.

Snow was falling again, soft and unhurried, the ordinary kind. Down in the valley the new walls stood black against the white, hung with iron, and beyond them the boundary stones were only stones, and beyond *them* the world went on being dangerous and enormous and ours to walk in.

Something crossed the snowfield at the tree line. Big. Low. Moving with a lope I knew better than my own heartbeat.

It stopped. It looked at me—I felt the look the way you feel sun through a window—and then it turned and went, unhurried, unhindered, needing nothing from me and leaving nothing behind but prints.

I stood in the falling snow a long time, counting them. Four. Always four.

And then, faintly, from the direction of the road—the spring road, the open road, the one that led away from everything we had built and toward everything we hadn't yet—a sound I had not heard in twelve years and had never once expected to hear again.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Coming fast.
