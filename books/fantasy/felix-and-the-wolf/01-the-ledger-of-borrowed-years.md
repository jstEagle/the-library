## 1. The Ledger of Borrowed Years

The bell in the Thornfell temple rang the ninth hour, and Marguerite Okafor set down their pen with the particular care of someone who had learned that every object in their life could be taken away.

Ink first. They stoppered the little horn pot and wiped the nib with a linen rag gone gray from years of the same motion. Then the page. The prophecy was still wet at the descenders—*the scribe who remembers both names*—and Marguerite tilted the vellum toward the candle to check for blots. There were none. There were never any. Twelve years of copying scripture and prophecy and temple writ had made their hand so steady it frightened people who watched too long.

*The scribe who remembers both names shall stand where the worlds wear thin.*

They read it once more before covering it with the blotting felt. It was a good line. Most prophecies were good lines—vague enough to mean anything, old enough to mean nothing. This one had come out of the deep archive that morning, a scroll brittle as autumn leaf, catalogued under *Covenant Fragments, Unclassified*, and Father Anselm wanted three fair copies by week's end because the Wolf King was dying and dying kings made temples nervous.

Marguerite did not think about the fact that they had copied this same fragment four years ago, or that they had copied it seven years before that. The deep archive was full of duplicates. That was all. Duplicates, recopied, forgotten.

That was how you survived an indenture: you became a person to whom nothing happened.

* * *

The smuggling began, as it always began, after Compline, when the temple's corridors went dark and the night brothers took up their snoring vigil in the south cloister.

It was not dramatic work. Marguerite wished people understood that. No masks, no daggers, no whispered passwords. Just a scribe whose duties included delivering sealed correspondence to the shrine-keepers at the mountain chapels, and who had learned over twelve years which chapel letters could be delayed a day, and which roads between here and there were unwatched, and what a vial of blessed chrism or a bundle of consecrated salt fetched in the valley towns where the temple's reach went thin as mountain air.

Small things. Enough coin to buy candles beyond their ration, paper beyond their allotment, and once a year, on the eve of their indenture-day, a cup of wine drunk alone in a room where no one could see them not being grateful.

Tonight's cargo was modest: two vials of chrism sewn into the lining of their traveling cloak, and a letter of introduction addressed to Sister Verity at the Chapel of the Nine Stones—a letter Marguerite had already read, because reading the mail was the first rule of smuggling and the second rule of staying alive. It said nothing interesting. It said, in Father Anselm's cramped hand, only that winter was coming and the chapel stores should be counted twice.

The pass road left the temple through the bone-yard gate, wound up through black pines, and climbed toward the notch the locals called the Shepherd's Tooth. Marguerite knew it by feel now, by root and stone, by the place where the wind funneled through and tried to take your hood off. They walked it in the dark without a lantern, because lanterns got you killed on smuggler roads, and thought about nothing, which was a discipline like any other.

Which is why the light in the shrine at the top of the pass annoyed them immediately.

* * *

The Chapel of the Nine Stones wasn't a chapel anymore, strictly speaking—the roof had gone during some grandfather's winter, and the nine standing stones did most of the holy work now, leaning together in the wind like conspirators. Travelers used the shelter. So did worse than travelers.

There was a fire going in the lee of the largest stone, small and disciplined, and a man sitting beside it with his boots off, drying his stockings with the methodical patience of someone who had done it a hundred times in a hundred bad places.

Marguerite stopped at the edge of the firelight and let him see them first. That was courtesy among people who met badly lit.

"Evening," the man said. He didn't startle. His voice was low and roughened, a voice used to being quiet in loud rooms. "Road's free. I'm just drying out."

"Shrine-keeper comes at dawn," Marguerite said, which was true, and also a way of saying: I know your kind, and I know when you'll be gone.

"Then I'll be gone before dawn." He smiled—not wide, but genuinely, and it changed his whole face from something watchful to something almost boyish. "Felix," he added, offering his name the way other men offered a hand.

Marguerite didn't offer theirs. "I'm expected."

"I know. You're the temple scribe. You come up this road twice a month, always at night, always carrying more than letters." He said it easily, without menace, the way you'd mention the weather. "Don't worry. I don't sell information. Bad margins."

Every instinct Marguerite owned stood up at once. But the rain was starting again, fine and needling, and the fire was warm, and turning back meant three hours re-walking a road they'd already walked. They sat down across the fire from him, cloak still fastened, hands visible, and told themselves this was reconnaissance and not company.

Felix went back to his stockings. Between his knees lay a ledger—small, fat, bound in green leather gone soft with handling, its corners rounded like river stones. A merchant's book. A moneylender's book.

Marguerite looked at it the way you look at anything on a long night: idly, then not idly at all.

Because the script on the open page was wrong.

Not forged-wrong. Not clumsy. Wrong the way a familiar face is wrong in an unfamiliar city. The hand was fluid and exact, with a peculiar hooked ascender on the letter *l*, and a habit of joining certain pairs of letters—*th*, *wh*—into a single ligature that no writing master in Thornfell taught, because Marguerite had been taught by every one of them and none of them taught it.

Marguerite knew that script the way you know your own reflection.

They had spent the morning copying a century-old prophecy in a fair hand ordered by Father Anselm. But underneath the fair copy, in their working draft, in the private shorthand they used for transcribing—and only there, only ever there—that same hooked *l* had appeared. That same joined *th*. Habits no one had ever taught them. Habits they had never been able to break, though they'd tried, early on, trying to be no one.

And here they were, in a stranger's debt-ledger, by firelight, on a mountain, a hundred miles and a hundred years from anywhere they should have been.

"What is that?" Marguerite asked. Their voice came out level. They were proud of that later.

"Hmm?" Felix glanced down. "Oh. Work." He turned the ledger slightly, reflexively, shielding it—an old gesture, automatic. Then he seemed to reconsider, or maybe he saw something in their face, because he turned it back and held it out across the fire. "Go on, then. Everyone wants to look. Nobody ever understands it."

Marguerite took it.

The entries were debts. That much was plain even in the strange script—names, sums, dates rendered in some notation of moons and seasons rather than months. Debts owed across... the column headings made no sense. *Owed against breath. Owed against name. Owed against the wall between.* And running down the margin, repeated like a refrain, in that impossible, familiar hand:

*The scribe who remembers both names shall stand where the worlds wear thin, and the debt of the borrowed years shall come due.*

Marguerite's hands did not shake. Twelve years of training. Their hands did not shake, and their face did nothing, and inside, very quietly, something that had been carefully, patiently invisible for twelve years began to crack.

"Where did you get this?" they asked.

"My employer writes it. I keep it." Felix shrugged. "He's old. Older than he looks, and he looks pretty old. He says the hand isn't his either—he copies it from a book older than him. Says it's the only script the covenant recognizes." He squinted at them across the flames. "You've seen it before."

"No."

"You have. You went white as the chrism you're smuggling in your left pocket."

Marguerite stood, slowly, and set the ledger down on the stone between them, aligned square, the way they aligned everything. "I'm expected at the chapel."

"It's four hours to the chapel."

"Then I should go."

"The prophecy in your margin," Felix said, still seated, still easy, watching them with those eyes that missed nothing and gave away less. "The one about the scribe with two names. My employer sent me to find that scribe. Been looking for—" He paused, and for the first time something flickered behind the ease, something almost apologetic. "Well. For longer than I've been keeping the ledger."

The wind came through the notch and bent the fire flat. Above the nine stones, clouds tore open for a moment, and the stars showed hard and cold, and Marguerite thought—with the strange clarity of a person standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing—*twelve years, and it ends on a mountain, by a stranger's fire, over a book of debts.*

"Who is your employer?" they asked.

Felix pulled his stockings back on. When he answered, the boyishness was gone from his voice entirely, and what remained was tired and honest and very far from home.

"That depends," he said, "on whether you're the scribe. Because if you are—I work for the Wolf King. And he owes your family an apology a hundred years overdue."
