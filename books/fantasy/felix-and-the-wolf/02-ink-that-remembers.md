## 2. Ink That Remembers

The archive of Thornfell temple kept its own hours. Day belonged to the novices and their dusting; night belonged to the scribes who could be trusted with the deep shelves, the ones where the vellum was older than the stone that held it. Marguerite had earned the night shift three years ago and had never once asked why. Asking questions was how people became interesting, and interesting people got noticed.

So they worked by lamplight among the dead, copying prophecy after prophecy into fresh ledgers with a hand so disciplined it looked printed. That was the trick of survival at Thornfell: make your writing beautiful enough to praise and anonymous enough to forget. Marguerite had spent eleven years perfecting both.

Tonight's task was a restoration. *The Concordance of the Hollow Altar*, a codex from the founding century, its spine cracked, its margins blooming with brown rot. The prior had ordered it recopied before winter, which meant Marguerite would spend the next month transcribing a hundred-year-old text about a covenant everyone pretended still functioned.

They trimmed the lamp, squared a fresh quire, and opened the codex to its first leaf.

And stopped.

The handwriting was theirs.

Not similar. Not an echo, not a family resemblance between two hands trained in the same scriptorium. It was *theirs*—the same slight forward lean on every ascender, the same habit of thickening the downstroke at the end of a word as if pinning it to the page, the same tiny hesitation-mark before proper names, a dot like a held breath. Marguerite knew that dot. They made it every day. They had never been taught to make it. No one else in the scriptorium made it. They had checked, years ago, out of nothing more than boredom, and felt strangely proud of the one thing about themselves that was unrepeatable.

Now it sat on parchment older than their grandmother, greeting them across a century like a face in a dark window.

Marguerite set down the quill. Their hands were steady—eleven years of practice at steadiness—but something behind their ribs was not. They turned pages slowly. The whole codex was written in their hand. Every line of the covenant rite, every marginal gloss, every correction where a younger scribe had blotted and scraped and begun again. Even the corrections were familiar. There was a particular way Marguerite scraped a blot—a short diagonal stroke, then a circular polish—and there it was, done the same way, a hundred years ago, on a page that should have crumbled before Marguerite learned to hold a pen.

*You are tired*, they told themselves. *You have copied this prophecy so many times your hand dreams in it.* That was almost plausible. Scribes did absorb texts into their muscles. But muscle memory did not reproduce a private habit no master had taught. Muscle memory did not put *your* breath-dot on someone else's page.

Marguerite did what they always did with unbearable things: they filed them. They fetched fresh ink, chose a neutral, careful hand—not their own, deliberately not their own—and began the transcription as if the original were merely old and strange. Copy first. Understand later, or never. Never was usually safer.

But the codex would not let them keep the distance.

Halfway through the second gathering, a phrase surfaced that Marguerite had copied only yesterday morning, from a different book, a different shelf, a different century:

*The covenant shall fail in the days of the borrowed ledger, and the scribe who remembers both names shall stand at the Hollow Altar with ink and blood.*

Yesterday it had been routine prophecy, one more line in the endless churn of apocalyptic verse the temple collected the way other houses collected taxes. Today, with Felix's ledger still folded inside their coat upstairs—the smuggler's payment-book full of debts recorded in a script Marguerite had recognized with a cold jolt even before they'd understood why—it read less like prophecy and more like instructions.

*The scribe who remembers both names.*

Marguerite had one name. Everyone at Thornfell had exactly one name, assigned at indenture, recorded in the rolls, spoken aloud at the annual accounting. Two names was heresy-adjacent, a superstition the inquisitors used to scare novices. And yet Marguerite had always, privately, without ever deciding to, thought of themselves as having had another name once—one worn smooth by time, like a coin rubbed blank. Not remembered. Just the shape of remembering, left behind when the memory itself was taken or lost.

They had never told anyone that. You did not tell the temple you suspected parts of yourself were missing. Parts of yourself being missing was, historically, how people ended up on the inquisitors' lists.

Marguerite looked down at the century-old page, at their own impossible hand, and felt the floor of their life tilt. Eleven years of careful invisibility, built plank by plank—and underneath it, apparently, a foundation they had never laid. Someone had written this book with their hand. Either some ancestor whose habits ran that true in the blood—

Or the blood itself was borrowed. Or the years were. *Borrowed ledger*, the line said. Felix's ledger was literally a book of borrowed years, debts measured in time owed across realms. Marguerite had touched it yesterday and felt, absurdly, homesick for a place they'd never been.

They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes until sparks bloomed. Stop. Copy. File it. Survive tonight; unravel tomorrow.

That was when the lamp behind them moved.

*

"You've found the Concordance."

Sister Vess stood at the end of the aisle, hood back, gray hair cropped close, holding her own lamp low so the light fell upward and carved her face into planes. She was the senior inquisitor attached to the archive, which meant she was the person whose attention Marguerite had spent eleven years avoiding, and she had a way of arriving silently that suggested she practiced it.

"Prior's orders," Marguerite said. "Restoration copy before winter."

"Mm." Vess came closer, unhurried, and Marguerite became acutely aware of the open codex, of their own transcription beside it, of the visible gap between the two hands. They had chosen a neutral script for the copy. Neutral was good. Neutral was forgettable.

Vess looked at the original for a long moment. Then at the copy. Then back.

"Curious," she said.

"The rot is bad in the third gathering. I'll need to work from the facsimile fragments for—"

"That's not what I mean." Vess set her lamp on the table. The light pooled over both books, old and new, side by side. "Look at the marginalia here. Leaf forty-one."

Marguerite looked. A gloss in the margin, small and cramped: *the altar does not want blood alone*. Written in their hand. With their breath-dot before the word *altar*.

"I see it," they said carefully.

"Do you?" Vess's voice stayed mild, which was worse than sharpness. Mildness meant she had already decided something and was simply watching you arrive at it. "Because I have catalogued every hand in this archive for twenty-two years, scribe. Yours included. I know your work the way a mother knows a cough." She tapped the ancient page once, lightly, as if waking it. "This is your hand. And this book has not left the restricted case since before you were born."

"There are hands that run in—"

"In families? Yes. In scriptoria, yes. But not this precisely. Not the hesitations. Hesitation is not inherited, Marguerite. Hesitation is *personal*." She said their name the way she said everything, evenly, but hearing it land next to that impossible page made Marguerite's skin prickle. "Explain it to me."

"I can't," Marguerite said. Truth was safer than invention here; Vess caught inventions the way spiders caught flies, without appearing to move. "I opened it tonight and saw it. I don't know what it is."

Something flickered in Vess's face—not belief, but consideration. "No," she agreed. "I don't think you do. Which is the only reason you're still standing at that table instead of sitting in my office." She straightened. "The codex will come to me now. All of it. You'll take the Prior's other commission instead—the smuggling manifests, the seized ledgers. Routine work."

Marguerite's heart performed a single hard knock against its cage. *Seized ledgers.* Somewhere in that stack, very likely, was a payment-book belonging to a smuggler named Felix, full of debts in a script that matched prophecies, currently logged under confiscation number whatever-the-hell-it-was.

"Of course, Sister."

"And Marguerite." Vess lifted her lamp. Her shadow stretched up the shelves, long and jointed, touching books a hundred years dead. "If you remember anything—anything at all—that explains this, you will bring it to me before you bring it to anyone. Before you write it down. Before you *think* it twice. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Marguerite said, which was true, though not in the way Vess meant.

Vess studied them a moment longer, then took the codex under her arm and went, her footsteps making no sound at all, which Marguerite now understood was not a skill but a warning.

*

They didn't sleep. They sat in their cell with the confiscated-manifests chit in one hand and Felix's stolen impression of his ledger—marginal notes, debt entries, the borrowed-years arithmetic—in front of them, and they compared it against the line they had copied yesterday morning and the line that had waited a hundred years for them tonight.

*The scribe who remembers both names.*

Two names. One worn smooth. One assigned. And somewhere in the Wolf King's fading realm, according to a smuggler who drank too much and lied charmingly, a debt stood recorded against Marguerite's bloodline—a debt the ledger dated, Felix had said, to *before you were born, which is the funny part.*

It wasn't funny. Nothing was funny. Marguerite's entire self was a room with a wall that had just swung open onto a corridor they had never seen, and down that corridor someone was walking toward them wearing their handwriting like a coat.

In the morning, they would request access to the seized ledgers. Officially. Calmly. A scribe doing routine work, invisible as ever.

But tonight, by the last of the lamp, Marguerite dipped their pen and wrote, in their own unmistakable hand, on a scrap they could burn: *Who wrote the Concordance?*

The ink dried. The breath-dot sat before the question mark like a held breath.

And beneath it, unbidden, their hand added a second line in a script they did not recognize and somehow knew: *You did. Both times.*
