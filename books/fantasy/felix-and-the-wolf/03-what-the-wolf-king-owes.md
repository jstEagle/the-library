## 3. What the Wolf King Owes

Felix had been robbed by better liars than Marguerite Okafor, but never by a quieter one.

They sat across from each other in the back room of the Drowned Bell, a dockside tavern where the ale was bad and the landlord asked no questions because Felix paid him not to. Rain hammered the shutters. Between them on the table lay the ledger, closed now, its leather cover dark with old water stains. Marguerite hadn't touched it since Felix had snatched it back an hour ago. They just watched it, the way a man watches a snake he's decided not to run from.

"You copied that page," Felix said. "The one about the scribe who remembers both names. Word for word."

"I copy a great many things."

"Not things written before you were born. Not in your own hand."

Marguerite's jaw tightened—a small motion, quickly mastered, but Felix had spent ten years reading faces across smuggling tables in three realms, and he knew what he'd seen. Fear. Not of him. Of something older.

"Tell me what you know," he said, "and I'll tell you why I came to Thornfell instead of selling that ledger to the temple for enough coin to retire."

"I didn't ask you to tell me anything."

"No." Felix leaned back until the chair creaked. "But you're going to listen anyway, because here's what happens if you don't. Tomorrow morning I walk into the archive annex and show the under-prior a ledger written in your handwriting, dated ninety-eight years ago, containing prophecy text the temple claims was sealed by Saint Aldric himself. You know what they do to scribes who forge holy script?"

"They don't do anything," Marguerite said. "Because it isn't forgery, and you know it isn't, which is the only reason you haven't done it yet."

Felix grinned despite himself. "You're smarter than the last three people who tried to blackmail me."

"Only three? You should meet more scribes."

* * *

He'd found her—found *them*, he corrected himself; the temple records said *Marguerite Okafor, indentured, third rank* without any pronoun worth trusting—in the copying hall on his second night in Thornfell, bent over a desk with six other gray-robed figures, all of them scratching away at vellum like a room full of patient insects. He'd come to deliver wine to the prior's steward, a legitimate delivery wrapped around an illegitimate errand, and he'd almost walked past.

Almost. Because through the open door he'd heard the scratch of pens, and beneath it something else—the faint, wrong hum that his kind of work had taught him to hear. The sound the ledger made when it was near something that belonged to it.

He'd stood in that doorway with a crate of Vellorine red on his shoulder and felt the ledger wake against his ribs where it rode in its oilskin wrap, and he'd looked at the third desk from the window, and he'd known.

That was two nights ago. Since then he'd followed the scribe through the temple district, learned their habits: up before the bell, ink-stained fingers, no friends among the gray robes, meals taken alone in the corner of the refectory with a book propped against the cup. A person built out of invisibility, brick by careful brick. Felix recognized the architecture. He'd lived in a house like that himself once, before the Wolf King's hunters burned it down around him.

Now, in the Drowned Bell, he opened the ledger again and turned to the page he'd shown them in the alley—the page that had made the color drain out of their face so completely he'd thought for a moment they might faint.

"The Wolf King is dying," he said.

Marguerite blinked. Whatever they'd expected, it wasn't that.

"He's been dying for a hundred years. Slowly. The way a fire dies when the wood runs out—not gone, just... less. Every year there's less of him. His court forgets rooms. Whole corridors in the Winter Hall go dark, not because the lamps fail but because nobody remembers the lamps were ever lit. My grandmother served in his kitchens. She said when she was a girl the realm had nine rivers, and by the time she died you could only find five, and the ferryman wept every crossing because he could feel the other four going out like coals."

"That's not possible," Marguerite said automatically. Then stopped, because they were a scribe, and scribes know better than anyone that impossible things are mostly just badly archived.

"It's the covenant," Felix said. "The pact between the human realm and the wolf realm. It's fraying. Has been for a century. And everything tied to it frays with it—the King most of all, because he's the anchor point. The oldest thing in either realm still holding to the original terms."

"And your ledger?"

Felix turned pages. The vellum was soft as cloth with age. "This belonged to my grandfather. He was a debt-keeper. In the wolf realm, debts are... more than money. A debt is a thread. It binds. When someone owes you—blood, service, truth—you hold a piece of them, and they hold a piece of you, and the holding keeps both of you real. That's the whole law of my realm, underneath everything else. Debt is memory. Memory is existence."

He laid the ledger flat and turned it toward Marguerite. "When the covenant started failing, the debts started failing too. Threads snapping. People forgetting what they owed each other, then forgetting each other, then—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound was very loud in the small room— "gone. Not dead. Just unwritten. So the King commissioned new ledgers. Debt-keepers who would carry the accounts into the human realm, where ink lasts longer than it does with us. Where remembering is a profession."

"And yours records..."

"Everything owed across the boundary. Every debt, every claim, every thread still holding." He tapped a line near the bottom of the current page. "Including this one."

Marguerite read it. Felix watched them read it, and this time he saw the fear arrive properly, settle in, make itself at home.

*Owed against the bloodline of Okafor: one life freely given, or the mending of what was broken. Interest accruing since the Breaking. Holder of record: the Crown of the Wolf Realm.*

"My family owes your king a life?" Marguerite's voice was very even. Too even.

"Your family owes my king *more* than a life. But the ledger only holds what can be collected, and this is the collectable part." Felix kept his voice gentle, which cost him something; gentleness wasn't his trade. "Here's what I think, scribe. I think you already know what was broken. I think you've known since you were old enough to wonder why a child ends up indentured to a temple with no family name anyone will speak of. I think that's why you keep yourself so small. Why you copy prophecies all day and never let a single word of your own onto the page."

"Don't presume to know me."

"I don't have to. The ledger knows you." He turned another page, and another, and stopped. "Look."

Marguerite looked. On the page was a single line, fresh ink, blacker than the rest—as if the ledger had written it recently, or wanted to:

*The scribe who remembers both names shall come to the Hollow Altar. The debt comes due at the turning of the year.*

"There's no date on that entry," Marguerite said. Their voice had gone quiet in a different way now. "Ledgers have dates."

"This one wrote itself three weeks ago. The night I left the Winter Hall. My grandfather's ledger has never done that before. Neither has any ledger in the history of debt-keeping, according to the archivist who nearly fainted examining it." Felix closed the book. "So. Options. One: you come with me, willingly, and we figure out together what the Hollow Altar wants with you. Two: I take you to the King's remaining hunters, who are less patient and much less polite than I am. Three: the temple finds out what you are before either of us moves, and you learn what heretic-scribes get. I've seen the pyres in Thornfell square. Have you?"

For a long moment Marguerite said nothing. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the shutters like impatient fingers.

Then they said, "You're lying about at least one thing. Possibly several."

"Probably."

"But you're frightened." They looked up, and their eyes were sharp and dark and entirely awake. "You talk like a man holding all the cards, but your hands aren't steady. You're frightened of whatever sent you, and you need me more than you're admitting. Which means the blackmail runs both ways, doesn't it? Because if I'm the scribe your ledger says I am—if I'm the one who remembers both names—then I'm also the only person alive who can prove whether your precious ledger is genuine. And if it's forged, your King's hunters will want to know who wasted their dying master's last strength on a fraud."

Felix stared at them. Then he laughed—a short, surprised bark, the first honest laugh he'd had in weeks.

"Mutual blackmail," he said. "Is that what we're calling an alliance these days?"

"In Thornfell we call it a contract. Both parties bound, both parties watchful, neither party able to betray the other without destroying themselves." Marguerite extended one ink-stained hand across the table. "Terms?"

"Terms." He shook it. Their grip was stronger than he expected. "I get you out of the city before the temple finishes whatever inquiry they started when that under-prior started pulling century-old registry books. You get answers. We split the danger down the middle."

"What danger? You've told me about a dying king and a fraying covenant and a debt against my blood. None of that is danger. That's history."

"The danger," Felix said, standing and pulling his coat on, "is that somebody in the King's court doesn't want the covenant mended. Somebody powerful enough to arrange for the debt-keeper carrying the one ledger that points to you to be ambushed on the road out of the mountains—which happened, by the way, four days before I reached Thornfell. I wasn't supposed to survive that ambush. Whoever set it assumed the ledger would burn with me."

Marguerite had gone very still. "And it didn't burn."

"And it didn't burn. Which means whoever it is knows by now that it's still moving. Still pointing at you." He slung his pack over his shoulder. "We leave tonight, scribe. Before the people hunting this ledger decide to stop hunting it and start hunting its readers."

Marguerite rose slowly, and for the first time since the alley, Felix saw them look afraid of something larger than exposure—afraid the way you're afraid when the ground shifts under a building you thought was solid.

"One condition," they said.

"Name it."

"When you tell me what my bloodline broke a hundred years ago—" their voice didn't waver, but their hand, resting on the table, had curled into a fist— "you tell me all of it. No merciful edits. I've had enough of those to last a lifetime."

Felix held their gaze and told the truth, because under the terms of a contract, nothing less would hold.

"All of it," he said. "Starting with the part the temple burned every record of: the reason the founders of your own church broke the covenant on purpose—and why they needed a bloodline like yours to exist at all."

Outside, the rain redoubled, and somewhere above the harbor, thunder rolled down off the mountains like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
