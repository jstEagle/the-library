## 4. Debt-Bound

They came for me at the hour of first bell, when the archive was coldest and my hands were still ink-stained from the night's copying.

I heard them before I saw them—three sets of boots on stone, moving without the shuffle of novices or the measured tread of librarians. I had time to cap the inkwell, to square the ledger of borrowed years beneath a false folio, to arrange my face into the blankness that had kept me safe for eleven years. Then the door opened and Inquisitor Vess stood in it, flanked by two temple guards whose halberds caught the lamplight like wet teeth.

"Scribe Okafor," she said. "You will come with us."

Not a request. Not even an accusation yet. Just the flat grammar of a sentence already decided.

I rose. My knees were steady, which surprised me. "Am I wanted for questioning?"

"You are wanted for *correction*." She let the word sit between us. Behind her, one of the guards shifted his weight, and I understood that they had rehearsed this—that somewhere in the temple's warrens, my name had been spoken in rooms I would never see, and decisions had been made about me the way decisions are made about weather.

We walked the long gallery above the scriptorium, past windows where dawn was only a rumor. Below, scribes bent over their desks, heads down, quills scratching. Not one of them looked up. That was the discipline of Thornfell: you learned early that curiosity was a debt, and debts were called in.

Vess stopped at the Chamber of Record—a room I had entered once as a child, when my indenture was signed with my mother's thumbprint and my own small hand. They sat me in the same chair. It was smaller than I remembered, or I was larger than I had been allowed to become.

"Yesterday," Vess said, unrolling a document across the table, "a folio was found in your cell. A copy of the Prophecy of the Hollow Altar, transcribed in full—against archive law, against indenture law, against common sense."

"It was practice," I said. "Penmanship."

"Penmanship." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Then you will not object to a comparison."

She produced two sheets. The first was mine—the transcription Felix's ledger had woken in me, copied out in the fevered hours before he knocked at my window. The second was older, brittle, sealed under wax: the original prophecy, a century old, recovered from who knew where by whoever watched me.

The hands were identical. Of course they were. I had known since the night I read Felix's ledger; I had simply hoped the knowing could stay private a little longer.

"Explain this," Vess said quietly.

And here is what I have learned about terror: it does not feel like running. It feels like standing very still while the floor of your life is pried up board by board, and underneath there is nothing—no foundation, no name, no self you can point to and say *this is mine, this has always been mine.*

"I don't know," I said. And it was almost true.

Vess studied me for a long moment. Then she took out a branding iron—not the great ceremonial ones used on cattle-thieves, but a scribe's iron, thin as a stylus, meant for marking margins. For marking *errors.*

"By order of the Conclave," she said, "Marguerite Okafor, indentured scribe of Thornfell, is declared heretic of the second degree. Your indenture is called due. Your labor belongs to the temple until such time as correction is complete." She paused. "Hold out your left hand."

I want to say I fought. I want to tell you I overturned the table, that the guards were clumsy, that some hidden blood in me rose up wild and wolfish and saved us both the trouble. But the truth is smaller and colder: I held out my hand, because two guards were stronger than one scribe, and because some part of me—the part that had spent eleven years being invisible—still believed that obedience might be mistaken for innocence.

The iron touched the back of my wrist below the palm. It hissed. The pain was white and total for three heartbeats, and then it settled into a deep burn, and into my skin sank a single character in the temple's oldest script: *errata.* The mark they give to texts that must be corrected or destroyed.

"There," Vess said, almost gently. "Now everyone will know what you are."

I looked down at my hand—at the neat, looping letters I had written ten thousand pages with—and thought: *you don't know what I am. Neither do I. But I am going to find out, and it will not be here.*

* * *

They locked me in a penitent's cell to await transfer to the Conclave's tower. The cell had one window, barred, facing the mountain wall—the same wall Felix had climbed the night we met. I sat on the stone shelf that served as a bed and pressed my branded wrist against the cold wall and let myself grieve.

Because that is what it was. Grief. Not for freedom—I had never had much—but for the shape of things. The morning bell I would not answer. The desk with its worn hollow where my elbow rested. Brother Anselm's terrible tea. The prophecy folios I had copied so many times I loved them the way you love a scar. Eleven years of careful, invisible living, and it had taken one smuggler with a ledger to burn it all down.

I should have hated him for it. Instead I found myself listening for him—for boots on the wall, for the soft whistle he'd used outside my window, three notes descending like something falling.

He came at dusk.

No whistle this time. Just a shadow crossing the barred window, then again, closer, and then his face upside-down at the bars, grinning like trouble itself.

"You look terrible," Felix said.

"They branded me."

His grin died. He dropped through the window space, caught the bars, hung there looking at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before—something older than mischief. "Let me see."

I held my wrist up. He turned it in the fading light, and his jaw tightened. "*Errata.* They're going to correct you. Do you understand what that means? The Conclave doesn't punish heretics, Marguerite. They *edit* them. There won't be enough of you left afterward to remember why you ran."

"Then run I will," I said. "If you're offering."

"I'm offering worse than escape." He pulled a coil of rope from under his coat. "I'm offering the smuggler roads. The passes the temple doesn't map because the temple can't see them. There's a road through the mountains that only exists if you already know it's there—and you do. Don't argue. You told me yourself, that first night, half-asleep over your wine: you grew up on these slopes before the temple took you. Some part of you knows the way even if the rest of you forgot."

"I never said that."

"You said it in your sleep, then. Same thing." He worked at the bars with a file, quick and rhythmic. "Here's the truth of it, scribe. The Wolf King sent me to find you, but the temple wants you edited, and someone in the King's own court wants you dead, and I am the only person foolish enough to be standing between all three. So yes. I'm offering. But I need you to know it isn't charity. You owe my king a debt—your bloodline does, written in my ledger in your own impossible hand—and I collect debts. That's the whole of me. Don't mistake it for kindness."

I looked at him hanging there against the darkening sky, this man who had walked into my life carrying a book full of my handwriting and a century of lies, and I felt the grief in my chest turn over, the way a log turns in a fire—same wood, new flame.

"Good," I said. "Keep it that way. Kindness gets people caught."

The last bar gave with a sound like a snapped tooth.

* * *

We went out through the laundry chutes, because every fortress is built by people who forget the laundry. Then down through the herb gardens where I had spent my childhood pulling weeds for the infirmary, past the bell tower, along the drainage channel that smelled of rot and rain. Felix moved like water finding its level; I moved like someone coming home by a road she'd sworn she'd forgotten.

Because that was the strange thing. At the garden wall, my feet turned left before my mind decided anything. At the fork below the terraces, I chose the low path without knowing why, and thirty paces later we passed beneath a hawthorn hedge with a gap in it exactly wide enough for two people, and Felix stopped and stared at me.

"How did you—" he started.

"I don't know," I said. And for the first time, saying it felt less like fear and more like a door opening.

The temple's dogs bayed behind us, far off, then nearer. Torches bloomed along the upper walls like fever spots. We went through the hedge gap single file, down a goat track that wasn't on any map, into the black throat of the foothills.

At the tree line I stopped once and looked back. Thornfell sat on its crag with its lamps lit, every window warm, every bell faithful. Somewhere inside it was a desk with a worn hollow shaped like my elbow, and a life that had been small but mine, and I understood that I was watching my own funeral from the outside. The temple had declared me an error. Very well. Errors get corrected—or they get out.

I pressed my branded wrist to my chest, felt the raised scar pulse like a second heartbeat, and turned my back on the light.

"Felix," I said. "This road of yours. Where does it start?"

He glanced sideways at me, and in the dark his grin came back, but quieter now. "It starts where names change," he said. "There's a pass in the high mountains where the border between realms wears thin. Travelers lose things crossing it. Years. Faces. Names." He paused. "Your name especially, I think. Whatever it was. Before."

The wind came down off the peaks carrying a smell I had no word for—wild and cold and old, like snow over a fire—and somewhere far above us, on a ridge no temple eye could reach, something howled.

Not a wolf. Not quite a voice. Something between, calling a name I almost remembered.

I started walking.
