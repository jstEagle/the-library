## 5. The Pass Where Names Change

**Marguerite**

The pass had a name once. Every map in the Thornfell archive said so, in the careful copperplate of cartographers who never walked it. But somewhere along the ridge, between the last shrine and the first cairn, the name slid off the mountain like snow off slate, and travelers forgot they'd ever known it.

Felix called it the Hollow Tooth. He said it like a joke, tapping his pipe against the rock spire that split the sky above us. "Old smugglers' name. You don't say the real one up here. The real one says *you*."

I should have asked what he meant. I was trained to ask—ten years of copying prophecy teaches you that every strange phrase is a door. But my head felt wool-wrapped, soft at the edges, and I told myself it was the altitude.

It was not the altitude.

The first thing to go was small. I reached for Felix's name and found it, but behind it sat another name, unbidden: *Aurel.* Not mine. His, or something under his—I could feel it the way you feel a stone through your boot sole. I looked at him and thought, *his mother called him Aurel,* and then thought, *how do I know that?* and then thought nothing at all, because the thought dissolved like sugar on the tongue.

The wind came down the pass carrying voices. Not words—just the shape of words, the way a shell carries the sea. I stopped walking.

"Keep moving," Felix said. "You stop here, the pass starts reading you."

"Reading me?"

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

We climbed. The cairns grew taller as we went, stacked by hands unknown, and each one hummed faintly when we passed, like struck glass. My memories began to loosen—not lost, just *unfastened*, drifting up off their pegs. The smell of the archive's tallow candles. The weight of my indenture chain, cold on the ankle for the first year until I learned to sleep around it. My teacher's voice saying, *you copy, you do not read, reading is for those with names worth remembering.*

And underneath all of it, rising like groundwater: a smaller hand holding a smaller hand. A woman's laugh. A word spoken over me in a language I had no right to know, warm as bread—

*"Sefu."*

I said it out loud before I could stop myself. It fell into the pass like a coin into a well.

Felix turned. His face had gone the color of old paper.

"That's not yours," he said. "That's—" He pressed both fists to his temples. "That's mine. Or it was. Before."

Before what? I tried to ask. But the question wouldn't hold its shape, and neither would his face, and the pass breathed around us, patient as an archivist, turning our pages.

* * *

**Felix**

I've been robbed before. Knife at the throat in a Vellgate alleyway, purse cut in a ferry crowd, a partner who took the take and vanished into the Salt Marches. None of it prepared me for being robbed by a mountain.

The pass took things in order of how tightly I held them. My mother's face went first—I knew I'd had one, knew she'd sung, couldn't find the tune. Then the oath.

No. Not the oath. The oath stayed. The oath was too heavy to lift; the pass only pried loose what was wrapped around it.

I'd sworn it at fourteen, kneeling on frozen ground in the Wolf King's court, one hand flat on the King's own shadow because the King was too faded to touch anymore. *My years are borrowed years,* I'd said, *spent in his service until the debt clears.* And Marguerite—walking three paces ahead of me, shoulders set like a scholar refusing to flinch from a bad line of text—Marguerite's presence pulled the memory open like a ledger falling off a shelf.

Because the debt wasn't mine alone. That was the thing I'd buried deepest, deeper than the broken oath itself. The ledger I carried—the one that had started all this, the one with the prophecy copied out in a dead scribe's hand—had a page near the back I'd never shown them. A bloodline entry. *Owed: one mender, born twice-named.* And beside it, in the column where debts were marked paid or outstanding:

*Outstanding.*

I hadn't known whose bloodline. I'd told myself it didn't matter, that I was a courier, not a collector. But the pass didn't let me keep that lie. It shook it loose the way it shook everything loose, and suddenly I was fourteen again, kneeling, and the Wolf King's shadow was cold under my palm, and his voice—a voice like wind through a burnt forest—said:

*When the mender comes, you will know them by the second name.*

And now I did know. Sefu. The name had come out of Marguerite's mouth like something exhaled after a hundred years of holding breath.

They stopped walking. They were staring at me with an expression I recognized from temple windows: the look of someone seeing a saint depicted mid-suffering and finding it beautiful.

"You're doing it wrong," I said, which made no sense, but sense was leaking out of me fast. "You're supposed to keep walking. If you stand still, it reads you."

"It already has." Their voice was steady. Steadier than mine. "I saw your oath. I saw the ground you knelt on. I saw—" They hesitated, and I watched them decide whether to say it, and I loved them a little for hesitating, and hated the pass for making hesitation necessary. "I saw that you've known about me longer than I've known about myself."

The wind picked up. Somewhere below us, a cairn toppled with a sound like a closing book.

* * *

**Marguerite**

We made camp in the lee of the great spire, because Felix's legs gave out and because the light was failing and because there was no pretending anymore that we were two strangers sharing a road.

He built the fire without speaking. I watched his hands—smuggler's hands, scarred across the knuckles—and understood that I was seeing them now with knowledge I hadn't earned. The oath. The kneeling. A king too faded to be touched. And the ledger entry, which he confessed to in a voice scraped nearly hollow: *Owed: one mender, born twice-named.*

"Born twice-named," I repeated. "Sefu."

"Don't say it again," he said sharply. "Not up here. The pass collects names. You give it one, it keeps a piece of whoever owned it."

Too late, probably. I could feel the shape of the name sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal. Not mine—but lodged in me now, the way a splinter lodges, working toward the heart. I had spent ten years building a self out of blankness: no family name, no history, just *the scribe, indentured, obedient.* The temple had taken my past and I had thanked them for the quiet. And now a mountain had handed me back one fragment—one warm word, a woman's voice saying it over a child's head—and I wanted the rest with a hunger that frightened me more than any inquisitor.

"Felix." I used his smuggler's name deliberately, a gift, since the pass had stolen his other one. "What happens when we cross? Do we get ourselves back?"

He fed a stick to the fire. "Nobody gets themselves back. You come down the far side with holes where the pass dug, and new things in the holes. Things that aren't yours." He glanced at me, quick and guilty. "Or things that are yours, and you just never knew."

"The second name. You think it's really mine."

"I think the ledger doesn't lie. I think the covenant's dying and the prophecy named a mender born twice-named, and I think you stood in this pass and a hundred-year-old name came out of you like it had been waiting at the border the whole time." He looked away. "I think I was sent to find you before I ever met you, and I let myself forget that, because forgetting was easier than what it meant."

"What did it mean?"

"That I'm not your rescuer, Marguerite. I'm your delivery."

The fire cracked. Above us the spire leaned against the stars, and I felt the pass listening, patient, turning pages.

"No," I said.

He looked up.

"I've been delivered before. To a temple. To an indenture. To a life with the past cut out of it." I heard my own voice go hard, harder than I'd ever let it in the archive, where hardness was contraband. "Whatever you were sent to do, whatever the Wolf King owes my bloodline—you can carry me to that altar like a parcel, or you can walk beside me. Those are the only two roads off this mountain."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he laughed—short, rough, surprised out of him—and pulled the ledger from his coat and held it over the fire as if considering it, and didn't burn it, and put it away.

"Beside you," he said. "Fine. But you should know what walks beside you. The oath I swore, I broke. Three years ago. I ran from the court with this ledger because staying meant becoming the kind of hunter they send after people like you. I'm a deserter, Marguerite. Both courts want me dead. You're tied to the one man in either realm with nothing left to lose."

"Good," I said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"Nothing left to lose means nothing they can hold over you." I lay back against my pack and looked up at the spire. "I spent ten years being safe by being empty. I'm done with it."

He was quiet a long time. When I glanced over, he was watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere in the archive of my learning—open, undone, like a man whose mask had been read aloud.

* * *

**Felix**

She slept—no. *They* slept. I kept catching it, the pronoun sliding in my head, and each time it slid I understood a little more why the pass had brought me here: not to deliver Marguerite Okafor, scribe and heretic, but to meet the person the temple had erased and the prophecy had promised. Two names. One of them still hidden even from themself.

I took first watch because I didn't trust what the pass might feed me in dreams.

It fed me anyway. Half-dreaming against the spire, I saw the kneeling ground again, but this time the Wolf King's shadow spoke with Marguerite's voice, and the words weren't the oath—they were older, a blessing in a language I almost knew: *what was sundered by clever men, let the twice-named make whole.* And beneath the blessing, faint as watermark, a child's hand copying letters, and the letters were mine, and the ink was a century old.

I woke before dawn with my heart slamming and Marguerite already awake, watching me, hugging their knees.

"You saw something," they said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I dreamed it too. Your oath. And something else—someone writing with your hand a hundred years before you were born." They unfolded, stood, looked east where the pass descended toward the wolf-realm's grey light. "Felix, the pass isn't stealing from us. It's *trading*. It's showing us each other's pages."

"Then it knows what's coming."

"Then it's trying to prepare us." They shouldered their pack. In the first light their face was resolute and terrified in equal measure, and I thought: *this is what a mender looks like. This is what I was sent to find, and what I will not deliver.* "Come on. The far side by noon."

We started down. Behind us, at the summit cairn, something hummed—a long, satisfied note, like a ledger closing.

And ahead, on the switchbacks below, small figures in temple grey were climbing up to meet us, moving fast, and among them moved shapes that were not human at all.
