## 6. Hunters of the Hollow Altar

Felix smelled the trap before he saw it. That was the first thing his mother had taught him on the smuggler roads—not how to fight, not how to run, but how to smell a lie in the landscape. A clearing too quiet. A stream with no birdsong. A bend in the trail where the moss grew wrong.

The pass below Thornfell's shoulder was lying to him.

He held up a fist and Marguerite stopped behind him, close enough that he could hear their breath—quick, controlled, the breath of someone who had spent three days learning to be afraid and hated every minute of it. They'd been good about it. Better than he'd expected from an archivist. But fear sat on them like wet wool.

"What is it?" Marguerite whispered.

"Smoke," Felix said. "Old smoke. Two days, maybe three." He crouched and pressed two fingers into the ash of a dead fire ring, half-buried under fresh snowfall. "And this wasn't travelers. Travelers burn small and leave. This burned big and stayed."

"Temple?"

"No." He rubbed the ash between his fingers. It was greasy, tallow-rich, the kind of fuel that burned hot enough to melt snow into mud. Temple inquisitors used oil of consecration—clean-burning, scentless, sanctimonious right down to their campfires. "This is wolf-country fuel. Rendered fat. Bone-meal kindling."

Marguerite went very still. "Hunters."

"The King's own." Felix stood, brushing his hands clean, and tried to keep his voice level. "Which shouldn't be possible. The King himself sent me. His seal is on my commission. No hunter crosses the passes without his leave."

"Then whose leave did they have?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Felix had been turning it over since the second night, when he'd found a marker scratched into a birch trunk—a hunter's trail-sign, the double-slash that meant *prey sighted, holding*. He'd told himself it was old. Told himself a lot of things.

"We keep moving," he said. "But not through the clearing. There's a goat track above the tree line. Steeper, colder, but nobody watches a track that hurts."

Marguerite looked up at the gray sky, then at him, and something in their face made him uneasy—the flat calm of a person doing arithmetic they didn't like. "You think they're ahead of us or behind us?"

"Yes," Felix said.

* * *

They climbed for four hours. The goat track was everything Felix had promised and less: a scar of loose shale stitched across the mountainside, wide enough for one set of boots, dropping away on their left into a white nothing that would kill them before they finished screaming. Marguerite walked it without complaint, which Felix had learned to read as its own kind of warning. Scribes, he was discovering, were like crossbows. Quiet until loaded.

At midday they stopped in the lee of a boulder to eat the last of the hard bread. Felix divided it with the precision of a man who had gone hungry before and intended to again, and watched Marguerite chew their share slowly, eyes on the valley below.

"They knew where we'd cross," Marguerite said at last. Not a question.

"The pass is the only way east that isn't temple-patrolled. If I were hunting us—"

"You're not hunting us. You're *with* us. That's the part I keep having to remember." There was no venom in it. That was worse. "Your King sent you. Your King's hunters are trying to take our heads. Someone gave them orders, Felix, and it wasn't the man who signed your commission."

Felix said nothing, because there was nothing honest to say. He thought instead of the Wolf King as he'd last seen him: vast and gray-muzzled in the ruined court of Hallowmere, his flanks translucent in places, showing not bone but *absence*—holes in the world where the world had forgotten to hold together. The covenant failing didn't just fray borders. It frayed kings. And a dying king's court was a pit of vipers all pretending to grieve.

"There's one name I haven't said out loud," Felix said finally. "Handler who gave me this job. Veyra. She runs the King's eastern hunts—knows every pass, every cache, every hunter by name. If someone wanted the covenant to die quietly, she's the one who could make it happen and call it loyalty."

"And you trust her?"

"I trusted her to give me a true commission." He turned the bread crust over in his fingers. "I'm no longer certain the commission *was* true. Maybe the King really did send me to find you. Or maybe Veyra sent me to find you so that someone else could follow me to you. I might not be your guide, scribe. I might be your bait."

Marguerite considered this with that terrible archival patience. Then: "If you were bait, you'd have been told what happens after the trap springs. Were you?"

No. He hadn't been told anything after. He'd been given a ledger, a seal, and a road, and Veyra had smiled at him with all her teeth and said *bring back the scribe who remembers both names*, and he'd been too desperate to clear his own debts to ask why a dying king needed a scribe brought anywhere.

"No," he admitted.

"Then we're both bait," Marguerite said, and handed him back the last of the crust. "Eat. Bait should be well-fed. It looks better on the hook."

* * *

The ambush came at dusk, and it came wrong.

Felix had chosen their camp for the night with care—a hollow beneath an overhang, one approach, stone at their backs. He'd strung his tripwire across the approach, banked a fire small enough to hide, and slept in shifts while Marguerite kept watch with a knife they held like a quill.

What woke him was silence. The fire had gone out. Not died—*gone out*, snuffed, and the cold that pressed into the hollow was the wrong kind of cold, the moving kind.

"Wake up," he breathed, and Marguerite's eyes were already open.

They came down the slope in shapes that barely qualified as shapes—wolf-hunters in their gray leathers, faces masked, moving with the terrible economy of people who had done this a hundred times and enjoyed perhaps forty of them. Three of them. Behind them, further up the slope, lantern-light bobbed: the inquisitors, letting the hunters do the bleeding work.

The tripwire sang. Felix was already rolling when the crossbow bolt buried itself in the ground where his chest had been.

After that it was noise and motion. He got a knife into the first hunter's thigh and took a boot to the ribs that folded him like wet parchment. Marguerite—gods, the scribe—didn't scream, didn't freeze; they swept the second hunter's legs with the pack strap and drove their knife up under a masked jaw with a fluency that Felix would have questions about later, if there was a later. The third hunter hesitated, and in that hesitation Felix saw the mask turn—not toward him, but toward Marguerite—and heard the words come out muffled and deliberate:

"*The bloodline dies tonight. King's mercy.*"

King's mercy. The King's phrase, in a renegade's mouth, spoken over a scribe the King had supposedly ordered found alive.

Felix put his knife through the hunter's throat before the sentence could finish being a sentence.

Then the lanterns came down the slope, and there was no more time for thinking. He grabbed Marguerite, grabbed the pack—half its straps cut, supplies spilling—and ran, down the hollow's far side, into the dark, with inquisitor voices ringing off the stone behind them chanting words that made the night itself lean toward them like a hand.

* * *

They ran until the chants faded, until the slope gentled into pine forest, until Felix's ribs screamed and Marguerite's breath came in ragged hooks. Then, in the black shelter of a lightning-split fir, they stopped, and Felix finally let himself fold against the trunk and take inventory.

Ribs: cracked, probably two of them. Knife: still his. Pack: gutted. The dried meat gone, the rope gone, the healing salve gone—everything scattered on the mountainside except the ledger, which he'd strapped to his chest like a second heart, and Marguerite, who was kneeling beside him in the dark, wiping their knife on pine needles with hands that had stopped shaking only recently.

"You've killed before," Felix said. It came out rougher than he meant.

"No," Marguerite said. "I've *copied* before. Battle accounts. Execution records. You learn where the blade goes if you write about it enough times." A pause. "I don't recommend knowing it this way."

"Felix." Their voice changed—flattened, careful. "The hunter spoke. Before you—" They stopped. Started again. "*King's mercy.* Those were the Wolf King's own words. His formula for execution. Why would his hunters say it over me, when his commission says bring me alive?"

Because the commission was a lie. Because the King—or the thing wearing the King's authority—had never wanted Marguerite found. He'd wanted them *ended*, quietly, far from Hallowmere, and Felix had been the leash and the blindfold both.

"I have to go back," Felix said. His voice sounded strange to him, stripped of its usual smuggler's polish. "To Hallowmere. After the Altar. Someone in that court has to answer for this, and I'm starting to suspect it's the person who taught me which end of a knife to hold."

"That's tomorrow's problem." Marguerite settled beside him against the split trunk, close enough that their shoulder touched his, deliberately, an anchor. "Tonight's problem is that we have no food, no rope, no salve, and two days' walk to the Hollow Altar through country that wants us dead."

"And yet," Felix said, "you're still here. Could've run during the fight. Inquisitors would've taken you back, maybe spared you. Instead you killed a man for me."

"For the covenant," Marguerite corrected. But their shoulder stayed where it was, warm through two layers of wool, and neither of them pretended anymore.

Felix closed his eyes. Somewhere above them, on the mountain, inquisitor lanterns were sweeping the slopes like slow yellow eyes, and somewhere beyond the passes, in a court of fading grandeur, a traitor was counting his failure like coin. He had served the Wolf King for nine years and trusted exactly one person in all that time, and he was sitting next to them.

"Dawn in four hours," he said. "We move at first light. There's an old shrine road past the ridge—hermits use it. Where there are hermits, there are archives, and where there are archives—"

"—there are answers," Marguerite finished softly. "And I have a question I've carried since the archive floor, Felix. About my handwriting in a book a century old." In the dark, Felix felt more than saw them look at him. "I think whoever built this trap built it a hundred years ago. And I think the hermit roads are where we'll learn who."

Above the split fir, the clouds tore open, and moonlight fell through the pines onto the two of them—bait, survivors, debt-bound—and lit, briefly, the ledger against Felix's chest, where the ink of a hundred borrowed years waited to be read.
