## 7. A Century of Small Betrayals

The hermit's house was built into the spine of the mountain, which meant it had no right angles and no warmth that didn't have to be argued for. Marguerite sat close to the peat fire while the old man—Brother Anselm, though he'd shed the title like a snake shedding skin—pulled books from a wall of niches with the tenderness of someone handling sleeping children.

Felix stood watch at the door, one hand never far from his knife. He hadn't slept since the ambush. Neither had Marguerite, but Felix wore it differently: like armor he refused to take off.

"You said you'd read the whole prophecy," Marguerite said. "Not the fragments. Not the sanctioned excerpts. All of it."

Anselm lowered himself onto a stool across from them. His eyes were milky at the edges but sharp in the center, the eyes of a man who had spent sixty years reading by bad light.

"I said I'd *show* you," he corrected. "There's a difference. Reading it is easy. Understanding what it costs—" he tapped his own chest, "—that took me forty years."

"Then let me pay it quickly," Marguerite said. "We don't have forty."

Something flickered in the old man's face—not amusement, exactly. Recognition. "No," he agreed. "You don't."

He set three books on the table between them. The first was the Thornfell codex, the official version, the one Marguerite had copied a hundred times until the words lived behind their eyelids. The second was older, its leather scarred with water damage, its margins dense with a hand Marguerite now knew better than their own.

The third book was bound in grey. No title. No ornament. Just a plain cover and a wax seal long since broken.

Anselm opened the grey book first.

"The temple teaches that the covenant between the realms was made by mutual consent," he said. "Two kings, two oaths, one altar. A marriage of worlds, sealed in blood and ink." He turned a page. "That is the story we were given. Here is the story that was written."

Marguerite leaned forward. The script inside was cramped, urgent—the handwriting of someone writing fast, or frightened, or both.

*"They did not agree to the covenant,"* Marguerite read aloud, translating as they went. *"The Wolf King came to the altar under truce and left bound. The human signatories used the rite itself as a snare—wove the binding into the words of welcome so that the moment he spoke his half, the pact closed around him like a jaw."*

Felix had gone very still by the door.

"The founding of Thornfell," Anselm said quietly, "was not a treaty. It was an ambush. And the covenant has been failing ever since, because a bond built on a lie cannot hold. Every hundred years it frays. Every hundred years the temple sends someone to patch it—and every hundred years, they send someone expendable."

Marguerite's hands had gone cold. "The scribes they send to the Hollow Altar."

"Never return," Anselm confirmed. "Officially, they die in service. Unofficially—" he shrugged, "—the rite demands memory as fuel. The temple knows this. They've always known. That's why they choose indentured scribes. People whose lives are already owned. Whose absence will be recorded as debt discharged rather than loss mourned."

*People like me,* Marguerite thought. *Exactly like me.*

"And my handwriting," they said. "In a century-old book."

Anselm turned to the second volume, the water-scarred one, and opened it to a marked page. There it was again—that looping hand, Marguerite's own, recording a prophecy about "the scribe who remembers both names."

"This is the work of Sera Okafor," Anselm said. "A temple archivist. Dead a hundred years this winter."

"My name is Marguerite Okafor."

"Yes," Anselm said. "I know. Look closer at the genealogical notes."

Marguerite looked. Their breath caught. In the margin, in that impossible familiar hand, ran a line Marguerite had never seen in any sanctioned text:

*"The line must be tended. Each generation, one child of the blood, raised blind, kept ignorant, placed where the temple can use them when the hour comes. The gift skips and hides, but it does not die. When the covenant fails, the rememberer will be ready—or will not be, and the realms will tear along the seam."*

"They bred us for it," Marguerite whispered. The words tasted like ash. "My family. For a hundred years. Maybe longer. They planted us in the archive like seeds in a field, waiting for harvest."

"Not the temple," Anselm said. "Sera did. She broke from them. She saw what they'd done at the founding, and she spent her life building a counterweight—a lineage of rememberers, hidden inside the very institution that would use them. Your gift isn't their design, Marguerite. It's her rebellion, passed down like a candle through a hundred years of dark rooms."

"But the temple raised me."

"The temple raised you *blind*. There's a difference. They think they've been keeping a useful tool dull. Sera's work made sure the tool remembers how to be a blade."

Felix crossed the room slowly and sat down beside Marguerite. He didn't touch them. He just sat there, solid and present, while the floor of Marguerite's whole life tilted beneath them.

"All those years copying prophecies," Marguerite said. Their voice sounded far away. "All those years I told myself I was invisible. Safe. I wasn't safe. I was *stored*. Like a relic on a shelf. Waiting for them to need me."

"Yes."

"And when they needed me—"

"They would have sent you to the Altar," Anselm finished. "Alone. Uninformed. To pour your memories into a lie and call it devotion. As they've done to every rememberer before you who trusted them."

Marguerite thought of the names in the archive rolls. Scribes sent on pilgrimage who never came back. There had been so many over the centuries, and Marguerite had catalogued their departures without once wondering why the returns column stayed empty. The fury arrived all at once, hot and total, rising from somewhere deeper than thought.

"They knew," Marguerite said. "The inquisitors. The high archivists. They've known for a hundred years what the rite costs, and they fed people into it anyway. Generation after generation. My mother's line. *My mother.*"

"Your mother was of the blood too," Anselm said gently. "She hid you as deep as she could. It wasn't enough. But it was love, doing what it could."

Marguerite pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. Behind the darkness, the archive rose up—the smell of vellum and lamp oil, the rhythm of the copying desk, the small warm certainties of a life. All of it a cage dressed as a calling. All of it built on an ambush and maintained by amnesia.

When they lowered their hands, the fury was still there. But it had changed shape. It had cooled into something with edges.

"Then we finish it," Marguerite said. "But not their way. Not blind, not alone, not as fuel for their lie. We go to the Hollow Altar and reforge the covenant *true*—built on what actually happened, witnessed, written down. If the rite demands memory, then let it take memory freely given, in service of a bond honestly made."

Anselm studied them for a long moment. Then he began, deliberately, to gather the three books together.

"That's why I'm giving you these," he said. "The codex, the prophecy, and the true account of the founding. Proof travels badly, but it travels. What you write at the Altar should stand on evidence, not faith."

He wrapped them in oilcloth and held them out—and paused.

"One more thing. The last page of the grey book. Sera wrote it the night before the temple found her. Read it before you sleep tonight. Not before."

"Why not?"

"Because it changes what you'll dream," Anselm said simply. "And you'll want your wits about you tomorrow. The pass ahead is the traitor's ground. Someone in the Wolf King's court sold your route—I'd stake my library on it. And Sera's last page tells you who."

Felix's head came up sharply. "You know who it is?"

"I know what Sera suspected," Anselm said. "And if she was right, then the person hunting you isn't working for the temple at all. Take the books. Take the road south before dawn. And whatever you do—" his milky eyes fixed on Marguerite, and for a moment they seemed to see straight through a hundred years, "—don't let anyone tell you what your sacrifice is worth. Decide that yourself. It's the only thing they never managed to steal from us."

Marguerite took the bundle. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Outside, the wind had shifted, carrying the smell of snow and something else—fur, faintly, and smoke. Felix noticed it too; his hand moved to his knife.

"Hunters?" Marguerite asked.

"No," Felix said slowly, listening to some instinct older than either realm. "Worse. That's a summons. Someone's called a moot of the packs." He looked at Marguerite, and in the firelight his face was grim. "The Wolf King wants to see you himself. Tonight. And Marguerite—if the traitor is who Anselm thinks, the King may already be dead."

* * *

That night, by the last of the peat fire, Marguerite unwrapped the grey book and turned to the final page.

Sera Okafor's hand shook here, the letters leaning like trees in wind. One line only:

*"The traitor wears the King's own collar. Trust the ledger-bearer—but check his debts. One of them is yours."*

Marguerite looked up across the dying fire at Felix, asleep sitting up against the door, his ledger still strapped to his chest.

One of his debts was theirs.
