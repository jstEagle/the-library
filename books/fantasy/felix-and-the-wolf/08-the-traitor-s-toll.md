## 8. The Traitor's Toll

**FELIX**

The tollhouse at Greywater Ford had been abandoned for thirty years, if the moss on its roof was any measure. Felix knew it from his smuggling days—a squat stone box wedged between the river and the cliff road, one door, no windows worth the name. A good place to hide. A good place to be cornered.

He knew they were being followed before Marguerite did. He'd been counting the signs all morning: crows lifting off the ridgeline in a straight line, like a hand dragging a comb through feathers; the absence of other travelers on a road that should have carried pilgrims; and once, faintly, the smell of temple incense where no temple was.

So when he saw the lantern light in the tollhouse window—warm, steady, *waiting*—his stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"Someone's in there," he said.

Marguerite stopped beside him in the shadow of the pines. They were thin now, thinner than when he'd first seen them bent over that ledger in Thornfell's back alley, and their eyes had the flat shine of someone who had stopped sleeping to save time. "Inquisitors?"

"No. Inquisitors don't light lamps. They want you to see them coming." He shifted the pack on his shoulder—the pack that held what remained of their supplies after the ambush, half of nothing. "This is an invitation."

"From whom?"

Felix didn't answer, because he already knew, and saying it out loud would make it real. There was only one person who would leave a lamp burning for him on this road. Only one person who knew every smuggler's shortcut between the mountains and the sea, because he'd taught Felix all of them himself.

*Master Vessik.* His handler. The man who had bought his debt at fourteen, taught him to read contracts as weapons, and never once—not once in eleven years—raised his voice.

"He'll want to talk," Felix said finally.

"Then we should decide," Marguerite said quietly, "what you're willing to hear before you walk through that door."

* *

The tollhouse smelled of woodsmoke and old stone. Vessik sat at the tollkeeper's desk like it was his own, hands folded, silver-haired, dressed not in temple grey but in traveling leathers—the uniform of a man who had come a long way personally because the matter mattered. He looked up when Felix entered, and smiled the way a banker smiles: pleased with the account so far.

"Felix. You look terrible."

"You look rich for a dead man's servant." Felix kept his hand near his knife. Marguerite stood just inside the doorway, back to the wall, exactly where he'd want them. Good. One of them was thinking clearly.

"The Wolf King is dying, not dead, and I serve the crown, not the corpse." Vessik gestured to a stool. "Sit. You've made me chase you across two ranges. The least you owe me is your attention."

"I owe you nothing. That was the whole point of the ledger."

"Ah." Vessik's smile thinned by a degree. "Yes. The ledger. Do you have it with you?"

"Why do you care? You wrote half the entries yourself."

"And I've spent the last month unwriting them." For the first time, something moved behind the old man's eyes—something tired, or hungry, or both. "Felix. Sit down. Let me make you an offer, and then let me tell you what happens if you refuse it. In that order, because I respect you enough to lead with the carrot."

Felix didn't sit. But he listened, and hated himself for listening.

* *

**MARGUERITE**

Marguerite watched the handler the way they'd learned to watch inquisitors: not at his face but at his hands. Hands told the truth. Vessik's hands were still, folded, patient—and his thumb moved once, twice, over the signet ring he wore, a small habit of a man rehearsing comfort he did not feel.

They caught fragments of the conversation through the door's gap. Words about absolution. About debts forgiven. About *delivering*—and Marguerite's own name folded inside that word like a knife in cloth.

They had known this might come. Not this precisely—they hadn't known the face of it—but some part of them had understood since the pass, since Felix had seen their stolen childhood name written in snow-melt and said nothing cruel about it, that love was a lever and someone, eventually, would find it.

The question was whether Felix would break.

Marguerite had watched him burn through fear, exhaustion, ambush, betrayal. He was loyal the way rivers are loyal—to the shape of the land, following the lowest path. And duty was a very low path indeed. Eleven years carved into a man doesn't wash out in one mountain crossing.

But then—

Then Felix laughed. Short, disbelieving, ugly. And Marguerite heard him say, clear enough to carry through the door:

"*Absolution.* You're offering me *absolution*. From whom? The King who sold my mother's debt to buy your silence? From the court that sent hunters after us—your hunters, Vessik, your seal was on their writs?" A pause. "You're not the crown's man. You're the crack in it. You've been the crack all along."

And Marguerite understood, with the cold clarity of ink drying, that the offer was real and the trap was real and both things could be true at once. Vessik wanted the covenant dead. Vessik also wanted Felix alive. Those two desires had just collided in a ruined tollhouse, and only one of them could walk out.

* *

**FELIX**

"Deliver the scribe," Vessik said, "and everything is forgiven. Every entry. Eleven years, struck clean. You walk back into the King's court with your head up and your name unstained. You could even keep the girl—or the boy, or whatever the scribe is; I confess I haven't checked, and it doesn't matter to the arithmetic." He spread his hands. "One delivery, Felix. You've made a hundred harder ones."

Felix stood very still.

That was the thing about Vessik—he never lied outright. He built truths into shapes that lied for him. Everything he'd just said was technically accurate and entirely rotten, and Felix felt the old machinery of himself start turning anyway, the gears installed at fourteen: *the debt, the debt, the debt.*

His broken oath. The village of Ashfen. The ledger's first page, written in his own blood because Vessik had insisted oaths bind better that way. Eleven years of carrying it.

"One delivery," Felix repeated.

"One delivery."

"And if I refuse?"

Vessik sighed, and for a moment he looked genuinely old, genuinely sorry. "Then the hunters take you both at the Altar, the covenant dies, the wolf realm comes apart at the seams, and everything your realm has bled to hold together for three hundred years unravels in your lifetime. You'll have your clean conscience and a short life to enjoy it in." He tilted his head. "I'd hoped you'd be smarter than the romantic sort, Felix. It's always the romantic sorts who die badly."

Felix reached into his coat.

Vessik's hands came off the desk fast—for an old man, fast—but Felix wasn't going for the knife. He brought out the ledger. The debt-ledger. Blood-written first page, eleven years of entries, every borrowed year and broken promise and small cruelty recorded in his own cramped hand. The book that had started all of this, the book that had matched word for word a century-old prophecy, the book that had been chains and compass both.

He'd carried it since Thornfell. Through the archive, through the flight, through the pass where names change, through the ambush. He had told himself he kept it because it held the map to the Altar. Because it held the prophecy. Because it was evidence.

All true. None of it the reason.

There was a brazier by the cold hearth—someone had lit it against the damp, coals still orange. Vessik saw where Felix was looking and stood up so fast his stool went over.

"Felix. That ledger is a crown instrument. That ledger is *three hundred years of the realm's obligations*—"

"It's a leash," Felix said. "And I know who's holding it."

He threw it into the coals.

* *

**MARGUERITE**

Later, Marguerite would remember the sound more than the sight: the wet gasp of old vellum catching flame, the sudden stink of blood-ink burning—a smell like iron and rot and, strangely, like rain. The fire took the pages greedily, entries curling black, names of debtors and creditors alike rising off the paper as smoke.

Vessik lunged for the brazier and Felix stepped into his path, and for one suspended moment the two of them stood chest to chest, the old man's composure cracked clean through, showing the thing underneath: not anger. *Loss.* A collector watching his collection burn.

"You fool," Vessik breathed. "You absolute—do you know what you've done? Without the ledger there's no record of the debt against the scribe's bloodline. No record means no claim. No claim means the covenant rite can't be *contested*—" He stopped. Recalculated. And Marguerite watched, sick and fascinated, as the truth crossed his face: Felix hadn't just freed himself. He'd burned away the legal weapon anyone—temple, court, traitor—could have used to stop the reforging.

"Yes," Felix said softly. "I do know what I've done."

Guards. Of course there were guards—two of them, moving in from the shadows of the back room, hunter's grey with the Wolf King's sigil defaced. Marguerite came off the wall with the only weapon they had left: the hermit archivist's flask of sanctified ink, thrown hard into the nearest guard's eyes. The man screamed. Felix's knife did the rest of the argument, fast and ugly, and then they were running, out the tollhouse door, into the dark along the river road while Vessik's voice followed them—not shouting, never shouting, that was the horror of it—calling evenly into the night:

"The Altar will be walled, Felix! Walled and waiting! Bring your scribe, bring your love, bring your clean conscience—it changes nothing! Some debts are older than books!"

* *

**FELIX**

They ran until the tollhouse lights were gone and the river noise swallowed everything, and then Felix stopped, hands on knees, lungs full of knives, and laughed—breathless, unhinged, free.

Marguerite stared at him. "What is wrong with you?"

"I'm lighter." He straightened up. "Eleven years, Marguerite. I've been carrying that book since I was fourteen years old, and I keep reaching for it and it's *gone*, and every time it's gone it feels like—" He shook his head, couldn't find the word, gave up. "I told him no. Did you hear? I told him no and it was easy. It was the easiest thing I've ever done."

Marguerite was quiet a moment. Firelight from the distant tollhouse glinted off the river behind them. "He offered you everything," they said. "Your name. Your freedom. Your oath mended."

"He offered me myself, back." Felix looked at them—at the scribe who remembered both names, whose bloodline was written into a covenant he barely understood, who had run with him through hell on smuggler roads and never once asked him to be anything but what he was. "And I realized I like who I am better than who I was supposed to be. That's all absolution ever was. A smaller self, pre-approved." He took their hand. "I choose this one. The bigger one. The unfinished one."

Marguerite's fingers closed around his, cold and certain. "The Altar's a day ahead."

"And walled and waiting."

"Then we go tonight. While they think we're licking our wounds."

Felix grinned into the dark, ledgerless, debtless, hunted from two realms, richer than he'd ever been. "Lead on, scribe. You know these roads better than I do."

They turned north, toward the Hollow Altar, toward the walls and the waiting—and somewhere behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound of a horn: long, low, and getting closer.
