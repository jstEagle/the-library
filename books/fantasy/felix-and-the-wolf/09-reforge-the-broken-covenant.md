## 9. Reforge the Broken Covenant

The Hollow Altar was not hollow at all. That was the first lie the mountain told us, and I should have known better than to trust a mountain that had already eaten my childhood.

We came down into the caldera at dusk, and the world was wrong there. Not broken—*unsewn*. The sky above the crater showed two dawns at once, one gold and one silver, bleeding into each other along a seam that pulsed like an infected wound. On one side of the basin grew Thornfell's black pines; on the other, trees I had no name for, silver-barked and leafless and *breathing*, their branches expanding and contracting like ribs. Where the two forests met, the ground flickered—stone, then moss, then something translucent that hurt to look at directly.

Felix stopped at the crater's rim and would not go further. I turned and found him gripping the strap of his satchel—the empty satchel, the ledger burned to ash in his hands three days ago—and his face had gone the color of old tallow.

"I can't," he said. "Marguerite. I can't cross that."

"You crossed the pass where names change."

"The pass only blurred things." He swallowed. "This is the seam itself. It knows what I am. It knows what I owe." His voice dropped. "It knows what I did."

I took his hand off the strap and held it. His fingers were cold as river stones.

"Then stay here," I said. "Watch the trail. If the inquisitors come—and they will come—you're the only warning we get."

"That's not what I—" He shut his mouth. Opened it again. Whatever he meant to say, he'd said versions of it every night since the toll bridge, and I had learned to let him keep some words unspent. "Come back out. That's all. Come back out of there, or none of this was worth anything."

"I'll come back out," I said, and lied, because he needed the lie more than I needed honesty. "Now give me the ash."

He reached into the satchel and brought out a small clay jar stoppered with wax. Inside: the burnt remains of his debt-ledger, a century of borrowed years and cross-realm debts reduced to grey powder. The hermit's book had been explicit. *The covenant was sealed in ink once. Let it be resealed in ink. What is written must be paid; what is burned must be given.*

Felix pressed the jar into my hands and held on a moment too long.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'm glad it was your handwriting I found. In the ledger. All those years I carried it and wondered whose hand that was." A crooked almost-smile. "I'm glad it was yours."

Then he let go, and I walked down into the fraying world alone.

* * *

The Altar stood at the exact center of the seam, and it was exactly what its name promised: a ring of stone with nothing inside it. No table, no idol, no carved gods. Just a circle of standing stones around an absence, and the absence was the oldest thing I had ever seen. Older than the temple. Older than the prophecy. It looked at me the way my own handwriting had looked at me from a book written before my birth—with terrible familiarity.

I set the jar down at the center. I unwrapped the hermit's page from against my ribs, where I had carried it through ambush and storm and Felix's burning of everything he owned. The rite was short. That was the cruelty of it. A hundred years of engineered bloodlines, two realms unraveling at the seams, my whole life spent copying prophecies in a cold scriptorium—and the mending of it came down to nine lines of instruction.

*I. Stand within the absence.*

*I speak the first name I was given.*

*I speak the name they took from me.*

*I speak each name between, oldest first.*

*And when the names are spent, if the seam still gapes, I give what remains: the memory of having had a name at all.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because my hands were shaking so badly the letters swam.

They had told me, in the archive, in the hermit's smoke-dark hut, that the rite demanded memory. I had understood it the way you understand arithmetic—abstractly, at a distance. Standing in the absence now, with the two dawns bleeding overhead and the silver trees breathing their slow breath, I understood it in my body. Every name I spoke would be a name I no longer possessed. Not hidden. Not buried. *Gone.* Eaten by the seam, the way the temple had eaten my heritage a name at a time when I was too young to fight.

I thought of Felix at the rim, waiting for someone to come back out who would not entirely be me.

Then I stepped into the absence.

* * *

The moment my foot crossed the ring of stones, the seam noticed me.

There is no other word for it. The fraying edge between worlds turned its attention on me the way a wound turns its attention on a hand reaching toward it—not welcoming, not hostile, simply *aware*, and hungry in the way wounds are hungry. The air thickened. My heartbeat slowed until each beat landed separately, like footsteps in an empty corridor.

I opened the jar and tipped the ash into my palm.

"Ink remembers," I said, using the hermit's words, because my own had deserted me. "What is burned must be given."

I spoke the first name.

Not aloud—I couldn't have. It rose in me from somewhere below thought, below memory, from the bedrock of me where the temple's erasures had never quite reached, and it came up my throat and out of my mouth in a language I did not know and somehow had always known. Two syllables. They tasted of woodsmoke and a woman's hands braiding my hair.

The seam drank them.

I felt it happen—a small, clean *pull*, like a thread drawn out of cloth. And then the taste was gone. Woodsmoke, gone. The woman's hands, gone. I knew there had been a woman; I could no longer remember why that mattered, or whether she had mattered, or whether *mattering* was even the right shape for what she had been to me.

Terror hit me then, full in the chest, and I want to be honest about that. This was not the clean sacrifice of the ballads. This was being unmade in installments while my own mouth worked against me. My body wanted to run. My legs locked. Somewhere far above, thunder rolled across the caldera—or hooves, I couldn't tell anymore, the sounds of both worlds bleeding together at the seam.

I spoke the second name.

Three syllables, tasting of snow and a door I had been carried through, screaming. Gone. The snow, gone. The door, gone. The screaming—mine, I realized, mine, a child's terror—gone, and worse than gone: I could no longer remember that I had ever screamed, only that something in my chest remembered the *shape* of having screamed, an echo of an echo.

The seam widened. Above me, the two dawns pulled apart like lips opening, and through the gap I saw—not sky. Something else. A third light, patient and vast, waiting behind the worlds the way a page waits behind the writing.

A fourth name. A fifth. Each one a small death. Each one a room in the house of myself going dark, and me standing in the dark knowing only that rooms were going dark, not what had been in them.

By the seventh name I was crying without knowing what I mourned. By the eighth, I had forgotten Felix's face—but not Felix. That was the strange mercy of it. The seam took the *names* first, the specificities, the furniture of a life. What it left, what it could not take because it was not made of memory at all, was the weight of him. The way my chest oriented toward the crater's rim like a compass needle. Love, it turned out, was not stored in the memories. It was stored deeper, in whatever part of me had copied a prophecy a hundred times and never known why the words felt like home.

The ninth name was my own.

I felt it rise—the true one, the birth-name, the one the temple had cut out of me before I could walk, the one the prophecy called *the scribe who remembers both names*. I felt it rise and I understood, finally, the last line of the rite. The nine names were payment. But the tenth thing—the memory of having had a name at all, the selfhood underneath all names—that was the seal. That was what would close the seam forever.

That was what I would not carry out of this circle.

Hooves now, definitely hooves, hammering down the far trail. Torchlight spilling over the crater's rim, and voices—Temple voices, chanting the Rite of Unmaking, the counter-rite, the one designed to kill the covenant rather than mend it. They had found us. Of course they had found us. And somewhere on that trail, I knew, Felix was standing between them and me with nothing but a smuggler's lies and an empty satchel.

I looked at the ash in my palm. Almost gone. Enough for one name.

"Here is what I remember," I said aloud, in plain Thornfell speech, because the seam deserved to hear it in the language of the life I was spending. "I remember being afraid my whole life. I remember hiding so well I hid from myself. I remember a smuggler with a ledger full of my handwriting, and I remember choosing him. I don't remember my mother's face. I don't remember my name. But I remember *that I had one*. I remember that I was someone before I was a scribe, before I was a heretic, before I was a prophecy. And I give that remembering freely—not to the temple, not to the Wolf King, but to the seam itself, so that both worlds will hold."

I tipped the last of the ash into the absence.

The ninth name left me.

* * *

There was no pain. There was no transcendence either, not the way the hymns promise it. There was only a vast, quiet *opening*, as if a fist I had been making of myself my entire life finally unclenched.

The seam closed.

I felt it close the way you feel a bone set—horrible and right at once. The two dawns snapped together into one ordinary evening sky. The silver trees stilled. The absence inside the ring of stones filled with plain grey mountain rock, as though it had always been there, as though nothing had ever been asked of anyone.

The torchlight reached the altar. Boots on stone. A voice I recognized—inquisitor's voice, the one from the archive, the one who had seen my impossible handwriting—shouting words of unmaking that no longer had anything to unmake.

And I stood in the middle of the closed covenant, whole and hollow, holding a blank page where the hermit's instructions had been, unable to remember what I had come here to do or why my face was wet or whose name I had just fed to the dark.

Only one thing remained in me, glowing like an ember in a burnt house:

Someone was coming down the trail. Someone I loved. I didn't know how I knew it. I didn't know his name, or his face, or what he had burned for me.

But I knew I would know him when I saw him.

I turned toward the torchlight, and waited.
