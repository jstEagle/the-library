## 10. Two Names, One Road

The silence after the sealing lasted longer than any silence Marguerite had ever known.

Not the silence of the archive, which was really the sound of a thousand small scratches—pens, page-turns, the temple's slow breathing. Not the silence of the mountain passes, which was wind and stone pretending to be empty. This was the silence of two worlds holding their breath at once, and then, slowly, letting it out together.

Marguerite knelt at the Hollow Altar with their hands flat against the stone and felt the covenant close like a wound knitting. It was not a sound so much as a sensation—a great seam drawn tight through the marrow of everything. The frayed edge where the human realm had been unraveling away from the wolf realm pulled taut, and the pull went through Marguerite's chest, through the place where their memories had been, and out the other side.

They had fed the altar everything. The name their mother whispered over a cradle in a village that no longer appeared on any map. The name the temple gave them when they took that one away. The face of the woman who had carried them across the border at night, singing low so the wardens wouldn't hear. The taste of the bread she'd broken with them at dawn. The word for *home* in a language Marguerite could no longer remember existing.

Name by name. Memory by memory. The altar drank, and the realms knit, and Marguerite stayed.

That was the miracle, if it was one. Not the sealing. The staying.

* * *

Felix found them still kneeling when the light changed.

The inquisitors had come through the eastern breach of the altar chamber an hour before—or had it been minutes? Time moved strangely now that the covenant held, as if the two realms had agreed to stop lying to each other about how long things took. The inquisitors had stood in the doorway with their iron brands and their warrants, and then one of them, a young woman with ash on her cheeks, had looked at the sealed altar and at Marguerite kneeling before it, and had slowly, deliberately, turned her brand around and set it down on the stone.

The others had followed. Not out of mercy. Out of recognition. You cannot arrest a hinge for holding a door.

"They're gone," Felix said now, crouching beside Marguerite. His voice was rough. He had burned his ledger two nights ago, and his hands still smelled of smoke and old ink. "Down the mountain. The temple will spin it however they need to—*the heretic ascended, the covenant was renewed by temple rite all along.* They've already started. I heard them arguing about the wording on the way out."

"Does it matter?" Marguerite asked. Their own voice sounded foreign. Lighter. Emptied.

Felix considered it honestly, the way he considered everything—like a debt to be assessed. "No," he said. "I don't think it does."

Marguerite tried to stand. Their legs had gone numb from the stone, and Felix caught their elbow without making a ceremony of it, and for a moment they stayed like that—leaning into him, the wolf-smell of him, pine and smoke and the cold iron of the passes—while the altar behind them hummed its low, satisfied note.

"I don't remember," Marguerite said. "I thought I would feel the shape of what I gave. The way you feel a pulled tooth. But there's just—" They pressed a hand to their sternum. "Room. A lot of room."

Felix was quiet a moment. "I remember some of it," he said. "The pass showed me your mother's face. The hermit's fire showed me the rest. I could give it back to you, piece by piece, if you wanted. I'd be a poor ledger-keeper if I couldn't."

Marguerite looked at him. There was a time—a life ago, three chapters ago, a whole self ago—when the offer would have felt like theft. When every scrap of their hidden past had been the only thing they owned, guarded more carefully than their life.

"No," they said, and heard the peace in it, and were surprised by the peace. "Don't. If it comes back on its own, it comes back. But I don't want to spend the rest of my life being a reliquary." They smiled, and it ached, and it was real. "I spent enough years copying other people's words. I'd like to write some of my own."

* * *

They walked out of the mountains the slow way, because neither of them was in any hurry to arrive anywhere.

The realm was knitting as they descended. Marguerite could see it now—everyone could, probably, but Marguerite had been made to see it, made for it, and the sight was theirs to keep. Where the veil had been thin, the two worlds overlapped like double-exposed pages. Wolves ran beside the road in the human realm's daylight, unbothered, their eyes catching the sun like amber. A shrine to the Wolf King stood at a crossroads where last month there had been a boundary stone, and an old woman was leaving bread there, and the bread did not vanish but sat in both worlds at once, whole in each.

The Wolf King himself was dying still—the covenant's renewal would not undo a century of decay in a day—but he was dying *held*, Marguerite thought. Dying in a realm that would remember him. Felix had gone to him once, before they left the passes, and come back with his eyes red and his ledger-hand empty.

"What did he say?" Marguerite had asked.

"He said the debt against your bloodline is paid." Felix had turned his hands over, palms up, as if showing empty pockets. "He said—" and here Felix had laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, "—he said *tell the scribe the book is finished.*"

Now, on the last morning of the passes, with Thornfell's smoke visible in the valley below like a grey thread, they stopped at a stream and Felix dug through the last of their salvaged supplies and came out not with food but with a book.

Marguerite went still.

It was a ledger. Not the burned one—this was new, or newish, its cover uncracked, its spine stiff. "Bought it in the last village," Felix said. "From the archivist's cousin, actually. Small world, the book world." He held it out. "I owe you a ledger. That was the arrangement. Debts across realms. You held up your side."

"Felix—"

"Open it."

Marguerite opened it. The pages were blank. Every one of them, blank as fresh snow, blank as the room in their chest, and they understood that this was the point, and their throat closed.

"The prophecy's gone," Felix said. His voice was careful, the way it was when he was handling something breakable that wasn't a thing at all. "The hermit checked. The archive copies, the smuggled fragments, the version in the Wolf King's court—all of it, blank now. The covenant didn't just get sealed. It ate its own instructions. There's no prophecy anymore. No scribe who remembers both names. No script that anyone's fated to write."

He tapped the blank page with one finger.

"So it's not a ledger of debts anymore. It's not a ledger of anything. It's just—" He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; Marguerite could see it, the man who had lived his whole life bound by entries and balances, offering an empty book. "It's just pages. Whatever you put in them is yours."

* * *

They came down into Thornfell at dusk, when the temple bells rang the hour and the streets smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions, and nobody looked at them twice.

That was the strangest part. Marguerite had spent years learning to be invisible, and now invisibility was a choice instead of a cage. They walked through the market square where they had passed a thousand times with their head down and their scribe's collar high, and this time they walked with their head up, and the world declined to end.

At the temple gate, a novice was posting the new notices. Marguerite stopped, because habit dies hard, and read the top one. *By order of the Archon's council: the indenture of the scribe Marguerite Okafor is hereby dissolved, the said scribe having rendered service beyond all valuation.* Below it, in smaller script: *The archive requests the honor of the said scribe's counsel, at their convenience, in perpetuity.*

"They dissolved it," Felix said, reading over their shoulder. His breath stirred their hair. "The heretic brand and all. Neat handwriting, whoever forged it."

"It's not forged." Marguerite touched the ink and felt it—felt it the way they had always felt ink, as a living thing with a pulse. "It's the young inquisitor. The one who set her brand down. She wrote it herself." They laughed, short and wet. "I taught her lettering, two winters ago. She had a heavy hand. I told her a scribe's first duty was to know what a sentence *does*."

"And what does that sentence do?"

Marguerite stepped back from the gate. Behind them the temple rose, all its spires catching the last light, and somewhere in its archive a hundred thousand pages waited, and somewhere in Marguerite's chest a hundred empty rooms waited too, and for the first time in their life both of those facts felt like the same kind of promise.

"It lets me go," they said.

* * *

They took a room at an inn near the south gate—the kind of inn smugglers used, where Felix was known and greeted with the wary warmth of men who had run cargo with him and never asked what was in the crates. They ate stew. They drank small beer. Felix told a story about a mule and a border warden and a wedding, and Marguerite laughed until their ribs hurt, and it occurred to them, somewhere in the middle of the laughing, that they could not remember the last time they had laughed without checking first whether it was safe.

Later, in the room, with the shutter open to the night air, Felix sat on the windowsill and Marguerite sat on the bed with the new blank ledger open on their knees, and a pen, and an inkpot, and nothing else in the world required of them.

"First entry," Felix said. "Go on. I want to see what a free scribe writes."

Marguerite dipped the pen. Held it over the page. The blankness looked back at them—not empty, they realized now. *Open.* The difference mattered. It had always mattered; they had just spent their whole life on the wrong side of the distinction.

They wrote:

*Marguerite Okafor. That is the name I have. It may not be the name I was born with, and I have given the born one away, and I am not sorry. I am the one who sealed the covenant, and I am the one who cannot remember their mother's face, and I am the one sitting in an inn in Thornfell with ink on my fingers and a man on the windowsill who burned a hundred years of debts to say one true thing to me. That is the whole of my lineage now. It is enough. It is more.*

*First entry ends. There will be others.*

They read it aloud, because it seemed important to say it into the air and let the air keep it. When they finished, Felix was quiet for a long moment.

"That's not a ledger entry," he said finally. "That's a first page."

"Same thing," Marguerite said. "Now."

He crossed the room and sat beside them, and took the pen from their fingers with a gentleness that would have astonished every smuggler and every handler who ever knew him, and wrote beneath their words, in his blunt, untrained, entirely honest hand:

*Felix. Witnessed.*

"Every book needs one," he said.

Outside, the two moons rose together over Thornfell—the human moon and the wolf moon, which were, as of three days ago, officially the same moon—and their light fell across the open page and made the wet ink gleam like something newly born.

Marguerite leaned into Felix's shoulder and watched the ink dry.

Somewhere far to the north, in a court that was learning how to be whole again, a dying king slept peacefully for the first time in a century. Somewhere in the archive, a young inquisitor with a heavy hand was writing letters that would change what sentences did. And somewhere in the world, Marguerite was certain—had no proof, but was certain—another blank page had appeared in another pair of hands, waiting.

The covenant held.

The road, in the morning, went everywhere.
