## 1. The Ledger of Small Crimes

The trouble with a life built on not being noticed, Sable Sandoval had learned, was that you spent an awful lot of time noticing other people.

They crouched in the shadow of a granite shelf above the Widow's Stair, watching the king's road below with the patience their grandmother had drilled into them before they could write their own name. Which was, of course, the point. No one wrote Sandoval names. Not on ledgers, not on toll rolls, not on the tax lists that the crown's clerks copied out each spring with their neat, doomed little flourishes. A Sandoval was a rumor with boots. A Sandoval was the reason a cart of untaxed wool arrived in Lowmark three days before it had officially left Thornfell.

Below, two soldiers trudged along the switchback, spears shouldered, breath steaming in the grey morning. One of them was singing. Badly.

"Third patrol this week," murmured Doss, Sable's second, a broad man whose beard held enough crumbs to feed a small expedition. He'd been eating again. He was always eating again. "Used to be one. Used to be we could set a clock by old Bramm and his bad knee."

"King's business," Sable said. "Or someone's."

"Whose?"

"That's the interesting question." Sable shifted, and the token at their throat shifted with it — a small disc of black stone on a leather cord, worn smooth as a river pebble. It had been in the family longer than anyone could say. Grandmother Crane-Sandoval had called it *the toll*, and made Sable wear it from the age of six, saying only: *So the mountain knows you.* Sable had assumed it was sentiment. Grandmother had not seemed like a sentimental woman. She had seemed like a woman who sharpened her knives while making eye contact.

The soldiers passed below, the singer's tune drifting up — one of the new hymns, actually, all about the king's radiant mercy. Sable filed that away too. Hymns to Aldric were common enough; hymns *this* cheerful usually meant a tithe collector was behind them somewhere.

When the road stood empty, Sable whistled twice, low, and the caravan came out of the trees like a thought nobody wanted to have aloud.

Six carts. Not carts, really — sledges on greased runners, because the smugglers' road above the Widow's Stair didn't believe in wheels. Wheels left ruts. Ruts left stories. Stories got people hanged. The sledges carried salt fish, iron nails, a chest of southern wine for the innkeeper at Hollow Bridge who paid double and asked nothing, and — wrapped in oilcloth beneath the fish barrels, because even smugglers appreciated tradition — forty bolts of undyed linen that would be dyed, cut, and sold in three different towns under three different names before the month was out.

"Stair's clear," Doss reported, wiping his hands on a coat that had once been white, in some distant era, possibly mythical. "Bramm's knee says we've got until the bell at Hollow Bridge, maybe less."

"Bramm's knee," Sable said, "is not a licensed surveyor."

"No, but it's been right eleven times running, which is more than I can say for the priest at Hollow Bridge, who told us the floods would come in summer and then drowned his own chapel in autumn."

Sable smiled despite themselves. This was the part of the work they loved, if they were honest — and they tried not to be honest, even privately, because honesty was a ledger entry waiting to happen. The choreography of it. Six drivers who knew the passes better than the goats did, moving through country that officially did not exist, carrying goods that officially had never been made. The kingdom above believed Thornfell was empty rock and worse weather. The kingdom above was welcome to its belief. Beliefs kept borders, and borders kept prices high, and high prices kept thirty families in the hidden valleys fed through winters that would otherwise eat them alive.

It wasn't crime, exactly. Or rather — it was, but so gently, so habitually, that Sable had long ago stopped thinking of it as theft and started thinking of it as weather. You didn't apologize to the rain. You built a roof.

"Move," they said, and the mountain swallowed the caravan.

* * *

The smugglers' road ran where no road was: along a shelf of stone barely wider than a coffin, past a waterfall that fell upward in high wind (a thing Sable had never explained to outsiders, because outsiders' faces when they saw it were one of life's few free pleasures), through a stand of pines so dense and so bent that the crown's surveyors had mapped the whole grove as "impassable scree." Grandmother had found the route. Or her grandmother had. The Sandovals had been finding things for a very long time — passes, paths, ways around, ways through — and had been paid for it, over the generations, in exactly one currency: being forgotten.

That was the toll, Sable had understood by ten. Not the stone at their throat. The other one. Every family that helped the valleys breathe did so by agreeing to be no one. No deeds, no names in parish books, no graves with markers. Grandmother lay under an unmarked cairn on the north ridge, and Sable visited her the way you visit a bank: regularly, respectfully, and without expecting conversation.

By midday they'd made the high shelf above the gate.

Everyone in the network called it the gate, though there was no gate. There was a cleft in the mountain's shoulder, a seam of darker stone perhaps twenty feet tall, veined with something pale that caught the light wrong — not quartz, not ice, something that seemed to hold the daylight a half-second after the sun moved on. The old hymns — the suppressed ones, the ones Isolde Vane's order used to sing before the crown burned their abbeys — called the mountain *the mouth of Freya*, mother-god, door-keeper, she-who-shuts. The smugglers called it the Gate and left small offerings: a nail here, a ribbon there, once an entire wheel of cheese that Doss still mourned.

Sable called it Tuesday. It was simply there, the way the weather was there, the way the toll was there. You went around it. You did not touch it. You did not, under any circumstances, *pray* at it, because Grandmother had been very clear on that point, clearer than she'd ever been about anything: *We are not a praying family, little thief. We are the family that stands between.*

Between what and what, Grandmother had declined to specify, on the grounds that specifying was how you ended up in songs, and songs were how you ended up dead.

The caravan rested at the shelf for the noon halt — cold bread, harder cheese, Doss telling the story of the cheese-wheel funeral for the eleventh time that season. Sable walked the line, checking runners, checking tempers, checking the sky. Routine. All of it routine, right down to the way young Pell from the valley farms kept glancing nervously at the gate-stone, the way he always did, the way everyone's first year went.

Then the courier came down off the north ridge, and routine died quietly in the snow.

* * *

They heard him before they saw him — or rather, the dogs heard him, and the dogs' opinion of things was generally worth more than most men's. He came stumbling along the shelf from the high pass with the particular gait of a man who has been riding hard for two days and walking on a bad leg for one of them, and he was not one of theirs.

That alone put every hand within reach of a knife. The network had rules, and rule one was carved somewhere in everyone's spine: *you do not come to the high roads uninvited.* Yet the man raised both palms as he came, and he was shouting a name, and the name was Sandoval, and that was stranger still, because nobody shouted that name. Nobody *knew* that name.

"Sandoval!" His voice cracked on it. "I need — the toll-keeper — I was told —"

He collapsed four yards from the gate-stone, and Sable reached him first, kneeling in the snow, turning him over with hands that stayed steady through long practice. Fifties, maybe. Grey stubble. Frostbitten ears. And across his belly, a wound that had been bound and rebound until the bandages looked like the cocoon of some desperate insect, blood soaking through in slow pulses.

"Who told you to find me?" Sable asked.

"A dying man," the courier said, and laughed, wetly. "Which is a profession I appear to have joined. Name's Corvin. I carry for the shrine at Ellswater. Carried." He coughed. "Before they burned it."

"The crown burned it?"

"The crown burned everything this winter." His eyes were fever-bright and terribly focused, the focus of a man spending his last coins. "Listen. Listen. I don't have time for your rules. There was a sister there, Sister Osla, and when the soldiers came she gave me this and told me — run north, run to the gate, find the keeper, tell them it's *waking*—"

And he pressed it into Sable's hand.

A token. Black stone, smooth as a river pebble, twin to the one hanging at Sable's throat.

Except that the moment Sable's fingers closed around it, the one at their throat began to hum.

Not vibrate. Hum. A low, resonant note, felt in the sternum more than the ears, like standing too close to a struck bell — and behind them, Sable heard Pell make a small frightened sound, and heard Doss stop chewing, which in eleven years Sable had witnessed approximately never.

The gate-stone had begun to answer.

The pale veins in the dark seam of rock brightened, slowly, the way coals wake, and the light they shed was warm and gold and utterly wrong for a midwinter mountainside, and every dog in the caravan lay down flat and put its paws over its nose.

"What is it?" Corvin whispered. He couldn't see the stone from where he lay, but he could feel it — Sable could see him feeling it, his face slackening. "That's — Sister Osla said it would know its own. She said the tokens were cut from the same—" Another cough, deeper now, rustier. "She said the keeper would understand. Do you? Understand?"

"I keep tolls," Sable said. "Roads. That's all."

"Then why is your stone singing, toll-keeper?"

Sable had no answer. They looked down at the two tokens in their palm — theirs, worn smooth by six generations of throats, and this new one, scarred. That was the detail that snagged, the one that would keep them awake for weeks after: the new token was not smooth. Its face was scored with fine lines, deliberate, patient, hundreds of tiny scratches — and it took Sable a long moment, with the humming rising around them like breath drawn to speak, to recognize what the scratches were.

Words. Prayers. Hundreds upon hundreds of prayers, cut into the stone by hand, by many hands, over years — *keep my daughter safe, let the rains come, forgive me, forgive me, forgive me* — prayers that had been given to this stone and never answered, accumulating like silt, like debt.

Until now. Until whatever slept behind the pale veins had begun, at long last, to pay attention.

Corvin grabbed Sable's wrist with the last of his strength. "She said — tell the keeper — it answers now. Everything you ask. Everything. That's how you'll know it's Him, and not Her." His eyes rolled toward the glowing seam, and something in his face was not fear exactly, but the exhaustion of a man who has seen the punchline of a very long joke. "The god under the mountain. She shut the door. But somebody's been knocking, toll-keeper. Somebody's been knocking for years, and now —"

He died between one word and the next, mid-sentence, with the unhurried rudeness of the genuinely finished. The wind took the rest.

For a long moment nobody moved. Snow drifted down through the golden light of the waking gate, settling on the dead man's open eyes, and the two tokens in Sable's hand hummed together, in harmony, like voices that had been separated a long time finding each other again.

Doss came and stood beside them. He didn't ask questions. He looked at the gate, and at the body, and at Sable's face, and then he said, very quietly, the kindest thing anyone had said to Sable in years:

"I'll get the shovels."

* * *

They buried Corvin at dusk, above the snowline, in ground too thin for a proper grave — a cairn, unmarked, in the family style. Sable said no words over it, because words were ledger entries, and this man had already been entered into too many. But they cut the prayer-scarred token's cord and hung it around their own neck beside the old one, because Sister Osla had sent it to the keeper, and whatever else Sable was or wasn't, they were the one the stone sang to.

That night, at the waystation, Sable sat alone with the tokens and a candle and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since noon.

The gate was waking. A burned shrine's last messenger had crossed a kingdom to reach a name that shouldn't exist, and died on the doorstep. Someone had been knocking for years. And the stone at Sable's throat — the family stone, the *toll* — had known the stranger's token on sight, the way you know a voice through a wall.

Grandmother's voice surfaced, unbidden, sharp as her knives: *We are not a praying family, little thief. We are the family that stands between.*

Between what and what?

Sable turned the prayer-scarred token over one last time, and by candlelight read the newest scratch on its face — shallower than the rest, done in haste, by a dying woman's hand:

*Find the keeper. Tell them: it is not answering our prayers. It is collecting them.*

Outside, far below the waystation, deep under the mountain's shoulder, something that had waited a very long time hummed against the stones — patient, attentive, and horribly pleased to hear from them at last.
