## 2. What the Mountain Answers

The trouble with miracles, Sable had learned by their thirtieth winter, was that they always arrived in the same season as the tax collectors.

They came down into Hollowmere on a gray morning with the smell of thawing sheep in the air and a pack full of things the valley wasn't supposed to have: salt that hadn't passed through the king's tollhouse, needles of good southern steel, a bolt of dye-cloth the color of crushed plums. Ordinary cargo. Ordinary run. The kind of errand Sable could do half-asleep, which was fortunate, because they had been doing it mostly asleep since the courier died in their arms three nights ago.

The token still rode against their sternum, wrapped in three layers of wool and one layer of denial. It had not hummed since the pass. Sable had checked twice on the descent road, pressing a palm flat to their own chest like a fool listening for a second heartbeat, and both times found nothing but their own stubborn pulse and the ache of old bruising where the courier's fist had clenched around theirs.

*Prayer-scarred*, the dying man had mouthed. *It knows your name.*

Well. Lots of things knew Sable's name. None of them were supposed to say it out loud.

Hollowmere sat at the bottom of its valley like a coin dropped in a cupped hand—thirty roofs, one shrine with a weathered statue of some harvest saint whose face the rain had long ago rubbed into anonymity, and a tavern called the Drowned Lamb that had never once, in living memory, drowned a lamb or anything else. Sable knew the village the way they knew all the villages on the low roads: by which back doors stuck, which widows paid in eggs, which farmers talked too much after the second pint. They had been coming here since they were nine years old, invisible as weather.

Which was why it was so deeply unsettling when Tam Wicker's youngest daughter spotted them across the market square and shouted, "There! That's the stranger! Mam, the stranger's come to see!"

Sable stopped in the mud.

They had been coming to Hollowmere for twenty years. In twenty years, no child had ever looked at them twice. Invisibility was not an accident; it was a craft, honed like any other—a slouch here, a hood there, a way of walking that made eyes slide off you the way water slides off oiled canvas. Sable had once walked through a wedding feast carrying a stolen reliquary under their coat while the bride's mother thanked them for coming.

And now a girl of perhaps six was pointing at them with the unerring delight of a child who has just located a hedgehog in the woodpile.

"Come to see her!" the girl repeated, grabbing Sable's sleeve with fingers sticky with something Sable chose not to identify. "You have to see her. Everybody has to see her."

"See who?" Sable asked, because refusing seemed somehow more dangerous than following.

"Mira," the girl said, as if this explained everything, and tugged.

* * *

The Wicker house smelled of bread and beeswax and, underneath both, something else—something green. Not the green of stored herbs or spring onions. A living, growing green, the smell of a meadow in high summer, which was wrong in every possible way, because it was the first week of thaw and the hills outside were still brown as old rope.

The whole village appeared to be crammed into the front room. Farmers stood shoulder to shoulder among the furniture, hats in hands, wearing the particular expression of people who have witnessed something enormous and are trying very hard to behave normally about it. At the center, in a chair by the window, sat Tam Wicker's middle daughter—Mira, eight years old, famous throughout the valley for two things: a sweetness of temper that survived even her brothers, and eyes that had never worked, born clouded pale as river ice.

She was looking directly at Sable.

Not toward. *At.* With focus. With the frank, unnerving attention of someone seeing for the first time and finding everything equally astonishing—including, apparently, a muddy stranger in a hood standing in her doorway.

"You're the one from the mountain," Mira said.

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when a hundred held breaths release at once.

"She sees," whispered the girl with the sticky hands, in case anyone in the back had missed it. "She sees *everything* now."

"I—" said Sable, whose profession depended absolutely on never being described afterward, "am nobody."

"No," Mira said, with the gentle certainty of the very young and, apparently, the newly sighted. "You're not." She tilted her head, studying Sable's face the way scholars study difficult texts. "You look tired. You should eat something. And you shouldn't keep that in your shirt, it doesn't like being wrapped up."

Every hair on Sable's body stood up and attempted to leave independently through the scalp.

Tam Wicker pushed forward, beaming, wringing his hat. "Forgive her, stranger, she says things like that now. Since the night." He lowered his voice to the register men use for church and scandal alike. "Three nights past. We'd given her up, see—the fever took her hearing in one ear in autumn, and then her eyes went dark at midwinter, and the physician from the toll-town said it was the blood, said nothing could—" He stopped, swallowed. "Her mother prayed. We all prayed. To the king's new god, the way the criers say, the way the proclamations tell us. And in the night the house filled up with the smell of summer, and there was singing under the floor, and in the morning—"

"In the morning I could see," Mira finished, "and my ear came back too, and the bread had risen even though nobody set it, and there were flowers growing up through the *floorboards*, Da, tell them about the flowers."

"Snowdrops," Tam said reverently. "Out of season. Out of *season*, stranger. Through the boards. And the priest from the toll-town came and said it was the king's piety, that King Aldric has taken up the old devotions and the gods above are pleased, and he wrote it in the parish book, and there's going to be a festival—"

"There was singing under the floor," Sable said.

"Aye." Tam nodded happily. "Under the floor. Like the mountain itself was humming."

Sable looked down at the floorboards. Old oak, silvered with age, laid over a root cellar that—Sable knew, because Sable knew everything about every cellar in this valley, cellars being professionally relevant—connected by a crawlway to the old mine adit, which connected to the deep ways, which connected, three valleys and four locked doors later, to Thornfell.

To the gate.

Nothing answered from above the snowline. That was the first law of the mountains, older than the kingdom, older than the hymns: the sky here was empty, the high passes were silent, whatever the lowland priests burned their incense to had never once bothered to look north. Everything that listened, everything that answered, everything that sang—all of it lived *under*. And the only thing under Thornfell was the thing the gate kept.

The token, through three layers of wool, gave a single soft pulse against Sable's chest. Like a sleeping thing turning over.

Sable smiled the smile they used on toll collectors and said, "A miracle. Well. Long live the king."

"Long live the king!" the room echoed, warm and easy and utterly certain, and Sable thought: *you poor lambs, you are thanking the wolf for the weather.*

* * *

They did eat something, because refusing Mira's hospitality felt like kicking a fawn, and because Sable had learned long ago that the safest place to hide is in plain sight, doing exactly what everyone expects. So they sat at the Wickers' table, ate bread that tasted faintly of honey no one had added, and let the room talk.

It talked plenty.

The criers had come through a fortnight ago with the king's new edict: worship restored, shrines reopened, tithes forgiven for the pious. King Aldric—who had spent six years being famously, aggressively indifferent to the gods—had undergone some change, the criers said. Had grown radiant. Had wept at his own coronation anniversary. Had ordered the suppressed orders' writings gathered up and read aloud in the squares.

"He's a good king," said Widow Marsh, patting Sable's arm. "A *holy* king. My knees, stranger. Forty years of bad knees. I prayed at the shrine two Sundays running, and I'll tell you what happened."

"They got better?" Sable guessed.

"I danced at my granddaughter's betrothal," Widow Marsh said, with the defiance of a woman daring anyone to make something of it. "Danced. The priest says the king's devotion flows down through the land like spring water. Like spring water!"

"And the flowers," someone offered. "Old Marta's whole orchard came in overnight. Blossoms in the frost. She's giving away apples by the basket, come autumn, if the fruit sets."

"And the Hendersons' cow."

"The cow gave milk again after five years dry, yes, but that might be the new feed—"

"It's not the feed, Aldous, don't be a fool, it's the *king*—"

Sable ate their bread and let the warmth wash over them and catalogued, with the cold little ledger-book heart their grandmother had trained into them, every detail that didn't fit.

Miracles that clustered near the deep ways. Singing that came from *under* floors, not down from skies. A blind child restored who could apparently see more than eyes were built for. And everywhere, everywhere, that green summer smell, rising out of the ground like breath.

The hymns said the old gods were gentle. The hymns said they answered prayers out of love, that they asked nothing, that they slept above the snowline in halls of cloud. Sable's grandmother had made Sable memorize the hymns at the same time she taught them which ones were lies, and she had been very clear on this point: *the gods of the hymns are a story we told so people wouldn't scream. Nothing that answers prayers does it for free. Ask what it eats.*

Ask what it eats.

Crops blooming overnight cost nothing. Milk and blossoms and a child's eyes—small coin, small wonders, the sort of generosity that costs a moneylender nothing and buys him a town's devotion. But Sable had carried enough smuggled goods to know how debts worked, and the kindest lenders were always the ones whose interest you couldn't see.

"So," said Tam Wicker, settling beside Sable with two cups of something hot and alcoholic, in flagrant violation of several laws Sable was personally responsible for circumventing. "You're from over the pass. You'll have heard about the king's renewal. The big rite at midwinter? They say he's going to renew his vows to the gods before the whole capital. Crown and relic and all. First time in a hundred years a king's done the full old rite."

"Hadn't heard," Sable lied smoothly.

"They say the kingdom will bloom from end to end after. They say sickness will end. They say—" Tam lowered his voice, glowing. "They say the dead will hear us again. Properly. That if you speak a name at the shrine, the dead will answer."

The token pulsed again. Twice this time. Eager.

Sable set down the bread.

"That would be something," they said, and their voice came out level, which Sable considered among their finer professional achievements. "Speaking with the dead."

"Oh, aye. Wonderful days coming." Tam clinked his cup against Sable's. "To the king."

"To the king," said Sable, and drank, and over the rim of the cup watched Mira across the room.

The girl was looking at them again. Looking at them the way you'd watch a door you weren't sure was locked. And when Sable caught her eye—when Sable, absurdly, found himself caught by an eight-year-old's gaze like a rabbit in a snare—Mira smiled, sweet-tempered as ever, and mouthed words with perfect clarity:

*It knows your name.*

* * *

Sable left Hollowmere an hour later, taking the long way along the streambed where no one followed, because no one ever followed, because Sable was weather.

Except the small footsteps behind them, which were not weather at all.

"You're leaving without saying goodbye," Mira said, catching up with the ease of a child who had spent eight years navigating the world by sound and found the sudden arrival of eyes merely convenient. "That's rude."

"It's discreet," Sable said. "Mira. Listen to me. Your eyes—your ears—when they came back. Did anything ask you for anything?"

The girl considered this with terrible seriousness. "It doesn't ask," she said finally. "It gives. That's what the singing is. It's so *happy* to give." Her brow furrowed, and for a moment she looked less like a blessed child than like a very small person standing at the edge of a very large dark. "Only. Sometimes at night, when the singing gets quiet, I think I can hear underneath it. Underneath the happy part. Like—like when the choir stops and you can still hear the big bell ringing, only backwards." She looked up at Sable, and her clear new eyes were the exact color of the glacier lakes above the snowline, where nothing lived. "Is that what you came to listen for?"

Sable crouched down to her height. Up close, the cloudiness was gone entirely; the eyes were new, unscarred, impossibly blue. Borrowed eyes, Sable thought. Lent eyes. Eyes with terms attached that no one in this valley had read.

"Mira," Sable said gently, "if the singing ever tells you my name—if it ever says it out loud—I want you to forget it. Can you do that? Forget it the moment you hear it. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because names are how things find you. And how you find things." Sable held out their hand, palm up, the way you'd offer one to a horse or a hawk. "Promise."

Mira put her small hand in Sable's scarred one. "I promise," she said. Then, with the merciless honesty of children: "But it already knows. It's known since the courier. It's been saying your name under the singing for three nights. It sounds *glad* about you." She cocked her head, listening to something Sable couldn't hear, somewhere below the frozen ground, below the roots, below the silence. "It says you're the key."

The token flared—once, hard, unmistakable, a struck bell against Sable's breastbone—and went quiet.

Mira released their hand, wiped her nose on her sleeve, and turned back toward the village, where the bells of the toll-town shrine were beginning to ring for evening prayer, and thirty households were preparing to give thanks to a king who was, at that moment, three hundred miles south, learning their names one by one.

"Supper," she said over her shoulder, cheerful as anything. "You could stay. Everyone's staying. Nobody wants to be alone anymore."

Sable stood alone in the streambed a long time after she was gone, snowmelt chuckling over the stones, the first stars pricking out above the snowline where nothing had ever answered and nothing was supposed to.

Then they unwrapped the token—three layers of wool, one layer of denial—and held it up to the last light.

It was warm. It was shaped, now that Sable really looked, like half of something: a broken seal, a snapped ring, a door with its mate lost. And scratched into its face, fine as spider silk, in handwriting Sable recognized because they had learned their letters copying it—

Their grandmother's handwriting—were the words:

*IT IS NOT THE KING WHO PRAYS. IT IS WHAT PRAYS WITH HIS MOUTH. COME HOME, KEEPER. COUNT YOUR PEOPLE.*

Sable counted, right there in the cold, from memory, the way they counted every run: souls currently on the network's rolls, guides and couriers and harbor-masters, every name in the ledger they kept in their head because paper could be seized.

Forty-one. There should have been forty-one.

They counted again.

Forty.

One name gone off the roll, unaccounted, unexplained—and Sable's blood went slow and cold as they realized whose voice had taught them the passes, whose handwriting had signed the training ledgers, whose boots had walked every mile of these roads ahead of them for thirty years.

Maro was missing from the count.

And Maro, Sable remembered with a lurch, had been the last person to carry supplies up to the waystation at Thornfell. Three weeks ago. Alone.
