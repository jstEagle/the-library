## 3. Isolde Vane, Defrocked

The trouble with being a defrocked priestess, Isolde Vane had decided over seven years of practice, was not the shame. Shame she could carry; shame had a shape and a weight and could be set down at night like a heavy coat. The trouble was that her hands still remembered everything.

They remembered how to fold for blessing, thumb to thumb, fingers laced in the pattern of the Ninefold Veil. They remembered the pressure of consecrated oil, the exact tilt of a cup so the sick could drink without choking. On bad nights they even remembered the liturgy — all of it, every forbidden verse of it, humming behind her teeth like a song she'd sworn never to sing again.

So when Tam Wicker's fever broke on the third night of the grey cough, and his mother fell weeping into Isolde's arms calling it a miracle, Isolde smiled the way you smile at a debt collector: pleasantly, and while calculating exactly how much you owe.

"There are medicines," she said gently, prying old Marta Wicker loose. "Willow bark. The honey-and-thyme. I boiled them myself, you watched me."

"The priestesses used to do this," Marta said, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Before the king's grandfather burned their houses. My gran told me. They'd lay hands on you and the fever would just — go." She looked at Isolde with an expression that had been getting more common lately, along the whole length of the border: hopeful, and slightly afraid of its own hope.

"I'm no priestess," Isolde said. "I'm a woman who knows where the willow grows."

It was true, in the way most comforting lies were true. She *was* a woman who knew where the willow grew. She was also, though she would have bitten off her own tongue before saying it aloud, a woman who had knelt beside Tam Wicker's cot at midnight, when the boy's breathing had gone thin as thread, and prayed.

Not the sanctioned prayers. There were none left to pray; the Church of the Concord had spent two generations sanding the old words down into safe little pebbles of gratitude, prayers that asked nothing because asking might wake something. No, Isolde had prayed the way she'd been taught before the teaching was outlawed — the old way, palms open, nameless god addressed by no name, *if anything is listening, I am asking.*

And something, apparently, was.

* * *

The border infirmary at Harrow's Ford sat where the river bent lazy around a hill, forty beds under one long roof, staffed by Isolde, two novice nurses the crown paid in coin that arrived late, and a one-legged orderly named Brannic who did the cooking and most of the complaining. It was, officially, a charity house of the Concord. Unofficially, it was where the Concord sent problems it didn't want to look at, which is why it had ended up with Isolde Vane.

She had been famous once. Not the good kind of famous — the kind that gets your portrait burned. Sister Isolde of the Veiled Choir, youngest cantor ever invested in the suppressed order, the girl whose voice could quiet a seizure ward. Then the Edict of Uniformity came down from the capital, and the order was named heretical, and Isolde had done the arithmetic every survivor does: *recant or burn.* She had recanted. Beautifully. Thoroughly. She had signed the renunciation with a steady hand and let them strike the veil from her shoulders, and she had told herself for seven years that the steady hand proved she'd made peace with it.

Her hands, as established, remembered everything. Including how to lie.

"More broth?" Brannic asked that morning, ladling grayish liquid into bowls with the enthusiasm of a man serving punishment. He was a good cook when he wanted to be, which was roughly twice a year.

"Thank you." Isolde took her bowl and sat on the bench by the window, where she could see the road.

The road was the thing. Roads were always the thing, if you paid attention, and paying attention was the last skill the Concord hadn't managed to strip from her. For three weeks now, the road past Harrow's Ford had been filling up with pilgrims. Not the usual ragged penitents trudging toward the capital's shrines — these walked *away* from the capital, out toward the mountains, Thornfell way, carrying tokens and candles and the particular bright-eyed exhaustion of people who had recently been answered.

"They say the king has found favor," said Sister Anwen, one of the novices, sliding onto the bench beside her. Anwen was nineteen, pious, and read banned pamphlets the way other girls read romances, which is to say guiltily and often. "That he prayed at his father's tomb and the frost lifted off the southern wheat overnight. That the plague pits in Callowmere emptied in a week."

"That's what they say," Isolde agreed.

"You don't believe it?"

Isolde stirred her broth. "I believe wheat can be lucky. I believe plague burns itself out. I believe kings find religion the way merchants find wars — profitable, and right on schedule."

"But the blind child." Anwen lowered her voice. "At Millbrook. Everyone saw. Born without eyes, they say — *without eyes*, Sister Vane, there was nothing under the lids — and after the king's procession passed through, he turned and looked at his mother and *saw* her."

Isolde's spoon stopped.

She had heard about the child at Millbrook. She had heard it from a drover who'd seen the boy himself, and the drover had repeated the detail that mattered, the detail everyone else was too dazzled to notice: the boy had eyes now. New ones. Grown in a fortnight, brown and wet and blinking, and when the drover looked into them — just for a moment, just in passing — he'd felt *looked back at.* "Like the eyes weren't only his," the drover had said, laughing nervously, wanting her to laugh too. "Like something behind them was taking a turn."

Isolde had laughed. She was good at that.

Now she said, "Miracles have a cost, Anwen. That's the first lesson of my old order, and it's why they burned us. Not because we healed people. Because we kept accounts."

Anwen frowned. "Accounts?"

"Everything answered must be paid. The old liturgy wasn't spells, it was bookkeeping. You asked for a healing, you offered a grief. You asked for rain, you offered a harvest. Balance." Isolde looked out at the pilgrims on the road, their little flames of hope bobbing toward the mountains. "Nobody's keeping books anymore. Everybody's asking. And nobody's offering anything except gratitude, and gratitude doesn't weigh anything."

"You talk like they're real," Anwen whispered. "The old gods."

Isolde drank her broth instead of answering, which was becoming a habit, and not a healthy one.

* * *

Tam Wicker rose wrong on the fourth day.

He didn't rise wrong immediately — that was the horror of it, the slow reveal, like watching milk turn. The first day he was a miracle: sitting up, color in his cheeks, asking for bread and complaining about the broth with the vigor of any nine-year-old. Marta wept again. Brannic, who pretended not to believe in anything except gravy, quietly put an extra heel of bread on the boy's tray.

The second day, Tam stopped sleeping.

"Not unusual, after fever," Isolde told herself, and then told Anwen, and then caught herself standing in the ward at two in the morning watching the boy lie perfectly still with his eyes open, staring at the rafters, not bored, not frightened — *attentive.* Like a man listening through a wall.

The third day, he began to speak in his sleep. Except he wasn't asleep.

"Who taught you that prayer?" Tam asked her at breakfast, clear-eyed, cheerful, mouth full of bread. His voice was his voice. The cadence underneath it was not.

"What prayer?" Isolde said.

"The one you did at night. With your hands open." He smiled at her, gap-toothed, sweet as anything. "It was a good prayer. Very well-formed. Grandmother's phrasing, mostly, but you've improved the meter."

The ward went very quiet around them. Somewhere a spoon clinked. Isolde became aware that she had gone cold all over, and that her hands — her traitor, remembering hands — had folded themselves into the Veil's pattern without her permission.

"Tamsin," she said carefully, using his formal name, the way you'd use a rope across ice, "who is talking right now?"

He considered this with the patience of something very old wearing a very new face. "You know," he said kindly. "You've known since the fever broke. You felt it take the cough and leave the change, like a tradesman. Fair price, fair work." He licked butter off his thumb. "It likes your prayers best, Sister Vane. It says yours taste like the old days."

"Tam—"

"It wants you to come home." The boy tilted his head, and for half a heartbeat his eyes did the thing the drover had described — the looking-back, the depth behind them, something vast and patient regarding her through the keyhole of a child's face. "There's a gate in the mountains. There's always been a gate. It remembers the ones who kept the books."

Then Tam blinked, yawned like an ordinary exhausted boy, and asked if he could have more bread, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

Isolde packed that night.

She was methodical about it, because panic is just disorder with better lighting, and she refused to give it the satisfaction. Two cloaks. Her medical kit. The bundle of contraband she'd carried for seven years sewn into the lining of her winter coat: a copy of the Veiled Liturgy in her own handwriting, a vial of consecrated oil, and her cantor's ring, plain iron, worthless to anyone but her. She left the rest. She left the infirmary itself in good order — charts updated, stores inventoried, a letter to the Concord's regional steward explaining, in terms bland enough to survive inspection, that she was going to visit her dying aunt.

"She doesn't have an aunt," Brannic said, appearing in her doorway with a lantern, having apparently materialized from the general principle that Brannic appeared whenever something interesting happened. "I've heard her mention her family. It's a mother she doesn't write to and a brother she owes money to."

"It's a figure of speech."

"It's a lie of speech." He set the lantern down and studied her with his one leg planted and his jaw set, a man preparing to be difficult. "The Wicker boy. Something's wrong with him. Don't tell me there isn't — I've cooked for that child for four years, he thinks my soup is a crime against nature. Yesterday he thanked me for it. Twice. And he watched me while I ate mine, like he was learning how."

Isolde closed her bag. "Brannic—"

"And you're leaving in the night, in winter, toward Thornfell, where half the countryside is walking with candles like it's a festival and the other half is locking their doors." He crossed his arms. "So either you tell me what's happening, or I stand here being inconvenient until you do. I'm very good at inconvenient. Ask anyone."

She almost laughed. That was the terrible thing about Brannic — he made you almost laugh at the exact moments laughter would break you. She sat down on the edge of her narrow bed instead, and told him. Not everything. Enough: the prayer, the fever breaking too clean, the boy's eyes, the voice that knew her order's phrasing. The word *gate.*

Brannic listened the way he cooked — slowly, skeptically, thoroughly. When she finished he was silent for a long moment.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "used to say the mountains were hollow. Said there was a door in Thornfell and what was behind it wasn't sleeping, it was *waiting*, and the difference mattered." He rubbed his stump, an old habit, weather-thinking. "We all thought it was just a story to keep children off the passes."

"Most stories are," Isolde said. "That's how the true ones hide."

"So what do you do? Walk up there and knock?"

"I follow the token." She drew it out from under her collar: a small disc of black stone on a cord, prayer-scarred, that had arrived three weeks ago in a courier's pack — a courier who'd died of the grey cough in her ward, delirious, clutching it, whispering *take it north, take it north, it's singing.* She'd meant to bury it with him. Instead it had hummed against her palm every night since, faint and insistent, like a cat scratching at a door. "Someone sent this from the mountain. Someone who knows what's waking. If there's anyone left up there keeping the old accounts—" she touched the stone, and it shivered warmly against her skin, "—they'll know what I am. What I was. Maybe they'll want a bookkeeper."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I'll improvise. I'm told defrocked clergy make excellent improvisers. All the training, none of the oversight."

Brannic snorted. Then, gruffly, because tenderness embarrassed him: "Take the east road as far as Stony Ford, the west one's washed out past the mill. And take this." He pressed a wrapped parcel into her hands — bread, cheese, a jar of the honey-and-thyme, and, she discovered later, a small iron poker from the kitchen stove, sharpened. "For the aunt," he said, deadpan. "In case she's difficult."

"Brannic. Watch the boy. If his eyes change — if he starts *praying*, or if anyone starts praying near him and he answers — send word to no one. Just get the ward away from him and bar the door."

"And you? Who bars your door?"

Isolde slung her bag over her shoulder and stood, and for the first time in seven years the old vestments of purpose settled onto her, invisible and familiar, like a coat taken down from a hook. "No one," she said. "That's rather the point. I'm going to see about hiring one."

* * *

The road north was full of songs.

That was the worst part, Isolde thought, three days out of Harrow's Ford, walking through falling snow past farmsteads where the fields stood green and impossible in midwinter. The pilgrims sang as they walked — old hymns, the *real* hymns, the ones the Edict had burned the presses to erase — and their voices carried strangely in the cold, harmonizing with each other across distances where harmony shouldn't have been possible. A widow two miles ahead of her would begin a descant, and a shepherd a mile behind would pick it up in tune, and Isolde, who had spent her childhood training her ear until it was the finest in the order, could hear that the music was *right.* Perfectly right. Guided.

Nothing guided that many voices for free.

At night the token pulled at her neck like a leash, warm as a living thing, and she followed it east and north, past Stony Ford, into foothills where the snow lay unbroken by any track but hers and the air began to smell of deep stone. On the fifth night she sheltered in a shepherd's hut and dreamed of her investiture — the veil coming down over her face, except it kept descending, past her shoulders, past her knees, a veil over the whole world, and beneath it something enormous breathing slowly, gratefully, like a patient whose fever had finally broken.

She woke with frost on her eyelashes and the token ringing against her sternum, high and clear, a struck bell.

Outside, dawn was coming up gray over the pass, and there — cut into the mountainside below the snowline, half-hidden by scree, marked with a smuggler's chalk sign she recognized from a lifetime ago — was a door. Not the gate. A waystation, a threshold, a place where someone who kept accounts might be found.

Isolde Vane stood in the snow with her stolen bread and her sharpened poker and her seven years of recanted faith, and knocked.

Somewhere inside, she heard footsteps stop. Then a voice, wary and low, asking who was there — and the token in her hand sang louder, and Isolde smiled for the first time since the Wicker boy had spoken with her grandmother's phrasing.

"A bookkeeper," she called through the door. "I hear you're owed one."
