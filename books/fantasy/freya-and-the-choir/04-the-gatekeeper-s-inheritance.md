## 4. The Gatekeeper's Inheritance

The waystation had three cellars, if you counted the one Sable's grandmother had pretended didn't exist.

Sable counted it. They had counted it since they were nine years old, when they'd found the seam in the second cellar's floor while chasing a runaway wheel of cheese — an event that had ended with the cheese lost, Sable bruised, and their grandmother gripping their shoulder hard enough to leave a week of fingerprints and saying, in a voice Sable had never heard her use before or since: *Some doors are load-bearing, little thief. You don't open them to see what they hold up.*

At the time Sable had assumed she meant the cheese cellar.

They had spent thirty years since assuming a great many convenient things about their grandmother, most of them involving her being merely a difficult old woman with a talent for maps and an allergy to gratitude. It was easier that way. Easier than the alternative, which was that she had spent her whole life standing watch over something, alone, and had trained Sable to stand watch over it too without ever once using the word *watch*.

Now the token sat on the kitchen table, wrapped in three layers of wool and one layer of denial, humming.

Not ringing. Not buzzing. *Humming* — low and steady, the way a struck bell hums long after you've covered it with your hands. Sable could feel it through the table, through the soles of their boots, through the floor. Through the floor was the problem. The sound went *down*.

"Right," Sable said to the empty kitchen, because talking to empty kitchens was one of the many habits their grandmother had instilled and never explained. "You're a coin. You're a strange coin. Coins don't hum."

The coin hummed.

Sable put on their coat, took the lamp, and went down to the second cellar, where the floor was load-bearing.

* * *

The seam was exactly where memory said it was: a hairline in the flagstones, running in a curve too regular for geology and too old for masons. Their grandmother had kept it honest by the simple method of stacking forty years of junk on top of it — broken sledge runners, jars of preserves gone to sugar, a taxidermied marmot of unclear provenance that Sable had named Reginald at age eleven and refused to rename since.

Sable moved the junk. It took an hour. Reginald watched with glassy disapproval from his new perch on a barrel, as he had watched every indignity of Sable's childhood.

Beneath the last of it, set into the stone, was an iron ring worn smooth as a river pebble. Sable knelt, took it in both hands, and found — with a small, unwelcome jolt — that they knew the trick of it without being taught. Pull, then turn against the grain, then pull again. The way you'd turn a key in a lock built for a hand exactly like theirs.

The stone lifted on a breath of cold air that smelled of snowmelt and, faintly, of incense. Old incense. The kind that had gone to resin and dust decades ago and kept its opinion anyway.

"Grandmother," Sable said to the dark, "if there are skeletons down here, I want it noted that I always liked Reginald best."

The dark did not answer, which was, as it would turn out, the last merciful thing about it.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the waystation had depth. Sable knew this the way you know a room is smaller on the outside — a wrongness in the knees, in the count of steps. Twenty steps should have put them under the goat pen. Forty put them under the goat pen. Sixty put them somewhere the goats had no jurisdiction whatsoever.

The stair opened into a round chamber, and the chamber was not a cellar.

It was a *throat*.

Sable stood very still with the lamp raised and made themselves look, because their grandmother had also taught them that the things you refuse to look at are the things that follow you home. The chamber was circular, its walls fitted with stones so old the mortar had gone the color of bone. And the stones were carved — no, not carved. *Grown*, if stone could grow: ridges and folds radiating outward from the center of the floor, where a ring of iron lay set flush into the rock, wide enough to lie down in, its surface crosshatched with script.

The script was the same script that crawled across the courier's token.

The hum in the coin had been the hum in the room, Sable realized. Or the other way around. The token hadn't been singing to the gate stones up on the pass. It had been singing *home*.

And the ring of iron in the floor — Sable crouched at its edge without touching it — the ring was not a decoration. It was a mouth. There was no other word for it. The ridges in the walls converged on it the way the ridges in a face converge on a mouth, and the air above it moved, very slightly, in and out. In. And out.

Something beneath the floor was breathing.

"Right," Sable said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Right. Hello."

The breathing did not change. That was somehow worse than if it had.

There was a niche in the wall opposite the stair, and in it, a chest. Sable crossed the room with the exaggerated calm of someone crossing a room with a breathing floor, and opened the chest, and found their grandmother's whole second life stacked inside in her terrible handwriting.

* * *

The journal was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its pages swollen and re-swollen with damp until the whole thing had the heft of a brick. The first entry was dated seventy-one years ago, in a hand so young and sharp it took Sable three pages to recognize it.

*They tell me I am the keeper now, since Mother died in the spring. Keeper. As if the word explains anything. Nobody will tell me what is kept. Father says the gate, and when I ask what gate, he says THE gate, and when I ask what it gates, he hits me. So. I will find out myself. I am writing it down so the next one doesn't have to be hit.*

Sable sat down on the cold floor — then thought better of it, and sat on the chest instead, with their spine against the wall and the lamp between their feet, and read.

Their grandmother, at seventeen, had been exactly what Sable had always been told she was: a smuggler's daughter with a head for routes. What the stories had left out was the rest of the ledger. Entry by entry, winter by winter, the young woman in the journal conducted the investigation her family forbade, and what she found was this:

The Thornfell gate was not a gate. Gates, she wrote, in an entry Sable would remember for the rest of their life, *have two sides, and both of them are places. This has one side. It is a door that opens inward only, and what is behind it is not a place, it is a mouth, and we are not gatekeepers, we are the ones who keep the mouth shut.*

*The old hymns have it backwards. They sing about Freya who opens the ways. There was a reason the order sang so loudly. You shout over a thing you don't want heard.*

The binding, she learned, was not stone. Stone was just what they'd built around it. The binding was a rite — a feeding rite, though she didn't have the stomach for that word yet and wrote *maintenance* instead, which Sable found so painfully like her that their throat went tight — performed at each midwinter by the keeper, at the gate, with blood and a name. Not the keeper's name. A *given* name. An offering.

*It doesn't want much,* she wrote, in an entry from her thirtieth year, and the relief in the handwriting was its own kind of horror. *A little. A little every year. Enough to keep it sleeping. The kingdom above got rid of the priests and the hymns and called the old gods dead, and it worked, because the thing under Thornfell never needed worship. It needed feeding. And the Sandovals have been the ones holding the spoon for nine generations.*

*We are not smugglers. We are jailers who took a side job.*

Sable read that line four times.

They thought of their grandmother teaching them the passes — every hidden road, every toll, every blind fold in the mountain — and understood, with the slow sick click of a lock finally turning, that the lessons had never been about moving goods. The passes radiated out from the gate like the ridges in the chamber walls. The smuggling network wasn't a business the Sandovals ran. It was a *perimeter*. Eyes on every road, couriers on every ridge, a hundred loyal thieves who thought they were moving brandy and were in fact a watch line. Their grandmother had built the network the way you build a fence around a well you don't want anyone drinking from.

And the tolls. The tolls every courier grumbled about, the small cut the network took on everything that crossed Thornfell. Sable had assumed it was operating costs. Their grandmother's journal, in an entry from her fiftieth year, was blunter:

*The toll is the tithe. A share of everything that passes, given freely by those who pass, keeps the mouth drowsy better than blood. Freemen's coin is cleaner than any sacrifice. I have built the whole road on small gifts and nobody knows they're praying. It's the best joke I ever told and I can never tell it.*

Sable laughed. It came out of them sideways, half a sob dressed as a laugh, and echoed in the throat of the room, and the breathing beneath the floor went on, in and out, indifferent.

* * *

The last third of the journal was different. The handwriting aged, loosened, then tightened again with effort. And the content changed from discovery to *arithmetic*.

*The binding weakens each generation. I have checked Mother's marks against mine against hers. Each keeper's rite holds a little less. Either the thing grows or the blood thins or both. My mother held it with a name and a pint of blood. I hold it with a name and two. The next one will need more. I have to write this plainly because I have spent my whole life not writing things plainly and it has cost us: the next one will need MORE, and I do not know what more is, and I am afraid I am leaving my grandchild a debt I only know how to make interest payments on.*

Sable looked up at that. Looked at the iron ring in the floor, breathing. In. And out.

The courier's token. The prayers being answered in the valley — the blind child, the blooming fields. The thing under the gate was *answering prayers*. Their grandmother had written, sixty pages earlier, in a hand gone fierce:

*It grants small things when it stirs. That's how you know. It buys goodwill the way a merchant buys loyalty — cheap goods, generous terms, and the bill comes due all at once. If the valley ever tells you the mountain answered them, do not celebrate. Check the seal. Then check your own hands.*

Sable checked their own hands. They were steady. They were, they noted, the steady hands of a person sitting in a breathing room reading their grandmother's confession, which was its own kind of alarming.

The final entries were short. The last dated entry was from eleven years ago, the winter before she died.

*I am tired and the rite costs more every year and the child is ready and doesn't know it, which is the only mercy I have managed. I taught them the roads. I taught them the tolls. I taught them to talk to empty kitchens so they'd practice talking to this. I could not make myself say the word jailer out loud to their face. Cowardice, or love, or both; the older I get the less I can tell them apart.*

*If you are reading this, then the seal is failing, or I am dead, or both, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry, because you are the only one who can hold it and I made sure of that the way my mother made sure of me. The name for the rite is on the last page. It is not my name. It is the name I was given for it, and where I got it, and what it cost, and I want you to know that I hated every year of this and I did it anyway, and that is the whole of the Sandoval inheritance: hating it, and doing it anyway.*

*It is not a gift. I want that written down where you'll find it. It was never a gift. It is a sentence, and we are the ones serving it, and the only thing we get to choose is how well.*

Sable sat for a long time after that, in the cold, with the lamp burning low and the floor breathing beneath them.

Then they turned to the last page.

The name was there, written in a hand shakier than all the others, as if she had written it once and quickly, the way you handle a hot coal. Below it, a single line:

*If the token sings, the seal is already cracked. Don't wait for midwinter. And don't go down alone.*

Sable looked at the iron ring. In. And out. And the hum of the token, three floors up, seemed to come up through the stone itself now, through their boots, through their teeth — the sound of something that had been sleeping on small tithes and had recently, somewhere above the snowline, been given a king.

"Don't go down alone," Sable repeated. They rubbed their face. "Well. There's exactly one person I know who'd follow me into a hole on no notice and no questions, and he's going to be insufferable about it."

They closed the journal, took up the lamp, and started up the stairs — and stopped, halfway, because from below, faint and unmistakable and for the first time in nine generations, something under the floor had begun, very quietly, to hum along.
