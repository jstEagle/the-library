## 5. Captain Renn of the Low Road

Renn Oskar Halloway had three rules for running the Low Road, and he'd learned every one of them by breaking a worse version of it first.

Rule one: never carry what you can't name. Rule two: never stop where you can't leave. Rule three: never fall in love with your guide.

He was currently breaking rule three in a way that had gotten so comfortable he'd almost stopped counting it as broken. Almost. He counted it the way a man counts a limp that doesn't hurt anymore — with a kind of fond resentment, on cold mornings especially.

"Captain," said Wrenna from the bow of the flatboat, "you're doing the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have the face where you're thinking about Sable and pretending you're thinking about cargo manifests."

Renn adjusted his collar against the river wind and gave her his most captainly expression, which had never once fooled anyone aboard the *Marrow's Kindness*. "I am thinking about cargo manifests."

"You're thinking about how Sable looked at the toll-stone last week like it bit them."

That was true, which was the annoying part. Renn thought about it more than he wanted to. Sable Crane — though nobody used that name, not even Sable; they were just *Sable*, the way the fog was just fog — had been guiding his runs through Thornfell for six years now, and in six years Renn had learned to read them the way sailors read weather. Not their face; Sable's face was a locked door with excellent manners. But their hands. Their pace. The way they touched stone.

And lately, Sable had been touching stone like it might touch back.

* * *

The trouble had started small, the way all trouble worth fearing did.

Three weeks ago, Sable had rerouted them off the Shepherd's Cut — the easiest pass through Thornfell, wide enough for mules, dry enough for sleeping — and taken them down the Hollow Stair instead, a route so miserable that Renn had once watched a grown man weep into his own boots on it.

"No reason?" Renn had asked at the time.

"Rockfall," Sable had said.

"There's no rockfall."

"There will be."

There hadn't been. There still hadn't, as far as Renn knew. And Sable didn't lie to him. That was the thing. Six years, and Sable had lied to him exactly once, about a bottle of plum brandy, and had confessed within the hour with the grim solemnity of someone turning themselves in for murder. So when Sable said *rockfall* and there was no rockfall, it wasn't a lie. It was something worse. It was a door closing.

Then there was the token.

Renn had seen it by accident, which was the only way anyone saw anything of Sable's. They'd shared a fire at the Halfway Cairn — one fire, three paces apart, an arrangement of great diplomatic precision that both of them maintained and neither of them believed in — and Sable had unwrapped their scarf against the cold, and there it was on its cord: a disc of dark metal, old, worn smooth, scored all over with fine lines that might have been script or might have been scratches or might have been both, the way prayers are.

It had been glowing. Faintly. A dull ember-light under the skin of the metal, pulsing slow, like something breathing under a blanket.

Sable had caught him looking and wrapped the scarf back over it, quick, and said nothing, and Renn, being a man with rules, had also said nothing, and the fire had crackled between them like a third party pretending not to eavesdrop.

But Renn had heard it. Not the token — himself. The little voice in his chest that sounded suspiciously like his mother saying, *You can love a person and still ask them questions, Renn. Those aren't opposites.*

His mother had also said never trust a man who names his boat after grief, so her record was mixed.

* * *

"Marrow's Kindness" was named for his drowned crew, actually, which made the joke less funny and more true, and this is why Renn generally steered conversations away from the name entirely.

He stood at the rail now, watching Thornfell come up out of the morning mist — those black shoulders of mountain, snow-capped even in autumn, hiding in their folds the roads that fed half the borderlands and appeared on no map drawn by any honest hand. Somewhere up in those folds was a waystation, and in the waystation was Sable, and in Sable was some worry wearing them hollow, and Renn intended to stand in front of all three of these things until somebody explained themselves.

"Supplies came in from Millbrook," Wrenna reported, appearing at his elbow with her ledger. "Flour, salt pork, lamp oil. Also forty pounds of iron filings, six crates of chalk, and a barrel of consecrated salt that the merchant swore up and down was 'agricultural lime.'"

Renn blinked. "Consecrated salt."

"That's what I said."

"Who orders consecrated salt labeled as agricultural lime?"

"Someone who expects us to ask questions and would rather we didn't." Wrenna tapped the ledger. "Order came through the usual cutout, but the handwriting's Sable's. Left-slanted g's. You know how I know everyone's g's."

Renn knew. Wrenna could identify a man by his letterforms faster than by his face, which she claimed was because faces lied and handwriting merely slanted. Iron filings, chalk, consecrated salt. That wasn't smuggling cargo. That was the shopping list of someone preparing a house for a siege, or a church for an exorcism, or — his mind supplied helpfully — both.

"Load it all," he said.

"Captain—"

"Load it all, Wrenna."

She looked at him for a long moment with the particular patience of a first mate who has watched her captain make the same category of decision for eleven years. "You're going to carry it up the mountain without asking what it's for."

"Yes."

"And then you're going to stand in Sable's kitchen drinking tea you hate—"

"It's a perfectly serviceable tea."

"—it's bark water, Captain, it tastes like a fence post — and you're going to wait for them to tell you."

"They'll tell me."

"Will they?"

Renn watched the mountain get bigger. "They've never kept anything from me except brandy and, presumably, other things I haven't noticed. Whatever this is, they're carrying it alone because they think carrying things alone is their job." He shrugged. "My job is making sure they don't have to do it alone. The asking-questions part comes after the not-being-alone part. It's a sequence."

Wrenna wrote something in the ledger. He suspected it was *captain's being an idiot again, affectionately,* but she had excellent g's herself and he couldn't prove anything.

* * *

The waystation sat in a fold of the mountain like a coin dropped in a pocket — low, stone-roofed, unremarkable unless you knew to look, and invisible if you didn't. Renn had delivered goods here maybe sixty times, and every time, the approach felt like being let in on something. Sable met them at the door the way they always did: already there, already watching, arms crossed against the wind, wearing that expression that managed to be welcoming and forbidding simultaneously, a trick Renn had never mastered and deeply admired.

"You brought the whole crew up," Sable said.

"Wrenna insisted. She says you keep better coffee than I do."

"I keep better everything than you do. Your ship smells like wet dog and ambition."

"My ship smells like *home.*"

"Your home smells like wet dog and ambition."

This was, for them, practically a sonnet. Renn grinned and shouldered the first crate, and they fell into the old rhythm — cargo up the steps, inventory called out, Sable's long fingers checking seals, the stove ticking warmth into the stone room. It was easy. It was the easiest hour of Renn's month, every month, and he treasured it with the specific greed of a man who knows exactly how much of his life is spent in weather.

It was going perfectly until he set down the last crate — the one with the salt — and saw the table.

Maps. Thornfell maps, Sable's own, annotated in that cramped left-slanting hand. But not trade routes. Renn knew trade routes the way he knew his own scars, and these weren't them. These were lines radiating outward from a single point high on the mountain's flank — a point marked with a circle, and inside the circle, pressed flat under a jar of ink, was the token.

Glowing. Brighter than at the fire. Bright enough to throw faint shadows of the jar across the maps, pulsing in a rhythm that Renn's hindbrain identified, unhelpfully, as *breathing.*

Sable followed his gaze. Renn watched them decide — he actually watched it happen, the flicker behind the eyes, the ledger of truths being balanced — and then Sable reached over and turned the jar so the token faced the wall.

"The light bothers me too," Renn said. "When people turn lamps away from me."

"It's nothing."

"Sable."

"It's an heirloom. It's been acting strange. I'm looking into it." Each sentence placed down like a card, face-up, minimal, final.

Renn nodded slowly. He poured the tea himself — the fence-post tea, God help him — and handed Sable a cup, and they drank standing up, and the silence had a new texture to it, one he didn't like.

Here is what Renn did not say: *I saw the maps. I know that's not a trade route. I know you've rerouted my last three runs around passes with nothing wrong with them. I know you ordered consecrated salt disguised as lime, and I know whatever that token is, it's getting louder, and I know — because I've known you six years, because I have loved you since roughly the second winter, quietly and without paperwork — that you are afraid.*

Here is what Renn said instead: "Wrenna wants to know if you'll take delivery of a piano next month. Some lordling's fleeing south. Says it's cursed."

"A cursed piano."

"So she claims. I told her curses are just cargo with a reputation."

Something eased in Sable's shoulders — barely, a rope paying out an inch — and they huffed what was, in Sable currency, a laugh. "Take it. If it's cursed, it can haunt the storeroom. Everything else up here does."

And Renn laughed, and the moment passed, and the lie of omission settled into place between them so gently that neither of them felt the weight of it yet. That was the thing about omissions. They were light as snowflakes going down. It was only the accumulation that bent roofs.

* * *

He stayed the night. This was normal — no captain with sense descended the Hollow Stair in the dark — and he took his usual corner bunk while Sable banked the stove, and the waystation settled into its creaks and silences.

He woke once, near the coldest hour, to a sound.

Not a sound, exactly. More the memory of one, the way you sometimes wake certain a bell rang somewhere. He lay still and listened. Wind in the pass. Stone ticking as it cooled. And underneath, faint as a pulse through a floorboard, a hum — low, sustained, coming not from the main room but from below, from beneath the floor, from the direction of the cellar door Sable kept locked and had never once explained.

The hum rose slightly. Fell. Rose.

Like breathing. Like something very large and very patient, drawing breath under a blanket of mountain.

Renn got up, padded to the main room, and found Sable sitting at the table in the dark, the token uncovered now, glowing bright enough to limn their face in ember-light. They weren't touching it. They were watching it the way you watch a snake cross a road — respectfully, from a distance, with a full accounting of teeth.

They didn't startle when they noticed him. Maybe they'd heard him wake. Maybe they'd heard him wake every night for years and simply never mentioned it.

"It's louder tonight," Sable said quietly. No deflection. Just fact, offered into the dark, perhaps because the dark made facts cheaper.

"Yeah," Renn said. "I heard it."

Sable looked at him then, and in the token's glow their face was open in a way Renn had almost never seen — tired, young, frightened, honest. On the edge of saying something enormous.

Whatever it was, they swallowed it. "Go back to sleep, Captain. Long descent tomorrow."

Renn stood there a moment longer. Every rule he had told him to sit down, to ask, to demand, to love out loud in the plain declarative sentences his mother had advocated. Instead he crossed the room, squeezed Sable's shoulder once — warm, brief, unmistakable — and said:

"Whatever's coming. However many crates. My boat holds it."

Then he went back to his bunk, because some doors you don't force. You just make sure they know you're standing outside them.

Behind him, in the dark, the token pulsed. And beneath the floor, patient as winter, something answered.
