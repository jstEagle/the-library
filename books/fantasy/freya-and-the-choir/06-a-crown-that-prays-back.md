## 6. A Crown That Prays Back

The letter arrived at the border infirmary folded inside a bale of bandages, which was fitting, Isolde thought later, because it would go on to cause far more bleeding than any of them could staunch.

It came from her sister, Maren, who had married well and lived badly in the capital—married to a wool merchant whose fortunes had turned radiant the same season the king had, and who now attended court functions with the glassy serenity of a man who had stopped asking questions. Maren wrote the way she always had: breathless, gossipy, dotted with little flourishes that pretended at wit. But this letter was different. This one had been copied out twice before sending, Isolde could tell from the ink ghosting through the cheap paper, and there were passages where the handwriting went careful and small, as though the writer feared the words themselves might be overheard.

*Dearest Izzie,* it began. *You will not believe what the city has become. Or perhaps you will, because you always believed in the old stories more than was good for you, and Mother said you'd end up either a saint or a cautionary tale, and I am beginning to fear both.*

Isolde read it by candlelight in the infirmary's back room, after the last lamps of the ward had gone down, with one ear cocked for the night sister's footsteps. She had been a priestess once. She was not supposed to be reading letters like this one. She was not supposed to be doing much of anything except binding wounds and minding her tongue, and she had gotten very good at both.

*The king is transformed,* Maren wrote. *You remember how he was—pale, sour, always coughing, everyone saying the crown sat on him like a debt. Well. He glows now. I do not mean this as poetry, Izzie, I mean it as report. There is light under his skin. The physicians say it is vigor. The priests say it is grace. Cook says it is whatever's in the wine, but cook says that about everything.*

*He has forgiven debts. Whole quarters of the city that hadn't paid taxes in years woke to find their ledgers burned in the square, and the king himself standing there in the ash, laughing. Laughing! Aldric! The same man who once fined a widow two coppers for letting her goat graze the palace verge. They are calling him Aldric the Generous already, and it has been only a season. Statues are being commissioned. The sculptors keep asking him to hold still longer than he likes.*

*And here is the part I nearly did not write, because Father says letters are read now, all letters, by men with soft voices and hard eyes. Three weeks ago I prayed. You know I never learned how properly—you got all the faith in the family like you got all the height—but I knelt by my window and I asked the king's blessing on Tomas's business, because everyone does it now, they pray to him directly, did you know that? Not through the temples. To him. His name, spoken aloud, at night, facing the palace.*

*He answered me, Izzie.*

*I don't mean I felt better. I mean the next morning there was a purse of silver on our doorstep with no footprints in the frost, and Tomas's worst debtor paid everything back with interest and wouldn't say why, just looked at me strangely when I thanked him, like a man remembering something he'd agreed to while asleep. And since then I have not slept. Not truly. I lie down and I close my eyes and I am somewhere else—a warm somewhere, a golden somewhere—and when morning comes I am more tired than when I lay down. Half the women at market have it now. We call it the King's Kindness and we laugh about it, but Izzie, we laugh the way people laugh on boats in weather.*

*Come visit. Or don't. Father says don't. But if you come, come before midwinter, because they're saying the renewal rite this year will be unlike anything in living memory. They've reopened the old cathedral—the suppressed one, the one with the doors that haven't opened since your order was scattered. Workmen went in first and came out changed. Quiet. Smiling too easily. One of them gave his wages away and walked into the river smiling, and they fished him out and he did it again, and now nobody talks about it.*

*Burn this letter. With love, your sister, Maren.*

Isolde did not burn it. Burning things had never been her gift; keeping them was. She tucked the letter into the false spine of a book of surgical diagrams, where she kept everything she wasn't supposed to have, which by now was most of a library.

* * *

The pilgrim came through eleven days later, on a cart, half-dead of cold and entirely dead of sleep.

His name was Odo, or so he said, and he was perhaps fifty, with the wind-scoured face of a man who had walked a long way telling himself stories to stay upright. He'd come from the capital on foot—he said the roads were free now, the tolls abolished, the inns open, the whole kingdom lit like a festival—and he'd prayed at the king's passing procession, and after that he simply hadn't slept again. Twenty-two days. The infirmary sisters counted. He had stopped counting at fifteen because numbers began to frighten him.

Isolde took his case the way she took all the strange ones: quietly, competently, and with her heart hammering like a novice's.

He was pleasant company, at first. Sleepless men often are, for a while—all that extra time gets spent on charm. He told her about the capital in a voice worn smooth as river stone. How the granaries overflowed without planting. How the wells ran sweet. How beggars had vanished—not arrested, just *gone*, and their corners taken up by flower sellers, because flowers were what the king liked, and the city had decided overnight that what the king liked was what the city liked.

"You should see the palace," Odo said, on his third day in the cot, propped up against pillows, eyes wide and unblinking and beautiful in the way of fevered things. "You can see it from anywhere now. It shines. Not gold-shine. Light-shine. Like a lamp behind a curtain."

"Like a lamp," Isolde repeated carefully, "behind a curtain."

"That's what I said." He smiled at her, pleased at being understood, and then his smile faltered, and he looked at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else. "Sister—I'm sorry, I shouldn't call you sister, you're not—"

"I was," Isolde said. "Once."

Something moved behind his face then. Recognition, maybe, or its poor cousin. "They brought your order back," he said. "Did you know? The White Choir. They sing at the palace gates every dawn now. The king restored them by decree. Everyone wept."

"I heard they were disbanded for heresy."

"They were disbanded for *losing*," Odo said, with the mild certainty of a man quoting scripture he'd learned yesterday. "But the king remembers the old ways. He says the old gods aren't dead, only sleeping, and a generous king wakes what sleeps." He paused. His hands had begun, very slightly, to tremble. "I prayed to him, you know. At the procession. Just a small prayer. My knees hurt and my feet hurt and I asked him for rest. Isn't that funny? I asked the king for rest."

"And did he give it?"

Odo looked at her for a long moment, and in that moment Isolde saw the thing she had seen once before, years ago, in an infirmary bed in another town, in a patient who had risen wrong-eyed and smiling and had to be held down by four strong sisters while she prayed over him until her voice broke. She saw the light sitting behind the eyes like a lamp behind a curtain.

"He gave me something," Odo said softly. "It isn't rest. But it's very generous."

* * *

That night Isolde did something she had not done in six years. She prayed.

Not to the king. Never to the king. She prayed the old way, the defrocked way, the way that had gotten her order scattered and her name struck from the rolls: kneeling, palms open, speaking to no one in particular, to whatever might still be listening above the snowline or beneath it, to anything at all that was not wearing a crown.

She prayed for Odo. She prayed for Maren. She prayed, in the stubborn private corner of herself where she kept the faith like a coal banked in ash, for guidance.

What she got was a headache, a vision of a mountain gate with frost on its teeth, and a patient who sat bolt upright at midnight and spoke in a voice that was not his own.

"Sister Vane," the voice said—it knew her name, her *taken* name, the one she'd hidden under—and Odo's face was slack and pleasant and lit from within. "You kneel so beautifully. Have you considered kneeling lower?"

Isolde did not scream, though every part of her wanted to. Six years of infirmary discipline held her spine straight. "Who speaks?"

"A friend of the crown. A friend of yours, soon. You were taught the deep liturgy, weren't you? The separating words? We remember you. We remember all our students." The smile widened past comfort. "We will need them soon. Midwinter comes. Bring your pretty voice to the renewal rite, and be restored, and be *radiant*, and never be tired or hungry or afraid again. So many have said yes. So few even hesitate."

"And those who hesitate?"

The lamp-behind-the-curtain flickered. For one instant Isolde saw Odo underneath—really saw him, the tired pilgrim, the man who'd walked a hundred miles on bad knees—and his mouth shaped a single soundless word: *run*.

Then the light settled back, smooth as oil, and the voice said, almost tenderly: "Those who hesitate are given more time to consider. We are generous."

Odo slumped back onto the pillow and slept—truly slept, for the first time in twenty-six days—and Isolde sat on the floor beside his cot until dawn, her palms still open, her mouth dry, understanding at last what she was looking at.

This was not a king blessed. This was a king *occupied*. And whatever wore him was gathering the kingdom the way she had once gathered lambs into a fold—gently, generously, one answered prayer at a time—because a flock that walks itself into the pen needs no dogs.

* * *

She packed that morning. She told the infirmary mother she'd had word of a sick relative in the mountain provinces, which was true in a way she didn't examine closely, because the vision had shown her a mountain gate, and mountains meant Thornfell, and Thornfell meant the Sandoval passes, and the Sandoval passes meant the one road in the kingdom that the shining generosity of King Aldric had somehow never managed to pave.

The token she carried—prayer-scarred, humming faintly, passed to her by a dying courier whose path had crossed hers two seasons ago in a story she still hadn't finished telling herself—had grown warmer every day since midwinter's approach began. It hummed now against her sternum like a second heartbeat, impatient, directional. North. Always north.

On her last evening she checked on Odo one final time. He was awake, calm, himself again—or near enough. The light had dimmed to an ember. Whatever had ridden him had lost interest once she'd refused the invitation, or perhaps it had simply gotten what it needed: confirmation of where she was going, and by which road.

"Sister," he said, catching her hand as she turned to leave. His grip was weak but urgent. "When I was under—when it spoke through me—I heard other voices. Underneath. Like singers very far off, holding one long note. And one of them was different. Older. Angry." His eyes were clear and frightened and human. "It sounded like it was behind stone. It sounded like it was *kept*."

Isolde stood very still.

"If you're going north," Odo whispered, "find whoever keeps the stone. Find whoever holds the note. Because the singing is getting louder, sister, and the king smiles wider every day, and midwinter—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Midwinter is coming fast."

She left before dawn, alone, on foot, following a humming token and a dying man's warning toward the frozen passes, carrying a stolen letter, a defrocked name, and the last of the deep liturgy in a head the crown's new church would very much like to open.

Behind her the capital glowed like a lamp behind a curtain, generous and sleepless and bright.

Ahead, the mountains waited, dark and silent—

—which was, Isolde knew now, exactly how a prison looks from the outside.
