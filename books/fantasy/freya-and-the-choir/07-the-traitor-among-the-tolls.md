## 7. The Traitor Among the Tolls

The trouble began, as most trouble did in the Thornfell passes, with a smell.

Sable noticed it on their Tuesday rounds—because a keeper of hidden roads kept rounds the way a housekeeper kept floors, and Tuesdays were for the low gate, the one the old maps called the Weeping Arch and everyone else called nothing at all, since calling it anything meant admitting it existed. The waystation above it had been in Sable's family for four generations, and in all that time the air beneath the arch had smelled of wet stone and cold iron and, faintly, of the pine pitch they used to seal the old cracks.

Now it smelled of tallow and something sweeter underneath. Something like honey left too long in the sun.

Sable stood at the mouth of the pass with their pack still shouldered and let the smell introduce itself properly before they went looking for its owner. This was a discipline their grandmother had drilled into them before she'd taught them to read a contour line: *the mountain tells you things first, if you let it finish its sentence.* Usually the mountain's sentences were about weather. Lately they had been about other things entirely.

They ducked under the arch.

There, wedged into the seam between two gate stones—a seam that had been empty since the thaw—sat an offering.

It was not a grand offering. That was what made it worse. Someone had left a stub of black candle, burned down to a coin-sized puddle; three copper coins, green with age, arranged in a neat triangle; and a strip of cloth folded with the kind of care that smugglers reserved for forged papers. Sable crouched and did not touch any of it, because their grandmother had also taught them that you never touched a thing until you understood who wanted you to touch it.

The cloth was the problem. Unfolded—and Sable unfolded it with a knife's tip, at arm's length, the way you'd open a letter from someone you suspected of being dead—it bore writing. Not ink. Blood-ink, the network called it: blood mixed with ash and tallow, the recipe every runner learned in their first winter because it was how you marked a route so the mark would hold through rain. Practical stuff. You wrote *safe* or *flooded* or *soldier patrol, wait till dusk* in blood-ink, and the mountain kept your words legible when everything else washed away.

This said none of those things.

It said names. Seven of them, written in a careful hand, each followed by a small mark like a knot. And beneath the names, in the same hand, smaller:

*Paid in advance. Hold until midwinter.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had been performed by the part of their brain that refused to accept input it didn't like.

Seven names. Souls prepaid. And the phrase *hold until midwinter*, which sat in Sable's stomach like a swallowed stone, because midwinter was coronation season, and everyone from the capital to the coast had been chattering about King Aldric's renewal rite—the pious young king, the beloved king, the king whose prayers were apparently answered so promptly these days that farmers set extra places at table for him.

Nothing answered from above the snowline. Sable had known that since childhood. Whatever had been answering lately lived below it, below the Weeping Arch, below the gate the Sandovals had spent four generations pretending was just a geological inconvenience.

And now somebody in the network—somebody who knew this seam, knew the blood-ink, knew the old marks—was feeding it.

* * *

Sable did what they always did with information that could get people killed: they carried it home, sealed it in oilcloth, and put it in the third loose stone behind the flour barrel, which was where inconvenient truths went to wait.

Then they made tea, because their grandmother had also taught them that no thought worth having survived an uncaffeinated mind, and sat by the window to think about hands.

That was the trick of it, Sable had learned over years of forging and un-forging documents for runners who couldn't write and captains who wrote too much. Every hand betrayed its owner eventually. The slant. The pressure. The way a person crossed their sevens or looped their letters or—this was the detail that mattered here—the particular flourish they used on a final stroke, like a signature they didn't know they were giving.

The offering-hand was careful. Patient. A hand that had been writing in bad light for decades and had learned economy. It pressed hard enough to score the cloth slightly, which meant either a heavy writer or someone whose fingers weren't as steady as they used to be and compensated with force.

Sable turned the memory of every ledger, every route-mark, every scrawled toll receipt in the network over in their mind like stones in a creek bed, looking for the one that matched.

There were only eleven people alive who knew the Weeping Arch existed. Sable counted them off. Two were dead last they'd heard—one of fever, one of a fall that everyone agreed had been a fall. One was Renn, who loved them, which was not evidence of innocence but was at least evidence of something. One was Sable themselves, and while they tried to maintain a healthy philosophical openness about the nature of self-knowledge, they felt reasonably confident they hadn't done it.

That left seven. And of the seven, six wrote like drunk spiders.

The seventh wrote like this.

Sable set down the tea and looked at their own hand shaking slightly around the cup, and thought: *Maro.*

Maro Vess. The man who had taught them the passes. Who had found them at fourteen, half-starved and furious, hiding in a culvert after the soldiers burned the farmstead, and had looked at them not with pity but with appraisal—the look of a man assessing whether a mule could carry loads—and had said, "You've got good balance and a bad attitude. Both are useful. Come on."

Who had taught them to read a snowfield, to hear a rockfall coming three breaths before it came, to walk a ridge in fog by counting steps. Who had taught them the blood-ink. Who had taught them, above all, the first law of the network: *we don't exist. No names on ledgers, no faces in memories, no debts on paper. Invisible is safe.*

Twenty-two years. Maro had been the closest thing to a father the mountain had given them, and the mountain was famously stingy with fathers.

And his hand was on the offering cloth.

Sable sat with that for a long time, the way you sit with a broken bone—not because sitting helps, but because moving makes it worse.

* * *

By evening they had done the sensible thing, which was to tell themselves several plausible stories in descending order of comfort.

Story one: Maro was old, and old men got strange, and maybe he'd taken up some folk-prayer habit, harmless, the kind of thing grandmothers did with saints' medals. Except grandmothers' prayers didn't say *paid in advance*. Prayers asked. This offered. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

Story two: someone was imitating Maro's hand. Possible. Difficult—his script had quirks you'd need twenty years of his receipts to copy—but possible. Except the imitation theory required an imitator who also knew the arch existed, which shrank the suspect pool right back down to the same seven people, minus one.

Story three, the one Sable kept circling back to because it was the least terrible: Maro had found the offerings and moved them, handled the cloth, left traces without meaning anything by it. A caretaker, not a giver.

Sable wanted story three very badly. Wanting it was, they understood dimly, exactly the problem. Their grandmother had a saying for that too: *the lie you want is the one that gets in free.* She'd said it about border guards, mostly, but borders were borders.

So instead of choosing a story, Sable chose a plan. They would watch the arch. Offerings left once might be grief or madness; offerings left on a schedule were commerce. And commerce had a supplier, and suppliers could be followed.

They told Renn nothing. This was the second hardest decision of the week, and they made it in about four seconds, which told them something about how much they already believed the answer.

* * *

Three nights later, the schedule revealed itself.

Sable had built themselves a watching spot in the rocks above the arch—a hollow behind a fallen slab, lined with their grandmother's old sheepskin, cold enough to make them respect their ancestors' toughness. They'd been there since dusk, chewing through a heel of bread and regretting the lack of the tea, when the sound came up the pass: footsteps. Unhurried. The walk of a man who owned the dark.

Maro came down the trail the way water comes down a channel, following shapes he'd known longer than Sable had been alive. He was sixty-eight now, broad still but stooped at the shoulder, his beard gone the color of frost-killed bracken. He carried a lantern with the shutter closed, letting out only a blade of light, and a satchel that clinked in a way satchels carrying honest cargo did not.

He stopped under the arch. He opened the shutter.

And Sable watched, from thirty feet up in the freezing dark, as Maro Vess knelt at the gate of the thing their family had been starving for four generations, and laid out his goods with the tenderness of a man setting a table for guests he loved.

A candle. Coins. A fresh strip of cloth, and a pot of blood-ink—heated gently over the candle flame, because cold blood-ink wouldn't take—and then, and this was the part Sable would carry for the rest of their life however long that turned out to be, Maro dipped the pen and paused, head bowed, like a man remembering.

He wrote three new names.

His lips moved as he wrote. Sable couldn't hear the words. They weren't sure they wanted to.

When he finished, he folded the cloth, tucked it into the seam, gathered his things, and stood there a moment longer with his bare palm flat against the gate stone. The stone hummed. Sable felt it through the rock they lay against, a vibration like a held note, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Thank you," Maro said aloud, quietly, to the stone. "Keep them safe. All of them. Even her."

Even her. Sable turned that phrase over the whole climb back to the waystation, and by the time they reached the door they had stopped trying to make it mean something innocent. There was no innocent version of a man paying a god in souls and asking it, tenderly, to keep someone named *her* safe.

* * *

The confrontation, when Sable finally steeled themselves to have it, happened two days later at the waystation, over stew, because Sable was their grandmother's student to the bone and believed ambushes went better fed.

Maro arrived expecting a routine meet—toll collections, route updates, the usual bookkeeping of invisibility. He hung his coat on the peg his own hands had carved forty years ago, sat in the chair that fit him like a glove, and said, "You look tired, cub."

"I've been sleeping badly."

"The mountain keeping you up?" There was something in the way he said it—light, almost teasing—that snagged against Sable's certainty like a burr in wool. He knew. Of course he knew. He'd probably felt them watching from the rocks; he'd taught them to feel watched himself.

"Something like that." Sable ladled stew into both bowls, set his down, and then reached into the shelf beside the hearth and brought out the oilcloth packet and laid it on the table between them, unfolded, the seven names facing up. "Tell me about the arch, Maro."

For a long moment he said nothing at all. He looked at the cloth the way men look at old photographs of themselves—young, foolish, recognizable. Then he sighed, a long sound like wind leaving a cave, and picked up his spoon, and ate a bite of stew, deliberately, unhurried, while Sable's heart hammered against their ribs.

"You watched me," he said finally. "Good. I'd have been disappointed if you hadn't."

"You're not going to deny it."

"What, lie to you?" He smiled, and it was the old smile, warm and crooked, the one that had gotten fourteen-year-old Sable out of a culvert and into a life. "I taught you to catch lies, cub. Insulting us both."

"Then explain it." Sable's voice came out harder than they intended. "Explain why the man who taught me the first law—we don't exist—is writing names in blood and paying them to the thing under the gate."

"Because the first law stopped working," Maro said simply. "Because we don't exist, cub, and look what that's bought us. Soldiers on the high passes every summer. Runners lost to weather because we can't afford lanterns. Children born in culverts." He tapped the table with one blunt finger. "I found you in one. Don't tell me you've forgotten."

"I haven't forgotten anything. That's rather the point."

"And now there's something up there—" he nodded vaguely southward, toward the capital, toward the crown "—that answers prayers. Real answers. Crops in a night. Blind children seeing. You've heard the same stories I have."

"I've heard stories," Sable said carefully, "and I know where the answers come from. Below the snowline, Maro. Below the arch. Nothing gentle lives under my gate."

"Under your gate." He laughed softly, not unkindly. "Listen to you. Keeper of a door you won't admit is a door." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment Sable saw the man from the culvert again, fierce and certain. "I'm not feeding it, girl. Boy. Whatever we're calling you this decade. I'm *bargaining* with it. There's a difference. The kingdom never gave us protection—we bought every mile of safety with sweat and silence. Well. Now there's a buyer on the other side of the table, and it pays better."

"It pays in souls."

"It pays in *safety*," Maro said. "The souls are the coin. Old ones. Sick ones. Names of folk who were going to die anyway—I'm not stealing years, cub, I'm redirecting them. Ask the blind child in Harrow Vale if the price was too steep. Ask her mother."

Sable stared at him. Somewhere in the stew steam between them, the whole architecture of their life was quietly collapsing. Because the worst part—the part they'd be chewing on for chapters yet—was that Maro wasn't lying, and wasn't mad, and wasn't even, in his own accounting, wrong. He was doing what he'd always done: trading in the gray market, finding the buyer nobody else would deal with, protecting his people by any means the lawless world allowed.

He'd just found a buyer that ate.

"Whose names?" Sable asked. "The new three. Whose?"

"Runners. Retired ones. Bad lungs, bad knees, folk the road already finished with." He said it easily, and Sable realized with a lurch that he genuinely had a list, a whole ledger of names, sorted and priced, and that somewhere in that ledger there might be a name Sable loved. Might be a name Maro thought Sable wouldn't miss.

Might be, Sable thought with a cold drop through their middle, a name Maro thought *they* wouldn't miss.

"Give me the ledger," they said.

"No."

"Maro—"

"Cub." He stood, and took his coat off the peg, and for a moment the room seemed colder for it. "You're a good keeper. Best I ever trained, and I trained you to be better than me on purpose. But you're keeping a door, and I'm feeding a wall, and when midwinter comes and that thing upstairs finishes its coronation business, one of us is going to be standing on the right side of history." He pulled his gloves on. "I hope it's you. I even pray it's you. But I'm not betting the network on your grandmother's superstitions."

"She wasn't superstitious," Sable said quietly. "She was scared. There's a difference, and the difference has teeth."

That landed. He flinched—actually flinched, the old man, the mountain's own father-figure—as if the words had come from somewhere deeper than Sable's throat. Then he was gone, out the door and down the trail, unhurried as ever, a blade of lantern light sliding away into the dark.

* * *

Sable washed the bowls. It seemed important, suddenly, to do a small ordinary task with their hands, to prove the world still contained tasks and hands and ordinary things.

They were drying the second bowl when they heard boots on the trail—two sets, one familiar, one not—and the door opened on Renn, snow in his beard, stamping the cold out of his boots, with a stranger behind him: a woman, gaunt and frost-burned, wrapped in a priest's traveling cloak that had clearly seen better decades. Her eyes were wild and steady at once, the eyes of someone who had run a long way on purpose.

"Sable," Renn said, and there was something in his voice they'd never heard there before—something between apology and alarm. "Found her collapsed at the Kettle Gap shelter, half-frozen, muttering about gates. Said she has to talk to the keeper of Thornfell." He hesitated. "Also she had this."

He held out his fist. Opened it.

In his palm lay a prayer-scarred token, humming faintly, glowing the color of deep ice—and around it, unmistakably, wound a thread of blood-inked cloth torn from a larger piece.

Torn from a piece bearing handwriting Sable now knew better than their own.

The stranger looked at Sable, and Sable looked at the token, and outside, far below the Weeping Arch, something patient turned over in its sleep.

"My name is Isolde Vane," the woman said. "And I think you and I need to compare lists of names."
