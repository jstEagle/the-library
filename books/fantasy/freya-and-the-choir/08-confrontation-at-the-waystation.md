## 8. Confrontation at the Waystation

The stranger — Isolde, she'd said, Isolde Vane, a name that tasted of incense and disgrace — spread her scraps of cloth across Sable's kitchen table like a fortune-teller laying out a bad future.

"Seven names," she said. "Written in blood-ink. I found six of them on bodies in my infirmary. The seventh walked out wrong-eyed and took a horse."

Sable didn't touch the cloth. They kept their hands wrapped around their tea bowl instead, which had gone cold an hour ago and was now serving its true purpose as something to hold so their fingers wouldn't shake visibly.

"And you traced them here," Sable said. "To Thornfell. To me."

"I traced them to the mountain. The mountain led me to your door." Isolde's eyes were the gray of old pewter, and they did not blink enough. "You're the gatekeeper."

"I'm a smuggler."

"You're a smuggler whose floorboards hum when I hold this token over them." She lifted the prayer-scarred disc, and beneath the table, under the flagstones, under the foundations, something answered — a low note, felt in the teeth rather than heard. Sable set down the tea bowl before it could rattle and give them away further.

"That," Sable said, "is the cellar settling."

"The cellar is singing, Master Sandoval."

"Sable. Just Sable." They stood, because standing made lying easier; you could pace, and pacing looked like thought rather than panic. "Here's what happens now. You drink that tea — fresh, I'll pour it, the first cup was a test and you failed by drinking it — and in the morning I take you down to Low Road camp myself, because there are people who need to see your face and hear about these names, and I am not making decisions like this alone at midnight in a kitchen that sings."

Isolde looked at them for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled, and it transformed her face from something carved into something merely tired. "You're stalling."

"I'm being *thorough*."

"You're terrified."

"I'm *thoroughly* terrified," Sable admitted, "but that doesn't make the tea less necessary."

It was at this precise moment — Sable would remember this forever, the cruelty of timing — that the door opened without a knock, and Maro came in out of the snow.

He was seventy if he was a day, built like a knot in old timber, with a white beard he trimmed himself badly and a laugh that could be heard across three switchbacks. He had taught Sable every pass, every false trail, every way to read weather in the behavior of birds. He had carried them, aged nine, through a whiteout on his back, telling jokes the whole way so they wouldn't cry. He was family in every way that mattered and several ways that didn't.

He was also, Sable realized with a lurch, wearing gloves indoors.

Maro never wore gloves indoors. His hands were his instruments; he said a man who couldn't feel stone couldn't read it.

"Evening, little gatekeeper," he said warmly, shaking snow off his shoulders. His eyes went to Isolde, then to the cloths on the table, then to the token still glowing in her palm, and the warmth didn't leave his face but it went somewhere deep, the way a fire goes behind a closed stove door. "Ah. Company. And here I hoped we'd have the night to ourselves."

"Maro." Sable's voice came out steady, which surprised them both. "Show me your hands."

"Now why would I do a thing like that?"

"Because I'm asking nicely, and because I have spent two weeks following blood-ink through my passes, and because you're wearing gloves in a heated room, and because—" Sable's throat tightened around the next words, and they forced them out anyway, because the whole point of truth was that it cost something going down, "—because I know your handwriting better than almost anyone alive. You taught it to me."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked against the shutters. Under the floor, faint and patient, the note hummed on.

Then Maro sighed, the sigh of a man setting down a heavy pack at the end of a long climb, and pulled off his gloves.

His right palm bore a fresh cut, neat and deliberate, the kind you make yourself with a clean blade. Around his wrist, half-hidden by his sleeve, wound a strip of cloth inked with names.

"How many?" Sable asked quietly.

"On me? Nine." Maro pulled out a chair — uninvited, comfortable, the entitlement of thirty years of family — and sat. "In the ledger? A hundred and forty. Give or take."

"*Give or take.*"

"Well, some of them are only partly given. It's not an exact art, little one. Even your grandmother's journal is vague on the mechanics, and she had the advantage of writing it herself."

The room tilted slightly. Sable gripped the back of a chair. "You've been in the vault."

"I've been in everything you've ever hidden, child. That's what mentors are *for*." He said it gently, horribly gently, and Isolde, still seated, had gone very still in the way of someone watching a fire decide which direction to spread.

"You gave them names," Sable said. "Souls. Prepaid. To the thing under the gate."

"To the *god*, yes." Maro corrected them with the mild pedantry he'd used correcting their knots and their mapwork since childhood. "And before you work yourself up to whatever speech you've been drafting — I can see it behind your eyes, you always did rehearse — let me tell you what a hundred and forty bought."

He counted on his bare fingers, the cut palm leaving a faint smear on each one.

"One: the Kettle Pass route stays open all winter. First time in forty years. No avalanches, no lost couriers, no frozen children in the snow — and don't look at me like that, we both know you carried one of those home yourself once and never told anyone, least of all me. Two: the toll-collectors on the king's road have stopped finding us. Every patrol that's come hunting the network this season has turned back with fog in its ears and forgetfulness in its head. Three: Captain Aldric's new piety has the kingdom looking up at the crown, not sideways at the mountains. We have never in our lives been this safe."

"We are not safe," Isolde said softly. "I've seen what 'safe' looks like when it's paid for in souls. I worked in the infirmary where they arrived."

"And who are you, exactly?"

"Isolde Vane. Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Bell."

Maro's eyebrows rose with genuine appreciation. "Defrocked. Well. The god does love its ironies." He turned back to Sable, and here his voice changed, dropped its performance, became the voice from the whiteout, the voice that had talked a frightened child down a mountain. "Little one. I know how this sounds. I've had weeks to hear how it sounds, alone, at night, and I'll tell you honestly: some nights I hate it. But then I think of Tam's daughter, who'd be dead of fever if the valley well hadn't turned sweet overnight. I think of the Ferro crew walking out of a collapse that should have buried them. I think of every name on that ledger and I ask — what was the kingdom ever going to do for them? Nothing. The kingdom has never done anything for people like us except hang us. This thing under the rock is the first power in history that ever offered us a *price we could afford*."

"It's not a price," Sable said. "It's an installment plan. And the final payment is due at midwinter, Maro. I found the journal. I know what happens when the crown touches the relic. It's not protection. It's a mouth opening."

"A mouth that's been whispering *our* roads clear," Maro said, "while every god above the snowline ignored us for three hundred years." He stood, unhurried, and began pulling on his gloves again. "You want to fight me, fight me. But understand what you're fighting for. Not the kingdom. Not even the dead. For a rule your grandmother followed and her mother before her: keep the mouth shut, keep the name hidden, keep paying just enough to stay quiet. I'm not breaking the family business, Sable. I'm finally doing it *honestly*. Openly. At scale."

"That's the most horrifying sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"I know." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the joke-at-three-switchbacks smile, and that was the worst part, that it hadn't changed at all. "Where's the ledger, little one?"

"Gone."

"Liar. You'd sooner burn your own hand than burn paper. Where is it?"

And that was when the second door opened, and Renn came in.

* * *

Renn Voss entered the waystation the way he entered everywhere: loudly, cheerfully, and covered in more snow than any one man should reasonably accumulate. He was stamping his boots and saying something about the mules having opinions about the weather when he registered the room — the drawn faces, the glowing token, the knife Sable had somehow acquired in their hand without either of them noticing the motion.

"...Bad time?" he said.

"Perfect time," said Maro pleasantly. "Captain Renn. Sit down. Your partner and I are discussing the family finances."

"Don't listen to him," Sable said. "Renn. Don't sit. Don't listen. In fact—"

But Renn had already seen the cloth on Maro's wrist, and Renn, whatever else he was — charming, infuriating, the best low-road captain in the range, the person Sable loved with the specific hopeless devotion of someone who had sworn never to love anyone visible — Renn was not stupid.

"That's blood-ink," he said. All the cheer had gone out of his voice like air from a punctured sail. "That's the stuff from the offerings. The ones that started showing up at the gate stones in autumn. We all noticed. We all agreed it was probably nothing." He looked at Sable. "We agreed it was probably nothing, Sable."

"It was probably nothing," Sable said, "right up until it wasn't."

"When? When did it stop being nothing? Because I've been watching you go gray for a month, and every time I asked, you told me it was the weather. You told me it was *routes*. You—" He stopped. Sable watched him arrive at it, watch the arithmetic complete itself behind his eyes, and hated every second of the journey. "The vault. The nights you wouldn't let me help carry things down. The journal. You knew. You've known for weeks and you told me *nothing*."

"I was trying to fix it before it became yours to carry."

"Oh, that's—" Renn laughed, one sharp note with no humor in it. "That's exactly what I do to *you*. That's my move. 'Don't worry about the debt, love, I've got it handled.' Do you know how much I hate that move?"

"Yes," Sable said. "That's rather why I used it."

For one absurd moment, despite everything, despite the traitor and the priestess and the humming floor, Sable nearly laughed too — because it was true, and because they had learned all their worst habits from loving him.

Maro moved.

He was seventy years old and he moved like water finding a crack. One moment he was standing by the chair; the next he had Isolde's token out of her hand — she gasped, snatched at air — and his knife was flat against the table's edge, not threatening anyone, merely present, and his other hand was sweeping the blood-inked cloths off the tabletop and into his coat.

"Those are evidence," Isolde said sharply.

"These are *names*," Maro said, "and they belong with the rest." He backed toward the door, and Sable lunged — genuinely lunged, meaning to take him down the way he'd taught them to take down men in doorways — and Maro caught their wrist, turned them, and put them against the wall with a gentleness that was somehow worse than violence. Up close, Sable could smell the blood-ink on him, coppery and sweet, like pennies and honey.

"Out of my way, little gatekeeper."

"You taught me the passes," Sable said, breathless, pinned. "Every one. You used to say a guide's first duty is knowing where the road ends."

"It does end," Maro said. "Just past the Weeping Arch. I've seen it. Have you?" He released them, stepped back, and for a moment his face was naked — old, tired, afraid in a way he would deny to his grave. "I'm saving them, Sable. All of them. The living and the named. Somebody has to pay the toll, and I'd rather it be me than you. That's the whole of it. That's the only honest thing I've said tonight."

"Then give back the names and say it again."

"No." And he was gone, out into the dark and the snow, moving downhill toward the deep routes with the sureness of the man who had mapped them, taking the token and the cloths and a hundred and forty souls with him.

Sable stood against the wall, breathing.

The kitchen was very quiet. Isolde had risen and was flexing her empty hand, muttering something that sounded suspiciously like liturgy. And Renn stood by the fire with his arms crossed, snow melting off his shoulders onto the clean floor, looking at Sable with an expression they had never once seen him wear in eleven years: closed.

"So," he said. "Your grandmother kept a journal about the gate. You've known for weeks. There's a hundred and forty names. And the man who raised you just robbed us on his way out the door." He unfolded his arms. Folded them again. "Anything else you'd like to mention, or shall I start guessing? Because I'm very good at guessing, and none of my guesses are pleasant."

"Renn—"

"No. Actually — no. You had your system. Silence, handled debts, protect everyone by telling no one. I respect the craft. I've used it." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and he pushed past it. "But I'm done being protected, Sable. So either you talk, or I walk down to camp and tell the crew everything I *don't* know, which will frighten them considerably more."

Isolde cleared her throat. "If it helps," she offered, into the ringing silence, "the rite must be undone before midwinter or the merge becomes permanent, and I know the forbidden liturgy of a suppressed order. Also, I believe your mentor just headed for the threshold, and if he reaches it with those names, the coronation completes early."

Renn stared at her. Then at Sable. Then, slowly, he sat down in the chair Maro had warmed, as though his legs had filed a formal complaint.

"Midwinter," he repeated. "As in... three weeks away. Midwinter."

"As in nineteen days," said Isolde.

Renn put his face in his hands. His shoulders shook once — Sable couldn't tell, even after eleven years, whether it was laughter or grief, and suspected Renn couldn't either. When he looked up, his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level.

"Nineteen days," he said. "Fine. Fine! Then you'd better start talking, love, because I am coming down that hole with you, and I refuse to do it ignorant." He pointed at the floor, at the faint patient hum beneath it. "Starting with what in the hells is *singing* under your kitchen."

Sable took a long breath. Outside, far below the Weeping Arch, something patient waited for its names to come home — and somewhere between here and there, an old man walked through the dark carrying them, whistling, the way he always had, the way that had once meant *safe*.

"It's a long story," Sable said.

"We have nineteen days," said Renn. "Talk fast."
