## 9. Isolde Arrives

The storm came down off the high passes two days after Maro vanished with the ledger, and it came the way bad news comes—sideways, all at once, and determined to get into every seam you owned.

Sable spent those two days doing what Sable did best, which was pretending everything was fine. They restocked the waystation's false shelves so the front room still looked like a modest trader's post selling rope and dried apples. They re-hung the gate wards in the back passage, though the prayer-scarred token had gone quiet since that night, sitting in its iron box like a wasp in winter. They ate. They slept badly. They did not talk about the way Renn had looked at them across the wreckage of the fight—not angry, exactly. Worse than angry. *Recalculating.*

"You're going to wear a groove in those floorboards," Renn said on the second evening, from his chair by the stove. He had not left. That was either a very good sign or a very ominous one; with Renn it was often both.

"I'm inventorying."

"You've inventoried that shelf four times. There are eleven jars of pickled onions and you've counted them eleven times." He turned his cup in his hands. It was chipped, and he'd refused every offer of a new one for six years, on the grounds that a captain's cup should look like it had survived something. "Sable."

"Don't."

"Maro taught me to read current off the Greywater when I was nineteen. I'm allowed to be—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I'm allowed to be upset."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell me what's under this mountain if I ask again?"

Sable looked at him. The firelight did kind things to his face that he didn't deserve while being stubborn. "Yes," they said finally. "If you ask again, I'll tell you everything. But I'd rather tell you once, properly, with a map, than twice, badly, at midnight."

Renn considered this. "That's the most honest non-answer you've ever given me."

"I'm growing as a person."

"You're stalling as a person." But some of the frost went out of his voice, and he refilled both cups, and for an hour the storm was just weather again.

It stopped being just weather shortly after midnight, when the dog they didn't have started barking.

* * *

There was no dog. There never had been. What there was, instead, was the gate—the deep one, the mouth beneath the waystation—and lately it had developed opinions about visitors. The first time it happened, three nights ago, Sable had nearly put a knife through their own foot in surprise: a sound like a hound giving voice somewhere below the floor, muffled by forty feet of stone, unmistakably a warning.

Now it barked again, twice, and Sable was out of bed with a lamp before the echo died.

"She's coming up the switchbacks," Sable said, shaking Renn awake. "Alone. On foot. In this."

"In *this?*" Renn sat up, hair flat on one side, looking like a man who'd been asked to believe in flying fish. "Nobody climbs the north switchbacks in a whiteout. Nobody climbs them in June without a guide and a death wish."

"Well, someone's doing it now, and the gate thinks we should open the door."

"The gate has a dog now."

"The gate has always been weird. We just didn't have ears for it before." Sable was already pulling on boots. "Lanterns on the lower posts. If she goes off the path above the icefall, she'll be dead before we find her."

They went out into a night that pressed like wet wool. The wind had dropped, which was worse—it meant the snow fell straight down, silent, erasing the world in long soft strokes. Sable knew these paths the way other people knew their own hands, but even their knees were muttering complaints by the time they reached the third post. The lantern there burned low and orange in its niche, and against it, twenty yards below, a figure clung to the rock like something the sea had rejected.

She was small. That was Sable's first coherent thought. A small woman in priest's grey gone stiff with ice, climbing with a technique that could only be described as *aggressive piety*—no skill in it whatsoever, nothing but refusal. She slipped, caught herself, slipped again, and each time she fell she made a sound that wasn't a scream. It was more like a word.

When they got her over the lip of the path, she fought them. Of course she did. She was half-conscious and entirely convinced she was being robbed, which said something depressing about her life, and she landed one surprisingly good elbow before Renn wrapped her in his coat and simply picked her up.

"Infirmary," she slurred against his shoulder. "Border infirmary. I have to—I was told—"

"Told by who?" Sable asked.

Her eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, exhausted—and around the pupils, faint as breath on glass, they were ringed with gold. Not sick-gold. *Lit*-gold. Sable had seen that color once before, in a dying courier's token, humming against the gate stones.

"You," the woman whispered, staring at Sable as if the storm had handed her a letter addressed in her own handwriting. "You're the keeper's blood. Oh thank every dead god. I climbed the wrong mountain for four days and it was still the right one."

* * *

By the stove, thawed and furious at her own body's weakness, the woman introduced herself as Isolde Vane, defrocked, order suppressed, currently unemployed and unemployable. She ate three bowls of barley soup with the focus of someone settling a personal grudge against starvation, and then she pulled a satchel from inside her frozen coat and set it on the table like a woman laying down a winning hand.

"Before anything else," she said. "Before questions, before sleep, before you decide whether to throw me back into the snow. Look at this."

Inside the satchel: papers. Dozens of them, water-stained, some scorched at the edges, wrapped in oilcloth with the care people give relics or explosives. Isolde unwrapped them with fingers still clumsy from cold.

"These are from the archive of my old order. The Choir of the Quiet Gate—we kept records the crown burned, mostly, but Brother Anselm hid a crate in a well in Vellmark and I fished it out in autumn because I am, apparently, the last paranoid woman in the kingdom." She spread pages across Sable's table: diagrams, columns of script, a drawing that made Sable's stomach drop—a mountain cut away in section, showing passages Sable knew intimately, and beneath them a chamber no map had ever shown, drawn as a mouth. Teeth marked in red ink.

"This is Thornfell," Sable said quietly.

"This is Thornfell as my order understood it eight hundred years ago." Isolde tapped the mouth. "And this"—she turned to a page dense with liturgical notation—"is the rite your king is preparing to complete at midwinter. The Renewal of the Crown. Every coronation since Aldric's line began has included it. Crown touches relic, relic touches king, king renews his covenant." Her finger moved down the page. "Do you know what covenant means, in the old tongue? It doesn't mean promise. It means *binding*. And the rite has a window. Before midwinter, the binding between god and king is a glove. After—if the crown completes the circuit—the merge sets. Permanent. Irreversible. You couldn't cut them apart with the whole liturgy of heaven."

The fire popped. Renn had gone very still by the door, the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"How permanent?" he asked.

"There are accounts," Isolde said, "of the last time. King Osric, four hundred years ago. The hymns say he died nobly. The archive says he stopped dying. His court wrote letters about him for six years after his funeral—letters describing how he attended council, how he ate, how he smiled at ambassadors with his eyes closed. Then the letters stop, and the order's records begin, and the first entry is the binding of the gate." She looked up at Sable, and the gold rings around her pupils caught the light. "Your family didn't build a wall around a prison, Keeper. Your family sewed a wound shut. And somebody upstairs has been picking at the stitches."

"That's why the prayers work," Sable said. The words came out flat, distant, like someone else's voice. "The crops. The blind child in Hollowmere. It's not generosity. It's a thing getting comfortable in its skin, flexing."

"It's a thing buying love," Isolde corrected. "Which is worse. Fear you can starve. Love feeds itself." She gathered the pages back into their careful stacks. "I prayed, after my order fell. Just small things. A fever breaking. A safe road. And when the answers started coming back too good—when a man I'd prayed over stood up from his sickbed with gold in his eyes and thanked me in a voice that wasn't his—I ran. Because I know what answering gods cost last time, and I know whose fault it was." Her jaw tightened. "Mine. Ours. We stopped watching. We let ourselves be scattered and called it peace."

* * *

They talked until the lamps burned low. Isolde laid out the mechanics with a scholar's grim pleasure: the descent road, the threshold chamber, the sequence of unbinding that required a keeper's blood to hold the gate's mouth open while a consecrated voice spoke the separation liturgy. She needed Sable. Sable needed her. Neither of them enjoyed the arithmetic.

"You understand my objection," Sable said, somewhere past the fourth hour. "You're a priestess of the order that fed this thing for centuries. Forgive me if I don't weep with gratitude at your defection."

"Defrocked," Isolde said, with the precision of a woman who had argued this point with bishops. "Defrocked, suppressed, excommunicated by a church that no longer exists in any form worth obeying. And you're the heir of jailers who were really wardens who were really, if your grandmother's journal tells the truth—and I read it while you slept, yes, I apologize, no, I'm not sorry—*inviters*. So we're both bastards of broken oaths, and the difference between us is that I confess mine."

Sable stared at her. "You read my grandmother's journal."

"Every page. You have excellent penmanship in your family. Terrible security." Isolde folded her hands, entirely unrepentant, and something in Sable—exhausted, grieving Maro, hollowed out by two days of silence—nearly laughed. It would have been the first laugh since the ledger burned its absence into the room, and it surprised them both.

"You're going to be unbearable," Sable said.

"I'm going to be *right*, which is worse." Isolde's expression sobered. "Keeper. Sable. I climbed four days through a whiteout because the token called and the gate answered and I have nowhere else in the world that isn't already listening to it. I am asking to help. I am not asking to be trusted—that takes longer, and we may not have longer. Midwinter is nineteen days off."

From the doorway, Renn cleared his throat. He'd been quiet so long Sable had almost forgotten him, which was a professional failure on Sable's part and a testament to Isolde on his.

"Nineteen days," Renn said. "Down the deep route. That's the Greywater in flood season, the Chimney, the singing dark where Marta lost her lantern crew. That's not a walk, that's a campaign. Which means supplies, ropes, and a captain." He looked at Sable, and whatever recalculation had been running behind his eyes for two days finished, and settled, and came up with an answer Sable hadn't earned yet. "I said I'd move supplies for whatever's coming without asking questions. I'm done with that part. So: ask me."

Sable held his gaze. "Come with us. All the way down. Guard the passage while we work. And hear everything first, tonight, no omissions—what's under the gate, what my family did, what Maro took, all of it."

"All of it," Renn agreed. "Starting with why the gate barks like a dog."

"That," said Isolde, "is actually in the archive."

"Of course it is," said Renn.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and clean behind the storm's retreat, and the three of them stood at the table over the section-drawing of the mountain, planning a road down into the thing's throat. Isolde annotated. Renn calculated loads. Sable found, to their own mild horror, that they were talking—openly, specifically, naming the passages and the dangers and the truth of the Sandoval inheritance aloud, in front of witnesses, as if invisibility were a coat they'd simply decided to hang by the door.

Nineteen days. Below them, patient as winter, the gate waited—and as they bent over the maps, faint through the floor, so soft it might have been the wind in the chimneys, it began, very quietly, to hum.
