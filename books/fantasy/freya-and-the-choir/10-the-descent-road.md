## 10. The Descent Road

The smugglers' deepest route had no name on any map, which was the point. Sable called it the Underway when they called it anything at all, and they had walked it perhaps a dozen times in their life—always alone, always carrying something that needed never to have existed.

Now they carried a defrocked priestess, a lovesick captain, and the last unburned truth of their family, and the mountain knew it.

"You're smiling," Renn said behind them, his voice bouncing off stone that had been swallowing sound for ten thousand years. "Nobody smiles down here. It's against the rules. I'm fairly sure it's in the rules."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your voice is smiling. I can hear it in your voice."

"That's called optimism, Captain, and I'd thank you not to diagnose me by echo." Sable held the lantern higher. The passage ahead sloped down through rock the color of old honey, veined with quartz that caught the light and threw it back wrong—warmer than it should be, like lamplight through a window where someone was home. "Isolde. How are your knees?"

"My knees," Isolde said from the rear, breathing hard, "have opinions about your family's architectural choices. Stairs carved for people half my height. Did your grandmother build this, or a spiteful child?"

"A spiteful child, probably. The Sandovals were short until my mother." Sable stepped over a crack in the floor where something pale grew—not moss, not root, but threads fine as hair that flinched from the lantern flame. They did not mention the threads. There was a great deal they were not mentioning, and the list grew longer every hour, and every item on it felt like a small theft from the two people walking into the dark on their word alone.

They had been underground since dawn. Above them, midwinter was coming on like a debt collector, and somewhere beneath all this honey-colored stone, a king was being slowly digested by a god while the kingdom sang his praises.

*Three days,* Isolde had said at the waystation, her frostbitten fingers counting on a rosary she no longer technically believed in. *Perhaps four. The rite completes when the crown touches the relic at the turning of the year.*

*Then we walk fast,* Sable had said, and Renn had looked at both of them the way he looked at a river crossing in thaw season—measuring, unhappy, committed anyway—and started packing rope.

Now the mountain began to test them.

Sable felt it first as a change in the air pressure, the way you feel a storm before the clouds arrive. Then the whispering started.

It came up through the soles of their boots, faint as water under ice: words without a mouth, prayers without a pray-er. *Keep us. Keep us. Keep the door.* The old jailer's litany, worn smooth by generations of Sandoval throats, now murmured by the rock itself in voices that almost—but not quite—belonged to the dead.

Isolde stopped walking. "Do you hear—"

"Yes."

"It's praying." Her hand found her rosary. "The mountain is praying. That's either the most beautiful thing I've ever heard or the worst, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"It's an echo," Renn said firmly. "Mountains echo. I've heard this one echo my own name in three different valleys, and I refuse to be flattered by it."

"It's not echoing you," Sable said quietly. "It's remembering me. My grandmother prayed that litany down here every day of her life. The stone learned it." They did not say: *and the stone has been learning what else we say, too.* They had noticed, over the last mile, that the whispers had begun picking up fragments—*midwinter*, *the crown*, *unbind him*—stitching their own conversation into the litany like bright thread into gray cloth. The mountain was listening. The mountain was reporting.

To whom, Sable did not want to say aloud.

They walked faster.

* * *

The first trial came at the Hollow Stair, where the passage dropped through a chimney of stone so narrow they had to turn sideways, and where—according to every map in Sable's head—the footing had been sound for sixty years.

Halfway down, it stopped being sound.

Renn went first, testing each step with his boot heel the way he tested river ice. He was three steps from the bottom when the step beneath him sighed—a soft exhalation of grit—and crumbled like stale bread. He dropped. Sable lunged, caught his wrist, and for one lurching moment hung there, boots braced against the chimney walls, holding the full weight of a man plus everything he carried, while below them the broken stair rained down into darkness and did not, as far as either of them could hear, ever land.

"Rope," Isolde said uselessly, already uncoiling it.

"Thank you," Renn wheezed, "for the rope. Very timely."

"Hold still. Both of you. If you struggle, the rest of the stair goes." Sable's arms burned. Their boots found purchase in grooves their grandmother's hands had worn, and they thought, absurdly, *she held people here too, she must have, this is a family trade after all*—and then the whispering rose around them, and it was no longer the litany.

It was Renn's voice. Coming out of the stone. From below.

*"Let go,"* the mountain said, in a fair copy of the captain's low rasp. *"You've carried enough. Everyone puts you down eventually, Sable. Names, ledgers, secrets—you've been everyone's pack mule since you could walk. Let go. Rest. The dark is very good at keeping things."*

Sable's grip tightened until their knuckles cracked.

"That's not me," Renn said, very calmly, from the end of their arm. "For the record. I would never say 'pack mule.' I'd say 'beloved beast of burden,' and I'd say it nicer."

"Shut up and climb," Sable said, and hauled.

He got his free hand onto a ledge; Isolde's rope found his other wrist; between the three of them they peeled him off the dark like a barnacle off a hull, and stood at the bottom of the Hollow Stair breathing hard in a chamber where the quartz veins pulsed faintly, disappointedly, like a lamp being dimmed.

"It offered me rest," Sable said, when they could speak.

"It offered me my crew," Renn said. "Back on the Low Road, before the wreck. Dree and the others. Whole. Laughing. It asked what I'd give." He was quiet a moment. "I told it nothing. But Sable—I want you to know it wasn't easy. I want you to know how long I stood there."

"I know," Sable said. "I watched your face."

"And you didn't say anything?"

"I figured you'd tell me. When you were ready." They hesitated, then added, because the mountain had taught them something about the cost of silence in the last quarter mile: "I'm glad you did. And I'm sorry about Dree. Truly."

Renn nodded once, the way men nod when a wound has been acknowledged and they'd prefer not to look at it directly. "What did it offer Isolde?"

They both turned. Isolde was standing apart, rosary wrapped twice around her fist, staring at a wall of quartz with an expression Sable recognized from mirrors: the look of someone who has just been shown exactly who they are and is deciding whether to be grateful.

"It quoted scripture at me," she said. "My order's scripture. The suppressed canon—the parts that were struck before I was even born. It knows the liturgy better than I do. It offered me..." She laughed, short and bitter. "It offered me my order back. Restored. Every altar relit, every sister returned, my name cleared in the eyes of a church that doesn't exist anymore. All I had to do was stop walking." She looked at them both. "I said no. But I want it noted that I said no *slowly*."

"Noted," Sable said. "In the ledger."

"What ledger?"

"The one I keep in my head. It's mostly crimes. Yours is the first mercy."

That got a real laugh out of her, thin and surprised, and Sable thought: *good. Keep laughing. The mountain feeds on fear, but I don't think it knows what to do with jokes.* It was a small theory. They clung to it the way shipwrecked sailors cling to driftwood—without examining whether it would hold.

They camped in a side chamber where the quartz had gone dark and sullen, and ate cold beans, and took watches in shifts, because none of them trusted the dark enough to sleep in it unguarded.

During Sable's watch, the whispering came again, and this time it used their grandmother's voice.

They let it finish the whole litany before they answered, under their breath, in the old cadence: *"Keep the door. Keep the door. And be kept."*

The quartz stayed dark the rest of the night. Sable chose to believe that was respect.

* * *

The second trial was not a temptation. It was a collapse.

They heard it coming—a deep structural groan, the sound a mountain makes when it shifts its weight in its sleep—and then the passage behind them filled with dust and thunder, and the passage ahead did the same, and the roof of the chamber where they stood began shedding stone like a dog shedding winter coat.

"Run!" Renn bellowed, and grabbed Isolde, and Sable grabbed Sable's own sense of direction and *used* it, because there was one way out of this chamber that wasn't on any smuggler's map—it was on the Sandoval maps, the ones burned in chapter four of the family's long history of burning inconvenient things—and it was a crack in the floor barely wide enough for a determined cat.

"The floor?!" Isolde shrieked.

"The floor! It's a drain channel! Old ritual plumbing!"

"This mountain has *plumbing*?"

"Everything holy has plumbing! Move!"

They went down into the crack single file, Sable first, sliding on their backs through wet stone while the ceiling came apart above them, Renn swearing with the fluency of a man who had grown up around sailors and improved upon the tradition, Isolde reciting something that was either a psalm or a threat. The drain channel dropped them—literally dropped them, the last six feet with no warning at all—into a lower gallery, where they lay in a heap on ancient flagstones while the mountain finished rearranging itself overhead and the dust drifted down like gray snow.

When the ringing in their ears faded, Sable sat up and looked around, and their stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

They knew this gallery. Not from maps. From the journal.

Long columns of black stone, each one carved over every inch with names—thousands of names, tens of thousands, in scripts older than the kingdom, older than the language. This was the Gallery of the Given. The place where the first Sandoval had written down what they owed.

"Oh," Isolde breathed. She had gone very still, the way she went still in the infirmary when a patient turned. "Oh, this is a temple. This was always a temple. Your family didn't build a prison, Sable. They built a *church* and lied about it."

"I know." Sable's voice came out flat. "I read the journal. I'm still angry about it, if you'd like a turn."

At the far end of the gallery, one column stood apart from the others, newer-looking, its carvings shallower. Sable walked to it because their feet insisted, and found near its base a cluster of fresh marks—names in blood-ink, the ink still dark, the handwriting achingly familiar.

Maro had been here. Maro had stood exactly here, brush in hand, adding souls to the account.

And beneath Maro's newest entries, in a cramped, furious, unmistakably feminine hand, was a line Sable had seen only once before, in a journal hidden under a waystation floor:

*I, Maren Sandoval, keeper of this gate, confess that we did not bind it. We fed it. May whoever reads this feed it less.*

Below that, in fresher ink, someone had answered her.

The writing was elegant, patient, almost kind. It said:

*She fed it less. You will feed it more. Or you will feed it everything. There has never been a third option, little keeper, though I admire your family's optimism about arithmetic.*

Sable stood there a long time. Behind them, Renn was saying something about the ceiling holding, and Isolde was saying something about the geometry of the columns, and neither of them had yet seen what Sable was seeing, and Sable found—to their own mild surprise—that they were not going to hide it. Not this. Not anymore. The mountain had spent two days offering them every comfortable lie in their inventory, and Sable was done shopping.

"Come here," they said. "Both of you. You need to see this."

They came. They read. The silence afterward had weight, like snowfall.

"So," Renn said at last, in the careful voice of a man handling something explosive. "The god writes. In your grandmother's handwriting-adjacent style. Down here. Where nobody was supposed to come for another generation." He looked at Sable. "It knew we were coming."

"It's known since the token hummed," Sable said. "Maybe since the first prayer got answered. We're not sneaking up on it, Renn. We never were. It's been setting the table."

"Then why let us walk this far?"

"Because," Isolde said quietly, and her hand found the rosary again, and this time she did not seem embarrassed about it, "it wants to see what we choose. That's what it does. That's all it's ever done, if the journal's true. It doesn't take. It *offers*. And it keeps meticulous accounts of who says yes." She looked up at the columns of names, thousands of years of yeses, and her jaw set. "Well. I've been defrocked once. I imagine I can survive being refused by a god."

Something in Sable's chest, tight since the waystation, loosened by a single degree. "The third option," they said. "Grandmother wrote there was never a third option. I'm starting to think she was wrong. I'm starting to think the third option is just—refusing the arithmetic. Refusing to feed it at all and taking the binding into yourself instead." They touched the column, the confession, the patient cruel answer beneath it. "It's never been tried. Because it costs the one thing no keeper's ever been willing to pay."

"Which is?" Renn asked.

"Being seen doing it."

Ahead, the gallery ended at a doorway that had no door—only a threshold of white stone, and beyond it, stairs descending into a cold so complete that the lantern flame leaned away from it like a child from a stranger. From far below, faint and rhythmic, came a sound none of them could mistake for echo or plumbing.

Singing. A choir of one, practicing.

Isolde crossed herself with a rosary she no longer believed in. Renn checked his knife, then, after a moment, checked it again, as though the second check might reveal it had become a bigger knife.

Sable shouldered the lantern, thought about pack mules and beloved beasts of burden, and stepped toward the white threshold.

Behind them, soft as breath, the mountain whispered their name—not the name they'd used all their life, but the true one, the buried one, the one their grandmother had hidden—and it did not sound like a threat.

It sounded like a welcome.

That was the worst part.
