## 11. Maro's Bargain

The ledger weighed more than a ledger should.

Maro had carried it for thirty years—first as a tally of debts owed to him, then as a tally of debts he owed others—and it had never once felt heavy until tonight. Now it dragged at his shoulder like a sleeping child, and the blood-ink names on its pages were warm through the oilcloth wrapping, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies.

He told himself that was the mountain. The mountain was always doing things like that now.

"Stop it," he muttered to the dark ahead of him. "You're an old man walking downhill in the snow. That's all this is."

The dark did not answer, which was, he reflected, one of the few courtesies left in the world.

* * *

Maro Quill had taught half the free roads to a generation of smugglers, and he had taught them the way his own mother had taught him: by walking them until their feet knew the way better than their heads did. Feet don't panic. Heads do. A head will tell you the pass is closed, the king's men are close, you're too old for this. Feet just keep going.

His feet kept going now, down the switchbacks below the Thornfell waystation, past the cairns that marked where the honest trails ended and the Sandoval roads began. Snow squeaked under his boots. His breath came out in little ghosts that the mountain seemed to consider, and then accept, and then keep.

That was new, too. He'd been coming to these passes since before Sable was born, and the mountain had never once wanted anything from him except that he not fall off it.

Now it wanted everything.

"You could say no," he said out loud, because saying things out loud was how he thought, and because there was no one up here to hear him be weak. "You could turn around. Go back to the low road. Drink yourself stupid in Renn's hold like a sensible old man and let the world end politely."

But even as he said it, his hand went to the ledger on his shoulder, and held it, the way you hold a promise.

* * *

Here was the thing about Maro Quill that nobody who loved him ever quite understood, though Sable had come closest: he had never once broken faith with anyone. Not the network, not the families in the valley villages who moved his cargo and looked away from his riders, not the fugitives he'd ferried over the snowline with their names scraped off and their faces wrapped against the cold. Thirty years, and every debt paid, every promise kept, every secret carried to the grave or nearly so.

It was the kingdom that had never kept faith with him.

He'd been eleven when the soldiers burned the chapel in Hollowmere—not because anyone there had done anything, but because the king's tax collectors had counted the pews and found the congregation short. He'd watched a priestess they later hanged try to stand between the soldiers and a family of weavers, and he'd learned the lesson that organized his whole life afterward, the way a keel organizes a boat:

Nobody is coming. Nobody with a crown or a collar or a god is coming. You build your own rescue or you drown.

So he'd built. He'd built roads that didn't exist on any map, tolls that fed the villages instead of the treasury, a network that answered prayers faster than any church ever had—a widow needed firewood, a shepherd needed medicine, a boy needed to disappear before the conscription carts found him—and the network answered, because Maro made sure it answered. He'd spent his whole life being the thing the kingdom refused to be: reliable.

And then, three winters ago, the kingdom had gotten religion.

* * *

He'd seen the first signs in the toll ledgers. That was the joke of it—the god had announced itself to Maro Quill through bookkeeping. Offerings left at the gate stones, small ones at first, bread and copper, then stranger things. Names written in ink that wasn't ink. And beside each name, in a hand Maro recognized because he'd taught it, a price.

Sable had noticed too. Of course Sable had noticed; Sable noticed everything, bless their paranoid heart, and had come to him with the token humming in their fist and fear written plain across a face built for hiding it.

"The gate's waking," Sable had said. "Something's answering prayers below the snowline, and it isn't the gentle kind."

And Maro had looked at his student—his best student, the child he'd pulled off a frozen hillside and raised into the finest guide the passes had ever known—and lied to them with a straight face, because that was what you did for people you loved. You stood between them and the truth until your body gave out.

"It's nothing," he'd said. "Old stones settling. Get some sleep."

Then he'd gone home and opened the ledger and read the names again, and this time he'd read them as what they were: a menu.

* * *

The threshold was a crack in the mountain that no map admitted to, three hours' climb below the waystation, behind a curtain of ice that only parted for people the mountain liked. Maro had found it forty years ago, back when finding it meant nothing—it had been a cold draft and a bad feeling, and he'd marked it on no map and told no one, because a bad feeling was just a bad feeling.

Tonight the ice curtain stood open like a door held by a servant.

"Well," Maro said. "Aren't you hospitable."

The passage beyond breathed warmth. That was the worst part, he decided—the warmth. Every story he'd ever heard about the thing beneath Thornfell involved cold, hunger, teeth in the dark. But the air rising out of the crack smelled like woodsmoke and baking bread, like his mother's kitchen in Hollowmere before the soldiers came, and Maro Quill, sixty-one years old and nobody's fool, stood at the mouth of hell and thought: *oh, you clever bastard. You've been reading me.*

"Course you have," he said. "You're wearing a king. Kings are just men who've learned to read rooms."

He stepped inside.

* * *

The descent road was worse than he remembered, and he remembered it perfectly, because he'd walked it once before, two months ago, the night he'd first brought an offering. Then, the walls had been stone. Now they were something between stone and skin, and they pulsed faintly when he brushed them, and he was almost certain—almost—that they leaned toward him as he passed, the way wheat leans toward the sun.

He kept talking. It helped. Talking was how he thought, and besides, if the thing wanted to eat him, let it digest a running commentary.

"I want you to understand something," he said, boots crunching on ground that crunched back. "I'm not here because I'm wicked. I've met wicked. Wicked burns chapels. I'm here because I'm *tired*, and because somebody has to make the deal that keeps my people breathing, and everyone else who could make it is either dead, defrocked, or too busy loving Sable Sandoval to see straight."

The word *Sable* echoed strangely, and Maro stopped walking.

"That's interest," he said slowly. "You know them. You've been listening to them." He laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Of course you have. They're the keeper's blood. You've probably got a whole shelf of Sandovals down here."

The warmth deepened. Somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the turning sounded almost like a voice clearing its throat.

"Yes, all right," Maro said. "Let's talk business."

* * *

He laid the ledger on a flat stone that presented itself with the eagerness of an innkeeper laying out a table for a paying guest. He unwrapped the oilcloth. The blood-ink names glowed faintly in the dark, row upon row, and Maro ran his thumb down the margin column where the prices were entered, and did the arithmetic one last time, the way he did every night before sleep.

Forty-one names. Forty-one souls, prepaid, offered up over two years in small installments so the gate would stay quiet while the rite ripened. A miller from Lowbridge whose debts were drowning him. A widow who'd asked, weeping, for her son to come home from the capital alive—and who had signed her neighbor's name when the god explained the exchange rate. A smuggler named Doss who'd sold out three rivals and called it business. Ordinary people. Desperate people. People the kingdom had failed so thoroughly that eternity seemed like fair payment for a winter's mercy.

Maro had collected those signatures himself. Door to door. Patient as a tax man.

"They're not sacrifices," he said to the dark, and heard how it sounded, and pressed on anyway, because a man halfway down a devil's throat might as well finish the sentence. "They're investments. Every one of them volunteered. Ask them—go on, ask them, you've got them, you can ask them—they'll tell you what the kingdom gave them for their trouble. Nothing. Less than nothing. I gave them a choice, and a choice is more charity than Aldric's court has managed in three hundred years."

The dark considered this. Or pretended to. With gods, Maro suspected, pretending was most of the job.

"And here's what I want in return." He straightened his back. Sixty-one years old, bad knee, good ledger. "When the rite completes—at midwinter, when the crown touches the relic—you take the king, whole, permanent, the merge Isolde Vane's lot used to preach about. Fine. The kingdom gets its radiant god-king, the churches fill up, everybody prays and everybody sleeps. But the mountains stay mine. The free roads, the passes, the valleys. No tithe collectors. No conscription carts. No soldiers burning chapels over short congregations. My people keep their own law, their own roads, their own names." He tapped the ledger. "You get your kingdom of kneeling souls. I get the ones who'd rather walk."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It pressed on Maro like a hand on the shoulder—no, like a hand on the head, the way a father weighs a son, the way a butcher weighs a cut.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like a hymn played backward through a landslide, the god spoke.

**YOU WOULD BE KING OF THE UNRULED.**

"I'd be a toll keeper with delusions," Maro said. "Don't flatter me. Flattery's for the ones you're eating."

**THE KINGDOM ABOVE IS ALREADY MINE. IT ONLY DOES NOT KNOW YET. WHY SHOULD THE MOUNTAINS COST ME EXTRA?**

"Because I'm asking," Maro said, "and because you need me to ask. That ledger doesn't walk itself to your altar. Those names don't finish themselves. There are nine entries still open, and I'm the only hand in these mountains that knows how to close them." He paused, and delivered the part he'd rehearsed for six nights on the trail, the part that tasted like ash and true coin both. "And because Sable Sandoval is coming down here to stop you, and you'll want them handled gently. And there is exactly one person alive that Sable trusts enough to listen to."

The warmth went very still.

**YOU WOULD BETRAY THE CHILD YOU RAISED.**

"I'd *save* the child I raised," Maro snapped, and his voice cracked on it, and he hated that, hated it with his whole chest. "You think I haven't run this conversation a thousand times? Sable walks into that prison with a defrocked priestess and a lovesick captain and a head full of their grandmother's journal, and they find out what our family really did—what we've all been feeding down here since before there were kings—and it breaks them. Or they win, and the binding snaps, and you come up the mountain hungry with no contract and no manners, and everything I built burns. Either way they break." He jabbed a finger at the glowing ledger. "This way, there's a shape to it. A border. A deal. Sable lives, the network lives, the valleys live. I'm not betraying them. I'm building the wall they're too young and too decent to build for themselves."

**AND IF THEY REFUSE THE WALL?**

Maro was quiet a moment. The passage dripped somewhere, patiently, like a clock.

"Then they refuse," he said finally. "And I'll grieve them proper, with drink and bad singing, the way we do. But I'll have tried everything else first. That's all any of us can do down here—all right, yes, I hear it, that's a lie, plenty of us could do better, starting with not making deals with you." He rubbed his face with both hands, rough palms, old scars. "Gods below. I sound like a priest."

**YOU SOUND LIKE A FATHER,** said the god, and the gentleness in it was the cruelest thing Maro had heard in sixty-one years. **THAT IS WHY YOU CAME. NOT FOR THE ROADS.**

"Don't," Maro said.

**THE MILLER'S SOUL WAS COIN. THE WIDOW'S WAS DESPERATION. BUT YOU HAVE COME DOWN MY THROAT IN THE DEAD OF WINTER TO BUY A FUTURE FOR ONE ANGRY ORPHAN WITH THEIR GRANDMOTHER'S EYES, AND YOU DRESS IT UP AS TARIFF POLICY.**

"I said *don't*."

**IT IS THE ONLY BARGAIN I HAVE EVER BEEN OFFERED THAT COST THE OFFERER ANYTHING.** The warmth gathered, concentrated, became almost a presence at his elbow, almost a person. **I ACCEPT YOUR TERMS. THE MOUNTAINS FOR THE LEDGER. AND WHEN THE KEEPER COMES—I WILL LET YOU SPEAK FIRST.**

"There," Maro managed. "See? Reasonable. That's all this ever was. Two reasonable parties negotiating."

**OF COURSE,** said the god, and Maro could have sworn it smiled. **NOW. SHALL WE DISCUSS THE NINE OPEN ENTRIES? I HAVE THOUGHTS ABOUT THE ORDER.**

* * *

They talked until the small hours, god and smuggler, terms and counterterms, and it was—Maro would admit this to no one, ever—the best conversation he'd had in years. The thing beneath the mountain understood logistics. It understood leverage, and timing, and the difference between a promise and a guarantee. If it had been born anywhere but under a prison, Maro thought wildly, it could have run the finest network in the known world.

Near dawn, climbing back up toward the waystation with the ledger lighter by nine closed entries and his heart lighter by nothing at all, Maro paused at the ice curtain and looked back down into the warm dark.

"One condition I forgot," he called. "When Sable comes—and they're coming, they're already packing, I know that walk of theirs—you don't wear anyone they love. No grandmother. No Renn. No faces from the box under their bed. Deal?"

The dark was silent long enough that Maro's stomach dropped.

**DEAL,** it said at last, warmly, agreeably, in a voice that reminded Maro—with a lurch—of his own.

He climbed out into the blue snow-light and stood a moment getting his breath, and that was when he saw them: tracks below him on the descent road, three sets, fresh, side by side, heading down.

One set narrow and light. One set heavy, a sailor's rolling stride. One set with the deliberate, evenly spaced tread of someone who had once prayed for a living.

Maro stood very still in the cold, an old man with a warm ledger and a fresh lie, watching the three people he loved most in the world walk straight into the bargain he'd just made without them.

"Well," he said to the mountain. "That's torn it."

And he started down after them, as fast as his bad knee allowed.
