## 12. Voices Under the Ice

The descent road ended at a door that was not a door.

It was more honest to call it a mouth, because that was what it had always been—Sable understood that now, standing before it with Isolde's lantern shaking in her own hand and Renn's breath fogging behind her like a small, nervous weather system. The gate stones above were only teeth. This, below, where the mountain's throat opened into blue-black ice, was where the swallowing happened.

"You're doing the thing," Renn said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at something horrible and make jokes about it so nobody notices your hands are shaking."

"My hands aren't shaking."

"They're shaking."

"They're *vibrating*. It's different. It implies control." Sable pressed her palm flat against the ice, and it was warm. That was the worst part, she thought later. Not the cold, not the dark, not the whispers. The warmth. Things that are imprisoned should be cold. Prisons are cold. This was warm as a sleeping animal, warm as bread, warm as the kitchen of a house where someone loved you once.

Isolde set her lantern down and began unpacking her liturgy kit with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had defrocked herself and never once looked back. "Once we're inside, don't answer anything," she said. "Don't answer questions, don't answer voices, don't answer if something calls your name in your mother's voice. Especially not that last one."

"Why especially?"

"Because I've seen what happens when people do." She said it lightly, the way one says *I've seen what happens when people lick frozen fences*, and somehow that made it worse.

Renn put his hand on Sable's shoulder. He didn't say anything clever for once, which from Renn was practically a eulogy. Then they went in.

* * *

The prison was not what the hymns described.

The hymns—the few that survived the suppression, the ones Isolde sang under her breath like a woman humming over a grave—described chains of iron and a pit of darkness and a god bound in sorrow. What Sable found instead was a hall of ice, and in the ice, faces.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Frozen mid-expression: some screaming, some singing, some simply talking, mouths open around words that would never finish. They weren't dead, exactly. They were *paused*, the way a song is paused, held a half-breath from its next note. And as Sable walked between them, the faces began to move. Slowly. Like things waking under water.

"Keeper," said a man with a farmer's sunburn. "Keeper, is it harvest yet? Keeper, my wife—"

"Don't answer," Isolde murmured.

"I wasn't going to answer."

"You were going to answer."

"I was *considering* it," Sable said, with dignity, and kept walking.

The voices layered then, the way choir voices layer, except no choir had ever been built from this many kinds of grief. A child asking for supper. An old woman reciting names. A soldier counting his company and stopping, every time, at the same number, and starting again. Sable had spent her whole life listening—to passes, to weather, to the lies people told toll-keepers—and she knew listening the way fish know water. But this was listening as drowning. Every voice wanted something. Every voice was aimed at her like an arrow with her name fletched onto it.

And then, underneath all of them, familiar as a scar:

"*Mireille.*"

Her grandmother's voice. Her grandmother's name, spoken by her grandmother's voice, which should have been impossible, because Odile Sandoval had been eleven years in the ground behind the waystation, under a stone Sable herself had carved, badly, with a chisel and more grief than skill.

The face in the ice was older than Sable remembered. That was the trick of the dead—you remembered them at their last age forever, and this face had kept aging, or the ice had decided it should have. White hair. The same unimpressed eyebrows. The same expression Odile had worn whenever young Sable asked a question she considered obvious, which was most questions.

"Grandmother," Sable said.

"Don't," Isolde started.

"She's dead. Whatever this is, it isn't her. Which means it can't hurt worse than losing her did, and I already did that." Sable stepped close enough to see her own breath bloom on the ice between them. "Talk. You've been waiting eleven years to talk. I can tell. You died mid-lecture, I'd bet my ledger on it."

The face almost smiled. Almost. The ice held even smiles halfway.

"*You brought the priestess,*" Odile's voice said, from everywhere now, from the whole hall, from the walls. "*Good. Stubborn girl. You'll need stubbornness. Listen, because I only get to say this once per keeper, and the ones before you never listened long enough.*"

"Per keeper?"

"*Every Sandoval who walks down here hears the same speech. None of them believe it. Tell me, Mireille. What did we tell you we were?*"

"Jailers," Sable said. "Gatekeepers. We keep the door shut. We hold the lock. Grandmother, you made me memorize the binding verses before I could read—"

"*We told you we were jailers.*" The voice was gentle now, which was somehow the cruelest register it had found yet. "*Now tell me. When a jailer feeds the prisoner, what do we call the jailer?*"

The hall went quiet. Even the paused faces seemed to lean in.

Sable thought of the offerings. Of course she thought of the offerings—Maro's blood-ink ledger, the names prepaid at the gate, the little dishes of milk and honey her grandmother had taught her to leave at the waystation's foundation stone every solstice. *Tradition*, Odile had called it. *Respect for the old compact.* Sable had left those dishes for twenty years. She had taught two novices to leave them. She had once scolded Renn, gently, fondly, for drinking the offering-wine.

"No," she said.

"*Yes.*"

"We fed it to keep it asleep. That's containment. That's—"

"*Mireille.*" The ice fogged, cleared, fogged again, like breathing. "*The first Sandoval did not build this gate. There was nothing here to gate. The valley was empty. The god was not beneath the mountain. The god was nowhere, because the god was not yet. Do you understand me? It had to be invited. It had to be *made*. And we made it.*"

"That's not—" Sable's voice cracked and she hated it. "The hymns say—"

"*The hymns were written afterward. By us. To explain ourselves to ourselves.*" A pause, and in the pause Sable could hear the other voices seeping back in, the soldier still counting, the child still hungry. "*Aldric the First came to Thornfell with a starving army and a starving god—a thing his priests had scraped together out of every prayer the kingdom ever wasted, every wish thrown at a sky that never answered. It was huge and hollow and it was dying of hunger, and he needed somewhere to put it. And our ancestor—Ysolt Sandoval, first of the name—looked at that starving god and saw what any sensible smuggler sees when someone offers them a monster.*"

"A price," Sable whispered.

"*A price. Ysolt took the god into the mountain and made a bargain dressed up as a prison: feed it a little, and it stays quiet. Feed it just enough. A soul at midwinter. A prayer answered here and there, to keep the faithful believing, because belief is food too. And every generation the hunger grows, because that is what hungers do, and every generation the feeding gets harder to hide, and every generation we tell the children a cleaner story. Jailers. Gatekeepers. Heroes of the long watch.*" The eyebrows, impossibly, arched. "*I believed it too, at your age. I stood exactly where you're standing and I called my grandmother a liar. Then I went home and left the dish of milk on the stone, because the alternative was finding out what happens when we don't.*"

Sable became aware, distantly, that she was crying, and that Renn had moved up beside her without touching her, which was its own kind of tenderness—he knew her well enough to know that right now touch might break something that needed to stay whole until it finished breaking.

"So it's not a prison," she said. "It's a larder. We're not jailers. We're *farmers*."

"*Worse,*" said the ice. "*We're shopkeepers. We sold the kingdom's prayers to a thing we invented, and we kept the change.*"

"And Maro?" The name came out flat. "Was he the first to sell souls outright, or just the first to write it down?"

"*He learned it from a Sandoval journal. Yours. My mother's. The ledgers go back further than the smuggling does, child. The smuggling was always the cover story. The roads, the tolls, the network—all of it built so that someone trustworthy would always be near the gate. So the feeding would never lapse.*" A sound moved through the ice then, through all the faces at once, a ripple like wind through wheat, and Sable understood with a lurch that it was laughter—or the memory of laughter, worn smooth by centuries of holding. "*You wanted to be invisible your whole life. Congratulations. Invisibility was the family business before you were born.*"

* * *

Afterward—she couldn't have said how long after; time down here was a rumor—Sable sat on a shelf of blue ice with her head between her knees, breathing the way Isolde had taught her, four counts in, seven counts out, while the dead hummed their unfinished songs around her.

Renn crouched in front of her. He had a flask. He always had a flask. He offered it without comment, and she took it, and it was the good brandy, the one he saved for funerals, which told her exactly how he'd categorized this moment.

"So," he said. "Your family founded the problem."

"Invented the problem, marketed the problem, and ran the delivery service for the problem for nine generations. Yes."

"Huh." He scratched his jaw. "In fairness, the smuggling part was genuinely good work. Those passes are elegant."

"Renn."

"I'm just saying, separate the art from the artist."

She laughed. It came out wrong, wet and cracked, but it came out, and Renn nodded like a physician observing a successful treatment. Above them, faintly, Isolde's voice rose and fell as she chalked the forbidden liturgy onto the ice—preparing, always preparing, a woman who had clearly been getting ready for the end of the world since before the end of the world was scheduled.

"It doesn't change anything," Sable said finally. "That's the awful part. I keep waiting for it to change something, and it doesn't. The gate still has to be held. The rite still has to happen. Midwinter still comes whether my grandmother was a hero or a—"

"Shopkeeper," Renn supplied.

"—a *shopkeeper*. The work's the same. Only now I know why."

"Is that better or worse?"

Sable looked up at the faces in the ice—at the soldier, still counting; at the child, still hungry; at her grandmother, whose expression had settled back into that patient, terrible stillness, waiting for the next keeper, keeping the same speech warm for whoever came down the road after her.

"Worse," she said. "But truer. I'll take truer."

Then the ice spoke again—not Odile this time, but all of them at once, the whole drowned choir finding one note, and the note was a warning, and the warning was a name:

"*He is coming down. The one who wrote the names. He brings the ledger. He brings the last meal.*"

Sable was on her feet before the echo finished. Somewhere above them, on the threshold where the mountain's throat met the dark, Maro was arriving—with everything he'd taken, and everyone he'd sold, and a bargain he meant to close.

"Isolde!" she shouted, already running. "How long do we have?"

From deeper in the ice, calm as a market bell, her grandmother answered for her:

"*Less than you need, Mireille. Less than you need.*"
