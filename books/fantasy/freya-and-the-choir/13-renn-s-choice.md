## 13. Renn's Choice

The mountain had stopped pretending to be a mountain somewhere around the third hour of the descent.

Renn had smuggled through Thornfell for nineteen years. He knew its moods the way he knew the moods of his ship—no, his *barge*, he corrected himself, because Sable would correct him first, and there was something steadying about the argument even now, even here, a hundred fathoms under stone where barges and ships were equally useless. The mountain sulked in rain. It grumbled in thaw. It held grudges against particular switchbacks like an old salt against a particular tide.

This was none of that. This was the mountain *thinking*.

The passage breathed. Not wind—breath. Slow, warm, smelling faintly of candlewax and river water, and Renn hated that he recognized it: it was the smell of the *Kestrel's* hold the night she went down, the night eleven of his crew went into the Greywash and did not come out, and he'd sat on a floating crate breathing that same smell until dawn took him ashore to begin the long business of not drowning every day for the rest of his life.

"Renn." Sable's voice came from ahead, low, careful. They'd been walking point with a lantern since the last collapse, and their voice had that flattened quality they got when they were managing him. "How are your feet?"

"My feet are fine."

"Your feet have been dragging for half a mile."

"My feet are *contemplative*." He caught up to them at a bend where the passage widened into a chamber, and Isolde Vane stood at its center with her stolen liturgy open across both forearms like a shield she'd forgotten how to use. The priestess looked terrible in the way that people look when they've been praying hard for days—hollowed, bright-eyed, running on something that wasn't food. "Everyone's feet are contemplative. It's a contemplative mountain."

"It's a hungry mountain," Isolde said, without looking up from her book. "Keep moving. It likes it better when we stop. Stopping is how it gets a word in."

And that was when Renn heard the water.

Not dripping. Not seepage. *Waves.* The small slap-and-hiss of water against a hull, the creak of mooring line, and underneath it—God help him—the sound of men laughing. Low and easy, the way sailors laugh at the end of a watch, tired and pleased with themselves and each other.

He knew those laughs. He knew them the way you know a song you learned before you knew words.

"Renn." Sable's hand closed on his sleeve. Their grip was strong and their face was pale in the lantern light, and they said, very quietly, "Don't listen to it."

"I'm not listening."

"You're listening so hard your ears have gone white."

"That's not—" He stopped. Because down the side passage, past a lip of black rock, there was light. Lamplight, warm and gold, the exact amber of harbor lamps seen from the water at dusk, and rising out of it came a voice he had buried so deep he'd told himself for six years that he'd never known it at all.

*"Captain? That you up there? Captain Renn, we kept your berth warm—"*

Dess. Dess Okafor, who'd been his bosun back when Renn still commanded anything bigger than a ledger of debts. Who'd gone over the rail laughing at a joke Renn couldn't remember anymore, which was the worst part, which was the part that ate him alive on quiet nights—that the last thing Dess ever heard from this world might have been Renn's own laughter, right before the wave took the rail out from under all of them.

"Dess is dead," Renn said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "I stood at the water's edge for three days. I know what the Greywash gives back, and it isn't people."

"No," Isolde agreed grimly. "It isn't. And neither is this."

But his feet were already moving. Contemplative feet. Traitor feet.

* * *

The chamber beyond the rock lip should not have existed. Renn understood this the way you understand things in dreams—with total clarity and no consequences. There was no room down here big enough for a harbor. There was no water down here at all; they'd crossed dry stone for hours. And yet the little dock stretched out ahead of him over black, gently moving water, lamplit, moored with the *Kestrel* riding at her lines as pretty as the day she was launched, and along her rail stood eleven men and women in oilskins, waving.

Waving. Like he'd only been gone to market.

"There you are!" Dess called, and her grin split her dark face the way it always had, wide and crooked-toothed and absolutely certain the world was a joke she was in on. "We said he'd come. Didn't we say he'd come? Six years, Captain, and you didn't age a day. Well. You aged. You got the grey coming in at the temples, looks distinguished, don't let anyone tell you different—"

"Dess." His voice cracked on the second syllable and he hated himself for it, cleanly and completely. "You're dead."

"Well, obviously." She rolled her eyes. "What do you think this is, a reunion? We're dead, Captain. All eleven of us. Cold and drowned and done. But—" and here she leaned on the rail, and her voice dropped into the register she used for explaining rigging to greenhands, patient and kind, which was somehow the cruelest thing yet, "—the one under the mountain says dead doesn't have to be finished. Says it can pour us back. Whole. Not ghosts, not echoes. Us. Warm hands. Bad jokes. The lot."

Behind him, at the mouth of the chamber, he heard Sable say his name. Just his name. Once.

He didn't turn around. That was the coward's move and he knew it, but if he turned around he'd see their face, and if he saw their face he'd remember what they'd told him in the vault-light two nights ago—*everything it offers is a hook, Renn, everything, there is nothing down here that isn't bait*—and God forgive him, he wanted the hook. He wanted it with a wanting that felt less like desire than like gravity.

"What does it cost?" he asked. His captain's voice. The one for negotiating tolls with men who carried knives.

Dess's grin flickered. Just at the corner. Just for a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat her face was wrong—not wrong-eyed like the risen patient Isolde had described, nothing so obvious, just... smoothed. Simplified. A portrait of Dess instead of Dess.

"Cost?" she said lightly. "Who talks about cost at a homecoming?"

"Every smuggler on the Low Road talks about cost at a homecoming," Renn said. "It's the first lesson. Nothing comes over the pass free. So I'll ask again, and I'll ask polite, because you taught me polite: what does it cost?"

Silence ran along the rail. Eleven faces, and one by one, going down the line, they stopped being quite themselves—the way a crowd stops being individuals when someone important enters a square. The lamplight bent toward the far end of the dock, where the darkness under the boathouse roof gathered itself into something with attention.

Then it spoke, and it didn't use Dess's voice. It used his mother's.

*She died in his fourteenth year, fevered, and her last coherent sentence had been a scold about wet boots.* It knew that. Of course it knew that; it had been listening under the world since before boots.

***"Nothing,"*** it said, warm as banked coals. ***"I ask nothing. A man should have back what the river stole. I am generous, Renn of the Low Road. Ask your friend the keeper what my generosity costs the ones who refuse it."***

"There it is," Renn said quietly. "There's the toll."

He made himselook at Dess. Really look. At the oilskin beaded with spray that never fell. At the lantern flame behind her that burned without consuming. At the way the water beneath the dock moved without ever once lapping the pilings.

"You're not her," he said. "And worse—you're not even a good copy. Dess Okafor never once in nine years let me get away with a question unanswered. She'd have told me the price before I finished asking. Whoever's wearing her tonight is cheaper goods."

The thing under the boathouse went very still, and the whole harbor went still with it, and in the stillness Renn heard—faintly, from somewhere below the water, below the stone, below everything—a sound like a great many voices inhaling at once.

***"Refuse, then,"*** it said, and the warmth was gone, and what remained was vast and patient and old as the mountain's anger. ***"They will wait. They are very good at waiting. And you will grow old on the Low Road, Renn, carrying other men's cargo past this door, and every year the door will be warmer, and every year you will be more alone, and one winter—I am very patient—one winter you will knock."***

"Yeah," Renn said. His eyes were burning and his throat was a knot and he walked forward anyway, right up to the dock's edge, close enough to see that Dess's reflection in the black water had no face at all. "Maybe. But not tonight, and not for a copy, and not for you. Dess deserved better than to come back as somebody's lure. So do me a favor."

He reached out and put his palm flat against the nearest piling, the way you'd touch a hull in farewell.

"Let her go. Let all of them go. If you've got any of the real ones left in there anywhere—any scrap, any echo—let them rest. That's not a bargain. That's not a trade. That's just the decent thing, and I notice decency's the one cargo you never carry."

For a moment—just a moment—he thought the harbor wavered. Thought the lamplight guttered toward something almost like grief. Then the whole scene folded up like a sail in a squall: dock, water, lamp, rail, eleven waving ghosts, gone, and he was standing in a cold stone chamber with his hand pressed flat against bare rock, and Sable's hand on his shoulder, and Isolde saying something sharp and ritual-flavored behind him that he'd later learn had probably saved his mind.

* * *

They made camp in a side gallery where the breathing of the mountain was fainter, and Isolde ward-marked the entrance with chalk and muttered scripture until the walls stopped listening quite so attentively. Then she posted herself at the passage mouth with her book and her stubbornness, and left the two of them sitting by a fire that gave more light than heat, because the mountain rationed heat like a miser.

Renn stared into the flames. Sable sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a long time neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of language between them. They'd built that language over years: the silences that meant *I'm here*, the silences that meant *take your time*, the silence that meant *I know you're composing a lie and I'll wait until you decide whether to tell me the truth instead.*

That last one was the current silence.

"I nearly said yes," Renn said finally.

"I know."

"You don't know. You weren't inside it. Sable, I *ached*. My teeth ached. It had Dess's laugh exactly right. Everything else was cheap, but the laugh was perfect, and I think—" He rubbed his face with both hands. "I think the laugh was real. I think it kept a scrap of her, the way you keep a coin from a dead man's pocket, and it spent it on me. And it worked. For about ten heartbeats, it worked, and in those ten heartbeats I would have traded every soul on the Low Road to sit on that dock and hear it again."

Sable was quiet a moment. When they spoke, their voice was rough. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you lied to me for weeks about the token." He said it gently, watching their profile. "Because you carried that humming bone alone and let me think the changed routes were about toll wars, and it nearly broke us, and we swore—after Maro, after the waystation—we swore no more of that. So here's mine. Full weight, no ballast. I refused the thing wearing my dead bosun's face by about the width of a knife-edge, and I need you to know that, because if you find out some other way—if it shows you, the way it showed me hers—you'll wonder ever after whether I'd have sold you out for a ghost crew. And I'd rather you hear it from me, ugly and true, than find it pretty and false."

Sable turned and looked at him. Lantern-light and firelight and that third light the deep places have, the one with no source. Their eyes were wet and furious and fond, all at once, which was a very Sable combination.

"You absolute idiot," they said thickly. "You brave, honest, self-flagellating idiot. You refused a god."

"Barely."

"*Barely counts.* Do you understand what barely means down here? Barely is the whole game. Isolde barely survived her infirmary. I barely survived being born a Sandoval. Nobody walks out of a place like this clean, Renn. You walk out *leaning*, and the trick is finding out who you lean on before the ground tilts." They took his hand. Their grip was cold and fierce. "You told me the worst thing about yourself while it was still fresh. Do you know how rare that is? Maro couldn't do it. My grandmother couldn't do it. Half the kingdom above us is built on people who can't do it. And you sat in the dirt and handed me your worst ten heartbeats like it was cargo you were declaring at the border."

"Honest declaration," Renn managed. "No contraband. That's the rule."

"That's the rule." Sable's mouth twitched—the ghost of the smile that had gotten them through a lifetime of invisible roads. "Besides. For what it's worth, I don't think you'd have traded us. Ten heartbeats of wanting isn't the same as a yes. I wanted my grandmother's journal to say we were heroes. I read the truth and I'm still here."

"Aye, but you're *stubborn*."

"I'm a Sandoval. It's hereditary."

He laughed—cracked, wet, real—and pulled them in, and they sat like that with the miser-fire burning low, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the harbor unclenched by one hard notch. Not all the way. Maybe not all the way ever again. But harder ground held better foundations; his father had said that about harbors, and his father had been right about harbors and wrong about most everything else.

"Renn." Sable's voice, muffled against his shoulder, had gone serious again. "When it offered you them—it said something. About my generosity costing the ones who refuse it. Did you mark that?"

"I marked it."

"It knows we're coming. It's not trying to stop us anymore. It's *pricing* us." They pulled back, and their face in the firelight had the set Renn had learned to fear and follow in equal measure: the keeper's face, the jailer's face, the face of someone doing arithmetic with lives. "Which means somewhere between here and the prison floor, it's already decided what we're worth. And there's one person it priced higher than any of us. One person who's been feeding it for months and thinks the bill's already paid."

Renn felt the cold come up through the stone before he understood the words.

"Maro."

"He's ahead of us," Sable said, standing, snuffing the fire with a twist of their wrist like snuffing a thought. "And if the ledger of names reaches the threshold before we do, every soul in it goes into the crown at midwinter—including, Renn, the souls of everyone who ever signed our toll-book. Everyone who ever trusted us."

Isolde was already on her feet at the passage mouth, chalk in hand, liturgy open, looking back at them with the expression of a woman who had heard everything and forgiven none of it and would walk into hell beside them regardless.

"Then we'd best stop being contemplative," Renn said, and shouldered his pack, and followed the keeper down.
