## 14. The Kingdom Above

### The Pilgrim

Marta Fenwick had walked eleven days from the river country to see the king, and her feet were of the firm opinion that piety was overrated as a form of transportation.

She was fifty-one years old, a widow, a baker by trade, and until this winter she had not prayed to anything more ambitious than the yeast. But the stories had reached even the river country: the king in his white-and-gold, walking the capital streets unguarded, touching the sick, and the sick *rising*. Grain that sprouted in the storehouses overnight. A well in the Tanner's Quarter that had gone sweet after three years of bitterness. And the king's new priests, robed in the old colors of a suppressed order, saying that the old gods were not dead at all — that they had merely been waiting for a king worthy of them.

So Marta had closed the bakery, kissed her sister's cheek, and walked.

The capital was *bright*. That was the word she kept reaching for and failing to improve upon. She had expected a winter city: gray skies, gray stone, gray faces. Instead the streets glowed as though lit from beneath the cobbles, and every window box held roses, and the beggars — she looked for them, because a baker notices who isn't eating — the beggars were gone. Fed, the priests said. The king's charity had fed them all.

"Everyone's fed," said a young woman selling candied chestnuts near the temple gate, and her smile was wide and white and did not move the rest of her face at all. "Everyone's *happy*, mother. Aren't you happy?"

"Delirious," Marta said, and bought chestnuts she did not want, because the woman's hands were trembling.

That night she lodged in a pilgrim-house that smelled of beeswax and something faintly metallic underneath, like a coin held too long in the mouth. She knelt at the little shrine in her room, because it seemed polite, and said the prayer the chestnut-seller had taught her. *Blessed king, beloved king, hear your servant.*

She slept beautifully. Better than she had in years. Dreamless, deep, like a stone dropped down a well.

She woke at dawn feeling rested and could not, for the life of her, remember her sister's face.

She told herself it was the walking. She bought a candied apple. She joined the queue for the king's blessing, which wound through four streets and smelled, faintly, of roses and iron, and she told herself that too.

### The Soldier

Corporal Daven Hult had been in the king's army for nine years, and in nine years he had learned exactly three things: keep your boots dry, never volunteer, and when an officer says *voluntary*, run.

So when the summons came — *voluntary pilgrims sought to witness the Renewal at midwinter, all expenses paid, honor of the realm* — Daven volunteered with the enthusiasm of a man stepping in front of a moving cart, and then spent the march to the capital trying to work out what he'd done wrong.

The thing was, the army was *fine*. Better than fine. The pay had doubled. The food was unbelievable — fresh meat twice a week, bread that didn't require a chisel. The officers had stopped shouting. Captain Vess, who once made a recruit cry over a crooked bedroll, now went about with a serene smile and told them all that they were *loved*, that the kingdom *loved* them, that the king *loved* them, and Daven would have preferred the shouting, honestly, because at least shouting was a human noise.

It was the desertions that worried him. Or rather, the non-desertions.

Because men *were* leaving — you'd wake and a bunk would be empty, kit gone, and nobody said a word. And then, a week later, the man would be *back*. Standing at attention. Smiling that wide white smile. And when you asked where he'd gone — and Daven had asked, three times, because he was a fool — the man would say, warmly, "Home. I went home. But I'm home *now*, aren't I?" and clap you on the shoulder, and his hand would be cold through your shirt like a fish.

The third man had been Tam Osk, who'd shared Daven's fire for six years and owed him four silver and an apology regarding a woman in Marrowford. Tam came back smiling and never once mentioned the debt, and Daven decided that was the most alarming thing he had ever witnessed. A man who forgets a debt is not a man.

"You're quiet, Corporal," said Captain Vess, appearing at his elbow with that smile.

"Thinking about the honor of the realm, sir."

"Wonderful." Vess gazed out over the training yard, where the roses in the window boxes bloomed against the frost like something rehearsed. "You'll be honored especially, at midwinter. Front ranks. The king will look at you."

Daven felt the words go through him like a spear of ice. "Look at me, sir?"

"Everyone the king looks at is *improved*," said Vess, and walked away humming.

Daven stood in the yard a long while. Then he went to the quartermaster, drew his winter kit, and requested assignment to the Thornfell border patrol, which everyone knew was the worst posting in the army — frozen passes, smugglers, nothing to eat but goat.

The quartermaster smiled his wide white smile and stamped the papers without a single question, and Daven told himself the relief he felt was just the usual cowardice doing its usual work.

### The Pilgrim, Again

Marta Fenwick had been in the capital nine days when she began keeping a list, on the back of a receipt, of things that were wrong.

Item one: the roses. She'd asked a gardener — a real one, with dirt under his nails and a face like a wet Tuesday — how they kept them blooming in frost, and he'd looked at her with naked terror and said, "They just do," and walked away at speed.

Item two: the bells. The temple bells rang every hour, and every hour they rang the same phrase, over and over, and it took her six days to realize it wasn't a melody at all but words, slowed down and stretched until they were only a feeling: *beloved king, hear your servants, beloved king, hear your servants.* She'd asked a priest about it and he'd beamed. "You can *hear* them? The prayers? Oh, blessed one — the king hears *everything* now."

Item three: the missing. The pilgrim-house keeper had a nephew, a printer, who'd written a pamphlet asking — politely, Marta read it before it was confiscated, it was *very* polite — whether perhaps the grain miracle might be verified by an independent accounting. The nephew had not been seen in eleven days. The keeper had stopped asking after him on day four. On day five the keeper was smiling the wide white smile and telling Marta how *happy* everyone was, and Marta had looked at the woman's hands and seen they were still trembling, and understood: whatever was wearing these people was wearing them *badly*, like a coat cut for someone else.

Item four — and her hand had shaken writing this one: she could not remember her sister's face, but she could remember the prayer *perfectly*. Every word. It sat in her head like a hook set in a fish's jaw.

On the ninth night she did not say the prayer. She lay in the dark and said her sister's name instead, over and over, like a counterspell, until she could almost see the crooked tooth and the flour always in the eyebrow.

In the morning there was a letter under her door. No name. The handwriting was small and urgent and smelled faintly of woodsmoke and something older, incense maybe, or ash.

*If you have begun to notice, you are not yet lost. There are others who notice. Go north. Go to Thornfell. Ask at the waystations for the keeper of the low road. Do not pray on the road. Do not sing. And whatever the mountain says to you — however kindly it says it — do not answer.*

Marta read it four times. Then she packed her bag, ate her last candied apple, and walked out the temple gate past the queue of pilgrims, all of them murmuring the prayer, all of them sleeping beautifully, and she did not look back, because she was fairly sure that if she did, she'd want to warn them, and she was fairly sure they'd smile at her with the wrong part of their face.

### The Soldier, Again

The Thornfell border patrol was, as advertised, miserable — and Corporal Daven Hult had never in his life been so grateful to be miserable.

The passes were white silence. The fort was a drafty stone fist with a garrison of twelve and a cook who considered garlic a personal enemy. Nothing glowed. Nothing bloomed. The men swore and complained and cheated at cards, and Daven drank it in like a man who'd been drinking perfume for a month and finally got water.

It was Sergeant Ila Rook who noticed him watching the mountains.

"You volunteered for this," she said one evening, on the wall, breath steaming. "Nobody volunteers for Thornfell. So either you're running from something, or you're watching for something."

"Can't a man just hate warmth?"

"He can," Ila said. "But you flinched at the bells in the capital. I saw it. Everyone flinched; most of them smiled while they did it. You just flinched." She handed him a flask of something that tasted like a barn fire and was therefore wonderful. "I've been on this border eleven years. The mountain folk trade with smugglers, the smugglers keep the passes open, and the crown pretends none of it exists, and that arrangement has kept the peace better than any treaty. Now the crown's sending priests up here. Old-order robes. Asking about *gateways*."

Daven went cold in a way the wind had nothing to do with. "What kind of gateways?"

"Stone ones." Ila looked north, where the peaks went black against the stars. "My grandmother used to say there was a door in Thornfell that was locked from our side, and we should be *grateful* to whoever held the key, and if we ever found out who they were, we should bring them bread and never bring them soldiers." She took the flask back. "Grandmothers say a lot of things. But last week a priest asked me to report any locals who 'keep the old tolls.' And I said I would." She smiled — a real one, crooked and tired. "I lied. First lie I've told the crown in eleven years. Felt wonderful. You want to know why?"

"Why, Sergeant?"

"Because the priest smiled at me," Ila said, "and it was the same smile they all have down south, and I have decided that I would rather freeze on this wall for the rest of my life than be smiled at like that again."

Below them, far off in the dark, a waystation light burned steady — one of the low road's little fires, the ones that weren't supposed to officially exist. Ila nodded toward it.

"Whoever keeps that light," she said, "I'd wager my pension they're about to have a very bad midwinter."

### The Priest

In the capital, in the temple that had been sealed for a generation and now shone like a lamp, the priest Orin Vale practiced his smile in a silver mirror, and it was perfect, and it was not his.

He knew that. That was the horror of it, the part he kept folded away in the last room of himself that still had a lock on the door: he knew, and the knowing changed nothing. When the thing that wore the king had come to him — had come *gently*, had come with his dead mother's voice and the smell of the seminary kitchen — he had said yes in under a minute, and he could not now remember why, only that it had seemed like the most reasonable thing he had ever done.

He was, he understood, a mouthpiece. Literally. The old order was being resurrected, rite by rite, from Isolde Vane's stolen books, and Orin was one of its new voices, and the liturgy had been *adjusted*. The old prayers had asked. The new ones *took*. Every pilgrim who murmured *beloved king, hear your servant* opened a little door in themselves, and the king reached through, and took a little — a memory here, a name there, a face of a sister, the taste of a debt — and gave back sleep, and sweetness, and roses.

It was sustainable, the thing wearing the king had explained, the way a farmer's field is sustainable. You did not kill the field. You *husbanded* it.

But there was a flaw in the field, and the flaw was north, and it was named Thornfell.

"There is a gate we did not build," the king had said, in the voice of a patient teacher, "and a blood that holds it, and a keeper who has not prayed. Unprayed-for things are *unclaimed* things, Orin. And unclaimed things attract thieves."

The ledger of names was nearly complete. The offerings at the far gate had been counted and found *good* — someone below the snowline had been faithful, generous, wise. All that remained was the Renewal: crown to relic, at midwinter, and the last soft border between king and god would dissolve like sugar in warm water.

"Midwinter," Orin said to the mirror, and the mirror said it back in his voice, and behind his voice, very faintly, something else said it too, in a register that made the silver ripple.

He smiled. It was perfect.

And in the last locked room of himself, the small remaining Orin — the one who still remembered his mother's actual voice, not the thing's imitation of it — pressed his hands against the door and whispered the only prayer left to him, the one the new liturgy had not thought to forbid, because it asked for nothing and gave nothing and was therefore worthless to a thief:

*Let someone come. Let someone come before midwinter. Let someone come.*

Far to the north, in a waystation under the snow, a prayer-scarred token hummed against the gate stones, and a keeper with their grandmother's journal open on their knees looked up sharply, as though hearing their name called from a very long way off.
