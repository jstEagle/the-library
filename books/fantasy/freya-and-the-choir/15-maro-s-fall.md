## 15. Maro's Fall

The descent road had a smell to it that Sable had never been able to name — not sulfur, not rot, but something like the inside of an old church left closed for winter: stone and dust and the ghost of incense. They had walked it a hundred times as a child, holding Maro's lantern, learning which passages breathed and which ones held their breath. Now they walked it alone, or nearly. Isolde was three turns back, moving slower than she'd admit, muttering liturgy under her breath the way other people muttered curses. Renn was behind her, carrying rope, oil, and the particular stubborn silence of a man who had decided not to say *I told you this would end badly* because he knew it wouldn't help.

Sable stopped at the third turning, where the passage forked around a pillar of blue ice older than any kingdom, and listened.

Below them — far below, down where the smugglers' maps simply said *do not* in Maro's handwriting — there was light.

Not torchlight. Torchlight flickered. This glowed the way the token glowed, steady and patient, the way a mouth glows when it's about to speak.

"He's already there," Sable said quietly, though no one needed telling.

Isolde caught up, breath fogging, and pressed her palm flat against the ice pillar. Her fingers were cracked from cold and she didn't seem to notice. "Then we've run out of clever options," she said. "Which is my favorite kind of situation, personally. All my best work has been done with no options at all."

"You're not coming down."

"I'm sorry, I must have misheard you. It sounded like you said something foolish."

"Isolde." Sable turned to face her. In the glow from below, the priestess looked older than her years and younger than her sins, both at once. "If Maro finishes the final offering before I stop him, the rite completes early. The king merges. Everything ends at midwinter instead of beginning there. Someone has to hold the upper passage — if the gate opens while we're all standing in its throat, we're not rescuers, we're the first course."

Isolde studied them for a long moment. Then she did something Sable hadn't seen her do before: she bowed. Not deeply, not courtly — just a small inclination of the head, the kind one keeper gives another.

"Hold the mouth open," she said. "Don't let it close on you. And Sable — whatever he offers you, whatever he shows you, remember that a man who has fed souls to a god for ten years is very good at sounding reasonable. That's how he sleeps at night. Don't take his sleep away from him by believing him."

"And if he won't be stopped?"

Isolde's mouth went thin. "Then you do what keepers do," she said. "You bind. But hear me, Sandoval — binding isn't killing. If there's anything of your teacher still in there when it's done, you'll want to have left him something to come back to. Mercy is a weapon. Use it like one."

* * *

Renn caught up just as Sable started down. He didn't ask to come. He simply fell into step beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched on the narrow shelf of rock, and after a hundred paces he said, "I'm not staying up there either, so don't waste breath."

"The passage—"

"Isolde can hold a passage. She held off six of Vask's men at the border ford with a prayer and a soup ladle; I heard the story twice." He glanced sideways. "You're going to face the man who raised you in the belly of a god's prison. You're not doing that alone. That's not loyalty, that's arithmetic."

Sable wanted to argue. They found, examining themselves honestly, that they mostly wanted to hold his hand. So they did that instead, and they descended together into the light.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was not a chamber. It was a throat — a vast oval hollow of black stone ribbed like the inside of a shell, and every rib was carved with names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Some were ancient, worn to shadows. Some were fresh, cut so recently the stone dust still glittered in the grooves.

And in the center, kneeling at a low altar of ice, his back to them, was Maro.

He looked smaller than Sable remembered. Ten days ago he had filled a doorway; now, in the god-light, he seemed folded in on himself, an old man in a good coat, writing carefully in a ledger with a pen made of something pale. Beside him lay the book of names — the real one, the one he'd escaped with, its pages swollen and dark.

He didn't turn around. "You walk loud for a smuggler," he said. "Always did. Your grandmother used to say the mountain would know you were coming a week out, just from your footsteps."

"She said that about you too."

Maro laughed — a real laugh, brief and warm, and it hurt worse than anything the god could have shown them. "She did. Sit down, little wren. Or stand. You'll want to be standing for this part."

"What are you doing, Maro?"

"Finishing." He dipped the pen. "There are two hundred and eleven names left. Two hundred and ten now." He wrote. "They're volunteers, before you make that face. Every one of them came to me. People the kingdom broke — miners buried by the crown's negligence, families taxed into nothing, girls sold north. They asked me for protection. I found them the only protection that's ever actually worked."

"It's not protection. It's purchase. You're prepaying souls—"

"I'm *investing* them." Now he turned, and Sable saw his eyes. They were wrong-eyed, like Isolde's risen patient — but only slightly wrong, only at the edges, like a portrait hung a half-degree crooked. "Look at the roads, Sable. Look at what we built. No tolls taken by bandits since the prayers started. No lost children in the passes. The kingdom never gave us a single thing except a price on our heads, and you want to lecture me about purchase? You've spent your whole life selling passage. I'm just selling it somewhere better."

"The last king thought that too," Sable said. "The one who got unmade."

Something flickered across Maro's face — doubt, maybe, or memory — and then smoothed away. "The last king was greedy. He tried to take without giving. I read the terms, wren. I always read the terms." He lifted the ledger. "Two hundred and nine."

Sable drew the token from their coat. It was humming hard enough now to feel through leather, and as they stepped forward, the carved names on the ribs began to glow faintly, one by one, like windows being lit in a dark house.

"Stop writing."

"No."

"*Maro.*"

"You want to know the funny thing?" He kept writing, calm as a man balancing accounts. "I taught you everything. Every pass, every blind turn, every trick of snow and shadow. And you think I don't know exactly how you fight? You fight fair, little wren. That was always your flaw. Fair men lose to prepared ones."

He snapped the ledger shut, and the chamber exhaled.

It wasn't wind. It was attention. The whole throat of the mountain turned its regard upon Sable and Renn, and the temperature dropped so fast their breath froze mid-air and fell as glitter. From the ribs, from between the names, voices began — soft, coaxing, layered like a choir heard through a wall.

*Sable Sandoval,* the mountain said, in a voice stitched together from everyone they had ever mourned. *Keeper. Jailor's child. We remember your grandmother. We remember her hands.*

"Don't listen," Renn said tightly. His hand was white-knuckled on his knife, and Sable could see the water rising behind his eyes — the drowned crew, the offer that had nearly broken him once already. "Whatever it says, it's using the same script on both of us."

"I know." Sable's voice came out steadier than they felt. "Maro. Last chance. Walk away. I'll burn the book either way, but you can walk away first."

"Burn it?" Maro smiled, and it was the smile that had reassured a thousand frightened clients on a thousand bad nights. "It's written in blood-ink, child. Blood doesn't burn. Ask any priest."

"Ask any *real* priest," Isolde's voice said dryly, from the passage above, where she had absolutely disobeyed instructions and was absolutely watching. "Blood burns fine. It's the *bargain* that doesn't. And bargains—" she came down the last steps, staff in hand, frost steaming off her shoulders, "—are contracts. And contracts require consideration. And consideration, gentlemen, requires *willing* souls."

She struck her staff against the floor. Light went up the ribs like fire up a fuse.

What happened next happened fast, and Sable would spend the rest of their life assembling it from pieces. The names flared. The voices shrieked — not in pain, Sable realized later, but in *relief*, the sound of two hundred people waking from a long dream all at once. Maro lunged for the ledger. Renn hit him like a landslide, and they went down together in a tangle of old love and older loyalty, and Sable snatched the book from the ice—

—and hesitated.

Because the book was warm. And through the cover, faintly, they could feel them: the named ones. Not gone. Not eaten. *Held.* Held the way a fist holds something, and a fist, opened, lets go.

"Mercy is a weapon," Sable whispered. "Use it like one."

They pulled the flint from their coat — smuggler's habit, always carried — and Isolde was suddenly beside them, tearing a page from her own prayer book, whispering the forbidden liturgy over the ledger's spine, unbinding the contract's seams. "Now," she said. "While the door's open. *Now.*"

Sable struck the spark.

The ledger caught like autumn. Not paper-fire — soul-fire, white and clean, and as it burned, the names rose off the pages in threads of light and poured upward through the throat of the mountain, past Sable, past Renn, past the shrieking ribs, up toward the thin cold air and the ordinary world. Two hundred and eight souls. Two hundred and seven. The last page curled. The last name — Sable never learned whose it was — lifted free like a bird off water.

And Maro screamed.

Not the god's scream. *His.* Because the bargain stripped away with the souls, and what was underneath it was just an old man who had spent ten years feeding people to a mouth, and the knowledge of it arrived in him all at once, undiluted, the way dawn arrives for someone who has been awake all night.

* * *

Afterward, they sat him against the altar of ice and gave him water from Renn's flask, and he drank it like a man remembering how.

The god-light was dying. Without the offerings, the chamber was only a chamber again — black stone, old names, cold. Somewhere above, muffled by miles of rock, the mountain had gone quiet in a new way. Not sleeping. *Waiting.*

"I meant it," Maro said at last. His voice was scraped hollow. "Every word. The roads are safer. The children don't starve. I'd do it again."

"I know," Sable said. "That's the worst part."

"You should kill me."

"Yes," Sable agreed. "Probably. By the law of about four kingdoms, certainly." They crouched to his level. "But the law never came up here, Maro. Not once, not for anyone. Justice on these roads was always ours to carry, and I'm carrying mine." They stood. "You stay."

Maro looked up. For the first time since Sable had known him, he looked his age entirely, every year of it, stacked and visible.

"Below?"

"There's a cell. Grandmother's journal mentions it — the old keepers' cells, past the second seal. Warm enough. Dry. The dead here don't lie anymore, now the names are free." Sable knelt and took his hands, and they were shaking. "You taught me the passes. You taught me to read snow and read men and read the terms. So read these terms, old man: you live. You stay where the thing you served can't reach anyone ever again. And every night, you listen at the gate with me — because someone who knows how a god negotiates is worth more to the living as a warden than as a corpse."

For a long moment Maro said nothing. Then he laughed — broken, wet, real. "Your grandmother would have liked that. Cruel and kind in the same sentence. It ran in the family, I suppose. Just skipped you until now."

"It didn't skip me. I was saving it."

He let them lead him to the second seal. At the threshold of the old keepers' cell he paused, and looked back at Sable with the crooked-wrongness gone from his eyes entirely, and said, "The rite. When you go down to do it — it will show you the gate opening. Freedom. It will look like mercy. It isn't. Whatever it shows you, wren — *read the terms.*"

Then he walked in, and Sable sealed the door with their grandmother's words, and did not cry until Renn put an arm around them and made crying possible.

* * *

They climbed back up through the quieting dark, Isolde leaning on her staff, Renn carrying Maro's abandoned lantern because it seemed wrong to leave it burning down there alone. At the third turning, by the pillar of blue ice, Sable stopped.

The token had changed. The hum was different now — deeper, faster, urgent. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them had heard before: singing. Far below. Many voices. The freed souls, perhaps, still rising. Or something else, something older, turning over in its sleep as the last anchors of its freedom burned away.

"Midwinter's in four days," Isolde said quietly. "And the king will feel this. Whatever Maro's bargain was feeding him, it just stopped. A starving man feels the kitchen close." She looked at Sable, and her eyes were tired and certain. "He'll complete the rite himself now, if he must. Which means we're out of time, out of tricks, and down one traitor."

"We're not down anything," Renn said. "We're exactly where we started, except now we know the way, and the door's open, and nobody's paying the god to keep it shut." He shouldered the lantern. "That's more than most people get."

Sable looked down the descent road, into the dark where the singing faded, and felt the binding stir inside their own chest like a second heartbeat — patient, hungry, waiting to see what they would dare.

"Then we go down," they said. "All the way. Tonight."

Above them, through a mile of stone and snow, the first bell of midwinter rang out from a capital that did not yet know its king was running out of time.
