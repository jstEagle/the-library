## 16. The Unbinding Rite

Isolde Vane had spent eleven years telling herself she had forgotten the liturgy.

She had burned her copy of the *Canticles of the Deep Order* in a copper pan the night the defrocking ink dried on her wrist—a small, tidy fire, very controlled, the kind of fire a sensible woman makes. She had recited the names of the saints backward to scrub them from memory. She had taken up practical work: bandages, fevers, setting bones in a border infirmary where nobody prayed to anything more ambitious than a good harvest and a quick death.

And yet here, at the bottom of the world, with the gate's mouth yawning open before her like a wound in the mountain, the words came back whole. Every syllable. Every pause. The way you never forget the shape of a thing that once lived inside your chest.

"They don't tell you that part," she murmured, chalking the first circle onto the black stone floor. "In the seminary. They tell you the liturgy is dangerous. They don't tell you it's patient. It waits for you to need it."

"Isolde." Sable's voice came from across the chamber, strained but steady. "Less sermon. More chalk."

Sable stood at the center of the gate itself—both hands pressed flat against the threshold stone, arms braced, holding the mouth open the way one holds open a door against a gale. The binding ran through them visibly now, silver-dark lines climbing their forearms like frost given ambition. They had not slept in two days. They had stopped pretending they could.

Renn was at the passage behind them, crossbow loaded, watching the dark tunnel with the expression of a man who had decided that if death was coming down that corridor, it was going to have to discuss terms first.

"Right," Isolde said. "Chalk."

* * *

The liturgy of unbinding had three movements. Her order had called them *the Naming*, *the Weighing*, and *the Severance*, though the old texts used older words still: *call it what it is; show it what it took; cut along the seam*. Simple, in the way that surgery is simple. You needed steady hands, true sight, and the willingness to look directly at the thing everyone else had agreed to describe only in whispers.

The problem—the problem her order had died over, historically—was the third movement. A god does not sit still for cutting. A god argues. And it argues with the only weapons it has ever truly owned: your own memories, worn like borrowed coats.

"Renn," Isolde said, drawing the second circle. "When I begin, whatever you hear from this chamber—screaming, singing, your mother calling your name—you hold the passage. Do you understand? Nothing comes through that tunnel. Including me, if it comes to that."

"That seems harsh," Renn said.

"It's the job."

"I'll hold it," he said, and then, softer, because he was Renn: "But if Sable screams my name, I'm allowed to be tempted for at least a full second."

"One second," Isolde agreed. "I'll allow one."

She looked at Sable then—at the keeper holding the door—and found them already looking back. There was something in Sable's face she hadn't seen in the weeks since the waystation: fear, yes, worn thin as everything else on them, but underneath it a kind of terrible calm. The calm of someone who had already done the arithmetic and accepted the answer.

"You'll want to say something meaningful," Sable said. "Don't waste it. Just start."

"Gentle humor under pressure," Isolde said. "You'd have made a terrible priestess."

"And you'd have made a decent smuggler. Start."

Isolde set the token—the prayer-scarred thing that had started all of this, humming now like a struck bell that refused to finish its note—in the center of the inner circle. She knelt. She breathed out, slow, the way the old mothers of her order had taught: *empty yourself until there is room for the truth to stand up in.*

Then she began the Naming.

* * *

The first words went into the chamber like stones into deep water.

The dark answered immediately. It always did. The god beneath Thornfell did not roar or rage—not at first. It *listened*, which was infinitely worse, and the listening had weight, a pressure against the eardrums, like standing underwater while something enormous turned its attention toward you.

"*Thou who art beneath,*" Isolde intoned, "*thou who art bound, thou who art fed by hand and hidden by hymn—I name thee.*"

The temperature dropped. Frost feathered up the walls in patterns too deliberate to be natural—spirals, eyes, mouths. Somewhere above them, the whole mountain groaned, a long tectonic complaint, as if the rock itself were being asked a question it didn't want to answer.

And then the memories began.

They came gently, at first. That was the horror of it. Isolde had expected violence and got instead a warm kitchen: her mother's infirmary in the delta country, bread on the board, her father humming off-key. She was nine years old and her hands were small and clean and had never held anything heavier than a spoon.

"You could have stayed," the kitchen said, in her mother's voice. "You could have been happy. Nobody asked you to come down here and die in the dark for people who defrocked you."

"The people defrocked me," Isolde said, and kept chalking. "The gods didn't. That's the difference, Mother. One of us has to know the difference."

The kitchen dissolved with something like a sigh. But the god was learning—it always learned fast—and the next assault came sideways, at Renn.

From the passage came the sound of water. Not dripping. *Drowning.* The specific sound of a river taking a boat, wood cracking, men shouting, and then the particular silence after shouting stops. Renn's drowned crewmates. Isolde knew the story the way you know a scar on someone else's body: he'd told it once, drunk, on the descent road, and never mentioned it again.

"Hold the passage," she said quietly.

There was a long pause. Then Renn's voice, rougher than usual: "One second. I'm using my one second."

"Take two," Isolde said. "I won't tell Sable."

A wet, cold voice came down the corridor, sweet as rot: *"Captain. The water wasn't so bad, once we stopped fighting it. Come see. We saved your bunk."*

"Renn." Sable's voice, strained, from the gate. "Their faces are lies. All of them. Say it back."

"All of them are lies," Renn repeated, and Isolde heard him rack the crossbow, heard his boots plant themselves. "Even the ones I miss. *Especially* the ones I miss." A pause, and then, with the particular stubborn warmth that was the man's entire soul: "Sorry, lads. Bunk's mine. I sleep above deck these days."

The drowning sounds stopped. In their place, briefly, something almost like respect.

* * *

The Weighing was harder.

This was the movement where the liturgy demanded the god be shown what it had taken—every stolen year, every eaten soul, every prayer answered with a hook in it. Isolde opened the ledger's ash-memory in her mind (she had not seen the burning herself, but Sable had described it four times, each time with more detail, and grief makes excellent witnesses) and spoke the names aloud. Maro's list. The named souls, freed and unfed, and the god felt every absence like teeth pulled.

It screamed then. Properly screamed, finally, no more kitchens and gentle voices—the sound of a thing that had been comfortable discovering it would not be permitted to remain so. The chamber shook. Chalk dust rose in ghostly clouds. The circles held, barely, glowing a sullen white.

And through the scream, wearing the scream like a mask, it found Sable.

Isolde saw it happen. Saw the keeper's spine go rigid, saw the silver bindings flare, saw Sable's face change—not into fear but into *recognition*, which was so much worse.

"Sable." Isolde's voice cracked across the chamber. "Whatever it's showing you—"

"My grandmother," Sable said. Their voice was level. Too level. "She's here. She says—" A breath. "She says she's sorry. She says she tried to warn me in the journal. She says the words were worn away because *she wore them away*, so no one after her would have to read them."

"Is it her?"

"No." And there it was, the terrible calm again, surfacing like a swimmer. "My grandmother has been dead for nineteen years, and even if she weren't, she'd never apologize. She once apologized to a mule and the mule bit her." Sable's hands pressed harder against the stone. "It's wearing her face because it thinks family is a lever. It learned that from Maro. Maro thought loyalty was a lever. Everyone down here keeps making the same mistake about us."

"What mistake?" Isolde asked, because the liturgy wanted questions, wanted dialogue, wanted the thing talking while the circles tightened.

"That we keep things because we're afraid of losing them." Sable smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and Isolde thought: *there is the jailer's blood.* "We keep things because we've decided they're worth keeping. There's a difference. It wouldn't understand. It's never decided anything—it's only ever *taken*."

The grandmother-face flickered, lost conviction, and slid off Sable's features like water off wax.

"Good," Isolde said. "Now hold still. The Severance begins."

* * *

Here is what the forbidden liturgy actually was, stripped of incense and terror: a conversation conducted in the oldest language, in which the priestess asks the bound thing a question it cannot survive answering honestly.

*What are you, without what you wear?*

That was the blade. Kings, crowns, worshippers, smugglers, keepers—all of it was clothing. The liturgy peeled. And the thing beneath Thornfell, which had spent three thousand years dressed in other people's faith, was about to be asked to stand naked in front of witnesses.

Isolde raised both hands. The final circle closed. The token in the center sang one clear, unbearable note.

"*Severance,*" she said. "*By the order that buried its own books: let the king be a king. Let the god be a god. Let neither wear the other. Cut along the seam.*"

The world went white at the edges.

What happened next, Isolde would spend the rest of her life failing to describe accurately. The best she ever managed, much later, over cheap wine with Renn, was: "Imagine pulling a splinter out of a hand, except the hand is a kingdom and the splinter is three thousand years old and *opinionated*."

King Aldric came apart from the god the way a drowning man comes apart from a river—violently, gratefully, and not entirely intact. Isolde felt him through the liturgy: a man, an ordinary man, tired and vain and genuinely kind in the boring ways, who had prayed once at the wrong altar and been *answered*. Three years of radiance sloughed off him like burnt skin. Underneath: a frightened human being who had not chosen any of this, which was somehow the saddest thing in the entire mountain.

The god fought the peeling with everything it had left. The chamber filled with faces—Maro's, young and laughing; the blind child from the valley village, seeing; Isolde's own mother; a choir of the dead singing hymns in perfect harmony, the hymns of her suppressed order, sung *correctly* for the first time in decades, and Isolde wept even as she kept speaking, because that was the cruelest temptation of all, aimed precisely at her: *come home, the choir misses you.*

"I know," she whispered, tears freezing on her cheeks. "I know. And I'm going to spend the rest of my life missing it too. But you don't get the king. You don't get anyone. Cut along the seam. *Cut.*"

The last thread parted.

And the gate, suddenly empty of a king to anchor it, began to close around nothing—which meant it would tear itself open instead, and everything they had done would pour out into the world like blood from a slit throat.

"Sable!" Isolde shouted. "It needs an anchor! Now! *Now!*"

Across the chamber, Sable looked at the closing mouth of the gate, and then at Renn, and Isolde watched them say goodbye without saying a word—the smallest nod, the longest look, a whole marriage conducted in half a second—and then Sable Crane stepped forward into the gate's mouth with their arms open, like someone stepping into a snowstorm they had finally, after a lifetime of hiding, decided to walk through rather than around.

"No—" Renn lunged forward, and Isolde caught his arm, and the gate's light swallowed everything—

—and the last thing Isolde Vane saw, before the whiteout took the world, was Sable turning back once, mouth moving, shaping words the light ate before they could land.

She would spend years wondering what they'd said.

The mountain exhaled. The circles went dark. And somewhere above them, midwinter arrived.
