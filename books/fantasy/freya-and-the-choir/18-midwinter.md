## 18. Midwinter

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain at the stroke of midnight.

Sable felt it first in their teeth—a deep, grinding shift, like a jaw deciding whether to open. The descent road beneath their boots went taut as a bowstring. Above them, through three hundred feet of stone and ice, the coronation bells of the capital began to ring, and every bell rang wrong: too slow, too sweet, each note landing like a hand pressed flat against the chest.

"Tell me that's the wind," Renn said.

"It's never been the wind," Sable said.

They stood at the mouth of the prison—the true mouth, not the gate above but the throat below it, a ring of black stone set into the ice like the pupil of an enormous eye. Isolde knelt at its edge with her liturgy spread out on the frozen ground, weighted at the corners with dagger-hilts and river stones and one smuggler's gold coin she'd refused to explain. Her breath came in white plumes that did not drift. They hung in the air, motionless, as if the mountain were holding them to read later.

"The rite has three parts," Isolde said, for the fourth time. Sable let her say it again. In the last six hours she had become the kind of person who repeated things when terrified, and Sable had become the kind of person who needed to hear it. "I speak the king loose. You hold the mouth open—your blood on the stone, your name spoken backward, your *will* planted in it like a stake. Renn keeps the passage clear." She looked up. Snow had begun to fall upward around her, fine as flour, rising toward some ceiling none of them could see. "And if any part fails—"

"Then we find out what midwinter tastes like from the inside," Renn said. "You've mentioned."

Isolde's mouth twitched. Even now—*especially* now—she had the defrocked priestess's habit of finding doctrine funny. "I was going to say we pray," she said. "But yes. That too."

Sable crouched and pressed their palm to the black stone. It was warm. It had been warm for two days, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and twice in the night they had felt something on the other side press back against their hand—gently, almost courteously, the way Maro used to tap twice on a door before entering.

*Don't think about Maro.* The empty cell below was dark. They'd checked on the way down. They would not check again.

"I can hear the choir," they said instead.

Renn went still beside them. "The dead ones?"

"No." Sable listened harder. Far under the ice, threaded through the ringing of the capital's bells, there was a sound like many voices singing in unison—but flattened, all the differences sanded off, ten thousand throats made into one instrument. "It's not the bound dead. It's the *prayers*. Everyone in the kingdom who's prayed to the king since autumn. He's been collecting them. He's going to sing them back all at once when the crown touches the relic, and every soul that prayed will be—" They stopped. Their grandmother's journal had used a word for it, in handwriting that got smaller and drunker the deeper into the book you read. *Harvest.*

"Then we're late," Renn said, and drew his knife, and grinned his terrible ferryman's grin, the one he wore boarding sinking ships. "We're always late. It's practically our brand."

* * *

Isolde began to sing.

Not the hymns—those had been scrubbed clean generations ago, bleached of everything dangerous. This was the old liturgy, the forbidden one, the one that had gotten her order burned out of its cloisters and her name struck from every registry in the kingdom. It didn't sound like worship. It sounded like argument. Each phrase rose and then doubled back on itself, questioning, demanding, a woman's voice built into a crowbar.

The black ring shuddered.

Frost raced outward from Sable's palm in fractal veins. The ice overhead groaned, and somewhere far above, muffled by stone, they heard the coronation crowd gasp—one voice, thousands strong, the sound of a kingdom watching its radiant king lift the crown toward the relic of Saint Verene.

"Now," Isolde said, between verses, not breaking cadence. "Hold it open. Whatever it shows you—*hold it open.*"

Sable spoke their own name backward.

It was worse than they'd expected. A name spoken forward is just a name; spoken backward it becomes a key, and keys turn both ways. The syllables left their mouth and the mountain drank them, and suddenly Sable could feel the whole shape of the gate—not the stone, the *mouth*—the way you feel your own tongue. Every keeper before them had held this same shape. Generations of Sandovals, pressing their names into this ring like thumbs into wax, each one a little weaker than the last, each one feeding the thing below just enough quiet to keep it sleeping.

*Just enough to stay quiet,* their grandmother had written. *That was the original sin. We didn't jail it. We paid it off.*

The mouth opened.

What came up out of it was not darkness. Darkness would have been a mercy. What came up was *memory*—the god-thing's oldest weapon, honed over centuries of listening at the gate, learning what the people above it loved.

Sable's mother stepped out of the ice.

She had been dead eleven years, drowned in the Whitefall crossing, and here she was dripping dry, smiling the exact crooked smile from the one portrait Sable had ever dared keep. "Little wren," she said. "You're so tall. Come away from that cold stone. Come home."

"You're not her," Sable said, and held the mouth open. Their voice only shook a little. "She tapped twice on doors. You don't even know that."

The memory flickered—annoyed, almost—and dissolved. Behind it came another: their grandmother, younger than any portrait, holding out the journal. Then Maro, arms open, saying *I only wanted to protect you all.* Then Renn—

No. Not Renn. Sable shut that door hard, nailed it, salted it. The thing below paused, tasting the refusal, and Sable felt it *note* the refusal, file it away, learn something about them.

"Hold," Isolde sang. "Hold, hold, hold."

Far above, faint as a coin dropped down a well, the crown touched the relic.

* * *

The world went white at the edges.

Sable felt it happen through the gate: the merge beginning, king and god threading together like two hands lacing fingers. King Aldric—poor, hollowed Aldric, who had climbed the mountain three years ago to pray for his dying queen and been answered by the thing that unmade the last king—was being swallowed from the inside. And the thing was being swallowed right back. That was the horror no hymn had ever admitted: it wasn't possession. It was a *marriage*, mutual, consummating, and once the vows finished there would be no seam between them ever again.

Isolde's song changed key. Deeper. Rougher. The tearing-song.

Above, the coronation crowd screamed—one long unified note of it, rolling down through the stone like water finding a crack. The bells stopped mid-peal. Somewhere in the capital a man named Aldric began, very quietly, to come apart from the thing wearing him, and the thing fought, and the fight was conducted entirely in memories.

It threw everything at Isolde first. Her cloister burning. Her sisters' faces in the smoke. Her own voice, years ago, praying a dying child well and getting exactly what she asked for. She staggered; her verse cracked; snow fell upward faster.

"Isolde!" Renn barked.

"I'm *singing*," she snarled, tears freezing on her cheeks, and picked the melody back up half a beat late, and it was enough, it held, because Isolde Vane had spent ten years doing penance in a border infirmary and a little more guilt was a hill she'd already climbed a hundred times.

So the thing turned to Sable.

It stopped offering memories. It offered the future instead—which was new, which was against the rules, which meant it was frightened.

*I can make the mountains safe forever,* it said, directly into the marrow of Sable's bones, in a voice like warm bread and banked fires. *No more frozen couriers. No more drowned crews. No toll of blood on the passes. Your network sheltered under my shadow and thrived. Say the word and I will wear the crown gently. I will be a good king. I have had three years of practice.*

And underneath it, softer, the temptation under the temptation:

*Or take me wholly into yourself, little keeper, and no one else ever has to carry this. Not your children. Not your children's children. The sentence ends with you. Isn't that what you've wanted since you found the journal? To be the last one?*

Sable's grip on the mouth's edge trembled.

Because it was true. Gods below and kings above, it was *true*—they had lain awake in the waystation reading their grandmother's cramped, drunken handwriting and thought, with a shame they'd never voiced: *let it end with me. Let me be the last Sandoval to pay this tax.*

"That's the trick, isn't it," they said aloud. Their breath hung motionless in the air. "Every generation, someone wishes it would end. And wishing is *feeding*."

The silence below had a texture. Attention.

"My grandmother wished it. Her mother wished it. All the way back to the first one, who invited you in and called it imprisonment because the truth was too ugly to write down." Sable leaned closer to the open mouth, close enough to feel its warmth, its terrible patience. "You don't want a keeper. You want a volunteer. Well—I refuse to volunteer. And I refuse to let you keep the king."

They reached into their coat and took out the prayer-scarred token—the one the dying courier had carried, the one that had started all of it, humming against the gate stones since autumn. Inside it, folded small, was the scrap of vellum Isolde had recovered from the burned archive: a single line of the first Sandoval's hand.

A name. The true name. The one their grandmother had hidden rather than speak, because speaking it meant admitting what the family had done.

Sable unfolded it and read it aloud into the mouth of the god.

* * *

Later, Renn would describe the sound as a mountain clearing its throat. Isolde would call it the unmaking chord—the one note the old liturgy was built to survive and nothing else was. Sable remembered it as their own name, spoken forward at last, by something vast, in a tone of grudging respect.

The gate *closed*—not on the god, but around it, and the binding, torn loose from a dying dynasty's bloodline, went looking for a new anchor.

There wasn't time to decide. There was barely time to breathe. The binding came up through the black stone like floodwater, and Sable had perhaps half a heartbeat to understand what Isolde had known since the infirmary, what the journal had hinted in its smallest handwriting, what the whole rotten inheritance had been building toward: *the gate needs a living anchor or the thing escapes entirely.*

They could have stepped back. Renn was reaching for them—they saw it, saw his face, saw him understand one full second before anyone should have—and Isolde was shouting something that was probably a warning and possibly a blessing.

Sable thought: *let it end with me.*

And then, laughing at themselves even now, even *here*: *no. Let it begin with me instead.*

They took the binding in.

* * *

Whiteout.

Not snow—though snow was falling everywhere at once now, downward and upward and sideways, the mountain's whole architecture of weather collapsing into a single blinding hush. Whiteout the way a page goes white when the ink is lifted off it. The choir of stolen prayers cut off mid-note. The bells above silent. Isolde's song ending on a note that hung in the air like a held breath.

Inside the whiteness, Sable felt the god settle into them—not violently, not like a possession, but like a guest finally shown the correct room after centuries of sleeping in the hall. It was smaller than they'd feared. It was lonelier than they'd imagined. It curled up against the binding and, very distantly, almost politely, tapped twice.

Somewhere above, a man named Aldric woke on the coronation dais with three missing years and a widow's ring clenched in his fist, and did not know yet why he was crying.

Somewhere below, a dark cell stayed dark.

And at the mouth of the gate, in the whiteout silence where a climactic battle should have ended with trumpets, Marguerite Crane's mountain held its breath around three small figures—one kneeling, one catching, one still singing softly, badly, out of sheer stubbornness—while the snow came down at last the ordinary way, downward, gentle, burying the black ring until it looked like nothing at all.

Until it looked, Sable thought, from very far away, almost like sleep.

Almost like peace.

Almost like the first night of the rest of a very long vigil.
