## 19. After the Silence

The first thing Renn heard when the whiteout broke was Isolde Vane swearing.

Not praying. Swearing. A long, fluent, unholy stream of it, the kind of language a woman only learns after her order takes away her vestments and leaves her the vocabulary. It was, he would think later, the most reassuring sound in the world. Gods did not swear like that. People did. And people were what he had left.

"Sable." His voice came out cracked, a dry hinge. He said it again, louder, and the word bounced off ice walls that hadn't been there three days ago and wouldn't be there tomorrow. "Sable, wake up. Come on. You don't get to do this."

They didn't move.

Sable lay across his arms where he'd caught them when the binding took — when the light under the gate went from a scream to a held breath, when the whole mountain exhaled and every hair on Renn's body stood up and lay down again all at once. Their face was gray as old tallow. Their lips were moving, very slightly, shaping something over and over that might have been a name or might have been a prayer or might have been *don't let go*, and Renn decided it was *don't let go*, because he needed it to be.

"Isolde!" he shouted. "Isolde, I need you here, not there!"

She came at a half-run, skirts frozen into stiff sculpted bells around her legs, her hands black with soot and candle-wax and something else he didn't want to identify. She knelt, pressed two fingers under Sable's jaw, and closed her eyes.

"They're alive," she said.

"Alive how? Alive like a person, or alive like—"

"Alive like a person who has made an enormously stupid and magnificent choice and will pay for it for the rest of their life." She opened her eyes. They were red-rimmed and steady. "Which is most of us, if we're honest. Help me turn them."

* * *

Getting Sable up the descent road took eleven hours, and Renn remembered perhaps four of them.

There was no road anymore, not really. The trials they'd passed on the way down — the collapsing passages, the whispering ice, the places where the mountain had offered each of them exactly the thing they wanted most — those had been tests. This was just damage. The god, torn out of its king and stuffed screaming into a new anchor, had left the prison the way a flood leaves a house: everything rearranged, nothing where you remembered putting it, water still dripping from ceilings that used to be floors.

Renn carried Sable in a sling made from his own coat and a length of rope he'd carried for fifteen years without once needing, which felt like the rope's whole personality. Isolde walked ahead with a lantern she'd relit from a flame she swore she'd kept cupped alive since the rite began, though Renn privately suspected she'd simply struck a match and lied about it with priestly authority. Behind them, somewhere far below, the gate hummed. Not the hungry hum of before. Something more like a struck bell fading, fading, refusing entirely to stop.

Every hour or so, Sable surfaced.

It was never for long. Their eyes would open halfway, unfocused, finding nothing — or finding him, briefly, which was worse, because then their mouth would twist and they'd try to say something and produce only a sound like wind through a keyhole. Once they managed two words.

"Cold," they said. Then: "Renn."

"I know," he said. "I've got you. I'm not putting you down."

Their eyes closed again. But their hand — the one hanging free of the sling, the one with the burn scars from twenty years of rope work — curled shut around a fistful of his shirt and stayed that way for the next six hours. He stopped feeling his shoulder around hour three. He decided not to mention it.

* * *

They stopped at the halfway shrine, a niche in the rock where smugglers had left offerings for longer than anyone could remember — bootlaces, mostly, and buttons, and once, memorably, a whole wheel of cheese that Renn had eaten in 1309 and never confessed to. Isolde built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. They fed Sable melted snow off Renn's knife, drop by drop, and Sable swallowed maybe one drop in five, but Isolde counted them out loud anyway, like a woman keeping score against death itself.

"One. Two. Three. Oh, don't be dramatic, that one counts. Four."

"You're a terrible nurse," Renn said.

"I'm a defrocked priestess carrying a person who is currently serving as the load-bearing wall of a divine prison." She tipped another drop between Sable's lips. "I am an *excellent* nurse. I'm simply honest about the prognosis."

"What is the prognosis?"

Isolde was quiet for a moment. In the firelight her face looked older than it had in the infirmary at Marrow's Ford, older even than it had in the vault beneath the waystation, older than it had at any point during the descent, as though the last three days were being added to her directly, hour by hour, like freight.

"I don't know," she said finally. "And I want you to hear me say that plainly, Captain, because everyone in this story has been lying to everyone else out of love, and look where it got us. I don't know if they wake up whole. I don't know what's left of Sable and what belongs to the thing behind the gate. What I know is that the binding holds — I checked it twice while you weren't listening, and once while you were — and that bindings hold better when the anchor wants to live. So our job is to make them want to live. Talk to them. Be annoying about it."

"Annoying."

"Tell them about the crew. Tell them about the roads. Tell them about the time you ate the shrine cheese. A soul that's holding a door shut needs reasons on this side of it." She fed drop five. "That's not theology, by the way. That's nursing."

So Renn talked.

He told Sable about Doss and Ferro, who were alive, who were waiting at the Low Road camp with hot wine and an increasingly creative set of explanations for why their captain had vanished midwinter. He told them about the new route through Kestrel Pass that he'd been saving as a surprise, which was now ruined as a surprise but would still be beautiful. He told them about the cheese. He told them about the night in the Wayfarer's Rest when Sable had beaten him at cards with a hand so blatantly crooked that he'd laughed until the ale came out his nose, and how he'd known, right then, that he was done for.

Somewhere around the card game, Sable's breathing changed. Deeper. Slower. Around the cheese, their fingers loosened in his shirt — and then, deliberately, moved, and took his hand instead.

He didn't tell Isolde. She saw anyway. She was insufferable about it for exactly four seconds, and then she turned away to bank the fire, and her shoulders shook once, and he understood that she was doing the same thing he was, which was refusing to hope out loud because hope out loud sounded too much like a prayer, and prayers, lately, got answered by things with agendas.

* * *

They reached the waystation near dawn on the second day, if dawn could be said to exist, which that week it barely could — the sky over Thornfell had been the color of dirty wool since midwinter, though whether that was grief or weather, nobody could say. The station itself stood untouched, which felt obscene. The kettle was where Sable always left it. The ledgers were stacked where Sable always stacked them. The hidden door to the vault below was sealed, and beneath it, faintly, steadily, something breathed.

Renn put Sable in their own bed. Their actual bed, narrow and hard and smelling of pine resin and lamp oil, and Sable sank into it like a stone into a well, and did not stir for a day and a half.

Isolde worked. She worked the way water works, constantly, invisibly, wearing grooves. She went down into the vault every few hours with candles and chalk and her order's forbidden liturgy written out on strips of parchment she'd sewn into her hem years ago, back when she still believed she'd never need it again. She came up gray each time and drank broth and went back down. When Renn asked what she was doing down there, she said, "Maintenance," in a tone that ended conversations.

He asked once more, at night, gentler, and she gave him a little more.

"The gate is half-bound," she said. "Half-bound means the thing inside can hear us, and we can hear it, and neither side can do much about it yet. My job is to keep the seam from widening until your—" she paused, visibly reorganizing a sentence, "—until Sable wakes enough to take the work back. Because it's theirs now. That's what taking a binding means. You don't get to hand it off when you're tired. Believe me, my order tried."

"And if they don't wake?"

"Then I take it," Isolde said, "and I will be spectacularly bad at it, and the mountains will probably eat us all within the year, and I would appreciate it if you didn't repeat that to anyone, including them."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Liar." But she smiled, briefly, tiredly, and it transformed her face the way a struck match transforms a room, and Renn thought: *there she is. The woman the hymns were trying to describe and got wrong.*

* * *

On the third day, the news came up the mountain.

It came the way news came to Thornfell, which was sideways and late and wearing a disguise: a mule driver named Petch, one of theirs, arriving with a load of salted fish and a head full of capital gossip he'd collected at the border markets. Renn met him in the yard, and Petch talked fast, the way he always did when the cargo was heavier than the fish.

King Aldric was awake.

Had been awake nine days. Woke in the middle of a privy council session, sat bolt upright, and asked — politely, in front of twelve lords — why there was a crown on his head and whose funeral they were all dressed for. Asked where Queen Maren was. Queen Maren had been dead two years. The council had exchanged looks over his head like men passing a hot coal, and then someone had laughed nervously, and then the king had laughed, and then everyone had laughed, and the kingdom had decided together, the way kingdoms do, that the king's confusion was a charming eccentricity of recovery.

But he didn't remember the last three years. Not the piety. Not the radiance. Not the generosity that had emptied the treasury into the streets and filled the streets with pilgrims and filled the pilgrim-hospices with people who no longer slept. He remembered a younger court, a harder one, a meaner one, and he was reportedly appalled at the state of the roads, the laxity of the guard, the general atmosphere of golden, humming, suffocating sweetness that had settled over the capital like honey over a flytrap.

"He's ordered an audit," Petch said, delighted. "Of the Crown's own charities. Lord Chancellor nearly fainted. And — here's the good bit — he's asked who's been signing the royal decrees for the past year, because the handwriting's wrong. Too smooth, he says. 'Like it was written by something that had practiced,' is what he said, according to the man who told me, who heard it from a chambermaid, who heard it from the king himself."

Renn listened with his arms crossed and his heart going like a drum in a parade. Beside him, wrapped in a blanket in the doorway because she refused to stay abed, Isolde had gone very still.

"It worked," she said quietly. "We actually did it."

"We did," Renn said. "Someone did. Someone's still doing it, flat on their back upstairs, and doesn't know yet."

Petch, sensing currents above his depth, unloaded his fish and made himself scarce with the tact of a lifelong professional.

Isolde watched the yard for a while. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, the kind that squeaked. Somewhere below them, under forty feet of granite, the gate hummed its fading bell-note, and Renn had learned enough in the past weeks to hear what Isolde heard in it: patience. The thing down there had worn a king for three years. It could wait out a convalescence. It had nothing but time and it knew, now, exactly where the door was and who was holding it.

"It'll test them," Isolde said. "When they wake. It always starts with dreams. Small ones. Kind ones. It'll show them their grandmother's kitchen, their mother's voice, the network safe forever, the passes open and warm. It's very generous, that thing. Generosity is its native language." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Our job is to be louder."

"Louder than a god."

"Louder than a god offering nice things." She glanced at him sideways. "You'd be surprised how much competition that is. Most people lose to it eventually. Most people *choose* it. That's the horror of the whole business, Captain. Nobody's dragged in. They're invited, and they say yes, and they say yes because the invitation is always, always reasonable."

"Not this one," Renn said. "This one says no."

"You think so?"

"I know so." He nodded upward, toward the narrow bed, toward the burned-out, gray-faced, stubborn, impossible person sleeping in it. "I've watched them say no to easier versions of that offer their whole life. Every toll they never raised. Every name they never wrote down. Every year they stayed invisible when being seen would have been safer and richer and kinder to them personally." He shrugged, and found his eyes stinging, and blamed the snow. "They're the worst person I know to make that offer to. The god's going to waste its best material."

Isolde looked at him for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is the closest thing to faith I have heard in twenty years, and it came from a smuggler standing in a fish-smelling yard." She pushed past him toward the door. "Come on. Broth's on. If she wakes while it's hot, we'll have achieved the single greatest medical victory in the history of this mountain, and I intend to be able to say I was present."

"She," Renn repeated. "You said 'she.'"

"I did." Isolde didn't turn around. "Problem?"

"No," Renn said, and followed her in, and discovered that the word fit in his mouth like it had always lived there, like a key he'd been carrying for years without knowing what it opened.

* * *

Sable woke on the fourth evening, during the broth, exactly as predicted.

Not all at once. First the eyes moved under the lids — Renn learned later that Isolde had been watching for that specific movement for two days, REM sleep, dreaming sleep, the sign that the mind was running again rather than merely idling. Then a long stillness. Then one hand lifted off the blanket, hovered, and pressed flat against their own sternum, over the heart, over the place where — Renn had seen it happen, had felt it through the sling — the binding had gone in.

Their lips moved. No sound. Then sound.

"Still here," Sable whispered. It wasn't clear whether they were asking or reporting. With Sable it was usually both.

"Still here," Renn said, and Isolde said it too, and then Sable's eyes opened all the way for the first time in nine days, dark and exhausted and unmistakably, ferociously their own, and fixed on the ceiling as though expecting to see through it, down through the vault, through the granite, to the thing they were now responsible for forever.

"How long," Sable said.

"Nine days," Renn said. "Midwinter's passed. The king woke up. Doesn't remember anything. Is ordering audits and complaining about the handwriting on his own decrees."

A pause. Then, in a voice still rough as gravel but already, already sharpening back into the blade Renn knew: "The ledger?"

"Burned. You burned it. We watched it go up. The named souls went free — Isolde confirmed it, whatever confirming means in this context, she did rites about it, there was chalk involved."

Another pause. Sable's hand was still on their sternum. Slowly, they turned it outward, palm up, and held it in the air between the three of them, and Isolde leaned in and studied the palm the way she studied everything, thoroughly and without flinching, and announced that the scarring was clean, the binding seated, the seams holding, and that Sable had been asleep for nine days and was therefore forbidden from getting out of bed, and that she, Isolde Vane, defrocked, disgraced, and armed with liturgy, would enforce this prohibition with every tool at her disposal.

"Which are?" Sable asked.

"Broth," Isolde said, "and guilt."

Sable almost laughed. It came out broken, a single syllable, half breath — but it was a laugh, aimed at the world, and Renn felt it land in his chest like a coal dropped down his collar. Nine days he had carried them up a mountain. Nine days Isolde had gone down into the dark and come back up. And here was the payoff, the entire payout, the whole wage of the worst month of all their lives: one broken syllable of humor, in Sable's voice, at Isolde's expense.

They were going to be all right. Maybe. Probably. As much as anyone in this story was ever going to be all right.

Sable's palm-up hand turned, then, and found Renn's, and gripped it, and did not let go, and said nothing, because some things didn't need saying, and Renn held on and also said nothing, because he'd learned that from them.

* * *

Near midnight, Isolde went below for her rounds, and Renn sat by the bed in the lamplight, pretending to mend a net he had no intention of mending, and Sable drifted at the edge of sleep and talked, quietly, the way people talk when they're not sure which side of the veil they're on.

"There's singing," they said at one point. "Down there. Faint. Under the hum. Did you—"

"No," Renn said. "Should I have?"

"No. No, it's mine to listen to. That's the job now." A long silence. Then: "It's not cruel tonight. Did Isolde tell you it's not always cruel? Tonight it's just... loud. Like a choir tuning up before anyone's given them the hymn." Their eyes closed. "Maybe that's all it ever was. Something enormous, waiting for someone to teach it the words."

Renn set down the net. He didn't know what to say to that, so he said the truest thing available, which was: "Get some sleep. The choir isn't going anywhere. Neither am I."

Sable slept. Renn sat watch. Outside, the snow kept falling, fine and dry and squeaking, burying the descent road inch by inch, erasing the tracks of the worst journey of his life, which felt right. Some roads should close behind you.

And far away, in a capital full of golden light and nervous lords, a king who remembered nothing was asking hard questions with a young man's voice, and somewhere beneath a mountain a bound thing was learning, note by note, what it was allowed to sing — and in a narrow bed in a waystation above it all, the keeper who had chosen to be seen was finally, blessedly, asleep.

Renn banked the fire low and kept the watch, and waited for morning, and for whatever the mountain asked of them next.
