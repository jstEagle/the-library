## 20. What Is Owed

Isolde Vane had faced down bishops, inquisitors, and one memorable abbess who threw a hymnal at her head, and she had never once been nervous. Standing outside the council chamber of the royal palace, dressed in her second-best traveling habit with ash still under her fingernails from the descent road, she discovered that she was, in fact, nervous.

It was not the room that frightened her. It was the arithmetic. She had spent three days drafting terms on scraps of paper by firelight, and every draft came down to the same uncomfortable truth: she was about to ask the most powerful people in the kingdom to agree to know less than they already knew, and to be grateful for it.

"You're doing the thing," said Renn beside her, arms crossed, looking like a man who had been talked into wearing his one good coat and regretted it deeply. "The face you make when you're rehearsing."

"I do not have a face I make."

"You do. Your jaw goes all—" he wobbled his hand. "Sermon-shaped."

"Captain Renn," Isolde said, "you are here as a courtesy witness, which means your entire job is to stand there and look reformed."

"I'm a smuggler. Reformed is the one thing I can't fake convincingly." He glanced at the doors, where two guards in midwinter livery stood with the particular stiffness of men who had heard rumors and were pretending they hadn't. "You really think they'll go for it? Amnesty? For all of us?"

"I think," Isolde said, "that the alternative is a story. And kings are far more afraid of stories than of smugglers. A smuggler takes coin. A story takes crowns."

The doors opened.

* * *

The council chamber had been rearranged since the last time Isolde had stood in it — eleven years ago, as a novice presenting evidence of liturgical error, back when she still believed errors could be corrected by presentation. Then it had been a room for argument. Now it was a room for listening. The long table had been pushed aside; chairs stood in a loose half-circle before the empty throne dais, where King Aldric sat not on the throne but beside it, on an ordinary wooden chair, as though the throne were a relative he was politely waiting out.

He looked better than she expected. Thinner. The radiant, terrible generosity was gone from him — the light that had made pilgrims weep in the streets — and what remained was a young man with tired eyes and a three-year hole in his memory, holding a cup of something hot and steaming with both hands like a man learning again how cups worked.

"Isolde Vane," he said. Not *the defrocked priestess*. Not *the woman who burned her own order's records*. Just the name, spoken carefully, the way one handles a borrowed tool.

"Your Majesty." She bowed the precise degree owed to a king whose rite had been interrupted. No more. She had spent a lifetime calibrating bows; this one she let sit exactly where doctrine put it.

Aldric gestured to the chairs. "Please. Both of you. Sit. I'm told I owe you a great deal, and that I should be careful how I ask about it, because everyone in this room has decided what I can bear to hear." His mouth twisted. "They may be right. But I'd rather hear it from the two of you than from Lord Chancellor Maren, who has explained to me four times now that nothing happened, at increasing length, which is how I know something happened."

Renn made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh escaping custody.

Isolde sat. "What do you remember, Majesty?"

"The coronation renewal. The crown coming down. Cold." He turned the cup. "Then waking up in spring, apparently, except it's midwinter, and my hand—" he flexed his right hand, and Isolde saw the faint pale seam across the palm where the relic had burned its mark and then, mercifully, released it — "my hand remembers things I don't. Sometimes I reach for things and my arm knows where they are before I do." He looked up. "That's not a question anyone else will answer honestly. So: what happened?"

And there it was. The trap she had walked three hundred miles to spring on herself. Because the truth was a door, and behind the door was a god, and if she told this young man everything — the ledger of names, the fed souls, the first Sandoval's invitation, the three years of answered prayers bought with other people's lives — then either he would believe it and spend his reign looking over his shoulder at a mountain, or he would disbelieve it and the disbelief would spread like rot through every honest record left in the kingdom.

Or — and this was the thought that had kept her awake on the road — he would believe it, and try to fix it. Kings fixed things. Kings raised armies against things. And there was no army in the world that could march on what lived under Thornfell, only keepers, and keepers were not made by decree.

"Majesty," Isolde said, "I'm going to offer you a trade. Before I do, I want you to understand that I've spent eleven years being punished for telling institutions truths they didn't want. I'm very good at it. It cost me my order, my name, and most of my faith. So when I tell you that some truths are load-bearing — that you can pull them out and watch the whole building come down on the people underneath — I am speaking as a professional."

Lord Chancellor Maren, a dry stick of a man with ink-stained cuffs, shifted forward. "Priestess, if you are suggesting—"

"I'm not suggesting, Chancellor. I'm negotiating. There's a difference, and the difference is that I have terms written down." She produced the papers. Three days of drafts, copied fair in Renn's surprisingly beautiful handwriting, because her own had gone to ruin somewhere around the ice fields. "May I?"

Aldric waved a hand — *get on with it* — and Isolde got on with it.

* * *

She laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only currency she had never counterfeited.

Term the first: no restoration of her order. The suppressed liturgy stayed suppressed. The old prayers stayed unspoken above the snowline. She would burn what remained of the order's rites herself, publicly, and the court would not resurrect the order as anything — not as penance, not as gratitude, not as the god's mouthpiece dressed in new vestments. "Because your Majesty spent three years being adored by something that ate worship," she said, "and I watched what it did to the faithful who gave too much. They stopped sleeping. Some of them stopped being entirely. If the order returns, someone will pray too well again, and next time there may not be a keeper strong enough to hold the gate."

Term the second: no hunt. No inquiry into the smuggling network, no pardons granted (pardons required convictions, and convictions required trials, and trials required testimony), simply a quiet administrative forgetting. The mountain roads belonged to themselves. "You cannot tax what you cannot find, Majesty, and you cannot find what a thousand people have spent generations making invisible. Accept that as geography."

Term the third — and here she paused, because it was the one that mattered, the one the whole edifice rested on — silence. Mutual, sworn, permanent. What happened under Thornfell would appear in no chronicle, no confession, no deathbed testament. The official history of the past three years would read: a king's illness, a fever of the realm, a recovery. The answered prayers would be attributed to the natural mercy of the seasons. The vanished dissidents would remain vanished, their names entered into mourning rolls without explanation, because explanation was a debt the truth could not pay without bankrupting everyone.

"And in exchange?" Aldric asked quietly.

"In exchange, the keeper holds the gate. Forever. You will never receive a petition about it. You will never send soldiers toward it. You will never, on pain of everything I know how to invoke, permit any rite, prayer, or ceremony within ten miles of the Thornfell passes." She leaned forward. "I know how that sounds, Majesty. I know what it costs a king to be told *there is a thing you must never look at*. But I have seen what happens when kings look. The last king looked. You looked — or rather, something looked through you — and it took three years and a smuggler's daughter to pry it out."

"She's not a smuggler's daughter," Renn said, before he could stop himself, and then went red. "Sorry. Habit. We don't — the network doesn't use family words much."

"What is she, then?" Aldric asked. And there was something in his voice, young and hungry, that told Isolde he had already guessed, and wanted to be told wrong.

"A keeper," Isolde said. "The last of a line that was never what the ledgers said it was. That's all any of you get."

* * *

Maren objected, of course. He objected on grounds of precedent, sovereignty, and the constitutional status of mountains, and Isolde answered each objection with the patience of a woman who had once spent six months arguing about the correct number of candles at vespers. Precedent: there was none, because nothing like this had ever happened, and pretending otherwise was how the order had died. Sovereignty: the crown's writ had never run below the snowline, and everyone in the room knew it; the treaty merely acknowledged existing facts, which was what treaties were *for*. The mountains: the mountains were not party to this negotiation and would not honor it regardless, so the crown might as well take credit for agreeing.

But it was Aldric himself who ended it. He set down his cup, and looked at his right hand, and flexed it once.

"When I woke," he said, "the first thing I did was call for the lists. Everyone I'd executed. Everyone I'd exiled. Everyone who'd stopped sleeping and gone strange and been taken away." His voice stayed level with visible effort. "Three years, Chancellor. Do you know what I found? The pages are blank. Not removed — blank. As if the ink was drunk." He looked up at Isolde. "Was that it? Was that the thing?"

"That was part of it," Isolde said. "Yes."

"And the rest — the crops, the healings, the child in the valley who—"

"Is well," Isolde said firmly. "She is well, and she sees, and what restored her sight was paid for by others, and she will never learn the price, and neither will you. That is term the third, Majesty. That is what silence buys. Not innocence. Mercy. They are not the same thing, and a king who confuses them becomes the last king."

The chamber was silent long enough that Isolde heard the fire tick in the grate.

"You speak of mercy," Aldric said at last, "as though it's a thing you arrange. Like grain shipments."

"It is exactly like grain shipments," Isolde said. "Nobody thanks you for it, everybody needs it, and if you do it loudly, half of it spoils before it arrives."

Something moved across the young king's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would eventually learn to live. "My tutors called me too literal for theology."

"Your tutors were fools, Majesty. Literal is the only way theology survives contact with people." She slid the papers across. "Sign, and we are done, and you will never see me again. Refuse, and I will tell you everything, in detail, on the record, with witnesses — and then you may do whatever a king does with a truth that eats thrones. I genuinely don't recommend it. But it's yours to choose. That's what makes it a negotiation instead of a demand."

* * *

He signed. All three copies, with the steady hand of a man writing his own future smaller than he'd hoped and larger than he'd feared. Maren witnessed. Renn witnessed, printing his name in those beautiful careful letters, and Isolde noticed that he wrote *Renn* and nothing else — no house name, no port of origin — and that the chancellor, wisely, did not ask.

At the door, Aldric stopped them.

"Priestess Vane." He hesitated. "The order that defrocked you. Was it — were they right? About the errors?"

Isolde considered the question for a long moment, the way she used to consider scripture, back when scripture still answered.

"They were right about the errors," she said. "They were wrong about everything else. The errors were the truest parts." She bowed her calibrated bow. "Keep well, Majesty. Sleep at night. Don't pray to anything bigger than yourself, and if you must pray, keep it small. Small prayers are the only ones that stay polite."

"And the keeper?" he asked, as she turned away. "Will she be all right?"

Isolde thought of Sable asleep in the narrow bed, breathing slow and even, the gate humming beneath them like a cat that had decided — provisionally, on trial — to purr.

"She's learning," Isolde said. "Which is more than most of us manage."

* * *

Outside, the capital was loud with ordinary winter: cart wheels, hawkers, children throwing dirty snow. A poster on the corner announced the postponed coronation renewal, rescheduled for spring, *by the king's own wish, that the realm might heal first.* Someone had chalked a small crude heart beneath it. Someone else had chalked, beneath the heart, *about time.*

Renn walked beside her in silence until they reached the city gate, and then, finally, he spoke.

"You didn't ask amnesty for Maro."

"No."

"He's alive. Down there. In the dark cell, with the ledger burned out of him."

"I know."

"He taught Sable everything. He built half the network. Doesn't he get — I don't know. A pardon nobody reads? A blank page with his name on it?"

Isolde pulled her cloak tighter against the wind coming off the plains. "Justice isn't a ledger, Captain. That was the whole lesson of the last three years. Maro's sentence isn't mine to commute, and it isn't the king's either. It's the mountain's. And the mountain," she added, "has shown itself to be a great deal more merciful than any court I've ever stood in. Just slower."

Renn grunted — the smuggler's verdict, neither assent nor dissent, merely acknowledgment that the argument existed and would keep.

They rode north the next morning. Somewhere past the second ridge, the air changed — cleaner, colder, with that faint mineral sweetness that meant the passes were near, and beneath them, the gate. Isolde felt it before she saw the waystation's chimney smoke: a low hum in her back teeth, familiar now, almost companionable. The thing below was singing its allowed notes. Carefully. Like a choirboy who had learned what happened to soloists.

Sable met them in the yard, wrapped in two cloaks and visibly thinner, standing upright in full daylight where anyone — a courier, a neighbor, the sky itself — could see them. That was new. That was going to take getting used to, Isolde thought, and then caught herself smiling, because it was also going to be wonderful.

"Well?" Sable said. "Did they sign?"

"They signed."

"And?"

"And," Isolde said, handing over the copy meant for the keeper's keeping, "you are officially, permanently, gloriously invisible to the crown of Aldric the Second. No hunts, no taxes, no priests. The kingdom above agrees to forget you exist." She paused, letting the wind carry the words. "So I suggest, keeper, that you do the one thing no Sandoval has ever done."

Sable looked down at the paper, and then up at the valley below, where the village chimneys smoked and the blind girl who could see was, at that very moment, presumably running through the snow.

"Go down and introduce yourself," Isolde said. "In person. With your name."

Sable stared at her. Behind them, Renn made a sound suspiciously like a man swallowing a laugh.

"You're serious," Sable said.

"Deadly. I've arranged for you to be forgotten by an empire. The least you can do is be remembered by forty farmers." Isolde smiled, and for the first time in eleven years, it didn't hurt. "Besides — I hear they leave excellent offerings at midwinter, and somebody responsible ought to be there to keep the prayers small."
