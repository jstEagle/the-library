## 22. Freya and the Choir

### Renn

Renn had never been a man for gardens. His hands were built for rope and rudder-pole, for the particular violence of a knot that had to hold or kill you. So it was a source of ongoing, low-grade bewilderment to everyone in the valley—including himself—that he now spent his mornings on his knees in the dirt behind the waystation, arguing with bean plants.

"You're doing it wrong," said Wren, the cooper's daughter, who was seven and had appointed herself supervisor of all agricultural endeavors within sight of her house.

"I'm doing it *exactly* how Isolde said."

"Isolde's a priestess. Priestesses talk to gods, not beans."

"Isolde would say that's the same thing."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then decided to let him off with a warning and ran off to torment the goats instead.

Renn sat back on his heels and looked at the waystation. It had changed over the winter. Someone—Sable, though they'd never admit it—had hung a proper door on the south side, painted it a deep blue that had cost an obscene amount at the spring market, and carved a small thorn-and-bell motif over the lintel. The toll ledger now lived on a shelf by the entrance, in plain view, because the tolls were now honest ones: a copper for the road, a copper for the infirmary Isolde was slowly building out of the old shrine room, and nothing at all if you were hungry, in which case there was soup.

It was, Renn thought, a strange kind of life for a smuggler captain to retire into. He hadn't entirely retired. The *Kestrel's Mercy* still ran the low road twice a season, and his crew still swore like dockhands and drank like the tide, and there were still goods that needed moving that the king's roads had no interest in. But the cargo had changed. Medicine, mostly. Seed. Letters from people who'd scattered during the bad years and wanted to find each other again.

And once, in the deepest part of winter, a crate he hadn't asked about and hadn't opened, which he'd carried down to the gate himself and set on the threshold without a word. Sable had taken it inside and come back an hour later with empty hands and a look he'd learned not to ask about. Some things still went down. Not many. Not often. But the gate was a gate, and gates had two sides, and Renn had made his peace with the fact that the man he loved was the hinge.

He went inside, washed the dirt off, and put the kettle on.

Sable was at the table with the ledger, doing the thing they did every evening now: reading back through the day's entries with a jeweler's squint, hunting for anything that hummed wrong. A name written too carefully. A toll paid in a coin that shouldn't exist. They had explained it to him once, late, in the dark, in the flat voice they used for the worst things: *The god is bound, but binding is a conversation, Renn. It talks all the time. I just have to make sure it's only ever talking to me.*

The kettle sang. Sable looked up, and the squint went out of their eyes, and they smiled the small private smile that Renn had spent eleven years and one apocalypse earning.

"Any trouble today?" he asked.

"A merchant tried to pay his toll with a prayer."

"A *what*?"

"He knelt down right there on the threshold and said, 'Keeper, bless my mules, they've been lame all season.'" Sable turned the ledger around so Renn could see the entry. *One toll, waived. One blessing, mules, minor.* "I told him I'd look into it. Then I went and looked at his mules."

"And?"

"They were fine. They were *suspiciously* fine." Sable closed the ledger. "I've told them a hundred times. Small prayers. Honest ones. A blessing for a sick goat, fine. But the moment somebody asks the gate for a harvest or a husband, I have to go and *un*do it, and un-doing gives me a headache that lasts two days."

"So the mules—"

"Are going to be lame again by Thursday, and that merchant is going to think I'm a fraud, and that is exactly what I want him to think." They said it with real satisfaction. Renn laughed, and Sable's mouth twitched, and for a moment the keeper of the living gate was just a tired person at a kitchen table, pleased with their own pettiness.

That was the discipline, Renn had come to understand. Not the listening at the gate every night—though they did that too, an hour in the dark, motionless, with their palm flat against the stone. The discipline was this: the joke afterward. The beans. The blue door. The refusal to let the mountain be the only true thing in the house.

"Isolde's coming for supper," he said. "She's bringing a student."

"Gods help the student."

"Gods help *Isolde*," Renn said. "The student asked her today whether the old gods were good."

Sable winced. "What did she say?"

"She said, 'Sit down, child, and let me tell you about the difference between good and *answering.*'"

Sable nodded slowly. "That'll take all supper."

"It'll take all spring," Renn said, and poured the tea.

### Isolde

Isolde Vane had been a priestess of the Choir for thirty years, defrocked for eleven of them, and reinstated by absolutely no one, which suited her fine. Institutions, in her long experience, were how the gods got bureaucratized, and gods were bad enough without paperwork.

She taught now in the old shrine room at the waystation, which Sable had cleared out and warmed and which still, on certain evenings, smelled faintly of incense no one had burned. She had four students in the valley and three who came down from the high passes, and she taught them the way she wished someone had taught her: slowly, skeptically, and with a great deal of sweeping.

"Sweeping is prayer," she told them, handing out the brooms. "You are clearing a space for something to arrive, or you are clearing a space so that nothing arrives, and knowing which is which is the entire discipline."

The students thought she was being metaphorical. She was not.

Today's lesson was the ledger of answered prayers—the one Sable kept, copied in Isolde's hand, every small miracle the gate had permitted since midwinter. There were forty-one entries. Isolde had vetted every one.

*Fever, broken, child of the miller.* Allowed. Fevers broke.

*Lame mule, walking.* Allowed with a wince. Mules recovered. Usually.

*Snow-melt arriving early, the high pastures.* That one had given Isolde a week of nightmares, because it was the kind of thing that started as a kindness and ended as a religion. She'd made Sable walk up to the pastures personally and *slow the melt back down*, and Sable had done it, and had come back gray-faced and furious and had not spoken to anyone for two days, and Isolde had sat with them and not spoken either, because that was also the discipline.

"Read me the last entry," she said to her students.

Toma, the cooper's boy, who was fifteen and too clever by half, read aloud: "*Prayer, spoken at the gate by an unknown traveler: 'Let me find my brother.' Answered: no. Note: the keeper found the brother themselves, in Lowmark, and sent word. The prayer was not necessary. The brother was found by ordinary means.*"

"And what do we learn from that?"

Toma frowned. "That the keeper did the god's job?"

"That the god's job," Isolde said, "is mostly a job a competent postman could do. That is the entire secret of the old faith, children, and the reason it was suppressed, and the reason it must never be *unsuppressed* in any official capacity. The moment prayer becomes policy, the answers become demands, and the demands become offerings, and the offerings—" She stopped herself. She always stopped herself there. Some lessons were for students, and some were for the long winter nights.

One of the pass children, a quiet girl named Brekka who had lost her family in the bad years, raised her hand. "Do you ever pray?" she asked. "Still? Even now?"

Isolde considered lying. She had been a liar for eleven years, professionally, and she was rather good at it.

"Every night," she said. "I pray that the gate holds. I pray that Sable sleeps. I pray that Renn's beans come up. And I pray that I never again hear an answer, because an answer means something on the other side is awake enough to give one." She looked around at their young, serious faces. "The old gods are not good. They are not evil. They are *large*, and they are *hungry for meaning*, and the only mercy they have ever shown is the mercy of being kept small. Your work, if you choose this work, is to be the smallness. To be the broom."

Brekka wrote that down. Isolde hoped, not for the first time, that the girl would become a carpenter instead.

That evening she walked down to the gate with Sable, as she did most evenings now, and stood in the antechamber while Sable pressed their palm to the stone and listened. The gate was quiet. The gate was usually quiet. Below, in the deep cell that had held Maro's bargain and now held nothing at all, the dark stayed dark.

"Anything?" Isolde asked, though she could read the answer in Sable's shoulders.

"Same as last night." Sable's voice was odd—careful, the way it always was when they talked about this. "Not silence. Something almost like—"

"Singing."

"Singing." Sable took their hand back from the stone. "It's not a hymn. It's not the Choir. It's not anything I know. It's just—" They searched for the word. "Patient."

"Is that worse or better than silence?"

Sable was quiet for a long moment. "I honestly don't know," they said. "I think it might be both."

Isolde nodded. She had spent her whole life being told what the gods were, first by the order and then by the order's absence, and the truest thing she had ever learned was that nobody knew, and that the not-knowing, held carefully, was the whole of the faith.

"Come to supper," she said. "Renn made stew. He made too much, because he always makes too much, because he's a sailor and sailors fear empty pots the way you fear open gates."

Sable laughed—really laughed—and for a moment the antechamber of the living gate was just a cold room with two old friends in it, walking back toward the light and the smell of onions.

### Sable

Sable's nights belonged to the gate.

This was the arrangement, and the arrangement was theirs, and they had built it the way they'd built every road through Thornfell: carefully, with an eye for where things might collapse. One hour, every night, palm to stone, listening. One hour in which they were not a smuggler, not a keeper of small prayers, not a person who argued with bean plants and overcharged merchants and pretended, for the health of everyone including themselves, to be ordinary. One hour in which they were what their grandmother's journal said the Sandovals had always been, underneath the ledgers and the lies: the thing that stood in the mouth.

The god did not speak. That was the first thing they had learned, and the most important. It *sang*, the way a cave sings, the way wind through a pass sings—not words, but the shape of words, meaning worn smooth by depth. And in that almost-singing, Sable had learned to hear the shape of the thing itself: vast, old, patient, and—this was the part that had taken them all winter to admit—not cruel. Not kind, either. It was a mountain's idea of a person. It had eaten a king because a king had offered himself like a meal. It had answered prayers because prayers were the sound of meaning, and it was starving for meaning, and it did not understand that a meal and a worshipper were different things.

Sable's job was to be the difference.

*Small,* they told it, every night, in the wordless language of the binding. *They are small, and you are small, and the singing stays small.*

And the singing, so far, stayed small.

Some nights it was harder. On the night the first spring thunder rolled down the valley, the singing swelled, and Sable stood at the stone for three hours instead of one, sweating, holding the gate the way you hold a door in a gale, and came back to the house at dawn to find Renn awake and waiting with tea he'd made twice because the first pot had gone cold.

"Bad night," Renn said. Not a question.

"Storm," Sable said. "It likes storms. It thinks they're singing back."

"And you told it—"

"I told it the storm is weather, and weather isn't worship, and if it starts answering thunder I will make its existence *miserable*." Sable wrapped both hands around the cup. "It laughed."

"It *laughed*?"

"Mountains laugh. It's a very bad sound. Like a rockslide with opinions." But Sable was smiling, tired and crooked, and Renn reached over and took their hand, and the gate and the god and the storm all receded to the edges of the room, where they belonged.

There was one more thing, and Sable had been putting off saying it, and this morning—thunder still muttering on the peaks, the beans coming up, the world absurdly, stubbornly green—they decided to stop putting it off.

"The singing changed last week," they said.

Renn set down his cup.

"Not louder. Not closer. Just—different. There are more voices in it. Or the same voices, arranged differently. And I've been listening every night since, trying to decide if it's the binding settling, or—" Sable looked at their own hand, the one that touched the stone, the one that had held the binding since midwinter. "Or if it's learning a new song."

"What kind of new song?"

"I don't know yet." Sable turned the cup slowly on the table. "But Isolde thinks we should write it down. Whatever we can hear of it. Before it finishes composing."

Renn was quiet for a moment. Then, in the voice he used for weather and other things that couldn't be argued with: "Then we'd better get Isolde a bigger notebook."

### The Valley

Spring came to Thornfell the way it always had, and also not.

The snow went early off the south faces and late off the north, which was correct, and the river ran high and clean, which was correct, and the valley village planted its fields and buried its dead and argued about the mill tax, which was all correct, all ordinary, all the small machinery of a world that had decided, without ever discussing it, to go on.

The village had also, quietly, changed its mind about the waystation.

It had happened without ceremony. In February the miller had sent his boy up with a sack of flour, no note. In March the cooper had come himself, hat in hand, to ask whether the keeper would look at a strange mark on his barn door—and Sable had looked at it, and said it was a woodpecker, and the cooper had laughed with relief and stayed for soup. By April there were three children who came up the path most afternoons on the pretext of delivering eggs, and who could be found, when the eggs were gone, sitting on the waystation steps listening to Isolde tell stories about the old days, which she edited heavily, and which were therefore mostly true.

Nobody said the word *keeper* in the village. They said *Sable*, the way you'd say a neighbor's name. That was the whole victory, and both of them knew it, and neither of them ever said so out loud.

At the midsummer fair—months yet, but already planned—the council had voted to invite the waystation to have a stall. Sable had read the invitation twice, gone very still, and then said, "What would I even *sell*," and Renn had said "Blessings," and Sable had thrown a bread roll at him.

### Sable

The last night of spring, Sable went down to the gate alone.

Renn was asleep. Isolde was in the village, teaching Brekka's mother to read, of all the small miracles. The waystation was dark behind them, the blue door shut, the ledger squared on its shelf. Sable carried a lamp down the descent road to the antechamber, set it in its niche, and put their palm against the stone.

The singing rose to meet them, as it always did.

Sable listened. The new voices were still there—had been there for nine nights now, woven into the old deep drone like a second melody line. And tonight, for the first time, Sable could almost pick the melody out. Almost follow it. It moved the way the smugglers' road-songs moved, the ones the network sang on the low road to keep time on the marches—call and answer, call and answer, a line offered and a line returned.

It was not the god's song. Sable was suddenly, coldly certain of that. The god's song was the drone underneath. This was something *above* it. Something the god was listening to.

Something the god was *learning from*.

Sable stood in the cold a long time, palm to stone, heart going hard and slow, and thought of their grandmother's journal, and of the first Sandoval who had invited a god in, and of every keeper since who had fed it just enough to stay quiet, and of the forty-one small prayers in Isolde's ledger, and of the mules that would be lame again by Thursday.

*What are you singing?* they asked the stone, in the wordless language of the binding.

And the gate—gentle, vast, patient, kept—answered with something that was not an answer, but was very close to a name.

Sable took their hand back. Stood in the lamplight. And then, because they were a keeper and a smuggler and a Sandoval, and because the first rule of every road they'd ever run was *know what's behind you before you open the next door*, they turned to the shelf where Isolde's copy of the ledger sat, took down a fresh notebook, and wrote, in a hand that did not shake:

*Night one. The singing has words now. It is learning them from us.*

Below, in the dark, something began, very softly, to hum.
