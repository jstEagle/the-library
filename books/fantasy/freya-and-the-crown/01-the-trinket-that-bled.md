## 1. The Trinket That Bled

The night market of Corvane opened the way a wound opens: slowly, reluctantly, and only after dark.

Ulyana Pemberton had worked it for twenty years, long enough to know its rhythms the way a sailor knows tides. The lantern-sellers came first, hooding their flames in smoked glass so the light fell amber and apologetic. Then the food carts, frying oil and charred onion, the smell of a city that fed itself on small comforts because it could afford nothing larger. Then, last and quietest, people like Ulyana—sellers of things that were not quite illegal, provided no one looked too closely at what they were.

Officially, of course, there was no such category as *not quite illegal*. There was law, and there was the Ministry of Unmaking, and between those two poles there was very little room for a person to stand. Magic had been abolished in Vessia two decades ago—not banned, which implied it still existed somewhere, but *abolished*, the way one abolishes a monarchy or a currency. The word had been chosen carefully. Ulyana had been sixteen when the edicts came down, an apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a guild sigil sewn into their collar, and they remembered thinking: *abolished*. As if you could legislate the tide out of the sea.

They had learned better since. You couldn't abolish magic, but you could starve it. You could erase a country from every map, strike its name from every tongue, dissolve its guilds and burn its god-houses and rename its rivers in the language of the Republic of Corvane-and-the-Provinces, until the gods who had lived on Vessian belief for three thousand years went hungry and thin and finally, blessedly, silent. That was the theory. That was what the Ministry's grey-coated clerks had spent twenty years ensuring.

And so Ulyana sold trinkets that did not work.

That was the trick of it, the beautiful legal sleight of hand that had kept them alive: the charms were inert. Dead brass and river-glass and knotted thread, pretty enough to hang at a wrist or a doorway, and utterly without power. A love charm that loved nothing. A luck-knot that held no luck. They sold because people wanted *the idea* of them—the shape of an older country, a souvenir of a nation that officially had never existed. Nostalgia was not against the law. Belief was.

Ulyana set out their tray on the usual corner, beneath the leaning eave of a shuttered chandlery, and arranged the stock with the muscle memory of two decades: copper sun-discs polished dull so they wouldn't catch an inspector's eye; braided red thread wound around splinters of rowan; little glass beads filled with ash from hearths that had burned before the Unmaking. Each piece carried a paper tag in Ulyana's careful hand: *Decorative. Traditional craft. No functional properties.* The tags were absurd and everyone knew it, and the absurdity was the point. It was a joke shared between seller and buyer and even, sometimes, the inspectors themselves—a wink passed down the whole length of a conquered country. *We all know these are nothing. Isn't it funny that we miss them anyway.*

Business was slow that night. Rain had come in off the estuary around dusk, a fine needle-fine rain that made the lantern light swim, and the market crowd huddled under awnings with their collars up, buying hot wine and moving on. Ulyana pulled their coat tighter and watched the water bead on the glass beads, and thought about rent, and thought about Sefa upstairs in her rooms over the chandlery, whose grandson had been coughing all week in the wet, and thought about nothing else at all, because thinking about anything larger was a habit they'd trained out of themselves years ago.

That was when they saw the light.

It came from the third row of the tray, from a charm Ulyana knew by touch better than their own hands: a small flat disc of greenish bronze, stamped with two interlocking rings, made twenty years ago in the last bright months of the guildhall. A love charm. One of the last true ones Ulyana had ever made, back when the words still sat in the metal like seeds in soil. They had sold it within a week of the abolition edicts, to a young woman with chapped hands who'd wanted it for a fisherman who never looked at her twice—and then the Ministry's cullers had come through the market with their null-irons, and everything Ulyana had left had gone dark and dumb, and stayed that way for twenty years.

Now the interlocking rings were glowing. Not shining—*weeping*. Rust-colored light seeped out of the stamping like blood through a bandage, slow and thick and warm against the rain-cold air, and where it touched the wet tray it hissed, faintly, like a coal remembering fire.

Ulyana's first instinct was to cover it. Their hand shot out and clapped over the disc, and the heat that came off it was wrong, was *alive*, was the exact temperature of a palm pressed to a fevered brow, and Ulyana snatched their fingers back as if bitten.

Around them, the market went on. Hot wine. Fried onions. Nobody else had seen. The light was small, guttering, visible only if you stood exactly where Ulyana stood—but it was *there*, and it was coming from Ulyana's own work, from a charm that had been legally, chemically, verifiably dead since before the fisherman's daughter's hair had gone grey.

"Working," Ulyana whispered, and the word tasted foreign in their mouth, a coin from a currency that no longer existed. "You're working."

Twenty years of craft-memory stirred in them like a sleeper turning over. They knew what this meant—or they knew what it would have meant, once, in the old vocabulary of the guild. Charms didn't wake on their own. Charms woke when something on the other end of them *answered*. A love charm bled rust-light when the god of hearthside affections—Vessa-of-the-Two-Rings, she'd been called, in the liturgy Ulyana's master had taught them—turned her attention toward it. Which meant somewhere, somehow, Vessa-of-the-Two-Rings was paying attention again.

Which meant the starving had ended. Or paused. Or—

"Don't," Ulyana told themselves aloud, barely a breath. "Don't think it. Don't even think it."

But hope is a charm too, and it works whether or not you believe in it.

* * *

The inspector came at half past the ninth bell.

Ulyana spotted the grey coat before the coat spotted anything: a wedge of institutional color moving down the market lane, parting the crowd the way a keel parts water. Inspector Ondrej Vasik. Ulyana knew him by sight and by reputation—he worked this district, methodical, humorless, the kind of man who read the paper tags on decorative charms with genuine attention. Twice a year he stopped at Ulyana's corner. Twice a year he found nothing, because there was nothing to find.

Tonight there was something to find, and it was sitting in the third row, weeping rust-colored light into the rain.

Ulyana's hands moved before their mind caught up. Tray tilted, sleeve swept, and the love charm dropped into the hidden pocket sewn along the tray's underside—the smuggler's channel every market veteran cut into their goods, the little lie built into the furniture of survival. The remaining charms rattled innocently. Copper sun-discs. Ash-beads. Nothing.

Vasik arrived like weather. He was a broad, grey man with a face like a filed-down nail, and he stood over the tray for a long moment while the rain dripped off the brim of his hat.

"Cold night for traditional craft," he said.

"Cold night for everything, Inspector."

"Mm." He picked up a sun-disc, turned it, set it down. His eyes moved across the rows with the flat patience of a man counting sheep he intended eventually to slaughter. "You've been at this corner a long time, Pemberton."

"Twenty years."

"Long time." He set another disc down, slightly out of alignment with the others, and watched to see if Ulyana would straighten it. Ulyana did not. "Guild records are being reviewed, you know. Old ones. Pre-Unmaking." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man mentions the price of bread. "Apprentice rolls. Ledgers. Names."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the lane a cart wheel screamed against its axle.

"I was sixteen," Ulyana said. "I braided thread and polished brass. Whatever my master signed, I signed nothing."

"No," Vasik agreed. "You signed nothing. That's rather the interesting part." He straightened his coat. "Keep your tags current, Pemberton. No functional properties. Say it with me—it's almost a prayer, isn't it? *No functional properties.*" And he smiled, a small dry crack in the filed-down face, and moved on down the lane, and Ulyana stood very still under the eave with their heart going like a trapped bird, and did not breathe properly until the grey coat had dissolved into the crowd.

Under the tray, in the smuggler's pocket, the love charm pulsed once against the wood. Warm. Patient. Like something waiting to be found.

* * *

The stranger came an hour later, when the market was thinning and the lantern-sellers had begun to hood their flames for the night.

He was big—not tall so much as *built*, a man put together out of shoulders and forearms, with a clerk's pallor warring against a laborer's frame, as though he'd been one thing and had tried very hard to become another and hadn't quite managed either. He wore a plain wool coat gone shiny at the elbows, and he stood at the tray a long time without touching anything, and when he finally spoke his voice was low and roughened, like a page kept too long under a weight.

"The green bronze," he said. "The two rings. You have one?"

Ulyana's mouth went dry. "Sold out."

"You haven't." The man's eyes came up, and they were pale grey, and they were not unkind, which frightened Ulyana more than cruelty would have. "I've walked this market three nights looking. I know what I'm asking for."

Every instinct Ulyana owned said *walk away*. Every instinct except one—the oldest one, the apprentice one, the one that had lain dormant for twenty years and had begun, tonight, to itch like a healed wound in changing weather.

"Who told you?" Ulyana asked quietly.

"No one. That's the point of prayers, isn't it? No one tells. You just—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "My wife has been gone eleven years. Tonight I said her name out loud, into the kitchen dark, the way you're not supposed to anymore. And the candle *bent toward me*. Like it was listening." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "I'm a grown man telling a trinket-seller about a candle. But you looked at your own tray tonight the way I felt in my kitchen. I saw it from across the lane. Something on your table woke up."

The rain ticked on the eave. Down the lane, the last lantern-seller hooded his final flame, and the market sank into ember-dark.

Ulyana reached under the tray, into the smuggler's pocket, and brought out the love charm. In the open air, away from the tray's shadow, it wept more freely—a thin runnel of rust-colored light tracing the interlocking rings, dripping warmth onto Ulyana's palm like a pulse.

The man made a sound low in his chest. Not words. Recognition.

"It's twenty years old," Ulyana said. "Made before the Unmaking, in the guildhall, by my own hands. It should be dead. Everything I ever made should be dead. If this is awake, then—" They stopped, because finishing the sentence felt like stepping off a roof.

"Then someone's answering," the man said simply. He fumbled in his coat and produced coins—too many coins, a small fortune in worn republic silver—and pressed them into Ulyana's hand and closed their fingers over them with a grip like a door being shut. "For my wife's sister. She's alone now. She needs—" He shook his head. "She needs to be loved from somewhere."

Ulyana gave him the charm. The moment it left their palm, the light steadied, as if content—as if *delivered*—and the man cradled it in both big hands like a bird with a broken wing, and looked at Ulyana with those pale grey eyes.

"They told us it was abolished," he said. "Twenty years they told us. And tonight I bent a candle with my wife's name." He leaned close, and the rain ran down between them, and his whisper was almost lost in it—almost, but not quite:

"Prayers are being answered, charmwright. All of them. Everywhere. And nobody knows yet what the gods want in return."

Then he was gone, into the dark and the rain, carrying twenty years of dead craft burning alive in his cupped hands.

Ulyana stood alone under the eave with a tray full of waking things and a pocket full of silver and the taste of dread and hope in equal measure, which is, they would learn, exactly how a debt arrives—quietly, warmly, glowing rust-red in the dark, asking only to be acknowledged.

Above the chandlery, in Sefa's rooms, a child coughed. And Ulyana, without deciding to, without permitting it, prayed—for the first time in twenty years, one word, small and terrified and irretrievable:

*Please.*

Somewhere far away, something enormous turned over in its sleep, and began, gently, to listen.
