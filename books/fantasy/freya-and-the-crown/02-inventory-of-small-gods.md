## 2. Inventory of Small Gods

Ulyana did not sleep that night. Sleep would have required believing the night market had been a trick of lamplight and exhaustion, and Ulyana had spent twenty years learning to tell the difference between a trick and a truth, because their whole livelihood depended on it.

The charm sat on the worktable where they'd set it down, still weeping. Rust-colored light gathered at its seam like blood at a wound's edge, welled, slid down the curve of the tin heart, and vanished before it touched the wood. It had been doing this for six hours. Ulyana had watched every minute of it with the particular horror of a locksmith watching a lock pick itself.

They had made that piece in their second year of trade. A love charm, commissioned by a ferryman named Osk who wanted to be brave enough to speak to a woman at the rope-ferry crossing. Ulyana remembered the making of it perfectly: cheap tin, a drop of tallow, three words of guild-cant whispered over the cooling metal purely for theater. The words meant nothing. They had never meant anything. The Charmwrights' Guild had been dead two years by then, its hall sealed with wax and law, and Ulyana—seventeen, hungry, alone in a country that no longer officially existed—had learned quickly that people would pay for the *shape* of hope even when the substance was gone.

The charm had never worked. Osk had come back once, sheepish, to say the woman had married a baker anyway, and Ulyana had refunded half and told him love was mostly timing. That was the craft as they knew it: honest fraud, tender fraud, fraud with a money-back policy on the worst outcomes.

And now the fraud was bleeding light onto their table.

Ulyana picked it up. The metal was warm—not sun-warm, not pocket-warm, but warm the way a hand is warm, warm from *inside*, warm like something alive and small and patient. When they held it to their ear, beneath the hiss of the lamp, there was a sound like very distant singing. Or breathing. Or someone saying a name over and over, so far away the name had worn smooth as a river stone.

"Osk," Ulyana said aloud, testing. The light flared.

Not Osk's name, then. Something that had heard Osk's name, twenty years ago, through three dead words of cant and a lie about love—and had *kept it*.

Ulyana set the charm down very gently, the way one sets down a sleeping animal or an accusation, and went to the shelf behind the loose brick in the pantry wall where they kept the ledger.

* * *

The ledger was not a guild document. Guild documents got you hanged. It was a grocer's daybook, bought for three coppers, in which Ulyana had recorded two decades of sales in a cipher of their own devising: item, date, district, buyer's first name only, and a small note of what the buyer had wanted. *Tin heart — Osk — courage to speak.* *Bone button — Maretta — her son home from the coast.* *Copper ring — unnamed soldier — to forget.* *Glass eye — child, didn't catch the name — to see fairies.* On and on, four hundred entries, maybe five hundred. Five hundred small hopes sold at market rates to a city that had outlawed hoping.

Ulyana read the whole ledger by lamplight, and then, as the sky went grey at the edges, began pulling stock.

The back room held the unsold inventory: forty-odd pieces in a cedar box, the dregs of two decades, things too ugly or too specific to move. Ulyana laid them out on the floorboards in rows, and by the time the grey light turned gold, every single one was glowing.

A knucklebone carved with a spiral, meant to bring rain: dripping water that fell upward. A scrap of blue ribbon, sold as protection against nightmares: humming a lullaby in a voice Ulyana did not know and could not quite bear to listen to. A bent iron nail, "for mending quarrels": too hot to touch, though the floorboards around it were cold. Each piece carried a different quality of attention, the way different windows carry different views of the same street. Some felt vast and slow, like weather deciding. One—the glass eye, god help them—felt *curious*, and turned slowly in its row to watch Ulyana work.

Every charm they had ever made was awake. Which meant every charm they had ever *sold* was awake, scattered through the districts of a city that executed residual practitioners, sitting in drawers and on mantels and around necks, glowing, humming, listening—

Listening to what?

Ulyana sat down hard on the floor among the small gods of their own manufacture and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they were going to have to visit every name in that ledger.

* * *

Sefa lived downstairs, which is to say Ulyana lived upstairs, in the rooms above Sefa's ground-floor flat, and had done for eleven years under an arrangement neither of them had ever put into words. Sefa took rent late without comment. Ulyana fixed her stair rail, carried her coal, and ate her cooking often enough that it barely counted as charity in either direction. She was sixty, built like a dock bollard, with forearms that had kneaded bread for forty years and opinions she applied to Ulyana's life with the same rhythm.

She opened the door before Ulyana finished knocking, which was normal; Sefa heard footsteps on her stairs the way sailors hear weather.

"You look like something the tide refused," she said. "Sit. There's eggs."

"Sefa." Ulyana stayed standing in the doorway, hands in pockets, and heard their own voice come out wrong—too careful. "I need to ask you something, and I need you not to ask me why I'm asking."

Sefa looked at them for a long moment. Then she stepped aside. "That bad?"

"Worse than bad. Possibly impossible." Ulyana came in but didn't sit. The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and onions and the particular mineral smell of the old kettle, and it was, Ulyana realized suddenly, the most real room they knew. "Eleven years ago. Did you ever buy anything from me? At the night market, or—"

"Eleven years ago I bought a charm off a skinny little ghost of a person who charged me too much and blushed the whole time." Sefa cracked eggs into a pan without looking away from Ulyana's face. "Blue bead on a cord. For Tam."

Tam. Tamir. The grandson. Ulyana found the entry in memory without opening the ledger: *blue bead — Sefa — the boy's lungs.* The boy had been born with breath like a paper whistle; the physicians of the republic had prescribed steam and patience and, privately, grief.

"What happened," Ulyana said. Not a question. They couldn't make it a question.

"He got better." Sefa turned the eggs. Her voice was level in the way of someone walking carefully across ice. "That winter was bad. He was in my bed for a month, coughing like an old man, and I sat up with him every night, and one night I couldn't stand it anymore and I took that bead in my fist and I prayed."

"You don't pray. Nobody prays. There's nothing to—" Ulyana stopped. Saying it aloud, in this kitchen, to this woman, it sounded obscene.

"I know what there isn't." Sefa plated the eggs, set them on the table, and pointed at a chair with the authority of a woman who had raised children through two governments. "*Sit.*"

Ulyana sat.

"I prayed to whoever used to be listened to. My grandmother's gods, the ones from before the maps changed. I didn't know any names. I just—I held the bead and I begged. And I'm telling you, Ulyana, the room *changed*. Not lightning, not voices. It was like the air got *interested*. Like something leaned in close to hear me better." She sat down across the table. Her hands, those great capable hands, were not entirely steady. "Three days later Tam's fever broke. The physician said he'd never seen lungs clear like that. Said it was a miracle, and then laughed, because there's no such thing, and wrote 'spontaneous remission' in his book."

"And the bead?"

"In my sewing box." Sefa stood, went to the bureau in the corner, and came back with a battered tin that had once held tea. Inside, on a fold of cloth, lay a blue glass bead on a fraying cord.

It was glowing. Softly, steadily, the color of deep evening water, and it pulsed—slow, rhythmic, unmistakable.

Like breathing. Like small, healthy lungs filling and emptying, filling and emptying, somewhere far away, being *listened to*.

Ulyana reached out and stopped their own hand an inch above it. "Don't touch it," they heard themselves say. "Or—or do. I don't know. I don't know anything anymore, Sefa. I sold you a lump of glass and a lie, and something *answered you through it*, and I need you to understand that I did not do that. I couldn't have. I've never done anything. Every piece I ever sold was empty. I was a fraud. I was a *kind* fraud, I told myself, because hope is cheaper than medicine and I gave discounts—but it was all theater, all of it, and now it's *awake*, all of it, and I don't—" Their voice cracked. Eleven years of composure, gone over eggs. "I don't know what I am if the charms were real. I don't know what I was *for*."

Sefa let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to. Then she pushed the plate of eggs six inches toward Ulyana, an order disguised as a courtesy.

"Eat," she said. "Then you'll tell me what woke them up, and what it wants, and how much trouble my grandson is in for breathing easy these eleven years. And then, Ulyana Pemberton, you and I are going to figure out what you owe."

"That's just it." Ulyana stared at the eggs, at the bead pulsing between them like a third guest at the table. "I spent twenty years thinking the guild left me nothing but a trade and a hiding place. But if the charms were never mine—if they were *payments*, if everything I sold was some kind of account—then I don't owe a debt, Sefa. I *am* one. I'm the last line in a ledger somebody else defaulted on, and whatever's waking up out there has come to collect."

Outside, far off toward the Ministry quarter, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not the fire bell. The third bell, the one that hadn't sounded since Ulyana was a child—the one that meant, in the old language of a country erased from every map, that the wards had been disturbed, and the clerks of Unmaking were moving.

Sefa looked at the window. Then at Ulyana. Then, with terrible calm, she took the blue bead out of the tin and dropped it into Ulyana's palm.

"Then you'd best finish your eggs," she said, "and start keeping better books."
