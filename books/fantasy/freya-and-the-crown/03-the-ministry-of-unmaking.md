## 3. The Ministry of Unmaking

Brannic Oss had spent eleven years learning how a country could be killed with paperwork.

Not with armies. Armies were loud, and loud things made martyrs, and martyrs made believers, and believers were the one crop the republic could not afford to grow. No—the Ministry of Unmaking killed with forms. With ledgers. With a slow, patient erosion of names.

He had filed the forms himself.

He sat now in the back room of the Lamplighter's Rest, a tavern in the Saltward district where the ceilings sweated brine and the ale tasted like regret, and he watched Ulyana Pemberton across a table scarred by forty years of elbows. The charmwright was turning the love charm over in their fingers—the one they'd sold that night in the market, the one that had wept rust-colored light—and their face wore an expression Brannic recognized because he had once worn it himself, at a desk, under a lamp, at the moment he understood what the documents in his hands actually did.

It was the look of a person reading their own obituary and finding it accurate.

"You said you were a clerk," Ulyana said. "Clerks don't talk like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a man confessing."

Brannic turned his cup. The ale moved in a slow brown circle. "I was a clerk," he said. "Third Grade, Bureau of Cartographic Integrity. Do you know what that bureau did?"

"Made maps?"

"Unmade them." He let that sit. Outside, a fishmonger was crying her wares in the flat, toneless voice the city preferred—loud enough to sell, quiet enough not to be noticed. In Vessia, before, the fishwives had sung. He remembered the songs. He was not supposed to remember the songs. "Sit down properly, Pemberton. What I have to tell you is long, and you're going to want to stand up and leave halfway through, and I'd rather you didn't."

Ulyana sat. They were thinner than they'd been at the market, he thought. Fear was eating them from the inside, the way it ate everyone the Ministry touched.

"Twenty-two years ago," Brannic began, "the Republic of Corvane fought a war it was losing. Not against Vessia. Against the gods. Or rather—against the *idea* of gods. Every prayer answered in Vessia was a prayer not answered in Corvane. Every harvest blessed in the hill country was a harvest the Corvanish plains couldn't match. You understand? The gods of Vessia were winning an argument simply by existing."

"So they invaded."

"They *dissolved* us." He said it flatly, because the horror was in the flatness. "No annexation. Annexation leaves a wound, and wounds remember. Instead, the Ministry drafted the Erasure Accord. Legally, formally, with seals and ribbons and a ceremony attended by four hundred dignitaries, the nation of Vessia ceased to have ever existed. Not conquered. Not absorbed. *Erased.* The borders were redrawn. The archives were burned. The language was reclassified as a 'regional dialect of no historical standing.' The names of the towns were reassigned. And the people—" He stopped. Started again. "The people were issued new histories. You were born in Corvane. You had always been born in Corvane. Your grandmother had never sung to the river. Your grandfather had never left bread on the doorstep for the hearth-god. There was no hearth-god. There had never been a hearth-god. There had never been a doorstep."

Ulyana's hand had gone still around the charm.

"And the gods?"

"Starved." Brannic's voice came out lower than he intended. "That was the whole design, Pemberton. The Ministry didn't ban magic. Banning is a fight, and fights feed gods—fear is worship, anger is worship, even hatred is worship if it's aimed at something divine. No. We did something worse. We made the gods *unmentionable*. A god with no believers, no prayers, no name spoken aloud, no festivals, no graves tended, no children told its stories—such a god doesn't die. It thins. It goes quiet. It becomes a rumor, then a dream, then nothing. The Erasure Accord was a famine, and we were the ones who locked the granaries."

The lamp between them guttered. Brannic found himself watching it the way a man watches a candle in a room where the door won't latch.

"I processed the guild records," he said.

Ulyana looked up.

"Charmwrights, spellbinders, bone-readers, the whole constellation of licensed practitioners. The Ministry didn't execute them—that would have been mercy, and it would have been *worship*, wouldn't it, fear of the mage burned at the stake. Instead we dissolved their charters. We ruled that their craft had never been licensed, because there had never been a guild, because there had never been a Vessia to license it. Their work was retroactively classified as fraud. Their names went into the Index of Residual Practitioners." He met Ulyana's eyes. "Yours is in that index. It has been for twenty years. Filed under apprentices, subcategory: collateral."

"Collateral," Ulyana repeated. The word came out strange, as if they'd found it on the floor and didn't know what it was for.

"Every guild charter had a surety clause. The last apprentice of record was bound to the guild's obligations. When the Charmwrights' charter was dissolved, the obligation didn't dissolve with it. It transferred." Brannic turned the cup again. "I know how that sounds. I filed it. I remember the exact weight of the page. Fourteen grams. I remember because I weighed it, afterward, in my hand, at my desk, at night, when I couldn't sleep. Fourteen grams of paper, and it was the heaviest thing I have ever held."

* * *

He told them the rest in the alley behind the tavern, because he could not say it under a roof. Roofs had ears; he knew this professionally. The Saltward district smelled of kelp and tar and the low tide's old grief, and the gulls screamed overhead like informants, and Brannic walked with his collar up and his shoulders curved, a man trying to make himself smaller than his guilt.

"The records surfaced," he said. "All of them. Three nights ago, the sealed archive in the Ministry subvault—the one holding the Charmwrights' charter, the guild ledger, the surety filings—opened. By itself. The locks were found intact and unforced. The doors were found open. And every document inside was found *glowing*."

"Glowing."

"Rust-colored." He watched Ulyana's face do something complicated. "The same color as your charm, I'm told. The Ministry's diviners couldn't read the pages. Ink that used to say *fraud* now says something else, in a hand nobody recognizes, and the clerks who try to copy it come away with headaches and, in two cases, with prayers they don't remember learning. The Director has classified the archive as an active containment breach. Which means—" he exhaled, "—the hunters are coming. Not inspectors. Not clerks like I was. Unmakers. The ones who don't file forms. The ones who make sure that after they visit, there's no form left to file."

They stood at the mouth of the alley, where the lamplight gave out and the harbor-dark began. Somewhere a ship's bell counted the hour, and Brannic thought, not for the first time, that the bell was the only honest thing left in the city. Bells didn't care what country they rang in.

"Why are you telling me this?" Ulyana asked. "You don't know me. You bought a charm off a market stall. You could have walked away."

"I could have." Brannic looked at his own hands. Clerk's hands, soft at the fingertips, ink-stained in the creases in a way that never washed out—twenty years of scrubbing and the ghost of the ink still lived in him, the way the ghost of Vessia still lived in the hills, in the old women who still set out bread, in the fishermen who still, when the storms came, said words under their breath that had no Corvanish translation. "Eleven years I filed those forms. I told myself it was just paper. Paper doesn't kill anyone. Paper doesn't burn anyone. And then one day I pulled a file—a routine file, a fisherman in the north villages who'd been reported for leaving offerings at a boundary stone—and I read the follow-up, and there was no follow-up, because the man didn't exist anymore. Not dead. *Unrecorded.* His house was reassigned. His wife's marriage certificate was voided. His children's birth records were amended. He had never been born. He had never lived. And I sat at my desk with his file in my hands and I understood that the paper *does* kill, Pemberton. It kills everything except the body, and then the body just walks around empty, and everyone pretends that's peace."

The gulls wheeled. The tide breathed against the pilings.

"I kept one file," Brannic said. "One. I stole it the night I resigned—resigned, not defected; there's a difference, and the difference is that defectors get hunted and I only got *watched*. I kept the Charmwrights' surety filing. Fourteen grams. I have read it so many times I could recite it. And three nights ago, when the archive opened and the reports came down to the watchers' desks, I read the name on the breach order, and it was the name I'd carried in my pocket for eleven years like a stone." He reached into his coat and set the file on a bollard between them, there in the dark, where no roof could hear. "Ulyana Pemberton. Last apprentice of record. Collateral."

Ulyana didn't touch it. Their hands had gone to their own pockets, the way a person checks for a knife, or a prayer.

"The guild," they said slowly, "has been dead for twenty years. My master is dead. The hall is sealed. What obligation could possibly—"

"The gods are waking up," Brannic said. "Your charms are working. The archive opened itself. Something is coming to collect, and the paperwork says it's coming to collect from *you*." He paused, and the shame in him, the old shame, the eleven-year shame, made the next words come out rough. "I filed the instrument, Pemberton. The least I can do is help you survive it."

Ulyana looked at the file a long moment. Then past him, out at the black water, where the horizon held no border and no name and no country at all—only the dark, and the rain beginning, and far off, so faint it might have been a trick of the wind, the sound of a bell ringing in a village that was not on any map.

"Then we'd better start," they said, "with my master's ledger."

Brannic felt the words land in his chest like a key turning in a lock he'd forgotten he carried. The guildhall. Sealed twenty years. He had processed *that* order too.

"You understand," he said, "that if we're seen there—"

"I understand." Ulyana picked up the file at last, and tucked it away, and for just a moment their fingers brushed the rust-colored charm in their pocket, and the light of it leaked through the weave like a heartbeat under skin. "But if the gods are waking up, then someone owes them something. And I want to know," Ulyana Pemberton said, "exactly what my name was used to buy."
