## 4. What the Guild Owed

The guildhall had not been sealed so much as *swallowed*. It stood at the end of Chandler's Row where the street gave up pretending to be a street and became a throat of old stone, and the Ministry's wax seals—twenty years of them, layered like scar tissue over a wound—had gone the color of old honey. Ulyana stood across from it in the shadow of a shuttered chandler's shop, breathing the smell of tallow and rain, and felt their own heartbeat behave badly.

They had walked past this building four thousand times. They had never once looked up.

Now they looked up, and the looking hurt. The guild sign still hung above the door, wrought iron shaped into a hand holding a flame, though the flame had been hammered flat by some diligent clerk with a chisel and a mandate. Below it, in letters that had once been gilded: *THE HONORABLE GUILD OF CHARMWRIGHTS, VESSIA.* The word *Vessia* had been scraped to a ghost. You could only read it if you already knew it was there—which, Ulyana thought, was probably the point. That was how you erased a country. Not by burning it, but by making its name legible only to the people who grieved it.

"You're doing the thing," Brannic murmured beside them. He had come dressed as what he no longer was—a gray clerk's coat, a ledger-case under his arm—and it was such a good disguise precisely because it was true. "Standing very still and rehearsing your own funeral."

"I'm deciding which seal to break first."

"The small one. Bottom left." He said it without hesitation, and then flinched at his own fluency. "I filed the sealing order. I know the—there's an inspection rotation. We have until the second bell after midnight."

Ulyana looked at him. In the dark his face was all angles and apology. "You filed it," they said. Not a question. They had known since the kitchen, since he'd said *I processed that order too*, but saying it aloud in front of the building itself made it heavier.

"I was nineteen. I needed the wage." Brannic's voice did something complicated. "I told myself the guild was already dead. That I was just... closing a door on an empty room." He swallowed. "It wasn't an empty room, was it."

"No," Ulyana said. "I was in it."

* * *

Inside, the guildhall smelled of dust and cold ash and, beneath both, faintly and impossibly, of myrrh—the way a church smells twenty years after the last service, when the walls have soaked up so much prayer that even abandonment can't wring it out. Ulyana's lantern found the great workroom first: forty benches, forty vices, the long stone troughs where molten charm-metal had been poured, all of it furred gray with stillness. Their boots left prints in the dust, and the prints were the loudest thing in the room.

They had expected grief. What they got instead was recognition, which was worse. Here was the bench where Master Odile had taught them to draw a warding-sigil without lifting the stylus. There was the corner where the apprentices slept, where Ulyana had spent nine years listening to eleven other children breathe. The hooks by the door where apprentice cloaks had hung were empty, but Ulyana could have named every cloak.

"Ulyana." Brannic's voice came thin from the master's office. "You should see this."

The office door had been sealed separately, with a black wax stamped by the Office of Dissolution itself. Brannic broke it with his thumb like a man killing something small and sick, and they went in together, and the lantern light fell across the master's desk—

—and onto a ledger bound in leather the color of dried blood, lying open, as if someone had meant to come back for it.

"It should have been confiscated," Brannic whispered. "Everything was confiscated. Inventoried, catalogued—" He stopped. He was reading the open page upside down, and whatever he saw there made him sit down slowly on a stool built for a much younger person. "Oh," he said. "Oh, no. Ulyana, this isn't a ledger of sales."

Ulyana picked it up.

The handwriting was Master Odile's—tight, slanted, economical, the hand that had corrected ten thousand of Ulyana's practice sigils. But the columns were wrong. Where a guild ledger should have listed clients and prices, it listed names Ulyana half-knew from stories their mother had refused to tell: *Veyra-of-the-Long-Rain. The Tallyman. Our Lady of Small Returns. The Kindler.* And against each name, entries in two columns. One headed **PAID**. One headed **OWED**.

And under OWED, in every single row, in the same tight hand, the same notation:

*Deferred. Collateral held: one (1) apprentice, Pemberton, U., age 6.*

Ulyana read it again. And again. The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the walls, water moved through old pipes with a sound like whispering.

"They didn't buy anything," they heard themselves say. Their voice sounded like someone else's, someone standing behind them. "The charms. Twenty years of charms. We weren't selling protection or luck or love. We were making *payments*. Installments." The page trembled; Ulyana steadied it with fingers that would not quite obey. "Every charm I ever soldered was a coin. A little piece of a god's attention, minted and handed over, and the client was just—the hand it passed through."

"That's why they're waking up," Brannic said quietly. "If the payments stop—if the guild defaulted—then everything the gods ever touched starts calling in its debts. Every charm. Every blessing. Everything with a scrap of them in it." He looked up, and his clerk's face had gone the color of the wax he'd broken. "Ulyana. What does 'collateral' mean? In a contract like this?"

Ulyana turned pages. Faster now. Past decades of entries, past other apprentices' names in the collateral column—struck through, one by one, each strike accompanied by a date and a single word: *redeemed*, or *forgiven*, or, twice, in a hand shakier than the rest, *lost*. Until they reached the last living entry, the ink still sharp, the only column never struck through.

**Collateral held: one (1) apprentice, Pemberton, U. Term: until majority + 20 yrs. Purpose: surety against full default. Upon default, collateral passes to creditor(s) in satisfaction of outstanding balance.**

Twenty years. Ulyana did arithmetic they did not want to do. Six years old when the guild fell. Six plus twenty.

"This year," they said. "My birthday is in the spring. I'm the term of the contract. I wasn't an apprentice." The words came out flat, factual, terrible. "I was an escrow account. She kept me alive because as long as I was alive and unclaimed, the debt stayed deferred. And when the term ran out—" They closed the ledger. Opened it again, because closing it felt like a lie. "When the term ran out, the whole balance comes due. All of it. Every god. At once."

Brannic said nothing. There was nothing clerical left in him to say; the forms and procedures had run out, and underneath them was just a man in a cold room watching someone learn they had been currency.

And then Ulyana found the last page. Not a column. A letter, folded into the binding, written in Master Odile's hand but slower, older, pressed hard enough to tear the paper—as if she'd been writing against time, or against a deadline she could see coming down the street like weather.

*Little wren,* it began. *If you are reading this, then the term has nearly run, and I am sorry, and sorry is a worthless coin, I know. Hear me. We never chose to be Charmwrights. The gods chose us to be their paymasters, and we chose, in the end, to stop paying. Not out of wickedness. Out of love. Every charm we sold bought a village a harvest, a mother a fever broken, a child a night without nightmares—and the price was always going to be collected from us, from our hands, our names, our children. So we defaulted. We let them starve rather than feed them you.*

*But debts do not die. They sleep. And something has woken them.*

*There is a Door. Ash on the hinges, fire in the frame. It is the one way out of Vessia, and the one way back in, and the gods will stand before it before you do. Whatever you decide—run, pay, burn the ledger—decide it as yourself. Not as collateral. You were never the payment, little wren. You were always the reason we withheld it.*

Ulyana read it three times. On the third reading, their vision blurred, and they realized distantly that they were crying—not sobbing, just leaking, the way the charm in their pocket leaked rust-colored light, steadily, without drama, because the pressure inside had nowhere else to go.

Twenty years. Twenty years of trinkets that didn't work, of hawking dead metal in night markets, of believing themselves the last shabby survivor of a failed trade, a fraud, a footnote. And the truth was that the failure had been a choice—the most enormous, most loving act of refusal in the history of a country that no longer officially existed—and Ulyana had been its beating heart, its held breath, its reason.

"I thought I was nothing," they said. "I thought I was what was left over."

"You're not leftover," Brannic said. "You're *principal*."

That startled a laugh out of them, wet and cracked, and the laugh broke something loose, and for a moment they simply stood in their master's office, in the smell of myrrh and dust, holding a ledger full of gods and a letter full of love, grieving and furious and—gods help them—proud.

Then the lantern guttered. Not from wind. From *attention*—the flame bending, all at once, toward the doorway, like a dog turning its head.

In the workroom beyond, the dust on forty benches began to rise, gently, all of it at once, hanging in the air as if the room had remembered gravity was optional. And in the troughs where molten charm-metal had once been poured, something rust-colored began to glow—faint, patient, spreading—lighting the guildhall from below like coals waking in a banked fire.

Brannic stood very slowly. "Second bell's not for an hour," he whispered. "This isn't the Ministry."

"No," Ulyana said. Their hand found the ledger, and the letter, and tucked both inside their coat, against their heart, where collateral apparently lived. "It's the creditors. They've come to inspect their asset."

The glow brightened. And from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like many coins being counted, something in the guildhall said Ulyana's name.
