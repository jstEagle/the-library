## 5. Found Family, Assembled Badly

Sefa had fed strangers before. She had fed boarders and beggars, nephews of dubious provenance, a tax assessor who wept into her lentils and confessed to falsifying her neighbor's levy. But she had never fed a table like this one: a fugitive charmwright who kept flinching at their own hands as if the hands belonged to someone else; a broad-shouldered stranger in a clerk's grey coat with the buttons torn off; a hollow-eyed man who kept laying papers on the table and then snatching them back as though they might bite; and a girl of perhaps fifteen who had eaten four rolls already and was eyeing the fifth.

"You'll burst," Sefa said, sliding the plate out of reach.

"I'll *grow*," said the girl, whose name was Wren and whose fingers were quicker than any charm Ulyana had ever sold. "You want me sharp, you feed me."

"Nobody's asked you to be here," Brannic rumbled.

"Nobody's asked anybody to be here." Wren stole the roll anyway, mid-sentence, a thing Sefa would have sworn was impossible. "That's what makes it a family instead of a job."

Ulyana laughed — a short, startled sound, like a bird hitting glass — and then pressed both palms flat against the table as if to hold themselves still. Sefa watched them do it and understood something she had been circling for two days: Ulyana Pemberton did not know how to be looked at kindly. Twenty years of selling broken miracles in the dark will teach a person that every gaze is either a customer's or an inspector's, and neither is kind.

So Sefa did the only thing she knew. She ladled more stew.

* * *

Brannic sat with his back to the wall because twenty-two years in the Ministry taught your spine things your mind refused to learn. The kitchen smelled of cumin and woodsmoke and, beneath it, faintly, of rust — the smell the charms made now, the smell of old iron remembering it was once blood-warm. He catalogued exits out of habit: the back door through the scullery, the window over the sink too narrow for him but fine for the girl, the roofline beyond it broken enough to climb.

He should not have come. Every hour he spent here was an hour his former colleagues spent narrowing a search grid around a night market stall, a boarding house, a name. *Ulyana Pemberton, apprentice of record, guild registry 4471.* He had pulled the file himself, years ago, when he still believed the Ministry's work was arithmetic. He had stamped it *dormant* and slept well afterward, which was the part that shamed him now — not the stamping but the sleeping.

"The Ministry doesn't send soldiers first," he said, because they needed to hear it plainly. "It sends paper. A summons. Then a survey team to assess the property. Then, if the property is deemed *residual*—" he used the word the way you'd spit out a seed "—the unmaking crew. They don't arrest people, mostly. Arrest implies you exist. They *edit* you. Registry, tenancy records, family rolls. By the time anyone comes to the door, there is no one at the door to answer it."

Wren had stopped chewing. Even Ivo's pen had gone quiet.

"They came for my master that way?" Ulyana asked. Their voice was careful, level, the voice of someone holding a full cup while walking a tightrope.

"Worse. Your whole guild was edited in one night. Ninety-one names." Brannic turned his cup slowly on the table. "I know because I filed the confirmation. I was nineteen. I thought I was filing weather reports."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a kitchen that officially does not exist, eating stew with a residual practitioner, waiting for the Ministry to notice I've stopped coming to work." He met Ulyana's eyes. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you what's behind us, so nobody looks backward by accident."

Sefa refilled his cup without being asked. It was such a small thing. It undid him more than any accusation could have.

* * *

Ivo had been a cartographer for eleven years, which meant he had been a professional liar for eleven years, and the lies were the only honest work he'd ever done. He spread his papers across Sefa's table now — between the bread and the salt cellar, under Wren's thieving hand — and let them look at what he had stolen from his own office on the night he defrocked himself.

Blank maps. That was what they saw first, and he watched them see it: sheets of fine vellum ruled with rivers that went nowhere, roads that looped like dropped thread, a country-shaped absence where a country used to be.

"This is Vessia," he said. "As the Ministry draws it. As the Ministry has drawn it since the Erasure. Look longer."

They looked. Sefa's brow furrowed first — she was the one among them who trusted her own senses over official paper, having survived three governments by doing so. "The blank parts aren't blank," she said. "There's writing underneath. Like a palimpsest."

"Bleed-through," Ivo agreed. "The Erasure was legal, not physical. You can legislate a nation out of existence, but ink remembers. And so does everything else." He drew from his coat the thing he had not shown anyone: a small stoppered vial of ink so dark it seemed to drink the lamplight. "Blood-ink. Pig's blood, lampblack, ash from a shrine candle, and — this is the part that got me stripped of rank — a spoken prayer over the mixing bowl. Old guild formula. I found it in a confiscated Charmwrights' manual I was supposed to burn."

"You didn't burn it," Ulyana said quietly.

"I burned a different book and reported the manual destroyed. There were forty-one lies in that sentence alone. I counted." His hands were steady; his voice was not quite. "When you draw with this, the erased borders come back. Not fully. Like a bruise showing through powder. Villages appear. Roads. Shrines. And in the northeast—" he tapped a ghost-line where the vellum showed only the faintest silver-grey shadow "—a mark my predecessor annotated before the Erasure and then tried to scrape off. He wrote one word under it. *Door.*"

The kitchen went very still. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"The Door of Ash," Ulyana said. Not a question. They said it the way you'd say the name of a person you'd mourned.

"It moves," Ivo said. "Or rather, the country around it moves, because the country isn't anywhere anymore. It's wherever it's remembered hard enough. My maps give us the shape of the memory. We follow the bleed-through until it stops fading — where the ink holds, the Door is near."

"That's weeks of travel," Brannic said. "Through countryside the Ministry watches precisely because it's supposed to be empty."

"Yes," Ivo said. "That is the plan's principal flaw. I have several others catalogued, if anyone wants them after dessert."

To his astonishment, Wren laughed, and then Sefa, and then even Ulyana — a real laugh this time, cracked down the middle but real — and Ivo found that eleven years of lying had never once felt as much like telling the truth as this moment of terrible honesty did.

* * *

Wren knew what all the grown-ups were too polite to say: none of them trusted each other yet, and none of them could do this alone, and those two facts were going to have to get married. She'd seen marriages worse than that.

She picked at the last roll and listened. Listening was her trade, same as picking locks. You got in through seams.

The charmwright — Ulyana, *they*, she'd better remember that, names mattered now more than ever, everyone at this table was busy becoming someone new — kept touching the inside of their wrist where a scar sat like a pale bracelet. Guild brand, half-scraped. Wren had one too, sort of: a scar on her thumb from the first lock she'd picked, age nine, in a house where the lock was the only thing standing between her and dinner.

And in her pocket, warm as a held bird, was the charm. Copper wire twisted around a river stone, bought off Ulyana's stall six years ago for a button and a lie about her age. It had never done anything, not once, not for six years — and then three nights ago it had gotten *warm*, and she'd tripped running from a constable, and a stack of crates had fallen exactly wrong-for-him and right-for-her, and she'd walked away unhurt and rich in the only currency that mattered, which was elsewhere.

Luckiest charm in Vessia, if Vessia existed. Which was the whole argument, wasn't it.

"So we go northeast," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to rot. "All of us. Because here's what none of you want to say out loud: every single person at this table is already dead on paper. Sefa's harboring residuals. Brannic's a deserter. Cartographer's a thief. I'm a thief too but the fun kind. And Ulyana—" she shrugged, gentler than her usual shrug "—Ulyana's the whole wanted poster. Nobody here gets to stay behind and be safe. So we might as well be safe *together*, somewhere the Ministry's maps don't reach."

"She's fifteen," Brannic said.

"She's right," said Sefa, Ivo, and Ulyana, nearly in unison.

Brannic looked around the table — at the old woman who fed fugitives, at the liar with honest maps, at the child with a god's luck in her pocket, at the charmwright who hadn't stopped trembling since the market but hadn't run either — and something in his chest, some clerical ledger he'd kept balanced for twenty-two years, quietly refused to balance anymore.

"Then we need provisions, papers, and a route that avoids the garrison road," he said, and heard, in his own voice, the sound of a man defecting in the past tense.

* * *

Later, when the others argued over routes in low voices, Sefa found Ulyana at her window, looking out at a city that had outlawed them.

"My grandson asks about you," she said. "Toma. He wants to know if the lady who fixed his lungs is a saint."

"I'm not—" Ulyana started.

"No," Sefa agreed. "Saints are for other people's stories. You're just a person who made a thing, and a child breathed, and now the debt's moving through the world like water finding its level." She put a wrapped bundle in Ulyana's hands — bread, cheese, the small immortality of being provisioned by someone else. "Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we walk into a country that isn't there."

Ulyana held the bundle like it might break.

It was Wren, half-asleep on the bench, who saw it first — or rather, felt it, because the charm in her pocket had gone suddenly, urgently cold. She sat up. Across the city, faint against the clouds, three points of white light rose above the Ministry quarter: signal flares, the kind Brannic had described at supper, the kind that meant a survey team had confirmed a residual signature.

"They found the market stall," Ulyana whispered.

Brannic was already on his feet, pulling the torn coat closed. "No," he said grimly. "Worse. They found the *ledger*. And Ulyana — your name is the next line in it."
