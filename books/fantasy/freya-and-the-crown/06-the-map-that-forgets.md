## 6. The Map That Forgets

Ivo had erased Vessia with his own hands.

That was the confession he could never say aloud, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: not that he had failed to draw it, but that he had drawn it beautifully—every river bend, every hill's shoulder, every village name in his careful clerk's hand—and then, on Ministry orders, had unmade it stroke by stroke until the parchment showed only the neighboring republics meeting in an empty stretch of nothing called, in the official gazetteer, *the Unremarkable Interior*.

He had been good at it. That was the worst part. He had won a commendation for it.

Now he knelt in the ditch behind Sefa's kitchen with a bowl of pig's blood cooling between his knees and a goose quill trembling in his fingers, and he tried to remember how to pray.

"You're holding it wrong," said the pickpocket, whose name was Wren and who had appointed herself Ivo's supervisor within an hour of their acquaintance. She crouched beside him, chin on her fists, watching the blood skin over in the cold. "You look like you're writing a tax notice."

"I *was* a cartographer of record for eleven years."

"Yeah, and now you're drawing a country back from the dead with blood in a ditch. Different job." She plucked the quill from him, dipped it, and held it out. "Sefa says you have to mean it. The prayer part. She says her grandmother used to say the words before kneading bread and the loaves always rose. It's not about being holy. It's about meaning it."

Ivo took the quill. His hand, which had once been steady enough to letter boundary stones by lamplight, shook like a man's in a fever.

He thought of the first map he'd ever erased. A young woman had brought it in—a schoolteacher, he remembered, from a town whose name he had personally scraped off three separate documents. She hadn't cried. She had just stood there while he inked over her home, watching each village vanish under the gray wash of the Ministry's nullifying compound, and when it was done she said, *Thank you,* because what else did one say to the man holding your country's corpse? He had gone home that night and drunk himself sick and told himself it was law, it was order, it was peace—the godless republic needed no haunted borders, no old altars smoking in the hills, no names that summoned things with teeth.

Eleven years of telling himself that. Eleven years of blank parchment where a nation should have been.

He dipped the quill in the blood, closed his eyes, and said—not to any god in particular, because he didn't know how to address them anymore, if he ever had—*Let me see what I helped take away.*

The quill touched paper.

And Vessia bloomed.

It came up through the parchment like water through cloth. Rivers he had drowned reasserted themselves in dark red threads; hills shouldered up beneath his hand as though the land itself were pushing against the underside of the page. Villages wrote their own names in handwriting that wasn't his—older hands, dead hands, the script of surveyors two centuries gone. And along every border, where the Ministry's erasure had bitten deepest, the lines came back wrong: doubled, ghosted, flickering like a candle seen through smoke. The true borders. The remembered ones.

"Saints," Wren breathed. "It's *moving*."

It was. The ghost-borders breathed. They swelled and contracted with something like a pulse, and when Ivo leaned close he saw why: the ink was drinking the light differently wherever people still remembered. Where memory was strong, the line burned bright and sure. Where it had faded—where the last grandmother who sang the old songs had died, where the shrine stones had been broken up for road fill—the border thinned to a whisper, a vein about to collapse.

"The country isn't where the maps say," Ivo heard himself say. "It never was. It's where it's *remembered*. We've been navigating by grief this whole time and calling it geography."

Behind them, Sefa's kitchen door banged open and Brannic filled it, broad and grim, with Ulyana a half-step behind, arms crossed against the dawn cold.

"Well?" Brannic said.

"It works," Ivo said. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat and tried again. "It works. But we should leave today. Blood-ink has a smell. And the Ministry still employs people who know exactly what that smell means."

* * *

They went north by the ghost-roads, following the bright places.

Ivo had expected the countryside to be empty—the Unremarkable Interior, officially uninhabited, officially nothing—but the land was thick with the stubborn residue of the erased. Farmhouses stood where no farmhouses were registered, their roofs sagging but sound, their gardens planted in defiance of the gazetteer. Smoke rose from chimneys that appeared on no census. And everywhere, everywhere, the small shrines: a stone niche in a hedgerow with a bowl of rainwater and a sprig of rowan; a painted eye above a barn door, watching for the return of some god whose name the painter had chiseled out themselves, so that even they couldn't be prosecuted for knowing it.

At noon they passed through a village that was not there.

That was how Ivo thought of it afterward, though it was there in every way that mattered. It stood in a fold of hills where his own erased maps had shown bare pasture. A well, a smithy, a chapel with its bell still hanging—unrung for twenty years, its rope frayed to a tassel. People moved through the lanes doing the ordinary work of living, and when they saw the five strangers walking the ghost-border, they didn't hide. An old woman set out bread and salt on a bench by the road without a word, the way you feed travelers, the way you fed them *before*, when feeding travelers was a rite and not merely a kindness.

"They think the country's coming back," Ulyana murmured, taking a piece of the bread. Their fingers left rust-colored stains on the crust—residue of the charms waking in their pack, Ivo had learned, a faint bleeding light that followed Ulyana everywhere now like a slow hemorrhage. "That's why the shrines are fresh. Why the borders are burning brighter here. Word travels down the ghost-lines faster than it ever traveled the post roads. Somebody prayed, and somebody answered, and now every village that remembers being Vessia is holding its breath."

"And when the Ministry comes," Brannic said flatly, "these are the villages that burn first. I've read the contingency orders. *Residual population centers.* That's what they call them."

The old woman who'd left the bread looked up sharply. She had heard. Of course she had heard; the whole lane had heard. She looked at Brannic—at his clerk's posture, his Ministry-trained diction—and then at the rest of them, and Ivo braced for the doors to shut, for the bread to be snatched back, for the whole bright thread of the border to dim with fear.

Instead she said, "Then walk fast, and tell whatever's waiting at the end of your road that we kept the lamps lit. Twenty years. Nobody asked us to. We just did."

Ulyana stopped. Turned back. Took the woman's hand in both of theirs, and Ivo watched the charmwright do something he had never seen anyone do in the Ministry's halls: they bowed. Not to a superior. To a keeper of lamps.

"We'll tell them," Ulyana said. "Whoever's waiting. I promise you."

The border-line flared as they spoke—Ivo saw it, saw the ghosted red thread running along the hedgerow pulse like a struck bell—and somewhere far off, or perhaps not far at all, something ancient turned its attention toward a village that officially did not exist, and found it full.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation chapel, and Ivo could not sleep.

He sat apart with the map across his knees, tracing the route ahead by moonlight. Two days to the place where the brightest thread converged—a ruined church, according to the oldest layer of ink, the handwriting of dead men, a place the maps had never managed to forget no matter how many times the Ministry scrubbed it. The Door of Ash, if Ulyana's guild-ledger could be believed. Ivo believed it. He had spent eleven years learning what the Ministry feared enough to erase, and they had erased this road more thoroughly than any other.

"You drew them," said Wren, dropping down beside him. Not a question. "The erasure maps. Your hand's all over the archive copies. I've seen them."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because he'd been twenty-two and hungry and brilliant and the Ministry had offered him a title, a salary, a warm room, and a story in which blankness equaled peace. Because it was easier to scrape a name off a map than to ask who had loved that name. Because he had told himself, every single day for eleven years, that he was removing a wound rather than amputating a body.

"I was good at it," he said finally. "That seemed like enough."

Wren snorted. "Ulyana sold broken charms for twenty years and hated every minute. Sefa prayed to a charm she bought off a stranger because the doctors were useless. Brannic filed the dissolution orders—he told us, first night, he wanted us to know. Everybody in this little parade has a thing they did instead of being brave." She picked a splinter of chapel wood and turned it in her fingers. "Difference is, now we're walking the other direction. That's all courage is, probably. Walking the other direction late."

From the chapel doorway, Brannic's voice cut the dark, low and urgent: "Lights. On the back road. Three lanterns, spaced like a sweep line."

Ivo knew that spacing. He had designed the spacing. Trackers moved in staggered triads so that no single lantern's blind spot exceeded forty paces, and they moved *quietly*, and they had found the blood-smell, and they had found it fast because Ivo had trained half of them himself.

The camp erupted into motion—Sefa gathering pots, Ulyana slinging the charm-pack, Wren already up the chapel wall like a cat. But Ivo stayed kneeling over the map a moment longer, and in that moment he saw the border-thread nearest the trackers begin to *dim*. Fear moving down the ghost-lines like frost. Somewhere ahead, villages that had kept their lamps lit for twenty years were feeling the old terror come home.

No. He put his palm flat on the parchment, over the bright convergence point, over the ruin where the Door waited.

"I'm sorry," he whispered—to the map, to the country, to the schoolteacher whose village he had scraped away. "I'm going to give it back. All of it. Even if they follow us the whole way."

The thread under his hand pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, and Ivo Pemberton's former cartographer rose, folded the blood-map into his coat, and ran into the dark after his family—with three lanterns closing behind them, and, far ahead at the edge of the ink, a small ruined church beginning, very faintly, to glow.
