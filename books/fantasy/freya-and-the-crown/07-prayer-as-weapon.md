## 7. Prayer as Weapon

The shrine had been a tollhouse once, before the republic decided that borders were embarrassing relics of the erased. Now it was a stone shell with a hollowed niche where travelers had left offerings to a god whose name had been scraped off the lintel so thoroughly that only the shape of the letters remained—shallow ghosts of them, like scars where a tattoo had been cut away.

Ulyana knew the shape of those letters. They had traced them a thousand times as an apprentice, in candle smoke and chalk dust, learning the old script the way other children learned their letters: *Vesh-Karann, Keeper of Thresholds, Who Weighs What Passes.*

They had not spoken that name aloud in twenty years.

The others were asleep in the shrine's back room, such as it was—a collapsed roof, a hearth full of cold ash, Sefa's blanket around her shoulders, Ivo sitting up even in sleep, one hand on his satchel of forbidden maps. The boy, Tam, had curled against Brannic like a cat against a stove, his stolen luck-charm still knotted at his throat. It glowed faintly now, even in sleep. All of Ulyana's work glowed now. Every trinket they had ever sold was a small lit window, and through every window something was looking back.

And behind them, on the road, the Ministry trackers moved without lanterns, because they did not need light to find what they hunted. They needed only the glow.

Ulyana stood in the doorway of the shrine and watched the dark fields for the flicker of unlit lamps moving in formation. Three of them, maybe four. They had been gaining since the river crossing, patient as arithmetic. The Ministry did not chase; it *converged*. Somewhere ahead, at the next crossroads, there would already be clerks waiting with ledgers open, ready to record Ulyana Pemberton's unmaking in triplicate.

There was no way through. There was no way back. There was only the shrine, and the scraped name, and the twenty years of silence between Ulyana and the thing that owned their apprenticeship.

*You could run*, said the coward's voice, the one that had kept them alive through two decades of selling dead charms to people who wanted to believe. *You've done it before. You're good at disappearing.*

But running meant leaving them. Sefa, who had prayed over a dying boy with a charm Ulyana had sold her as a fake and charged her too much for. Tam, who guarded a luck-piece like a dragon guards its first coin. Brannic, carrying his shame like a second spine. Ivo, whose maps bled when you prayed near them.

Ulyana looked down at their own hands. Charmwright's hands, scarred with solder-burns and stained with metals that didn't officially exist. Hands that had made payments without knowing it. Hands that belonged, by the terms of a contract signed before they could read, to something waiting on the other side of this scraped-off name.

*Well*, Ulyana thought. *If I'm collateral, let's go talk to the creditor.*

They stepped into the shrine. They knelt in the ash of the hearth—it was not a hearth yet, but it would be, someday, if any of them lived—and they folded their hands the way Master Ellery-Voss had taught them, thumbs crossed, fingers laced, a little door made of bone.

And for the first time in twenty years, Ulyana Pemberton prayed.

Not well. Not beautifully. The words came out rusted, half-remembered, in a language the republic had legislated out of existence and that Ulyana's mouth had never quite forgotten. *Vesh-Karann. Keeper of Thresholds. I know what I am to you. I know what my master took from you. I'm here to settle accounts.*

The silence answered.

It came up through the floor first, through the stone, through the ash—a pressure like deep water, like standing at the bottom of a well and hearing someone speak at the rim. The scraped letters on the lintel began to fill in, not with paint but with shadow, shadow in the exact shapes of the missing strokes, until the name hung above the door complete and black and older than the country that had tried to erase it.

Then the god spoke, and it did not speak in words. It spoke in *transactions*. Ulyana felt each sentence arrive as a weight placed in one hand and removed from the other, a ledger balancing itself inside their chest.

**YOU CALLED.**

"I called," Ulyana said aloud, because gods deserved the courtesy of voice, even starving ones. "I've been calling for twenty years without knowing it. Every charm I ever made was a letter to you. You're late in answering."

**I WAS NOT PERMITTED TO ANSWER. YOUR GUILD SEALED THE DOORS OF MY HOUSE AND PAID ITS LAST COIN INTO ANOTHER'S HAND. I HAVE BEEN STARVING IN A COUNTRY THAT DOES NOT EXIST, WAITING FOR ONE BELIEVER. YOU ARE THE FIRST IN TWENTY YEARS WHO PRAYED WITHOUT BEING PAID TO.**

The honesty of it nearly broke Ulyana's heart. This was not the devouring god of the guild's frightened stories. This was a creditor who had waited two decades at a locked door, counting the interest on a debt no one would acknowledge, growing thin and strange and hungry.

"Then let's settle it," Ulyana said. "Name your terms."

The pressure in the room shifted, gathered, became almost gentle—which was worse, somehow, than any thunder. The god laid its offer into Ulyana's mind the way a merchant lays goods on a counter: carefully, openly, everything visible.

**THE DEBT IS THIS: ONE CHARMWRIGHT, WHOLE, IN SERVICE. YOUR NAME—PEMBERTON, THE GUILD NAME, THE BOND-NAME—SURRENDERED TO ME AS SIGNATURE. YOUR CRAFT GIVEN TO MY KEEPING. IN EXCHANGE: THE FOUR WHO SLEEP BEHIND YOU PASS EVERY BORDER UNMARKED. THE MINISTRY WILL NOT SEE THEM. THEY WILL LIVE, AND GROW OLD, AND BURY EACH OTHER, AND NEVER KNOW WHAT IT COST.**

Ulyana's breath went out of them slowly.

It was a good bargain. That was the obscene part. It was *fair*—fairer than anything the republic had ever offered anyone. Four lives for one name and one pair of hands. Sefa would see her grandson grow. Tam would grow up. Brannic might even forgive himself, given fifty years to try.

And Ulyana would become what the guild had always intended: a payment walking upright, a debt instrument with a heartbeat, serving a god in a country that wasn't on any map, forever.

*You'd be safe*, whispered the coward's voice. *Safe and owned. You've been owned your whole life. At least this owner keeps its promises.*

Ulyana knelt in the ash and thought about their master. Old Ellery-Voss, who had taught them solder and scripture side by side, who had died owing a god everything and leaving the bill to a child. Had he known? Of course he had known. He had signed the default with clear eyes because the alternative was the guild serving Vesh-Karann forever, generation after generation, and he had loved his apprentices too much—or feared for them too much—to pass the chain down intact. So he had broken it instead, and broken it crooked, and the crooked break had landed on Ulyana.

He had bought twenty years of freedom with Ulyana's future. And Ulyana had spent those twenty years selling fake miracles, which meant the freedom had been wasted, which meant—

Which meant nothing. Which meant everything. Which meant that the one thing Ulyana Pemberton had learned in the night markets of a godless republic was how a bargain is structured, and where the hidden teeth are, and that no contract is unbreakable if you can find the clause nobody read twice.

"No," Ulyana said.

The silence sharpened.

**IT IS GENEROUS.**

"It is generous," Ulyana agreed. "That's why I checked it twice. Generous bargains from starving gods are like free drinks from strangers—you pay later, with interest you didn't agree to. Here's my counter." They lifted their chin. "You don't get my service. Service is what the Ministry sells—coerced belief, enforced worship, prayer at gunpoint. You've been starving for twenty years on coerced nothing. Do you truly want another servant who kneels because the contract says so? You'd be fed, but you'd never be *believed in*. There's a difference. Even you can taste it."

**THEN WHAT DO YOU OFFER, CHARMWRIGHT?**

"Time," Ulyana said. "And a door held open from the wrong side. But not tonight, and not on your terms. Tonight I offer you defiance, which is the most honest prayer there is, and I'll tell you why: because I mean it. Every word. No coercion in it. That's more belief than you've tasted since the maps burned. Take it or leave it."

For a long moment the god did nothing at all, and Ulyana understood that they were being *weighed*—that this was the threshold-god doing the one thing it existed to do, measuring what passed, deciding what was worth letting through.

Then, softly, with something that in a smaller creature might have been amusement:

**YOU ARE YOUR MASTER'S WORK AFTER ALL. VERY WELL. WEIGHED AND FOUND WORTH NEGOTIATING WITH. BUT THE MINISTRY COMES, LITTLE COLLATERAL, AND YOU HAVE NOTHING LEFT TO BUY TIME WITH EXCEPT WHAT YOU CARRY HERE.**

It touched, lightly, the place behind Ulyana's eyes where the craft lived—the twenty years of technique, the memory of every alloy and sigil and word of making, the whole architecture of who they had been.

Ulyana had known this was coming. Had known it from the moment the god named its price. There was only one currency in the shrine tonight besides a name, and Ulyana had already decided, kneeling in the ash, which one they would rather keep.

"Yes," they said. "Half. You choose which half—I won't. That way it's a gift, not a theft."

**THAT IS THE MOST FOOLISH—**

"That's the most *honest* thing I've ever offered anyone," Ulyana said. "Do it."

The god reached in.

It did not hurt the way fire hurts. It hurt the way a house hurts when a wall comes down—load-bearing, structural, a loss you feel in everything that was leaning on the lost thing. Ulyana felt whole wings of themselves fold up and blow away as ash: the formulas for binding light to iron, gone. The nine names of copper and what each one dreamed about, gone. The memory of Master Ellery-Voss's hands guiding theirs on the first soldering iron—gone, gone, gone, and Ulyana wept without sound as it left, because grief needs no craft to survive.

Half their craft-memory, burned as offering. In exchange: the trackers at the treeline stopped as one, turned, and began walking toward the eastern road, following a false glow Ulyana's own burning charm-work cast three miles away—a decoy written in the language they no longer fully remembered, paid for with the memory of writing it.

When it was done, Ulyana knelt alone in the shrine, hollowed and shaking, trying to recall the sigil for a simple warming charm and finding a smooth blankness where it had been. Half of everything they were: spent.

But the god remained. Fainter now. Fed a little. Waiting.

**KEEPER OF THRESHOLDS,** it said, and the title settled over Ulyana like a mantle that fit better than it should have. **WE ARE NOT FINISHED, YOU AND I. THERE IS A DOOR OF ASH, AND IT DEMANDS A KEEPER, AND I FIND I AM... CURIOUS WHAT YOU WILL OFFER ME NEXT.**

Behind Ulyana, in the ruined back room, Tam sat bolt upright in the dark, wide-eyed, his luck-charm blazing like a struck match.

"They're coming," the boy whispered. "All of them. And something else is coming with them—something wearing a clerk's coat, but it doesn't walk right."

Ulyana rose on legs that barely held, half a craftwright, wholly out of options, and reached for the one thing they had left.

"Wake the others," they said. "We run for the Door."
