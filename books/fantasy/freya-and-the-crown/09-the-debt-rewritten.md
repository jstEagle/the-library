## 9. The Debt Rewritten

The gold did not burn. That was the first thing Ulyana learned, standing in the spiral of ash and light: it had never been fire at all, only hunger given a color.

It moved through them like warm water through cloth, tasting the edges of their craft-memory—the half that remained after the border shrine, the half they had burned to buy an hour—and Ulyana felt it cataloguing what was left with the terrible patience of a creditor reading a ledger one final time before demanding payment in full.

*So little left,* the god said, and its voice came from everywhere now, from the hearth and the rafters and the hollow of Ulyana's own chest. *Half a life's work, spent on a prayer you didn't even believe in.*

"I believed in them," Ulyana said. "The others. That was enough to make it work."

*Belief.* The word turned over slowly, like a coin examined by lamplight for clipping. *You speak of belief as though you know it. Your guild spoke of it too, once. They wrote it into contracts. Clause upon clause, covenant upon covenant—belief as collateral, worship as interest, devotion amortized across generations.* A pause, and something in the vast attention bent closer, curious and wounded at once. *And when the payments stopped, when your masters chose extinction over obligation, do you know what I lost?*

Ulyana thought of the ledger in the guildhall cellar. Of their master's steady hand writing *default* beside a name that had once meant everything. "Everything," they said quietly. "You lost everything."

*I lost the* paying,* yes. But that was never the wound.* The gold dimmed, and for a moment the herald's borrowed face looked almost human—tired, the way Sefa looked tired, the way Brannic looked when he talked about the files he'd stamped. *A god can fast. A god has fasted twenty years and will fast twenty more if it must. What I lost was the* choosing. *No one has knelt to me freely in two hundred years. Every prayer I ever received was invoiced. Every hymn was a receipt. Do you understand what it is to be fed for centuries by people who were only feeding you because they owed you?*

The church was very quiet. Behind Ulyana, Sefa's grandson shifted against her side; Brannic's lantern had gone out, but no one relit it, because the hearth gave light enough for all of them. Somewhere beyond the broken doors, the Ministry's ranks stood in their grey lines, waiting for an order that had not yet come.

"I think," Ulyana said slowly, "that I might be the only person in Vessia who does understand."

They reached into their coat—not for a charm, this time, though every charm they had left hummed awake at the motion—and drew out the thing they had carried since the guildhall: not the master's ledger itself, but a single blank page torn from its back. Paper that had waited two decades for a contract that was never written.

"You want payment. My name. My service. Forever." Ulyana held the page up so the light caught it. "That's the old contract. That's the one my master defaulted on, and the one the republic was built to make impossible to pay. And if I sign it, you get a servant who prays because she has to, and you starve anyway, just slower. You'll have traded one bad debt for another."

*Speak plainly, charmwright.*

"Rewrite it." Ulyana's voice didn't shake, which surprised them more than anything else that night. "Not a debt. Not servitude. A stewardship. The Charmwrights' Guild died because it was built to owe—you, and every god who signed with it. So we don't resurrect it as it was. We rebuild it as keepers. Of the Door. Of the crossing between what Vessia was and what it chooses to become next. And we're paid—" they took a breath, because the next words were either the truest or the most foolish they would ever say, "—we're paid in worship freely chosen. Not invoiced. Not contracted. If someone kneels, they kneel because they mean it, or they don't kneel at all. You get fed the way gods were always meant to be fed. And you get it from people who stayed because they wanted to, not because a signature bound them."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Ulyana to count their own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then the god laughed.

It was not a cruel sound. It was the sound of something enormous setting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it wasn't part of its body.

*A stewardship,* it said. *Freely given worship. Do you know how many mortals have stood where you stand and offered me less? Kings offered me temples. Emperors offered me blood. Your own masters offered me* efficiency. *And here stands the last apprentice of a dead guild, half her craft burned away, offering me the one currency I have never once been paid.*

"And if you refuse," Ulyana said, "you get nothing. Not my name, not my service, not one more coerced prayer. I'll stand in this doorway until I die of old age, and I will never sign the old contract, and neither will anyone who follows me through that Door. You've read my craft-memory. You know I don't bluff. It's the one skill my master made sure I kept."

*Oh, I know.* The warmth in the voice was unmistakable now, and terrible, and tender all at once. *I have tasted twenty years of your trinkets. Little charms sold to people who needed them, priced so the poor could afford hope. You have been practicing this negotiation your entire life without knowing it. Every kindness you extended on credit was a clause in this page.*

Ulyana looked down at the blank paper. Ink appeared on it—not written, exactly, but remembered into being, the ghost of the original contract rising through the paper like a bruise through skin. Clause upon clause. Covenant upon covenant. Their own name already waiting at the bottom, in the space reserved for the debtor.

"No," Ulyana said. "Not that one. We start clean."

They struck the page through with one line, and the old contract screamed—a thin, high sound like a wire snapping under strain—and dissolved into ash that joined the golden spiral above the hearth.

Then Ulyana knelt, right there on the cold church floor, and wrote.

They wrote with the stub of charcoal Sefa pressed into their hand, kneeling over the page while their found family watched: Sefa with her grandson's hand gripped tight in hers; Brannic standing straighter than he had stood in twenty years of filing; Ivo with his ruined maps spread at his feet like shed skins; the pickpocket girl crouched on a pew, watching with the sharp-eyed stillness she brought to locked drawers. Ulyana wrote the new covenant in plain words, no archaisms, no clauses designed to be misunderstood:

*The Guild of the Door shall keep the passage open. None shall be turned away who comes in good faith. None shall be compelled to worship who does not choose it. The keeper serves the Door, and the Door serves those who pass. Payment: belief, freely given, in whatever measure the giver chooses. Term: as long as both parties find the bargain fair.*

At the bottom, where the old contract had wanted *Pemberton*, Ulyana hesitated—then wrote their own name, the one they had been saving, the one that belonged to no debt and no master and no erased country.

The paper drank the ink. The hearth flared.

*Signed,* said the god, and the word was not thunder but something quieter and far more frightening: relief. *Witnessed. Oh—witnessed.* And the light poured outward through the broken windows, across the churchyard, into the grey ranks of the Ministry.

What happened next, Ulyana would spend the rest of their life trying to describe.

It began with the young clerk—the one who had whispered his first prayer at the door. He sank to his knees in the frost-killed grass, and this time he did not whisper. He prayed aloud, clumsy and off-key, a prayer with no technique and no training and no invoice attached, and the god drank it like water after drought.

And then the clerk beside him knelt. And the one beside *him*. An inspector tore off her grey gloves as though they'd been burning her for years. A captain of the Unmaking dropped his writ of seizure in the mud and covered his face with both hands, and the sound he made was not grief, exactly, or not only grief—it was the sound of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

The Ministry did not collapse. It *converted*, rank by rank, the way a frozen river converts to moving water—one crack, then another, then a rushing that no order could recall. Somewhere in the dark, someone began to sing—an old Vessian harvest song, banned for two decades, sung badly and joyfully—and the grey ranks took it up verse by verse, forgetting themselves, remembering everything.

Brannic found Ulyana's shoulder with his hand. His eyes were wet. "Twenty years I stamped their names into oblivion," he said. "And all it took to undo it was letting them pray."

"It didn't take that," Ulyana said, watching the hearth, where the golden light had settled into something steadier now, warmer, a fire instead of a hunger. "It took giving them something worth praying *to*."

Sefa came forward then, her grandson asleep against her shoulder despite the singing, and she looked at the hearth, and at Ulyana, and at the new contract glowing softly in Ulyana's hands.

"So," she said, in the tone she used for announcing dinner. "When do we open?"

Ulyana laughed—really laughed, for the first time in twenty years—and turned toward the Door of Ash, which stood wide and patient and *theirs*, waiting only for the first traveler to step through.

But as they raised the contract to seal it into the hearthstone, the light flickered—and in the flicker, Ulyana saw the other side of the Door clearly for the first time.

Not ruins. Not ash.

A road, running green and whole through country Ulyana had never seen but knew instantly, bone-deep, the way you know a face from a dream. And on that road, figures were walking toward the light—hundreds of them, thousands, all the erased and scattered faithful of Vessia, drawn home by a prayer they could finally feel answered.

And at their head, carrying a lantern of the old guild-make, walked a woman Ulyana recognized from a portrait in the sealed guildhall.

Their master.

Very much alive. Very much smiling.

And saying, in a voice that crossed twenty years and one rewritten contract as easily as a threshold:

*"Well, apprentice. You're late. We have so much work to do."*
