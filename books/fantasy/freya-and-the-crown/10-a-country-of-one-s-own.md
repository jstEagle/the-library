## 10. A Country of One's Own

The guildhall smelled of bread and beeswax now.

Ulyana noticed it every morning, the way you notice a scar that has stopped hurting: the old reek of ash and scorched oak had been scrubbed out of the beams, replaced by the warm animal comfort of a building that was lived in again. Someone—Sefa, almost certainly, who could not walk past an unlit oven without feeding it—had already started the day's loaves. The smell drifted down through the floorboards and mingled with the other smells of the hall: tallow, ink, river-damp wool drying by the Door, and underneath it all the faint mineral breath of the hearth itself, like a struck flint that never quite went cold.

Ulyana stood at the long counter where the sales ledger used to live—the ledger of trinkets, of small mercies sold at three coppers apiece to people who needed them and got nothing—and unrolled the new one.

It was bound in grey leather, stitched with red thread in the pattern Ivo insisted on calling *the ghost border*, because he could not stop being a cartographer any more than Sefa could stop being a grandmother or Brannic could stop apologizing. The paper inside was thick and cream-colored, milled in one of the villages that had remembered itself back into existence. At the top of the first page, in Ulyana's own careful hand, were the words:

*Ledger of the Charmwrights' Guild, Reborn. All debts forgiven. All names recorded freely.*

Below it, a single line waited for ink.

* * *

Outside, the morning market was assembling itself in the square the way it did now, six days out of seven. Not the furtive night market of twenty years ago—no shuttered lanterns, no whispered prices, no watching for the grey coats of the Ministry—but a proper daylight thing, stalls and shouting and children underfoot. Half the sellers were refugees who had come through the Door with nothing but what they carried; half were neighbors from the streets around the guildhall who had decided, in the slow way neighborhoods decide things, that if magic was coming back anyway it might as well come back honest.

Ulyana watched from the window as a woman set out a table of charms—not Ulyana's work, not yet, but workmanlike, each piece tagged with a little card in a child's handwriting: *what this does, what it costs, what it asks of you.* That last line was new. That last line was the whole revolution, if you wanted to be grand about it, though Ulyana preferred not to be grand before breakfast.

The Ministry was gone. Not destroyed—Brannic had been very clear about this, in his careful clerk's way, over many cups of tea. Institutions did not die; they molted. But when its own clerks had knelt in the ruined church and prayed, when the grey coats had found themselves weeping into their ledgers of unmaking, the authority had gone out of the thing like heat out of a dead coal. There were still offices. There were still men with stamps. But nobody believed in them anymore, and belief, Ulyana had learned, was the only currency that had ever mattered.

* * *

"You're stalling," said Sefa, from the doorway, flour to her elbows.

"I'm reviewing."

"You've been reviewing that blank page for an hour." She came in wiping her hands on her apron, which was the same apron she had worn the night she'd confessed her grandson's fever and Ulyana's charm and the terrible arithmetic of answered prayers. Tam was in the yard behind the hall now, hauling water, fourteen years old and healed and loud about it. He still wore the luck-charm at his throat. He would wear it until it wore out, he said, and then he'd have Ulyana make him another, and pay properly this time.

"A name is a serious thing," Ulyana said.

"So is bread, and I don't stand in front of the oven wringing my hands." Sefa looked at the ledger, and her voice gentled the way it did when she talked about her grandson or her dead husband or the country that had been taken off the maps while she was learning to cook in it. "Pemberton wasn't yours, love. It was handed to you by people who thought of you as collateral. Write down what you call yourself when nobody's listening."

And that was the trouble, wasn't it. When nobody was listening, Ulyana called themselves *apprentice*, and *last of the Charmwrights*, and sometimes, in the worst hours before dawn, *the debt*. Twenty years of selling dead charms had been twenty years of answering to a name that belonged to a contract.

But there were other moments now. Moments when Tam laughed so hard at his own joke he had to sit down on the water barrel. Moments when Brannic read aloud from the old Ministry files—not the cruel ones, the absurd ones, the memos about the correct angle at which to fold an erased map—and the kitchen shook with laughter. Moments when a stranger came through the Door of Ash blinking and weeping and saying *I didn't think anywhere was real anymore*, and Ulyana took their coat and hung it up and felt something in their chest settle like a house settling onto its foundations after a long storm.

In those moments, Ulyana had begun to notice, they were not thinking of themselves as anything at all. They were simply busy. Simply here.

Maybe that was the name. Maybe the name was the work.

* * *

The apprentice arrived at midmorning, as arranged, and nearly walked past the door twice, because the rebuilt guildhall did not look like what she expected a guildhall to look like. It looked, Ulyana had been told, like a bakery that had ambitions.

Her name was Wren Halloway, sixteen, sharp-elbowed, with ink stains on her fingers that predated any apprenticeship. She had come through the Door in the second month, from one of the villages that existed only in stubborn memory, carrying her grandmother's prayer book and a satchel of salvaged charm-parts she had been assembling blind since the gods came back. She had sold three of them before anyone caught her. Two worked. One had set a fence post on fire.

"The fence," she said, standing in the middle of the hall with her chin up, "was asking for it."

"Everything is asking for something," Ulyana said. "That's the first lesson. Sit down."

They sat at the long counter, the two of them, and Ulyana laid out the tools of the trade: not wire and glass and cold iron, those were just the bodies of charms, but the true instruments—a bowl of clean water, a candle, a scrap of bread, and the ledger open to its first blank page.

"Old guild made payments," Ulyana said. "Charms were invoices. Prayers were debts. You paid whether you wanted to or not, and the gods starved anyway, because a debt isn't belief, it's just fear wearing a nicer coat." They turned the ledger toward Wren. "We do it differently. Every charm we make gets written down: what it does, what it costs, what it asks. And the third line is the one that matters. If the answer to *what does it ask* is *nothing freely given*, we don't make it. No exceptions. Not for kings, not for gods, not for me."

Wren looked at the page for a long moment. "What if someone can't give anything? What if they're desperate?"

"Then we give first," Ulyana said. "Freely. And we write that down too, and we wait, however long it takes, for them to give back if they ever can. Gratitude is the only coin the Door has ever accepted. We're not going to counterfeit it."

"That's—" Wren stopped. Started again. "My grandmother used to say the gods left because we stopped loving them. I thought she meant it like a scolding."

"She meant it like a diagnosis," Ulyana said. "Now. First lesson. Take the candle. Tell me what it asks of you."

Wren picked it up. Held it wrong, then right. Outside, the market noise swelled and broke like weather, and somewhere in the back of the hall the Door of Ash breathed its faint flint-smell, holding open, holding on, patient as a hearth.

"It asks," Wren said slowly, "to be lit by somebody who means it."

Ulyana smiled until their face hurt. "Second lesson tomorrow."

* * *

Evening came down soft over Vessia, which existed again, on nobody's map but its own. Ivo had offered, once, formally, with charts, to draw it—to fix the borders, name the rivers, commit the whole resurrected country to vellum. Ulyana had thanked him and declined. A country on a map could be erased from a map. A country in a ledger of freely given names, in a boy's luck-charm, in a baker's loaves, in a market where charms were sold honestly in daylight—that kind of country had to be unmade one heart at a time, and the Ministry had tried, and failed.

At dusk, when the market had packed up and the lamps were lit, Ulyana sat alone at the counter with the ledger and the good pen and the ink Ivo mixed himself, blood-ink reborn as ordinary black, prayer folded in like salt in bread.

They wrote the date. They wrote the founding lines, the ones already inscribed. Then they dipped the pen again, and above the first entry, in a hand steadier than they had any right to expect, they wrote:

*Keeper of the Door, Charmwright, First of the New Guild.*

No surname. Or rather—all the surnames. Pemberton could rest. Whatever came next would be given, not inherited: earned in bread and lamplight, in lessons and ledgers, in the small daily miracle of staying.

The door opened. Brannic, with tea; Sefa, with the first loaf; Tam, dripping, having raced Wren around the yard and lost; Wren herself, cheeks flushed, clutching the candle she had lit and meant it.

"Come on, then," Ulyana said, and signed the ledger, and closed the shop, and turned the sign to *open*.
