## 1. A Taste for Bitterness

The soup arrived under a silver dome, and Juniper Lockridge counted the ways it might kill them before the lid ever lifted.

Steam first—no almond sweetness, which ruled out cyanide of the bitter kind. No metallic tang riding the vapor, so no salts of the heavy metals the court physicians feared. The broth itself was pale gold, flecked with dill, and beneath the dill something else. Something green and wrong.

Juniper dipped the spoon. Held it to the light. The court expected theater from its taster: the pause, the swallow, the ten-count of silence while a hundred eyes decided whether they were watching a servant or an execution. Juniper gave them the theater. What they did not give them was hurry.

*Foxglove,* thought the part of Juniper that had been trained by a woman with ink-stained fingers and no patience for fear. *No—not foxglove. Foxglove announces itself. This is quieter.*

They tasted.

Bitterness bloomed at the back of the tongue like a door opening in a dark house, and behind it, faintly, a numbness beginning at the root of the throat. New. Genuinely new. Juniper's body was a ledger of everything the usurper's enemies had ever tried to slip past his guards—arsenic entered on the ninth of Frostmonth, year one; hemlock twice; once, memorably, a stew laced with powdered glass that had cost them three days of bleeding and taught them more about their own endurance than any teacher had. Every poison had a signature. Every signature had been recorded, cross-referenced, survived.

This one had no entry.

Juniper swallowed, set down the spoon, and stood very still through the count of ten while the hall watched. The bitterness spread. Their fingertips began, very slightly, to prickle.

"Acceptable," they said, in the flat voice that was their uniform as much as the grey livery. "Though the dill is stale."

The Lord Steward frowned at the dill, because a taster who complained of seasoning could not possibly be complaining of anything else, and the kitchen would be punished for herbs instead of praised for murder averted. That was the trick of it. Invisibility was not silence. Invisibility was misdirection—you gave them a small thing to see so they never looked for the large thing standing right in front of them.

Juniper bowed and withdrew along the wall, counting their own pulse. Ninety. A hundred. It climbed but did not gallop. Whatever this was, it was slow-acting or dilute, and either way they had perhaps an hour to decide whether to report it, and to whom, and how much.

They had an hour also to wonder why, after six years of service, someone had finally sent the king's table something the ledger had never seen.

* * *

The Ember Guild kept its books in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, where the smell of hot wax covered every other smell, including blood, including fear, including the particular sourness of people who had not eaten in two days because their names had been struck from the rolls and no baker would sell to the unrecorded.

Juniper arrived after midnight, as they always did, letting themselves in through the coal chute with the ease of long habit. The cellar was warm. It always was. Odile said heat made people honest, though Juniper suspected what she really meant was that cold made people quiet, and quiet people died without anyone writing it down.

"You're limping," Odile said, not looking up from her mending.

"I'm walking carefully."

"You walked carefully into my chair three nights ago too." She looked up then. Odile Vasse had a face like a folded letter—all creases, all contents hidden—and eyes that had read enough false entries in her sixty years to price a lie at a glance. "Sit. Show me your hands."

Juniper sat. Held out their hands. The prickling had become a fine tremor, barely visible, the kind only another keeper of accounts would catch.

Odile caught it. She turned Juniper's wrist over, pressed two fingers below the ear, watched the pulse the way she watched ledgers. "New?"

"New."

"How new?"

"No entry," Juniper said, and heard the pride creep into it despite themselves. Six years of tasting, and tonight was the first time the body's ledger had come up blank. It should have frightened them. It did frighten them. But underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing they never said aloud at court: the small, fierce satisfaction of having found the edge of the map.

Odile grunted. She went to the shelf where she kept the physick books and the vials, and while her back was turned Juniper did what they always did first, before warmth, before words: opened the guild's true ledger.

It lived in a locked drawer disguised as a drawer of candle molds, and it was written in Juniper's own hand in a cipher of their own devising, because a ledger that could be read was a ledger that could hang everyone in it. Tonight's entries waited in their head, carried up from the palace like contraband:

*Two souls out via the tanner's yard, south wall, paid in bread and boot-leather. Cost: 4 coppers bribe to the gate-ward, 1 loaf, goodwill of Tanner Brann (owed).*

*One soul, the cooper's widow, re-recorded under a dead cousin's name in Millbrook. Cost: 12 coppers, one favor called in from the parish clerk who owed us since the winter of the bad flour.*

*One soul lost.* — Juniper still wrote the losses. Odile insisted. *The ferryman's boy, taken at the census checkpoint at Weirbridge. Nothing recovered. Debt noted against the regime, interest accruing.*

That last line was Odile's invention, and Juniper had argued against it—the ledger was for arithmetic, not anger—but she had looked at them over the candle flame and said, "Every account must balance, child. If we don't enter what they owe, the books lie by omission." So Juniper entered it. *Debt noted. Interest accruing.* It balanced nothing. It balanced everything.

"The tremor," Odile said, returning with a vial of something amber and foul. "Drink. All of it. If your mystery poison is slow, this buys you time; if it's fast, at least you'll die complaining about the taste, which is more dignity than most of us get."

Juniper drank. It tasted like pine tar and regret. "You didn't ask what I found."

"I never have to ask. You wear your findings like a cook wears onions." Odile settled into the chair opposite, mending in her lap again, needle flashing. "A new poison at the king's table means someone new is trying to kill him, or someone old has gotten clever, or—" she paused, needle stilled, "—or someone wants the taster tested. Which is it, do you think?"

Juniper hadn't let themselves think the third thing. Now that Odile had said it aloud, it sat in the room with them, patient as debt.

"If someone wanted me tested," Juniper said slowly, "they'd need to know I was worth testing. And nobody at court knows I'm anything. That's the whole point of me. I'm furniture that swallows."

"Furniture that keeps our books." Odile's voice was mild. Her eyes were not. "Furniture that walks out of a palace every third night carrying the names of forty-one people in its head. Furniture is only invisible until someone looks for it, Juniper. Then it's the first thing they find, because it was there all along."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, the chandler's shop creaked as it cooled, and a cart passed on Tallow Row, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which was the whole miracle the guild existed to protect.

"I'll be careful," Juniper said.

"You'll be careful *and* you'll stop going alone. Marta walks you home from now on."

"Marta is twelve."

"Marta is twelve and can outrun a constable, which is more than I can say for you." Odile bent back to her mending, conversation closed, and Juniper understood they had been given both a guard and a witness: if Juniper vanished, Marta would know, and if Marta knew, the guild would know, and if the guild knew, the names in Juniper's head would pass to other heads before the palace finished whatever it intended.

It was, Juniper reflected, a strange kind of family that showed its love by planning for your disappearance. But then, every family Juniper had ever known had been strange in exactly that way. The village that burned when they were nine. The road after. The woman with ink-stained fingers who had found them starving outside Corveil and asked, not *who are you*, but *what do you remember?*—because memory, she'd said, was the only property the unrecorded owned, and the guild's whole business was helping people carry theirs somewhere safe.

Juniper opened the true ledger and made the night's entries in cipher, the pen scratching in the dark like a small animal. Two souls out. One widow renamed. One boy lost, debt accruing. At the bottom of the page, as always, they drew the little mark that ended every session: a single stroke, like a candle flame, like an ember, like the first letter of a word no one at court would ever think to look for.

* * *

The poison ran its course by dawn, which meant it had been a message rather than a murder—a slow, bitter whisper saying *we are here, we are new, we know how to reach you*. Juniper reported it to the Lord Steward as a spice error. The kitchens flogged a scullery boy. The court moved on. Juniper moved on, outwardly.

Inwardly, walking back through the grey morning with Marta trotting at their elbow chattering about a cat she'd smuggled into the safehouse, Juniper turned the problem over the way they turned a spoonful held to the light.

Someone new at court. Someone clever. Someone who wanted the taster tested—or wanted the taster *watched*, which amounted to the same arithmetic with different numbers.

And then, as they came up out of the coal chute's shadow onto Tallow Row proper, they saw the thing that ended the morning's ordinary world altogether: a man leaning against the chandler's doorframe as if resting, except that his hand was pressed to his side and his side was dark, and his eyes found Juniper across the street with the desperate precision of a man who had been looking for one specific face and had finally, at the very end of his strength, found it.

He pushed off the doorframe. Took one step. Said a word Juniper did not yet know would rearrange every ledger they had ever kept—

—and fell.
