## 2. The Courier's Last Delivery

The knife came out of Juniper's boot before the door finished opening.

They had learned the geometry of the Ember Guild's back room the way they had learned the palace kitchens: by shadow and repetition. The room sat behind the tallow-chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, one room deep and two rooms wide, and it had exactly one door that mattered and one door that lied. The lying door—the one that opened onto the alley—was the one that had just moved.

"Ember and ash," said the shape in the doorway, and the countersign was wrong. It was *ash and ember*, the way it had been for eleven years, and Juniper was already moving, low and silent, when the shape folded at the knees and came down like a sack of wet wool.

The smell reached them a heartbeat later. Blood, and under it something scorched—hair, cloth, flesh cooked at the edge. Juniper had catalogued enough poisons to know the smell of a body burning from the inside. Ember-root, perhaps, or the new thing they'd tasted at court three nights ago, the one that had made their tongue go numb and their ledger of the dead grow by one line.

They lit the stub of candle on the shelf and turned it on the stranger.

A courier. That was the first read, and it was written in the body itself: the hard calves of someone who walked for a living, the boots resoled twice and then patched rather than resoled again, the leather satchel strap worn to a shine across the chest. The rest was harder to read. The courier's left side was soaked dark from ribs to hip, and the dark was still moving, still spreading, a slow black tide across the floorboards. Their lips were gray. Their eyes were open, and they had already found Juniper, and they were not afraid, which told Juniper more than the wound did. People who were afraid begged. People who were not afraid had already spent their fear somewhere worse.

"You're the taster," the courier said. The voice came out in pieces, like coins counted one at a time. "The one who keeps the second set of books."

"I keep the first set," Juniper said. "The second set keeps itself."

A smile flickered at the corner of the courier's mouth and died there. "Then you'll want this. Before the elders talk you out of it."

"Talk me out of what?"

The courier's hand went to the satchel. Juniper watched the motion with a taster's attention—the tremor in the fingers, the way the arm shook under its own weight, the precise arithmetic of how many minutes that body had left. Not many. The tide was winning.

"Ember and ash," Juniper said quietly, giving the countersign back to them, corrected, and knelt. "You came through the lying door."

"There wasn't time to be clever." The courier's hand emerged from the satchel clutching something wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was scorched black at one corner, and when the fingers opened, the thing inside was a map. Folded once, twice, a third time, and burned through at the fold like something had tried to eat it and been interrupted.

"Take it," the courier said. "Take it and read it and don't give it back to them."

"Them being—"

"The elders. The council. Anyone who says it's too old, too dangerous, too *gone*." A cough took the courier then, deep and wet, and Juniper's hands did what their hands always did when a body was failing in front of them: they moved without being asked. They rolled the courier onto their side so the blood could drain instead of drown. They pressed the heel of their palm below the ribs. They knew, with the calm part of their mind that kept accounts even now, that they were performing the gestures of saving rather than the act.

The coughing passed. The courier looked at Juniper with eyes that were going glassy at the edges, and said, "Vessany."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Juniper did not move. That was the training. Eleven years of tasting had taught them that the face was a ledger too, and that every flicker in it was an entry someone else could read. So they kept their face flat and their voice level and said, "There's no such place."

"There's no such *entry*," the courier said, and the ghost of the smile came back. "That's different. That's the whole point, taster. There's a difference between a thing that doesn't exist and a thing that's been struck out. You of all people should know the difference. You spend your life in the margin between them."

Juniper took the map. They told themselves it was only so it wouldn't fall into the blood.

It was warm from the courier's body and lighter than it looked, and through the burn at the fold Juniper could see the edge of ink, a line of coastline drawn with the kind of confidence that only came from having stood on it. They did not open it. Not yet. Opening it was a decision, and Juniper had survived this long by making decisions slowly and in private.

"Who cut you?" they asked instead.

"Vanguard riders. On the Salt Road, two nights out. They weren't looking for me specifically." The courier's breathing had gone shallow and fast, a candle burning down to the tallow. "They were looking for anyone carrying anything. There's a purge coming through the free cities, taster. A census, they're calling it. And behind the census—" the hand lifted, trembling, and tapped the map once, "—behind it, the thing this opens. They can't let it open. And we can't let it stay shut."

"What does it open?"

But the courier's eyes had gone past Juniper's shoulder, to the rafters, to something that wasn't in the room at all. "Tell Odile," they said. "Tell her Maret kept the last delivery. Tell her—" and then the word again, softer, the way you'd say the name of someone you loved: "*Vessany.*"

And then the courier's hand went slack, and the tide finished its arithmetic, and Juniper knelt alone in the candlelight with a dead stranger's blood soaking through their trousers and a burned map in their hands.

* * *

The elders convened at dawn, which was to say that Odile came down the back stairs at first light with her gray braid unbound and her reading spectacles pushed up into her hair like a crown she'd forgotten she was wearing, and found Juniper exactly where they had been when the candle guttered out: sitting on the floor, the courier's body covered with the good wool blanket, the map untouched on their knee.

"You didn't open it," Odile said.

"You told me once that the first rule of accounts is never to total a column before you've read every line."

"I told you that about *money*." Odile crouched, joints cracking, and looked at the dead courier with an expression Juniper had never quite learned to translate. Grief, but grief that had been in the family a long time and knew its manners. "Maret," she said. "Of course it was Maret. That woman could outrun a rumor." She reached out and touched the blanket over the dead shoulder, once, and then stood, and the grief went back wherever she kept it. "The elders are meeting in the counting room. Bring it."

The counting room was the guild's true heart, more than the back room, more than the safehouses: a windowless chamber lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, twenty-two years of them, every person smuggled, sheltered, fed, and ferried out from under the usurper's nose recorded in a hand so small and even it looked printed. The elders sat around the scarred oak table like a tribunal, which, Juniper thought, was what they were.

Odile held out her hand, and Juniper gave her the map, and watched five faces change as she opened it.

The map was of the northern reaches—or where the northern reaches should have been. Past the free cities, past the Salt Road, past the border every atlas in the realm marked with a polite blank and the words *UNSETTLED*, the map showed coastline, harbors, roads, towns. Names in a script Juniper didn't know, and beneath them, in smaller, older ink, translations: *Vessa. The Ninefold Harbor. The Door.*

"There is nothing there," said Elder Corvane, and his voice had the flatness of a man reciting doctrine. "There has been nothing there for twenty years."

"There has been nothing *written* there for twenty years," Odile said.

"Which is the same thing."

"It has never been the same thing. That is the entire reason this guild exists." Odile set the map down and turned it so the burn at the fold faced the table, like a wound presented for diagnosis. "But Corvane is right about one thing. This is a dead country's map, and dead countries are the most dangerous kind of property. The regime burned every record of Vessany two decades ago. The people who carried its name were struck from the rolls, and the people who carried its *papers* were struck from the world. If the court learns this exists, they will not come for the map. They will come for every hand that touched it." Her eyes went around the table. "We vote. Burn it, and Maret's last delivery dies with her."

Juniper heard themselves speak before they had decided to. "You can't."

Five faces turned.

"You can't burn it," Juniper said again, and their voice was steadier than they felt, which was the only kind of steady they had ever been. "Because it's not a stranger's map. Look at the hand. Look at the *handwriting*."

They crossed to the shelf behind Odile, to the ledgers no one touched anymore, and drew out the first of them—the founding book, the one whose first entry Juniper had copied out as a practice exercise nine years ago and never forgotten, because the hand that wrote it had been beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. They laid it open on the table beside the map.

The entries read: *One family, four souls, out by the mill road. Paid the ferryman double; he asked no names. Ember keep them.* And the map, along its southern edge, in the same small, even, blade-beautiful script: *The road ends where the water begins. Knock, and be named.*

Same hand. Same slant on the descenders. Same habit of crossing the *t* with a single impatient stroke, as if the writer were always in a hurry to reach the next word.

"That's her," Juniper said. "That's the founder. The one nobody will name. She wrote this map, and she wrote our first ledger, and she wrote them with the same pen." They looked up at the elders. "You've been keeping her books for twenty-two years. This is the rest of the set."

Silence, of the kind that has weight and temperature.

Corvane recovered first. "Even so. Even if it's hers. A dead woman's map to a dead country, and the vanguard already killing couriers over it. The arithmetic is simple, taster. We are two hundred souls in this city and perhaps two thousand across the free cities. One map is not worth two thousand lives."

"The census is coming," said Elder Ives quietly, from the end of the table. "You've all heard. Every name in every parish, every roll, every register. They're not counting the living, Corvane. They're counting the *leftover*." He nodded at the map. "And when the leftover runs out, they'll need new paper."

"Then we burn it and survive," Corvane said.

"Or we open it and *live*," Juniper said, and heard the difference in their own voice, and hated how loud it was, and could not make it quiet. "I taste for a usurper who signs death warrants between courses. I have kept your accounts for nine years and I have never once written my own name in them. I am very good at being no one. But Maret bled out on our floor to put this in my hands, and she said *don't give it back to them*, and she meant you." They picked up the map. Folded it along its old creases. Slid it inside their shirt, against their skin, where the palace's knives couldn't reach without an appointment. "So I'm not giving it back. I'm sorry. I'll take whatever the guild decides that costs me."

Odile was looking at them with the spectacles still pushed up in her hair, and her face, for once, was perfectly legible. It was the face of a woman watching a column refuse to total the way she'd expected.

"The vote is deferred," she said. "Corvane, Ives—we'll speak tonight." And then, as the elders rose and filed out, muttering, she caught Juniper's sleeve, and her grip was iron, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"You recognized the hand in under a minute," she said. "I've had that map in my sight for ten minutes and I still can't be sure. What else do you know about our founder, Juniper Lockridge?"

"Nothing," Juniper said. "That's what frightens me. I know her *hand*. I've been reading her handwriting for nine years and I never once asked who she was." They touched the map's edge through the cloth of their shirt. "And now she's asked me something back."

Outside, on Candlewick Lane, the town criers had begun their morning rounds, and their voices carried through the chandler's shutters, thin and official and new:

*"By decree of the Lord Protector: a general census of all souls, all parishes, all cities. Every resident will be counted. Every count will be verified. Praise order. Praise the crown."*

Juniper stood very still in the back room, beside the blanket-covered dead, and listened to the empire begin to count.

Then they took the first ledger down from the shelf, and a pen, and ink, and turned to the last blank page, and began—carefully, in their own small, even hand—to copy the map.
