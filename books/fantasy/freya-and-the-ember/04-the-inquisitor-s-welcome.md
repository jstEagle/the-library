## 4. The Inquisitor's Welcome

The census writ arrived at dawn, and Sable Vane read it three times before the wax had finished cooling on her acknowledgment.

*By order of the Lord Protector, a full accounting of persons residing within the realm and its tributary cities shall be conducted...*

A full accounting. She turned the phrase over the way she'd once turned river stones as a child, looking for what lived underneath. Twenty years of service to the Office of Records, and she knew what an accounting meant. It meant lists. It meant names crossed out and names underlined. It meant doors knocked upon at hours when honest people were still abed, and questions asked in tones that made lying feel like confession.

It also meant travel. The census would roll through the free cities like a threshing drum, and someone would have to walk ahead of it, tasting the water before the state drank deep.

Sable set down the writ and looked out her window at the capital smoldering grey in early light. Somewhere down there, in the palace kitchens, Juniper Lockridge was probably already awake.

She hadn't let herself think that name in years. She'd buried it so thoroughly that unearthing it now felt like disturbing a grave she'd dug herself—carefully, with ceremony, with all the correct words spoken over it. *Dead. Gone in the fire. A pity.* She had been eleven years old and very good, even then, at writing things into ledgers that made them true.

But last night, at the Protector's winter feast, she had seen him.

Her.

Them. Sable still didn't know which pronoun fit the person Juniper Lockridge had become, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed coin.

* * *

The feast had been tedious in the usual way: forty courses, three toasts to the Protector's health, a visiting margrave who laughed too loudly at his own wit. Sable had attended in her official capacity, seated below the salt but above the servants, close enough to observe the high table without being observed herself. That was the inquisitor's art, really. To be furniture with eyes.

And then the taster had come forward for the fourth course—a quince custard, gilded, absurd—and Sable's spoon had stopped halfway to her mouth.

She knew that walk. Not the walk itself; the walk was new, trained, invisible, the walk of someone who had learned to be a shadow among shadows. But underneath it, in the set of the shoulders and the particular way the head tilted before a decision, she knew it the way you know a song from your childhood—the words gone, the melody intact.

The taster lifted the spoon. Tasted. Waited the full count, jaw still, breath shallow, eyes fixed on nothing. Then nodded to the steward, and the court fell upon its custard.

Sable did not eat hers. She watched instead, and the watching became unbearable, and so she did the thing she had trained twenty years never to do at table: she stared.

The taster felt it. Of course they did—you didn't survive poison duty by missing a stare. Their gaze came up, touched hers for half a heartbeat, and slid away with a smoothness so perfect it could only be practiced. No flicker of recognition. No widening of the eyes. Nothing.

That was the worst part. Not that Juniper might have changed. That Juniper had become so good at not being anyone that even Sable couldn't find the girl who used to dare her to steal plums from Widow Marsh's tree.

Except—there. As the taster withdrew along the wall, one hand brushed the table's edge, quick and light, two fingers tapping twice. An old habit. A counting habit. Juniper had always counted things: steps, plums, stars through the smoke-hole of the roof they'd shared as children in Ashfall Hollow. *Two for luck, Sable. Three for a lie.*

Two taps. Luck, then. Or a lie.

Sable had left the feast early and walked the long way home through the frozen streets, telling herself she was mistaken. Telling herself that grief made ghosts of strangers. But she had spent her whole career learning to tell a forged signature from a true one, and there was no forgery in those two taps. Only the original hand.

Juniper Lockridge was alive. Had been alive for twenty years, ten feet from the throne, wearing the Protector's livery, eating his poisons for him.

And had never once come home.

* * *

The fire was the first entry in the ledger of Sable's life, though no one had ever called it that. Ashfall Hollow, autumn, twenty-two years ago. Eleven houses burned. Nine dead. Among them, by every record kept afterward, the entire Lockridge family: mother, father, one child.

Sable remembered it differently. She remembered waking to orange light, her mother dragging her into the lane, the sound of the Lockridges' roof falling in like a held breath released. And she remembered Juniper standing in the crowd beside her, small and soot-streaked and silent, watching the house where their parents slept become a black skeleton against the stars.

By morning, Juniper was gone. By the following spring, so was any record that they had ever existed. The parish rolls listed nine dead. The Lockridge name appeared nowhere. When Sable, older and employed by the Office of Records, had gone looking—quietly, off-duty, ashamed of how much she wanted to know—she found the page for Ashfall Hollow that year clean as a licked plate. Someone had erased an entire family from the books, and done it well.

She had told herself then what she told herself now: that records were the state's memory, and the state forgot what it chose to forget, and it was not her place to remember harder than her office allowed.

She had been promoted four times on the strength of that discipline.

Now she stood at her window with the census writ in her hands and understood, with the cold clarity she usually reserved for other people's crimes, that she had just been handed the instrument to dig up everything she'd buried.

A census counted everyone. That was its purpose and its terror. Every soul in the realm and the tributary cities, entered, numbered, filed. If Juniper Lockridge existed anywhere in the world, the census would find them—or find the space where they should be, the hole shaped exactly like a person.

Unless someone got to them first.

* * *

She drafted the request that afternoon, and she made it flawless, because everything she wrote was flawless, because flawlessness was the only armor the Office issued.

*To the Master of Household: Inquisitor Vane requests the assignment of a qualified food-taster to accompany the census progress through the free cities, the duration of said progress being estimated at four months. The roads are uncertain, the cities restive, and the Protector's representatives will dine in places where loyalty is thin and arsenic is cheap. I request the taster Lockridge, whose service record is exemplary.*

Every word true. That was the beauty of it. She had learned from the best forgers that the most durable lies were built entirely of true sentences, arranged in an order that betrayed them.

She read it over twice. Her pen hovered at the bottom, and she almost—almost—added a personal line. *I knew them once. I would vouch for them.* She struck the thought before it could reach paper. Vouching was a debt, and debts were entries, and entries could be read back to you in rooms without windows.

She sealed it and sent it by the fast courier, and then she sat in her office while the light went blue and cold, and let herself do the thing she had forbidden herself since the feast: she remembered.

Plums, stolen warm off the south-facing branch, juice running down their wrists as they crouched in the hedge laughing with their mouths shut. Juniper teaching her which mushrooms were liars—which ones dressed themselves up as safe and killed you anyway. *Taste a little first, always. Spit if it stings your tongue-root.* She had thought it a game. Juniper had been training, even then, at eight years old, for a life of tasting death in small doses. Who trains a child for that? What parent, what master, what guild looks at a child and thinks: *this one will survive what kills everyone else*?

The question turned over in her, slow and ugly. Because there was another question underneath it, the one she had carried for twenty years like a stone in her boot:

Why did the records burn cleaner than the village did?

Fires destroy papers, yes. But someone had rebuilt the parish rolls after Ashfall Hollow. Someone had written the new ones. And in the new ones, the Lockridges had never lived at all.

Sable rose, lit her lamp, and pulled the file she had started the night before—the one that lived, for now, only in her head and in a locked drawer that officially contained nothing. On its first page, in her own precise hand, she had written a single line:

*J.L. — alive. Position: taster to the Protector. Status: ?*

Status unknown. That was the honest entry. Was Juniper a fugitive hiding in plain sight? An agent? A survivor who had simply, sensibly, never looked back? Or something worse—something that explained why a whole family had been scraped from the record by hands more careful than fire?

She didn't know. And Sable Vane had built her life on the principle that not-knowing was not a condition to endure but a defect to repair.

The reply from the Master of Household came within the day, fast enough to suggest her request had pleased someone important. *Granted. The taster Lockridge will present themselves to your progress at the River Gate, first light, three days hence.*

Three days.

Sable stood at her window again as the lamps of the capital came alight below her, thousands of small flames in a city that believed itself recorded down to the last chimney. Somewhere among them moved people the books had never heard of. People like Juniper. People like whoever had erased Juniper.

In three days she would stand face to face with the only witness to her childhood who was still breathing, and she would have to decide, in the first five seconds, whether to say *I know you* or to let the silence do its work.

An inquisitor's whole craft was the second option. Let them talk. Let them think themselves unseen. Watch the hands, not the mouth.

But her hands, she noticed, were not steady. She folded them behind her back, the way she did in interrogations, and made them be steady, and failed, and folded them again.

*Two taps for luck,* Juniper had taught her, laughing, plum-juice on her chin. *Three for a lie.*

Sable looked at her own reflection in the darkening glass—grey-eyed, grey-cloaked, a woman made of accurate entries—and wondered which one she was about to give.

At the River Gate, in three days, the census would begin. And whatever else happened on that road, she would keep Juniper Lockridge close—close enough to watch, close enough to question, close enough, when the moment came, to open like a lock.

She only had to decide, by then, whether she was picking it or breaking it.
