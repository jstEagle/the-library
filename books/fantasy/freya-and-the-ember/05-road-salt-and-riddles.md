## 5. Road Salt and Riddles

The progress left the capital in a column of silk and dust, forty wagons of the usurper's court rolling north through the free cities like a wound being dragged across the land. I rode in the third wagon, beside the salt cellars and the spice chests, because a taster's place was always one step from the stores and two steps from the privy. Nobody looked at me. That was the point of me.

Nobody looked at me, which meant nobody saw me slip out of the camp at Wrenford with a covered dish of candied plums and a message folded into the wax seal.

The Ember safehouse in Wrenford lived behind a dyer's shop, up stairs that smelled of mordant and vinegar. The woman who opened the door had blue-stained fingers and Odile's training written all over her—the way she checked the street twice before letting me in, the way she counted my escort's positions by sound alone.

"Plums," she said, taking the dish.

"From the court kitchens," I said. "Sweetened with honey from the southern valleys. There are eleven jars unaccounted for this season."

Her eyes flicked up. Eleven. The guild's old signal: *we have work, we need hands.* She peeled back the wax, read what I'd hidden beneath it, and her face did something complicated—hope and dread fighting for the same ground.

"The census is coming here next week," she said quietly. "You know that."

"I know that." I looked at the dye vats, the hanging skeins of wool gone red and violet and rust. "Which is why the guild needs people who can walk out ahead of it. People who know how to be uncounted."

She was silent a long moment. Then: "There's a boy upstairs who ran from Odile's house three months ago. He's been sleeping in my loft and eating my profits and driving me half mad. He wants to matter to something." Her stained fingers drummed the dish. "Take him before I strangle him."

* * *

Perrin was seventeen, all elbows and grievance, with ink on his knuckles and Odile's cipher running in his head like a second heartbeat. He'd copied ledgers for the guild since he was twelve; he'd run because Odile refused to send him on deliveries, and because seventeen-year-olds mistake protection for prison.

"You're the taster," he said, when I climbed into the loft. Not a question. "Odile has your handwriting memorized. She says you write numbers like they're prayers."

"Odile says a lot of things."

"She says you're the only one who could carry this." He jerked his chin at my satchel, where the scorched map lay wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs. He didn't ask to see it. That, too, was Odile's training: never ask about what you can't unknow. "I'm coming with you."

"You're a runaway apprentice with no name in any census roll. You're exactly the kind of person the Ember Guild exists to protect, not spend."

"I'm exactly the kind of person," Perrin said, "who knows every safehouse between here and the Saltmarsh, because I copied their accounts. Every one. With margins notes." He grinned, and there was something fierce and frightened under it. "I counted myself out of existence three months ago. I'd like to count for something instead."

I thought of Odile's voice in my memory, dry as old paper: *Everyone who joins you will be someone else's loss, Juniper. Get used to carrying other people's grief alongside your own.*

"Pack light," I said. "We leave before the census riders arrive."

* * *

The deserter cook found us, which was how Tammeran insisted on telling it ever after, though the truth was that I bought him.

He was working a stew pot at a crossroads inn outside Marrowbridge, feeding the census clerks who'd stopped to water their horses, and I watched him ladle out portions with a soldier's economy and a cook's contempt. When I ordered, he gave me the flat look of a man who recognized another professional.

"Your broth is oversalted," I said.

"Your palate is spoiled by palace stock," he said. Then, lower: "But you're right. It is. Deliberately. Salt hides the taste of things you don't want to think about."

I sat down at his end of the counter while the clerks argued over their rolls. "Tammeran Vetch. Fourth Company, siege kitchens. Deserted at Corven's Ford after you were ordered to cook for a town your company had orders to burn. You've been moving west ever since, cooking for whoever doesn't ask questions."

His ladle stopped midair. "Who told you that?"

"A ledger." I let that sit. "The Ember Guild keeps accounts too, cook. Yours says you've fed forty-one unrecorded people out of this kitchen in the last year. Leftovers, you called them. Extra portions. You've been running a safehouse out of a soup pot and pretending it's charity."

For a moment I thought he'd deny it. Instead he laughed—a short, cracked sound—and wiped his hands on his apron. "Forty-one. You people actually counted." Something moved behind his eyes that I recognized, because I kept it in my own chest too: the strange relief of being seen by someone who understood the arithmetic. "What do you want?"

"A head cook for a company going somewhere dangerous. Someone who can feed eight people out of nothing and make soldiers believe he's one of them."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I eat my stew and compliment the salt, and the census finds you within the month anyway. They're checking kitchen workers first. Feeding the unrecorded is now classified as sedition." I watched him absorb that. "I'm not threatening you, Tammeran. I'm just reading the ledger out loud."

He untied his apron before I finished speaking.

* * *

The deaf cartographer took the most finding, and cost us the most fear.

Her name was Isbet, and she worked out of a map-seller's stall in the Saltmarsh market, drawing charts so precise that smugglers paid triple for them and never asked why a woman who couldn't hear the auction noise around her could render a coastline down to the individual rock. Perrin knew of her from the guild's expense books—*I. of the Saltmarsh, charts, 4 crowns*—and it was Perrin who figured out what I'd missed: she'd been drawing coastlines that no living surveyor had mapped. Coastlines from memory. Coastlines of a country that officially did not exist.

We approached her stall on a gray morning with gulls screaming over the fish-cries. She saw us coming—saw everything, always—and set down her pen with the deliberate calm of someone who'd been waiting years for this conversation.

I wrote on my slate: *We have a map. We need someone who can read it.*

She wrote back without hesitation: *Show me.*

In the back room, among rolled vellum and jars of cut quills, I unwrapped the scorched map for the third time since Wrenford. It lay on her table like a wounded thing—burned at the edges, its ink faded to the brown of dried blood, showing roads and rivers and place-names in an alphabet none of us could fully parse. Perrin had spent two nights on it and gotten fragments. Tammeran had stared at it and said it looked like a country having a fever.

Isbet bent over it. And then she did something none of us expected.

She pricked her thumb on her pen-knife—one sharp motion, barely a wince—and pressed the bead of blood to the map's center, where a burned-away crest sat like a missing tooth.

The map woke.

I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it properly. Ink moved. Lines that had been faded or burned drew themselves again, darkening like veins filling, and the unknown alphabet resolved—not into words, but into shapes, into *geography*, rivers braiding and unbraiding, mountain ranges rising off the vellum in faint relief as though the paper remembered being terrain. And across the whole surface spread a warmth, a low amber glow, like embers breathing under ash.

Perrin made a small sound and stepped backward into a shelf. Tammeran swore with a soldier's fluency.

Isbet went very still. Then she began to laugh—silent laughter, shoulders shaking, tears sliding down her face—and wrote on her slate with a hand that trembled:

*I know this country. My mother sang me its lullabies. She told me never to tell anyone where she was born, because the people who erased it would come for anyone who remembered. I thought she meant it as a story.*

She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and blazing.

*It wasn't a story. It was a survival instruction. And I am the last person alive who draws these coasts from memory.*

I wrote slowly, aware that whatever I put on the slate next would change both our lives: *The map opened for your blood. The founder of our guild came from Vessany. We think the map leads to a door—an escape route out of the free cities before the armies arrive. The guild elders were too afraid to use it. I'm not.*

Isbet wiped her eyes, picked up her knife, and pricked her other thumb. She pressed it beside the first print and wrote beneath them, deliberate as an engraver:

*Two drops. One for the country they erased. One for the one I choose. Where do we start?*

* * *

We left the Saltmarsh at dusk, five days ahead of the census riders, and I want to record honestly how strange it felt—that small procession on the north road. A runaway apprentice arguing with a deserter about the correct way to pack a stew pot. A deaf cartographer riding point, reading the road with her whole body, alert as a deer. And me, the invisible man, the no one, suddenly responsible for four lives and a burning map and a door I couldn't yet imagine opening.

Found family, Odile would have called it, and sneered at the sentimentality even as she practiced it. But riding through the cold gold of evening with Tammeran humming off-key and Perrin quizzing Isbet in slate-scrawl about Vessani river names, I understood something I'd never let myself understand at court:

Being no one had kept me alive. But it had never once made me feel alive.

That night, camped in a hollow with the map glowing faintly in Isbet's lap like a tame fire, Perrin took the first watch and I took the second, and during mine I saw it: far off on the southern horizon, past the dark shoulder of the hills, a line of lights that did not belong to any town.

Too regular. Too many. Moving.

Fires of an army on the march—or fires of a census column burning the records of everyone it caught.

I woke Isbet and showed her the slate. She studied the lights a long time, then wrote one word and held it up to the ember-glow of the map:

*Faster.*
