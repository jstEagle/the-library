## 6. The Census Rolls Through

The river town was called Brackenholt, and it smelled of fish guts and wet rope, and Sable Vane hated it with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of a woman who had grown up smelling of woodsmoke and sheep.

She had set her table in the market square beneath the town's one surviving elm, because the census was meant to be seen. That was half its function. A ledger no one feared was just paper; a ledger people feared was an instrument. She sat behind a folding desk of government oak, the imperial seal stamped in gold on its front, and beside her stood Corporal Ives, who was large and dull and useful in exactly those proportions, and behind them both waited the wagon with its iron brazier, unlit for now.

"Name," she said to the first in line, a woman with flour to her elbows.

"Marta Ellery."

"Trade."

"Baker."

"Children."

"Two. Both living."

Sable wrote it down. The ink caught the morning light and shone like something wet. *Both living.* In twenty years of rolls she had learned that people volunteered that phrase the way they touched iron—reflexively, superstitiously, as if saying it could keep it true.

By midmorning the line stretched past the well. By noon she had recorded two hundred and eleven souls: millers, coopers, a midwife, three boys who claimed to be fourteen and were not. It was ordinary work. It was good work. Every name entered was a name made real, fixed against chaos, anchored to the state like a boat to its mooring. Her father had taught her that before he taught her letters—*a thing written down can't be taken away*—and she had built her whole life inside that sentence.

Then came the ferryman.

He was a narrow man, sun-browned, with hands knotted from rope. He gave his name as Aldous Ferren, trade ferryman, wife living, no children. All correct. All smooth. But when Sable asked him to speak his mother's name for the record—a new requirement, bloodlines being this year's obsession—he paused. Just a breath's length. Just long enough.

"Yelena," he said.

Sable wrote it down without looking up, and then, because she was thorough rather than clever, she wrote it again in her private notebook, where she kept things that did not belong in official records. Because Yelena was not a name from anywhere in the nine provinces. Yelena was a Vessani name. She knew that the way she knew the weight of her own sword—not from study but from handling. There had been Vessani names in the archives she'd catalogued during her training, old ones, pre-annexation ones, kept in a sealed room in the capital that clerks called the Dead Registry. Names that ended in -ena and -issa and -or. Names that belonged to a country struck from every map so thoroughly that schoolchildren now believed the eastern coast had always been empty cliffs.

"Aldous Ferren," she said pleasantly. "And where was your mother born?"

"Here," he said. "Brackenholt."

"Born here, in Brackenholt, named Yelena." Sable smiled at him the way she smiled at suspects whose alibis were too polished. "That's a lovely old name. Very regional."

His throat moved. "It's just a name, inquisitor."

"It is indeed." She closed the record book. "Next."

But she watched him walk away, and she watched the way two other people in the line turned their heads to track him—not with curiosity but with fear, the specific fear of people watching someone else step near an edge they themselves were standing on—and she understood that Brackenholt had more than one secret in it.

* * *

She found the second thread that evening, going through the town's parish records by lamplight in the vestry, because the census required cross-verification and because Sable Vane had never in her life done a thing halfway.

Burials, mostly. Twenty years of burials. And there, scattered through the decades like coins dropped in grass: *Yelena Ferren, née Ostrava.* *Mikael Dornissa.* *A child, Anouk, surname unrecorded.* Each one buried under a provincial name in the church register and each one, if you knew how to look, wearing its Vessani bones underneath. Families that had come east after the annexation—or been brought east, or fled west, the records didn't say, because the records had been ordered not to say—and had spent a generation sanding themselves smooth, filing off the syllables, teaching their children to answer questions with provincial flat vowels.

Except they hadn't quite managed it. A grandmother's lullaby survived. A naming custom survived—the Vessani gave mothers' names at burial, not fathers'; she'd found four instances of it, all consistent, all invisible unless you went looking. A country erased on paper does not die in the mouth. It goes underground and keeps breathing.

Sable sat back in the vestry's creaking chair and felt something she did not have a word for. Not doubt. Doubt was a luxury for philosophers. This was closer to vertigo—the sensation of standing on solid ground and learning the ground was a floor, and under the floor, a cellar, and under the cellar, who knew.

Twenty years ago the empire had annexed Vessany, and then it had done something worse than conquer it. It had declared the conquest complete by declaring the country imaginary. No province status. No representation. No rolls. The people were dispersed, renamed, absorbed—and anyone who persisted in calling themselves Vessani was, by definition, a liar, because there was no such thing to be. She had read the edicts. She had admired their elegance, once. You don't need to kill a people if you can make them impossible.

*You're thinking like a clerk,* she told herself. *The edicts are the law. The law is the ledger. The ledger is the state.* Her father's voice, her instructors' voices, twenty years of certainty, all reciting in chorus.

And underneath the chorus, quiet as a buried name: *the ferryman's hands shook.*

* * *

The third thread came to her instead of being sought, which was how she should have known it mattered.

A boy brought it. Fourteen, maybe, barefoot, waiting until the last of the day's line had gone before approaching her table with the exaggerated casualness of the very young doing something very dangerous. He set down a scrap of paper, said "found it by the ferry landing," and walked off too fast.

The paper was a fragment. Burned at one edge, water-stained at the other. Most of it was a drawing—coastline, a river's mouth, contour lines drawn by someone who loved terrain the way other men loved women. And in the corner, a stamp: a small ember shape pressed in grey wax, half melted away.

Sable knew that mark. Every inquisitor knew it. The Ember Guild—the smugglers, the erasers' enemies, the network that moved people the empire had struck from existence out through the free cities and into lives the ledgers couldn't follow. Three years she had hunted them. Three years of cold trails, because you cannot catch an organization whose entire genius is that it keeps no records.

*No records,* she thought, turning the fragment over. Except this was a record. A map, drawn by hand, marked with their seal. Which meant somewhere there was a keeper of it. An archivist. Someone who wrote things down.

Her whole career, the Ember Guild had been a ghost story. Now, suddenly, it had handwriting.

She thought of Juniper Lockridge then—she had been trying not to, all day, the way you try not to touch a bruise. The taster. The court's quiet shadow with the careful face. She had known them the instant she saw them across the throne room, before they'd even turned around: the way they held still while everything moved, the economy of their gestures. Tam. From the village that burned. Who had vanished the same week as the fire, leaving nothing—not a body, not a grave, not a single line in any record Sable had ever been able to pull. As if someone had erased them. As if someone had been *practicing.*

Tam, who tasted poison for a living and never flinched. Tam, who had been assigned to her progress through the free cities with paperwork so smooth it had to be arranged, and Sable had signed the assignment herself and told herself it was strategy: keep your suspect close, watch the hands.

She looked at the map fragment, at the burned edge, and thought: *someone carried this, and someone chased them, and the courier died or nearly died getting it delivered.* To whom? To what door?

The lamplight guttered. Outside, Brackenholt settled into night, the river muttering to itself, and somewhere in that town, she would have bet her commission, the ferryman was telling someone that the inquisitor had asked about his mother.

* * *

Two days later, her superiors answered her report about the Vessani names.

They answered with fire.

She had requested guidance—proper procedure, proper channels: *several residents appear to be of unregistered origin, possibly descended from annexed populations, requesting instructions regarding classification.* It was the correct bureaucratic language. Classification, not accusation. She had even, though she did not write this part, imagined a reasonable outcome: retroactive registration, an amnesty schedule, names entered into the rolls at last. That was what the ledger was *for*. That was the entire point. A thing written down can't be taken away.

The instruction came back by courier, sealed with the chancellor's own signet, and it was one line long:

*Unrecorded persons are not persons. Eradicate the anomaly.*

She read it twice. Then she rode to the address the local constable had given her—the Ferren house, at the end of the lane past the salt meadows, because of course it was the ferryman's family, of course it was the man whose only crime had been a pause before a name.

She was too late by twenty minutes.

The house was still burning when she arrived, and the constable's men stood around it in a loose ring, keeping order, keeping watch, keeping their faces carefully blank in the way of men following orders they hope will be forgotten. The fire made a sound like the river, only angrier. Sable sat on her horse at the edge of the lane and did not dismount, because if she dismounted she would have to do something, and whatever she did would end her, and some cold surviving core of her noted even now that she calculated this—that she stood in the heat of a family's annihilation and *calculated*—and hated herself for it with a hatred that felt almost like relief, because it proved she was still capable of hating.

Aldous Ferren was dead in the yard. So was his wife. The neighbors would find no bodies to bury under mothers' names. The anomaly was eradicated. The ledger stayed clean.

Corporal Ives cleared his throat beside her. "Orders, ma'am?"

Sable looked at the burning house. Somewhere in the roar she heard her father's voice—*a thing written down can't be taken away*—and understood, finally, with the clarity that comes too late and costs everything, that he had it backwards. A thing written down can be taken away. It happens every day. What can't be taken away is what people refuse to stop being, what survives underground in lullabies and naming customs and pauses before a mother's name. The empire had twenty years of edicts and a sealed room full of dead names, and it had just lost to a fishing family in a river town, because the family had been real and the edicts were only paper.

The ledger-state, she saw now, was not a record of the world. It was a weapon aimed at it. And she had spent her whole life polishing the barrel.

"Ma'am?" Ives said again.

"The census is concluded in Brackenholt," Sable said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that. "We move on tomorrow. Free cities route, as scheduled." She turned her horse. Behind her the fire collapsed inward with a sound like a held breath released, and sparks went up into the dark, dozens of them, rising, uncounted.

That night she took out her private notebook and, beneath the entry *Yelena*, wrote a single line in her smallest, most controlled hand:

*The Ember Guild keeps accounts of the erased. Find the keeper. Find the map. Find Lockridge.*

Then, after a long moment, she added one more line, and it was not an order to herself, and it was not strategy, and she did not let herself examine it:

*Tam. If you can hear this. Run.*
