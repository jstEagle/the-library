## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bone had been soaking in lye water for three days when Ulyana Pemberton finally took it out to carve.

It was a shinbone from a yearling ewe, chosen for its density and its pale, unblemished grain — the kind of bone that held a name the way river ice holds a stone: clearly, until spring. She dried it with linen, turned it under the lamp three times, and set it on her workbench beside the awls and rasps and the little bronze burin she'd inherited from her mother along with everything else that mattered.

Outside the workshop window, Thornmere Valley went about its evening. Smoke rose straight from the chimneys of Low Row. Someone was beating a rug on a porch rail, the dull thud-thud carrying across the still air like a slow heart. The boundary stones stood at the valley's mouth where they had always stood, gray as old teeth, wearing their moss like mourning veils. Nothing crossed them. Nothing ever did.

Ulyana picked up the burin.

*His name was Tam,* she reminded herself, though she didn't need reminding. *Tam Wickerby. Six years old. Fell through the millrace ice on the fourth of Thawmonth.*

She had not been there when they pulled him out. She never was. But the body had come to her workshop within the hour, wrapped in the miller's own coat, because in Thornmere Valley everyone knew what Ulyana Pemberton did with bones. Not magic — the elders were very clear about this, from the pulpit and the market square and the long table at council meetings, *there is no magic in Thornmere, there has been no magic since the Founding* — but craft. Craft was permitted. Craft was a woman bent over a bench with tools in her hands, doing honest work. Whatever else it might be, no one said aloud, and Ulyana had learned not to say it either.

She set the burin's tip against the bone's crown end and began the first line of the memorial script, the deep vertical stroke that meant *a life*, and the boy's voice came back to her.

Not his voice exactly. Not sound, even. It came the way it always came — behind her ears, in the marrow of her own skull, a pressure that resolved into words if she let it. The dead left their loudest moment clinging to them like riverwater, and when she carved their names into bone, the moment loosened and poured out.

*I can touch the bottom. Mama, look, I can touch the—*

Ulyana closed her eyes. Breathed. Kept the burin moving.

This was the part no one asked about, and she had stopped offering it. When the Wickerbys came tomorrow to collect the finished charm — a smooth white rod the length of her forearm, the boy's name spiraling down it in letters fine as root-hairs, ending in a small carved wren because Tam had loved wrens — they would hold it and feel something settle in their chests, and they would say *thank you, carver, thank you,* and they would hang it above their hearth, and they would sleep a little better. They would not ask why the charm worked. In Thornmere Valley, you did not ask why the things that worked worked. You asked whether the boundary stones needed repointing. You asked whether anyone had seen wolves beyond the pass. You kept your questions small enough to fit inside the valley, because the valley had spent four hundred years teaching itself to forget, and it did not appreciate being reminded.

But Ulyana remembered. Remembering was her whole trade.

She carved the second stroke, the crossbar that meant *beloved*, and the voice again, thinner now, further downstream:

*—cold, it's so cold, I want to go home, I want—*

"I know," she said quietly, to the bone, to the boy. "I'm taking you home."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first stars were pricking through the blue over Thornfell, and somewhere down in Low Row a dog began to bark at nothing in particular.

* * *

Her grandmother had called the gift *the listening*. Ulyana's mother had called it nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer. Both women had done the same work Ulyana did now — bone-charms for the dead, cut in the old script that no book in the valley recorded because no book in the valley admitted it existed. Three generations of Pemberton women, bending over benches by lamplight, hearing the dead say their last words, and never once writing down what the words were. Some things belonged to the dead alone.

The gift was strongest at the edges. That was the strange arithmetic of it, the thing Ulyana had mapped over twenty years without ever telling anyone she was mapping. Here in the village center, a hundred paces from the meeting hall, she heard only whispers — fragments, half-syllables, the dead murmuring in their sleep. But walk out past the tannery, past the last fence post, to where the fields gave onto the tree line and the nearest boundary stone stood a quarter mile off across the meadow, and the voices came clear as bells. As though the stones themselves pressed down on the world like a hand pressing cloth under water, and everything the cloth held floated up at the seams.

Magic could not cross into Thornmere. Everyone knew that. A hedge-witch from the hill country had tried it once, forty years ago, and her spells had died at the stones like moths against glass. Charms failed. Blessings curdled. Even the weather seemed to arrive already domesticated, rain falling gentler inside the valley than outside it, as if the sky itself had agreed to behave.

And yet the listening stayed. Ulyana had wondered about that all her life, in the private chamber of her mind where she kept the questions too large for the valley. If the covenant — for it was called a covenant, in the one fragmentary verse her grandmother had taught her, *the covenant holds, the covenant keeps, sleep, gods, sleep* — if the covenant silenced everything, why not her? Why leave one thin thread of the old world dangling into Thornmere like a root through a cellar wall?

She had no answer. She had a workbench, and lamplight, and a bone that needed finishing.

By full dark the spiral was complete. She blew the bone dust from the grooves — it drifted gold through the lamplight, briefly alive — and ran her thumb down the length of the charm. Tam's voice had gone quiet now, the way it always did when the work was done. Released, or emptied; she'd never decided which. The wren at the charm's foot looked ready to fly.

She wrapped it in linen and sat for a while with her hands flat on the bench, listening to the ordinary sounds of the valley settling into night. The rug-beating had stopped. The dog had given up. Water moved somewhere under the ground, in the old channels, going wherever water goes when no one is asking it to be anything.

Loneliness, at this hour, had a texture. Ulyana knew it well: smooth and cool, like river-polished bone. She had built her whole life around it the way you build a house around a hearth. There was competence, which filled the days. There was the town's gratitude, which filled some of the gaps. And there was the listening, which filled the rest — badly, wonderfully, the way a fire fills a room with light and smoke both. The dead were company of a sort. They were also proof, every one of them, that she would end up on the other side of the silence someday, and that between here and there she would always be the one who heard and was never quite heard.

She was blowing out the lamp when the prayer came.

* * *

It happened the way weather happens. One moment the night was the night she had known for thirty-one years; the next, the air over the garden path changed pressure, the way it does before a storm, and a voice spoke — a real voice, out loud, in the open air, made of the darkness itself.

*Please. Please, whoever's left. Whoever remembers. My daughter is sick and the herbs aren't working and I don't know what else to do. Please.*

Ulyana froze with her fingers on the lamp key.

The voice was a woman's, ragged with crying, and it was coming from everywhere and nowhere — from the direction of the old shrine-garden on her grandmother's land, maybe, or from underneath the world. It hung in the air the way thunder hangs after the flash. And then, before Ulyana had taken a single breath, something answered.

Not another voice. A *presence*. A vast slow attention turning over in the dark like a whale surfacing, like a mountain deciding to notice the weather. Ulyana felt it move through the valley the way she felt rain coming in her bad knee — a pressure, a weight, an immense reluctant wakefulness — and then it *spoke*, and the speaking was not sound at all but meaning laid directly into the world, into the bean rows and the fence posts and the marrow of her bones:

***I remember.***

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The stars did not move. Somewhere far off, at the valley's edge, every dog began to howl at once, and just as suddenly stopped.

Then it was gone. The night resumed. Crickets started up again as if they'd only paused to listen, and the smoke rose straight from the chimneys, and Thornmere Valley slept on in its four-hundred-year conviction that nothing old lived here anymore.

Ulyana stood in her doorway for a long time, one hand pressed flat against her sternum, feeling the words fade out of her bones the way warmth fades out of a stone after sunset.

A prayer she had never spoken. An answer she had never heard. In the valley where magic could not cross the boundary stones, something had just reached across them anyway — or worse, something had been *inside* all along, sleeping, and had just opened one eye.

Her grandmother's land. The shrine-garden. Overgrown since before Ulyana was born; she'd used it, as a child, as the setting for games she couldn't explain even then, hiding among the tumbled stones of a well that had no bottom and talking to someone she'd called the Green Lady, the way children talk to people who aren't there. Her mother had forbidden the garden. Her grandmother had only ever smiled.

The listening had always been strongest at the edges. And the shrine-garden sat at the very edge of the Pemberton land, closer to the boundary stones than any other point on the property — close enough, Ulyana realized now, standing in her doorway with her heart going like a millwheel, that whatever lay sleeping there had spent decades listening to *her*.

To her carvings. To her voice calling the names of the dead into bone, night after night, year after year, in the old script, in the old tongue. A lullaby sung to something that was supposed to stay asleep.

*I remember,* it had said. Two words, and they had rearranged the entire architecture of her life. Because if it remembered, then it had once been awake. If it had once been awake, then the covenant — the great forgetting the whole valley was built upon — was not a law of nature. It was a door, and doors are made by hands, and hands can be undone.

Ulyana looked down at her workbench, at the linen-wrapped charm that held the last words of a drowned boy, at the burin lying where she'd set it down, its tip still bright with bone dust. Her tools. Her craft. The one thread of the old world that had never been cut.

The crickets sang. The dogs were silent. Down in Low Row, Tam Wickerby's family slept their first night with an empty bed, and somewhere beyond the boundary stones, in the wide world Ulyana had never once seen, the night went on being ordinary, or pretending to.

She took her cloak from its peg.

The garden path was silver with starlight and wet with dew, and her feet knew the way without being told, the way feet know the way to a grave. At the gate to the shrine-garden she stopped, breath fogging in air that should have been warm for another month, and listened — truly listened, opening herself the way she did over a fresh-cut bone.

From inside the overgrown rows, faint as a heartbeat under snow, something green and enormous and impossibly patient said, in a woman's voice that remembered being much larger:

"You may as well come in, bone-carver. We have a great deal to discuss."

Ulyana lifted the latch.
