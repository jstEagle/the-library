## 2. A Voice in the Bean Rows

The voice had come from the north end of the valley, from the old Pemberton land that had gone to bramble and memory since Grandmother Sable died, and it did not stop.

Ulyana tried to make it stop. She lit the good candle in her workshop and worked the drowned child's bone until midnight, carving the little runic gutter that would carry his last words into whatever came after, but the prayer kept threading through the walls like smoke through a keyhole. *Please. Please. Please.* Not her prayer. She had never prayed in her life — the dead talked enough without her adding her own noise to the chorus — and yet the word arrived in her skull with the intimacy of a thought she'd been ashamed of having.

*Please,* said the voice, small and cracked and terribly alive. *Please, somebody.*

She set down her knife. The child's femur lay across her cloth like a pale reed, half-finished, his last words already caught in the groove: *it's cold, Mama, why is it cold* — and beneath that, fainter, older, the sediment-voices of every bone she had ever carved, murmuring their archive of endings.

But this new voice was not dead. That was what made her hands shake. The dead spoke *through* things; they needed bone or ash or river-stone to lean on. This voice stood on its own, raw as a wound, and it was coming from the shrine-garden.

Nobody called it a shrine anymore. On the maps it was simply the north field, and in the elders' mouths it was *that tangle*, and in Ulyana's childhood it had been forbidden in the soft, unexplained way that made children want it more. Her grandmother had gone out there every evening of her life with a lantern and come back with dirt under her nails and an expression Ulyana had never been able to read — not peace exactly, and not guilt either. Something between them. Something like a woman keeping an appointment she both dreaded and could not live without.

Ulyana took the lantern down from its hook before she could talk herself out of it.

The night was warm for early summer, thick with the smell of cut hay and the river's green breath, and the valley lay around her in its usual hush. Thornmere was always hushed — that was its whole character, its whole promise. No magic crossed the boundary stones. The elders said it like a blessing: *here you are safe, here the world is only the world.* And mostly it was true. Mostly the valley felt to Ulyana like a room with the windows painted shut — safe, and airless, and hers.

Her gift was the one crack in the paint. It should have died at the stones like everything else. Instead it lived on, stubborn as moss, fed by the bones she carved and by something at the valley's edge she had never understood. The elders knew. They had always known. They let her work because grief needed tending and because her grandmother had vouched for her with a look that dared anyone to object.

Now, walking the overgrown lane toward the north field, lantern swinging, Ulyana felt the hush thinning around her like fog burning off. The crickets went quiet in a slow wave ahead of her, as if something were walking before her through the grass, announcing her.

*Please,* said the voice again, closer now, and it broke in the middle of the word, and she heard — she would have sworn on her own grave — that it was answered. Something vast and tired said, in a tone like a woman saying *yes, I'm here, I'm still here:* **I hear you.**

Ulyana stopped so hard the lantern swung wild.

Prayers were not answered in Thornmere Valley. Prayers were not even *heard*. That was the entire architecture of the place; that was the covenant, though the elders used softer words — *the old superstitions cannot follow us here, we are a practical people.* And yet she had just heard a god answer a prayer the way a mother answers a child calling from another room.

She should have gone home. She knew that the way you know a stair is missing in the dark. Instead she lifted the latch of the garden gate — iron, cold, older than any fence had a right to be — and stepped into her grandmother's secret.

* * *

It was a ruin wearing the costume of a garden.

Bean rows ran in crooked lines through waist-high weeds, and yet the beans themselves were flourishing, absurdly, obscenely — vines heavy with pods in the dark, leaves glossy as if polished by hand. Fruit trees that should have died forty years ago leaned over the walls, blossoming out of season, petals falling upward in the lantern light. At the center stood a well no wider than a barrel, ringed with stones carved in letters Ulyana almost recognized, worn nearly smooth, and beside the well a low stone bench where someone had recently sat: the moss was crushed flat in the shape of a body.

And in the third bean row, kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, was a woman.

She did not startle when the lantern light found her. She simply looked up — and Ulyana forgot, for one full breath, how to be afraid, because the woman's face was ordinary and extraordinary at once: sun-browned, freckled, mid-forties perhaps, with laugh lines and dirt in the creases of her knuckles and eyes the grey-green of deep water, eyes that held the particular patience of someone who has waited a very long time and is not finished waiting.

"You're the bone-carver," the woman said. "Sable's granddaughter."

"You know my grandmother."

"I knew her for fifty years." The woman rose, brushing soil from her knees with unhurried care. "She brought me beans and gossip every week until the end. You have her chin. And her habit of standing in doorways deciding whether to run."

"I'm not in a doorway."

"No," the woman agreed, and something flickered across her face — amusement, and beneath it a sorrow so old it had gone smooth, like sea glass. "You're in my garden. Which is braver, or stupider. I haven't decided which."

"Your garden." Ulyana kept the lantern high, kept its circle of light between them like a fact she could point to. "This is Pemberton land."

"It is." The woman tilted her head. "And who do you suppose your grandmother inherited it from? Land doesn't stay in a family four hundred years by luck, carver. It stays because of a bargain." She wiped her hands on her apron — a plain linen apron, mended at the pocket, the most mortal garment Ulyana had ever seen — and extended one earth-dark hand. "Freya."

The name landed in Ulyana's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She knew it the way she knew all old things: from the bones. Three times in her life, carving the oldest remains, she had felt a name rise through the marrow like a bubble through tar — *Freyja, Freyja* — scratched into coffin lids by hands that hoped being remembered was the same as being saved.

"That's a goddess's name," she said.

"Yes," said Freya, agreeably, as if Ulyana had remarked on the weather. "It's also mine. Both things are true, which is the trouble with names. Would you like to see the beans? They're doing beautifully. It's the one thing I'm still genuinely good at."

* * *

They walked the rows while the lantern burned, and Freya talked about beans the way scholars talk about scripture.

"Broad beans want cold feet and warm heads. Runners want their roots left alone — everyone fusses at runners, and it's rudeness, they're perfectly capable. These—" she touched a vine heavy with pods, and the vine leaned into her fingers like a cat, actually *leaned*, and Ulyana's skin prickled from wrist to jaw— "these are patient. They were the first thing I learned to grow after I was put here, and they've never once let me down. People do. Gods do. Beans don't."

"You're saying you're a goddess," Ulyana said. "Bound into a bean garden. In Thornmere. Where nothing magical can exist."

"And yet." Freya smiled, and it was a gardener's smile, wry and weathered. "Magic can't cross the boundary stones, girl. But I didn't cross them. I was *put* inside them, the way you'd put a sword in a scabbard, or a prisoner in a cell. The rule isn't *no magic in Thornmere*. The rule is *no magic moves in Thornmere*. There's a difference, and the difference is me."

The crickets had begun again at the garden's edge, cautiously, like witnesses returning to a courtroom. Ulyana became aware that her hands had gone cold around the lantern handle.

"Why are you telling me this? You've been here — what, since my grandmother was young? Longer? Why now?"

"Because three nights ago, for the first time in sixty years, I heard a prayer from beyond the wall of myself." Freya's voice changed — dropped its gardener's dryness and went quiet and precise, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "A child, lost near the river ford, praying to whoever might listen. And I *answered*. I shouldn't have been able to. The binding should have held. Something has cracked, carver, and cracks in bindings do not heal on their own. They spread."

"The child," Ulyana said. "The drowned boy. He's on my table right now."

Freya closed her eyes. For a moment her whole face changed — aged, or un-aged, Ulyana couldn't say — and the garden seemed to hold its breath with her. Every leaf stilled. The upward-falling petals hung in the air like snow deciding.

"I know," Freya said softly. "I felt him go past me on his way out of the world. He was frightened, and then he wasn't, because something kind met him at the edge. Was that you?"

"I carve the bones. I catch the last words. That's all."

"That is not *all*," Freya said, with sudden force, and opened her eyes, and they were not a gardener's eyes now. "Do you know what you are? Do you have any idea how rare — how *impossibly* rare — a mortal ear that hears the dead clearly, cleanly, without madness? In the old days there were perhaps a dozen living voices like yours in all the nine realms, and kings built temples around them and wars over them. Your gift is not a quirk, Ulyana Pemberton. It is a *keystone*. And I think — I have begun to think — that it is the reason your valley exists at all."

The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no wind.

"What do you mean, the reason the valley exists?"

Freya looked at her for a long moment. Then she sat down on the crushed-moss bench, heavily, suddenly mortal again, and patted the stone beside her in a gesture so ordinary, so grandmotherly, that Ulyana's fear loosened its grip despite everything.

"Sit. This is a long story, and I've had sixty years to rehearse it and no one to tell it to, so forgive me if I tell it badly." She waited until Ulyana sat, the stone cold through her skirt. "You believe the gods left the world. That's what the elders teach, yes? That the old powers faded like embers, naturally, sadly, and wise mortals built the boundary stones to keep out the *superstitions* that remained."

"That's what everyone believes."

"Everyone believes it because it was *made* to be believed." Freya's hands folded in her lap, dirt and all. "The gods did not fade. We were *muted*. All of us, everywhere, in a single night — our voices taken, our names scraped from the world's memory, our temples left to rot so that within two generations no one alive remembered we had ever answered at all. A god no one remembers is a god who cannot hear. Prayer is a door, carver, and they locked every door in the world at once." Her thumb moved over the mend in her apron pocket, back and forth, back and forth. "I survived because your grandmother's grandmother — and then your grandmother — kept my name alive in this one garden, in this one valley, a single coal banked in ash. And I endured the muting itself because I was already bound here, behind the stones, where their working could not fully reach."

"Muted by whom?" Ulyana asked, though some cold part of her already knew the shape of the answer. "Gods can't be muted by weather. By time. Someone did it."

"Not someone," Freya said. "*Someones.* Mortals. Mortal hands performed the rite — it had to be mortals, that was the cruelty and the genius of it, a covenant must be broken from the side that benefits. The gods were bound away from mortals, and mortals were bound away from wonder, and the ones who did it told themselves — I have spent sixty years imagining how they told themselves — that it was mercy. That a world without gods was a world without floods sent in anger, without jealous curses, without demands of blood. A safe world. A *quiet* world." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Ulyana had ever heard, worse than any death-voice, because it was a goddess laughing like a woman who has been alone too long. "And then they built your valley on top of the wound, like a bandage over a bite, and called the silence peace."

The lantern had burned low. Somewhere beyond the garden wall, far off toward the mountains, thunder muttered — though the sky was clear, Ulyana noted distantly, the sky had been clear all night.

"If what you're saying is true," Ulyana said slowly, "then Thornmere isn't a sanctuary. It's a lie with a fence around it."

"It's a lie with a fence around it that *my* friend helped build," Freya said quietly. "I loved your grandmother, carver. Don't mistake this for accusation. She tended me like a widow tends a grave — faithfully, and with guilt, and without ever quite telling me the whole truth of what she knew. I think the truth was heavier than she could carry, so she carried pieces of it, and handed the heaviest piece down to you without ever naming it." She turned, and the grey-green eyes caught the last of the lantern light. "You hear the dead. So ask them. Don't take my word for any of it — I am, after all, a madwoman in a bean garden, by every sane measure. Ask the oldest bones in your workshop what happened the night the gods went silent. The dead keep better records than the living. The dead have no reason to lie."

Ulyana stood. Her legs were steadier than she expected. The garden breathed around them — beans and blossom and old stone — and beneath it all, so faint she might have imagined it, she felt the well at the center humming like a struck bell that had been ringing for centuries below hearing.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Really. If the elders are part of this — if my own valley is built on it — why hand me a torch?"

Freya looked out over her impossible beans for a long moment.

"Because tonight a child prayed and I answered, and the binding cracked, and I felt the crack run *outward*, past the stones, into the world. And what I felt on the other side, carver, was cold." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Something is coming apart. The covenant wasn't just broken — it's *dying*, badly, the way a snapped bridge dies, both halves falling toward each other. When it finishes falling, there will be no silence and no sanctuary. Only winter. I have watched one woman grow old tending me, and her granddaughter after her, and I promised myself I would not spend my last strength frightening the third. But you're not frightened, are you?" A ghost of the gardener's smile. "You're furious. I can see it. Good. Fury keeps better than fear."

Ulyana picked up the lantern. At the gate she paused, hand on the cold iron latch, and did not look back, because looking back would have meant admitting how much she wanted to.

"The oldest skull in my workshop," she said, "belonged to a man buried before the valley had a name. If he tells me you're lying—"

"Then I'll pull the beans and plant turnips and be only a madwoman," Freya said. "That's a fair wager. Go home, Ulyana Pemberton. Sleep if you can. And when you talk to your dead tomorrow — listen carefully. Because if I'm right, one of them has been trying to reach you for a very long time."

The gate closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting. All the way down the dark lane, the lantern steady in her hand, Ulyana did not hear the small cracked voice praying anymore.

What she heard instead, threaded through the cricket-song and the river-hush, was the oldest skull's voice — dry as autumn, patient as soil — repeating a single word, over and over, the way the dead repeat things when they have waited decades to be asked:

*Muted. Muted. Muted.*
