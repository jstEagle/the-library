## 3. What the Bones Remember

Ulyana did not sleep.

She sat in her workshop with the lamp turned low and the drowned boy's memorial bone finished on the bench before her — a small thing of river-ivory, carved with reeds bending in a current that no longer moved him — and she thought about what the woman in the bean rows had said.

*Muted,* Freya had said. Not banished. Not gone. *Muted*, the way one mutes a bell by packing its throat with wax, the way one mutes a screaming child with a hand over the mouth. And someone mortal had helped do it.

It was nonsense. It had to be nonsense. The valley's law was older than any living memory: magic does not cross the stones. The gods were far away things, stories for midwinter nights, faces painted on the beams of old barns to frighten crows. Whatever walked in her grandmother's garden wearing a warden's body and speaking of covenants, it was not evidence. Evidence was bone. Evidence was what the dead left behind when they stopped being people and started being witnesses.

And so, an hour past midnight, with the moths ticking against the window glass and the valley lying silent as a held breath outside, Ulyana Pemberton went to her shelves and took down the oldest skull she owned.

* * *

His name — the name he'd had, before the name wore away like paint on a doorpost — was Aldric. She knew this because he had told her, forty years ago, when she was nine years old and her grandmother first set her hands upon him and said, *listen*. He had been dead longer than Thornmere Valley had been a valley. His skull had been found beneath the roots of an oak when they dug the foundation of the meeting hall, and some long-dead carver had thought him too fine a piece to bury again. He sat now on the top shelf, wrapped in wool against the damp, his cranium polished to the color of old honey, his sutures traced with faded ink in patterns Ulyana had never learned to read. Older work than hers. Perhaps older than the valley's work altogether.

She unwrapped him and set him on the bench between two candles, and she did the thing she had done ten thousand times: she laid her fingertips against the bone and opened the door in herself that let the dead speak through.

The silence hit her like cold water.

Not the ordinary silence of Aldric's long rest — she knew that silence, gentle as snowfall, the hush of a man who had finished saying everything he meant to say centuries ago. This was different. This was *pressure*. This was the feeling of pressing her ear to a door and hearing, on the other side, not quiet but someone standing very close to it, breathing, unwilling or unable to make a sound.

"Aldric," she said aloud, because sometimes the voice needed a shape in the air to follow. "Old man. I need you."

The pressure shifted. Something moved behind the bone under her fingers, vast and slow, like a whale turning beneath ice. And then — faintly, terribly, as though the words were being pushed up through fathoms of water — she heard it.

*They took our voices.*

Ulyana snatched her hand back. The candle flames leaned sideways though there was no draft. Her heart was going hard against her ribs, and she made herself breathe, made herself count, the way her grandmother had taught her. Four in. Six out. The dead could not hurt her. The dead had never once hurt her. They only ever told her what they carried.

She put her hand back on the skull.

"Who took them?" she whispered. "When?"

The answer came slowly, in fragments, each word costing him something. She understood then why the oldest dead were the hardest to hear — not because their voices faded, but because theirs had been *taken deepest*, packed down under more wax than the newer ones, buried under layer after layer of whatever had been done to them. Aldric had to dig for every syllable like a man clawing through a collapsed roof to reach daylight.

*Before your grandmother's grandmother. Before the stones.* A pause, long enough that she almost pulled away. *We did not leave, little carver. We were never asked to leave. We were—*

The word came not as sound but as sensation, pressed directly into her palm where it touched the bone: the feeling of a hand clamped over a mouth. The feeling of singing into a pillow. The feeling of a scream that has somewhere to go and is not permitted to go there.

*Muted.*

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and never heard it land.

"That isn't possible," she said, and hated how young her voice sounded. "The covenant — everyone knows the covenant. The gods went beyond the world, and the valley was set apart, and that was the bargain. That's what the elders say. That's what the founding stone says."

*The founding stone,* Aldric said, and something in the dead voice that might have been laughter, if laughter could be flattened and frozen and pushed through forty feet of silence, *was cut by the same hands that cut us.*

* * *

She worked until the candles guttered, and what she learned in those hours she would carry for the rest of her life, the way one carries a scar: completely, and without ever quite getting used to the sight of it.

Aldric remembered. That was the horror of it. However thoroughly they had been muted, however deep the wax was packed, the memory remained — memory being, apparently, the one thing they could not take from the dead without taking the dead themselves. And so Ulyana knelt at her bench like a woman at a confessor's grille and listened, hour after hour, while a skull older than her valley told her how the world had been broken.

There had been gods walking among mortals once. Not distant ones. Near ones. Gods who sat at tables and drank from cups and argued about weather and children, gods whose presence was less thunder than hearth-warmth, less miracle than company. The covenant had not been a wall. That was the lie at the center of everything. The covenant had been a *bridge* — a living thing, woven between the realms, so that prayer flowed one way and blessing flowed the other, and neither side could forget the other existed.

Then came the sickness. Aldric showed her — pushed the memory into her hands the way he might have pressed a coin into a beggar's palm — a summer of coughing blood, of carts stacked with bodies, of prayers rising from Thornmere like smoke from a burning house. Prayers for cure. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

And the gods had answered: *we cannot.*

Not *we will not*. *We cannot.* Some sicknesses are woven into the pattern of things, and even a god cannot unpick one thread without unraveling the cloth. The gods had come to the dying and held their hands and wept, and told them the truth, which is the one gift mortals most often refuse.

But grief does not want truth. Grief wants someone to blame. And there had been a family — Aldric's memory darkened here, names blurred like faces seen through frosted glass, all except one — a family of the valley, proud and terrified and grieving their own dead, who went looking for a way to make the pain stop. And they had found it. Or been given it. Or made it; Aldric could not say which, and perhaps none of those were quite the right word.

*A cutting,* he called it. *They cut the bridge. But a bridge that size does not fall quietly, little carver. It falls screaming. Every god in the world felt the rope go slack. So they did not only cut it. They silenced the scream.*

Someone mortal had stood in a circle with the elders — the founding families, the same names still carved on the meeting hall lintel, the same names still spoken with respect at every harvest — and someone mortal had lent their hands, their voice, their *craft* to the working. Because it could not be done without mortal hands. That was the last thing Aldric gave her before the pressure of his muting closed over him again like water over a sinking lantern:

*A god cannot mute a god. The silence had to be sung by throats that could die.*

* * *

Dawn found her sitting on the floor of the workshop with her back against the bench, the skull wrapped and shelved again, her hands shaking too badly to hold a carving knife and not much caring.

Her craft. Her gift. The thing that had made her strange and necessary and alone in Thornmere for thirty years — the ability to hear the dead through the bones they left behind — had survived the muting. Had survived it *because* it worked on the dead, she realized, and the thought arrived with a nausea that turned her stomach over. The muting had been built to sever the living from the divine. But the dead were already severed. The dead were already on the other side of the line. No one had thought to gag the corpses, because no one imagined the corpses could still testify.

Except they could. Except they had. Except she had spent her whole life as the one open channel in a valley built on a sealed wound, and no one — not her grandmother, who had taught her the craft with such careful gravity; not the elders, who tolerated the bone-carver's strangeness with a courtesy that now looked suspiciously like surveillance; not Marta, who —

No. She would not think about Marta yet. She was not ready to point that particular lamp into that particular corner.

She got up. She washed her face in the basin. She watched the light come gray and then gold over the garden wall, over the tangle of her grandmother's abandoned plots, over the overgrown shrine-garden beyond them where a woman who claimed to be a goddess was presumably already awake, already barefoot in the bean rows, already watching the house to see whether the bone-carver would come out believing or come out with a torch.

Ulyana looked at her own hands. Carver's hands, scarred and capable, hands that had given the dead a voice for three decades without ever asking why the dead needed one so badly. Why the dead spoke to her across a boundary that nothing else could cross. Why her gift had flourished at the exact edges of the valley where the binding wore thin, like grass growing through cracks in a pavement — through cracks in a *seal*.

Her whole life had been evidence. She simply hadn't known what it was evidence *of*.

She took down her satchel. She packed it without letting herself think about what packing it meant: the good knives, the waxed thread, a loaf, the small finished charms she always carried because you never knew which dead soul you'd meet on a road who'd want something of their own making to hold. Last of all, after a moment's hesitation, she took Aldric down from the shelf and wrapped him in fresh wool and settled him into the satchel's heart, where he knocked gently against her spine as she moved.

"You're coming with me, old man," she murmured. "If they muted you once, they'll finish the job if they find out you talked. And I find I've developed a taste for testimony."

The pressure against her awareness was faint, tired, amused. *Little carver,* he seemed to say. *You have no idea what you've just become.*

"I know exactly what I've become," Ulyana said, shouldering the satchel and opening the workshop door onto the bright, unknowing morning. "I've become a witness."

The garden path was silvered with dew, and at its end, past the collapsed trellis and the gone-to-seed lettuce, the shrine-garden waited — and the woman in it had stopped mid-motion, one hand full of ripening beans, staring past Ulyana at the satchel, at the shape of the skull inside it, with an expression that was not triumph and not relief but something rawer. Something like a soldier watching reinforcements crest a hill she had been defending alone for a very long time.

"Well?" Freya said. "What did they tell you?"

Ulyana walked toward her through the wet beans, and her voice, when it came, did not shake at all.

"Tell me about the founding families," she said. "Tell me their names. Because I think I know someone whose grandmother's name is going to be on that list."
