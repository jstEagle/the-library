## 4. The Boundary Stones

Freya had counted the mornings the way the old gods once counted centuries — by what changed in them.

The bean rows changed. The light on the bean rows changed. The small, stubborn green of the world changed, leaf by leaf, and she had learned to read it the way she had once read the turning of stars: patiently, hungrily, with her whole attention pressed against the surface of things.

But this morning did not change gently.

She woke with frost in her mouth.

Not metaphor — she was done with metaphor; mortality had burned the taste for it out of her — but actual frost, a thin rime of white across her lips and the blanket's edge, glittering in the grey pre-dawn like salt. She lay still beneath it and listened to the garden breathe, and the garden was breathing wrong. The air had a knife-edge to it. Midsummer, and the air had a knife-edge to it.

She rose before the sun cleared the eastern ridge and walked the rows barefoot, because cold was information now, and she wanted all of it against her skin. The beans were fine — for the moment. Hardy things, beans. They forgave a great deal. But the squash leaves wore a silvered rim of ice that would blacken by noon, and the basil had already given up, drooping like a congregation after bad news, and when Freya crouched and touched the soil it was cold all the way down past her knuckles, cold in the way soil should not be in the month of honey and haymaking.

*It is starting,* she thought. *Or continuing. Or finishing.* She no longer knew which word fit. The covenant had been cut so long ago that its dying might have been going on for generations, slow as rot in a great beam, invisible until the roof came down.

She stood in the middle of her garden — her garden, hers by tenancy if not by nature, hers by the sweat of nine years of hands that used to hold thunder — and felt the homesickness rise in her like water filling a boot.

That was the thing about being bound into flesh that no one warned you, because no one had ever been bound quite like this. It was not the hunger or the aching back or the indignity of blisters. It was this: that some mornings the world arrived through her senses too small. Colors came in slightly the wrong size. Sounds ended too soon. She would look at the sunrise and feel the way a starving woman feels looking through a window at someone else's table — not envy exactly, but a grief so specific it had a flavor, coppery and old.

Once, dawn had come *through* her. Once she had been one of the reasons dawn happened at all.

Now she grew beans.

She pulled on her boots, wrapped the shawl Ulyana had left draped over the fence rail two days ago — deliberately, Freya suspected, the way you leave a door open for a cat — and went to find the bone-carver. There were things Ulyana needed to see, and Freya had run out of reasons to keep them from her.

* * *

Ulyana Pemberton worked the way rivers ran: without apparent effort, without pause, and with something enormous underneath.

Freya watched her from the workshop doorway for a long moment before speaking. Morning light came through the south window and lay across the bench in a gold bar, and within that bar the woman's hands moved — small knife, smaller strokes — turning a length of deer rib into something that was becoming a lark. You could see it happening. Not carved into it; *waking up* in it. That was the part Freya could not stop watching, the part that made her chest ache in a way she refused to name even privately. Mortal craft. The thing the covenant had been built to protect, and built so badly, and broken so completely, and here it was anyway, stubborn as a weed through flagstone, making beauty out of the dead.

"You're staring," Ulyana said, without looking up.

"I'm admiring."

"Same thing, from you." But there was no bite in it. There hadn't been since the night at the shrine-garden, since the skull in the workshop had confirmed everything Freya had told her — *muted, not banished; mortal hands helped do it* — and the wariness between them had shifted into something more dangerous and more useful: shared purpose, still awkward, still testing its weight like ice on a pond.

Ulyana set down the knife and blew a curl of bone dust from the lark's wing. "You have your outside face on," she said. "The one you wear when you've decided something and you're deciding how much of it to tell me."

"I'm going to show you the boundary stones."

The knife stopped moving entirely.

"Today?" Ulyana said. "Past the—"

"To the stones themselves. Not past them." Freya stepped into the light. "Not yet. But you've heard the dead say the word *muted*, and you've seen my garden frost in midsummer, and you deserve to see the wound itself. What was done here. Where it was done." She hesitated, and hated hesitating; hesitation was a mortal habit she'd caught like a cold. "And I want—" She stopped again. Started plainer. "I want to stand near them with someone who knows what they are."

Ulyana studied her for a moment with those grey eyes that had spent thirty years listening to people who couldn't defend themselves, eyes that had learned to weigh every word like a market vendor weighing flour. Then she reached for her coat.

"All right," she said. "Show me where my whole life has been standing."

* * *

They went north and west, up out of the valley floor, following a track that began as a road and degraded honestly into a path and then into a suggestion. Thornmere's houses fell away behind them, then the last terraced fields, then the orchards, and the land opened into high moorland purple with heather and loud with wind. Freya walked half a step ahead, the way she always did, and told herself it was habit from leading processions and not the older habit of putting herself between people and danger.

"The stones aren't marked on any map," Ulyana said as they climbed. "I've looked. Ivo's maps, the elder's survey, all of it. The valley just... ends on paper. Blank space beyond the ridge."

"That's the first sign of it. Paper forgets first." Freya ducked under a low hawthorn bent sideways by decades of weather. "A wound in the world doesn't bleed. It *omits*. Things slide off it. Names, roads, memories. Your valley isn't hidden, Ulyana. It's *unwritten*."

"And you found it anyway."

"I remembered it anyway." She said it flatly, and kept walking, and felt Ulyana's gaze rest on her back for three strides longer than comfort allowed.

The truth was worse than flat. Nine years ago, mute and diminished and falling out of heaven like a shot bird, she had felt one warm place in the whole cold sprawl of the world — a seam of uncut ground, a place where the muting had been done so violently that the violence itself had left a hollow, and in the hollow, magic pooled like rain in a hoofprint. She had crawled toward it across two hundred miles of country that no longer knew her name, and she had poured what remained of herself into an overgrown shrine-garden on the edge of that hollow, and she had become — this. A woman with dirt under her nails who talked to beans.

She had never decided whether the valley was her refuge or her prison. Both, probably. Most true things were both.

"There," she said.

The boundary stones stood in a rough arc along the crest of the ridge, and they were nothing like what Ulyana must have expected, because Freya heard her breath catch and saw her stop walking entirely.

They were not standing stones. They were *fallen* ones — seven of them, maybe eight, tumbled like teeth knocked out of a jaw, half-sunk in the moor, furred with moss and lichen. Each one was taller than a man, each one carved, though the carvings had weathered to suggestion: spirals, interlaced figures, what might have been hands clasping across a gap. And between the stones — this was the part that made the hair rise on Freya's arms even now, even after nine years — between the stones the air itself was different. You could see it if the light was right, and the light was right: a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in reverse, like looking through the surface of very still water from underneath.

Beyond the shimmer, the moor continued. Same heather, same wind, same sky.

And falling out of that same sky, soft and silent and utterly wrong, came snow.

"Midsommer," Ulyana whispered. She had gone very pale. "There's snow. Beyond the stones. In midsummer."

"Yes."

"But it's not snowing *here*." Ulyana took a step forward, then another, until she stood close enough to the shimmer that Freya's hand twitched toward her — and stopped. Ulyana raised one hand slowly toward the haze, the way you'd test a fire, and where her fingers neared it the air seemed to thicken, to resist, gently but absolutely, the way deep water resists. She pushed. Nothing gave. She pushed harder, and the shimmer dimpled around her hand like the skin of a drum and held.

"It won't let me through," Ulyana said. Not frightened, Freya noted. *Furious.* The bone-carver's fury, quiet and precise. "My whole life. My whole life there's been a wall around me and I thought it was the world."

"No." Freya came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. "Your whole life there's been a wall around the *magic*, and you thought it was the world. Look at your hands."

Ulyana looked. Her gift — hearing the dead through the bones she carved — had never been silenced by the valley. Everyone said the valley forbade magic, and everyone was almost right, and almost right had been enough to hide the truth for four generations. But Ulyana's gift persisted, muted at the edges, muffled, like music played in another room.

"Why?" Ulyana asked. "If the wall keeps magic out, why can I still hear them?"

"Because the wall doesn't keep magic out." Freya turned to face her fully. This was the speech she had rehearsed for two days and would now deliver badly, because rehearsal meant nothing when the audience was this woman. "The wall is where the covenant was *cut*. Do you understand? A covenant isn't a rule. It isn't a law written on tablets. It's a — a joining. Gods and mortals, woven together, each feeding the other. Their prayers give us shape; our presence gives their work power. When it's whole, magic flows across it like blood through skin. When it's cut—"

She drew her knife — a plain garden knife, wooden-handled — and dragged the point lightly across the back of her own forearm, hard enough to raise a white line.

"—you don't get two clean pieces. You get a wound. And the wound scars, and the scar is numb, and everything inside the scar is numb forever. Your valley isn't protected from magic, Ulyana. It's *amputated* from it. The elders will tell you the stones guard you. They're guarding a scar and calling it a blessing."

Ulyana was silent for a long moment. Wind moved the heather in silver waves. Beyond the shimmer, the snow fell and fell onto summer grass, and did not melt.

"My grandmother said the founders came here fleeing something," she said finally. "Plague, she thought. War. Something the gods wouldn't save them from. So they made a place the gods couldn't reach."

"They made a place the gods couldn't reach," Freya agreed, "and then they made sure the gods couldn't reach anywhere else either. Because you cannot cut a covenant in one place. The covenant is one fabric. Cut it here and it tears everywhere. Every temple in the world went quiet the day these stones were set. Every prayer stopped being answered. Every god started starving." Her voice did something she didn't authorize it to do — cracked, slightly, on the last word — and she pressed on before Ulyana could hear it. "The muting wasn't a shield raised around Thornmere. It was a knife drawn across the throat of the whole world, and your valley is just the hand that held the knife."

* * *

They sat among the fallen stones while Freya told her the rest — some of it, most of it, not the worst of it, because the worst of it had names in it that Ulyana would recognize, and Freya was not yet cruel enough, or perhaps not yet brave enough, for that.

She told her about the covenant's making, in the time before calendars, when the first humans had looked at the lightning and the harvest and the dark and had *asked*, and the asking had been so naked and so hopeful that something vast had leaned down to answer, and the leaning-down had changed both parties forever. She told her about the long middle ages of the bargain: temples fed, fields blessed, storms turned, the dead guided, prayers rising like smoke and grace falling like rain, a conversation ten thousand years long that neither side had ever thought to fear would end.

"And then?"

"And then a handful of mortals, in one valley, decided the price was too high." Freya picked up a fragment of fallen stone, turned it in her fingers. Moss came away on her thumb. "They weren't evil. That's the trap of it. They were grieving. Something had happened — something the gods could not or would not undo — and they concluded that a world with silent gods was safer than a world with gods who sometimes said no. So they learned the old words. The binding words. And they cut."

"What happened to them? The ones who cut it."

"They lived long lives," Freya said, "and died respected, and their grandchildren inherited the valley, and none of them ever knew — or let themselves know — what their grandmothers' safety had cost. That's how wounds work, Ulyana. The pain stays at the site. Everything downstream just learns to live without the limb."

She looked out past the shimmer at the snow. It was coming down thicker now, fat flakes, unhurried, the kind of snow that intended to stay. Somewhere beyond the ridge, in the white and widening world, the Hollow Choir was singing — she could feel them the way you feel a storm front in an old scar, a pressure, a wrongness. Gods who had starved too long. Gods whose names had worn away from disuse until only the hunger was left. Prayers were being answered out there again — the muting was failing, the covenant's death throes shaking loose old powers — and they were being answered the way a drowning man answers a hand reaching into water.

"And the winter?" Ulyana asked quietly. "Is that the covenant dying too?"

"The covenant is the only thing holding the seasons to their courses. It's the loom. Cut the warp and the weft slides." Freya closed her hand around the stone fragment until the moss compressed to wet darkness. "Out there, winter isn't weather anymore. It's *forgetting*. The world forgetting warmth, the way your valley forgot magic. First the crops. Then the rivers. Then memory. Then—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "Then us. Whatever's left of us. Gods need remembering the way fires need air. When the last person who remembers a god dies unmourned, the god goes out. The Hollow Choir are the ones who went out badly."

Ulyana was looking at her with an expression Freya had learned to dread and crave in equal measure: total attention, unsentimental, the look of a woman listening to bones. As if Freya herself were a relic on the bench and the truth was surfacing in her grain by grain.

"You're guilty," Ulyana said. It wasn't an accusation. It was an observation, delivered with the same care she'd use noting a hairline crack in ivory. "You talk about the covenant like a debt you owe. Why? You didn't cut it. You're the one it was cut *against*."

And there it was, the question Freya had walked up this hill intending to dodge, and found, sitting in the open air among the fallen stones, that she could not.

"Because godhood was expensive," she said. "Do you understand what divinity *is*, Ulyana? It isn't power. Power is the least of it. Divinity is being *answerable*. Every prayer that rises, you hear. Every child lost in the snow calls your name and you must either come or refuse, and either way you carry it. For ten thousand years I carried every prayer ever spoken to me — every mother in labor, every sailor in the swell, every farmer watching the sky over his wheat. Billions of voices. I held them all. It was—" She searched for the word and found the true one and flinched from it and said it anyway. "It was *heavy*. And when the muting came, when the voices stopped — do you know what my first feeling was?"

Ulyana said nothing. The snow fell beyond the stones. Somewhere below, a curlew cried over the moor.

"*Relief,*" Freya said. "One heartbeat of pure, shameful relief. Silence. No more weight. I remember thinking: oh. Oh, so this is what rest feels like. And then the relief curdled, because silence isn't rest, silence is starvation, and I understood what I'd lost only when it was gone, and by then it was too late to be anything but grateful and horrified at once." She laughed, short and joyless. "So yes. I'm guilty. Not of cutting the covenant. Of being glad, for one heartbeat, that it was cut. And of spending ten thousand years taking mortals' prayers for granted, as if their hope were a tax they owed me rather than a gift they lent me. The covenant broke because we — all of us, gods and mortals both — forgot it was a *relationship* and started treating it like infrastructure."

The wind moved. Ulyana reached out — slowly, the way she moved her hands over fresh-carved bone — and touched the back of Freya's wrist, where the white line from the knife-point was already fading.

"You were tired," Ulyana said. "Ten thousand years is tired."

"It's not an excuse."

"I didn't say it was." Those grey eyes held hers, steady, unafraid. "I carve memorials for the dead, Freya. Do you know what I've learned from thirty years of listening to ghosts? Nobody wants absolution. They want *witness*. Somebody to know what actually happened and not look away." Her hand stayed where it was, warm through the shawl's wool. "So. Show me the rest. All of it. Even the parts with names I know."

Freya's breath caught. *Even the parts with names I know.* She had not mentioned Marta's family. She had not needed to; the dead had already begun saying it.

Before she could decide how much truth the moment could bear, Ulyana stiffened. Her head turned sharply toward the shimmer, toward the snow, and Freya saw the particular stillness come over her — the listening stillness, the bone-carver's trance.

"What is it?" Freya asked. "What do you hear?"

Ulyana's lips parted. The wind snatched at whatever came out. When she spoke again her voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of a woman translating from a language she wished she didn't speak.

"A dead man," she said. "Frozen, I think — his voice is slow, like syrup. He's been dead two winters, out there in the white. He keeps saying the same thing over and over." She swallowed. "He says: *the quiet ones sent him. The quiet ones in the valley. Ask the granddaughter. Ask the granddaughter of Sila.*"

The name landed between them like a dropped stone.

Sila. Freya knew the name. Four generations back, carved in the valley's founding rolls, honored in the elder's hall: Sila Vane, the woman the histories credited with *protecting* Thornmere. Grandmother of Marta.

Ulyana was already on her feet, and her face, when she turned to Freya, was the face of a woman standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing.

"Marta," she said. "I have to go ask Marta."
