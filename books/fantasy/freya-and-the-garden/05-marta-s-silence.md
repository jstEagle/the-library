## 5. Marta's Silence

The bell over the door of the Pemberton workshop had not rung in three days, and when it finally did, Ulyana knew before she looked up that it was Marta. She knew the particular hesitation of it — half a ring, then a pause, as though the hand on the door had considered turning back.

"Your hinges need oil," Marta said, letting herself in anyway, because she always let herself in, because she had been letting herself in since they were seven years old and Ulyana's mother was still alive to scold them both for tracking river mud across clean floors.

"Your knock needs practice," Ulyana said, and did not look up from her work.

She was carving. Of course she was carving; she was always carving these days, ever since the drowned boy, ever since the voice in the bean rows, ever since Freya — and the word *goddess* still snagged in Ulyana's mind like a fishhook every time she thought it — had told her things about the valley that made the boundary stones feel less like protection and more like bandages over a wound nobody would admit to. The bone in her hands was sheep's femur, smooth and pale, destined for no one yet. She carved when she could not think, and she could not think now, so the knife moved and the shavings curled away like small white secrets.

Marta hung her cloak on the peg by the door — third peg, always the third peg, the one closest to the stove — and came to stand at Ulyana's shoulder the way she had done ten thousand times. Ulyana felt her there without looking: the smell of tallow soap and woodsmoke, the faint creak of the leather apron Marta wore for her work at the tannery, the warmth of her at a distance of exactly one forearm, which was how close Marta stood to people she loved and no closer.

"You've been avoiding me," Marta said.

"I've been working."

"You've been *working*," Marta agreed, "in the way that a woman hides in a room full of knives." She reached out and turned the sheep bone over in Ulyana's hands, studying the half-finished knotwork along its length. "This is good. This is better than good. Who is it for?"

"No one yet."

"Ulyana."

"It's for no one *yet*, Marta. Sometimes the bone tells me who it belongs to and sometimes I have to wait for the dead to catch up with the living." She set down the knife and finally looked up, and found Marta's face doing something complicated — the smile still in place but the eyes elsewhere, somewhere behind Ulyana's head, somewhere out past the window and the garden and the shrine-garden beyond it where a woman who remembered being a goddess pulled weeds by daylight.

"You went back there," Marta said quietly. "To your grandmother's land."

"I did."

"The old shrine-garden. That everyone says is cursed."

"That everyone *says* is cursed," Ulyana said, "because everyone in this valley has gotten very comfortable saying things instead of knowing them."

Something flickered across Marta's face — quick as a minnow under ice, gone before Ulyana could name it. Hurt? Fear? Guilt? She had known this woman for twenty-three years. She had held her hair back when Marta drank too much plum brandy at the harvest festival. Marta had sat with her through the whole night after her mother died, saying nothing, just holding her hand, because Marta understood better than anyone that some griefs needed silence more than words. And yet in this moment Ulyana looked at her oldest friend and felt as though she were reading a letter written in a language almost her own — close enough to recognize the shapes, too far to make out the words.

"I heard you asked Elder Soren about the founding families," Marta said.

Ulyana kept her voice light. "I ask lots of people lots of things. It's my worst habit."

"You asked him who owned the shrine-garden before your grandmother. You asked him about the families who signed the original covenant with the stones. You asked him—" Marta stopped. Started again. "People are talking, Ulya. People are saying you've been asking questions that are better left unasked."

"Who's saying?"

"You know how it is here. Everyone says everything to everyone."

"*Who*, Marta?"

Marta's jaw tightened. For a moment — one long moment in which the stove ticked and the wind worried at the shutters and the sheep bone lay between them like a small pale moon — Ulyana thought her friend might actually answer honestly. Then Marta laughed, a bright hollow sound, and waved her hand as if brushing away smoke.

"Oh, everyone. Soren. Old Petra at the well. Half the market, probably. You know how a question travels in Thornmere — faster than gossip, slower than plague." She dropped onto the stool by the stove, uninvited, unwelcome in a way she had never been before, and started warming her hands as though she'd walked in from a winter that hadn't arrived yet. "Anyway. I didn't come to scold you. I came because Ivo the mapmaker's been asking after you at the tavern. Says he owes your mother a debt. Says he can get you past the stones."

Ulyana's blood went cold and hot at once. "How does he know I want past the stones?"

"Does he need to? A mapmaker sees what people look at. He sees where their eyes go when they talk about the world." Marta rubbed her hands together harder than the cold required. "You're really thinking of going, then. Out there. With frost falling in midsummer and prayers getting answered wrong."

"I'm thinking of finding out what's true."

"Truth." Marta said the word strangely, tasting it, and for a heartbeat her eyes were wet and furious and frightened all at once. "You always did think truth was a thing you could dig up and hold. Like a root. Like a bone." She stood abruptly, retrieving her cloak from the third peg. "Some roots go down into poison, Ulya. Some bones are buried deep for reasons. Maybe the reason is mercy."

"Whose mercy?"

But Marta was already at the door, already pulling the cloak across her shoulders, and when she turned back her face had smoothed itself into something almost like her usual self — warm, wry, steady Marta, the Marta of twenty-three years, except that Ulyana had seen the other face underneath and could not now unsee it.

"Be careful with the bone-carver's gift," Marta said. "It was given to your family for a purpose, and purposes have keepers. Ask yourself who keeps yours."

And she was gone, the bell giving its half-ring, the door swinging shut on the smell of cold air and tallow soap, leaving Ulyana alone with the knife and the bone and the terrible new knowledge that she had just watched her best friend lie to her — not clumsily, not badly, but carefully, the way a person mends a tear in cloth they cannot afford to replace.

*Purposes have keepers.*

She picked up the knife. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook.

* * *

She worked until the light failed, and then she lit the lamp and worked longer, because the alternative was lying in bed listening to her own thoughts circle the same three questions: What did Marta know? When did she know it? And why did a woman who loved her look like she was drowning while telling her to stay on dry land?

The workshop at night was a different country. By day it belonged to the living — customers, neighbors, the ordinary commerce of grief and memory. But after dark, when the lamp burned low and the valley settled into itself, the dead came closer. Not all of them. Not loudly. But Thornmere Valley was old, older than its founding families liked to admit, and the ground remembered even when the records burned and the gravestones weathered blank.

Her gift had never worked properly inside the valley. That was the joke of it, the quiet cruelty: a bone-hearer born in the one place magic couldn't cross. At the edges — near the boundary stones, near the shrine-garden, near the places where the valley's skin grew thin — the voices came through like sound through water. Muffled. Fragmentary. Enough to carve by, never enough to trust completely.

Tonight, though, something was different.

She felt it first as a pressure behind her ears, the way deep water presses on a diver. Then the lamp flame leaned sideways though the window was shut. Then the sheep bone in her hands — the one meant for no one yet — grew warm, warmer than her palms could account for, warm as a thing freshly alive.

"Oh no," she said softly. "No, I'm not ready—"

But the dead do not wait for readiness. They never had.

The voice came up through the bone the way water comes up through chalk, slow and unstoppable, and it was a man's voice, an old man's, worn thin as a psalm page. She knew him at once — or knew his kind. One of the founders. One of the men whose names were carved into the covenant stone in the meetinghouse square, worn nearly illegible now by two hundred years of thumbs tracing them without knowing why.

He was whispering. That was the terrible part. Not speaking, not crying out, but *whispering*, as though he had spent two centuries learning to be quiet and could not now remember any other way.

*"...she led them... she told us the gods had abandoned us, that the plague was our punishment for calling... she said cutting the covenant was mercy..."*

Ulyana's knife stilled. "Who?" she whispered. "Who said?"

The voice gathered itself. She felt the effort of it, the way she felt effort in the dying sometimes — the soul gathering its last strength toward one final utterance. Two hundred years dead, and still he had strength left for this, which meant he had spent two hundred years saving it up.

*"Aldith."*

The name landed in Ulyana's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It took a long time coming.

*Aldith.* She knew the name. Every child in Thornmere knew it the way children know the names of storms — Aldith Vane, first matriarch of the Vane family, the woman whose portrait hung in the meetinghouse, whose charity endowed the school, whose grave in the old cemetery was the largest and most ornate in the valley. *Blessed Aldith, Mother of the Valley.* Children laid flowers there on Founding Day.

Marta was a Vane. Marta's grandmother had been the last Vane matriarch before Marta's mother had married out and taken her husband's plainer name. Marta had grown up on stories of Blessed Aldith the way other children grew up on fairy tales — the wise founder, the protector, the woman who saved the valley in its darkest hour.

*"Muted,"* the old man whispered, and the word came through the bone with a weight that made Ulyana's teeth ache. *"We muted them. Gods forgive us — we muted them, and she taught us to call it salvation. Ask the stones. Ask the stones what they seal. Ask her granddaughter's granddaughter—"*

The voice frayed. Snapped. The pressure behind Ulyana's ears released all at once, and the lamp flame straightened, and the bone in her hands went cool and ordinary and dumb.

She sat very still for a long time.

Outside, the summer dark pressed against the windows. Somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up. The stove ticked as it cooled, marking time the way it always did, indifferent and faithful.

*Marta's grandmother's name,* whispered alongside the word *muted*. And worse — worse than the name, worse than the word — the old man's last sentence, unfinished, reaching forward across two centuries like a hand out of a grave: *ask her granddaughter's granddaughter.*

Ask Marta.

Ulyana looked down at the sheep bone in her lap, at the half-finished knotwork, at the small curling shavings scattered across her apron like snow that had fallen indoors. She thought about Marta's face in the lamplight an hour ago — the minnow-flicker of guilt, the careful mend, the lie worn smooth as river glass. *Some roots go down into poison. Some bones are buried deep for reasons. Maybe the reason is mercy.*

*Purposes have keepers.*

And beneath all of it, quieter than the dead man's whisper but far more devastating, the simple unbearable fact: Marta had come here tonight to warn her off. To steer her. To stand in this workshop with twenty-three years of friendship between them and use every ounce of that friendship as a wall.

Unless she was protecting me, Ulyana thought. Unless she thinks she's protecting me.

That was the trap of loyalty, she realized. It made every betrayal wear the mask of love. It made it impossible to know whether the hand on your shoulder was holding you up or holding you down.

She rose, went to the window, and looked out across the dark gardens toward the shrine-garden on her grandmother's land, where a single candle burned in the ruined doorway — Freya's candle, lit every night, patient as a star. Beyond it, past the hedgerows and the orchards, she could just make out the grey suggestion of the boundary stones, moonlit, silent, keeping whatever secret they had been cut to keep.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would go to the cemetery and stand before Blessed Aldith's grand marble monument, and she would lay her hands against the cold stone, and she would ask the dead woman herself what she had done, and why, and what it had cost.

But tonight — tonight she took a fresh bone from the chest, a long one, dense and white, and she began to carve. Not a memorial. Not a charm. Something else. Something she had never carved before: a question, cut into the marrow-deep grain, sharp enough to make even the most practiced dead speak plainly.

And as the knife moved, in the silence of the workshop, in the silence of the valley, in the silence that two hundred years of careful lies had built into the very stones — Ulyana heard, faint and far away beyond the boundary, a sound she had never heard in her life:

Winter, beginning.
