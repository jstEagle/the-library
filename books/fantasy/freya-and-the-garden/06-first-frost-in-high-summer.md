## 6. First Frost in High Summer

The frost came on the night of the Longmarket, when the whole valley smelled of roasting lamb and honey cakes and the river ran loud with children who should have been asleep.

Ulyana knew it first through the bones.

She was working late — a wrist bone from a woman dead eighty years, being reshaped into a charm for a granddaughter's wedding — when the workshop went cold around her. Not the ordinary cool of a stone room at midnight. This cold had intention. It slid under the door like something hunting, and the bone in her hands grew so chill it burned, and every voice she carried in her head went quiet at once, the way birds go quiet before a storm breaks.

Then the dead began to whisper. Not to her. To themselves. A low rustle of unease passing through them like wind through wheat, and beneath it, one word repeated so many times it lost its shape:

*Cold. Cold. Cold.*

She set down the bone and opened the shutters.

The garden was white.

Every leaf of her grandmother's beans, every frond of the fennel, every petal of the old roses Freya had been coaxing back into bloom — all of it silvered over, stiff, glittering under a fat midsummer moon as if the night had been dipped in glass. Beyond the fence, the meadow grass stood in frozen ranks. The river shallows had laced themselves with ice that clicked and groaned as the current pushed against it.

Ulyana stepped outside barefoot and the ground bit her feet like teeth. Her breath bloomed white. In high summer, in Thornmere Valley, where the longest day had been celebrated only that morning with garlands and dancing until dawn.

She looked toward the shrine-garden at the edge of her grandmother's land, where the bean rows grew wild and a woman who remembered being a goddess slept beneath a roof of woven hazel.

The frost had not touched it.

Inside those low stone walls, the beans hung green and heavy, the soil dark and warm, the air soft as breath. A circle of living summer in a valley gone to winter overnight, and at its center, wrapped in a wool blanket, Freya sat awake, watching Ulyana across the distance with eyes that caught the moonlight the way ice does.

*It has begun,* her mouth said, too far away to hear, though Ulyana read the words easily enough. She had grown fluent in that mouth, those hands.

*What has begun?*

Freya did not answer. She only looked past Ulyana, north, toward the boundary stones, and her face held an expression Ulyana had never seen on her before — not the wry patience of a gardener, not the distant grief of a god remembering herself.

Fear.

* * *

By morning the market square was a graveyard of produce.

Ulyana walked through it at dawn with her collar up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and everywhere she looked the story was written in ruined green. The greengrocers' stalls sagged under baskets of blackened lettuce. The peach seller — old Bram, who had bragged for a week about his earliest harvest in twenty years — stood turning a fruit over and over in his palms, its flesh split and weeping where the frost had gotten inside it. The wheat sheaves stacked for the blessing had gone brittle as straw left from autumn. Children poked at puddles skinned with ice and were scolded by mothers whose breath smoked in the morning air.

Nobody was selling. Everybody was talking.

"—came up the Ashfall road, my cousin swears it, a whole family walking out of the north with frost in their hair—"

"—the millpond froze solid, top to bottom, and Tam Fellows broke a hole in it and the water underneath was *warm*, what does that mean, what kind of—"

"—my grandmother used to say the valley keeps its own weather. Keeps it, like a purse. Well, someone's been picking the purse—"

They fell quiet as Ulyana passed. Not rudely. Worse than rudely — carefully, the way people speak around a sickbed. She was the bone-carver. She spoke with the dead. If anything in Thornmere knew why summer had died in the night, they believed she would be the one to hear it first.

She bought nothing. There was nothing to buy. She walked home the long way, along the river, and found the reeds bent and glittering, encased in ice so clear they looked like glass sculptures of themselves, a thousand small deaths arranged in beautiful rows.

At the shrine-garden, Freya was harvesting beans as though it were any other morning, her basket filling with pods fat and unblemished while the world beyond her walls lay stricken.

"You knew," Ulyana said.

"I suspected." Freya snapped a pod free and dropped it in the basket. "I hoped I was wrong. Gods are rarely wrong about winters, Ulyana. We invented most of them." She looked up, and the fear from last night was still there, banked now, controlled, but present. "This is not weather. Weather doesn't have a direction. This is coming *from* somewhere."

"From beyond the stones."

"From beyond everything you've ever let yourself imagine." Freya set down the basket and wiped her hands on her apron, and when she spoke again her voice was gentler, which somehow made it worse. "The covenant didn't just mute the gods, love. It bound the seasons to the bargain. While gods and mortals kept faith with each other — even at a distance, even muted — the wheel turned as it should. But the covenant isn't sleeping anymore. It's dying. And when it dies, both realms unravel together. Your summer. Our—" she paused, corrected herself, "—*their* winter. There's no separating them anymore. There never was."

"The elders say the valley protects us. That the stones keep out magic and sickness and war."

"The stones keep out truth," Freya said. "Everything else gets in eventually."

* * *

The refugees arrived on the third day, and with them, the questions Ulyana could not answer.

They came up the north road in a ragged column of perhaps forty souls — farmers, a tinker with his cart half-abandoned, two sisters carrying a grandmother between them on a chair with its legs sawn off for poles. They wore their summer clothes, because three weeks ago it had been summer where they came from, and now they wore blankets over the linen and their lips were cracked and their eyes had the flat shine of people who had stopped expecting the world to make sense.

The elders met them at the gate. Ulyana stood at the edge of the crowd, because Marta had come to find her — *you should be here,* Marta had said, gripping her arm too hard, *they'll ask about the bones, they'll ask about you* — and so she watched from beside her oldest friend as Elder Corwin, white-bearded and shaking, demanded to know what had happened beyond the stones.

An old man answered him. A farmer named, he said, Hessel, from a village called Marrowbridge that Ulyana had never heard of, though it could not have been more than six days' walk.

"We prayed," Hessel said. His voice was steady, which was more frightening than screaming would have been. "That's what happened. We prayed, and we were answered."

Murmuring ran through the crowd like a current. Prayer was a private thing in Thornmere, a habit kept small and safe precisely because the valley's founding law forbade magic, because prayers unanswered were harmless, because the stones made sure of it. To hear a man say *we prayed and were answered* aloud, in the market square, was like hearing someone confess to murder in church.

"Answered how?" Corwin asked.

"Literally." Hessel laughed, a dry terrible sound. "A woman in our village lost her son to fever. She prayed to whatever god would listen: *give him back.*" He let that sit. "Something gave him back. He walked home on the fourth day. He was cold as a grave and his eyes were frosted over and he smiled all the time, and he stood at the foot of her bed every night saying *Mother, you asked, Mother, here I am,* and she — " His voice finally broke. "She's the one who told us to run. She burned her own house with him in it. Or tried. You can't burn what's already frozen."

Silence, absolute, in a square that should have been roaring with midsummer trade.

"Another family," Hessel went on, quieter. "Their well was failing. They prayed for water. Their fields flooded — black water, ice-cold, rising out of the ground itself, drowning the barley, drowning the sheep. They prayed for it to stop and it stopped, all right. Everything stopped. The rain, the wind, the birdsong. A stillness came over our valley like a held breath, and the frost came behind it, and it hasn't lifted since. Whatever's answering us out there, it remembers being worshipped and it has forgotten everything else. It answers the *words*. Only the words. Never the meaning."

Ulyana felt Marta's hand close around her wrist. When she turned, her friend's face was pale as the frost-killed peaches.

"That's what muting protected us from," Marta whispered. It was almost too soft to hear. Almost. "You see? This is why. This is exactly why."

And there it was — the thing Marta had never quite said in all the years of their friendship, surfacing now in a single slip, in a crowded square, with the elders ten feet away. *This is why.* As if Marta knew the reason the way one knows a family recipe. As if the muting were a thing done *for* people rather than *to* them.

Ulyana looked at her, and Marta realized what she'd said, and something flickered across her face — guilt, or fear, or the beginning of a confession — before Elder Corwin's voice cut through the square.

"The bone-carver."

Every head turned. Ulyana's stomach dropped.

"Ulyana Pemberton." Corwin beckoned, and there was no refusing an elder's summons in front of forty refugees and half the town. She walked forward through the parted crowd with Marta's grip sliding from her wrist, and stood in the open space before the gate, feeling very small and very visible, the way she imagined a bone must feel on the carving table.

"The dead talk to you," Corwin said. It wasn't a question. Everyone knew; it was the valley's worst-kept secret, tolerated because Ulyana's charms comforted mourners and her gift stayed small and quiet and hers. "Tell us. Do the dead know what's happening?"

*Yes,* she thought. *They're whispering about it constantly. They're afraid. The dead are afraid, and no one alive seems to understand what that means.*

But she thought of Freya's warning — *the stones keep out truth* — and of the skull in her workshop, the oldest one, and what it had confirmed about the muting, and about mortal hands helping to do it. She thought of Marta's grandmother's name, whispered by a dead man alongside the word *muted*. She thought of the founding families, of whom Marta's was one, and of the elders standing before her now wearing expressions of authority stretched thin over something that looked, to anyone who carved faces for a living, remarkably like guilt.

If she spoke, she would not be able to speak halfway. Truth was like that. Like frost — it got inside the fruit and split it.

"I can tell you what the dead feel," she said carefully. "They feel the cold. They feel the covenant — the old bargain between this valley and the gods — coming apart. What I can't tell you is how to stop it, because stopping it will require this valley to learn things about itself that some of its founders chose, deliberately, to bury."

Corwin's face went grey. Beside him, Elder Sanna — Marta's great-aunt — drew a sharp breath, and her eyes darted sideways, just once, toward Marta.

It was a small motion. Ulyana would have missed it a month ago. But she had spent her life reading what people did not say, and she saw it plainly: recognition. Fear with a name attached.

"You presume a great deal," Sanna said coldly, "for a woman who plays at séances with chicken bones."

"I carve memorials," Ulyana said. "The chickens are your metaphor, not mine."

Someone in the crowd laughed — a short, startled bark — and the tension shifted, just slightly, the crowd's sympathy tilting. Corwin raised a hand for quiet.

"The council will consider what the bone-carver has said," he announced, in the tone of a man closing a door on a room he does not wish to enter. "Refugees will be housed at the tithe barn. Market is suspended until the frost lifts. Go home. All of you. Pray quietly, if you must pray at all."

*Pray quietly.* As if volume were the danger. As if the gods out there were simply hard of hearing instead of starving.

The crowd dispersed in knots of murmuring. Hessel and his people were led toward the barn, the grandmother on her sawn-off chair bobbing above the heads of the crowd like a strange banner. Marta appeared at Ulyana's elbow, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"You shouldn't have said that," Marta murmured. "About the founders. About what they buried."

"Shouldn't I? People are freezing to death six days from here, Marta. In their summer clothes. Because of something this valley's founders *did*."

"You don't know that."

"I know the dead don't lie to me. It's the living I have trouble with." She regretted it the moment it left her mouth — saw it land, saw Marta flinch as if struck — but she did not take it back, because taking it back would have been a lie too, and she was so tired of those, and she had a suspicion, growing colder and heavier in her chest by the hour, that she was going to be tired of them for reasons far worse than friendship before this winter was done.

Marta's jaw tightened. "Be careful, Ulyana. Please. Just — be careful who you say things to. Some truths hurt more people than they help."

"Some lies do too," Ulyana said. "They just take longer."

Marta left without another word, and Ulyana stood alone in the emptying square among the ruined crops, and the frost on the cobbles had not melted by noon, and would not melt at all.

That evening, crossing the meadow toward the shrine-garden, she heard it — faint, from beyond the northern hills, carried on a wind that smelled of iron and deep places under glaciers.

Singing.

Many voices, wordless, weaving in and out of each other like a round that never resolves. It was beautiful the way a wolf's eyes are beautiful, and it was getting closer, and the dead in her head had all gone silent again, listening to it too.

At the garden wall, Freya stood waiting, her face tilted toward the sound, her hands white-knuckled on the hazel fence.

"You hear it," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"Yes."

"Then we have less time than I thought." Freya turned to her, and the last light of the drowned sun caught her eyes. "That's the Hollow Choir, Ulyana. And they've learned your name."
