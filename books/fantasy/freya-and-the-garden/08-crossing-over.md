## 8. Crossing Over

The boundary stones did not look like the edge of a world.

They looked like what they were: seven leaning granite teeth in a mossy ring at the top of Shepherd's Rise, each one taller than a man and older than any name Ulyana knew, their faces worn smooth by weather that had been working on them since before her grandmother's grandmother learned to carve. Lichen mapped them in continents of grey and gold. A crow had nested in the hollow of the tallest one for as long as anyone could remember, and this morning it watched them come with one flat black eye, unbothered, as if travelers crossed out of the valley every day.

Perhaps they had, once. Before the muting. Before the covenant was cut and the stones became not a border but a lid.

Ulyana stopped three paces from the nearest stone and found that her feet had opinions of their own.

"You can still turn back," Ivo said behind her. He was doing a poor job of sounding casual, which she appreciated. His whistle hung around his neck on its leather cord; his hands kept finding it and letting go again. "No one would think less of you. Well. Marta would think less of me, but that's already settled."

"I'm not turning back." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm just — listening."

That was the truth of it, or near enough. All her life she had lived inside a strange quiet. The valley forbade magic; the elders said so every founding-day, standing before these very stones with their candles and their careful histories. And yet her gift had persisted, thin as frost-flower on glass, at the edges of things. The dead spoke to her from graveyards, from wells, from the drowned places under the mill-race — but faintly, always faintly, like voices heard through a wall, through wool, through winter ice. She had thought that was simply what the dead sounded like. She had built her whole craft, her whole self, on that assumption: that the dead were distant, that hearing them was like hearing rain from indoors.

Freya stood beside her at the ring's edge, and the morning light did something wrong to her — caught in her hair as if it remembered being worshipped there. She wore her warden's coat, patched at the elbows, dirt still under her nails from the bean rows they'd left at dawn. She looked like a woman. She looked like a garden gate left open.

"When you cross," Freya said quietly, "it will be loud."

"I know."

"You don't. You can't. I've tried to describe it and everything I say is a lie, because I have never been *mortal* about it. So I'll say only this." She turned, and her eyes were the green of deep water over deep stone. "Whatever happens, however loud it gets — I am here. Hold on to that. Hold on to me if you have to. There's no shame in it. The first time I fell into a body, I screamed for a season."

"You've never mentioned that."

"There are many things I have never mentioned." Almost a smile. "Come. The tide doesn't wait, and neither does the winter."

Ivo hung back at the moss line, arms folded, jaw set, a man determined to witness something he'd rather not see. Ulyana took one breath. Two. On the third she stepped between the stones.

---

The world tore open.

Not gently. Not gradually. One stride she was walking through morning air, birdsong, the smell of crushed mint where her boot had found the path's edge — and the next stride carried her across some threshold no eye could find, and every dead voice within miles of her struck her all at once, everywhere, inside her skull and behind her eyes and down the length of her spine, a cathedral organ played by a thousand hands at once.

She heard the drowned boy from the mill-race, the one whose memorial bone she'd carved in spring, singing a skipping song he'd half forgotten. She heard an old shepherd counting sheep who had been dead two hundred years and was still counting, patient as stone, *forty-one, forty-two*, though the flock had long since scattered into soil. She heard plague-dead in a barrow under the eastern ridge, whole families murmuring to each other in a dialect the valley had lost, comforting children who had been dust for centuries. She heard soldiers. She heard a bride. She heard something vast and slow beneath the hills that might have been a giant or might have been the hill itself, dreaming in a voice like glaciers learning language. She heard prayers — old ones, unanswered ones, prayers pressed into the dark like flowers in books, still holding their shape. She heard grief so old it had become geology.

It was ecstasy. It was terror. It was both at once and neither could survive the other, and Ulyana Pemberton, who had spent her whole life believing herself strong because she could bear the dead's whispers through the wall, understood in one white-hot instant that she had never once heard the dead at all. She had heard echoes of echoes. This — *this* was the living chorus of everyone who had ever died within sight of these hills, and it was pouring into her through a door the valley's silence had held shut her entire life, and the door was not going to close.

Her knees went. The sky tilted. Somewhere very far away Ivo was shouting her name, and somewhere closer than that Freya was saying hers — *Ulyana, Ulyana, look at me* — but the names were small boats on an ocean, and the ocean was speaking every language death had ever invented—

Then hands. Warm, human, calloused from bean-rows and pruning-knives, closing around hers and pressing her palms flat against a chest that rose and fell. A heartbeat. Slow. Deliberate. Beating in a rhythm that was not a rhythm at all but a *pattern* — three beats, rest, three beats, rest — the rhythm of someone planting seeds in rows, the rhythm of tending, of patience, of *gardens*.

"Count with me," Freya said, and her voice cut through the flood like a blade through fog. Not loud. Precise. "One row. That's all. One row of beans. In. Out. The soil is warm. One row."

Ulyana clung to it. One row. The beans in Freya's garden grew in nine rows, and Ulyana had weeded them twice, and the fourth row always went crooked because the ground there hid an old foundation stone. She knew that ground. She put herself there, kneeling in the dirt, fourth row, crooked, and the heartbeat under her palms kept its patient time, and slowly — terribly slowly, like a flood finding its banks — the ocean of voices drew back from drowning her and became, instead, a landscape around her.

Still vast. Still everywhere. But *shaped*. She could stand in it. She could breathe in it.

"Oh," she said. It came out as a whisper, cracked and wondering. "Oh, it's — they're not gone. They're not *gone*, Freya. They're all still here. They never left. The valley just—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "All my life I thought I was hearing through a wall. There was never a wall. There was a *lid*, and I was underneath it, and I thought the muffle was the world."

"I know," Freya said.

"They're singing. Some of them. Did you know they sing?"

"I suspected."

Ulyana laughed — a wet, unsteady sound — and realized she was still pressed against Freya's chest, hands fisted in the warden's coat, forehead nearly touching the hollow of her throat. She should pull back. She didn't. For a moment longer she stayed, counting heartbeats, letting the rhythm do its quiet work, and Freya let her, one hand rising hesitantly to rest between Ulyana's shoulder blades, light as a leaf deciding whether to land.

"You screamed for a season," Ulyana said at last, into the coat's rough weave. "When you fell into a body."

"Mm."

"Did anyone hold you like this?"

A pause. Under her ear, the heartbeat stumbled — once — and resumed.

"No," Freya said. "There wasn't anyone. That's rather the point of falling."

Ulyana pulled back then, but only far enough to see her face. Freya's composure was intact the way a frozen lake is intact — perfect surface, enormous depths, and somewhere beneath, a current moving fast. Ulyana had spent years reading faces of people who couldn't speak to her anymore; she read this one easily.

"Well," she said. "There's someone now."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It simply arrived, the way true things do, without asking permission. Freya looked at her for a long moment, and the morning moved through the space between them — wind in the grass beyond the stones, the crow shifting on its nest, Ivo ostentatiously studying his map fifty paces off — and something passed over Freya's face that was too large to be a smile and too quiet to be anything else.

"Yes," she said. "There is."

Then, briskly, before the moment could become a thing they'd have to carry openly: "Can you walk? Can you bear it, at distance? Because it will not lessen, Ulyana. Out here the dead are not a whisper. They are weather. You'll need to learn to live in weather."

Ulyana straightened. Tested herself. The chorus was still there — the shepherd still counting, the plague-families still murmuring, the vast thing under the hills still dreaming — but she could hold it at arm's length now, the way she'd learned to hold a grieving client's sorrow while carving their mother's name. Craft. It came back to craft, always. You did not silence the material. You listened to all of it, and you chose what to answer.

"I can walk," she said. And then, because wonder kept leaking out of her despite everything: "Freya — the drowned boy. Tam. The one I carved the bone for. He's *singing*. He sings the same four notes over and over, and he's happy. He's been happy this whole time, and I carved him with tears in my eyes thinking he was afraid."

"The dead are rarely what the living make of them," Freya said. "That goes for gods, too." Something flickered across her face — the memory of Vaskr, perhaps, or of the Hollow Choir, whose name she spoke only in a lowered voice — and then she shouldered her pack. "Come. We have miles of weather ahead of us, and worse than weather behind."

---

They walked north through country Ulyana had never seen, though she had known it her whole life.

That was the strangest part, she decided, as the morning wore on. Every story, every map, every elder's sermon had painted the outside as emptiness — blank spaces, wasted lands, the world that had chosen to forget Thornmere. Instead there were hedgerows gone wild with dog-rose. There were lanes sunk to their hips in meadowsweet. There was a village ruin with swallows nesting in its chimney, and a shrine to some hearth-god whose name had worn off the stone but whose niche still held a clay cup someone had left, recently, the glaze bright. The land was not empty. It was *unattended* — a great house with the family gone, everything waiting to be picked up again.

And everywhere, the dead. She was learning to move among them the way one moves through a crowded market: acknowledging, not stopping. A woman dead in a field sang to her cows as she'd done for forty years, and the ghost-cows lowed back, and Ulyana smiled at the duet and walked on. A child chased a hoop along a road that no longer existed, laughing, passing through Ivo without either of them knowing. Once, on a ridge, Ulyana felt something enormous turn its attention toward her from far below the earth — curiosity, ancient and unhurried — and she bowed to it, instinctively, the way she bowed to clients' dead, and the attention withdrew, satisfied.

"You're adapting fast," Ivo said, when they paused at a stream. He filled the waterskins without looking at her directly; he still flinched slightly when she cocked her head at empty air. "Faster than I expected."

"I've been practicing my whole life," she said. "Just through a wall. Take away the wall and it turns out the room was always the same room."

He grunted. Then, quieter, wringing a sock dry on a stone: "What do they say? The dead. About all this." He nodded north, where the horizon had developed a white seam, like scar tissue, along its lowest edge. Frost-country. Winter's front line. "About the freezing."

Ulyana listened. She was getting better at asking specific questions of the general chorus, tuning her attention like she tuned a knife-edge. The dead, it turned out, knew a great deal and understood very little — they saw the land from below, root-level, and what they reported was strange. Cold moving through the soil like a slow worm. Roots dying in order of depth. Old graves heaving, not with ghosts but with *frost-heave*, out of season, as if the earth itself were shivering.

"They say the ground is forgetting how to be warm," she said at last. "Which isn't how they usually talk. They're usually very concrete. But that's the phrase three different voices used. *Forgetting how to be warm.*"

Freya had gone still by the stream's edge, water dripping from her cupped hands, and the look on her face made Ulyana's stomach drop.

"That's what it felt like," Freya said softly, "when they muted us. When the covenant was cut. Not pain. *Forgetting.* Like waking and being unable to remember your own name — except the name was warmth, was summer, was the whole agreement between sky and soil. If the dead feel the covenant dying from below..." She stood, flinging the water from her hands. "Then it's faster than I feared. The Worldroot is the anchor-point. Everything unravels outward from it, and we are walking toward the unraveling, not away."

"Cheerful," said Ivo.

"It's why we're going," Ulyana said. But her hand had drifted, without her noticing, to the charm-bag at her belt — the bones she'd carved, her mother's awl, the tools of a trade that had just become, overnight, the most important craft in two worlds.

---

They made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, walls waist-high, roof long gone, stars for rafters. Ivo took first watch without argument, wrapping himself in his coat at the wall's gap, facing south — facing home, though he'd deny it.

Ulyana lay on her bedroll and listened to the night.

It was nothing like the valley's nights. In Thornmere, sleep came wrapped in cotton-silence, the dead reduced to murmurs at the edges of dreams. Here the darkness was *populated* — the shepherd still counting somewhere east, the field-woman humming to her vanished herd, the plague-barrow families telling stories in their lost tongue, generation after generation of voices layered like sediment, and above it all, faint and far, the vast dreaming thing beneath the hills had begun, very softly, to hum.

It should have been terrible. It was terrible, a little. But lying there in the ruins with the stars wheeling overhead, Ulyana found the feeling underneath the terror was something else entirely. Loneliness — her loneliness, thirty years of it, the bone-carver who talked to the dead because the living found her unsettling — that loneliness had quietly died sometime today, out on the road, and she hadn't even noticed it go.

Footsteps, soft. Freya settled beside her, not touching, close enough that their shoulders shared warmth. For a while neither spoke. The dead hummed. The frost-seam glittered on the northern horizon like a blade laid on the world's throat.

"In the valley," Ulyana whispered, "I used to think the worst thing that could happen to me was losing the voices. Deafness. Silence. I'd lie awake and dread it like other people dread drowning." She turned her head. Freya's face was pale starlight and shadow. "Today I walked into ten thousand voices at once and nearly drowned anyway. And the thing that saved me wasn't quiet. It was a heartbeat. Three beats, rest. Fourth row, crooked."

"I remember which row you mean," Freya said.

"I know you do."

Silence again — the good kind, the kind that has people in it. Then Freya's hand found hers in the dark, deliberate this time, no pretense of accident, fingers lacing through hers with the care of someone planting something they intend to keep alive.

"Tomorrow we cross the Ashfall road," Freya murmured. "After that, the country changes. The Hollow Choir hunts along it. If they find us—"

"Then they find us together," Ulyana said. "I'm done being spoken for and done being alone. Both at once, if necessary."

Freya's breath caught — barely, but Ulyana heard it, the way she now heard everything, the whole ringing world — and her hand tightened.

From the wall, Ivo's voice, low and suddenly sharp: "Do you hear that?"

Ulyana listened past the dead, past the hum, past the counting and the lullabies — and caught it, threading underneath everything like wire through cloth.

Singing. Wordless. Many voices, perfectly in unison, coming from the north.

Coming from the frost.
