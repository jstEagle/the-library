## 9. The Hollow Choir

Freya had forgotten what it was to be afraid.

Not the small fears — the flinch of a hand near a hot kettle, the startle at thunder. Those had stayed with her through the long centuries of muting, lodged in her borrowed body like stones in a riverbed. But true fear, the kind that lived in the marrow of gods, the kind that remembered the shape of endings — that had gone quiet when her name went quiet, and she had almost been grateful.

Now it woke again, all at once, on a road of grey gravel beneath a sky the color of old pewter, and she understood why mortals built so many walls.

She felt them before she heard them: a wrongness in the air ahead, like a held breath stretched over miles. The wind had stopped moving. Not stilled — *stopped*, as if the world between here and the horizon had been pressed under glass. The birches along the roadside stood with their leaves turned upward, silver undersides showing, and not one leaf trembled.

"Freya." Ulyana's voice came low beside her. The bone-carver had stopped mid-stride, her charm-bag clutched to her chest with both arms, and her face had gone the pale grey of ash. "The dead just went silent."

"The dead?"

"All of them. Every voice for miles. It's like—" Ulyana swallowed. "It's like standing in a house where every room is suddenly empty."

Ivo shifted the cart's weight on his shoulder and looked from one woman to the other. "What is it? What do you feel?"

*Everything,* Freya thought. *I feel everything I lost.*

"They're coming," she said aloud, and heard how strange her own voice sounded — layered now, doubled, the warden's rough alto underneath and something older ringing above it like a struck bell. It had been growing stronger since they crossed the boundary stones, since the world beyond Thornmere had begun pouring divinity back into her the way rain pours into a dry well. She could feel her name again. She could feel the frost coming behind them like a tide. And she could feel, ahead, in the stopped air —

— hunger.

"Off the road," she said. "Now. Into the trees."

Ivo didn't argue; he'd learned that much about her in three days of travel. He hauled the cart toward the treeline while Ulyana ran, and Freya turned once to look back down the empty road, because some part of her — the part that had been a goddess before she'd been a woman, before she'd been a garden, before she'd been nothing at all — needed to see them with her own eyes.

They came out of the north like weather given legs.

There were seven of them. They did not walk so much as arrive, each step placing them where they wished to be, and they were shaped like people the way a statue is shaped like a person — correct in outline, wrong in every particular that mattered. Their skin was the white-blue of deep ice. Their faces were frozen mid-expression, each one caught forever in the moment of its last feeling: one weeping, one laughing, one screaming without sound, one wearing an expression of such perfect serenity that Freya's stomach turned to look at it. Frost bloomed and died across their bodies in slow pulses, like breathing.

And they were singing.

That was the worst of it. That was the thing she would carry in her chest for the rest of her life, however long or short that life proved to be. They sang prayers — human prayers, mortal prayers, the small desperate beautiful petitions of farmers and mothers and drowning sailors — but they sang them *back*. Distorted. Inverted. A child's prayer for rain became a hymn of endless drowning. A widow's plea for one more day became a psalm of days without end, gray and identical, stretching into forever. They wore the voices of the people who had prayed to them like stolen coats, and none of the voices matched their frozen faces, and the dissonance of it made the air itself ache.

"The Hollow Choir," Freya said. Her mouth was dry. "Gods who went feral in the silence. Prayers used to feed them, Ulyana. For a thousand years, every whispered hope was a meal. Then the covenant cut the line, and they starved — but hunger doesn't die. It only changes what it eats."

"What does it eat now?" Ivo called from the trees.

"Prayer," Freya said. "Any prayer. Even the ones they have to take."

Ulyana appeared at her shoulder, breathing hard, her dark eyes wide and fixed on the seven approaching figures. "Can you fight them?"

"I don't know." The honesty cost her something. She had spent three days being the one who knew things — the road, the dangers, the names of dead gods — and she hated how thin that authority suddenly felt. "I'm not what I was. I'm a fraction. A memory of a memory. Against one of them, maybe. Against seven—"

One of the Choir stopped.

Its head turned — a smooth, grinding rotation, like ice shifting on a lake — and its serene face oriented toward Freya with the precision of a compass needle finding north. Then, slowly, its mouth opened, and from it came a voice Freya knew better than any other voice in creation.

Her own.

*"O Freya, who remembers, who tends, who was forsaken — hear us, who were forsaken also. O Freya, come back to us. O Freya, come back to us. O Freya—"*

"That's my prayer," Ulyana whispered. "From the shrine-garden. From the night I first—"

"It kept it." Freya's hands had closed into fists without her permission. "It keeps everything it's ever been asked for. And now it's using yours to call me home."

"Home to what?"

"To the silence." She pushed Ulyana toward the trees, harder than she meant to, gentler than she wanted. "Run."

* * *

They did not make the trees.

The Choir did not hurry. Hurrying, Freya understood with sudden terrible clarity, was a mortal anxiety; the Hollow had all the time that freezing things possess. One moment the seven stood a quarter-mile distant on the grey road, singing their inverted hymns, and the next the nearest stood ten feet away, close enough that Freya could see the frost-flowers opening and closing in the hollows of its cheeks, close enough to smell it — cold iron and candle-snuff and the particular sweetness of flowers left too long in a sealed room.

Its frozen face was the serene one. Its stolen voice was Ulyana's.

*"O Freya, come back to us—"*

"I am not yours," Freya said, and put herself bodily between the thing and the bone-carver.

The Hollow tilted its head. Frost crackled down its neck. When it spoke again, it spoke with a voice Freya had not heard in nine hundred years — her own true voice, the one she'd worn when the Worldroot was green and her temples smelled of beeswax and crushed mint.

*"You kept your name, little sister. We lost ours in the dark. Give us yours. Fairness. Fairness. Fairness."*

"No."

*"Then we will take the one who gave it back to you."* The serene face did not move, but somehow it smiled. *"The little maker. The little listener. Her craft smells of the old work. Her charms are doors, sister. Did you know? Her charms are doors, and we are so hungry, and doors open from both sides."*

Cold flooded through Freya that had nothing to do with the Choir's proximity. She glanced back — Ulyana stood three paces behind her, charm-bag clutched tight, and the carved bones within it were *glowing*, faintly, each one lit from inside like a lamp behind horn. Of course. Of course they were. Mortal craft had always been a vessel for divine fire; that was the whole secret of the covenant, the whole reason gods had needed mortals at all. Prayer flowed downhill into made things — into idols, amulets, painted eyes, carved bone. Ulyana's work was good work. Honest work. Work done with love for the dead.

Doors indeed.

"Ulyana," Freya said quietly, without turning around. "Give me your bag."

"You said never to touch me with your power, that it burns—"

"*Give me the bag.*"

A pause. Then the strap slid over Ulyana's head, and the bag landed in Freya's palms, warm as a living thing, humming with the residue of a hundred careful deaths honored and a hundred careful hands at work. She reached inside and drew out the first charm her fingers found: a small rib-bone carved with a running stag, made — she knew without asking — for a boy who had died chasing one.

The Hollow raised its hand. Ice leapt from its fingers like a lunging serpent.

Freya spoke the stag-charm's purpose into it — not a spell, nothing so tidy, but a *naming*, the old naming, the thing she had done ten thousand times at ten thousand altars: *run, swift one, run and do not stop* — and threw it into the air.

The charm detonated into light.

For one heartbeat the rib-bone was a stag — antlers like winter branches, hooves striking sparks from the gravel — and then it was motion itself, a streak of golden fury that hit the Hollow's outstretched arm and shattered it to the elbow. The creature did not scream. Its frozen face could not change. But the singing faltered, all seven voices stumbling at once, and the sound of that stumble was the most hopeful thing Freya had heard in nine centuries.

*It can be hurt. It can be hurt.*

"More," she breathed. "Ulyana — what have you got?"

"Wh-what are they *for*?" Ulyana was digging in the bag, pulling out carvings, holding them up one by one. "Tell me what they're for and I'll tell you which—"

"The hare — grief, and the outrunning of it. The wolf — boundaries. The salmon — knowledge hidden in deep water. The moth—"

"Moth is for Marta's mother. It's for letting go."

"Then keep the moth." Freya caught the hare-charm as Ulyana tossed it, and the second Hollow was already upon them — the weeping one, its frozen tears glittering, its many stolen voices keening a lullaby backwards. She named the hare into waking and hurled it, and light exploded across the road, and the weeping god reeled back with a wound across its chest that bled not blood but snowfall, soft and endless and wrong.

But there were seven, and Freya was a fraction, a memory of a memory, and she felt her strength going out of her like heat from an open door. Each naming cost her. Each charm burned a little more of the divinity the road had poured back into her, and there was no well here to refill from, no shrine, no sleeping worshipper dreaming her name. The third Hollow — the screaming one — caught her full in the chest with a fist of black frost before she could raise the wolf-charm, and she went down hard on the gravel, and the sky wheeled above her, pewter-grey and pitiless.

Through the ringing in her skull she heard Ulyana scream her name.

No — not scream. *Pray.* Ulyana Pemberton, who did not pray, who had told her once that prayer was a luxury for people with someone to listen — Ulyana knelt in the frozen gravel with her hands bloody from the bag's strap and *prayed*, raw and wordless and desperate, and Freya felt that prayer strike her like sunlight through a broken roof.

*Mortal craft,* she thought dazedly, pushing herself up on her elbows. *Mortal craft, mortal prayer. Doors open from both sides.*

"Ulyana!" Her voice cracked. "Your charms — they're not weapons, they're *vessels*. They hold whatever I pour into them. But I can't reach them all — so *you* throw them. You name them. Your hands made them; your voice will carry further than mine!"

"I don't know how—"

"You've been doing it your whole life! Every memorial you ever carved, you named the dead into peace! This is the same work! *This is the same work!*"

Something changed in Ulyana's face. Freya would remember it afterward the way mortals remember lightning: the exact moment fear became something else. The bone-carver snatched the wolf-charm from the bag, held it up before her, and spoke — not a goddess's naming, formal and ancient, but a craftsman's, plain and fierce and utterly certain:

"Wolf. You held the line for the village of Hallowmere in the year of the red fever. Hold it now."

The wolf woke howling.

It was not gold like the stag or silver like the hare; it was every color at once, the color of a boundary defended, and it struck the screaming Hollow center-mass and drove it backward twenty feet, thirty, its frozen face finally cracking — a single hairline fracture across the cheek, from which poured light instead of blood.

Ulyana laughed. Actually laughed — a wild, disbelieving sound — and pulled the salmon from the bag. "Salmon! You swam up the falls at Vaskr when every net failed. Swim now." Light. Motion. The fourth Hollow staggered, its song shredding into static.

They fell into a rhythm then, the two of them, back to back in the ruined gravel, and Freya thought: *oh. Oh, this is what it was for. This is what the covenant was always for.* Not worship groveling upward, not power raining down, but this — a god's remembrance poured into a mortal's making, a mortal's certainty lifting a god's failing hands. Two halves of one bridge. The very thing that had been severed at the valley's edge, rebuilt here in miniature, in terror, in the space between their shoulders.

The fifth charm broke the laughing one's jaw. The sixth froze the serene one mid-step, locked in its own element. The seventh—

The seventh was the moth.

The Choir had regrouped — four still standing, singing now with fury instead of hunger, their inverted hymns stacking into a wall of sound that made the gravel dance. And Ulyana, bleeding, breathless, glorious, held up the moth-charm and hesitated.

"This one's different," she said. "This one I carved for a living woman. It's not finished. It can't be finished until she lets go, and she hasn't, and I won't force it, Freya, I *won't*—"

"Then don't finish it," Freya said. "Name it as it is. Name the *not-letting-go*. Name the holding-on. That's honest work too."

Ulyana stared at her. Then she lifted the unfinished moth to the grey sky, and her voice shook, and did not break:

"Moth. You are the love that stays past all reason. You are the hand that will not open. *Stay now.* Stay, and hold, and do not let go — of *us*."

The moth rose from her palm like a lantern unchained, and it did not attack the Choir. It simply hung above them, vast and luminous, wings beating a slow pulse — and everywhere its light fell, the stolen voices fell silent. The inverted hymns guttered out one by one, because no stolen voice could sing inside the radius of a love that refused to let go. The Hollow stood mute in the moth-light, mouths open, faces frozen, and for the first time Freya saw them clearly for what they were: not monsters. Orphans. Gods who had starved so long they had forgotten they were gods, wearing the last prayers they'd ever been fed like children wearing their mothers' clothes.

"We can't kill them," she said quietly. "We never could. Come on. While they're quiet — *run*."

* * *

They ran. Ivo met them a mile south with the cart and a face like a man who had watched his own funeral, and none of them spoke until the birches gave way to pine and the pine to bare granite hills, and the sun — weak, watery, real — broke through the pewter sky.

They camped in the lee of a boulder. Ivo built a fire with shaking hands. Ulyana sat turning the spent charms over and over in her fingers — the stag cracked, the hare dim, the wolf split cleanly in half, the moth still faintly warm — and Freya watched her from across the flames and felt something rearrange itself permanently in her chest.

Nine hundred years ago, she had commanded storms. She had spoken and mountains had considered moving. And none of it — none of it — had ever moved her the way watching a mortal woman name an unfinished carving into a shield of stubborn love.

"You knew," Ulyana said at last, not looking up. "Back on the road. You knew what my charms could do."

"I hoped. There's a difference."

"There isn't, for you. For you hoping is practically a commandment." Ulyana looked up then, and firelight moved in her eyes. "You could have told me. You could have prepared me. Instead you threw yourself in front of it and started handing out orders like I was — like I was someone to be *protected*."

"You were. You are."

"And you nearly died for it." Ulyana's voice was low and steady and more frightening than any shout. "Freya. If you speak for me again — if you decide for me what I can bear — we're going to have the same problem you and the Choir have. Understand?"

The words went into Freya like a splinter, finding a truth she'd been avoiding. *Protection* was the oldest sin of her kind. It had built the covenant. It had built the muting. Somewhere in Thornmere Valley, someone had decided that mortals were safer silent, and had loved them enough to do it anyway.

"I understand," she said.

Ulyana held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded, and looked back down at the broken wolf in her palm. And after a while, without either of them deciding to, she moved around the fire and sat beside Freya, shoulder to shoulder, and passed her the cracked stag-charm to hold.

They sat that way until the fire burned low.

It was Ivo, near midnight, who broke the silence — rising slowly from his bedroll, staring east with an expression Freya couldn't read until she followed his gaze and felt her stomach drop.

On the far horizon, against the stars, the land was glowing. Not with dawn. With *fire* — a single point of orange light, impossibly distant and impossibly steady, burning in the darkness like a lamp set in a window.

"A beacon," Ivo murmured. "Nobody lives out there. Nobody *can* live out there — that's the Ashfall Reach. Nothing burns out there. Nothing has burned out there in a hundred years."

Freya stared at the distant flame, and felt, faintly, across all those miles of dying country, a warmth she recognized. An ember that refused to die. A hearth that had burned down its own temples rather than go dark.

Someone out there in the frozen waste was keeping a light on for them.

"Torvald," she whispered. "He knows we're coming."
