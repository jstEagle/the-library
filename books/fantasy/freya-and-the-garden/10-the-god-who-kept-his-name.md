## 10. The God Who Kept His Name

I smelled them before I saw them — three travelers and a dead thing's worth of cold clinging to their coats, coming up the goat track through the ash pines with the Hollow Choir's song still caught in the wool of their hoods like burrs.

I had been tending my fire, as I have tended it for four hundred years, in the ruin of the last temple anyone ever built to me. The walls were black stone now, cracked open to the sky like a broken bowl, and the snow came down through the gap in fat, unhurried flakes that hissed when they drifted too near my hearth. That is the one law of my existence: nothing freezes within reach of my fire. It is not much of a divinity. It has had to be enough.

The tall one walked first — a mapmaker by his gait, all economy and watchfulness, one hand always near his satchel. Behind him came a woman who moved like weather, slow and vast, wearing a mortal body the way a river wears a bridge: patiently, and with the constant suggestion of something underneath that could take the bridge apart. I knew her before she cleared the treeline. You do not forget Freya. Even muted, even folded down into flesh and dirt under her fingernails, she carried the smell of green things breaking through ice.

But it was the third one who stopped my breath.

A small woman, dark-haired, frost-burned at the cheeks, carrying a bag of carved bones against her hip the way other people carry infants. And around her — I had to sit back on my heels and stare — around her hung the dead like moths around a lantern. Not ghosts, exactly. Not the pale echoes the Choir harvests and wears. These were *hers*: the quiet company of every soul whose bones had passed through her hands, riding her shoulders, murmuring at her ear, keeping her warm in a way my fire could not.

A mortal who heard the dead. A god who remembered herself. And between them, a man with a cartographer's guilt written all over his face.

"Well," I said to my fire, "they've finally done it. They've sent me a story instead of a pilgrim."

* * *

They came into the temple ruins because there was nowhere else. That is the truth of the high passes in early winter — you shelter where the walls are thickest, and my ruined temple had the thickest walls in the Ashfall Reach, because I had built them myself, in the years when mortals still climbed mountains to bring me offerings of bread and salt and first-cut firewood.

Freya saw me before the others did. Of course she did. To mortal eyes I am only an old man sitting by a fire in a roofless hall, broad-shouldered, grey-bearded, hands scarred from centuries of pulling brands out of flames bare-handed. But Freya looked at me the way you look at a scar on someone you love — recognition and grief in the same glance.

"Torvald," she said. "You're still burning."

"And you're still small." I rose and opened my arms, and to her credit she came into them, this goddess in her borrowed body, and let me hold her the way you hold a memory that has gotten thin from handling. "Look at you. Beans. They gave you *beans*."

"They gave me a garden," she said into my shoulder. "It was kinder than they knew."

The bone-carver watched us with the wariness of a creature deciding whether a clearing is safe. Ulyana Pemberton — I knew her name already; the dead had been chattering about her for weeks, a rustle of excitement moving through the quiet country below like wind through wheat. She stood just inside the doorway with her charm-bag clutched to her chest, and I watched her do the thing that made my old heart ache: she tilted her head, listening, and whatever the dead told her made her relax by degrees.

"The fire says you're welcome," she reported. "And that you haven't slept in eleven days."

"The fire talks too much," I said. "Come in. All of you. There's stew if the pot remembers how to make it, and bread if the mapmaker hasn't eaten it."

Ivo — for that was his name, though he flinched when I used it, which told me he had not yet confessed whatever debt was riding him — Ivo set out our food without being asked. A practical man. Guilty men often are; usefulness is the penance they can afford.

We ate while the storm found its rhythm against the old walls. And then, because I had waited four hundred years to say what I was about to say, and because hope is a thing that goes bad if you store it too long, I set down my bowl and told them about the night I burned my own name into the world.

* * *

"You have to understand what the muting felt like from inside," I said. "You've heard it described as silence. It wasn't. Silence would have been a mercy."

Ulyana sat closest to the fire, cross-legged, her bones spread on a cloth before her — she had been sorting them while we ate, an unconscious habit, the way I poke my coals. She looked up at that. "What was it, then?"

"Muffled," I said. "Imagine the most important conversation of your life happening in the next room, behind a door, forever. You can hear the shape of the words. You know someone is speaking. You press your ear to the wood until your ear bleeds, and you get — almost nothing. A syllable. A warmth. The gods were not banished from the world, girl. We were left *in* it, wrapped in wool, watching. Every prayer still reached us. None of us could answer."

Freya had gone very still. "I don't remember it that way."

"No," I said gently. "You wouldn't. You were bound deepest of any of us — they didn't mute you, little sister, they *buried* you, folded you down into soil and seed and the long patience of growing things. You got the kindest version of the sentence. The rest of us got the listening."

Ivo shifted. "The Hollow Choir. Are they —"

"The ones who went mad from it, yes." I fed a knot of pine into the fire and watched it catch, green-sap hissing, sweet-smelling. "Think about what we are. A god is not a mind, exactly. A god is an *answer*. We exist in the space between a plea and its reply. Take away the reply and you don't get a quiet god. You get a question eating itself. Some endured. Some slept. And some — the ones who loved their people too loudly, or were loved too loudly in return — some began answering anyway, with no voice to answer *with*, so they answered with weather. With rot. With frost in midsummer. They meant mercy. They delivered catastrophe. And when their worshippers fled or died, the Choir turned to the only prayers left — each other's. They sing requests at one another, out there in the white, and no one can grant them, and they have been doing it for four hundred years."

The bone-carver's hand had gone to her charm-bag. "The frozen faces," she said quietly. "In the pass. They wore faces like — like carvings left out in weather."

"Faces are the last thing to go," I said. "A god forgets his miracles before he forgets his face. Hold onto that. It matters later."

Freya spoke from the shadows near the wall, where she had drifted, the way she drifts, as if standing still were a discipline rather than a habit. "Torvald. How are you *whole*? The muting took everyone. I've spent a year digging myself out of a garden and I'm barely a shadow of —" Her voice caught, and I heard, underneath it, the vast sound trying to get through, the way a river sounds under ice.

"Because I cheated," I said.

I stood, and I let them see it — really see it, dropping the mortal mask for the length of a heartbeat, letting the firelight show them what I actually was: a hearth the size of a horizon, a warmth older than the valley, the first fire any of their ancestors ever dared carry indoors. Then I folded myself back down into the shape of a tired old man, because even I cannot hold my true size for long anymore, not alone, not unfed.

"I refused," I said. "That's all. When the elders cut the covenant, every god was given the choice — be muted and remain, or be cast out and fade. Most took the muting, because remaining mattered more than speaking. Freya's jailers buried her alive rather than risk her refusal. But I —" I laughed, and it came out like a log splitting. "I had already lost everything I cared about. My temples were full. My people were fat and safe and had begun leaving offerings out of habit rather than hunger. So when they came with their knives and their covenant-words, I said: no. And I burned my own temples down around their ears, every one, seven of them, so there would be nothing left to bind me *to*. No altar, no anchor, no stone with my name on it. A god with no temple cannot be muted in it."

"You burned your own worship," Ivo said slowly. "To keep your voice."

"I burned my own worship to keep my *self*," I corrected. "The voice was the self. And it cost me, boy, don't think it didn't. Four hundred years of hearing prayers and never once being able to answer them properly — I could warm a traveler, nudge a coal, keep a child's fever from cresting in the night. Small mercies. Firelight mercies. I have spent four centuries being a draft under a door." I looked at Ulyana. "Until now."

* * *

She met my eyes without flinching, which I liked about her immediately. Most mortals look away from a god, even a shabby one. She looked at me the way she must have looked at difficult bones — assessing the break, planning the mending.

"The dead told me the covenant was cut deliberately," she said. "By mortal hands. In Thornmere."

"They told you true."

"Can it be reforged?"

And there it was. The question I had waited four hundred years to hear, and the one I had dreaded, because hope with a price tag attached is crueler than despair — despair at least lets you stop walking.

I crouched by the fire and drew in the ash with a poker, because some truths want to be drawn, not spoken. Two shapes. A circle split down the middle.

"The covenant was not a wall," I said. "Your valley's elders think of it as a wall — magic outside, safety inside. Wrong. The covenant is a *bridge*. Two halves. One half anchored in the divine — the Worldroot, the first tree, the place where the gods were dreamed into being. The other half anchored in the mortal — in a voice, a living voice, capable of hearing what lies on the far side of death. When both halves stand, gods and mortals speak across the span, and the world stays in balance. Winter comes and winter goes. Prayers rise and are answered. That is the whole machinery of creation, girl, and it is *simple*, which is why breaking it was so easy and why mending it will not be."

I tapped the severed halves.

"When Marta's grandmother — yes, I know the name, the whole whispering country knows the name now that the dead have started talking again — when she led the cutting, they didn't destroy the bridge. You cannot destroy it. It is older than destruction. They *severed* it, and the halves fell, and they hang there still, beneath the roots of the first tree, swinging in the dark like a snapped rope. The Worldroot half waits. It has always waited. It is patient the way stone is patient."

"And the other half?" Freya asked. Though I think she already knew. I think she had known since the moment the bone-carver first spoke in her bean rows.

I looked at Ulyana Pemberton, at the bag of the dead on her hip, at the faint crowd of souls that gathered closer when she was afraid, the way sparrows gather on a wire before rain.

"A mortal voice that hears the dead," I said. "There may have been others, once, scattered here and there across the world — but the winter is killing them faster than it kills crops, and even if there were a hundred, it would not matter. Because the second condition is rarer than the first. The bridge needs a god who remembers her name. Not her title. Not what frightened farmers called her. Her *name* — the word she was before the world needed words. Do you understand how rare that is? The Choir forgot theirs generations ago. The muted gods are forgetting even now; I can feel them going out like lamps. And Freya —" I turned to her, and my voice gentled despite myself. "Freya spent four hundred years as soil. By every law of godhood, she should be a rumor by now. A feeling gardeners get. Instead she is sitting in my ruin eating my stew."

"Because Ulyana prayed to me," Freya said. "Not to a name. To *me*. To the woman in the beans."

"Because someone finally asked for the god and not the gift," I agreed. "Yes. That is the thread that kept you knotted to yourself. And that is why the two of you —" I gestured between them with the poker, scattering my ash-drawing. "— are not two conditions of a prophecy. You are one condition. The bridge needs both ends at once. A voice that hears the dead, holding the mortal half. A god who remembers, holding the divine half. Standing together at the Worldroot, speaking the reforge together, or not at all."

The fire popped. Snow hissed past the broken roof. For a long moment nobody spoke, and I let the silence do its work, because I am a hearth-god and I know the value of a held quiet.

Then Ulyana said, in the flat careful voice of a woman reading fine print: "What does it cost?"

Ah. There she was. The bone-carver, who dealt in remains, who knew better than anyone that everything finished leaves something behind.

"I don't know," I told her honestly. "No one does. The covenant has only been forged twice — once at the making of the world, and once after the Godswar, and neither time was there a witness left standing to write down the price. But I will tell you what my four hundred years of listening suggest." I leaned toward her, and I let a little of my true warmth come through, enough that she felt it on her face like an open oven door. "Bridges are built from sacrifice. Always. Something goes into the span and becomes part of it, permanently, so that the crossing holds. When the covenant was first forged, the price was paid by the gods — we gave up our wildness, our appetite, our right to walk the world uninvited. That was the old toll. A new covenant will need a new toll, and this time —" I shook my head. "This time I believe the mortal end must pay it. Whatever defines you most completely. Whatever you would least choose to lose. That is what bridges are made of."

Ulyana's hand rose, very slowly, and touched her own throat.

I saw it. So did Freya. Neither of us said anything, because it was not ours to say.

* * *

Later, when the others slept — Ivo curled by the fire like a man practicing for poverty, Ulyana breathing in the deep rhythm of the truly exhausted — Freya stayed awake, sitting beside my hearth, poking my coals with a stick as if she were twelve years old and we were both young gods arguing about the nature of dawn.

"She'll pay it," Freya said at last. "Whatever it is. She'll pay it without telling me, and she'll smile while she does, and I'll hate her for it."

"Yes," I said.

"I could refuse. I could turn us around. Take her back to the valley, let the world freeze politely, keep her whole."

"You could," I agreed. "And she'd hate you forever, and rightly. Listen to me, little sister." I put a hand on her shoulder, and felt the enormous sleeping thing underneath her skin stir at the contact, the first time in four centuries anything had touched the buried goddess directly. "I refused the muting and it cost me everything except myself. You accepted it and it cost you yourself — but you are getting yourself back, which is more than any of the rest of us will ever say. There is no path through this that costs nothing. There is only the choice of *what* you pay, and *who* pays it, and whether you pay it lying to each other or standing in the truth. She chose truth over loyalty once already — I can smell it on her, that particular grief. Don't make her choose again between honesty and kindness. Let her spend herself honestly. It is the only coin she respects."

Freya was silent a long moment. The storm screamed against the walls. Somewhere out in the white dark, faint as a memory of music, the Hollow Choir sang a prayer to no one.

"Torvald," she said quietly. "Come with us."

I looked into my fire. Four hundred years I had tended it, and it had never once gone out, and I had told myself that was devotion. Sitting there, with a goddess asking me to leave, I understood at last that it had been fear. A god with no temple and no courage is only a hermit with delusions.

"The road to the Worldroot passes through the Choir's country," I said. "And the descent beneath the roots — no fire of mine has ever burned down there. I would be walking into the dark as nothing but an old man."

"Yes," Freya said. "So would I, mostly. That's rather the point of us."

I laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startling the snow off the rafters. Then I stood, and took up the iron poker I had carried since the burning of my seventh temple, and pushed the coals of my four-hundred-year fire apart, letting it gutter low for the first time in living memory.

"Give me until dawn," I said. "A hearth-god should leave a warm room behind him. It's the only blessing I have left that works."

And that was how we might have left, come morning — three travelers and a god, walking east into the teeth of the winter —

If the dead had not chosen that exact hour to start screaming.

Ulyana sat bolt upright from sleep with her hands pressed to her ears, though her ears were not the problem, and her voice came out cracked and terrible in the dark:

"They've found the valley. Something's crossed the boundary stones. The dead are running — oh gods, the dead are *running*, and they're all calling the same name —"

She looked at Freya.

"They're calling yours."
