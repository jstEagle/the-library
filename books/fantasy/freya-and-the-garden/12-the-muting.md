## 12. The Muting

The skull had no name that anyone living remembered. Ulyana called her Grandmother Ash, because the bone had gone the color of old hearth-sweep, and because when Ulyana held her palms against the cranium's cool curve she felt something like the warmth a fire leaves behind in a room long after it has gone out.

She had not meant to go this deep. She had meant only to ask — as she had asked a hundred skulls before this one, in a workshop that smelled of tallow and juniper smoke — *who were you, and what did you love?* The dead answered such questions gladly. They were starved for asking.

But Grandmother Ash was old. Older than the boundary stones, older than Thornmere's founding charter with its careful ink and its careful omissions. And when Ulyana pressed her thumbs into the hollows behind the ears and let herself fall down through the dark water of the dead's remembering, the skull did not offer her a life. It offered her an era.

*Hold on,* said the voice of the bone, which was not a voice at all but a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like iron and rain. *This is heavy. This is heavier than you know.*

"I can carry it," Ulyana said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the hanging bundles of yarrow and the little brass bell that never rang because there was nothing in the valley for it to warn against. "I have to."

And she let go of the surface, and sank.

* * *

She came up standing in a summer that did not exist anymore.

It took her a moment to understand that she was not *there* — that her feet made no prints in the dust of the road, that the people streaming past her toward the village green could not see the woman standing among them in her own body, wearing her own face, dressed in the plain grey wool of a Thornmere bone-carver. She was a memory inside a memory. A ghost watching ghosts.

But oh, the valley knew her. That was the terrible thing. The hills were the same hills. The river ran the same silver bend past the same alder roots. The chapel stood where it stood now, though its roof was thatch instead of slate, and the market stalls leaned at the same tired angles, and somewhere a dog barked in a way that made her chest ache with recognition, because dogs did not change, only their owners' names did.

Except — she looked closer — everything was *wrong*, too. Wrong the way a room is wrong when the furniture has been rearranged. There were boundary stones here, but they were few, and small, and half-swallowed by grass: markers of property, not of exile. Farmers walked out past them without pausing, without the faint nausea Ulyana had always felt when she so much as leaned against one. A boy chased a hoop all the way to the treeline and back, laughing, and nothing stopped him, and nothing in him was stopped.

A world where magic moved freely. A world where the gods were close enough to touch.

She saw it then, on the green: a shrine. Not the overgrown ruin on her grandmother's land where Freya slept in her garden — a whole row of shrines, seven of them, each one tended, each one hung with offerings. Bread and salt. Ribbons. Little carved figures that made Ulyana's hands itch with professional envy. People knelt at them between errands, the way one might pause to greet a neighbor over a fence. Casual. Unafraid. A woman left a cup of milk at the smallest shrine and patted its stone the way you'd pat a cow's flank, and walked on.

Ulyana stood in the middle of the remembered green and felt her throat close. This was what had been taken from them. Not magic — she had never missed magic; she had her gift, and her gift was enough. This. The *nearness*. The sense that the world was inhabited all the way down.

Then she heard the coughing.

It started at the edges of the crowd, a dry ratcheting sound, and spread inward like wind through wheat. She turned, and saw them coming up the road from the lowlands: carts, and families walking beside the carts, and children too still in their mothers' arms. Their faces were grey. Their lips were cracked and dark. When they coughed they folded forward as if bowing to something invisible, and some of them did not straighten again.

The plague. Of course. Every founding story had its flood or its fire or its famine, and Thornmere's charter mentioned a sickness in the most glancing of terms — *a season of hardship, weathered together.* Weathered together. Ulyana watched the healthy villagers shrink back from the carts, watched mothers pull their children close, watched the seven shrines fill up with desperate offerings within the hour — bread, salt, ribbons, a wedding ring, a whole lamb dragged bleating to the altar stone — and she understood that she was not here to see the sickness.

She was here to see the answer.

* * *

The gods came at dusk.

Ulyana felt them arrive before she saw anything, a pressure change like the moment before a storm breaks, and every hair on her arms rose, and the dead-memory around her seemed to hold its breath. Then light gathered above the largest shrine — not descending, exactly, but *condensing*, the way dew condenses, drawn out of the air itself — and a figure stood there, tall and wrong-angled and beautiful in the way that mountains are beautiful, which is to say without any interest whatsoever in human comfort.

She could not look directly at it. Its face kept sliding away from her attention like water off oiled cloth. But she heard it, and the hearing went through her bones, through the bones she was not currently wearing, and she understood why the dead spoke of the gods the way drowning survivors speak of the sea.

*"We hear you,"* the god said, and its voice was kind, which somehow made it worse. *"We have weighed your prayers. We have counted your lambs and your tears."* A pause, vast and gentle. *"The sickness is not ours to cure. It is woven into your flesh, as winter is woven into ours. To unweave it we must unweave you. We will not do this. Grieve, and be healed by grieving. Bury your dead, and be bound by burying them. This is the covenant: we walk beside you, but we do not carry you."*

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats.

Then a woman stepped out of the crowd, and Ulyana's whole body went cold, because she knew that face. She had known it her entire life, softened by age into kindness, framed by white braids, smiling at her across a kitchen table while Marta's grandmother pressed honey-cakes into her hands and told her she was the daughter the Fletchers never had.

Sera Fletcher was perhaps thirty years old in this memory. Her hair was dark. Her hands were steady. And her face, as she turned to look at the god of the harvest-shrine, held no grief at all.

"Then what good are you?" Sera asked.

The crowd gasped. The god's light flickered — not in anger, Ulyana thought. In *weariness*. As if it had been asked this question in a thousand valleys by a thousand dying summers, and had never once found an answer that satisfied.

*"We are good for remembrance,"* the god said. *"For the naming of things. For the long patience—"*

"My daughter is dying." Sera's voice did not rise. That was the horror of it. It stayed level, reasonable, a craftsman's voice, a carver's voice, and Ulyana recognized the tone because it was the tone she used herself when she worked. "My daughter is six years old, and she asked me this morning whether she would see the snow, and I could not answer her, because you will not answer me. You give us patience. Patience is what you give people when you intend to watch them suffer."

*"Mortal—"*

"No." Sera raised a hand, and Ulyana saw that other elders had moved to stand with her — she recognized the surnames later, when the memory showed her faces: Halloway, Voss, Pemberton. Her own blood. Her own name, standing in that ring of decision. "No more. You've had our lambs and our daughters and our patience. Tonight we take back what's ours."

"Sera," said an old man at the edge of the elder-ring — and his voice cracked with fear, real fear, the first honest emotion Ulyana had seen since the plague carts arrived. "Child, you don't know what you're asking. Cut the covenant and the gods don't just leave us. They *forget* us. Everything they are, everything they remember of us—"

"They're forgetting my daughter right now," Sera said. "They're forgetting her while she drowns in her own lungs. Let them forget the rest of us too. At least then it'll be equal."

And the elders closed the circle, and began to cut.

* * *

Ulyana tried to look away. She discovered that in a memory, looking away was a courtesy the memory did not extend.

They used a song. That was the thing no one had ever told her, the thing the charter buried under *a season of hardship*: the covenant was not a wall but a *word*, a shared word spoken continuously between gods and mortals since the first hearth was lit, renewed with every prayer and every answer, and to sever it required not a blade but a counter-song, sung by mortal throats, sustained until the word frayed through.

Sera Fletcher led the singing. She had a good voice. That was the detail that would follow Ulyana out of the vision and into her sleep for the rest of her life: Sera Fletcher had a *good* voice, clear and true, the kind of voice that should have been raised in praise, and she sang the unmaking of heaven with it while her fellow elders held the harmony, and the seven shrines went dark one by one like lamps being snuffed down a corridor.

The gods fought it. She felt that too — felt the whole valley shudder as divine attention swung toward them, vast and bewildered, the gaze of things that had never been disobeyed by the small creatures they loved. Light broke over the green like waves. Somewhere a child screamed. The plague carts rocked on their axles.

And the gods — the gods *stopped*.

Not because they were defeated. Because they understood. Ulyana saw it in the last shrine's fading light, saw the harvest-god's face resolve for one single instant into something she could bear to look at, and what was on it was not rage. It was the expression of a parent who has caught a child doing something unforgivable and loves the child anyway and does not know what to do with either fact.

*"You are cutting yourselves off from us to save your children,"* the god said, and its voice was breaking, actually breaking, splintering like ice on a thawing river. *"Do you know what you become, without us? Do you know what we become, without you? We are each other's mirrors. We are each other's *names*—"*

"You'll live," Sera said. "So will they. Isn't that what you wanted? For us to endure?"

And she sang the last verse, and the god's mouth kept moving after the sound died, shaping words no one would ever hear, and the light went out, and the valley went silent in a way that Ulyana had lived inside her whole life without ever once questioning — that flat, safe, muffled quiet, the quiet of a house where something has been smothered.

The boundary stones rose that night. She watched them come up out of the earth like teeth, watched the elders walk the perimeter laying hands on them, watched Sera Fletcher kneel at the last stone and press her palm flat against it and whisper something too low for even a ghost to catch.

Then the memory turned, the way memories do, and showed Ulyana the thing she had come to see, the thing she had dreaded without knowing its shape.

Sera went home. Sera sat by her daughter's bed. The girl was grey-lipped and burning, and Sera held her hand through the night, and in the morning the fever broke.

It broke in other beds too. Not all of them. Not most of them. But in enough — in a handful scattered through the valley like seeds flung from a torn pocket — and Ulyana stood in the remembered sickroom and understood, with a lurch like the floor dropping away, that the gods had not refused to cure the plague out of indifference.

They had refused because the plague was already *loosed* — because curing it wholesale would have meant unmaking the weave of flesh and season, as the god had said — but it could be *eased*, here and there, life by life, the slow way, the covenant way, the way of walking beside rather than carrying. And Sera Fletcher had known that. Had known that if she waited, if she begged night after night at the shrines like everyone else, her daughter might live and might not.

And so she had traded heaven for a coin toss. Had muted every god in the world, had orphaned every worshipper beyond the stones, had built a homeland on a lie and a boundary out of fear — for the sake of one six-year-old girl who grew up, and married, and bore children, and whose grandchildren included a loud, loyal, infuriating, beloved woman named Marta Fletcher, who baked bread for the widow down the lane and defended the valley's honor with her fists and her whole heart, and who had never once been told what her grandmother's clear, true, beautiful voice had done to the sky.

* * *

Ulyana came back to herself on the workshop floor, on her knees, with her arms wrapped around Grandmother Ash's skull and tears running freely down her face and into the grain of the old bone.

The juniper smoke had burned down to nothing. Through the window, the light had gone the color of weak tea. How long she had been under she could not say — an hour, an evening, a season. Her knees ached. Her throat ached worse.

"It wasn't malice," she said to the skull, because saying it aloud was the only way to make it real, and making it real was the only way to survive it. "That's the worst part. It wasn't cruelty. It was a mother with a sick child and a god who told her to grieve." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "I'd have done it. Don't you see? If it were Freya lying in that bed — if someone told me *grieve, and be healed by grieving* — I'd have sung the song myself. I'd have sung it louder than Sera Fletcher ever did."

The skull said nothing. The dead were generous, but they were not comforting; they simply held what they held, and let you take it or leave it.

She thought of Marta's face when Ulyana had asked about the founding families. The evasiveness. The flush. *My gran always said the stones keep us safe, and that's the end of it.* Marta didn't know. Ulyana was almost certain Marta didn't know — and almost was a knife's width wide, because Marta's family had kept this secret for four generations, and secrets that old require tending, and tending requires tellers.

And Marta had grown up on honey-cakes and stories in Sera Fletcher's kitchen, same as Ulyana. Marta loved this valley with the fierce, unquestioning love of a dog for its yard. What would truth do to her? What would it cost her to learn that the safety she'd sworn by was a wound dressed up as a wall?

What would it cost Ulyana to tell her?

She set the skull down gently on its shelf of folded wool, the way she set down every bone in her care, and stood, and her legs shook, and she let them.

Outside, the evening air had a wrongness in it. She noticed it the way you notice a note played flat in a familiar hymn: frost on the garden gate in high summer, ferns of ice feathering the wood, and beyond the eastern ridge — beyond the boundary stones — the peaks wearing new snow that no midsummer sky should have permitted. The winter was not waiting politely beyond the walls anymore. The covenant was dying everywhere at once, and every day they spent deciding, deciding, *deciding*, the cold chewed further in.

Freya was waiting for her at the edge of the bean rows, a pale shape in the dusk, and Ulyana walked to her because there was nowhere else in the world her feet wanted to go.

"You saw it," Freya said. Not a question. The warden's face was unreadable in the failing light, but her hands — her gardener's hands, dirt under the nails, a goddess's hands pretending very hard to be ordinary — were clenched at her sides. "You went down through the oldest bone you had, and you saw it. I can tell because you're looking at me the way people look at a grave."

"I saw my valley founded on a lie," Ulyana said. "I saw your brothers and sisters go out like lamps. I saw the woman who did it, and she was kind to me my whole childhood, and she did it *for love*, Freya, and I don't know what to do with that. I don't know how to hate her and I don't know how to forgive her and I don't know—" Her voice cracked. "Marta doesn't know. And tomorrow I have to decide whether she learns it from me or from the winter."

Freya reached out and took her hand. Her grip was warm, and human-warm was somehow the most divine thing about her.

"Then rest tonight," the goddess said quietly. "Because whatever you choose, we cross the stones at first light. Torvald's ember won't wait, and neither will the ice."

Beyond the ridge, thunder rolled — or something that was not quite thunder — and the first flakes of the out-of-season snow came drifting down through the summer dark, settling on the bean rows, on the boundary stones, on the whole lying, beloved, freezing valley, like ash.
