## 13. Winter's Teeth

Ivo had mapped the Ashfall Reach twice in his life — once in spring, when it was a green shelf of high meadow stitched with streams, and once in autumn, when the heather went rust-colored and the wind carried the smell of distant peat fires. Neither map would serve now. The Reach had become a country he did not recognize, and a cartographer who could not recognize a country was a man navigating by faith alone.

He knelt at the edge of their camp and breathed on his hands. His breath came out as smoke that fell instead of rose. That was wrong. He had noticed it three days ago and said nothing, because there are some wrongnesses that grow larger when named aloud.

Behind him, Ulyana slept sitting up against her pack, the charm-bag cradled in her lap like a child she refused to put down even unconscious. Freya stood watch at the camp's edge, and Ivo had stopped trying to understand how the woman did not shiver. She stood in the wind the way a standing stone stands in wind — not resisting it so much as being older than it. Marta slept too, curled small, her family crest ring turned inward on her finger where she thought no one saw it. Ivo saw everything. It was his trade. He had spent thirty years reading coastlines and river bends for the truth they tried to hide, and people were only landscapes that walked.

The truth Marta's landscape was hiding was going to get them all killed, or save them, or both. He had not decided which yet.

He unrolled his maps across his knees anyway, out of habit, and looked at the ink lines of the Ashfall Reach — the neat blue threads of the Sorrowmere tributaries, the dotted trail over the saddle between the Two Sisters peaks. All fiction now. The streams were frozen to their beds. The trail was buried under snow that had no business falling in what should have been late summer's last green week.

*The winter is accelerating,* Freya had said two nights ago, in the flat voice she used for truths too large for emphasis. *It is not weather. Weather has patience.*

Ivo folded the maps away. Some instruments failed not because they were broken but because the world had stopped agreeing to be measured.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, if dawn could be said to exist — the sky was a bruised grey lid pressing down, the sun a pale coin behind wool. They moved in single file along the frozen spine of the old drover's road, Ivo leading, then Ulyana, then Marta, with Freya at the rear like a lighthouse walking.

By midmorning the wind arrived. Not a gust — an arrival, a presence, something with intent. It came down off the northern peaks in a white wall and hit them sideways, and Ivo learned in the first ten seconds why the old drovers' songs called this place the Reach's Teeth. The wind found every gap in clothing, every seam, every weakness of flesh, and worried at them like a dog with a bone. Within an hour his face had gone past pain into a strange burning numbness, and he knew from hard experience that burning numbness was the body's way of lying to you before it started killing parts of you.

"We need shelter!" he shouted back, and watched the words tear away into nothing. Ulyana read his lips — he'd taught her that trick on the second day, without knowing why it would matter — and pointed east, toward a dark shape in the whiteout that might have been rock or might have been wishful thinking.

It was rock. An old drover's waystation, roof half-collapsed, walls of stacked stone furred with ice. They crowded inside and Freya, who had explained many times that her divinity was a memory rather than a power, nevertheless did something with her hands and her voice — a low hum, almost a song — and the cold in the waystation drew back a few feet, sulking, like a wolf deciding whether the fire was worth the risk.

"It won't hold long," Freya said. "An hour. Maybe two."

"Then we rest two hours," Ivo said, "and we talk about what I've been counting."

Ulyana unwound her scarf, her lips blue, her eyes sharp. "Counting?"

"Signs." He held up fingers, and was disturbed to see how badly two of them wanted to stay curled. "One: my breath falls instead of rising. That started three days ago. Air that cold doesn't happen on earth — it happens when the air itself is forgetting how air works. Two: the stars. Last night I checked our position by them, out of habit." He paused. "There were fewer of them. Not clouded. *Fewer.* As if the sky is coming apart at the edges and the far stars have gone first."

Marta pulled her cloak tighter. "You're saying the world is unraveling."

"I'm saying I'm a mapmaker, and the map and the territory have stopped matching, and in my experience one of two things is true when that happens. Either the mapmaker is mad—" he tapped his own temple, "—or the territory is dying."

Silence, except the wind worrying at the stones like a creditor at a door.

"The covenant," Ulyana said quietly. She had the charm-bag open in her lap, checking each carved piece with her thumbs, the way other people counted coins. "Freya told us. The realms are bound together through it. If it's dying—"

"Then both realms die together," Freya finished. Her face in the dim light was calm in a way that frightened Ivo more than panic would have. Panic believes something can still be done. Calm like hers had already done its arithmetic. "The gods fade, and the mortal world loses its shape. Winter isn't coming here. Winter is what unraveling looks like from inside."

Ivo looked at his hands again. At the two fingers that wouldn't straighten. He thought about the debt he owed Ulyana's mother — a debt paid once in maps and once in silence, and now being paid a third time in frost — and he thought, with the peculiar clarity of cold, *I will finish this road or leave my hands on it.*

He didn't say it aloud. Sacrifice spoken aloud becomes performance, and performance is a kind of lie, and he had lied enough in his life to know the taste.

* * *

They moved again when the wind eased, and the afternoon gave them the worst hours of the crossing.

The Ashfall Reach in summer was a plateau. In winter it became a sea that had forgotten how to be water — dunes of snow sculpted into shapes that hurt to look at, all wrong angles and impossible curves, drifts taller than a man rising and vanishing like the breathing of something enormous asleep beneath them. Twice the crust broke under Marta and Ivo hauled her out by the arms while she gasped apologies that no one accepted because no one blamed her. The cold didn't allow blame. Cold only allowed the next step.

It was during the third hour of the afternoon march that the charm-bag strap snapped.

Later, Ivo would replay it a hundred times and never make it slower. One moment Ulyana was walking ahead of him, the bag slung across her body; the next, the worn leather — weakened, he supposed, by weeks of weather it was never made for — parted with a sound too small to hear over the wind, and the bag swung free, hit a crust of ice, and began to slide.

Down the slope. Toward the crevasse field.

He didn't think. Thinking was for afterward. His body simply understood, in the wordless way bodies understand things, that the woman ahead of him had given up her hearing for the dead, was planning to give up whatever remained of herself at the Worldroot, and that the bag held every charm she had carved since leaving Thornmere — including the ones Freya had used to drive off the Hollow Choir, including the ones that were, as far as any of them knew, the only weapons in the world that could hold a god's name.

He dove.

The slope took him the way a river takes a leaf. He slid, rolled, felt the world become white and spinning, and caught the strap with his left hand just as the ground opened beside him — a crevasse, blue-black, breathing cold up out of the deep places of the world like the throat of something buried alive. He swung, slammed shoulder-first into the ice lip, and hung there with the bag in his fist and his legs dangling over a darkness that had no bottom he could see.

The cold found his hand immediately. It was not like cold on skin. It was like cold *replacing* skin, moving inward joint by joint, patient and thorough, an accountant liquidating him finger by finger.

*Ivo!* Ulyana's voice, thin and shredded by wind. Then Freya's shadow blotted out the pale sky above him, and her hand closed around his wrist, and her grip was impossible — warm, iron, absolutely certain — and she drew him up out of the dark the way you pull a nail from wood, one smooth inexorable motion, until he lay gasping on the crust with the charm-bag crushed against his chest.

His left hand would not open.

"Let go," Freya said gently.

"I can't."

"You're holding it too tightly. Let go, Ivo."

He understood then that she didn't mean the bag. He let go of everything — the fear, the arithmetic of debts, the careful distance he kept between himself and other people's lives — and sobbed once, a short ugly sound the wind stole, and Ulyana pried the bag from his fist with fingers gone gentle and expert, the way she handled bone.

His hand came free of the ice with a sound like tearing linen.

* * *

The waystation they found at dusk was better than the first — a shepherd's bothy, whole-roofed, with a hearth and half a stack of old cut peat. Freya lit it, and the fire was the first honest thing Ivo had seen in days.

Ulyana took his hand without asking. She was like that. She turned it palm-up in the firelight and went very still.

The little finger and the one beside it had gone the color of old candle wax, waxy-white shot through with faint grey, and they did not hurt at all, which was the worst thing about them. Pain means the flesh is still arguing. This flesh had conceded.

"How bad," Ivo said. Not a question. He'd seen frostbite in the northern passes. He knew the taxonomy.

"They'll blister, maybe blacken." Ulyana's voice was steady, but her thumb moved over his knuckles in small circles, as if warmth could be persuaded downward by affection. "You may lose the tips. Maybe to the first joint. We can't thaw them fast — fast kills them sure. Slow warmth, tonight and tomorrow."

"Marta," Ivo said. "Your kit. Willow bark, if you have it. And that spirit you drink and pretend you don't."

Marta was already moving, already digging, and Ivo watched her and thought: *there is the woman who betrayed us, kneeling to fetch medicine for the man she paid to betray us.* He had decided somewhere on the ice that he was not going to forgive her, and also that he was not going to stop traveling beside her, and he had discovered these two positions could coexist in a man without splitting him down the middle. Loyalty and truth, he was learning, were not the same animal. They only looked alike from a distance, the way a wolf and a dog do.

Freya crouched across the fire from him. "Why?" she asked. Just that. Gods, he supposed, got to ask questions like knives.

"Why what?"

"You could have died. For a bag of carved bone."

Ivo looked at his ruined hand in his lap, at the wax-colored fingers he was already beginning to mourn the way you mourn the living — preemptively, dishonestly, hoping to be proven wrong.

"I've spent my whole life drawing lines on paper," he said. "Borders. Roads. Coastlines. Lines that tell you where one thing ends and another begins. And I've watched men kill each other over those lines, and I've drawn lines myself that got people killed, and I'll tell you what I've learned, goddess." He raised his eyes to hers. "There's exactly one line worth anything. It's the line between the thing you'll sacrifice for and the thing you won't. Everything else is decoration." He nodded at the charm-bag, sitting safe against Ulyana's side. "That bag is on the far side of my line. So am I, come to think of it. So is she." A tilt of his head toward Ulyana. "So, apparently, is Marta, though the gods alone know how."

Freya studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or grief, or the memory of having once been worshipped by people exactly like him.

"The old poets had a word," she said finally. "*þrek.* Endurance that is also devotion. When a thing is suffered not because it must be but because of whom it is suffered for." She reached across the fire and touched his damaged hand, feather-light. "You are doing þrek, cartographer. I want you to know it is seen. Being seen is the only worship I have left to give."

Ivo found, to his considerable embarrassment, that his throat had closed. He covered it by drinking Marta's spirit, which burned all the way down and pooled in his chest like a second, smaller fire.

* * *

He woke in the black heart of the night to Ulyana sitting upright by the dead hearth, coals banked, her head tilted in the listening posture he had come to know — chin lifted slightly, eyes unfocused, the posture of a woman hearing a room full of people no one else could perceive.

Except.

He had watched her listen for weeks. This was different. Her face was wrong. Not strained — *frightened*, and Ulyana Pemberton did not frighten easily, not at the Hollow Choir, not at the crevasse, not at any horror the road had offered.

"What is it?" he whispered.

Her eyes focused on him slowly, as if surfacing from deep water.

"The voices," she said. Her whisper had a crack in it. "They're changing. The dead always speak to me in the language they died in — that's how it's always been, every ghost, every skull, every grave. But tonight—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her ear, hard, as though something were leaking in. "Tonight they're all speaking the same language. Every one of them. Dead Northerners, dead Southerners, dead children, dead kings. One language. One voice, almost. And Ivo—"

She looked at him, and in the ember-light her face was young and ancient at once, the way Freya's was.

"They're not talking to me anymore. They're talking past me. To something under the Reach. And they keep repeating the same words, over and over, like a warning, like a prayer, like a name." She swallowed. "They're saying *the roots remember*. And underneath that, getting louder every hour — they're saying *she is waking up*."

Outside, the wind stopped.

Not eased. Stopped — all at once, completely, the way a heart stops, and in the ringing silence Ivo heard something he had never heard in thirty years of travel: the Ashfall Reach, groaning. Not the ordinary complaint of settling ice. A long, tectonic exhalation, rolling up from miles below the frozen plateau, from whatever lay beneath the roots of the first tree.

From the direction of the Worldroot.

Freya appeared in the doorway of the bothy, silhouetted against a sky that had cleared to reveal a hole where stars used to be, and her voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"She's not waking up," the forgotten goddess said. "She's been awake for some time. And she knows we're coming."
