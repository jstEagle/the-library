## 14. The City of Answered Prayers

Freya had forgotten how cities smelled.

Not the filth of them — she had been a warden of gardens long enough to know that living things piled close together made their own honest stink, compost and smoke and bodies working. No, what she had forgotten was the smell of *wanting*. Vaskr announced itself three miles before its walls did, on a wind that came off the frozen plain carrying incense, tallow, burnt feathers, and beneath it all something sweeter and worse: the scent of ten thousand prayers being spoken at once, like bread baking in an oven that had no business being lit in this cold.

She stopped on the ridge and looked down at it, and her hands — mortal hands, chapped at the knuckles, dirt worked so deep into the creases that Ulyana said she'd never be clean again — curled into fists without her permission.

The city sat in a bowl of ice like a coal that refused to die. Its walls were old, older than the valley, older perhaps than her silence, black stone furred with frost. But it was not the walls that made her chest ache. It was the lights. Every rooftop burned something — lanterns, braziers, pyres built on garden sheds and cart beds — and every light marked a shrine. She could see them from the ridge, hundreds of them, little islands of flame, and around each one huddled figures in winter cloaks, swaying, singing, begging.

"They've multiplied," Ivo said beside her. He spoke carefully now, around the stubs of his missing fingers, his left hand bound in wool and wax that Ulyana changed each morning with the tenderness she usually reserved for bone. "When I mapped this road two years ago, Vaskr had maybe forty shrines. Old ones. Established." He shook his head. "Now look."

Freya looked. She could not stop looking.

*They are making gods,* she thought. *Out of panic. Out of want. They are doing what mortals have always done, only faster now, because they are afraid, and fear is the richest soil there is.*

"Come on," Ulyana said quietly. Her hand found Freya's shoulder — she had learned that about her, that Freya did not always like being touched on the hands, that the shoulder was safer, more human. "Cold's coming down off the Reach. We need walls before dark."

Marta said nothing. She had said very little since the Ashfall Reach, since Ivo's confession and the seal on the letter and everything that had cracked open between her and Ulyana like river ice. She walked at the back of their little line of four, wrapped in a borrowed coat, watching Vaskr come closer with the expression of a woman being led toward her own trial.

They went down.

* * *

The gate took two hours to reach and another hour to pass through, because everyone in the frozen world, it seemed, was trying to get inside. The road was choked with carts and sledges and people walking in family chains, holding ropes tied around each other's waists so no one would wander off into the white and lie down. Freya saw a man pulling a sledge stacked with house doors — just doors, pried from frames, as if a door alone might keep the winter out. She saw a woman carrying a goat. The goat wore mittens.

Inside the walls, the cold was different. Not gentler — nothing in this world was gentle anymore — but *busier*, full of breath and woodsmoke and the press of bodies, and Freya realized with a start that she had missed crowds. That some deep root of her remembered market days from before the covenant, before the muting, before she had been folded down small into a garden warden's body with beans to pick and slugs to drown, and those memories rose in her now like bubbles through dark water.

Then she heard what the crowd was praying to, and the warmth went out of her.

"—Saint Ferrous of the Boiling Kettle, who keeps water hot against all reason—"

"—Mother Nine-Fingers, who counts what is lost—"

"—The God of the Third Step, who holds the stair when the ice takes the first two—"

She stopped in the middle of the street. A woman jostled past her with a brazier on a pole, and Freya barely noticed.

Third Step. Nine-Fingers. Boiling Kettle.

These were not gods. These were *wishes*, grown legs. Somewhere in the last weeks, as the winter accelerated and the old names stayed silent, the desperate people of the north had begun praying to whatever would listen — and something had started listening. Something was *answering*, quickly, literally, hungrily, and every answered prayer fed its god until the god stood up out of the wish like a figure rising out of a pond, dripping, half-formed, wearing whatever shape the pray-ers had imagined for it.

She had seen one already, on the road. A village shrine to a god called Only-Warm-Hands, carved from a fence post by someone with a knife and grief, and the thing that lived in it had smiled at her with a mouth that was almost right. Almost. The proportions were wrong the way a dream is wrong. It had said, *You smell like before,* and she had walked faster.

"Freya." Ulyana's voice, low, at her elbow. "You've gone white. Whiter."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. You look like you've seen—" Ulyana paused, chose her words with the precision she brought to everything, even carving. "Like you've seen your own funeral."

Freya laughed. It came out wrong, a dry click.

"Close enough," she said. "Come on. There's an inn Ivo knows. I need to sit down before I fall down."

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, and it was full to bursting with refugees, map-sellers, and — this was new — *theologians*. Freya counted four separate arguments happening within earshot, all of them about the same question: were the new gods real, or were they thieves?

"The old ones are dead," declared a gaunt man with ink-stained cuffs, standing on a bench. "Dead or fled. What answers prayers now is something else. Something local. Something *hungry*."

"And what would you know of hunger," snapped a broad woman whose forearms were scarred with burn marks — a shrine-tender, Freya guessed, one of the new order. "The Lady of the Thawed Pipe kept my whole street's water running through the worst week of the Reach. Is that hunger? Is that a thief?"

"It's a *parasite* wearing your gratitude for a coat—"

Ulyana steered them to a corner table by the fire, and they ate what passed for stew — mostly barley, one heroic carrot sliced thin enough to serve eight — while the argument raged on around them. Ivo spread his remaining maps on the table despite the grease, because he was Ivo, and traced their route south with his nine fingers.

"Worldroot's a month away if the passes hold. Less if Torvald's ember does what he promised." He glanced at the little clay lamp at the center of the table, where a single coal glowed with a heat that had nothing to do with fire. It had not gone out since the hearth-god had pressed it into Ulyana's charm-bag and told them, *Follow the warm.* "But we can't buy passage or provisions without coin, and we can't earn coin without work, and nobody hires a deaf-to-the-dead bone-carver and a bean-witch in a city like this without asking questions."

"I'm not deaf to the dead," Ulyana murmured. "Just muffled. Like shouting through a wall." She flexed her fingers over her charm-bag, and Freya watched her do the thing she did a dozen times a day now: listen, strain, catch fragments. Her face tightened. "There are so many dead here. Under the streets. This city is old, and it killed a lot of people building itself, and they're all talking at once and I can't make out a single word. It's like standing in a bell tower during a wedding."

"Charming," said Marta, from the edge of the group. It was the first thing she'd said in hours. Her voice was flat, careful, a bridge walked on tiptoe. "And the living? What are they saying?"

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. Whatever was between them — the broken thing, the mended-in-progress thing — flickered across her face like weather.

"The living are saying the same thing everyone's saying," she said finally. "*Why won't the old gods answer.* And underneath that: *what did we do wrong.*"

Freya set down her spoon.

What had they done wrong. Oh, there was a prayer she could answer, if anyone thought to ask her. They had cut a covenant to hide a plague. They had chosen comfortable silence over hard truth. They had built a valley on a lie and called it sanctuary, and now the whole world was paying the interest on that loan, and the payment schedule was written in frost.

But she said none of it. She was tired, and Marta was sitting right there, and some wounds you don't salt in public.

Instead she asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the ridge.

"Who's answering them?" she said quietly. "The new gods. Who's answering?"

Ivo shrugged. "Priests say the gods themselves. Skeptics say con men. My mother used to say that every prayer gets answered eventually — either by a god or by a consequence."

"No." Freya shook her head slowly. "Prayers don't just get answered. Someone has to *pick up*. And the old pantheon is muted — I'd feel it if one of my kin slipped loose, even a small one, even feral. This is something else." She stared into the fire, and the fire stared back with the particular blankness of ordinary flame, which after everything was almost comforting. "It's the wishes themselves. Enough wanting, pressed together in the dark, starts to *stand up*. Mortals have always made gods the way bees make honey. It's just that usually it takes centuries. Usually the wish has time to become worthy of worship first."

"And now?" Ulyana asked.

"Now they're making them overnight. Out of terror. And terror makes *terrible* gods."

* * *

She dreamed badly that night, in the inn's attic room, wedged between Ulyana's warmth and a wall that breathed frost.

She dreamed she was standing in a great hall of ice, and the hall was lined with statues, and every statue was her.

Not quite her. That was the horror of it. Each one had gotten something right and everything else wrong. One had her hands — the gardener's hands, the strong blunt fingers — but a face like a fish, because someone had once prayed to *the lady of the river garden* and the wish had taken *river* and run with it. One had her face exactly, her true face from before, but stood ankle-deep in skulls, smiling, because somewhere someone had begged a goddess of gardens to *take* the blight, and take it she had, and the prayer had remembered the taking and forgotten the mercy. One statue was only a pair of boots and a watering can, and yet Freya knew, with sick certainty, that somewhere a congregation loved it dearly.

In the dream, worshippers moved among the statues, lighting candles, and each candle whispered a name for her that was not her name.

*Green Mother. Frost-Breaker. She-Who-Takes. The Bean Saint. Keeper of Small Growing Things. The Kindly Rot.*

None of them were Freya. None of them were the name her mother-god had spoken over her when the world was young and everything grew wild and no one had yet thought to build a wall between the divine and the dirt. And the dream-statues drank the candles' light and grew *more solid*, more fixed, more *certain*, while the true memory of herself — the feeling of rain on divine skin, of being vast and green and uncontained — grew fainter with every wrong name, like a photograph left too long in the sun.

She woke gasping in the dark, heart slamming, and for three full seconds she could not remember her own name.

Three seconds. An eternity. In those seconds she was nothing at all — not goddess, not warden, not woman — just a shape of panic in a freezing room.

Then Ulyana stirred beside her and said, sleep-thick, "Freya? You alright?" — and the name landed on her like a blanket thrown over a shivering dog, and she remembered, and she was.

"Bad dream," she managed.

"Mm." Ulyana's hand found hers in the dark, clumsy and warm. "You were saying names in your sleep. Lots of them. None of them sounded like yours."

Freya lay awake a long time after that, listening to Ulyana breathe, staring at the ceiling where frost grew in feather-patterns, and thought about what survives of a self when others define it.

She had spent forty years — mortal years, slow years — being almost no one. Garden warden. Bean-tender. The madwoman at the edge of the valley, if anyone thought of her at all. It had been a diminishment, yes, a prison, a grief. But it had also been, she understood now with terrible clarity, a kind of *protection*. No one prayed to the bean-witch. No one needed her to be anything. She had been free to remember herself at her own pace, in her own words, and the self she remembered had stayed *hers*.

Here, in Vaskr, she could see the alternative laid out in candlelight and ice. Gods made by panic. Gods shaped entirely by want. Gods who were nothing but the sum of their worshippers' needs, hollow behind the eyes, growing stranger and hungrier with every answered prayer, until whatever had originally stood up out of the wishing was worn away completely and only the *function* remained.

To be remembered wrongly, she thought, is worse than being forgotten. Because the forgotten can be found again. But the wrongly remembered — they are replaced. Overwritten. A new thing wears your face and answers to your name and does your miracles wrong, and the world calls it you, and eventually even you begin to wonder.

Somewhere out in the frozen city, a bell rang — not a church bell, she realized, but a *shrine* bell, one of the new ones — and a tide of voices rose to meet it, thousands of them, praying to gods that had not existed a season ago.

*We have to move,* she thought. *Tomorrow. However we manage it. Because if I stay in this city much longer, I'm going to start believing one of these names. And then I'll be one of them.*

* * *

Morning came grey and hard. They went out into the streets to find provisions and passage, and the city showed them what it had become.

They passed a shrine to the God of the Last Match, a candle-stub on a saucer, surrounded by a queue of people waiting to pray. They passed the Church of the Unbroken Window, whose congregation swept frost from their glass with the devotion other faiths reserved for scripture. They passed a woman preaching on a crate about the Merciful Fog, which had come, she swore, to blind the wolves. Behind her, in the alley, Freya saw something move that was shaped like fog and had too much intention in it, and she pulled Ulyana along quickly without explaining why.

At a provisioner's stall, while Ivo haggled over dried fish with the fervor of a man protecting his remaining fingers, Freya drifted toward a wall where notices had been nailed up, layer on layer, the way cities grow their histories in paper.

Most were mundane. Grain prices. Lost relatives. A reward for a stolen goat (no mention of mittens).

But one notice was fresh, its ink barely dry, and it stopped her breath.

It bore a crest she knew. She had seen it seared into Ivo's confession-letter, held up in firelight on the frozen Reach while Marta's face went the color of the snow.

A thorn and a mortar — the arms of House Vale, Marta's house, the founding family of Thornmere Valley.

And beneath the crest, in formal script:

*By order of the Elders of Thornmere: Let it be known that the bone-carver Ulyana Pemberton has strayed beyond the stones under false guidance. Any who assist her passage — any who provide her food, lodging, road, or rumor — act against the valley's law and forfeit its protection. She is to be returned to Thornmere unharmed. Reward offered for true information as to her whereabouts. Reward doubled for the return of the woman called Freya, concerning whom the Elders possess certain knowledge.*

Freya read it twice. Three times. The words did not change, though the frost crawling across the notice-board tried its best to blur them.

*Certain knowledge.* What knowledge? What did the elders know about her — about the warden who tended beans at the valley's edge and never aged quite right and never left? Had Marta's grandmother's records survived the generations? Did they know what she was? Did they know what she *carried* — the ember, the charms, the half-reforged hope of the Worldroot?

And *doubled*. Not for Ulyana. For her. As if she were the dangerous one. As if she were the cargo.

"Freya?" Ulyana's voice, close. "You've stopped breathing again. You do that when—"

Freya took the notice down off the board, folding it with hands that were steadier than she felt, and handed it to her.

Ulyana read it. Freya watched her read it — watched the blood leave her face in stages, watched her jaw set in the way it did when she was about to carve something difficult, watched her glance up and scan the crowded street with new eyes, seeing watchers everywhere, seeing the whole frozen city suddenly full of ears.

"Doubled," Ulyana said at last, very quietly. "For you."

"Yes."

"What do they know about you, Freya?"

And before Freya could answer — before she could decide, herself, what she knew and what she feared — a horn sounded from the direction of the city gate. Long, official, imperious. The crowd around them turned as one, and began, with the terrible fluid speed of a mob remembering its purpose, to flow toward the walls.

From somewhere near the gate, cutting through the cold, came a voice amplified by some shrine-born trick of acoustics, announcing arrivals and demanding papers — and then, above the noise, clear as a bone snapping:

"—and detain the travelers! All four of them! By authority of Thornmere Valley and House Vale!"

Marta was nowhere in sight.
