## 15. Marta at the Gate

Marta had rehearsed the speech for eleven days.

She had rehearsed it on the road, in the way she rehearsed everything — quietly, obsessively, turning each sentence over like a stone in her pocket until it was smooth and weightless and could be set down without cutting anyone. She had rehearsed it to the horses, who did not care. She had rehearsed it to the frost-rimed fields of the Ashfall Reach, where the wheat stood dead and silvered in its rows like a congregation waiting for a god who would never come. She had even rehearsed it once, shamefully, to her grandmother's memory — and her grandmother's memory had laughed at her, the way the old woman had laughed at everything soft, and said: *You'll never get through the first line.*

Her grandmother's memory was right. Marta never got through the first line.

She saw them coming up the pilgrim road an hour before they reached Vaskr's gate, because she had been standing there since before dawn, since the sky was still the color of a bruise going yellow at its edges. The city rose behind her — Vaskr, the City of Answered Prayers, all crooked spires and smoke and the smell of tallow burning in ten thousand street-shrines — but Marta kept her back to it. She watched the road instead. The road came down off the hills in long switchbacks, white with out-of-season frost, and on it, small with distance, walked four figures.

One of them moved like Ulyana.

You would think that after everything — after the letters, after the seal, after sending Ivo's name to the elders with instructions that made her sick to write — you would think Marta would have stopped being able to pick her friend out of a crowd of four. But no. Twenty years of friendship does not dissolve just because you have betrayed it. Ulyana walked the way she always had, head slightly bowed as though listening to something just below the ground, hands tucked into her sleeves against the cold, and beside her — beside her walked *her*, the warden woman from the shrine-garden, tall and unhurried, with frost not touching her the way frost touched everything else.

Marta's stomach turned over. Not from fear. From the old, stupid, animal relief of seeing someone you love when you believed you might never see them again — relief arriving, as it always did, several hours ahead of forgiveness.

*You'll never get through the first line.*

The first line had been: *Ulyana, I need you to listen before you hate me.*

What she actually said, when the four of them stopped three paces away and the wind carried the smell of woodsmoke and cold iron between them, was:

"You look terrible."

It came out cracked. Ulyana looked terrible — thinner, wind-scoured, a new scar along her jaw that Marta wanted immediately and violently to ask about and would never ask about — and behind her, the mapmaker Ivo had his left hand wrapped in filthy bandages where three fingers should have been, and a young man Marta didn't know stood watchful at the edge of the group with an ember-glow banked somewhere under his coat, and Freya — the goddess, the garden warden, whatever she was — looked at Marta with eyes the color of deep water and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said.

"I brought a cart," Marta said, because the speech was gone, burned off like morning fog, and what remained was only the truth wearing whatever clothes it could find. "Two horses. Provisions for the road home. The elders sent me to bring you back."

"Did they," Ulyana said.

Not a question. Marta had known Ulyana Pemberton for twenty years and had heard her carve a hundred bones, and she knew the sound of a voice that was listening to more than one conversation at once. She wondered — with a lurch, with a cold slide of dread — whether the dead of Vaskr were whispering about her right now. Whether some drowned sailor or plague-child or old woman dead two centuries was leaning close to Ulyana's ear and saying, *that one, that one is the granddaughter of the woman who cut the world's throat.*

"They did," Marta said. "They're frightened. The frost is inside the stones now, Uli. It got past the boundary last week. The millpond froze solid and it's midsummer. People are saying the valley itself is dying, and they're saying it's because—" she stopped.

"Because of me," Ulyana said.

"Because of what you're doing." Marta spread her hands. "I'm not here to fight you. I'm here because I asked to come. I told the elders I could reach you where their riders couldn't." She swallowed. "I told them I was the only one you'd listen to."

"And are you?" Freya spoke for the first time. Her voice was quiet and level and had the strange quality of a voice that had once been used to command weather. "The only one?"

Marta met those eyes and found, to her surprise, no hatred in them. Only attention. A goddess's attention, patient as winter, and somehow that was worse.

"No," Marta said honestly. "Probably not anymore."

* * *

They did not talk at the gate. Vaskr's gate was no place for anything true — pilgrims streamed past in their hundreds, pressing copper coins into the shrines, shouting prayers so specific and desperate they curdled the air. Marta heard a woman begging a god named Saint Ferrow-of-the-Third-Frost to let her daughter's fever break by morning, and a man offering his own teeth to something called the Kindly Hunger if it would only make his landlord forget a debt. New gods, born overnight, fat on panic. The whole city stank of tallow and terror.

So they took a room at a pilgrim inn called the Drowned Bell, and Marta paid with the elders' coin, and then sat across a scarred table from Ulyana while the others withdrew — Ivo to sleep with his ruined hand cradled against his chest, the ember-man Torvald's follower to stand watch, and Freya to the window, where she stood looking out at the burning shrines with an expression Marta could not read and did not dare try.

Tea came, gray and overbrewed. Neither of them touched it.

"Eleven days," Marta said finally. "That's how long I've been trying to figure out how to say this."

"You could try starting anywhere," Ulyana said. "You've had practice talking around things. Your family's had generations of it."

The words landed exactly where they were meant to land. Marta flinched and did not look away, because looking away would be another thing done *to* Ulyana instead of *in front of* her, and she had sworn on the road — sworn to the dead silver fields, sworn to her own reflection in frozen streams — that she would not manage this conversation. That she would not soften it or steer it or do the thing her family had always done, which was to bury the wound under so many layers of care that no one could ever find it again to heal it.

"You know," she said. "Don't you. About my grandmother."

"The bones told me." Ulyana's voice was flat. "A skull older than the valley. It remembered the muting. It remembered your grandmother leading the elders down beneath the roots with knives of boundary-stone, and it remembered why." Her hands, wrapped around the untouched tea, were steady. Marta envied them. "The plague. The gods wouldn't cure it, because cures weren't theirs to give, and so your grandmother decided that if the gods wouldn't save her children, the gods would never be allowed to speak to anyone's children again. And she hid the plague behind the covenant. Sealed the sickness in with the silence. Built a whole valley on top of both."

"Yes," Marta said. It was almost a relief, saying it plainly. Almost. "Her name was Sefja. Everyone in Thornmere still says her name like a blessing. *Sefja the Shield.* There's a statue of her at the well. Children put flowers at her feet every spring festival." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I put flowers there too. Every year. I was so proud of her. Do you understand? I grew up worshiping the woman who murdered the world's voices, and nobody ever told me, because *nobody knew*, because we buried it so deep we buried it from ourselves—"

"Marta."

"—and I found out the same week you left. In her papers. In her own hand, Uli, her own handwriting, the whole account of it, written out like a ledger entry, like she was balancing grain stores. *Cut the covenant. Hide the sickness. Let the gods go deaf.* And I sat in her study all night reading it, and I thought, I will tell Ulyana tomorrow. And then the elders came asking questions about you, and I thought, I will tell her next week. And then Ivo came asking after roads, and I gave him your route, and I gave it to them sealed with our crest, and I told myself it was to protect you, and Uli, I swear to you, I told myself so many things—"

"*Marta.*"

She stopped. Her face was wet. She hadn't noticed crying; it seemed to be happening to someone else at another table.

Ulyana was watching her with an expression Marta had seen her wear over the carving bench a thousand times — the expression of a woman reading the grain of a bone before deciding where the knife goes. Not cruel. Not kind. Just *attentive*, which was its own kind of terrible.

"Why are you really here?" Ulyana asked. "Not the elders' errand. Yours."

And there it was. The real question, laid open on the table between the cold tea and the twenty years, and Marta found that the speech she'd rehearsed for eleven days was gone entirely, and all that was left was the thing underneath it, the thing she'd been carrying since the night she read her grandmother's ledger-hand and understood what she was made of.

"Because I'm afraid," Marta said. "Not of the gods. Not of the Choir, not of the winter, not of any of it. I'm afraid of *you*. Of what you're about to do. You're going to reforge the covenant, aren't you? At the Worldroot. You're going to open the door my grandmother nailed shut, and you don't even know what's behind it. None of us do. The gods went silent for a reason, Uli. Maybe the reason was rotten — fine, I believe you, it was rotten, it was cowardice dressed up as protection. But a hundred years of silence grew over that wound like bark over a broken branch. And you want to tear the bark off. You want to wake everything. The Hollow Choir. The winter. Whatever else is out there that's been dreaming in the dark for a hundred years with nobody singing to it." Her voice shook. She let it. "My grandmother did a monstrous thing. I know that now. But she did it because she couldn't bear to lose her children, and I am standing here telling you that I can't bear to lose mine. You're mine, Uli. You and the whole stupid valley. And I would rather live in a lie that keeps everyone breathing than in a truth that gets us all killed."

The words hung in the air, and Marta heard them as if someone else had spoken them, and hated them, and knew they were true.

"That's it," she said quietly. "That's the whole of it. My grandmother traded the gods' voices for our lives, and I'm standing in her shadow asking you to trade yours. Let them die quietly, Uli. The old ones. Let the covenant stay broken. Let the silence hold. We'll get through the winter — valleys get through winters, it's what valleys *do*. Plant different crops. Move the granaries. Endure. People endure. They always have. But if you open that door and something comes through it that we can't endure—" She reached across the table, slowly, giving Ulyana time to pull away, and took her friend's hand. It was cold, and rough with carved-bone calluses, and it did not pull away. "Then I'll have helped kill you twice. Once with a seal on a letter, and once by holding the door while you walked through it. And I can't do both. I haven't got it in me to be sorry twice."

For a long moment Ulyana said nothing. Outside the window, a bell tolled somewhere in Vaskr — one of the answer-shrines, calling worshippers to some newborn god — and Freya turned from the glass, and her gaze settled on their joined hands with an unreadable gentleness.

"You rehearsed this," Ulyana said at last. "Didn't you. Eleven days of it."

"Twelve," Marta whispered.

"What was the first line?"

Marta laughed — a broken, wet sound — and wiped her face with her free hand. "*Ulyana, I need you to listen before you hate me.*"

"I don't hate you." Ulyana's thumb moved once across her knuckles, an old gesture from childhood, from scraped knees and stolen apples, from a friendship built before either of them knew what betrayal cost. "I want to. It would be easier. But the bones taught me something nobody wants to hear: that people are mostly fear wearing courage as a coat. Your grandmother was. The elders are. You are." She drew her hand back, gently, and the absence of it was enormous. "But Marta — the winter isn't weather. I've felt it now, out past the stones. It's the covenant dying. Both realms unraveling together, thread by thread. If we do nothing, the silence doesn't hold. The silence *ends*. Everything ends. Your grandmother bought a hundred years, and the bill has come due, and hiding from it won't pay it. Only reforging will."

"Or it kills us faster," Marta said.

"Or it kills us faster," Ulyana agreed. "But at least we'll die facing the truth instead of smothering under the lie. That's the choice, Marta. That's the whole choice. There's no third door where everyone lives comfortably and nobody has to be brave."

Marta looked down at her hands, at the space where Ulyana's warmth had been, and felt the full weight of her grandmother's inheritance settle onto her shoulders — not the statue at the well, not the flowers, but the real inheritance: the knowledge that love, unexamined, becomes a cage, and that she had spent her whole life building cages and calling them shelter.

"If I beg," she said, very quietly, "will it change anything?"

"No."

"If I order you home, in the elders' name?"

"No."

"If I get on my knees right here in front of the whole inn and swear I'll never forgive myself—"

"Marta." Ulyana's voice softened, and it was the softness that broke her. "You already know the answer. You've known it since you read her handwriting. Why do you think you came all this way yourself, instead of letting the elders send riders? Riders would have dragged me back. You came to say goodbye properly. Or—" and here Ulyana paused, and looked at her oldest friend with those bone-reader's eyes that saw the grain beneath the surface, "—or you came hoping I'd prove you wrong."

Marta sat very still.

Because that was the unbearable thing, the thing she had not admitted even to herself on twelve days of frozen road: that some small, stubborn, starving part of her had wanted Ulyana to be right. Had wanted the Worldroot to be real and the reforging to work and the winter to break, because if Ulyana was right, then Marta's family was not damned — merely mistaken. If Ulyana was right, then Sefja the Shield was not a monster but a frightened mother, and Marta herself was not a traitor but a daughter, and the whole rotten architecture of lies could be renovated instead of demolished.

If Ulyana was wrong, then Marta had betrayed her best friend for nothing.

"Come with us," Ulyana said.

Marta's head came up sharply.

"Not to fight. Not to be forgiven — I can't give you that yet, maybe I never can, and I won't pretend otherwise. Come as a witness. That's all. When the covenant is reforged, someone from Thornmere has to be there who knows the truth of the muting. Someone who carries Sefja's name and Sefja's guilt and can stand at the Worldroot and say: *this was done, and we know it was done, and we do it differently now.* Otherwise it's just another silence, Marta. Just another thing done *to* mortals instead of *in front of* them." Ulyana leaned forward. "Your grandmother cut the covenant in secret. Let her granddaughter mend it in the open. That's the only absolution on offer. It isn't forgiveness. It's better than forgiveness. It's *truth*."

Outside, the bell finished tolling. Somewhere in the burning, praying, freezing city, a crowd began to sing to a god none of them had heard of a month ago, and the sound drifted through the window like smoke.

Freya spoke from the window, without turning. "The Choir knows we're here. They've been circling Vaskr since dawn. They sing the names of everyone who travels with us — I've heard them try yours, Marta Okonkwo's-daughter, and they cannot finish it. Something in them recoils from your bloodline." She turned then, and her deep-water eyes held Marta's. "Which means when we descend, they will save you for last. Think carefully about what you carry with you when that moment comes."

Marta looked from the goddess to her oldest friend, and felt the last of the rehearsed speech — the elders' orders, the cart, the provisions, the careful twelve days of managed words — fall away like frost off a warming stone.

And what remained, when everything else was stripped away, was the same thing that had remained in her grandmother's heart a hundred years ago, in the dark beneath the roots, knife in hand: love, terrified and enormous, with nowhere honest to put itself.

Except this time, Marta thought. Except this once.

"When do we leave?" she said.
