## 16. What Loyalty Owes

Marta had rehearsed the argument for three days, all the way from Thornmere to Vaskr, and now that she stood in front of Ulyana at the city gate, every word of it had turned to ash in her mouth.

The gate was iron-banded oak older than either of them, scarred by centuries of hands. Beyond it, Vaskr seethed — a city Marta had only ever heard about in her grandmother's disapproving stories, where gods were born overnight like mushrooms after rain and prayers were currency spent carelessly. The cold came off the plain behind her in waves, though it was midsummer. Frost furred the grass at the roadside. Somewhere out there, the world was freezing solid, and she had been sent to bring Ulyana home before it swallowed her too.

*Home.* The word sat wrong in her chest. It had been sitting wrong since the night she'd found her grandmother's journal and read what the Pemberton valley was actually built on.

"You look terrible," Ulyana said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part — Ulyana was never unkind, even now, even with everything Marta had done and everything she hadn't said. She stood with Freya half a step behind her shoulder, the garden-warden woman with her too-still eyes and her hands stained green to the wrist, watching Marta the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has made the mistake of standing in the open.

"So do you," Marta managed. "You've lost weight."

"I've been walking." Ulyana's mouth twitched toward something that wasn't quite a smile. "The road doesn't feed you the way the workshop does."

Behind them, the mapmaker Ivo leaned against the gatepost with his bandaged hand cradled against his chest, frostbitten fingers wrapped in wool, saying nothing. He had already told them, Marta realized. The seal on the letter. The payment. Her family's crest pressed into grey wax, the twin birches of House Aldermoor. He must have told them days ago, and they had let her walk up to this gate believing she still had the advantage of surprise.

That was its own kind of answer.

"The elders sent me," Marta said. "They want you home. They want—" *they want you to stop*, she didn't say, because the elders' actual instructions had been more specific than that, and more shameful. "They want the valley to stay safe."

"Is that what you want?" Ulyana asked quietly.

And there it was. The question Marta had crossed two hundred frozen miles hoping never to be asked directly, because she had prepared answers for every version of this conversation except the honest one.

She looked at her oldest friend. They had been girls together in the shadow of the boundary stones; Ulyana carving bones in her grandmother's workshop while Marta learned which silences mattered and which could be broken. Twenty years of shared bread, shared grief when Marta's mother died, shared laughter over nothing on long winter evenings. And underneath all of it, like a root under a floorboard, the thing her family had done — the covenant cut, the gods muted, the plague hidden, the whole valley founded on a lie so old it had fossilized into virtue.

"I want everyone to live," Marta said finally. "I want the winter to stop. I want you to come home and carve your bones and be safe, and I want to go back to pretending I don't know why the gods went quiet." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's what I want. I've never once pretended otherwise."

"No," Ulyana said. "You haven't. You're the most honest liar I've ever known, Marta Aldermoor."

Freya moved then — not forward, just a shift of weight, the way she did when she was about to speak. Marta braced herself for the goddess's judgment. She had heard enough about the Hollow Choir, about gods gone feral from silence, to know what divine anger looked like these days.

But Freya only studied her with those still, deep eyes and said, "You walked through the Reach alone?"

"With two hired guards until Ashfall. After that, alone." Marta's hands were shaking; she clasped them to hide it. "The guards wouldn't go past the frozen river. They said the ice sings." She had heard it herself, that night camped on the riverbank — a sound like a hymn played backward, rising out of the white. She had not slept again until dawn.

"It does," Freya said. "Those are the ones my sister-gods used to be." Something moved behind her face, some grief so old it had worn smooth as river stone. "You crossed their country to reach her. Why? If you believe the elders — if you believe the muting should stand — you could have sent anyone. A letter. A hired blade."

"A letter wouldn't have brought her back."

"And a hired blade?"

Marta met the goddess's eyes. "Wouldn't have worked. I know her. She'd have carved a charm from the assassin's own finger bones and turned him around before he reached the second milestone."

For a moment — the first moment since the gate — something that might have been warmth flickered across Freya's face. "Yes," she said. "She would."

"Marta." Ulyana stepped closer. Close enough that Marta could see how tired she was, how the road had carved lines around her eyes, how her hands — those precise, patient hands — were chapped and nicked from weeks of travel. "Tell me why you really came. Not the elders' errand. Yours."

The wind came down off the plain and stirred the frost-grass, and somewhere far away the ice sang its backward hymn, and Marta understood that whatever she said next would decide not just Ulyana's road but her own.

So she told the truth. All of it, in order, the way you'd set out bones on a table for examination.

"I found my grandmother's journal in the spring. After the first frost killed the market crops, after the refugees started coming — I went looking for anything that explained it, and I found it in the cedar chest under the floorboards of the east room. Her handwriting. The whole account." Marta's throat tightened. "The plague year. How it came up the valley in autumn and the prayers went unanswered because the gods were already going strange, and how the elders decided that if the gods wouldn't save them, they'd make sure the gods couldn't punish them either. My grandmother led the cutting. She held the knife at the Worldroot ceremony — no, not the Worldroot, they didn't go that deep, they cut the covenant at the boundary stones, a shallow wound, enough to mute the voices without killing the gods outright. Enough that no one would ever hear an accusation again." She swallowed. "She wrote that the dead in the valley stopped speaking to the bone-carvers within a season. Your grandmother's mother wept for a month, the journal says. And then the valley forgot, because forgetting is what valleys do best."

Ulyana had gone very still. Beside her, Freya's hand came to rest between her shoulder blades — a small gesture, steadying, intimate in a way that made something twist in Marta's chest that she refused to name.

"And the guards," Ulyana said softly. "The letter with your crest, paying Ivo to lead us into the Choir's path. Was that the elders too?"

"No." The word cost her everything she had left. "That was me."

The silence stretched. Ivo shifted against the gatepost. Freya's hand did not move from Ulyana's back.

"After I read the journal, I thought — if they reach the Worldroot, if they reforge the covenant, the truth comes out. Everything comes out. The valley learns what it is. My family's name becomes the name traitors give their children as a warning." Marta heard her own voice go thin and strange. "I told myself it was for the valley. I told myself a comfortable lie was kinder than a true wound. I paid Ivo through a broker in Lowmarsh so it couldn't be traced, and I chose the Hollow Choir's territory because I thought — I thought if the gods took you, it would look like the road's ordinary danger, and the winter would keep coming or it wouldn't, but at least the lie would survive, and the lie was holding us up, Ulyana. The whole valley stands on it. Take it away and—"

"And it falls," Ulyana finished quietly. "It falls anyway, Marta. It's falling now. You've seen the frost. You walked here through it. The covenant is dying whether we speak the truth or not, and when it dies, everything goes with it — both realms, unraveling together. There is no comfortable silence left to protect. There's only how much of the world we can still save."

"I know." Marta's eyes burned. "I knew it by the time I reached Ashfall Reach. I stood on the bank of that singing river and I understood that I had tried to murder my best friend to preserve a lie, and that the lie was killing the world, and that I had become my grandmother." The tears came then, hot and fast, and she did not wipe them away. She owed Ulyana the sight of them. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking absolution. I'm asking you to come home, because it's the last selfish thing I'll ever want, and I wanted you to hear me refuse to ask for anything else ever again."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved. In the city beyond the gate, someone was shouting a prayer at a shrine — Marta caught fragments of it, *answer me, answer me, I named you myself, you owe me* — the desperate commerce of Vaskr, where gods sprang up overnight from panicked wishes.

"You could have stayed home," Ulyana said at last. "Sent the letter, kept your hands clean, let the elders carry the blame. Instead you walked through the Reach alone to say all of this to my face. That doesn't undo what you did. But it tells me who you're choosing to be now."

"I don't know who that is yet."

"Neither did I, when I left the valley." Ulyana almost smiled. "We find out by walking."

Then Marta saw it — the choice arriving in her friend's face like weather, like a change in pressure before a storm. Ulyana looked past her, out at the frosted plain, at the white road running north toward the mountains and the roots of the first tree beneath them. Then back at Marta.

"I'm not coming home," Ulyana said. "Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. The covenant gets reforged at the Worldroot or it doesn't get reforged at all, and if it doesn't, there won't be a Thornmere Valley to come home to — just another stretch of ice with some stones in it. So I'm finishing the road. I'm sorry, Marta. I know what it costs you. I know exactly what it costs you, better than anyone alive. But I choose the truth. I choose it over the valley, over the elders, over the quiet. I'd have chosen it over you, if you'd asked me to choose." Her voice gentled. "You didn't ask. That matters."

Marta stood in the cold and felt the last structure of her life come down — not collapsing, the way she'd always feared, but settling, the way snow settles off a branch, leaving the branch bent but whole. She had crossed two hundred miles to bring her friend home, and her friend had refused, and the refusal was right, and being right hurt worse than any wrong ever had.

She thought of her grandmother's journal, the careful script, the sentence Marta had read forty times: *A valley that forgets is a valley that survives.* Her grandmother had believed it with her whole heart. Her grandmother had been wrong, and the wrongness was freezing the world.

"Then I'm coming with you," Marta said.

Ulyana blinked. "Marta—"

"Not to stop you. Not to watch you. With you." The words came faster now, tumbling, as if they'd been waiting years behind her teeth. "You'll need someone at the Worldroot who knows the old rites — I know them, I was raised on them, the real ones, the ones the elders still practice in secret because they can't bear to admit the gods are listening again. You'll need someone the valley will believe afterward, when the truth comes out. An Aldermoor confessing is worth fifty Pembertons testifying. And you'll need—" her voice broke and she pushed through it, "you'll need someone who owes you a debt no living person could ever pay, and intends to spend the rest of her life trying."

Freya spoke, low and even. "The road ahead is worse than the Reach. What's beyond Vaskr would freeze the marrow in your bones. The Hollow Choir knows your name now, Marta Aldermoor. They sing to the ones who muted them."

"Good," Marta said. "Let them sing. I'll sing back. I'll tell them I'm sorry, and I'll tell them my grandmother's name, and if they want to take it out of my hide, they can form an orderly line." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, done with crying. "I'm done protecting people from the truth. It's the only thing I have left to give, and I'm giving it to you."

Ulyana looked at her for a long, long moment. Whatever passed between them then was not forgiveness — forgiveness was a door neither of them could open yet, maybe ever. It was something rawer and more durable than that: two people standing in the wreckage of twenty years of friendship, surveying the damage honestly, and choosing to build on the cleared ground rather than walk away from it.

"All right," Ulyana said simply. "Come on, then. We leave at first light, before the gate crowds. Torvald's ember burns low and the Worldroot won't wait for our convenience."

She turned toward the gate, and Freya fell into step beside her, and after a heartbeat Marta followed — through the iron-banded doors, into the roaring strangeness of Vaskr, where shrines smoked on every corner and strangers gripped charms and shouted names at gods who hadn't existed last month.

Ivo fell in beside her, favoring his bandaged hand. "You know," he said quietly, "the last person who switched sides mid-journey told me it was because the other side had better arguments. You're the first to do it because yours ran out."

"Keep walking, mapmaker."

He grinned and kept walking.

But that night, in the rented room above a shrine to a god called the Mother-of-Small-Mercies, Marta lay awake listening to the city pray itself hoarse, and she felt it — faint, far off, beneath the floorboards and the foundations and the frozen earth itself. A pulse. A slow, vast rhythm, like a heart buried under the roots of the world.

And threaded through it, growing clearer as the night deepened, a sound she recognized from the banks of the Ashfall Reach: singing. Many voices. Cold and sweet and wrong.

They were not singing her name anymore.

They were singing Ulyana's — and this close to the Worldroot, Marta realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the frost, they had begun to harmonize with it.
