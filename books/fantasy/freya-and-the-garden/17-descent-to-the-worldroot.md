## 17. Descent to the Worldroot

The ember did not flicker.

That was the first thing Ulyana noticed about it, holding it cupped in her palms as Torvald had pressed it there — a coal no larger than a robin's egg, glowing the deep hearth-orange of a fire that has burned in the same room for three hundred years. It gave heat without smoke, light without wavering, and it sat in her hands with the patient weight of something that intended to outlast her.

"It will not gutter," Torvald had said, when he pressed it into her grip at the mouth of his ruined temple. His frozen face — half of it always stone, half of it still flesh and warmth — had softened into something almost like tenderness. "It is my last ember. The last piece of me that was ever worshipped honestly. It knows the way down because it *is* the way down. Everything I ever was was built on roots."

"And when we reach it?" Freya had asked.

"Then it goes into the work," Torvald said simply. "And I go wherever embers go." He had looked at Ulyana then, at her hands, the way he'd looked at them since the day they met — as if hands like hers were a kind of miracle he had thought the world had stopped making. "Carve well, bone-carver."

Now they stood at the edge of the Ashfall Reach's far side, where the ground began, very gradually, to tilt downward into dark. Behind them lay the frozen waste they had crossed; ahead, a cleft in the earth so old that trees had grown along its rim and died and rotted and been replaced by other trees, generation upon generation, until the cleft itself seemed less a wound than a feature of the world, something the land had always meant to have.

The entrance was a hollow between two roots.

Not roots like the ones she knew from Thornmere — pale, modest things that lifted flagstones and cracked garden walls. These roots were the color of deep amber and dark blood, thick as temple pillars, emerging from the earth and plunging back into it in vast slow arcs, like the shoulders of something sleeping just beneath the soil. They came together overhead and wove themselves into an arch, and through the arch Ulyana could see stairs. Not carved stairs. Grown stairs. Each step a knot of rootwood, worn smooth down their centers by feet that had climbed them when climbing them had been pilgrimage rather than desperation.

"The first tree," Freya said quietly. She stood at the arch with her hand flat against the rootwood, and even in the ember-light Ulyana could see what the touch cost her — the small shudder that went through her shoulders, the way her breath caught and held. "Every god remembers it. Even the ones who forgot everything else. We were all... *grown* here, in a sense. Pruned into shape. Given names the way a gardener gives a trellis." Her fingers curled against the wood. "I haven't been home since before your valley had a name."

"You remember it," Ulyana said. "Even muted?"

Freya turned. In the ember's glow her eyes were the green of new leaves held up to sun, and for once there was nothing warden-like in them, nothing of the woman who weeded bean rows and argued with crows. There was only the goddess, looking at the door of her childhood.

"I remember it," she said, "the way you remember your grandmother's voice. Not the words anymore. Just the shape of being heard."

Ivo shifted behind them, adjusting the strap of the charm-bag across his chest. His bandaged hand — nine fingers now, the ninth lost to the Reach's cold in a single careless hour — hovered near the knife at his belt, the way it had hovered ever since the ambush, ever since the Choir. "Stairs going down," he said. "In my experience, stairs going down are where stories stop being survivable."

"In your experience," Marta said, "stories are things that happen to other people."

"They were," Ivo said. "Then I met all of you."

Ulyana almost smiled. Almost. But the ember was warm in her hands, and warm things made her think of the dead, and the dead had been getting louder the farther south they walked — not louder exactly, but *closer*, their voices losing the underwater quality they had beyond the boundary stones, arriving in her skull with edges on them now, with breath. Somewhere behind them, miles back along the frozen road, the Hollow Choir had picked up their trail again. She could feel them the way she could feel weather coming: a pressure, a hush, a wrongness in the air's texture.

They were singing her name.

They had been singing it since the Reach.

* * *

They descended as the light failed above them.

The stairs wound down through the root-arch and kept winding, spiraling around the trunk of the first tree itself — for it was a tree, or had been, though its trunk was wider than Thornmere's meeting hall and its bark had gone gray and smooth as river stone, ancient past the point where age meant anything. The ember lit perhaps ten paces in any direction. Beyond that, the dark was total, and in the total dark Ulyana heard things.

She heard the dead, first. That was ordinary. That was her whole life. But here the dead were different — older, stranger, fewer words and more *hum*, voices that had forgotten language and kept only its music. They drifted up from below like cold air from a cellar, and some of them noticed her passing and turned toward her attention the way flowers turn toward light, wordless and hungry and sad.

Then she heard the living dead — the gods the muting had broken. They clung to the roots as they descended, or so it seemed to her gift: presences packed into the wood like bees in a hive, some sleeping, some whispering fragments of prayers they had absorbed over centuries and could no longer answer. A prayer for rain, repeated ten thousand times until it wore a groove in the rootwood. A prayer for a child's fever to break, worn so deep it had become part of the tree's grain. A prayer of thanks, only one, offered by someone who had remembered to be grateful, and Ulyana felt it pass through her like sunlight and had to stop walking, one hand on the great gray trunk, until she could breathe again.

"Ulyana." Freya's hand on her shoulder. Warm. Solid. Smelling of woodsmoke and crushed green things, of the garden, of home in a way that had nothing to do with Thornmere anymore. "How much are you hearing?"

"All of it," Ulyana whispered. "Freya, it's like — do you know what it's like when you open a door you've kept shut for years, and everything behind it falls out at once? Every prayer anyone ever said down here is still *in* these roots. Nobody ever answered them. They're just — waiting. Stacked up. Like letters never sent."

Behind them, Ivo made a small sound. Ulyana glanced back and saw him looking at the roots with an expression she hadn't seen on his face before — not fear, exactly. Recognition. "My mother prayed," he said, to no one in particular. "All her life. To whatever was listening. I used to tell her nothing was listening." He adjusted the charm-bag strap with his maimed hand, carefully, the way he'd done everything since the frost took his finger — without complaint, without ceremony, endurance worn like a coat. "I owe her an apology."

"She'll hear it," Ulyana said. "One way or another. Everything gets heard eventually. That's the whole trouble."

Marta walked last. She had walked last since Vaskr's gate, since she chose to follow instead of stop them, and she had not asked to walk anywhere else, and no one had offered. Ulyana heard her boots on the root-stairs, steady and deliberate, and heard — beneath the boots, beneath twenty years of friendship — the silence Marta carried now, the silence of a woman who had confessed the worst thing about herself and her family and been neither forgiven nor condemned, simply *continued with*. Ulyana did not know yet what to do with that silence. She suspected she would be learning for a long time.

Down. And down. And down.

Hours passed, or what Ulyana thought of as hours, though time down here moved strangely, pooling in the root-hollows like water. Twice they rested, sharing hard bread and melted snow from Ivo's waterskin, the ember sitting between them like a fifth companion, radiating its patient orange calm. Once, Freya slept — truly slept, for the first time since Ulyana had known her, curled against the trunk with her head on Ulyana's thigh, and Ulyana sat unmoving for the whole of that rest with her hand resting featherlight on Freya's hair, listening to the dead hum below and thinking: *this is what I am choosing. Not the Worldroot. Not the covenant. This.* The thought frightened her more than the Choir did, and steadied her more than any prayer ever had.

When Freya woke, she looked up at Ulyana from below, hair full of root-dust, and said nothing, and touched Ulyana's knee once, briefly, before rising. It was enough. It was more than enough. Neither of them had found the words yet for what they were to each other, and Ulyana was beginning to suspect they never would, and beginning to suspect that this too was fine — that some things, like the ember, needed no flickering to burn.

* * *

The stairs ended at the roots' meeting place.

They came out onto a cavern floor of packed dark earth, and Ulyana understood immediately why Torvald's ember had wanted to come here, why it pulled gently downward in her hands like a compass needle finding north. The roots of the first tree converged here from every direction — hundreds of them, thousands, pouring down the cavern walls and across the ceiling and plunging into the floor, and at the center of the convergence, suspended in the air where the roots thinned and failed—

The covenant hung.

Two halves of it. Ulyana had imagined parchment, tablets, a book, something with writing. What hung in the center of the cavern was simpler and more terrible: two lengths of living wood, each as long as a bridge-plank, grown from opposite sides of the cavern and reaching toward each other across a gap of perhaps six feet — and torn. Not cut. Torn, the way a branch tears in a storm, the break jagged and splintered, fibers splayed like snapped sinew. From the torn ends dripped something that was not sap and not blood but both, falling in slow gleaming drops into a pool below that had been filling, drop by drop, for two hundred years.

And the whole cavern was cold. Not Reach-cold, not winter-cold. *Unfinished*-cold. The cold of a sentence that stops mid-word.

"That's it," Marta said. Her voice came out thin. "That's what my grandmother—"

"Yes," Freya said.

Ulyana stepped closer to the gap, the ember raised, and looked at the severed halves, and her gift — her cursed, beloved gift — rose up in her like a tide. Because the covenant was not dead. That was the horror of it. Cut, torn, abandoned for two centuries, and still *alive* on both ends, the two halves straining toward each other across the gap with the slow blind persistence of roots seeking water. She could hear them. Faintly, faintly, a sound like a heart heard through a wall: the covenant, beating. Waiting to be joined. Waiting for a voice that could speak for the dead and a name that could speak for the divine, written into living wood.

Torvald's ember leaned toward it like a child toward its mother.

"We can do this," Ivo said softly. He had come up beside her, and his face in the ember-light was stripped of all its irony, all its mapmaker's detachment. "We actually — this is real. We're really here."

"We're really here," Ulyana agreed.

And then the singing started.

It came up through the floor first, through the roots, through the soles of her boots — her own name, in a hundred voices, in a thousand, layered and harmonized and *wrong*, each voice a little off from human, stretched slightly, chilled slightly, the way a bell sounds wrong when it has cracked. *Ulyana. Ulyana Pemberton. Bone-carver. Hearer. Daughter of the valley that forgot us.* The Hollow Choir rose out of the dark passages below the cavern floor the way frost rises on a window: gradually, then all at once, faces forming in the gloom — faces she knew from the ambush, frozen mid-expression, beautiful and terrible, mouths open on her name.

They did not attack. That was worse. They arranged themselves around the cavern's edge, dozens of them, scores, a congregation of the muted and the mad, and they sang her name in rounds, in lullabies, in funeral dirges, weaving it into melodies she recognized — valley songs, Thornmere songs, songs sung over graves she herself had carved the markers for.

"They've been following us to lead us here," Freya breathed. She had stepped in front of Ulyana, arms slightly out, and her form flickered — for one heartbeat Ulyana saw through the warden's body to something vast and green and furious underneath, a goddess at full stretch, and then it folded away again. "Or they've been following us because we're walking toward what they lost. I don't know which is worse."

"Doesn't matter," Ulyana said.

Her voice surprised her. It came out level. Calm. She looked down at her own hands, at the ember resting in them, and found that the dread she had carried since the Reach — since the first frost, since the drowned boy's memorial, since the prayer answered aloud in her grandmother's garden — had changed shape somewhere on the long stairs. It was still there. It filled her entirely. But dread and purpose, she discovered, were made of the same substance; they differed only in direction. Dread pointed away from the thing feared. Purpose pointed at it.

And she pointed at the torn covenant.

"Marta," she said. "Give me the carving tools."

Marta crossed the cavern floor with the Choir singing Ulyana's name around her like weather, knelt, and opened the roll of oiled leather with steady hands. The knives caught the ember-light. Ulyana chose the smallest one, the hook-blade her grandmother had used, the one that fit the curves of small bones and small truths.

Above them, at the cavern's far entrance, the singing changed.

The thousand voices stopped. All at once, mid-note, the way a heart stops. And in the ringing silence that followed, every frozen face in the Hollow Choir turned toward the covenant hanging torn in the center of the dark — and every mouth, in perfect unison, began to sing something new.

Not her name.

A warning. Or a welcome. Ulyana couldn't tell which, and the dead in the roots couldn't either, and as the melody swelled up through the floor and into her bones, Freya went white as birchbark and said, in a voice Ulyana had never heard her use:

"They're singing the old rite. The words of the forging." Her hand found Ulyana's and gripped it, hard, desperate. "Ulyana — they know what we've come to do. And they're going to help us whether we want them to or not."
