## 18. The Choir's Bargain

Freya had forgotten how it felt to be sung to.

Not worshipped — she remembered worship well enough, the greasy warmth of ten thousand throats chanting her name in unison, the way devotion pooled like tallow at the base of her altars. Worship was a transaction. But singing — singing was a gift freely given, and the last time anyone had sung to Freya of the Gardens, harvest had still meant something, and her name had been spoken with flour on the lips and dirt under the fingernails of everyone who said it.

Now the Hollow Choir sang, and every note was a theft.

Their voices came up through the dark beneath the Worldroot like water rising through a drowned man's lungs. The cavern was vast beyond sight — the roots of the first tree hung from a ceiling that might have been stone or might have been sky, pale and thick as the columns of a fallen temple, dripping sap that glowed faintly gold where it fell. The severed halves of the covenant stretched across the abyss between them: two great arcs of living bone, each longer than a cathedral, their broken ends reaching toward one another across a gap no bridge could span. They looked, Ulyana had said, like hands that had almost finished a prayer.

And below them, filling the hollow of the world, stood the Choir.

They had been gods once. Freya could see it in what remained of them — the architecture of divinity still visible beneath the frost, the way a burned house keeps the shape of its rooms. There was a tall one who might have been a god of rivers; ice had filled his mouth and throat until he was a frozen waterfall wearing a crown. There was a small one, child-sized, who had perhaps been a spirit of doorways and thresholds, and now stood in the doorway of nothing, waiting to be let in forever. Their faces were smooth as eggs. Their mouths moved when they sang, but no breath fogged the air, because they had no breath left to give.

They were singing Ulyana's name.

*Ulyana. Ulyana Pemberton. Carver-of-charms. Hearer-of-the-dead. Daughter of the valley that forgot us.*

The song was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It wound through the cavern like smoke through a keyhole, and Freya watched it find the mortals — watched Ivo grip his maimed hand against his chest, watched Marta's face go slack with something that was not quite peace, watched Torvald's last ember gutter in its iron lantern as if the song were wind. Even the dead voices that clung to Ulyana's charm-bag went quiet, hushed, listening.

But it was Ulyana herself that Freya could not look away from.

The bone-carver stood at the edge of the abyss with her chin lifted, and the song poured over her like light over water, and for one terrible moment Freya saw what the Choir saw: not a woman, but an instrument. A voice that heard the dead. A key shaped exactly like the lock they had spent three centuries freezing shut.

*Come down,* the Choir sang. *Come down, little hearer. We have been so quiet. We have been so patient.*

"They're lying," Marta whispered. Her voice cracked on the word. "Gods always—"

"No." Freya stepped forward, putting herself between Ulyana and the abyss, and felt the old anger rise in her chest like heat off summer stones. "No, they're not. That's what makes them dangerous."

Because it was true. That was the thing no one understood about the Hollow ones, the thing the stories got wrong. They did not lie. Lying required wanting, and wanting required a self, and the silence had eaten their selves down to the marrow. What remained was only function. A river-god frozen mid-flow wanted only to flow again. A threshold-spirit wanted only a door. And the Choir, collectively, all of them together, wanted only what they had been made for before the muting: to answer.

They would answer anything. They would answer *accurately*. And accuracy, Freya knew better than any being alive, could be crueller than any lie ever told.

*We offer,* sang the Choir, and the cavern changed.

It happened the way weather happens — suddenly, totally, without negotiation. The frost on the Choir's faces bloomed outward, spreading across the floor in feathered patterns, and in those patterns images formed, the way frost forms ferns on a windowpane: the valley of Thornmere, whole and warm. The boundary stones standing. Children in the market square. Bread rising. The dead quiet in their graves, quiet in the way the living mean when they say *rest*, and the living free of voices, free of the weight of other worlds, each soul sealed in its own small warm house of flesh and silence.

*Restore the covenant as it was,* the Choir sang, and now there was something almost tender in the frozen harmonies, something that ached like an old lullaby half-remembered. *Every thread back in its place. Every god behind the veil. No more winter. No more dying realms. Your valley will never know cold again. Your friend's family will sleep easy. The mapmaker keeps his fingers. The hearth-keeper keeps his ember.*

The frost-patterns shifted, and Freya saw herself rendered in ice: a woman in a garden, hoeing beans, ordinary, mortal-seeming, content. *And you, forgotten one. You will keep your garden. You will keep your hands in the soil. No one will remember you wrongly, because no one will remember you at all — and is that not the mercy you have prayed for, every night, into the dark?*

Freya's breath stopped.

Because it *was*. Somewhere in the long centuries of being bound into warden's flesh, she had prayed exactly that. She had knelt in her own garden — the garden that had once been her temple, her body, her name — and asked whatever was left listening to let her be small. To let her be a woman who grew beans and did not remember being a goddess. She had thought those prayers fell on nothing.

The Choir had heard them. The Choir had been saving them.

*All of this,* they sang, *for one small price. The old covenant, restored exactly. And the old covenant requires a muting. The muting requires a muter. A voice that hears the dead must be the voice that silences them. Yours, little carver. Yours, and then never yours again — you will carve bones that are only bones, and you will live, and you will be loved, and you will never hear another ghost weep or whisper or call you by name. Comfortable silence. Forever. For everyone.*

The image in the frost changed one last time, and Freya felt something in her chest turn over.

It showed Ulyana old. It showed her in a garden — *their* garden, the one they had planned in the small hours on the road, the one Freya had described with her hands while Ulyana laughed at her precision — with grey in her hair and sun on her face and no shadows at the edge of her vision, no dead murmuring at her shoulder, nothing but the ordinary blessed noise of a life. It showed everything Freya had secretly, shamefully wanted for her.

It showed Freya's own love, weaponized. Offered back to her as bait.

"Say yes," Ivo breathed beside her. His ruined hand trembled. "Saints' blood, say yes. It's everything we—"

"It's a cage," Freya said.

"It's *spring*," Marta said, and her voice broke. "It's the valley alive. It's my family safe. How is that a cage?"

Freya didn't answer her. She was looking at Ulyana.

Ulyana had not moved. She stood with her arms at her sides, her scarred carver's hands open, and her face was doing something complicated that Freya had learned to read across a hundred miles of frozen road: the small furrow between her brows that meant she was listening past the words to what lived underneath them. The tilt of her head that meant the dead were speaking, even here, even now — because somewhere in that charm-bag were bones old enough to remember the first muting, and the dead did not stop testifying just because the world grew loud.

And Freya realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Ulyana was going to say no.

That she was already saying no, in the way she stood, in the set of her shoulders, in the way her thumb found the carved edge of the charm at her wrist — the little bone fish Freya had watched her make by firelight, the first gift she'd ever given her, worth nothing, worth everything.

And Freya realized something else, something worse:

She did not want Ulyana to say no.

She wanted Ulyana to say *yes*. Some small, starved, cowardly corner of her — the corner that had prayed to be small, the corner that hoarded bean-harvests and quiet mornings and the weight of a sleeping head against her shoulder — that corner wanted the bargain accepted. Wanted the winter ended. Wanted Ulyana safe and deaf and hers, in a garden, forever, with the great grinding machinery of heaven locked away where it could never again ask anything of either of them.

She wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

And Ulyana opened her mouth to answer —

"No."

The word came out of Freya like a spearcast. It rang off the roots and the bone-arcs and the frozen faces of the Choir, and it was too late to take it back, and some distant part of her watched herself do it the way one watches a cart start to slide toward a cliff.

"She refuses," Freya said, louder, stepping forward, arms wide, putting her whole body between Ulyana and the frost-written promise of a peaceful life. "Do you hear me, you hollow echoes? She refuses. We did not walk across the Ashfall Reach. We did not bury a mapmaker's fingers in the ice. We did not carry a hearth-god's last ember through the dark to trade it all for a prettier cage. The covenant will be reforged *new* — or not at all."

The Choir stopped singing.

The silence that followed was worse than the song. Three hundred years of silence, concentrated, pressing down on the cavern like deep water. The frost-images shattered — Thornmere, the garden, the grey-haired Ulyana — all of it bursting into powder that drifted down into the abyss and did not land anywhere.

*You speak for her,* said the Choir. Not singing now. Just speaking, flat and cold, each word a chip of ice. *She has a voice. We asked her voice. You took her answer from her mouth.*

"I know what's best for—"

*Do you?*

The question dropped into Freya like a stone into a well, and she heard, in the long moment before it struck bottom, every echo it would make.

"You don't understand," she said, and heard her own voice climb, heard it crack along fault-lines she hadn't known were there. "I've seen what the old days cost mortals. I was *there*. I answered prayers until answering hollowed me out, and the things they asked for — the cruelties done in my name, the harvests bought with blood because I taught them I favored it — I know what gods do to the people who love them. Silence was a mercy. Someone built a wall between your kind and mine, and it was the kindest act any god or mortal ever—"

"Freya."

Ulyana's voice. Quiet. Which was worse than shouting. Ulyana shouting was weather; Ulyana quiet was a knife laid flat on a table.

"Don't," Freya said. "Ulyana, whatever you're about to—"

"You spoke for me." Ulyana stepped forward, out from behind her, around her, until they stood side by side at the edge of the abyss, and she did not look at the Choir at all. She looked at Freya, and her eyes were bright and furious and hurt in a way that made Freya feel every one of her thousand years. "In front of them. In front of *everyone*. They asked *me*, and you answered, and you didn't even wait to hear what I'd say."

"I didn't need to wait. I've seen you nearly die four times since the stones. I wasn't going to stand here and let them—"

"That's not your choice!" It tore out of Ulyana like something that had been growing in her for weeks, maybe months, maybe since the day a goddess had walked out of a bean row and started deciding things. "My hearing. My craft. My *voice*. You want to reforge a covenant about gods and mortals choosing each other freely, and your first act at the Worldroot is to take a choice away from a mortal because you think you know better?" Her breath shuddered. "Marta's grandmother did that. Did you know that? She took a choice away from the whole valley because she was afraid, because she loved them, because she was *sure* she knew best. And three hundred years later the world is freezing to death for it."

The words landed like blows. Freya stood in them and could not lift her arms.

Because Ulyana was right, and Freya had known she was right since the moment the word *no* left her mouth, and knowing it changed nothing about the terror that had driven the word out. That was the filthy truth of love's arrogance: it never felt like arrogance. It felt like protection. It felt like watching someone you loved walk toward a cliff and grabbing their wrist, and only afterward — only when they flinched — did you realize that from their side, your hand and the fall had looked exactly the same size.

"I was trying to protect you," Freya said, and heard how small it sounded, how ancient, how worn-through, the same sentence every god had ever offered as apology and every tyrant too.

"I know." Ulyana's voice gentled, which somehow cut deeper. "That's what makes it unforgivable and sweet at the same time. That's the trap, Freya. Love that protects until it owns." She turned, at last, to face the Choir. "I'll answer for myself. I should have from the start. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it?"

The frozen faces turned toward her. All of them. Dozens. Hundreds. The whole drowned pantheon of the muted world, waiting.

"So here is my answer," Ulyana said, and her voice carried in the vast dark the way only a voice that had spent a lifetime speaking for the dead could carry. "No. Not your bargain. Not the old covenant, not the comfortable silence, not the pretty garden you painted out of *her* prayers—" she flicked a glance at Freya, and Freya's face burned, "—because I've heard the dead, every day of my life, and I will tell you what silence actually sounds like. It sounds like a drowned boy under the ice, calling for a mother who can't hear him. It sounds like the oldest skull in my workshop, holding a testimony no one would take for three hundred years. Silence isn't rest. Silence is a room with the door bricked over. I won't build that room. Not for me. Not for anyone."

The Choir did not move. But the frost on their faces began, very slowly, to change — new patterns forming, and Freya read them with a heart going cold and colder.

They were not angry.

They were *recalculating*.

*Then reforge it,* the Choir said, and the tenderness was gone from their voices now, and what remained was older and more terrible: need, pure and enormous, the need of a frozen river to flow. *Reforge it your way, hearer. Bring your new covenant to the broken halves. Speak your mortal truth into the bone. We will watch.*

And then, softer, in a harmony that raised every hair on Freya's arms:

*And if your truth is not strong enough to hold the weight of two realms — if the bridge breaks with you standing on it — we will still be here. We are very good at waiting. Ask the winter.*

The frost-patterns collapsed inward, drawing back into the Choir's bodies like breath drawn in, and the vast cavern settled into a silence that was not empty but *attentive* — the silence of an audience settling in its seats, of judges taking their places, of a snare already set and patiently armed.

Torvald's ember flared in its lantern, once, like a heartbeat.

"Well," said Ivo into the quiet, his voice thin. "That went well."

No one laughed.

Ulyana was already unbuckling the charm-bag. Her hands were steady. Freya watched them and thought: *she has decided, and she decided it herself, and I nearly stole that from her, and I will spend whatever remains of eternity earning back the right to stand beside her while she does it.* She reached out — slowly, deliberately, asking this time, not taking — and Ulyana looked at her hand, and after a moment, covered it with her own.

"We do this together," Ulyana said. It was not quite forgiveness. It was terms.

"Together," Freya agreed.

Above them, the severed halves of the covenant hung in the dark, their broken ends reaching and never touching, and beneath the Worldroot, in the attentive silence, the carving bone waited for a knife.

Ulyana drew hers.

* * *

The first cut was the hardest, as Ulyana had warned it would be — living bone remembers, and resists, and grieves. But that work belonged to the next hour, and the next, and Freya stood at her shoulder with her true name gathered behind her teeth like a held breath, and tried not to count how many ways everything could still break.

She failed utterly. And carved anyway.
