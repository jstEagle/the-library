## 19. Reforge

The Worldroot did not look like a tree.

It looked like the idea of one — a trunk so vast that its bark had weathered into valleys of its own, ridges and ravines of grey wood going down into darkness, roots thick as rivers spreading out beneath us into a cavern that had no ceiling I could find. The air tasted of loam and lightning. Somewhere far above, water dripped in slow percussion, each drop landing with the weight of a bell.

And hanging before us, suspended in the root-hollow like the ribs of some drowned leviathan, were the two halves of the covenant.

They had been bone once. I knew bone the way a baker knows bread, the way a blind man knows his own doorframe, and even across the distance I could read them: ancient, immense, carved all over with script that predated any alphabet I had ever been taught. They hung at an angle, snapped clean through the middle, the broken ends splayed and pale as frostbite flesh. Between them stretched a gap of empty air, and through that gap I could hear — not with my ears, but with the gift that lived behind them — a silence so complete it had texture. It was the shape of everything that used to be said there.

Torvald's ember floated ahead of us, a coal no larger than a thumbnail, burning without fuel in the cold. His voice came from it, thin and warm, a hearth-fire reduced to its last word.

*The halves will not join themselves,* he said. *A bridge is not built by the river.*

Freya stood beside me, and I felt her more than saw her — the garden-warmth of her, green and growing, the only living heat in that enormous dark. She had been quiet since the descent. Quiet the way a woman is quiet when she is about to say something she has rehearsed for a thousand years.

"You'll need to carve," she said finally. "Not dead bone. Living."

"I know." I had known since Torvald told us, three nights ago, huddled in the ruin of his last temple while the winter screamed outside. *Living bone takes the carving into itself,* he had said. *Dead bone remembers. Living bone becomes.* "Where?"

She turned to me then, and in the ember-light her face was both faces at once — the warden who pulled weeds from bean rows with dirt under her nails, and something older underneath, something whose eyes held weather systems. "Me," she said.

The word dropped through me like a stone through well-water.

"The covenant was made of a god's oath and a mortal's voice," Freya went on, steady, as if she were explaining irrigation. "The old one was cut from the Worldroot's own heartwood, but heartwood forgets. That was the flaw. That was why it could be muted." She lifted her arm, bared to the shoulder, and in the ember-light I saw the bone beneath the skin — not metaphorically. Her forearm had gone translucent at the wrist, the bones visible, faintly gold, alive. "I am the last god who remembers her name. My body remembers how to be divine. Carve me."

"No." The word came out of me flat and immediate, the way a hand comes up to catch a falling cup. "Absolutely not."

"Ulyana—"

"You don't know what my chisels do." My voice cracked on it. "I carve memorials. I take what's finished and give it dignity. What you're asking—" I looked at her arm, at the gold thread of bone under skin that I had held crossing frozen rivers, that had steadied me when every dead voice within fifty miles tried to pour through my skull. "Living bone feels everything. And I would be cutting into a goddess."

"A forgotten goddess," she corrected gently. "There's a difference, and the difference is you."

I wanted to argue. I want it noted, wherever such things are written down, that I wanted very badly to argue. But the Hollow Choir had found us at the mouth of the descent, and they were singing already — I could feel it coming up through the soles of my boots, through the living rock, a harmony with no melody, voices layered on voices wearing faces like ice on pondwater. They sang prayers back at their askers. They had sung my name for six days now, in voices I knew: my grandmother, the drowned child Tam, my mother, all of them wrong, all of them slightly flattened, like songs remembered by someone who had never heard music.

"They're coming," Ivo said from the darkness behind us. He had his back to us, facing the tunnel we'd come down, his maimed hand wrapped and useless against his chest, his good hand on his knife. Frostbite had taken two fingers below the second knuckle; he joked about it the way soldiers joke, which is to say, terribly. "Marta and I can buy you time. Not much. Some."

"How much is some?" Marta asked. She stood beside him, and she had not spoken to me gently since Vaskr's gate, since she had begged me to let the gods die quietly and I had refused her. But she was here. She had chosen this over home, over her family's name, over safety, and she held her grandmother's iron poker like a sword because it was the only weapon she'd brought.

"Enough," Ivo said. "Probably."

*Ulyana.* Torvald's ember drifted closer, settling near my shoulder like a tame bird. *There is a price. You know this. Say it aloud, so that the covenant hears you choose it.*

I knew. I had known since the night in the bean rows, maybe. Since the first prayer answered. Everything in this story had been walking me here, step by patient step, the way water walks a stone smooth.

"To finish the carving," I said, and my voice sounded very small under all that root, "the work has to pass through me. All of it. Every voice the covenant carries — the dead speaking to the living, the living praying to the gods, the whole conversation that makes a world — it goes through the carver's hands. And hands that hold that much voice—" I stopped. Started again. "My hearing goes. Not just the dead. All of it. The gift and the ears together. The sense that defines me, spent all at once, forever."

The silence after was the worst kind — the kind where everyone is being kind.

"It doesn't have to be tonight," Marta said quietly.

"Yes," I said, "it does. The Choir is here. The winter is here. There is no version of this where I get to think about it longer." I looked at my hands. They were good hands. Forty years of bones had passed through them, and every one of those bones had talked to me, and I had listened, and listening had been the whole architecture of my life. Who was I, deaf? A woman who made pretty carvings. A woman who could no longer hear her mother's voice in the memory-bone, or Tam's last gurgle of laughter, or the low continuous murmur of the honored dead that had kept me company since I was nine years old and lonely beyond describing.

And then I thought of the valley freezing. Of refugees with blackened fingertips. Of half-remembered gods answering children's prayers with cruel literalism because no one had taught them gentleness in eight hundred years of silence. Of Freya, tending beans by day and grieving divinity by night, asking nothing, offering everything.

"Carve me," Freya said again, softer now. She rolled up her sleeve fully and laid her arm across the flat stone at the center of the root-hollow, an altar of living wood worn smooth by hands ten thousand years dead. "And Ulyana — while you cut, I'm going to speak. My true name. Into the bone, through your hands. When you hear it, hold it. Don't let it go. Whatever happens, whatever you hear — hold it."

"What happens when I hear it?"

Her smile was small and crooked and entirely the warden's. "Everything," she said. "Hold everything."

* * *

I took out my tools with hands that did not shake, and I am proud of that, and I am lying about it — they shook, and I made them stop, the way I make everything stop, by giving the shaking somewhere to go.

The first cut was the hardest thing I have ever done.

The chisel touched her skin and sank through it as though skin were a rumor, and met the gold bone beneath, and the bone *sang*. Not pain — or not only pain; she breathed in sharp and held it, brave as a saint — but song, a single clear note that rose out of the wound and filled the hollow, and every root around us leaned toward it like grass toward sun.

And then the voices came.

Because that is what living bone does. It opens. The moment my blade bit into Freya's divinity, the floodgates of the world opened through me — every prayer rising off the frozen world above, every dying whisper, every dead voice in every graveyard between here and the sea, all of it pouring down the Worldroot and into the channel I was carving, and I was not a woman holding a chisel anymore, I was a riverbed, and the river was everyone who had ever hoped anything.

I heard a farmer pray for his cows. I heard a child in Vaskr pray for her brother's fever to break, specific and desperate, naming the fever like an enemy. I heard the drowned boy Tam, laughing, telling me the water wasn't scary once you stopped fighting it. I heard my mother, whose face I barely remembered, humming a lullaby into bone eight hundred years old. I heard gods — real ones, muted ones, pressing their mouths against the inside of their silence like children against frosted windows.

And underneath it all, threading through it, Freya began to speak her name.

I cannot write it. I have tried, in the weeks since, and the ink refuses, and I understand now that this is mercy. It was not a word. It was a *weather*, a season of the soul, a sound that meant green things breaking soil and the patience of gardens and the terrible tenderness of things that grow whether or not anyone watches. Each syllable she spoke, I carved, and the carving took it, and the two halves of the covenant began to drift toward each other above us, drawn by the note, groaning like ships in ice.

That was when the Hollow Choir arrived.

They came down the tunnel like winter itself, and the temperature fell so fast our breath froze midair and fell as crystals. They wore faces — stolen ones, frozen ones, the faces of everyone anyone had ever prayed to in desperation. At their head was a figure wearing my grandmother's face, and it smiled at me with her smile, and sang my name with her voice, and it was perfect and it was nothing like her.

*Marta!* Ivo shouted — I felt it more than heard it, the world becoming vibration — and then the battle was on, and I could not look. I could not look. I carved.

I carved through the sounds of iron on ice. I carved through Marta screaming a word I couldn't parse. I carved through the moment — I will know this moment until I die — when Ivo's knife shattered and he went down laughing, laughing, the mad brave fool, swinging his ruined hand like a club. I carved through Freya's name reaching its final syllable, the one that meant *home*, the one that meant *stay*.

The Choir broke through. Frozen hands closed on my shoulders, my arms, my hair, cold as the bottom of a lake, and they sang into my face, my own prayers given back to me warped and starving, and I understood suddenly what they were: gods who had loved mortals so long without answer that love had curdled into hunger. They didn't want to kill me. They wanted to keep me. Forever, in the ice, where someone would finally sing to them.

The last cut was the deepest. I remember deciding. That is the thing I need the record to show: I decided. No one took it from me. Freya was pinned, Marta was bleeding, Ivo was down, and the covenant halves hung a handspan apart, and the gap between them was shaped exactly like the rest of my life.

*I hear you,* I told the dead, with everything left in me, with the whole of the gift, a farewell forty years in the making. *All of you. I always heard you. Be well. Be witnessed. Go on.*

And I gave it. All of it. The river poured through me one final time and out through the chisel and into the living gold bone, and the two halves of the covenant swung together—

—and the world went white, and then it went silent, and I mean *silent*, silent the way stone is silent, silent as the inside of the earth, and the last sound I ever heard was Freya's name completing itself in a voice that was half goddess and half girl, and it sounded, of all things, like spring.

Then nothing. Hands on my face. A mouth shaping words at me — her mouth, frantic, beautiful — and no sound coming out of it at all.

The Hollow Choir was dissolving. I saw it rather than heard it: figures of ice loosening, softening, running down into pools that reflected light instead of swallowing it, and in the pools, briefly, faces — calm now, unclenched, each one mouthing a name that someone, somewhere, still remembered. Mourned, not destroyed. Freya had promised them that, in the end. Or perhaps I had. The covenant was whole, and it was new, and it carried both mercy and memory in its grain.

I sat down hard on the altar stone, next to the place where I had carved a goddess, and I put my palms flat against the living wood of the Worldroot, and I felt — through my hands, through my bones, through the last channel open to me — the tree's heartbeat. Slow. Enormous. Turning over in its sleep toward warmth.

Above us, impossibly far away, the winter cracked.

I did not hear it break. I felt it, a shudder through the root, a great exhalation, and then Freya's arms were around me and Marta was kneeling at my side pressing my hand to her throat so I could feel her saying something, over and over, and Ivo was propped against a root grinning through bloodied teeth, and none of it made a sound, and all of it, somehow, was the loudest thing I had ever known.

Freya pulled back and looked at me, tears cutting through the dirt on her face, and she spoke slowly, deliberately, shaping each word so I could follow her lips:

*Can you hear me?*

I shook my head. Then I reached up and touched her cheek, and tapped the rhythm against her skin — the old carver's code, knuckle on bone, the language of my hands, which were still mine, which would always be mine.

*No,* I told her. *But stay close. I intend to spend the rest of my life learning yours.*

She laughed — I felt it in her chest where we pressed together, a tremor like the first thaw — and above us, through miles of stone and root and frozen ground turning soft, the long winter of the world began, drop by drop, to melt.

We had a valley to go home to, and elders to face, and a history to drag into the light. But that was tomorrow's work. Tonight, in the heartbeat-dark beneath the first tree, I held onto the only voice I had left — the warm, living pulse under my palm — and I did not let go.
