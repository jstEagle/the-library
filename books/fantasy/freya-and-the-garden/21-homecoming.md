## 21. Homecoming

The thaw reached Thornmere before we did.

We saw it from the ridge above the Ashfall road — a green haze lying over the valley like breath on cold glass, the boundary stones wearing crowns of melting snow. I had left that valley in high summer with frost on my sleeves and my heart already grieving it in advance, the way you grieve someone who is dying slowly and might yet be saved. I came back to water running loud in every ditch, to mud that pulled at our boots with something like welcome, to the smell of wet earth and woodsmoke and, beneath it all, the faint mineral sweetness of stone remembering warmth.

I could not hear the dead anymore. I want to say that first, because everything else in this story is measured against it. The valley was full of voices when I left — not only the dead of Thornmere but the dead of everywhere, carried to me on the wind like seeds. Now there was only wind. When I stood at the ridge and looked down at the rooftops, at the market square where Ivo's cart rattled and Marta walked beside me with her hands folded like a woman attending her own trial, I heard nothing but the ordinary world: cart wheels, a dog, someone laughing badly at a joke.

It should have been a desolation. Some mornings it was. But that morning, standing in the meltwater light with Freya's shoulder warm against mine, it felt like standing in a room after years of never being alone in it — and finding the room beautiful.

"They'll be waiting," Marta said. Her voice was rough. She had not slept properly since Vaskr; none of us had, though for different reasons. "The elders. They've had a week to decide what story to tell."

"Then we'd best give them the true one before they finish deciding," Freya said. She said it lightly, but I felt the weight under it. She had been a goddess once, and she remembered what councils of frightened mortals did with truth: they buried it, or they burned the messenger, or both.

"They won't burn anyone," Marta said. Then, quieter, honest even now, especially now: "I don't think they'll burn anyone. Grandmother always said fear makes people stupid, not cruel. Usually."

*Usually.* The word hung between us like a bird deciding whether to land. Marta had confessed everything at the gate of Vaskr — her family's crest on Ivo's payment, her grandmother Sable's hand on the knife that cut the covenant, the plague hidden, the gods muted, the valley built over the wound like a house built over a grave. She had begged me to come home and let the old gods die quietly. And then, when I chose the road instead, she had done the one thing I had not imagined: she had turned around and followed me into the winter.

She had lost two fingers to frost on that road. She held her bandaged hand against her chest now, the way you hold a thing you are still learning to carry.

"Marta," I said. "You don't have to stand up there with us."

"Yes," she said, "I do. That's rather the whole point."

* * *

They gathered us in the meeting hall, which had never in my life held so many people. Every bench was full, every window ledge, children sitting cross-legged in the aisles because there was nowhere else. Word had traveled ahead of us the way word does — faster than any cart, arriving distorted, arriving enormous. They said the bone-carver had gone to the roots of the world. They said the winter broke because of her. They said she came back deaf. All three were true, and still the versions circulating in the square were stranger than the truth, which is perhaps the most hopeful thing I learned that day about human beings.

The elders sat in their curved row at the front, seven of them, old Aldous Vane in the center with his chain of office dull as ditchwater around his neck. He looked at me across the length of the hall, and I looked back, and I understood that he had spent his week deciding exactly what Marta had predicted: what story to tell.

I decided to take the choice away from him.

I stood before they called me forward. My legs shook. I let them.

"My name is Ulyana Pemberton," I said, and my voice — my plain mortal voice, the only voice I had left — carried further than I expected. Perhaps because everyone was leaning toward it. "For twenty-six years I carved bones for your dead, and I heard their last words while I worked. You knew this. You brought me your drowned children and your mothers and your fathers, and you never asked how the gift survived inside a valley where magic cannot cross the stones. I never asked either. That was our arrangement, yours and mine: I would not look too closely if you would not make me."

Aldous opened his mouth. I kept speaking.

"I have looked closely now. I have seen the wound the boundary stones were set around. Your valley is not holy. It is a scar. A hundred and forty years ago, the elders of Thornmere — led by Sable Halloway, whose blood sits in this hall today — cut the covenant between gods and mortals to hide a sickness the gods refused to cure. They muted every god within reach. They built the stones to keep the silence in. And the silence has been killing both worlds ever since, slowly, the way a snapped bridge kills the towns on either bank." I paused. My hands found each other in front of me, the way they do when I carve, steadying each other. "The winter you feared last summer was not weather. It was the covenant dying. It is mended now. Not restored — *mended*, which is different, and I will explain why, and then the elders will answer questions, and no one will be sent away, and no part of this history will be carved out and buried again. We have done enough burying."

The hall made a sound I had never heard it make — not a gasp, not an outcry, but a long collective exhale, like a held breath finally given permission.

Old Aldous rose. For a moment I thought he would deny it, and I watched the denial assemble itself behind his eyes — I know that look, I had seen it on the faces of the dead a hundred times, the last lie assembling before the tongue commits. But then his gaze went past me, to Marta, standing beside the bench where we sat, upright, her bandaged hand visible, her chin level.

"You are Halloway's granddaughter," he said.

"I am," Marta said. "And I'm here as a witness, not a defender. Everything Ulyana said is true. I can name the elders who voted for the muting. Their names are in my grandmother's ledger, which I have brought, along with the ledger itself." She lifted a satchel onto the bench, and the sound it made — heavy, leather-bound, final — went through the hall like a stone through ice.

What happened after that took four hours and I will not render all of it here, because grief and argument look the same from the outside and you have already endured enough of both in this account. Elders resigned. One elder, Widow Crane, stood and said, in a voice like dry paper, that her own great-grandmother had died in the plague year and that the family had been told the gods simply could not help — and she looked at Marta and said, "You should have let us rage. Rage heals. Silence rots." Marta bowed her head and said, "Yes." Just that. Yes. I have rarely admired anyone more than I did in that moment, watching my oldest friend accept a wound without flinching from it or pretending it wasn't deep.

By dusk, the hall had emptied into the square, where someone — I never learned who — had lit the great bonfire early, weeks ahead of the thaw festival, and people were burning the old founding pageant scripts, the ones with the pretty lies about the valley's holy origins. Freya stood at the edge of the firelight with her hood back, letting the sparks rise past her face, and I went and stood with her, and neither of us spoke, because some silences are not absences. Some silences are full.

* * *

Spring, when it truly settled, made the valley unrecognizable in the gentlest way.

The boundary stones remained — we had argued about this, Freya and I, long into the nights of that first week home. She wanted them pulled down. *A scar should breathe,* she said. But I had stood at the Worldroot; I had carved the new covenant into living bone with my own hands, and I knew what was written there: that gods and mortals would meet freely, but that every threshold needs a door, and doors need frames. So the stones stayed, but we re-consecrated them — Torvald's ember, the last of it, passed from Ivo's lantern to each stone in turn until it guttered out on the seventh, having done its work. Now the stones marked nothing but a crossing. Pilgrims came through them by midsummer. Gods came too, quietly, in ones and twos — small gods, hearth-gods, a river-goddess who asked permission before touching the water. The valley filled with a new kind of traffic, and the children grew up thinking it ordinary, which is the finest thing a miracle can become.

Marta stayed. She took the Halloway house, which half the town had wanted burned and the other half had wanted sealed, and she did neither: she opened its shutters, aired its rooms, and turned her grandmother's study into an archive where the ledger lay open on a stand, the muting-vote recorded in Sable Halloway's own hand, available to anyone who wished to read it. People came. At first they came to stare. Later they came to copy names, to add marginalia, to leave flowers on the desk for reasons I confess I never fully understood — except that I did understand, being a carver of memorials. People forgive faster than we expect, but they need somewhere to put the grief, and grief will find a shelf anywhere.

I kept my workshop. The sign came down and went back up with one word changed: BONE-CARVER, it said now, and beneath it, smaller, *art & remembrance*. No more charms for the dead. I could not hear them; the work would have been hollow, a séance performed by a mute. But the carving itself — my hands had not forgotten, and my hands, it turned out, had never needed the ghosts. They needed only the material: the dense pale weight of it, the grain revealed under the blade like a secret told slowly, the way a curve wants to become a heron or a sleeping child or a knot of interlaced roots. I carved the Hollow Choir's names that autumn, all forty-one of them, recovered from the dissolving song by Freya's memory and Marta's ledger between them, and set them in a ring of stones above the river. Mortals came to mourn them. Gods came too. It was, Freya said, the first funeral the feral gods had ever been given, and she wept at it, and I held her hand and felt her crying without hearing a sound, and it was enough. It was more than enough.

And in the dooryard of my workshop, inside the boundary stones where magic was supposed to end, I planted Freya's garden.

She directed. I dug. That was the whole division of labor, and we honored it religiously. She named things from the other realm — frost-lilies that bloomed black, a bean variety she swore she had tended in Asgard's shadow when she was vast and terrible and wore the sky like a cloak — and I coaxed them into valley soil with compost and stubbornness. Half of it failed. The frost-lilies sulked and died twice. But the beans took, and a creeping vine with silver-veined leaves took, and by midsummer there was a patch of impossible green in the middle of the most rational valley in the world, and children pressed their noses against my fence to look at it, and I let them.

Freya worked it every morning at dawn, barefoot, her hair loose, humming songs in a language that predated language. Watching her, I understood something I had not understood at the Worldroot: she had not lost her godhood in becoming a garden warden. She had *distilled* it. What remained was pure — attention, tending, the slow insistence that living things be allowed to live. Any garden is a covenant. I just hadn't known the word for it until I carved one into the root of the world.

One evening in late summer, a child climbed the fence — little Wren Ostler, six years old, gap-toothed, fearless — and crouched at the edge of the bean rows where I was tying vines to their strings.

"Is it true you used to talk to dead people?" she asked. Children in Thornmere had stopped being careful with me. This was another gift of the honest valley: nobody tiptoed anymore.

"It's true," I said.

"Do they still talk to you?"

"No," I said. And then, because Wren was watching my hands and my hands were reaching for Freya's where she knelt in the next row, soil to the elbows, sun in her hair, smiling at me across the beans with an expression I no longer needed any gift at all to read — I finished it honestly:

"I finally learned to listen to the living."
