## 22. Freya and the Garden

The garden had learned to grow impossible things.

Ulyana noticed it first in the beans — of course the beans, because Freya would have taught the garden divinity through beans before she trusted it with anything grander. They climbed their poles in spirals that turned against the sun, pods striped silver on one side and green on the other, as if each one couldn't decide which realm it belonged to. By midsummer they had stopped pretending. There were roses in the north bed that bloomed with frost still clinging to their petals, cold roses that never wilted and smelled like snowmelt and something older, something like starlight remembered secondhand. There was a patch of wheat near the wall that ripened in three days and sang — very quietly, a sound Ulyana felt more than heard, a hum in her sternum like a held breath. And along the southern fence, where the morning light came first, grew a row of flowers Freya refused to name, blue-black and heavy-headed, that turned to watch people pass.

"They're not watching," Freya had said, when Ulyana pointed it out. "They're listening. There's a difference."

Ulyana had learned the difference too, over a year of learning everything differently.

She sat now on the workshop step with a hare's femur in her lap, turning it in the good afternoon light, and the silence around her was so complete it had texture — a softness, almost, like wool packed into the ears, like snow falling on deep snow. A year ago that silence would have been unbearable. A year ago she had never known a moment without voices: the dead murmuring their last words through every bone she touched, the valley's accumulated grief humming in the walls of her house, her mother's voice rising out of old keepsakes when she least expected it. She had built her whole life inside that noise the way a snail builds its shell, and she had believed, right up until the Worldroot, that she was the noise — that without the dead speaking through her, there would be nothing left of Ulyana Pemberton at all.

The bone was smooth under her thumbs. She carved anyway. That was the thing no one had understood, not Marta, not the elders, not even Freya at first — that the carving had never only been about hearing. It was about the shape of grief given somewhere to go. The dead had needed her hands; but so had the living, who came to her door with a lock of hair or a milk tooth or a name they could no longer say aloud, and left with something they could hold. She still made those things. She simply made them blind now, in the old sense — trusting the grain of the bone, trusting her own two hands, trusting that love did not require proof to be real.

This one was for the Halloran boy's grandmother, who had died in her sleep in the spring, ninety-one years old and unafraid, and whose family wanted a charm with a wren on it because she used to whistle like one while she baked. Ulyana had never heard her whistle. She never would. But Marta had described it — *high and off-key, like the wren was arguing with the tune* — and Marta's hands had moved while she said it, quick and bright, and Ulyana had watched those hands the way she watched everything now, and thought: *there. That's the truth of her. I can carve that.*

She worked the wing feathers into the bone with a burin older than she was, and the sunlight moved across the step, and from the garden came the small sounds she had spent a year teaching herself to hear instead of ghosts.

* * *

Freya gardened the way other people prayed.

Ulyana had watched her enough now to know the liturgy of it: the way she tested soil between two fingers like reading a letter; the way she spoke to seedlings in a language that predated any tongue still spoken, low and rhythmic, less words than weather; the way she stood at the edge of a bed at dusk with her hands open at her sides, doing nothing, being nothing, simply present to the green growing world with an attention so total it looked, from a distance, like worship. Which, Ulyana supposed, it was. The temples were burned or frozen or forgotten. The altars had gone to moss. But a goddess who remembered her name could make an altar of anything, and Freya had chosen dirt.

She was on her knees among the frost-roses now, sleeves pushed up, dirt to the elbows, her dark hair coming loose from its braid in wisps that caught the light. She was talking to them. Ulyana read her lips across the garden — she was very good at it now, could follow Freya's mouth at forty paces — and caught fragments: *— yes, I know, I know, you'll have your winter when it's your turn, greedy thing —* and then laughter, head thrown back, at some joke the roses had apparently made.

That was another thing Ulyana had learned: what laughter looked like from the outside. For most of her life she had experienced laughter primarily as sound, as vibration, as one voice among the many. Now she experienced it whole-bodied. She saw it start in Freya's shoulders before it reached her face. She saw the way it bent her forward over the rose bed until she had to brace a hand in the dirt. And she felt it in her own chest, this answering lift, this warmth that needed no ears at all — and she thought, not for the first time, that the dead had never once made her feel this. The dead had needed things from her. Freya made her laugh back.

Freya looked up then — goddesses always knew when they were watched, even small ones, even garden ones — and her whole face changed, the way it always did, the way it had done since the first morning in the bean rows when Ulyana had come over the wall with a bone knife in her belt and suspicion in her heart and found a woman kneeling in the weeds who remembered being worshipped by three generations of the northern clans.

Freya said something. Ulyana read it easily: *You're staring.*

"You're worth staring at," Ulyana said, and knew from the way Freya's mouth curved that she'd read her lips in return, all the way across the garden. They had gotten shameless about it. Whole conversations carried on across the vegetable beds, silent as prayer, visible as daylight.

Freya beckoned. Ulyana set down the bone, wiped her hands on her apron, and crossed the grass.

* * *

The garden had grown stranger over the year, and neither of them had ever quite decided whether to be worried about it.

After the covenant was reforged — after Ulyana's blood and bone-carving and Freya's true name spoken into living root, after the Hollow Choir dissolved into a chorus of remembered names and the winter broke like a fever — the boundary stones had remained. Thornmere was still Thornmere, warded and quiet, its magic still thin at the edges. But the stones had been recut, and the new covenant ran through them like a new grain through old wood, and things leaked now. Gently. Things crossed.

Hence the roses with frost on them. Hence the singing wheat. Hence, last month, the mushroom ring behind the compost heap that opened — briefly, at dawn, twice — onto a hillside under a sky with two moons, where Freya had gone walking for an hour and come back with dirt on her knees and tears on her face and would say only, "It's still there. Some of it is still there."

And hence the tree.

It had come up in the corner of the garden in late autumn, unbidden, from a pit none of them had planted. It grew fast, the way Otherrealm things grew, and by midsummer it stood taller than the workshop chimney, with bark like grey river-stone and leaves that were green on top and silver underneath, so that when the wind moved through it the whole tree flickered like fish turning in a stream. Freya said it was a seed that had ridden through the reforging on the covenant itself — a piece of the Worldroot, taking root in mortal soil. Ulyana said it was a tree. They were both right, which was, they had agreed, the nature of most true things.

"It's going to drop," Freya said now, standing under it, looking up. Her lips moved and Ulyana read: *First fruit. Today or tomorrow. Watch.*

Ulyana watched. The fruit hung heavy and low, one single globe, skin the color of dawn — that exact color, the color the sky went in the minute before sunrise, which no painter had ever managed and no fruit should have been able to hold. As they stood there it swelled, visibly, the way a note swells.

"Should we—" Ulyana began.

"Wait." Freya's hand found hers without either of them looking. Their fingers laced together with the ease of long practice, and Ulyana felt, as she always felt, the double truth of that hand: calloused like any gardener's, warm like any woman's, and underneath both, faint as a pulse in the deep water, the old vastness, the thing that had once held winters in its palm. She had learned to hold both truths at once. That had been the hardest lesson of the year, harder than lip-reading, harder than silence: that Freya was not a woman pretending to be a goddess, nor a goddess pretending to be a woman, but both entire, simultaneously, the way the tree's leaves were green and silver.

The fruit fell. Freya caught it one-handed, the way she caught everything, and weighed it in her palm, and her expression did something complicated.

"What?" Ulyana asked.

"It's ripe," Freya said slowly. "Which means it wants to be eaten. Things from my side don't ripen unless they want something." She turned it over. Dawn-colored skin, faintly warm. "A fruit from the Worldroot's line, growing in a mortal garden, wanting to be eaten." She looked up, and there was wonder in her face, and underneath the wonder, carefully leashed, hope. "Ulyana. Do you know what this might mean?"

"That we should ask Torvald before we eat mysterious god-fruit?"

"That it might not stop here." Freya's grip tightened. "One crossing is an accident. Two is a leak. But a *seed* — a seed means the boundary isn't just patched, it's *permeable*. Chosen. If the realms can trade life again — small things, gardens, seasons — then the covenant isn't a wall anymore. It's a door. A door with both sides holding the handle." She laughed, that shoulder-first laugh, and pressed the fruit into Ulyana's hands. "I've been tending this garden for a year thinking I was making a memorial. What if I've been making a beginning?"

Ulyana held the fruit. It was warm as a sleeping bird. And she thought of the valley below them, the market square rebuilt, the elders' public reckoning in the spring — old Sorel standing in front of the whole town with the founding records open, reading aloud the truth about the muting, about the plague, about Marta's grandmother, while the valley listened in frozen silence and then, slowly, thawed — and she thought of Marta beside her in that crowd, not defending, not apologizing again, just *witnessing*, which was harder and better than either.

"Then we plant more," Ulyana said.

* * *

The child came at golden hour, the way children always did, appearing at the garden gate as if conjured by the smell of the beans.

She was Wren Halloran's little sister — six years old, gap-toothed, with the family stubbornness written all over her face — and she stood at the gate gripping the top rail and staring at Ulyana with the frank, unblinking audacity of the very young.

"You're the bone lady," she announced.

"I'm the bone carver," Ulyana agreed. "Same thing, mostly."

"Did you make Granny's bird?"

"I did. Wren on a branch. Your brother picked the wood for the setting."

The girl absorbed this, chewing on it along with whatever else she'd carried up the hill. Then, with the directness of someone who has not yet learned which questions cost:

"Do the dead still talk to you?"

The question landed the way it always landed — softly, and heavily at once. Ulyana had answered versions of it all year. The village had taken time to understand; some of them still didn't. Old Ilsa still brought bones to the door sometimes, hopefully, and Ulyana had to shake her head and point to her ear and see the disappointment settle over the old woman's face like frost. The children, though — the children asked plainly, and Ulyana had decided early that they deserved plain answers.

She could have said *no* and left it there. But the girl was watching her with Wren's eyes, and beyond her the valley lay gold and smoke-blue in the evening, chimneys just beginning to kindle, and beyond the valley the world went on turning toward autumn that would come on time this year, and Freya was standing close enough that Ulyana could feel the warmth of her arm.

So Ulyana took Freya's hand.

"No," she said. "They don't. Not anymore." She let the girl sit with that for a breath — because the loss was real, and she would not teach a child that loss could be skipped — and then she smiled. "But I finally learned to listen to the living. Turns out they were saying things all along. We just weren't taught how to hear them."

The girl considered this with tremendous seriousness. Then: "Can you teach me?"

Ulyana laughed — and felt Freya laugh too, through the joined hands, a tremor of shared amusement passing skin to skin, a whole conversation in five fingers. "Come back tomorrow," she said. "Bring your brother. First lesson's free: it's called *watching people's hands.*"

The girl grinned, gap-toothed and total, and pounded down the hill toward the lights of home.

* * *

Evening came on soft. They ate on the step — bread, cheese, the last of the spring honey, and half the dawn-colored fruit split between them, because Freya had decided that anything a garden offered should be accepted, and because it tasted, Ulyana thought, like the smell of the air after the first true rain of spring, like beginning.

Below them the valley kindled, window by window. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere Marta was putting her own children to bed in a house that no longer stood on a lie, and Ivo — who had stayed, who drew maps now of a world with doors in it — was probably in the tavern telling the story of his missing fingers for the hundredth time, each version more heroic than the last. The wheat hummed its low note. The frost-roses opened for the night. In the workshop behind them, on the bench, the finished wren-charm waited to be delivered, and eleven more pieces of bone waited to become somebody's grief given shape, and that work would go on, quiet and human, for as long as Ulyana's hands held out.

Freya leaned her head against Ulyana's shoulder. Her lips moved, and Ulyana didn't need to read them; she felt the words more than saw them, the way she felt the wheat's song, the way she felt everything now — through the body, through the bond, through the patient daily miracle of paying attention.

*I was a goddess,* Freya said, silent against her shoulder. *And I am this. Both are true. You taught me the second one.*

"You taught me the first," Ulyana said aloud, and felt her laugh more than heard it, and held her hand tighter.

Above the garden, the impossible tree stirred though there was no wind, and its leaves turned silver all at once, all together, like a held breath — and at the tip of the highest branch, where no fruit had been that morning, a second bud was already swelling, dawn-colored, waiting.

The garden, it seemed, was not finished growing.
