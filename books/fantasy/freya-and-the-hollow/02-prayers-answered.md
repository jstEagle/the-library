## 2. Prayers Answered

The trouble began with water.

Juniper Vane learned of it the way they learned most things in Corvane: secondhand, overheard, half-believed. They were restocking their stall at dawn—the hour when the night market's last stragglers shuffled home and the day vendors hadn't yet claimed their corners—when two fishwives passed beneath the awning, arguing in the low, urgent voices of people who have decided not to be afraid and haven't quite managed it.

"—dry since the king's grandfather, my gran said so, and now it's running like spring snowmelt—"

"That's not natural."

"Neither is a daughter alive when she should be dead."

They turned the corner before Juniper could hear more, but the words stayed, settling into Juniper's chest like stones dropped into a well. *A daughter alive when she should be dead.* The mother with the grey shawl. The coin warm from her palm. The charm that should have been nothing—a knot of riverstone and thread, a placebo, a kindness, a lie told gently.

Juniper stood very still among their jars of dried lavender and their bundles of harmless twine, and listened to the city breathe.

Corvane at dawn was all smoke and gulls and the groan of cart wheels on cobbles worn smooth by three centuries of commerce. It was a good city for hiding in, if you were the sort of thing that needed hiding. Seven years, and Juniper had learned its rhythms the way they'd once learned coastlines: where the patrols turned, which alleys swallowed sound, how long a shadow lasted on the steps of the tannery in late autumn. Seven years of smallness. Seven years of selling charms that were only charms in the way a lucky rabbit's foot was only a foot.

And now wells were running dry no longer.

Juniper closed the stall early. Their hands were steady. That frightened them more than shaking would have.

* * *

By midday, the stories had multiplied like mold.

The baker on Cordwainer Street swore his oven had never gone cold—not once, not even overnight—and that his bread rose higher than it had in his father's day. A widow on Tanner's Row found her husband's grave plot blooming with crocuses out of season, white flowers pushing up through frozen ground as though winter had simply agreed to step aside. The fountain in Sallow Square, dry and birdless for a generation, ran again overnight, and children who had never seen moving water stood around its rim with their hands out, laughing at the cold.

Each story arrived wrapped in the same word, spoken half-laughing, half-frightened: *prayer.* Someone had prayed. Someone had prayed, and something had answered.

Juniper heard them all from behind counters and market stalls, keeping their face arranged into the mild, forgettable blankness they'd practiced for seven years—the face of a person selling luck-knots and headache salves, nothing more. But inside, beneath the blankness, an old instrument was tuning itself. Juniper could feel it the way a sailor feels weather coming: pressure behind the eyes, a hum in the teeth, the sense of vast attention turning slowly toward one small point on the map.

Toward a knot of riverstone and thread.

Because here was the thing Juniper knew and the fishwives did not: prayers did not answer themselves. Prayer was a door knocked upon. Something on the other side had to open it. And for seven years, the other side of every door in Corvane had been empty—swept clean, sealed shut, walled over by edict and fear and the slow grinding machinery of the Hollow King's peace. The gods were gone. Everyone said so. The temples were museums; the priests were historians; the prayers were habits, mouthed by old women who no longer expected anyone to listen.

Except someone was listening now. And every answered prayer Juniper heard about that day traced back, along some invisible thread, to the moment a desperate mother had pressed a copper into Juniper's hand and asked for anything, *anything*, to save her daughter.

Juniper packed the satchel that evening while the light died amber behind Corvane's rooftops.

It was not a large satchel. That was the discipline of it—seven years of living ready to leave, of owning nothing that couldn't be carried at a walk. Instruments first: the brass compass whose needle pointed not north but toward whatever the holder most needed to find, currently wrapped in wool and pretending to be broken. The folding rule of blackened oak. Six pens, because good pens were impossible to replace and bad ones ruined good work. A stoppered vial of ink mixed with ash from a fire seven years ago, because some maps wanted to be drawn in what had burned.

Then the papers. Blank stock, heavy and cream-colored, worth more than the instruments. And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom, a single sheet Juniper had never been able to burn—a fragment, a corner torn from something larger, showing the edge of a coastline that existed nowhere in this world. Juniper looked at it for exactly as long as it took to feel the old ache, then wrapped it away again.

Last, the charms. Juniper hesitated over the tray of them—riverstone knots, knotted twine, little clay beads fired with salt and patience. Harmless. They had always been harmless. Juniper picked up one of the riverstone knots and held it to the fading light, and felt, faintly, distantly, like warmth through a wall: something on the other side, pressing.

Not asking to be let in. Just letting Juniper know it remembered the way.

"You're going to get us both killed," Juniper murmured to it, and set it down, and left it there.

That was the plan, such as it was. Leave the charms. Leave the stall. Take the name Vane off the ledger of Corvane and become someone smaller, someone newer, in some town far enough south that the frost came late and the king's men came later. It had worked once. It would work again. Juniper repeated this to themselves the way one repeats a rope-bridge litany, and did not examine the small, treacherous part of them that noted the daughter would live, that the crocuses were beautiful, that the children at the fountain had laughed.

Some doors, once you've opened them, stay ajar forever. Juniper had known that seven years ago too, and burned the maps anyway, and called it enough.

* * *

The knock came at the shutter just past midnight.

Three raps, evenly spaced. Not the fist of a neighbor, not the soft scratch of a customer after hours. Juniper lay in the loft above the stall, fully dressed, boots on, satchel already strapped across their chest, and watched the lamplight under the shutter split into four shadows.

"Inquisitor's office." A voice like a filed-down key. "Open by order of the Crown."

There was a protocol for this. Juniper had rehearsed it for seven years without ever expecting to use it: go limp, go stupid, go small. Open the door rubbing sleep from your eyes. Be the charm-seller, the harmless one, the person whose file—if they even had a file—read *peddler, minor, unremarkable.*

Juniper climbed down the ladder, unlatched the shutter, and gave the performance of a lifetime to an audience of lantern-light.

Two of them. The front one wore the grey-and-silver of the Inquisition with the ease of long habit, a lean man with a clerk's face and a clerk's patience, holding a paper that he did not offer to show. Behind him stood a second figure, taller, hooded against the drizzle, and something about the set of that second figure's shoulders made the hair rise on Juniper's neck—not menace, exactly. Recognition held in reserve.

"Vane," the clerk-faced one read from his paper. "Charm-seller, night market, third row by the tanners' gate."

"That's me." Sleep-rough voice. Confused blink. "Is there trouble?"

"There's been a great deal of trouble." He consulted the paper again, though Juniper suspected he knew it by heart. "You sold a charm Tuesday last. Riverstone. To a woman in a grey shawl."

Juniper let the silence stretch a beat too long—the honest pause of a man searching his memory. "I sell a dozen riverstone knots a week. Half of Corvane's mothers buy them. Against fever, against drowning, against bad dreams." A shrug, apologetic. "They're comfort, master inquisitor. Thread and stone. You know the law—I'm licensed. Nothing in them works."

"Nothing in them works," the inquisitor repeated, mildly. And then, without any change of tone at all: "The child recovered. Fever broke in a single night. The physicians at Saint Aldery's are calling it a miracle, and miracles, as I'm sure you know, are classified as unlicensed workings under Edict Nine." He folded the paper away. "We'll need to look at your stock."

"They can come in," said the hooded figure, and stepped forward into the lantern-light, and Juniper's rehearsed calm cracked down the middle like river ice in thaw.

Sera Ilvane. Even demoted, even hooded, even stripped of the silver rank-chain that had once hung at her throat—Juniper had seen her portrait sketched in a broadsheet years ago, back when her name was spoken in the same breath as the king's. *The crown's finest hound,* the engraving had captioned her. She was younger than the portrait, or the portrait had flattered her, or the intervening years had carved her down to essentials: sharp grey eyes, a scar bisecting one eyebrow, and the particular stillness of a person who has stopped believing the thing she is paid to believe and hasn't yet admitted it aloud.

She looked past Juniper into the stall, and her gaze went straight to the tray of charms with the certainty of a hawk that has already marked the hare.

"Those," she said quietly, "are the same work."

"Sera." The clerk-faced one's tone shifted, careful. "The warrant names the seller, not—"

"I know what the warrant names." She was already inside, lifting a riverstone knot between finger and thumb, turning it to the light. Her expression did something complicated and quickly hidden. "I've seen this knotwork before. Years ago. On evidence that was ordered destroyed." She looked up, and those grey eyes fixed on Juniper with terrible, patient interest. "Funny, isn't it. How things survive."

Juniper's mind raced through exits—the window over the dye-house, the culvert behind the tannery, the roofline to the chandler's—all of it useless, because she was standing between Juniper and everything, and because some traitorous part of Juniper wanted, badly, to know what evidence. What case. What piece of the burned past had survived in an inquisitor's memory.

"The stock stays," Sera said, setting the charm down. "All of it. Sealed pending examination. Master Vane will remain in the city and available for questioning." She paused at the shutter, silhouetted against the drizzle. "For questioning," she repeated, with the faintest emphasis, as though translating the sentence into another language entirely. Then both of them were gone, and the rain filled the space where they'd stood.

Juniper stood in the dark of the stall, heart hammering, and understood with perfect clarity that the game had changed. An ordinary inquisitor would have dragged them to the Grey Tower tonight. Sera Ilvane had left a door open—an inch, a crack, a seam—and Juniper had spent seven years learning to read seams.

She wasn't hunting them. Or rather: she was, but she wanted something else more.

It didn't matter. By morning, the order to seal the stall would travel upward, and somewhere above Sera Ilvane there were men who did not leave doors open, and the questions would stop being questions.

Juniper shouldered the satchel, banked the coals, and left the charms gleaming dully in the dark like a field of small sleeping eyes.

* * *

The night market was burning when Juniper came back for the last thing.

They hadn't meant to go back. The last thing—the fragment of coastline in its oilcloth—was already in the satchel; Juniper checked twice. But the route south ran past the tanners' gate, and Juniper told themselves it was caution, wanting to see the shape of the pursuit, wanting to know whether the net was cast wide or narrow. It was neither true nor false. Some part of a cartographer always wants one last look at the terrain before leaving it.

The market was an orange roar against the black sky.

Torches first, then pitch, then the stalls themselves, dry as seven-year paper. Grey-and-silver figures moved through the smoke with methodical calm, dragging crates into the flames, and Juniper stood in the mouth of an alley with fifty other silent watchers and watched seven years of careful smallness go up in sparks. The charm-stalls first. Always the charm-stalls. Then the spice sellers, the candle-dippers, the fortune-teller's painted wagon—guilt by proximity, contagion by association. Somewhere in the crowd a woman was screaming a name over and over, and the sound cut through the crackle of the fire like a needle through cloth.

*They're not looking for me anymore,* Juniper thought, with a strange floating clarity. *They're erasing the place where I was.*

Smoke rolled down the alley, thick with lavender and char. Juniper pulled their collar over their nose and turned south—

—and stopped.

At the edge of the firelight, apart from the other watchers, stood a child.

Small, maybe eight years old, barefoot on freezing cobbles, in a smock too big and too clean for the night market, as though the child had been dressed by someone who didn't understand clothes. Hair pale as ash. And the child was not watching the fire. The child was watching Juniper—with an absolute, unblinking attention that had nothing childish in it at all, and when the child spoke, the voice that came out was layered, many-voiced, a chord instead of a note, old as wells and winter:

"*Door-maker.*"

The word went through Juniper like a blade finding the gap in armor. Around them, the crowd stirred, wept, shouted at the flames—but the child's voice reached Juniper alone, pitched beneath the noise, meant beneath the noise.

"*You closed your eyes. We kept the road. The road remembers, door-maker. Come and see what remembers you.*"

The child tilted their head, and in the firelight Juniper saw that their eyes reflected the blaze wrong—not orange but a deep, shifting green, like light through water, like something seen from underneath.

Down the row, a grey-and-silver helmet turned. A torch swung. The chord-voice fell silent, and the child simply walked backward into the dark between two burning stalls, unhurried, as though the fire were a curtain and not a wall, and was gone.

"Hey—you there! In the alley!" Boots on cobblestone. More than one pair.

Juniper ran.

They ran the way they had run seven years ago, through routes their feet remembered better than their mind did—down the dye-house stairs, along the culvert's lip, through the gap in the chandler's fence that wouldn't exist by summer. Behind them the market roared and the screaming woman screamed, and ahead the southern road waited, dark and empty and going anywhere but here.

But the child's voice rode with them, layered and patient, keeping pace beneath the drum of their own heartbeat:

*Come and see what remembers you.*

Juniper reached the south gate as the sky went grey with a dawn that smelled of smoke, and stood a long moment in the shadow of the wall, satchel strap cutting into their shoulder, watching the road unwind toward hills they had once mapped and sworn never to draw again.

Every instinct said south. Smaller towns, slower inquisitors, a new name, a new smallness. The plan. The rope-bridge litany.

Instead, Juniper Vane turned north, toward the hills, following a feral child and a chorus of dead gods—and told themselves, as the gate guards waved them through without a glance, that it was only to learn what the child knew. Only to understand the shape of the trap before choosing which direction to run.

The land under their boots hummed, faint and familiar, like a string plucked years ago and still ringing.

It knew the way.
