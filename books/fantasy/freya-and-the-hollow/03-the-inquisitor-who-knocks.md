## 3. The Inquisitor Who Knocks

The summons came at the hour when Corvane's bells forgot themselves.

Sera Ilvane was awake anyway. She was always awake at that hour now—had been for three years, since the demotion, since they took her office with its leaded windows and gave her a desk in the records annex where the ink froze in winter and the cases went to die. She sat by the candle with her coat still on, because a woman who had nothing left to lose could at least keep the habit of readiness, and read the summons twice.

*By order of the Office of Inquiry: Inquisitor Ilvane is assigned to the matter of unlicensed thaumaturgy, Night Market district. Locate the charm-seller. Report directly to Undersecretary Voss.*

Report directly to Voss. Not to her own chain of command. Not through the usual channels, the slow grinding mills of the bureaucracy she had once served so well that they'd ground her along with everything else.

She turned the paper over. Blank. Of course it was blank. Nothing in Corvane carried an explanation anymore; explanations were one of the first things the edicts had eaten. Seven years of decrees issued in the king's name, each one stranger than the last—the closing of the archives, the burning of genealogies, the prohibition on naming the old gods even in complaint—and each one signed with the same careful hand, the same royal seal, and never once accompanied by a reason. When Sera had asked for reasons, early on, earnestly, in the way that had made her the crown's finest inquisitor by thirty, the answers had come back not as arguments but as weather. Cold fronts. A transfer. A reassignment. A door quietly locked.

She had learned to stop asking. She had not learned to stop noticing.

Now they wanted her to find a charm-seller.

She dressed properly for the first time in months—boots polished, coat brushed, the grey sash of the Office knotted at her shoulder where it belonged rather than stuffed in a drawer. If they wanted an inquisitor, they would get one. Whatever game Voss was playing, whatever trap this assignment was baited with, she would walk into it with her eyes open and her hands steady. That much of herself they had never managed to take.

Outside, Corvane was grey in the way it was always grey now—not the grey of weather but of subtraction, as if someone had gone down the streets with a sponge and lifted the color out of shutters and awnings and children's coats. She had stopped being able to say when it started. That frightened her more than anything: that forgetting could be so gentle you mistook it for normalcy.

The Night Market was already breathing when she arrived, lanterns strung between stalls like low constellations, the smell of fried dough and tallow and wet stone. She did not go in as a hunter. She went in as a buyer, hood up, walking the rows slowly, letting the market's rhythm wash over her. A woman selling luck-knots that were only knots. A man with dried herbs and a patter about vigor. And everywhere, threaded through the ordinary commerce, the small furtive economy of hope—the charms sold in whispers, the amulets passed palm to palm, all of them harmless, all of them dead, because magic itself had been outlawed and, more to the point, magic itself had *stopped*, seven years ago, everywhere, all at once, like a river deciding it was finished with its bed.

Except that it hadn't stopped. Not anymore.

That was why she was here. Three days ago the reports had begun arriving at the annex, and she had been the one filing them, because dead files went to the annex too. A well in the Tanner's Quarter that had run dry for years suddenly sweet and brimming. A widow's garden blooming out of season, roses in the frost. A fevered child whose mother swore the girl woke whole after sleeping with a paper charm beneath her pillow. Each report dismissed by her superiors as rumor, hysteria, fraud. Each one filed under *unverified*. But Sera had spent ten years learning to read reports the way other people read faces, and every one of these had the same texture: true, terrified, and pointing, all of them, toward the Night Market.

Someone was selling working charms. In Corvane. Under the Hollow King.

She found the stall on her second pass—or rather, she found the queue, which was worse. Six people standing in the cold outside a shuttered booth at the market's edge, waiting their turn with the guilty patience of the desperate. An old man clutching coins. Two sisters whispering. A young father who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes.

The stall opened. A figure inside—slight, hooded, face shadowed—spoke briefly with the old man, took his coins, pressed something small into his hand. The transaction lasted less time than buying bread.

Sera didn't approach. She watched instead, and what she watched for was the moment the old man walked away and stopped, mid-stride, and stood very still under a lantern with his fist closed around whatever he'd been given, and his shoulders began to shake.

Not grief. She knew grief's shape. This was something else—this was a man feeling something he had believed impossible, and having no room in his body left to hold it.

She waited until the queue emptied and the hooded figure began shuttering the booth, and then she moved—but not forward. Sideways, into the shadow of the dye-merchant's awning, because across the lane, two figures in the white-and-slate livery of the Office of Inquiry had entered the market, and they were not browsing.

They were sweeping. Methodically, stall by stall, the way she herself had once swept dens of smugglers in the river districts. Her own colleagues. Her own methods. Someone else had read the reports too, or heard them, and someone above them had decided the charm-seller was worth finding before Sera could file anything.

Which meant this was not an assignment. It was a race, and she had been handed a losing position on purpose.

She watched the inquisitors work. She watched the charm-seller's booth go dark, the figure slipping away through the crowd with the unhurried gait of someone who had done this many times, and she made her choice the way she had stopped making choices years ago—quickly, entirely, without asking permission.

She followed the seller, not the inquisitors.

* * *

The alleys behind the Night Market were a geography of evasion, and whoever she followed knew it intimately. Left at the cistern, right through the gap between the tannery and the wall where the boards had been pried loose and replaced so often they looked decorative, down a stair that wasn't on any city plan Sera had ever memorized—and she had memorized all of them, once, in another life. The figure moved without hurry, which told her more than speed would have. This was not flight. This was routine. Seven years of routine, worn smooth as a stair-stone.

She let them think they'd lost her. Then she stepped out of the doorway ahead of them, into the narrow throat of the alley where the walls leaned close enough to touch both sides at once, and said, quietly:

"The Tanner's Quarter well. The widow Brandt's garden. The child on Coopers' Row."

The hood came up. Beneath it was a face she couldn't have described afterward—young and old at once, sharp-boned, watchful, the face of someone who had practiced being unremarkable until the practice became architecture. Their eyes went from her face to her sash and back, and Sera saw the exact moment they recognized what the grey knot of cloth meant. Saw, too, the strange absence of fear that followed it. Recognition, calculation, and then something almost like resignation—as if they had known this meeting was coming for seven years and had only been negotiating the date.

"Inquisitor," the charm-seller said. Voice low, level, genderless in the dark. "You're a long way from the annex."

"You know the annex?"

"I know where dead files go." A pause. "I know what your office does with questions."

Sera kept her hands visible and empty, an old courtesy between professionals. "Three days ago I filed a report on the answered prayers in this district. This morning I was ordered to find the charm-seller responsible. Tonight two of my colleagues are sweeping the market for the same person. I'd like to know which of those things happened first, and who decided it."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because I've spent three years watching my office bury things instead of investigating them, and I would very much like to know if I'm holding a shovel or a torch."

Something moved behind the seller's eyes—surprise, maybe, or its cousin. They glanced past her, down the alley, listening to the far-off sounds of the market still turning over, and when they spoke again their voice had dropped lower.

"You were ordered to find me. So find me. Say you did. Take the credit, collect whatever they're paying, and go home."

"That's not how I work."

"No," the seller said softly. "It isn't, is it. Sera Ilvane." Her name, spoken back to her in the dark, landed like a stone dropped down a well—she listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing. "I asked around, after the demotion. Old habit. You were the one who closed the Marrowfield smuggling ring. The one who found the missing tithe-fleet. The one who—" the seller stopped, and something flickered across their face, some thought arriving and being refused entry, "—the one who asked about the king's decrees."

"And got demoted for it."

"And got demoted for it," the seller agreed. "So you understand the arithmetic. Questions cost. Around here, lately, they cost more than answers do."

From somewhere beyond the rooftops came a sound that ended the conversation before Sera could answer it: a bell, ringing wrong. Not the hour bell, not the alarm bell—a third sound, ragged, urgent, the kind of ringing that happened when someone pulled the rope without authority. Then voices. Then, faintly, the smell of smoke threading down the alley, thin and acrid, wrong for cooking fires.

The Night Market was burning.

The charm-seller went rigid. For one unguarded instant Sera saw through the practiced stillness to what lay underneath—seven years of careful invisibility catching fire in real time—and then the mask came back down, though slower than before, and the eyes that met hers were no longer calculating. They were asking.

"They'll sweep the alleys next," Sera said. "Standard procedure. Perimeter first, then the grid. We have perhaps a quarter hour before this street fills with white-and-slate."

"*We*," the seller repeated.

"I'm not arresting you." It came out of her flat and final, a door closing on her own hesitation. "I should. It's my duty, and three years ago I'd have done it and slept well. But I was forbidden to close a case once—told to seal the evidence and forget it—and the evidence in that case matches the work you sell so exactly that I've been sick since I read the reports. So either you tell me what I want to know, or we both take our chances with the inquisitors. Those are the roads."

For a long moment the alley held its breath. Smoke drifted between them. Somewhere close, boots struck cobbles in the disciplined rhythm of a search line forming.

Then the charm-seller did something Sera had not expected. They laughed—one short, exhausted note, without any humor in it at all.

"A case I was forbidden to close," they echoed. "You and everyone else in this cursed city. Fine. Ask your question, inquisitor. Quickly."

But the question never got asked, because that was the moment the child stepped out of the wall.

There was no better way to describe it. One heartbeat the alley was empty behind the seller; the next, a small figure stood there in the lee of the stones, barefoot despite the frost, wrapped in a coat three sizes too large, watching them both with eyes that caught the distant firelight and held it wrong—held it the way water holds the moon, whole and trembling and older than the light that made it. A child of eight, maybe nine. Filthy, feral, utterly calm.

And speaking in a voice that was not a child's voice, that was not one voice at all but many braided together, layered like bells heard through deep water:

"*Door-maker.*"

Sera's hand went to her knife and stopped there, useless, because the word had not been aimed at her. The charm-seller had gone white as bone.

"We remember you," the child said, and the many voices folded over one another, tender and terrible. "*We remember the hand that drew the road. The door stands hungry, door-maker. The crown is hollow. The hollow thing is waking, and it remembers you too.*"

Down the alley, the search line's boots were coming closer, steady as a clock. The fire-glow strengthened against the rooftops. And Sera Ilvane, disgraced inquisitor of Corvane, stood in the narrowing space between a hunted charm-seller and a god-speaking child, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever case she had been forbidden to close had just opened itself—and it had been waiting for her all along.
