## 5. What the Child Remembers

Wren remembered the temple before it was eaten.

Not with their own eyes—Wren's eyes had only ever seen Corvane, the grey streets, the market stalls, the faces of people who looked through them like they were a stray dog. The memory belonged to someone else. It had been given, or left behind, or dropped like a coin from a pocket, and Wren had picked it up because that was what Wren did. They picked up everything. Every prayer that went unanswered, every name that slipped loose from its owner's tongue and lay dying in the gutters of the city. Wren gathered them the way other children gathered stones.

The memory was warm. That was the first thing Wren knew about it, before they knew anything else about it at all. Warmth, and the smell of beeswax candles, and a sound like many voices breathing together.

*The temple of Vessia-of-the-Turning-Year.*

Wren sat in the dark under the fishmonger's awning, knees pulled to their chest, and let the memory open like a door.

Stone floors worn smooth by ten thousand pilgrim feet. A ceiling painted with the year itself—a great wheel of seasons turning around a golden hub, spring's green giving way to summer's gold, autumn's rust, winter's white, and then green again, forever, because that was the whole point of Vessia: nothing ended, everything turned. Priests in robes the color of new leaves sang the morning song, and their voices rose into the dome and came back down changed, richer, as if the temple itself were singing along.

And beneath the song, beneath everything, *she* was there. Not a body. Not a face. A presence like the moment when winter finally loosens its grip—felt in the bones before it is understood by the mind. Vessia-of-the-Turning-Year, who had been worshipped in that valley for two thousand years, whose name every child learned before they learned their mother's.

Wren held the memory up to the light of their own small mind and examined it, the way they examined all the things they carried.

Then they held the second memory beside it. The one that was not warm.

*The same temple, later.*

The wheel on the ceiling still turned—or seemed to. But the priests were gone. The candles had burned down to stubs no one replaced. And the presence beneath the song was—

*Thin.* Stretched. Like a voice calling from very far away, like a fire burning its last log. Vessia was still there, but she was *smaller*, and Wren did not know yet that a god could be smaller, could be diminished the way a river diminishes in drought, and the knowing would come later and it would hurt worse than anything.

And then the third memory. The last one. The one Wren kept at the very bottom of themselves, wrapped in all the other memories like a coal buried in ash, because looking at it directly made something behind Wren's ribs scream.

*The temple, empty.*

Not abandoned. Empty the way a shell is empty. The wheel on the ceiling had stopped mid-turn, frozen between autumn and winter, and the paint was flaking, and where the altar had been there was only a shape of clean stone—cleaner than the stone around it, polished smooth, as if something had licked it. No dust anywhere in the building. Dust settles everywhere; Wren had learned that much from watching the world. But in that temple there was no dust, no cobweb, no beetle, no moss in the cracks. Nothing lived there anymore, not even the small things.

Something had eaten Vessia-of-the-Turning-Year. Eaten her temples, eaten her songs, eaten the two thousand years of her name—and had left the building standing because buildings have no taste.

Wren closed the memory and hugged their knees tighter.

Under the fishmonger's awning, Corvane went on being Corvane. Carts rattled past. A woman argued about the price of eels. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and the bell sounded wrong tonight, thinner than it should, and Wren wondered if anyone else noticed that sounds were going thin all over the city, like cloth worn through at the elbows.

They had been following the charm-seller for three days.

That was how Wren thought of them—not Juniper Vane, not cartographer, not any of the names the whispers used. The charm-seller. The one whose stall smelled of cedar and hot wax, whose hands moved over little carved tokens the way a mother's hands move over sleeping children. Wren had watched them from rooftops and doorways, from the gaps between crowds, the way Wren watched everything: hungry, patient, unseen.

But the whispers had names for the charm-seller too. The whispers came from everywhere now—the drains, the old wells, the mouths of statues whose gods were gone. *Door-maker,* the whispers said. *The one who drew the road. The one who opened the way. Find the door-maker.*

Wren did not understand what door. Wren understood only this: the thing that ate Vessia had come from somewhere. Roads go both ways. And the whispers wanted the door-maker found, and Wren had learned long ago that when the voices wanted something, it was usually because it mattered.

So Wren followed. Three days through the night market's smoke and noise, watching the charm-seller sell their quiet charms to quiet people, waiting for the moment the whispers meant.

The moment came tonight, and it came like a struck bell.

Wren had been crouched on the awning above the charm-stall when the desperate woman bought the token for her sick daughter, and Wren had felt it happen—felt the charm *wake*, felt the old power move through it like water finding an old channel, felt the sick girl across the city draw a breath that went all the way down for the first time in weeks. Felt, too, the charm-seller's fear, sharp and sudden as a snapped thread.

And felt, underneath everything, far away and getting closer, a vast attention turn toward the city like an eye opening.

After that, everything happened quickly, the way endings do.

*

The inquisitors came at moonrise.

Wren heard them before anyone—their boots, their iron, the particular silence of men who have been given permission. They moved through the night market in a wedge, torches unlit, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a keel, and Wren, flattened against a rooftop gutter, watched them stop at the charm-stall and find it already empty, the seller gone, the cedar smell still hanging in the air like a confession.

Then the torches came out, and the burning began.

Wren did not stay to watch. Watching was a luxury for people with nothing to carry. Wren had a city full of voices in their head and one task in their chest, and the task said: *the door-maker is running, and you must run faster, because the thing behind the king has noticed, and when it notices, it sends riders.*

Wren went over the rooftops the way water goes downhill—without thought, without hesitation, along paths their body knew even though Wren had never consciously learned them. That was another thing the voices gave: knowledge without origin. Wren knew the city's bones the way they knew their own hands. Knew which walls had handholds worn into them by centuries of climbers. Knew which roofs would hold and which would groan. Knew, most importantly, the way out.

Because every city has a wound, and Corvane's wound was old.

It had been a gate once, before the Hollow King's grandfather walled it up—a postern gate cut into the cliff beneath the old quarter, leading down and out through the rock to the river gorge. The kings had sealed it because sealed things are easier to rule. But stone forgets slower than kings, and the voices remembered, and so Wren knew: behind the tannery, below the collapsed stair, there was a door in the cliff face hidden behind forty years of ivy and rubble, and the lock on it had been broken since before Wren was born.

Wren dropped from the last roof into the alley behind the charm-stall's escape route—an alley that smelled of tallow and wet stone—and found them there.

Both of them. Which was wrong, according to everything the whispers said. The charm-seller, satchel clutched to their chest, backed against the wall with a knife held low and steady. And facing them, hand on her sword but not drawing it, a woman in the grey-and-black of the Inquisition—but wrong somehow, the uniform worn at the cuffs, the insignia scratched half away, the stance of someone standing *beside* the prey rather than over it.

"You," the charm-seller breathed, staring at Wren. "You're the child from the market. You've been—"

"Following you," Wren said. Their voice came out layered, the way it always did when the voices pressed close—three tones braided into one, a child's voice with older voices underneath it like rocks under shallow water. "Three days. You walk like someone reading a map nobody else can see."

The inquisitor's hand tightened on her sword. "God-touched," she said, and the word came out strange—not disgust, not quite. Recognition.

"The market's burning," Wren said. "They'll sweep the alleys next. Then the river quarter. I know a way out. Both of you, or neither—the gate doesn't care, but I do."

"Why?" The charm-seller's eyes were wide, darting between Wren and the inquisitor. "Why help us? Who sent you?"

Wren tilted their head. How to explain? *The dead gods whisper to me through drain-grates. Something ate them and it wears your king like a glove, and the voices say you're the one who made its road.* Instead Wren said only:

"They call you door-maker."

The effect was immediate and terrible. The charm-seller went white—not pale, *white*, the color draining from their face like dye from cloth in rainwater—and the knife dipped, and for one long moment they stared at Wren as if Wren were a ghost, or a mirror, or a debt come due.

"I don't know what you mean," they whispered. It was a lie. Everyone in the alley knew it was a lie. Even the inquisitor knew it, and her expression shifted—calculation, decision, resignation—like clouds crossing a cold moon.

"We can stand here discussing it while the inquisitors arrive," she said flatly, "or we can move. Child—which way?"

"Wren. My name is Wren." Nobody had asked before. Wren said it twice, silently, just to feel the weight of it. "This way. Don't stop for anything. If you hear singing, don't answer it."

"What sings?" the charm-seller asked.

"Nothing good," said Wren, and ran.

*

Behind them, the night market burned.

Wren heard it more than saw it—the crackle of flame taking the canvas stalls, the crash of a collapsing awning, shouts, and beneath it all the thin iron voices of inquisitors calling orders. Once, glancing back from the mouth of the tannery lane, Wren saw the glow reflected orange on the wet cobblestones, and above it sparks rising like reversed snow, and a line of hooded figures moving through the smoke with lanterns, checking doorways, hunting.

Hunting the charm-seller. Hunting, though they didn't know it yet, the god-touched child. The Hollow King's men had orders about the god-touched. Wren had heard those orders whispered through a drain-grate two winters ago, spoken by a man washing blood off his gloves: *the voices in them are contraband. Burn them out or bring them in.*

Wren ran faster.

Through the tannery yard, past the lime pits with their terrible sweet stink, down the collapsed stair where the steps crumbled to gravel underfoot. The charm-seller kept pace—better than Wren expected, with the sure-footedness of someone who had walked a thousand wild places—and the inquisitor ran behind them both, sword drawn now, watching the darkness at their backs.

"Here." Wren stopped at the base of the cliff wall, where the ivy hung thick as hair. "Help me pull."

Beneath the ivy: stone. Beneath the stone's false face: the gap. Wren set their shoulder against the slab and pushed, and the voices pushed with them—Wren felt them pour strength down their arms, dozens of faded presences lending what little weight they had left—and the slab ground aside with a sound like a grinding tooth, revealing a black throat of tunnel sloping down into the rock.

Cold air breathed out of it. River-smell. Old air, air that hadn't been breathed in forty years.

"It goes under the wall," Wren said. "Down to the gorge. There's a path on the far side. The inquisitors don't know it exists. The kings made sure no maps showed it." A glance at the charm-seller, whose face had gone strange again. "No maps at all."

"That's not possible," the charm-seller murmured. "I mapped these cliffs. Seven years ago. There was no gate here."

"There was no gate on your map," Wren said simply. "Things exist whether they're drawn or not. That's the first thing the voices taught me."

The inquisitor looked back up the alley one last time. The fire-glow was brighter now, spilling orange into the lane, and somewhere close—too close—boots on stone. She sheathed her sword, took the charm-seller's arm, and stepped down into the dark.

Wren went last. At the threshold they paused, turned, and looked back at the burning market one final time—the sparks, the smoke, the hooded figures moving through it like beetles through a ruined nest—and spoke aloud, though no living person could hear, in the braid of voices that was theirs alone:

*"Hold on. I'm bringing them to you. Whoever's left of you—I'm bringing the door-maker home."*

From somewhere far away—farther than the city, farther than the hills, from wherever diminished gods wait in the thin places of the world—something answered. Not words. Just warmth, faint as a candle seen across a winter field.

Vessia's warmth. Or what remained of it.

Wren slid the stone slab shut behind them, and the dark took them all.

*

The tunnel was long, and cold, and dripped.

They walked by touch and by Wren's certainty, which never wavered even when the passage forked, even when it narrowed until they had to turn sideways, even when the charm-seller's breathing went ragged and the inquisitor muttered curses under her breath with the fluency of long practice. Hours passed, or what felt like hours. Time moved strangely underground, in the dark, among old stones.

It was the charm-seller who broke first, as Wren had known they would. People always broke in tunnels. Tunnels asked questions.

"Child. Wren." The voice in the dark, low and careful. "These voices you hear. The ones who call me door-maker. What exactly do they say?"

Wren considered the question while their feet found the next step, and the next.

"They say a road was drawn," Wren said. "A road between worlds. Drawn by the greatest cartographer the realm ever produced, hired by a young king to chart the spaces between. And the road went both ways, and something walked it, and the something was hungry." A pause. Dripping water. "They say the door-maker burned the maps and ran and hid. But roads can be redrawn. Doors can be reopened. And the thing behind the crown has spent seven years trying to find the hand that drew it."

Silence, except for dripping, and footsteps, and the inquisitor's controlled breathing.

"And you believe them," the charm-seller said at last. "The voices. You believe what they say."

"I believe the temples are empty," Wren said. "I believe the gods are almost gone. I've carried Vessia's last morning in my head since I was small enough to fit in a bread oven. I remember her temple full of song, and I remember it empty, and something ate the space between." Their voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and the braid of voices behind it trembled. "So yes. I believe them."

More silence. Then, quietly, from the inquisitor: "What temple?"

"Vessia-of-the-Turning-Year. In the Callow Vale. Two hundred miles east." Wren glanced back, though there was nothing to see. "You'd know it. Everyone used to know it."

"No one knows it," the inquisitor said slowly. "There's no such place. I'd swear on anything you like—there has never been a temple of Vessia in the Callow Vale. I grew up near the Callow Vale."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "You did. And it was there when you were small, and you've forgotten it, and you don't even know you've forgotten, because forgetting feels exactly like remembering nothing at all." They put their hand against the tunnel wall, cold stone under a cold palm. "That's how it eats. It doesn't burn things down. It eats them *out*. The temple stays. The name goes. The memory goes. The god goes last, because gods are mostly memory anyway."

The dripping filled the pause.

"How much is left of her?" the charm-seller asked. Barely a whisper.

"A voice," Wren said. "A warmth. Maybe a name, if someone says it right." They started walking again. "That's why the voices want you, door-maker. Not to punish you. To *use* you. You drew the road in. You're the only one alive who could draw it shut."

Ahead, faint but unmistakable, the tunnel mouth opened onto grey pre-dawn light and the sound of the river gorge, and Wren quickened their pace, and behind them the other two followed—one fleeing, one falling, one carrying a city's worth of grief in a body barely bigger than a bread oven.

The light grew. The air sweetened. And as they emerged onto the ledge above the roaring gorge, Wren felt it again—that vast, patient attention turning toward them from the direction of the palace, from the direction of the throne, searching the city street by street for a charm-seller, a disgraced inquisitor, and a child full of stolen prayers.

It had not found them yet.

But it had begun to look.
