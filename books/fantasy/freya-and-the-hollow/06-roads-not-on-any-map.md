## 6. Roads Not on Any Map

The road out of Corvane ended three miles past the walls, the way all roads did now. Juniper felt it go—felt the paving stones beneath their boots thin into suggestion, then into nothing, as though the king's edicts reached even into the earth and unmade what it disliked. Beyond that point there was only grassland gone to seed, rolling north in grey-green waves toward hills that no map had named in seven years.

Juniper had made those maps once. They tried not to think about that.

Behind them, Sera walked with the stiff, deliberate stride of a woman who had spent her life in cities and resented every mile of this one. Her inquisitor's coat was gone—burned with the night market, along with Juniper's stall, their awning, their little brass scale for weighing charms—and without it she looked younger and more dangerous, all sharp angles and watchful eyes. She carried a stolen horseman's mace and said almost nothing.

Wren bounded ahead through the wet grass like something born of it, bare feet black with mud, occasionally stopping to crouch over a stone or a fencepost and whisper to it. The child spoke to everything. Most of it answered, if you knew how to listen. Juniper was trying very hard not to know how to listen.

"Door-maker." Wren turned, walking backward, hair full of burrs. "The land is *loud* out here."

"Loud how?" Sera asked.

Wren frowned, searching for words too big for a mouth that small. "Like a room where everyone's been told to be quiet for years, and someone just opened the door."

Juniper kept walking. The satchel against their hip held the instruments they'd saved from the burning: dividers, a plumb line weighted with river-iron, three stubs of charcoal wrapped in oilcloth, and a folded square of vellum so old its edges had gone soft as cloth. They hadn't opened the vellum. They would not open the vellum. There were things drawn on it—or there had been, before the fire—and some part of them understood that opening it would be like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt.

It still hurt. That was enough knowing.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen boundary marker, a granite post carved with letters worn to ghosts. Wren touched each ghost-letter and giggled at whatever the stone said back. Sera built a fire with the economy of someone who'd done field work before her promotion, and sat sharpening the mace's edge with a whetstone she produced from somewhere impossible, the rhythm of it steady as a heartbeat.

"You should sleep," she said, without looking up.

"I will."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the horizon like it owes you money." She tested the edge with her thumb. "I've interrogated forty people, Vane. I know deflection when I see it sitting across a fire from me."

"Then you know I won't answer."

"I know you'll answer eventually. Everyone does. It's just a question of how uncomfortable I make the waiting." But there was no cruelty in it, only tiredness, and after a moment she went back to the whetstone and let the silence stand between them like a third traveler.

Juniper lay back and watched the first stars come out over the hills, and that was when the land began to speak.

---

It started as a hum behind the eyes, faint as a fly in another room. Then the hum found a rhythm, and the rhythm found a shape, and the shape was a road.

Juniper sat up so fast the blood rang in their skull.

There—a hundred paces east, running straight through the empty grass where an hour ago there had been nothing—there was a road. Not a track, not a game trail. A *road*, wide enough for two carts, edged with cut stones furred in moss, running arrow-straight toward the northern hills until the dark swallowed it. Wagons moved along it in Juniper's vision, lanterns swinging, voices carrying thin and clear across a distance that could not exist: a woman laughing, a man calling a name—*Tamsin! Tamsin, mind the axle!*—and the smell of bread baking somewhere out of sight, warm and specific and impossibly present.

Nobody else saw it. Sera was watching the dark; Wren was asleep in a nest of Sera's spare cloak. The road existed only for Juniper, laid over the world like ink on oiled paper.

Because it wasn't a road anymore. It was the *memory* of a road, sunk into the ground the way a name sinks into a grave-marker, and Juniper's gift—the one they had spent seven years drowning—had surfaced without permission and was reading it like a signpost.

*The King's Way North,* whispered something in the back of Juniper's mind, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like their own, younger and prouder. *Surveyed in the spring of the old calendar by—*

Juniper clamped down on it. Shut their eyes. Breathed the way they'd taught themselves to breathe, slow and shallow, keeping the surface of the mind flat as still water.

When they opened their eyes again, the road was gone. The grass was only grass.

But something else was gone too.

They searched for it the way you search for a word on the tip of your tongue, probing the shape of the absence. Their mother's face—they still had that. Corvane's streets, the night market, the smell of charm-smoke—that was intact. Their apprenticeship, the guild hall, the first map they'd ever sold—

The name of their first master. The cartographer who'd taken them in off the docks, fed them, taught them to hold a pen like a compass needle. Juniper knew he had existed. Knew his hands, his cough, the way he'd tapped twice on any map that pleased him. But his *name*—the two syllables they must have heard ten thousand times—was simply not there. A smooth blank where a word should be, like a patch of wall where a painting used to hang.

Juniper sat very still in the cold, and did the arithmetic they had been refusing to do for years.

The visions had been coming for a long time. Not like this—not since the gods woke—but small ones, for years. A flash of a bridge that wasn't there while crossing a real one. A moment on a stairwell in Corvane where they'd suddenly seen the building around them as it was sixty years ago, candles and voices and a wedding party long dead. They'd told themselves it was fatigue. Nerves. The price of hiding, paid in odd coin.

But the gaps in their memory weren't new either. They'd blamed the fire. Of course they had—seven years ago they'd burned everything, and smoke does strange things to a mind. Convenient, that. Blame the fire, blame the fear, blame anything except the truth standing in front of you:

Every time the land showed you a road, it took a toll. And you had been paying it in yourself.

*How many?* Juniper wondered, lying rigid under the indifferent stars. *How many pieces have I already traded away without watching the scale?*

And underneath that, quieter, in the voice of the person they were trying never to be again: *And why does the land trust you with its roads at all, door-maker? What did you do to earn that?*

---

Dawn came up the colour of skimmed milk. They walked.

By midmorning Juniper stopped pretending they couldn't feel it. The land here was saturated—layer on layer of erased history pressed flat beneath the king's seven-year silence, and now that the gods were waking, the pressure was lifting, and everything rose at once. Walking through it was like wading through a flooded archive.

"There," Wren said suddenly, pointing. "Again. You went all *far away*. Your eyes went silver."

"They did not."

"They did," Sera said. "Both times since we crossed the old milestone. You stopped walking and stared at nothing, and then you came back looking like you'd been robbed." She planted herself in Juniper's path. "What are you seeing?"

"Nothing."

"Vane."

"*Nothing,*" Juniper said, and pushed past her, and immediately regretted it, because pushing past her meant stepping off the sheep-track, and the moment their boot touched open ground the land *spoke* again.

This time it took them whole.

One instant: grey morning, wet grass, Sera's voice snapping their name. The next: a market town spread below them from a hilltop, complete and blazing with life—two thousand souls, slate roofs, a bell tower, banners drying on lines, the smell of tallow and rain and horses, a cart-driver swearing cheerfully at a mule—and threaded through it all, glowing like veins of ore, the roads. Seven roads radiating out from the town like the spokes of a wheel, each one singing its own note, each one crowded with the accumulated weight of everyone who had ever walked it. Juniper stood outside time and read them the way they'd once read coastlines: this road carries salt and pilgrims; this one carries soldiers, recently, badly; this one hasn't been walked in a generation because of the fever year, poor souls, poor souls—

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing they had felt in seven years, and that was the horror of it, because some ravenous part of them leaned into the beauty like a man leaning into a warm window in winter, and the land gave, and gave, and took.

A name went out of them. They felt it leave—felt the exact moment, like a tooth coming loose. Someone's name, someone who had mattered. They groped for it even as the vision released them and dumped them gasping onto their knees in the wet grass, and found only the hole where it had been, and around the hole, damningly, the *shape* of the hole: the sense of a person loved, argued with, forgiven. Someone whose forgiveness had once been the hinge of their whole life.

Who? *Who?*

"Juniper." Sera's hand on their shoulder, hard, shaking them. "Look at me. Look at me."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding."

Juniper touched their own nose. Blood, bright red—red, they noticed distantly, the first strong colour they'd seen in days—dripping onto the grass. Behind Sera, Wren watched with an expression far too old for the small face, and said nothing, which from Wren was louder than screaming.

"It's the land," Juniper managed. "It's—it shows me things. Roads. Old ones. Ones that were—" They stopped, because saying *erased* meant saying who had erased them, meant following that thread to the palace and the throne and the thing wearing it, and they weren't ready, they might never be ready.

Sera crouched, forearms on knees, studying them with those inquisitor's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Shows you. In exchange for what?"

"Nothing. It doesn't want anything."

"Everything wants something." She glanced at Wren, then back. "You come back from these smaller. I've watched people pay debts all my life, Vane. I know the look of a man counting coins he no longer has."

Juniper wiped their nose and said nothing, and hated how much the silence admitted.

---

They walked on. The visions came anyway, whether Juniper willed them or not—three more before nightfall, each one deeper, each one costing more.

A ford where a river had shifted, and with it, the memory of learning to swim, gone like breath off glass. A buried shrine-stone humming with old devotion, and with it, the taste of a meal they'd shared with someone whose face now floated just beyond reach, familiar as their own hands and utterly unnamed. A stretch of moor that had once been a battlefield, and with that one the land took something Juniper didn't discover until they tried to pray, out of pure reflex, to a god whose name they could no longer find—the god they'd prayed to every night of their childhood, the one whose little clay figure had sat on their mother's shelf.

Gone. All of it. Paid out coin by coin to the hungry ground, and they had never once watched the scale.

That night they sat apart from the fire, turning it over, and finally let themselves ask the question properly, because Sera was right: everything wanted something, including the truth.

*Why didn't you notice?*

And the honest answer rose up ugly and undeniable: *Because you arranged it.*

Not consciously—never consciously—but look at the pattern. Seven years of refusing the gift, yes, but also seven years of never writing anything down. No journal. No letters. No list of names, no dates, no anchors. A cartographer who kept no maps of their own life. When the gaps appeared, they'd shrugged. When a name slipped, they'd assumed the fire. They had built a life with no fixed points, and then acted surprised when they drifted.

Some part of them had known. Some part of them had *wanted* the erosion—had stood at the shore of the past and quietly, guiltily, welcomed every wave that took a little more of it away. Because the past contained the door. The past contained the young king's commission, and the survey of the spaces between worlds, and the map they had drawn with such pride, such *craftsmanship*, the finest work of their life, the map that opened—

Juniper's stomach turned over. They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes.

*You burned it. You ran. You hid. And the land is handing you back the roads because the gods are waking, and the gods don't care what it costs you, and neither, apparently, do you.*

"Door-maker."

Wren stood beside them, silent as a cat, having crossed the camp without sound. Up close, in the firelight, the child's eyes had that layered quality, like light through deep water—as if more than one gaze looked out.

"You're leaking," Wren said.

"I'm what?"

"Leaking." Wren crouched, head tilted. "You give the land your remembering, and you don't take it back, and it pools. The land is starting to know you, door-maker. It's starting to say your name." A pause, and then, gently, terribly: "But it says your *old* name. The loud one. The one from before the fire. Do you want me to tell it to stop?"

Cold walked up Juniper's spine. "You know my name?"

Wren smiled, and it was not entirely a child's smile. "Everyone knows it. It's written on the door."

Before Juniper could speak—before the panic could find words—Wren's head snapped north, toward the hills, and the child went rigid, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear. The god-voices, Juniper realized. The chorus. And something in the chorus had changed key.

"What is it?" Sera was already up, mace in hand. "Riders?"

"No riders." Wren's voice had gone strange, doubled, a child's voice with wind underneath it. "Something's wrong with the land ahead. There was a village there. There *is* a village there. But the land can't remember it." The child's small fists clenched. "The land remembers everything. Even the burnt things. Even the drowned things. But this place—" Wren shuddered, and every god speaking through that small body seemed to flinch at once. "This place is *smooth*. Like a stone the river got hold of. Like something ate it down to the bone and licked the plate."

Sera looked at Juniper. Juniper looked north, where the hills folded together around a valley they could not see, and felt the old gift stir traitorously in their chest, offering—one more vision, one more road, one more piece of themselves on the scale.

"How far?" Sera asked.

"A day," Wren said. "Maybe less. We should go around."

"We can't go around," Sera said grimly. "Around means the lowlands, and the lowlands mean patrols. Whatever's wrong with that village, we go through it." She slung the mace across her back and looked at Juniper one last time, and her voice dropped, quiet enough that only Juniper could hear it. "Whatever price you've been paying out there—stop paying it. Whatever you're trading away, I need you whole when we get there. Understood?"

Juniper nodded, and lied, and lay down facing north, and did not sleep.

Because they had understood something else, in the dark, that they would not say aloud: the land remembered everything. Everything except that village. And there was exactly one thing in all the world that ate memory—and seven years ago, Juniper Vane had drawn the road that led it in.
