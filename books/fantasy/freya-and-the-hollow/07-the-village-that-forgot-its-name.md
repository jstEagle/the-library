## 7. The Village That Forgot Its Name

Sera Ilvane had spent eleven years learning to read a room the way other people read letters—quickly, silently, and for what it concealed. She read this one in the space of three heartbeats.

The village sat in a fold of hills where the road thinned to a suggestion and then to nothing, as if the land itself had grown tired of being traveled. Thatched roofs, a well with a moss-slicked lip, a shrine at the crossroads whose statue had been worn faceless by weather—or by hands; Sera could not tell which from a distance, and the distinction mattered more than she wanted it to. Smoke rose from chimneys. Goats grazed behind a fence that someone had mended recently and badly.

It should have been ordinary. It was not.

The children were too quiet. Not frightened-quiet, not wary-of-strangers quiet, though both of those came quickly enough once Wren was spotted. No—the children had been quiet before they saw the travelers. They stood in the lane watching Juniper and Sera approach with the flat, patient attention of birds on a wire, and when one small girl opened her mouth to shout a warning to her mother, she paused first. Sera watched her do it. Watched the girl's lips move, shaping a name, and stop. Watched her frown as if she'd reached into a pocket and found it empty.

Then she shouted anyway—"Strangers! Strangers on the road!"—and the word *strangers* sounded rehearsed, like something she'd been taught to say in place of whatever she'd lost.

"Juniper," Sera said quietly. "Something's wrong here."

"I know." Juniper's voice had gone thin. They hadn't spoken much since the hill-shrine, since the god's voice had scraped the truth out of them like marrow from a bone. They walked with their satchel clutched against their chest the way a soldier carries a wound. "I can feel it. The land here is—" They stopped, visibly searching for a word that wasn't terrible. "Quiet."

"The land is never quiet," Sera said. "You told me that yourself. You said it remembers everything, that roads are just memories people keep walking."

"I did say that." Juniper's jaw tightened. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

They were given bread and a place by the fire because that was what villages did, or had done, once—and because the elder who met them at the well had the particular desperation of a man who has forgotten why he wants company but knows he wants it very badly.

His name was Aldous. He told them so twice within the first minute, and both times there was a flicker of relief in his face when he got it right, as though each recitation were a coin he'd found on the road and couldn't quite believe was real.

"You're welcome here," he said. "We don't get many travelers. We used to get more, I think. There was a reason we got travelers." He frowned. The frown stayed. "There was a reason."

"A fair?" Sera offered gently.

"Yes!" His whole face lit. Then dimmed. "Or a market. Or... my wife would know. My wife knew things." He looked around the firelit common room as though she might be standing somewhere in the shadows. "My wife," he said again, softer, and Sera understood with a cold settling in her chest that he was not looking for the woman. He was checking whether he still remembered having one.

"She passed," said a woman by the hearth—not unkindly, the way you remind someone of a thing they've been told many times. "Seven years back, Aldous. You know this."

"Seven years back," Aldous repeated, and nodded, and the nod had the hollow obedience of a man agreeing to arithmetic rather than grief.

Sera counted without meaning to. Seven years. Seven years since the Hollow King's coronation. Seven years since the decrees began—no unlicensed magic, no old rites, no names of the elder gods spoken aloud in any public place. Seven years since the inquisitors' ledgers started filling with confiscations that made no sense: not spellbooks, mostly. Not relics. *Records.* Parish rolls. Genealogies. Annals. The boring, beloved paperwork of ordinary lives.

She had filed those reports herself, once. She had assumed the crown was erasing history to make room for its own version. Tyrants always rewrote; that was practically the definition. But rewriting left seams, and seams could be picked. She had built half her career on picking them.

This was not rewriting.

She looked at Aldous, clutching his own name like a talisman, and felt the first true vertigo of her life.

* * *

Wren would not come inside.

The child sat on the woodpile behind the common house with their knees drawn up, and when Sera brought them bread they took it without looking at her and ate in small, mechanical bites, eyes fixed on the village.

"They're all wrong," Wren said finally. "In here." They tapped their own temple. "Like rooms after a fire. You can see where the furniture was."

"Do the voices say anything?"

Wren was silent long enough that Sera thought they wouldn't answer. Then, in a voice that wasn't entirely Wren's—older, layered, a chord struck on an instrument built for single notes—they said:

"*We walked this valley when the river ran north. We taught them the words for snow that falls in summer. Their grandmothers sang our names at the planting. And now—*" The chord broke off. Wren blinked, came back into their own face, and looked suddenly, terribly young. "Now there's nothing. Not even the shape where the song was. The others have holes. This place has—" Wren's hands curled. "*Excavations.*"

"Excavations," Sera repeated.

"Like something dug it all out. Carefully. All the way down." Wren looked up at her, and in the dusk their eyes caught light that wasn't there. "Why would anyone dig out a whole village?"

Sera didn't answer. She was thinking about the girl in the lane, reaching for a warning word and finding a hole where it should have been. She was thinking about excavations, about careful work, about appetite.

She went inside and asked Aldous about the founding of his village.

* * *

He was delighted to be asked. That was the worst part. He straightened in his chair like a boy called on by a favorite teacher, and he reached beneath the bench beside him and drew out a stone tablet, and Sera's breath stopped in her throat.

She knew tablets like that. Every settlement older than a century kept one—a founding record, cut into stone precisely because stone outlasted paper and tyrants alike. Names of the first families. The covenant with whatever god had blessed the ground. The boundaries drawn and sworn. A village's tablet was its spine. Inquisitors were trained to catalog them; she had catalogued dozens. Even blank ones were recognizable by their format, their wear, the ritual borders carved along the edges.

The edges were all still there. The border-work was crisp, lovingly maintained, generations of fingers having traced the grooves. The center of the stone was smooth as a frozen lake.

Not defaced. Not chiseled clean—that leaves scars, and Sera had learned to read scars the way Juniper read roads. This surface had never held writing at all, or had been made to forget that it ever had. It was not erased history. It was history rendered *unwritable*, a page that rejected ink.

"This," Aldous said proudly, "is our founding stone. Cut by Aldric Vane, first headman, in the year the river turned."

"May I?" Sera held out her hand.

He gave it to her gladly, and it was heavier than it looked, and colder. She turned it over. The back bore the same pristine blankness. She ran her thumb across the central field and felt nothing—no ghost-grooves, no shadow of lettering under the polish. Just stone, smooth and complete, like a mouth that had never learned words.

"And Aldric Vane," she said carefully. "What year was the river turned?"

Aldous beamed. "Oh, I couldn't say. Before my time. Before everyone's time." He waved a hand at the room, at the villagers gathered in it, at the whole warm weight of human continuity. "Everyone's gone now, of course. The old families. The way things were. Nobody remembers much past seven years back—it's strange, isn't it? I've thought about that. I've thought about it a great deal." His pride faltered into something naked and pleading. "Have you come from Corvane? Do they remember things there? Longer than seven years?"

The question hung in the smoky air. Around the fire, faces turned toward her—dozens of them, and every single one leaning in with the same hunger, the same terror dressed up as casual curiosity. A woman with flour on her forearms. Two brothers with the same crooked nose. An old man who had been asleep and woken specifically to hear the answer.

*Do they remember things there?*

"No longer than anywhere else," Sera heard herself say, because the truth was a knife and these people had been cut enough.

The relief that moved through the room was the most horrifying thing she had witnessed in eleven years of inquisitorial service. They were comforted. They were comforted to learn that everyone else was as hollowed out as they were, because the alternative—being alone inside the forgetting, being the only family in the realm that had lost its dead twice—was unbearable.

Aldous patted her knee, grandfatherly, grateful. "That's good," he said. "That's good. It wouldn't be fair otherwise, would it? Us being the only ones."

"No," Sera said. "It wouldn't be fair."

Her hands, resting on the founding stone, had begun to shake.

* * *

She found Juniper at the well after midnight, standing in the dark with their palms flat against the stones, and she knew before she spoke that they had reached the same conclusion by a different door.

"Tell me what you feel," she said.

Juniper didn't turn around. "You first. You're the one who interrogates things for a living."

"All right." Sera leaned against the well housing, arms crossed against the cold. "The village has no memory older than seven years. Not suppressed—suppression leaves resistance, resentment, fragments people protect in secret. I've seen communities under memory edicts. They hide their histories in cellars and teach their children forbidden songs in whispers. These people have nothing to hide because there's nothing left. And it's not just events. It's capacity. Aldous can't hold a fact about his own wife's death without it sliding out of him. The founding stone won't take writing. Whatever was done here didn't remove the past. It removed the *container*."

"Now me." Juniper's voice was very steady, which meant it was costing them. "When I touch the land, I should feel everything. Roads, graves, harvests, weddings, feuds—all of it, layered down like sediment. That's how it's always been. Even burned out as I am, even trading pieces of myself away, the land speaks. Here—" They lifted their hands from the stones and stared at them as though they'd touched something diseased. "Here the land doesn't speak. It *listens*. Like it's waiting for instructions. Like it's been swept clean and left ready for occupancy."

"Swept clean," Sera repeated. "By whom?"

"You already know. You've known since the tablet."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear it outside my own head."

Juniper turned. In the starlight their face was grey, and older than seven years, older than the seven years they could account for. "The king's edicts forbid memory. Everyone assumes that means he punishes remembering. Confiscates records, burns archives, silences priests. Enforcement. Law." They swallowed. "But laws are for people who might disobey. You don't write a law against a flood. You don't legislate against winter. And you don't need a decree to enforce forgetting if forgetting is just..." They gestured helplessly at the sleeping village, the blank stone sitting between them on the well's rim where Sera had set it down. "...digestion."

The word fell into the dark and lay there.

"He's consuming it," Sera said. Flatly. Because if she gave it any other inflection she would have to feel it, and she could not yet afford to feel it. "The Hollow King isn't enforcing an edict. The edict is a menu. Every archive seized, every rite banned, every name outlawed—he's not hiding those things from the realm. He's *eating* them. Seven years of reign, seven years of meals, and this village is just—what? A table he cleared early?"

"Or a larder," Juniper said. "Marked and kept. Look at them, Sera. They're alive. Healthy. Functioning. If he only needed to feed once, why leave them breathing? Why leave the *borders* of the stone carved and cared for?" They touched the tablet's edge, tracing the ritual grooves with one fingertip. "Because the frame matters. The frame is what makes the emptiness hurt. A village that knows it has lost its story will go looking for it. Will pray for it. Will ache in exactly the shape of the missing thing." Their hand closed. "And ache is a kind of prayer, isn't it? And prayer is—"

"—food," Sera finished. Her mouth had gone dry. "The gods woke because prayers started getting answered. What if the waking wasn't the cause? What if the answered prayers are just—the smell of cooking drifting out of the kitchen?"

Juniper flinched as if struck.

"I'm sorry," Sera said, and meant it, and did not take it back. "But you need to hear it plainly, because I need to hear it plainly. I spent six years asking questions about the king and getting demoted for every one. I thought I was fighting a cover-up. Cover-ups have an end—you dig far enough, you find the body, you close the case. This—" She looked at the founding stone, smooth as a lie, heavy as a coffin lid. "There is no bottom to dig to. He hasn't buried the truth. He's eaten it, and he's still hungry, and every year the realm gets a little smoother, a little quieter, a little more like this village. And nobody notices because noticing requires—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because noticing requires memory. He's eating the evidence that he eats. It's the perfect crime. It gets cleaner as it goes."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The well breathed its cold mineral breath between them. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as if it had forgotten what it was barking at.

"We have to warn them," Juniper said at last. "Tell them what's happening. Write it down, carve it, anything—"

"With what?" Sera lifted the tablet. "Watch." She spat on her thumb and pressed it hard against the stone's blank face, dragging a crude line through the moisture. For an instant the mark gleamed. Then the surface drank it, and the stone was smooth again, and Sera felt the small wrongness of it crawl up her arm like a spider. "It won't hold. Nothing here holds. We could carve it into their doorposts tonight and by spring they'd be polishing the wood and wondering why it felt important." She set the tablet down with exaggerated care, the way you put down something venomous. "You can't warn people out of a hunger that eats the warning."

"Then what do we do?"

It was the first time Juniper had ever asked her that—asked *her*, the disgraced inquisitor, the woman whose faith had been a slow leak for six years and had finally, tonight, torn wide open. And Sera found that she had an answer, and that the answer frightened her more than the blank stone did.

"We follow the hunger upstream," she said. "Every meal leaves a trail. Every feeding has a direction. Your god on the hill said the king devours memory—fine. Then somewhere there's a place where the devouring is thickest, and at the center of it there's either a king or a thing wearing one, and either way it has a throat." She looked east, toward Corvane, toward the pale smear on the horizon where the capital's lights should have been and weren't. "I spent six years trying to close a case they wouldn't let me close. This is the case. It always was. I just didn't know how big it was."

Behind them, a floorboard creaked. Wren stood in the doorway of the common house, silhouetted against the banked fire, and the child's voice came out doubled and tripled, a chorus wearing one small body:

"*The table is not yet empty,*" Wren said. "*The eater grows careless when it feeds near the source. Come to the city. Come to where the first course was served. One of us still lingers there, in the deep places, under the vaults—old enough to remember the king's face before it went hollow. Old enough to tell you what he swallowed first.*"

The chorus faded. Wren swayed, and Sera caught the child before they fell, and over the small warm weight of them she met Juniper's eyes across the well.

"Under the vaults," she repeated softly. She knew those vaults. Every inquisitor did—the confiscated magic, the seized records, the deep archive beneath Corvane where the crown locked away everything it forbade the realm to remember. She had signed requisition forms for that place. She had wondered, idly, over the years, why nothing ever seemed to come back out of it.

Now she knew. Nothing came back out because nothing was meant to last. It was not a prison.

It was a pantry.

"Pack your satchel," Sera said to Juniper, lifting Wren into her arms. "We ride for Corvane at first light."
