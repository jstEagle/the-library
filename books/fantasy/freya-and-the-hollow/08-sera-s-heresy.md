## 8. Sera's Heresy

The elder's hands would not stop shaking.

Sera watched them from across the hearth—old knuckles like river stones, trembling around a blank tablet of grey stone that should have held the village's founding story in carved lines. He had carried it to the fire as if warmth might coax the words back. It had not worked. Nothing worked here. The tablet was smooth as an egg, and so were the faces of everyone who looked at it.

*We had words once,* he kept saying. *I can feel the shape of them. Like a tooth that isn't there anymore.*

Sera sat with her back to the wall, her inquisitor's coat folded over her knees because wearing it in this room felt like a kind of violence. Wren slept in the corner, curled against Juniper's satchel, and even asleep the child hummed—not a tune Sera recognized, but something older, a sound like wind through a doorway. Juniper stood at the window, watching the road they'd come by, and did not turn around.

Sera should have been watching Juniper too. That was her duty. The charm-seller was still, technically, her quarry; the order stood in her breast pocket, soft from handling, its wax seal broken and re-broken a dozen times as she told herself she was only reviewing it.

She was not reviewing it. She was thinking about a servant named Ollen Bray.

* * *

Seven years ago. The palace at Corvane, in the first spring of the new reign, when everything still smelled of fresh paint and fresh fear.

They brought him to her at the third bell of morning, two guardsmen gripping his elbows, though he wasn't struggling. Struggling required wanting something, and Ollen Bray wanted nothing at all. That was what struck her first—the absence. She had interviewed murderers who wanted their names spoken correctly into the record, thieves who wept for mothers they'd invented, traitors who preened. Every human being she had ever put in a chair had wanted *something*.

Ollen Bray sat in the chair like a coat hung on it.

"Found him in the west gallery," the younger guard said. "Three hours before his shift. Just standing there. Wouldn't answer."

Sera leaned forward. "Master Bray. Do you know why you're here?"

His eyes came up. They were open, wet, functional—and empty behind, the way a house is empty when the furniture has been carried out but the family portrait hooks are still in the walls. Something had lived there. Something had been removed.

"He wears it wrong," Bray said.

"Wears what?"

"The crown." His voice was flat, patient, the voice of a man reciting a timetable. "He holds his head like he learned it. You learn a head. You don't learn your own head." A pause. "It eats going down. Did you know that? Memory. It eats going down. First the far things go. Your mother's voice. The smell of the sea if you ever saw the sea. Then the near things. Then—" his mouth made a shape that might have been a smile on another face, "—then the wanting. Then there's nothing left to eat but the eating."

Sera wrote it all down. That was her mistake, or her virtue; history would never decide which. She wrote: *subject claims the king is not the king.* She wrote: *subject describes consumption of memory in structured stages—suggests observation, not delusion.* She wrote: *recommend holding subject for further—*

The order came down before the ink dried. Not from her direct superior. From above him. From the office of the Lord Inquisitor himself, whose signature she knew the way you know a scar.

*Subject remanded to palace physicians. Case closed. File sealed under Crown prerogative.*

Bray went up the stairs to the physicians' wing and never came down. Sera asked after him twice, politely, the way you ask about weather. The second time, the clerk who answered her didn't remember any such case, and then didn't quite remember asking her to repeat herself, and then looked at her with the faintest flicker of that same hollow patience before blinking it away.

She told herself it was grief-madness. Servants broke sometimes; palaces ground people fine. She told herself the seal meant state secrets, and state secrets were the price of order, and order was the whole reason inquisitors existed—because without someone asking the hard questions *in service of the crown*, the questions ate everything.

She believed it for six years. Belief is a load-bearing wall. You don't notice it until someone pulls it out and the whole house comes down on your head.

* * *

"You've gone somewhere," Juniper said, without turning from the window.

"I'm remembering."

"Dangerous habit. Lately." Now they turned, and the firelight caught the cartographer's face—that careful, guarded face, seven years of hiding worn into it like water wears stone. "What do you remember?"

"A man who saw the king clearly and was erased for it." Sera fed a stick to the fire. "I thought I failed him. I thought—I was too slow, too junior, too trusting. I built my whole disgrace out of that failure. Carried it like a penance. It made me feel *clean*, do you understand? Failing loudly. Asking my questions until they took my rank. At least I was fighting."

"And now?"

"Now I think I was never fighting. I think I was permitted." The word tasted like ash. "You don't demote the crown's finest inquisitor for asking questions. You promote her somewhere quiet, or you bury her in paperwork, or—if you're careful—you let her make noise until the noise itself becomes her cage. Everyone knows Sera Ilvane, the heretic questioner. Who listens to a woman defined by her questions? My disgrace was a muzzle shaped like a medal."

Juniper was quiet a moment. "The village," they said. "No one remembers past seven years. And no one remembers *forgetting*. That's the part that—" They stopped. Started again. "Forgetting leaves edges. Gaps. People grieve gaps. These people don't grieve. There's nothing left to grieve *with*."

"That's what I keep coming back to." Sera pulled the cracked leather folder from her pack—the one thing she'd saved from her old life, the one file she'd copied by hand before it was sealed. She had told herself for years that she kept it out of stubbornness. She had actually kept it because some part of her had known, even then, that the day would come when the file was the only proof the world had happened. "Edicts. Decrees. Seven years of them. I catalogued every strange one, back when I still thought strangeness was corruption. Look at the pattern."

She spread the pages on the floor between them, weighted with stones from the hearth.

*Year One: Edict of Unified Records. All parish chronicles surrendered to the Crown Archive for standardization.*

*Year Two: Edict of Memorial Reform. Public monuments older than the Accession to be re-carved.*

*Year Three: Closure of the Antiquaries' Guild. Their library purchased and removed.*

*Year Four: Ban on genealogical societies. Family records held to be divisive.*

*Year Five: Standardization of the calendar. The old festival names retired.*

*Year Six: All maps of the realm recalled for correction.*

*Year Seven: —*

There was no Year Seven edict. There didn't need to be. By Year Six the machinery ran itself, because the people who might have objected had forgotten there was anything to object to.

"Re-carved," Juniper said softly. "Not destroyed. Re-carved. It doesn't just eat memory. It *replaces* it. It writes."

"It's building something," Sera said. "Or it's hungry in a way that needs the meal to look like it was always its own."

From the corner, Wren stirred and spoke without waking, in a voice that was not entirely the child's—a chorus compressed into one small throat, harmonics stacked like rings in old wood:

*"He counts the doors. He counts the doors. Only one was ever opened wide."*

Then the child rolled over and slept on, breathing the ordinary breath of an ordinary kid, and the fire popped, and outside the wind moved through a village that could not remember why it had gathered here, of all places, beside this river, under this hill.

* * *

That night Sera dreamed of the palace.

In the dream she was walking the west gallery where they'd found Ollen Bray standing, and the portraits on the walls were all blank—not unfinished, not vandalized, but *smooth*, faces like eggs, and beneath each frame a brass plate with a name she could almost read. She walked faster. At the end of the gallery stood a door she had never noticed in six years of service, tall and dark, and from behind it came a sound like a great slow chewing, patient as continental drift.

In the dream she knew the door. In the dream she had walked past it every day of her career and her eyes had slid off it like rain off glass, and now—

Now she woke with her hand on her knife and the elder's dog barking at nothing, and Juniper already awake, sitting up, staring at the dark with an expression Sera was beginning to recognize as the cartographer's version of prayer.

"You felt it," Sera said.

"There's something north of here." Juniper's voice was rough. "Under a hill. Wren's been pointing at it since sundown. The voices want us to go."

"The voices," Sera said carefully, "or the thing that sends the voices?"

Juniper flinched as if struck. "You think I'd lead us into its mouth?"

"I think," Sera said, "that everything I trusted for ten years led me into its mouth while smiling. I think the crown sent me to find you, and I no longer believe the crown wants what I want. I think Ollen Bray saw the truth and was eaten for it, and I helped carry him to the table by writing my neat little report." She heard her own voice rise and did not stop it. "So forgive me if I ask whether the gods' voices are cleaner than the king's. Forgive me if I ask *anything*, because asking is the last tool I have that hasn't been turned in my hand."

Silence. The dog had stopped barking. Somewhere a shutter knocked in the wind.

"I don't know," Juniper said at last, and the honesty of it was worse than any lie. "I don't know what's calling us. But I know the Hollow King's servants burn night markets and take children, and whatever's under that hill woke up the same week my charms started working again. If those are on the same side, then I'm the door they both came through, and either way I need to see it." A pause. "Come or don't."

Sera looked at the fire. At the spread-out edicts, the tidy record of a kingdom being swallowed in bureaucratic increments. At the sleeping child who spoke in choruses.

Ten years ago she had sworn an oath on iron and ink: *to guard the realm against powers that prey upon its people.* She had assumed, without ever examining the assumption, that the realm and the crown were the same thing. That the throne was the realm's heart, and loyalty to one was loyalty to the other.

But a heart could be replaced. She had seen the evidence with her own eyes, seven years ago, in the hollow-eyed man who held his head like he'd learned it.

If the crown was the predator, then her oath had never changed. Only her understanding of whom it bound her against.

"I'm coming," she said. "And Juniper—when we get there, whatever speaks to you, whatever shows you your own face and calls it mercy—you tell me. Everything. Because I've spent seven years watching a city get lied to so smoothly it thanked its liar, and I will not stand in another room where the truth is being managed. Not even kindly. Not even for my protection."

Something moved behind Juniper's eyes—fear, maybe, or gratitude, or the particular loneliness of a person who has been carrying a secret so long that being seen feels like being flayed.

"All right," Juniper said. "Everything."

Sera nodded and lay back down and did not sleep. She listened instead to the wind off the hill to the north, and beneath the wind—so faint she could believe she imagined it, and chose not to—she heard something like a great slow chewing, patient as stone, getting closer.

And beneath *that*, deeper, older, threaded through the child's sleeping breath: a second sound. Voices. Many voices. Rising.

Like names being remembered.
