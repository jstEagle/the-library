## 10. Seven Years Ago

The god beneath the hill did not let Juniper leave.

They had tried. They had stood, knees aching on the cold stone of the shrine, and said, *No. You have the wrong person,* and turned toward the dark mouth of the hollow where Wren slept curled against Sera's cloak, and the voice had come up through the ground like water rising in a well—patient, ancient, terribly kind.

*You drew the door, door-maker. Come and see it again.*

"I don't know what you're talking about."

*You do. You simply paid not to.*

The stone beneath Juniper's palms warmed. Not the warmth of fire or sun—something older, like the warmth of bread long gone from an oven, warmth remembered rather than felt. And then the memories came home.

* * *

It began with ink.

That was how Juniper would remember it afterward, when they could bear to remember it at all: that everything had begun with ink, good ink, the iron-gall kind that bit into vellum and stayed. They had been twenty-six years old and the finest cartographer the realm had ever produced, though they had not been modest enough to leave the saying to others. They could read land the way priests read scripture. Where other mapmakers measured, Juniper *listened*—to the grain of stone, the lean of rivers, the way old roads remembered the feet that had worn them. Their maps did not merely show the world. They showed what the world had been, layered like rings in a tree, and kings had paid in gold for the privilege of unrolling them.

Then the young king had summoned them to Corvane, and the gold had been different. Not coin. A commission.

"The spaces between," the king had said. He was young then—younger than Juniper, barely crowned, with his father's war still fresh on his hands and something hungry already behind his eyes that Juniper, flush with flattery, had mistaken for ambition. "Every map shows what is. I want one that shows what lies between what is and what else might be. The folds. The seams. The places where the world doesn't quite meet itself."

No cartographer had ever mapped those spaces. There was a reason. Every tradition, every guild charter, every half-remembered warning from every dying elder agreed on one point: *the between places are not empty.* But no one had ever told Juniper why, and Juniper had never thought to ask, because asking questions was for lesser talents, and the king had called them *the only mind in my realm capable of this*, and they had believed him.

Gods, they had believed him.

Juniper knelt in the shrine seven years later and lived it again, and their hands—the same hands, scarred now, ink-stained at the cuticles in a way that never washed out—shook against the stone.

* * *

The work took two years.

They built instruments that had never existed before: compasses that pointed toward absence instead of north, plumb lines that hung crooked over certain valleys as if pulled by a weight that wasn't there. They walked the realm's edges and found the places where distance lied—where a mile of road took a morning going out and an afternoon coming back, where two hills stood a hand's width apart on the horizon but a day's ride from each other on foot. They learned to feel the seams the way a tailor feels cloth, thumbing the world's weave until they found the loose threads.

And all the while, the king wrote to them. Letters in his own hand, eager, brilliant, increasingly strange. *What do you see there? What does it look like? Does anything look back?*

Juniper had written back, laughing over their wine in some roadside inn: *Nothing looks back, Majesty. It's only emptiness with better posture.*

They had been wrong. But by the time they knew it, the map was nearly done, and the king had already begun building the frame for it—a great chamber beneath the palace, ringed in stone imported from quarries no one could name, and Juniper had been too deep in the work, too drunk on their own genius, to wonder how he'd known the map's dimensions before it was finished.

The last line was drawn in autumn. Juniper remembered the scratch of the pen, the smell of the ink, the candle guttering in the draft from a window that should have been shut. They remembered setting down the pen and looking at what they'd made—and feeling, for the first time, the map look back.

Not the paper. The *passage*. Because that was what it was; they saw it now, with seven years' hindsight and a god's patience holding the memory open so they could not look away. Their map wasn't a picture of the spaces between. It was a set of instructions. A key drawn in lines. Every seam they'd charted, every fold—they hadn't recorded them. They had *aligned* them. The whole map was a corridor lying flat on vellum, waiting for someone to stand it up.

And the king intended exactly that.

* * *

Juniper had confronted him. That was the part they had traded away first, sold earliest, the memory they had dug out of themselves like a splinter—and now it came back whole, and it hurt worse than all the rest.

They had gone to the palace at night, past the new guards who didn't salute, down into the chamber beneath the palace where the map's frame stood waiting, tall as three men, carved with symbols Juniper recognized from none of the realm's traditions because they belonged to no tradition of this world at all. The king had been standing before it, bareheaded, his crown on a cushion at his feet, reading Juniper's draft maps by candlelight with an expression Juniper had never seen on a human face before. Not ambition. Not hunger, even.

*Recognition.* Like a man reading letters from home.

"You've seen it," Juniper had said. "Whatever's on the other side. You've seen it before."

The king had turned, and smiled, and for one moment—just one—his eyes had been wrong. Not empty. Worse than empty. *Crowded.* Full of something looking out through him the way light looks out through a lamp.

"It has been so patient," the young king had said, in a voice that was mostly his own. "So very patient. And you, cartographer—you have built its road. Do you know what it will give me? Everything. Every answer. Every year of history anyone has ever forgotten. It eats memory, do you understand? It has eaten *worlds'* worth. And everything it takes, it keeps. All I have to do is open the door and hold out my hands."

Juniper had run. That was the truth under all the truths, the one they had paid the most to bury: they had not fought, had not warned the court, had not smashed the frame or burned the palace. They had gathered their instruments and their pride and fled into the night like a thief, telling themselves they would find help, raise the guilds, alert the temples—and they had kept running for three days until they heard the bells of Corvane change their voices, and looked back from a ridge, and seen the sky above the city the wrong color.

The door had opened anyway. The king had needed no cartographer to stand the map upright; he had the frame, and the rituals, and whatever crowded thing had taught him the words. Juniper's map had merely shown him where to build.

They had watched the color of the sky from that ridge, and understood that they had not failed to stop it.

They had *drawn* it.

* * *

The burning came next, and the shrine gave Juniper that too, mercilessly.

Three weeks of flight. Three weeks of the realm unraveling behind them—villages going quiet, roads forgetting where they went, temple bells ringing notes that no longer meant anything because the gods they named were being swallowed name-first. Juniper had reached the coast, found a fishing boat, bribed its captain with instruments of gold and silver, and sailed not away from the disaster but along the edge of it, mapping the damage as it spread, because even in ruin the habit held: *record it, record it, someone must record it.*

And everywhere they charted the spreading grey, they found the same signature. Their own linework. Their own conventions, their own hand, scaled up and rendered in the flesh of the world. The entity was not merely eating the realm. It was *redrafting* it—erasing the old features and laying down new ones according to a geometry Juniper knew intimately, because Juniper had invented it.

Their style. Their shame. Written across the land in disappearing ink.

They had come back ashore with a single satchel: the master copy of the between-spaces map, every draft, every working note, every letter the king had written them. Evidence, they told themselves. Proof for whoever would eventually rise against the thing wearing the crown.

There was no one to show it to. The guild masters they'd trusted had blank eyes and pleasant, looping conversation. The temples were silent. One by one, the people Juniper sought out either didn't remember why any of it mattered—or worse, remembered *wrongly*, their pasts rewritten smooth, their griefs edited out, smiling the contented smiles of the well-fed and the hollowed.

On the last night, in a room above a tavern in a town whose name Juniper would later pay to forget, they had taken the master map out one final time. Unrolled it across the floor. Stood over it with a candle.

And understood, finally, what they were actually holding.

It wasn't evidence. Evidence informed. This thing *commanded*. Anyone who found it—any desperate soul, any would-be hero, any second young king hungry for answers—could open the door again. The map was the door. Burning the palace wouldn't close it. Killing the king wouldn't close it. Only this—only the lines themselves, and every note explaining them, and every letter proving they'd existed—had to go.

Juniper had fed the map to the candle flame corner by corner, and it had caught eagerly, hungrily, the ink flaring colors that ink should not flare, and in the last moment before the passage-lines curled and blackened, Juniper had sworn—out loud, alone, to no one—that no one would ever follow their work into the between places again.

Including themselves. *Especially* themselves.

The drafts went next. Then the notes. Then the letters, the king's elegant hand curling in the fire like living things. And when it was done, when the satchel held nothing but ash and the smell of it, Juniper had sat on the floor of that rented room and faced the last problem, the one no fire could solve:

Themselves.

Because the map still existed in the only vault that couldn't be raided. Their memory. Every seam, every fold, every measurement—perfect, total, indelible. As long as Juniper Vane remembered how to draw the door, the door could be drawn again. By them, in weakness. In anger. In a bad winter, a desperate hour, a moment of the old pride whispering *you were the greatest, you could fix it, you could use it right this time.*

They had found a hedge-witch in the marsh country a month later. Old, half-blind, honest about her limits. She could not erase a memory—no one could—but she could *unhook* it, tuck it away behind a price, make it something Juniper would have to seek out deliberately, painfully, piece by piece. And she had asked, as such women ask, what Juniper was willing to pay.

"Everything it costs," Juniper had said. "Take it in trade. Whatever I value. However much it takes."

She had studied them with her clouded eyes and taken, over three nights, the things Juniper loved best: the memory of their mother's kitchen, traded without hesitation. Their first map, the one drawn at nine years old in charcoal on a barn door. The face of the teacher who had first said *you listen to the land, child, don't lose that.* Each trade had left a smooth blank place, and each blank place had made the next trade easier, and by the end Juniper had traded so much of themselves that the remaining self—the new self, the small careful charm-seller self—had seemed like plenty. Had seemed like *enough person*.

The witch had taken the door-map knowledge last, and she had wept while she took it, though Juniper never learned whether the tears were for what was being removed or for what was being left behind.

* * *

The memory released them.

Juniper came back to themselves kneeling on the shrine's cold stone, and the stone was wet, and it took them a moment to understand that the wetness was theirs—that they were crying without any memory of having started, their whole body shaking with the effort of having lived seven years ago twice in one night.

Above them, the diminished god's voice moved through the hill like wind through wheat.

*Now you remember.*

"Yes." Their voice came out ruined. "Now I remember."

*Do you see, then? Why you must be the one?*

Juniper pressed their forehead against the stone. "I see that I'm the reason it's here at all. I see that everything since—the edicts, the grey, the villages that forgot their own names, that child sleeping ten feet from me with dead gods speaking through her—all of it stands on a map I drew with these hands." They lifted their head. The shrine was dim, lit only by the faint luminance that clung to Wren even in sleep, and Juniper's voice cracked apart in the dark. "I also see that I am not brave enough, or good enough, or whole enough to undo it. I sold the wholeness, do you understand? I *paid it away*. What's left of me ran for seven years and sold charms and kept its head down. That person can't do what you're asking."

*No,* the god agreed, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. *That person cannot. But the person who drew the door is the only person alive who knows how to unmake it—and you have just proven the trades can be unwound. What the witch tucked away can be brought back. What you sold can be reclaimed.*

Cold walked up Juniper's spine. "Reclaimed from where?"

*From the one who ate your memories, little door-maker. From the Hollow King's vaults—for that is where all sold and confiscated magic goes, all the forgotten things, all the names. It hoards what it devours. It cannot help itself. Hunger always keeps the bones.*

Juniper stared into the dark of the shrine, and the shape of it rose in their mind unbidden: the vault beneath the palace they had glimpsed once, years ago, in another life—the chamber where the king stored everything his inquisitors seized. Every charm. Every relic. Every stolen name.

Every piece of Juniper Vane.

"And if I take them back," they said slowly, "if I make myself whole again—I get him back too. The one who drew the door. The arrogant fool who thought he was the finest mind in the realm. You're asking me to become that person again. On purpose."

*I am asking you to become the only person who can finish what they started.*

Behind them, in the hollow's mouth, Wren stirred in her sleep and spoke—not in a child's voice, but in the layered chorus of the returning gods, clear across the dark:

*"The door-maker remembers. The door-maker will come to the vault. And the thing in the crown already knows she's coming."*

Juniper went cold. "What did you say?"

Wren's eyes opened, luminous, fixed on nothing and everything. "It felt you remember," the child said, in her own voice this time, small and frightened. "Just now. When the memories came back—it felt it, all the way in the city. It's been waiting seven years for you to want them back." She sat up, clutching Sera's cloak. "It left the door to the vault unlocked for you."

* * *

Sera was awake before Juniper finished telling her, and Juniper watched the inquisitor's face do the arithmetic that hers had done: the vault, the memories, the trap so obvious it was almost insulting. Walk into the heart of the Hollow King's power, reclaim the very knowledge the entity craved, and trust that hunger's greed would keep the cage door open wide enough to walk out again.

"It's a trap," Sera said flatly.

"It's an invitation," Juniper corrected quietly. "Which is worse. Traps are built for enemies." They looked down at their ink-stained hands, the hands that had drawn the end of the world, and felt—for the first time in seven years—the full weight of the person they used to be pressing against the inside of their skin, wanting back in. "Invitations are built for guests. It thinks I'm coming home."

Sera's jaw tightened. "Then we don't go."

"We go." Wren said it, and both adults turned. The child stood at the mouth of the hollow, silhouetted against the pre-dawn grey, and the voices moved under her words like current under ice. "The gods say the vault holds more than her memories. It holds *ours*. Our names. Our temples. Everything it ate." Her small fists clenched. "If she opens the vault to take herself back, the door stays open long enough for us to reach in and take the gods back too."

Juniper closed their eyes. Seven years ago, they had drawn a door for a hungry thing because a king had flattered them, and the world had paid the bill ever since.

Now the world was asking them to draw one more door—this time on purpose, this time knowing the cost, this time walking through it themselves.

"All right," Juniper said, and the word tasted like ink and ash. "Corvane, then. The vault of sold things." They shouldered their satchel—instruments, paper, the last blank maps they owned—and found that their hands had almost stopped shaking. "But we do it my way. I've walked into that city once as the greatest cartographer who ever lived, and I burned everything I touched. This time we go in as nobody. Pilgrims. Charm-sellers. Ghosts."

Sera snorted softly in the dark. "You're going to disguise the most wanted woman in the realm as a pilgrim walking straight toward the palace."

"I'm going to disguise all of us as exactly what the Hollow King stopped believing in years ago," Juniper said, turning toward the east, where the sky was beginning, faintly, to remember the color grey gives way to. "People coming home."
