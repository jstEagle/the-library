## 11. Wren Is Taken

Wren heard the horses before anyone else did.

Not with ears—ears were slow, clumsy things—but with the chorus. The voices that lived in Wren's chest like birds in a hedge went quiet all at once, the way sparrows go quiet when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass. Nine gods and a handful of nameless smaller things, all of them holding their breath at once.

Wren stopped walking and put one bare foot flat on the road.

The road was old. Roads always remembered being walked, even when people forgot them, and this one remembered iron shoes and torchlight and men singing a marching song with the words worn off it. It remembered those things coming from the east, behind them, fast.

"Riders," Wren said.

Juniper turned around. They had been walking ahead, the way they always did, as if the road might change its mind if they didn't watch it. Their satchel was slung across their body the way they carried it now, not on the shoulder but tight against the ribs, like something that could be stolen. "How many?"

Wren closed their eyes and let the road talk. "Six horses. Heavy ones. Armor that doesn't fit right—one of them is wearing armor made for a bigger man. And—" Wren frowned, because this part was strange, this part made the chorus press up close under their skin like children at a window. "And something else. Something riding with them that isn't a man."

The voices had names for it. Most of the names were very old words that meant *hunger*.

Sera had her sword half-drawn before Wren finished speaking. That was the thing about Sera: she moved like a door slamming. "Off the road. Now. There was a cut in the hillside back a hundred paces—"

"They've already seen us," Juniper said quietly. "If they're riding hard enough for the horses to be lathered, they've seen us."

They had. Wren could feel it now too, once the chorus pointed it out—the attention, far off but fixed, like a finger pressed against the back of the neck. Six riders cresting the long brown swell of the moor behind them, small as toys, coming down at a canter that would become a gallop and stay there.

"Run," Sera said. "We run. We're two days from anything that looks like cover, so we don't stop running until—"

"No," said the god who lived deepest in Wren, the one whose voice came out of Wren's mouth sometimes without asking, low and resonant as a bell struck underwater. Wren felt their own lips shape the word and didn't fight it. *No* was what the god knew how to say. It had been saying *no* to the Hollow Thing since before Corvane had walls.

Everyone looked at Wren. Everyone always looked at Wren when the deep voice spoke, and Wren hated it, because Wren was nine years old and nine years old was not old enough to be the thing everyone looked at.

"The child is tired," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. They had learned to read which voice was talking by now, mostly. "The child needs to rest, and we need to keep moving, and both of those things can't—"

"*No,*" the voice said again, and this time Wren understood what it meant, because understanding came with it like water filling a footprint. The god meant: *you cannot outrun what has already chosen you.* The god meant: *it smells me in you. It has smelled me since the shrine. Running only decides where it takes you.*

Wren's own voice, small and cracked, came up through the god-voice like a sprout through stone. "It wants me," Wren said. "Doesn't it. That's why the riders. Not you. Me."

Nobody answered, which was an answer.

* * *

They ran anyway. People always ran; the gods said it was one of the things humans did that the gods had never managed to learn, and that they admired it even as it broke their hearts.

Wren ran with Juniper's hand gripping theirs, over the heather and the wet black peat, while Sera fell behind them to watch the riders come. The moor was all brown and bone-white sky, no trees, no stones big enough to hide behind, nothing but miles of open breathing land. Wren's legs burned. The chorus sang to them as they ran—not a song with a tune, more a feeling, the feeling of hands cupped around a candleflame in wind—and it helped, it helped some.

Then the crossbow bolt took the ground a stride ahead of Juniper's foot and burst into splinters, and running stopped mattering.

"Hold!" a rider shouted, reining in a horse-lengths away, and then six of them were everywhere, circling, and the circle was made of chainmail and grey cloaks and the crown's black sun stamped on the breastplates, and in the middle of the circle, where the wind couldn't reach, the air had gone wrong. Thick. Grey. The color-drain, Wren thought, remembering Juniper's word for it. Where the king's hunger pooled, the world lost its paint.

One rider dismounted. He walked toward them unhurried, pulling off his gauntlets finger by finger, and Wren saw that his armor fit wrong the way the road had said—a bigger man's armor, or maybe just armor made for someone with a straighter spine. His face was ordinary. That was somehow the worst part. He looked like a man who sold fish.

"You'll come with us," the fish-faced man said. "All three, if you make it easy. One, if you make it hard. The orders are specific about which one." He smiled, and his teeth were ordinary too. "The child especially. The child is wanted alive and *whole*. Those were the words. Alive and whole."

"What does it want with a child?" Sera's voice. She had stepped in front of Wren, sword out now, no pretense left about it. Wren watched her back, the set of her shoulders, and understood something new: Sera stood like that in front of things she had already decided to die for. She'd done it at the shrine. She'd done it at the gate. She kept doing it, and each time Wren thought, *this time she'll get tired*, and each time she didn't.

"The child isn't your concern, Ilvane." The man said her name like he'd been told to say it exactly that way, with a little curl of contempt at the end. "Though I'm told there's a standing question about your oath. Desertion, aiding a proscribed practitioner, consorting with—" he gestured at Wren with one bare hand, delicately, the way you'd gesture at filth, "—*that*. Come home, Inquisitor. Kneel nicely. Maybe they only take your rank this time."

"I resigned," Sera said.

"You scribbled a letter nobody accepted. The crown doesn't accept resignations. It accepts confessions."

Wren felt the moment tilt. Felt it the way you feel a table tip before the dishes slide. The chorus rose up loud and bright inside them, every voice at once, and underneath all of it, Juniper's hand tightened around Wren's fingers—tighter than comfort, tighter than reassurance. Tight like a decision.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to remember exactly what happened next, and never quite manage it, because it happened fast and happened sideways and happened mostly to other people.

Juniper moved first. Not toward the riders—away, dragging Wren backward, and Wren realized with a jolt that Juniper was doing the arithmetic out loud, half-whispered, the way they did when they were frightened: *two of us, six of them, open ground, no cover, Sera's blade against crossbows, no, no, no.* Juniper's eyes flicked to the left, to a shallow fold in the moor where the heather grew tall, and Wren saw the plan form behind them like a drawing taking shape: *there, we can make there, we can lose them in the—*

"Go," Juniper breathed. To Sera. "Take Wren and *go*."

"And you?"

"I'm the one they want second-most. I can lead them away. I've spent seven years getting good at being led away from." A terrible quick smile. "Go."

And here was the thing Wren would carry afterward, the thing that sat in the chest like a swallowed stone: Juniper meant it. Juniper was going to do it. Was already turning, was already reaching into the satchel for whatever trick would buy the seconds, was already choosing Wren's life over their own with the same brisk efficiency they used for everything, as if self-sacrifice were just another route on a map, marked and measured.

But the fish-faced man had orders, and the orders were specific.

"Take the child," he said, bored, almost gentle. "Kill the rest."

The crossbows came up.

What happened next happened because of the chorus. Wren didn't choose it—couldn't have, at nine, with six armed men and a tipped table and the world sliding—but the gods chose it, or the oldest of them did, the bell-underwater one, and it poured itself up through Wren's throat and out into the air between them all.

"*NO.*"

The word hit the moor like weather. The nearest horse screamed and went down on its knees. Two crossbow bolts flew wild, one burying itself in peat, the other splitting against a rider's pauldron. Every man flinched as if slapped. And in the middle of the circle, the grey thick air *shivered*—shivered with something like delight, something like appetite, and Wren felt it feel them, felt the vast far-off attention snap fully open like an eye, and the eye was looking at Wren, only Wren, and it was *smiling*.

"There," the fish-faced man breathed, and for just a moment his ordinariness cracked and something else looked out through his face, something patient and enormous and starving. "There it is. Oh, *there* it is."

Hands. Rough hands, gauntleted, closing on Wren's arms, lifting. Wren kicked and bit and the chorus shrieked and the world became a smear of grey sky and Juniper's face—

Juniper's face, and this was the part Wren kept, the part that mattered:

Juniper had been running. Had been three strides into the heather-fold, into escape, into the plan. And Wren saw them stop. Saw the exact moment the choice landed, saw it hit Juniper like the bolt had hit the ground—saw them stand still for one full heartbeat, one full breath, everything about them screaming *go, go, you can still go*—

—and turn back.

"Juniper!" Sera shouted, because she was fighting now, blade ringing off a spear-shaft, blood on her cheek, buying with her body the seconds that would kill her. "Don't—you *fool*, don't—"

But Juniper was already kneeling in the wet heather with the satchel open, tearing paper, and Wren, hoisted upside down over a rider's shoulder, saw what they drew. Not ink. Blood—Juniper had opened their own palm on a knife without hesitating, without looking, the way you'd break bread. And where the blood touched the page, the page began to *remember*. Lines crawled across it like roots, like veins, like roads—roads that weren't on any map because they'd been erased, unmade, eaten—and the moor beneath Juniper's knees shuddered as the land's memory woke up and answered.

A memory-map. The dangerous kind. The kind that cost.

Wren saw the cost happen. Saw it move across Juniper's face like frost crossing a window. Whatever Juniper paid to draw that line, they paid it all at once, and something went out of their eyes—some piece of themselves, some year, some name—and they didn't even stagger. They just got up, folded the map into their bloody fist, and started to walk after the riders.

After. Not away. After.

* * *

The saddle was cruel and the rope was crueler, and the grey air rode with them the whole way, wrapped around Wren like a second cloak. The farther they went from Juniper and Sera, the louder the grey thing got—not in sound, in *attention*, a pressure like deep water, and through it the chorus sang to Wren desperately, fragments, comforts, warnings:

*Hold, little door-echo. Hold your name. It will ask for your name. Never give it your—*

"It knows what you are," said the fish-faced man, riding beside Wren's horse, conversational, horrible. "You know that? It's been listening to you for weeks. Every prayer you've channeled, every dead god's whisper—it's been tasting them secondhand and it has developed quite an appetite." He leaned closer. Up close his eyes were wrong, filmed, like river ice over moving water. "Do you know what you are, child? You're not god-touched. You're a *chalice*. A cup the old things pour themselves into. And cups exist to be drunk from."

Wren bit him. Hard, on the wrist, drawing blood, and laughed when he cursed, because the laughter was also a god's, and gods laugh at wolves.

Behind them—far behind, but not gone, never gone—Wren felt two small stubborn flames following. One that tasted of ink and blood and forgetting. One that tasted of steel and broken oaths.

Both still coming. Neither turning around.

* * *

They rode until dark, and past dark, and the land changed under them—greener, older, the roads remembering grander things—and near midnight they came down into a valley where a pale light burned ahead, and Wren understood where they were being taken, and the chorus went silent with dread.

Corvane. But not the city Wren had glimpsed from the hills. This was the heart of it, the palace district, and above the walls the sky itself had drained to the color of old milk, and the stars were missing, and where the palace should have had lights there was only one vast soft glow, hungry and patient, like a mouth deciding.

The gates opened before the riders reached them. Of course they did. The thing wearing the king had known they were coming since noon.

As Wren was lifted down from the horse, as the grey air gathered close and began, gently, almost tenderly, to ask for their name—the chorus broke its silence one last time and sent a single thread of fire racing back along the road, back through the dark, back to two pursuing figures, and the message was not fear and was not warning.

It was coordinates.

*Come,* the gods said, in Wren's cracking child-voice, aloud, to the empty night behind them, knowing Juniper's map would hear it. *We are here. Bring the door-maker. Bring the door.*

And somewhere behind, faint but unfaltering, Wren heard—or imagined hearing, or the gods delivered it, it hardly mattered—two sets of footsteps, still coming, neither turning around.
