## 12. Into the Pale City

The road to Corvane was full of pilgrims, and that was the whole of our disguise.

We fell in with them two miles out, where the pilgrim road bent through a stand of birches gone white as bone. There were dozens of them—hundreds by the time the walls rose out of the morning haze—walking in loose knots, carrying candles wrapped in waxed cloth, carrying the sick on litters, carrying nothing at all but their hunger for something to be true. They sang, some of them, low and tuneless, songs whose words had been worn smooth as river stones. Half of them didn't know what the words meant anymore. They sang anyway.

Sera walked with her hood up and her inquisitor's bearing folded away somewhere deep, replaced by a stoop she'd practiced in front of a stream's reflection until it looked honest. Wren walked between us with a rope belt and ash rubbed into their hair, and I had spent an hour of the previous evening teaching them the one lesson that mattered: *be dull*. Gods lived in Wren like bees in a wall, and bees hummed when they were frightened, and if a single voice slipped out of that child on the road into Corvane, we would all burn.

"Remember," I murmured, "you're just a poor orphan going to pray at the Sepulcher."

Wren looked up at me with eyes the color of weather. "The gods say you were a liar before you were a door-maker."

"Then they'll know I'm good at it."

Something flickered across the small face—not quite a smile, but its cousin—and Wren pulled the hood lower and became, miraculously, forgettable. It was a skill, I realized. A god-touched child who could make themselves unremarkable had survived seven years in a city that ate the remarkable. I filed that away and did not ask where it had been learned. Some questions keep better than others.

I had not seen Corvane in seven years. I had told myself I remembered it perfectly—the copper roofs going green along the Weepers' Quarter, the night market strung with lanterns dyed saffron and madder-red, the river running brown-gold with silt from the eastern hills, the banners of the king snapping crimson over the citadel. I had carried that city in my head like a pressed flower, colors and all.

It was grey.

Not dim. Not overcast, though the sky was that too. *Grey*—the way a fire is grey when it has stopped being a fire and started being ash. The walls of Corvane should have been honey-colored limestone; they were the color of old dishwater. The banners over the gate hung in folds of faded violet so pale it might once have been red. And as we passed beneath the arch, past the guards who barely glanced at our papers—pilgrim papers, bought from a forger in Millbrook whose prices had gone up since the gods woke—I felt it happen to me. A thinning. Like walking from sunlight into deep water.

The color drained wherever the king's influence thickened. I understood that now, watching it pool and ebb around us like fog with intent. Near the guard posts it lay deepest; near a beggar woman singing to herself in the shadow of a shrine-niche—empty now, its god long scraped away—the grey thinned, and for a moment her shawl showed a thread of actual blue, and her song had a note in it that made three passersby stop and listen before they remembered themselves and hurried on.

"What is it?" Sera asked quietly, seeing my face.

"It's not a metaphor," I said. "Everyone thinks the greyness is despair, or poverty, or the weight of the edicts. It isn't. It's *him*. It leaks." I kept my eyes forward. "Memory has a color. He eats it, and what he leaves behind looks like this."

Wren tugged my sleeve. When I leaned down, the child whispered in a voice that was half their own and half something older, something with wind in it: "The city remembers being bright. That's why it hurts."

* * *

Inside the walls, we moved with the pilgrim crowds toward the tent-city that had grown up in the Plaza of Nine Fountains—nine dry fountains now, their basins cracked, though I'd heard the wells elsewhere in the city had begun to refill, and every such rumor pointed back to a charm sold in a night market by a seller who no longer existed. We found space among the pilgrims' shelters, a lean-to of oiled canvas against a wall that smelled of tallow and old rain, and Sera went out alone to walk the streets in her borrowed stoop and learn what could be learned.

She came back after dark looking like a woman who had swallowed something that wouldn't go down.

"They're burning the market district," she said. "Not all of it. Sections. The Lantern Row first—that's where your stall was?"

"Was," I said. "Seven years ago."

"The inquisitors are calling it purification. Clearing ground for a new temple to the king's glory." She sat down on our bedroll, and for a moment the stoop fell away and she was only tired. "Juniper, there are people living in those sections. The poor quarters. They're posting notices giving families three days to leave, and the notices are nailed up the same day the fires start. I watched a man argue with a clerk about it. The clerk wrote his name down." She said it flatly, but I heard what was underneath: *I used to be the one they sent when names got written down.*

"They'll do worse than that when they find out the charm-seller came back," I said. "Every answered prayer in this city points at me like a compass needle."

"Then we find the needle's friends before the needle finds us." She looked at me across the candle stub we allowed ourselves. "You've been selling charms here for seven years. Quiet ones. Harmless ones, you said. Who bought them?"

I hadn't wanted to answer that question even to myself, because the answer was a map, and maps of people are more dangerous than maps of land. But I had drawn it already, in the days since the hill-shrine, in the privacy behind my eyes: the midwife on Tanner's Row who'd bought a charm to ease a breech birth. The ferryman who paid for safe passage in fog. The widow who ran the soup-house off Candle Street and bought charms against fever every winter, five winters running. The lamplighter with the bad lung. The girl who copied books and wanted, desperately, to remember her mother's face—a charm against forgetting, which I had sold her knowing full well it was nothing but carved bone and suggestion, and which had apparently worked last month, because everything worked now.

"Hundreds," I said. "Maybe more. Small mercies. That was the rule—one mercy per customer, never twice, never the same street twice in a season. I thought I was keeping a low profile." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I was building a network. Seven years of small mercies, and every one of them is a thread, and threads make webs, and webs are how you catch things—or how you get caught."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "Or how you hold a city together while it decides what it wants to be."

That was the thing about Sera Ilvane. She had spent her life serving a machine built for finding guilt, and somewhere along the way she had learned to see the other shape hiding inside every net.

* * *

We went to the soup-house first, because the widow—Maren, her name was Maren, and I had sold her a fever-charm the winter her grandson nearly died—was the likeliest door, and doors were my trade.

Candle Street smelled of woodsmoke and onions. The soup-house was a converted chandlery with a long scarred table and benches that held forty souls on a good night, and when we pushed through the door with our pilgrims' hoods up, the room went quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet when everyone in them is deciding whether to know you.

Maren stood at the far end with a ladle in her fist, a broad woman gone soft at the edges and hard at the center, and she looked at the three of us for a long four-count. Then she set down the ladle, walked the length of the table, took my chin in a hand rough as a brick, and turned my face to the light.

"You look terrible," she said. "Sit down. You'll eat first, and then you'll tell me why the fool cartographer came back to the one city that wants him dead."

"I never told you I was a cartographer."

"You didn't have to. You've got the hands for it." She steered me onto a bench. "Ink under the nails for years, and you held your fingers like you were measuring the table. Sit. All of you. The little one can have the stool by the fire."

And that was how it began—not with oaths or councils, but with soup. Maren fed us barley and salt pork and asked questions in a low voice between ladlings, and by the time the bowls were empty, the room had filled with the evening regulars, and Maren had done something without appearing to do anything at all: she had introduced us. Not by name. By need. "This one needs a place to sleep that isn't the plaza." "This one needs to hear about the tunnels under the chandlery." "This one—" nodding at Wren, who was watching the fire with eyes that reflected it a shade too brightly "—this one needs to stay away from windows."

They came to us one at a time after that. The book-copyist, Liss, who touched the bone charm at her throat and said, "It worked. I remember her face now. I remember her *voice*. What did you do?" and I said, "Nothing. Something else did it through me," and she nodded as if that were the most sensible thing in the world, because in Corvane now, the impossible had become merely the new.

The lamplighter, Odo, coughing, who mapped the city for free in his own way—who knew which watchmen took bribes and which took their duty seriously, which alleys had second exits, which cellars connected. He drew it all in charcoal on the underside of a table top, upside down, from memory, and when Sera saw it she went very still and said, "This is better than the palace survey," and Odo shrugged and said, "Nobody watches a lamplighter. That's the whole trick of lamps."

And the midwife from Tanner's Row, who arrived late, breathless, and said the words that turned our fragile hope into a plan: "They're coming for the charm-buyers. Not just the sellers. Notices went up tonight. Anyone who purchased 'unlicensed talismans' is to report to the Sepulcher for questioning." Her jaw tightened. "Half the women on my row bought something from you over the years, whether they'll admit it or not. They won't report. Which means come morning, the inquisitors start knocking, and they'll knock hardest where the prayers were answered loudest."

The room murmured. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed. The fire popped, and in its light I saw the greyness pressing at the shuttered windows like weather trying to get in, and I understood that we were sitting inside one of the last pockets of color in Corvane, and that the pocket was shrinking.

"There are maybe two hundred of them," Sera said quietly, when the midwife finished. "People who owe you a mercy. Scattered across six quarters. If we scatter them further, hide them, we save some and lose the city."

"If we gather them," Maren said, "we give the inquisitors a list with faces."

"Neither," I said.

They all looked at me. Wren looked up from the fire, and I felt the child listening with more than ears.

"A list with faces is exactly what the Hollow King wants," I said. "He doesn't just eat memory—he eats *connection*. Names attached to names. Debts. Gratitude. Every person in this room owes someone else something small, and that web of owing is the oldest magic there is, older than the gods, and it's the one thing he can't digest cleanly. So he makes us afraid to owe each other. Makes gratitude contraband." I stood, and my legs ached, and I let them see me ache, because these people trusted tiredness more than confidence. "So we don't scatter and we don't gather. We *continue*. The soup-house stays open. The charms—what few I dare make—keep moving, but through hands that don't know each other's names. Maren knows me. I know Odo. Odo knows the ferryman. Nobody holds more than one thread. A web where every strand only touches two others."

"That's not a rebellion," Liss said.

"No," I agreed. "It's a map. Rebellions get burned. Maps survive in pieces, and any piece is enough to redraw the whole." I looked around the scarred table, at the lamplight and the steam and the faces turning toward me with a hope I hadn't asked for and couldn't refuse. "I burned my maps once because I was afraid of what I'd drawn. I'm done burning things."

* * *

Later—past midnight, when the soup-house slept and Sera kept the first watch and Wren curled against the warm bricks of the oven—I climbed to the roof with a scrap of charcoal and a sheet of paper I'd been saving, and I let myself feel it: hope, fragile as ice on a spring pond, creaking under my weight.

From the rooftops, Corvane spread out grey and vast, chimney smoke rising straight in the still air. Here and there a lit window glowed amber—a pocket of warmth, a pocket of remembering. I counted them. Eleven that I could see. Eleven fires in a city of forty thousand.

But eleven was more than zero. And seven years ago there had been none.

Wren came up beside me without a sound, the way children do, and stood at my elbow looking out at the dark.

"The gods want to know what you'll draw," the child said.

"Tonight? Nothing. Tonight I rest my hand."

"They say door-makers always say that." Wren tilted their head. "They say you drew the brightest thing they ever saw, once. Before the door."

My chest tightened. "They remember that?"

"They remember everything he ate. That's the worst part." The child's voice went very quiet, very old. "Nothing is gone, Juniper. It's all just... inside him. Every temple. Every name. Every bright thing." Wren looked up at me, and the firelight from the vent below caught their eyes. "Even the part of you that you sold."

Below us, in the sleeping street, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell, not the alarm. The Sepulcher bell, tolling slow and deliberate, the sound it made when the crown announced a great purge. Lights bloomed in windows down Candle Street. Doors opened. Voices rose.

Sera appeared at the roof hatch, sword already in her hand, her face pale in the starlight. "Notices," she said. "On every corner. They've announced it for dawn tomorrow." She swallowed. "Juniper—they're not purging the market districts anymore."

"Then what?"

"They're sealing the gates. Nobody in or out. And they've posted a name on the Sepulcher doors." She met my eyes. "*Yours.*"

Out across the grey city, the Sepulcher bell tolled on, and every lit window I had counted began, one by one, to go dark.
