## 13. The King's Empty Eyes

The seal on Sera Ilvane's old credentials had not faded. That was the first strange thing.

She turned the brass medallion over in her palm as they crouched in the shadow of Corvane's pilgrim gate, dawn still an hour off and the city around them grey as ash. *SERA ILVANE — FIRST CIRCLE, OFFICE OF INQUIRY.* The letters were crisp. The wax backing was supple. Seven years of demotion, of clerking in a basement, of being politely erased—and her credentials looked like they'd been minted yesterday.

"They should be dead," she murmured. "Credentials expire. Mine were revoked."

"Maybe they forgot to revoke them," Wren said. The child sat cross-legged on the cold cobbles, chewing a strip of dried apple, eyes fixed on the palace spires rising above the rooftops like needles pushed through grey cloth. "Maybe it can't eat what it doesn't know is there."

Juniper glanced up from the map they'd been sketching by feel—no ink, just finger-traces in the paper that somehow stayed. "Or maybe it kept them on purpose. A leash it forgot it was holding."

"Either way," Sera said, closing her hand around the medallion until its edges bit her skin, "it opens doors. And I'm the only one of us who knows how to walk through them without looking guilty."

She'd rehearsed this for two days. Not the words—those came easy, the liturgy of procedure she'd spoken ten thousand times—but the posture. The First Circle walk: chin level, pace unhurried, the particular way an inquisitor's gaze slides over people without ever landing on them, because landing means caring, and caring is a vulnerability the Office trains out of you by the second year. She had spent seven years trying to unlearn that look. Now she put it back on like a coat she'd outgrown, and hated how well it fit.

* * *

The pilgrim queues at the palace gates moved slowly under the eyes of crown riders, but there was a second entrance—the Inquisitors' Door, iron-banded, unmarked except by the small brass plate worn smooth by generations of gloved hands. Sera had entered through it four hundred times. She presented her medallion to the door-warden, a young man with a shaved jaw and the flat stare of someone paid to notice nothing.

He looked at the medallion. He looked at her face. Something flickered behind his eyes—not recognition, exactly. More like a man reading a page he'd read before and finding the words slightly wrong.

"First Circle," he said. "You're expected?"

"I'm expected everywhere," Sera said. "That's what First Circle means."

It wasn't true. It had never been true. But the sentence had the right shape, the right cadence, the bureaucratic music the Office ran on, and the warden's frown smoothed itself away like water closing over a stone. He stamped her ledger—she hadn't known they still used ledgers; everything had been glass-slates when she left—and waved her through into the corridor beyond.

That was the second strange thing: the smell.

Palaces have smells. Stone dust, lamp oil, the tallow-and-roses reek of power. This one smelled of nothing. Not cleanliness—nothing. Her own sweat, her leather coat, the road-grime of three weeks' travel, all of it seemed to vanish three steps past the threshold, absorbed into air that tasted like the inside of a closed mouth. The lamps along the corridor burned with steady, colorless flames. No shadows pooled beneath them. Shadows need something to be cast against, and the walls here gave back light without giving anything else.

Servants passed her in the corridors. She counted eleven before she reached the central stair, and she realized on the eleventh that none of them had made a sound. Soft shoes, yes. But no rustle of fabric, no cough, no murmur between pairs walking together. They moved like figures in a dream where everyone else has been told the rules and you haven't.

One of them—a woman carrying a stack of folded linens—turned her head as Sera passed. Their eyes met for half a heartbeat. The woman's face was pleasant, unremarkable, fortyish. And behind it, behind the eyes, there was a room with the furniture covered in white sheets.

Sera kept walking. Her hands were steady. She noted, distantly, that she would pay for that steadiness later, when the shaking arrived all at once, the way it always did.

* * *

Getting audience was almost insultingly easy. She walked into the Office of Petitions, laid her medallion on the counter, and said, "Ilvane. First Circle. I require an audience regarding irregularities in the king's public addresses." The clerk—a grey man with grey hands—stamped a form, rang a small bell, and told her His Majesty would receive her after morning devotions.

No questions. No verification. No alarm at a name that had been struck from the rolls seven years ago.

Because it couldn't see her, she thought, climbing the private stair toward the royal apartments. It sees patterns, not people. My credentials are a pattern it recognizes. My name is a pattern it deleted. I am walking through the Hollow King's house wearing a shape it remembers and a face it doesn't, and it cannot tell the difference, because telling the difference requires remembering that faces change.

The thought should have been comforting. It felt like walking across ice and hearing it sing.

Two honor guards flanked the doors to the audience chamber. They stood at parade rest, halberds gleaming, and as Sera approached she watched their chests and saw that neither of them breathed. Not shallowly. Not slowly. At all. Their armor rose and fell with perfect mechanical stillness, and when she stopped before them, both heads turned in unison—one beat, two, like clockwork—and four empty eyes regarded her.

"Halt," said the left guard. "State your purpose."

"Sera Ilvane, First Circle, Office of Inquiry. I hold an appointment with His Majesty."

A pause. Somewhere far below, she imagined she could hear the great feeding-halls of the palace, the sound of a thousand servants eating meals they couldn't taste.

"Purpose confirmed," said the right guard. "Devotion precedes audience. Kneel."

She knelt. What else do you do? The guards' voices had been identical—not similar, identical, down to the grain of the consonants. Two throats, one speaker. She kept her eyes on the floor and recited, silently, the names of every village she'd ever served as inquisitor. Forty-one names. She held onto them like rungs on a ladder over a drop.

The doors opened.

* * *

The throne room of Corvane had been built to make visitors feel small before God and king in equal measure. Vaulted ceiling painted with the Ascension of Aldric the Founder, pillars carved with the lineage of kings, windows of stained glass throwing reds and golds and deep blues across a floor of polished marble.

The colors were gone.

Not faded—gone. The ceiling fresco showed figures in shades of grey moving through a grey sky. The stained glass let in light the color of old teeth. Even the marble underfoot had gone the pale, dry white of bone left in the sun. Sera had seen this room at nineteen, on the day she took her oaths, and she remembered—oh, she remembered—the blue of that glass, blue like the deep sea, blue like a bruise healing. The memory ached in her like a missing tooth.

And on the throne, the King of Corvane sat waiting for her.

He was smaller than she remembered. Or perhaps the throne had grown. He wore the full regalia—the crowned helm of state, the mantle worked with gold thread, the scepter laid across his knees—and every piece of it fit him the way a costume fits a scarecrow. His hands, emerging from sleeves that swallowed them, were clean and ageless and utterly still. He did not shift. He did not breathe heavily. He sat with the patience of furniture.

"Approach," he said, and his voice was warm. That was the worst part, Sera thought later. It was a good voice. A king's voice, rich and kind, the voice of a man who had once been loved. It came out of that still face like music from a broken instrument, beautiful and meaningless.

She crossed the marble. Knelt again, because protocol demanded it, and because kneeling gave her an excuse to keep her eyes lowered while she fought her body's animal insistence on running.

"Rise, daughter of the realm." The warm voice again. "Corvane rejoices in her faithful. These are golden years. The harvest is abundant. The harvest is abundant. The granaries stand full, and the hearts of the people stand fuller."

Sera rose. Looked up. Met the king's eyes.

There was nothing in them.

She had seen dead men's eyes. She had seen the eyes of the hollow-eyed servant, Tam Voss, babbling in his cell about *the thing wearing his crown*. She had thought herself prepared. She was not prepared. The king's eyes were open, wet, alive in every physical particular—and they reflected nothing. Not the grey light from the windows. Not her own face, though she stood close enough to count the pores of his skin. Light went into those eyes and did not come back out, and some deep, pre-verbal part of her brain screamed that this was wrong, wrong the way a face without a mouth would be wrong, wrong at the level of God's design.

They weren't eyes. They were holes shaped like eyes, and something behind them was looking through.

"Your Majesty," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, professional, the First Circle voice. "I've come regarding irregularities in your public addresses."

"Irregularities." The king smiled. The smile reached every muscle it was supposed to reach. It was flawless and it was dead. "Speak freely, daughter. A king who fears speech fears his people. The harvest is abundant."

"The address given on the Feast of Lanterns," Sera said. "And the address given yesterday. Your Majesty spoke the same passage in both. Word for word. 'The granaries stand full, and the hearts of the people stand fuller.' Also the passage concerning the traitor cartographer. Identical phrasing, identical pauses, identical—" she made herself say it— "identical errors. You named the same dead minister twice as living."

Something moved behind the king's face. Not expression. Weather. Like cloud-shadow crossing a field.

"Errors," the king repeated, tasting the word. "There are no errors in the king's speech. The king's speech is the realm's speech. The realm's speech is the king's speech. These are golden years." The warmth drained out of the voice mid-sentence, like a tide going out, and what remained was flat and vast and interested. "You have brought me a question, little clerk. Questions are seeds. Seeds are food. Ask."

Every hair on Sera's body stood up. *Little clerk.* Not the king's diction. Not any human's diction. She had interviewed murderers and madmen and once, memorably, a man who believed he was a hive of bees, and none of them had produced this—the sense of a vast attention folding itself down small to peer at her through the keyhole of a face.

She made herself ask the question she'd come to ask. "Your Majesty, who wrote today's address?"

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted. In a room that size, with guards at the doors and servants somewhere in the walls, four seconds of silence is a chasm.

"Why," the king said at last, and now the voice was the warm voice again, restored like a scene replaying, "I wrote it myself, of course. As I write all my addresses. As my father wrote his. As his father wrote his. 'Let the fields remember the sower, and the sower the fields, and both the crown that binds them.' A fine line. My father's finest. I use it often. I use it often. I use it—"

The king stopped.

His head tilted. Slowly, joint by joint, like a puppet finding a new position its strings allowed. And for one moment—one suspended, screaming moment—the stillness of the face broke apart, and Sera saw what was underneath the performance: nothing performing. A man-shaped absence, wearing forty years of borrowed gestures, running through speeches recorded so long ago that the original audience was dust.

Then the warmth returned, seamless. "Is there anything further, daughter of the realm? The king is generous with his time. The king is generous. The harvest is abundant."

"No, Your Majesty." Her voice held. She would think about that later, how it held. "Blessings on the crown."

"Blessings on the crown," the king echoed, and smiled his dead smile, and Sera bowed and walked backward out of the presence of the King of Corvane with her spine straight and her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

* * *

She made it down the private stair, through the Office of Petitions, past the grey clerk who didn't look up, and out the Inquisitors' Door into the ordinary stink of the city before her legs informed her that they were done pretending. She found an alley between a dyer's and a shuttered chapel, put her back against the wall, and slid down it until she was sitting in the muck, breathing.

The shaking came, as scheduled. She let it. She'd learned long ago that fighting it only extended the lease.

*A puppet of meat and ritual.* Juniper had said it weeks ago, beside a dying god's shrine, and Sera had wanted to believe they were wrong—or right, but in the survivable way, the way where a monster wears a king and you can cut the strings. She had walked into that throne room holding onto one secret hope: that somewhere in there, buried under the thing, was a man. A hollowed man, a ruined man, but a man. Someone to save. Someone whose rescue would make all of this mean something.

There was no one in there.

She had looked into the king's eyes and seen the exact place where a soul goes when it's eaten, and there was no prisoner behind the walls of that absence, no seed of Aldric IV waiting to be watered back to life. Whatever the entity had taken, it had taken all of him, decades ago, and what remained was a glove. A glove that smiled. A glove that remembered speeches its hand had never written and loved subjects it had never seen and would go on doing it, forever, while the realm starved on its own forgotten past.

Nothing left to save but the body.

The thought arrived with terrible clarity, the clarity that comes after hope dies—clean, cold, almost restful. Because it simplified things. You cannot negotiate with a glove. You cannot reform it or redeem it or weep it into humanity. You can only take it off the hand that wears it.

And the hand—that devouring, patient, hungry thing—could be dealt with too. Juniper had drawn the door it came through. Juniper could close it. Everything Sera had lost—her rank, her faith, the Office she'd given her life to—all of it had been the entity tidying up loose threads, silencing anyone who noticed the seams. She hadn't been demoted. She'd been *edited*. And editing could be undone.

She got up. Washed her hands in the dyer's runoff, which stained them blue, and found she didn't mind. Color. She wanted color around her, anywhere, even accidental.

Wren was waiting at the end of the alley, perched on a rain barrel like a small dark bird, and the child's eyes were wide and wet.

"You saw him," Wren said. It wasn't a question. The god-voices were quiet in the child's mouth now, just Wren's own voice, which was somehow worse. "You saw what's in the crown."

"I saw," Sera said.

"He talked to me too. Before. When they took me." Wren hugged thin knees. "It said it liked the way I sounded. It said the gods' names were the best meal it ever ate, and it was still hungry, and soon it was going to eat the whole city's remembering at once. All of it. Every name, every grave, every mother's face. One big feast." The child looked up, and in the grey light their eyes caught something—grief, maybe, or the reflection of gods too diminished to shine. "It's almost ready. I heard it getting ready."

Sera's blood went cold and then, strangely, calm.

"Then we're out of time," she said. "Come on. We need to find Juniper."

Behind them, above the grey rooftops, the palace bells began to ring noon—and Sera realized, with a lurch, that she had been in the throne room for less than half an hour, though her body ached as if she'd stood before that hungry absence for a year.

The bells rang twelve times.

Then, softly, out of rhythm, they rang thirteen.
