## 22. The Road Out

The city woke before I did, the way cities do—shutters banging, water sloshed into gutters, a baker's boy singing badly two streets over. I lay on the narrow bed in the room above the map-shop that was no longer mine and listened to Corvane being ordinary, and I thought: *this is what we fought for. This. The terrible, beautiful nothing of a morning.*

Seven years ago I had slipped out of this city in the dark with ash on my hands and my own name torn out of me like a bad tooth. Today I would walk out in full light, carrying every memory I had once sold to be rid of.

It is strange, the arithmetic of healing. You spend so long paying anything to forget, and then you pay everything to remember, and at the end of it you are left standing in the same body with all the same debts, only now you can name them.

I rose. I dressed. I did not look at the bed across the room where Wren had slept for the last month, because Wren had left before dawn already, running barefoot through the dew to the village that had remembered its name again, and if I looked too long at the small dent their head had left in the pillow I would not have been able to do what came next.

There was a knock. Two knocks, unhurried, knuckle rather than fist. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat.

"Come in, Sera."

She came in wearing her old grey coat—the inquisitor's coat, though she'd had the sigils cut off it, leaving pale ghosts of thread where they'd been. Her hair was shorter than when I'd met her; there was a new scar along her jaw from the throne room, thin as a drawn line on a chart. She carried two cups of tea and the particular expression of a woman who has rehearsed an argument she expects to lose.

"You're leaving today," she said. Not a question. She set the cups down on the table by the window, where the light came in gold and honest.

"I'm leaving today."

"The council offered you the Surveyorship. Lands, title, a hall full of apprentices." She ticked them off like charges. "Wren asked me to tell you they've started a shrine-map of the eastern hills and they want your hand on the first page."

"I know."

"And still you're going."

I picked up the tea because my hands needed something honest to do. "You saw what's under the vault," I said. "You held my head while I screamed. You know what the person who drew that door was made of." I turned the cup in my fingers. "Corvane doesn't need that person back. It needs the people who stopped her. And those people—" I gestured vaguely at myself, at the whole tired architecture of me, "—do better at a distance. Like gods. Like weather."

Sera sat down across from me. For a long moment she said nothing, which from Sera was a whole speech.

"When I was demoted," she said finally, "they took my office, my cases, my seal. They gave me a desk in a corridor and told me to be grateful. And I stayed, because I thought staying was loyalty. It took me seven years to learn the difference between loyalty and being kept." She looked up. "I'm not making your argument for you, Juniper. I'm telling you I understand it better than you'd wish."

"Sera—"

"I'm coming with you."

The words landed in the room and just sat there, enormous.

"Don't," I said. "Don't do this. You spent your whole life earning your way back into that city's good graces. You're the one who insisted the king face a public reckoning instead of a quiet tribunal. You built something here. They'll need someone who knows how justice works when the machinery doesn't."

"They'll have a dozen people who know how justice works." She said it flatly, without pride. "What they won't have, in about a month, is anyone who remembers what it looks like when a city starts forgetting itself. I've seen the beginning of that disease twice now. Once in the palace. Once in a village with a blank stone." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "Besides. Someone has to keep you honest. You did promise to draw honestly this time. I intend to hold you to it, cartographer. Every road. Every border. Even the ones that make you look bad."

"That could take years."

"Good." The ghost of a smile. "I'm told exile is very restful."

* * *

I spent the morning doing the last of the leaving.

The shop below had been given over to the network of small mercies—the charm-buyers, the well-keepers, the woman whose daughter had been the first prayer answered in seven years. Marta ran it now, a broad, capable woman with ink permanently stained into the creases of her knuckles, and she had turned my old counter into a school bench where three apprentices were learning to read the land's memory from practice-charts I'd drawn in my last week in the city.

I stood in the doorway and watched them a while. One of them, a boy of perhaps twelve, was frowning at a chart of the river district, his eyes half-lidded the way mine went when I listened for the roads underneath. Suddenly he pointed. "Here. There was a street here. Before. It went down to the water and then it didn't anymore, and everyone thinks it never did, but it *did*—"

"That's right," Marta said quietly. "That's exactly right."

He looked up, flushed with it, and caught me watching. He knew who I was. They all did, in this city; that was part of why I couldn't stay. He didn't gawk or bow or ask for a blessing. He just said, "Will you draw us more charts? For practice?"

"As many as you can wear out," I said.

Marta walked me to the door. At the threshold she stopped and pressed something into my hand: a small paper packet, sealed with plain wax. Inside, I knew without opening it, would be one of my own quiet charms—a fever-charm, a finding-charm, something harmless and kind.

"For the road," she said. "In case the world gets loud again."

"Marta—"

"Don't argue with me. I bought enough of these off you to know how stubborn you get." Her voice roughened. "You sold small mercies in the dark for seven years, Juniper Vane. Whatever you were before—whatever you think cancels that out—it doesn't. A kindness is a kindness even when the person giving it is running from themselves."

I am not ashamed to say I could not answer her. I hugged her instead, brief and hard, and went out into the street before either of us could make it worse.

* * *

The last thing was the hardest, and it wasn't a thing at all. It was a hill east of the walls, and a child sitting on top of it.

I climbed up through the long grass with my satchel bumping my hip, and Wren heard me coming the way they always did—not with ears but with something older. They didn't turn around. They sat cross-legged in the wind, a stick in their hand, drawing in the dirt: little spirals, little stars, the shorthand of returning gods.

"You're going," Wren said.

"I'm going."

"The voices say you shouldn't." They scratched another spiral. "The hill-god says doors open both ways. The river-one says people who run always come back. The little one under the well says—" they paused, listening to something I couldn't hear, and their brow furrowed, "—the well-one says you taught it that even a crack lets the light through. It likes you."

"Tell the well-one I liked it too."

Wren finally turned. Their eyes had settled since the throne room—still god-touched, still lit from behind, but *theirs* now, anchored in a child's face instead of flickering between a hundred borrowed ones. "Are you going because of the bad memories? Because of who you were?"

"Partly."

"The voices say that's stupid." But there was no cruelty in it; there never was. "They say a person isn't one thing. They say I'm a hundred things and none of them and me, all at once, and nobody tells *me* to leave." They considered. "Although I did burn down a granary once. Before you found me. So maybe they have a point."

I laughed despite myself, and the laugh hurt, the way good things hurt on the far side of grief. I sat down beside them in the grass.

"Wren. Listen to me. This village remembered its name because of you. Those shrines are waking up because of you. You don't need me here—and more than that, Corvane needs to learn to love what you are without me standing behind you taking the credit or the blame. That's how it stays real after I'm gone."

They were quiet for a long time. Then, grudgingly, in the tone of a child conceding a strategic point: "The voices say that's actually quite wise. Which is annoying."

"I know."

"But—" and here they leaned against my shoulder, small and warm and smelling of grass and woodsmoke, "—you'll come back. When the maps are done. When you've walked everywhere there is to walk. You'll come back and show me."

I looked east, past the village roofs, past the hills, to where the land went soft and blue with distance, and beyond distance to the places no chart had ever reached. The spaces between worlds. The blank margins of the great atlas. All of it waiting, patient as paper.

"Yeah," I said. "I'll come back. That's a promise with a road attached, and you know what I think about roads."

"That they're how people find each other," Wren recited, bored, having heard it a hundred times. "Yes. Fine." But they squeezed my arm hard enough to mean it.

* * *

We went out through the eastern gate at dawn the next day, Sera and I, on foot, because horses were for people with destinations and we had only a direction.

The gate wardens let us pass without ceremony—one of them saluted Sera, awkwardly, out of old habit, and she returned it with the flat courtesy of a soldier retiring a flag. Then the wall was behind us and the road unrolled ahead, pale in the early light, worn by ten thousand feet toward places neither of us had ever been.

For the first mile neither of us spoke. The city smoke hung behind us like a grey banner. Ahead, the fields were silvered with dew, and somewhere off to the north a lark was spending itself recklessly against the sky.

"You know what I keep thinking," Sera said at last. "That first night. The alley. I had you cornered, and every rule I had ever believed in said *arrest them*, and I let you go because I wanted answers more than I wanted to be right." She shook her head. "Seven years of training, undone by curiosity. My superiors would have called it corruption."

"Your superiors fed their city to a hungry god," I said. "I'd stop citing them."

"Fair."

We walked. The sun cleared the hills and the dew burned off and the road warmed under our boots, and I felt it then—that thing I had been afraid of feeling, the thing I'd traded away pieces of myself to avoid ever feeling again. The land began to speak.

Not loudly. Not the screaming flood of the vault, where every stolen year had come home at once. Just a murmur, low and constant, like a river under ice. The road remembered the pilgrims who had worn it. The field remembered a fire, long ago, and the green that came after. An old boundary-stone remembered the names of two farms that had argued over it for a century and loved each other all the same.

And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, at the very edge of hearing: *further*. The sense of unmapped country beyond the hills, realms folded against our own like pages pressed together in a closed book. Places where the old gods had gone to wait out the hunger. Places with roads that had never been drawn, waiting for someone to listen and write them down true.

My hand ached for a pen.

I stopped, right there in the middle of the road, and dug into my satchel, and brought out what I had bought three days ago from a stationer in the Lowmarket: fresh paper, heavy and cream-white, bound into a book with nothing written in it anywhere. Empty pages. Blank as the elder's stone tablet had once been. Blank as I had tried to make myself.

"What is it?" Sera asked.

I held the book up to the light. "A first page," I said.

I opened it on my knee, right there in the road, and at the top of the first leaf I wrote, in a careful hand:

*Road east from Corvane. Dawn, clear skies. Travelling with Sera Ilvane, formerly of the Inquisition, currently of no institution whatsoever, by choice.*

Then, beneath it, after a moment's thought:

*Drawn honestly.*

Sera read it upside down. "No mention of the door?"

"No mention of the door. That story's told. This one's just starting." I closed the book and stowed it, and we walked on, and the road went on with us, remembering.

Behind us, Corvane's bells rang the hour—ordinary bells, marking ordinary time, for a city that would argue its way forward without us. Ahead, the hills opened like a gate. Somewhere beyond them, the blank countries waited, and the gods who had hidden in them, and whatever else the wide world had been keeping safe while we put our own house in order.

I had been a mapmaker who burned her maps, a seller of charms who feared her own gift, a door-maker, a debt, a warning. I had been, for seven years, a person shaped entirely by what she refused to remember.

Now I was only this: a woman on a road at dawn, with ink and paper and company, walking toward everything she hadn't seen yet.

It was, all things considered, a very good place to begin.

* * *

Three days out, at a crossroads where four worn ways met under an ancient signpost stripped of its letters, we found the first impossible thing.

Sera spotted it—or rather, she spotted the absence of it, which is an inquisitor's trick I was starting to learn. "Juniper," she said slowly. "This crossroads should be on your charts. Every trade route in the eastern provinces passes through here. There should be a marker. A shrine, at minimum. Crossroads like this *collect* shrines."

"There's nothing," I agreed. "No marker. No shrine. No—" I stopped, and crouched, and pressed my palm flat to the earth, and listened.

The land-memory was there, deep and rich as ever. But at the crossroads' heart, precisely where the four ways crossed, there was a smoothness. A blankness. Not eroded—*eaten*. A circle of silence maybe ten paces wide, neat as a coin, as if something had knelt down here once, long ago or recently, and drunk the crossing dry.

And at the circle's edge, half-buried in the grass, a single object: a strip of pale bark, peeled from some tree that did not grow in these provinces, marked with a spiral in charcoal.

The same spiral Wren drew. The shorthand of returning gods.

But this one had been crossed out.

Sera drew her knife—not from fear, exactly, but from the habit of a woman who had learned what blankness costs. "Something got here first," she said.

I stood, and looked east, where the road ran on into country no chart of mine had ever touched, and I understood that the world we had saved was larger than the wound we had healed, and that hunger, like mercy, travels.

"Well," I said, taking out the fresh book, opening it to the second page. "Then we'd better find out what's eating the crossroads."

And I wrote it down—honestly, completely, every detail—because that was the vow now, the whole of it, the road and the record and the two of us walking into the unmapped dark with our eyes open.

The lark sang. The pages waited. We went on.
