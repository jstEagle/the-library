## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tomorrow

The dead of Thornfell did not believe in sleeping in.

They began before dawn, as they always did — a low murmur like wind through a hollow reed, rising from the bones Ansel kept in the workshop's cedar cabinets and the ones buried deeper in the mountain, the ones that needed no cabinet at all. Ansel had learned long ago to sort the voices the way a shepherd sorts sheep: the old grievances in one pen, the lost ones in another, the angry ones left to bleat at the fence until they wore themselves out.

*Cold,* said a woman who had died in the winter of Ansel's grandmother's grandmother. *It is always cold up here.*

*You put me facing the wall again,* complained a man who had been dead so long his name had worn smooth, like a river stone. *I want to see the door.*

"You're a rib," Ansel said, not looking up from their work. "Ribs don't have eyes."

*I had eyes once.*

"Then you'll forgive me for prioritizing the ones who can still use them."

Ansel Oyelaran worked by lamplight in a workshop built into the shoulder of the mountain, half carved from living rock, half timbered with black pine that had been standing when the Crown was young. The air smelled of bone dust and beeswax and the sharp resin of juniper smoke, which they burned not for the dead — the dead had no noses — but for themselves, because it was easier to breathe around grief when grief smelled of something green.

On the bench before them lay a femur, pale and clean, no longer than a forearm. It had belonged to a child. That was the whole of what Ansel knew, and the whole of what mattered: a child, drowned in the Whitemere river two valleys east, washed down through the gorges until the water gave her up against the stones below Thornfell Pass. No name came with her. The river had taken that too, or her family lived far enough away that no one had come asking.

So Ansel would give her one.

That was the work, when it came down to it. Not the smuggling — though the smuggling paid for the lamp oil and the juniper and the bread — but this. Bone-carving was an old trade in the mountains, older than the Crown, older than the roads the smugglers used. Every life deserved a name cut into something that would outlast the flesh. Most carvers worked from memory or from parish rolls. Ansel worked from the source.

*Her name was Liss,* said a voice near the window, faint as frost forming. One of the pass-dead, a mule driver killed in a rockslide forty years back. He liked to watch Ansel work; he had been a talker in life and saw no reason to change. *Lissenna. Her mother called her Liss. I carried her father's grain sacks the summer she was born. He sang to her belly, bad singing, off-key.*

"Liss," Ansel repeated, and tested the sound against the bone in their mind. The dead murmured agreement, a ripple through the workshop like wind over barley. Somewhere in the cedar cabinets, the woman who was always cold went quiet, listening.

Ansel picked up the finest graver, the one made from a hawk's leg-bone hafted in antler, and set the first stroke.

There was a discipline to it. Cut with the grain of the bone, never across. Name first, then the line beneath it — *the river holds, the mountain remembers* — then the small marks that told the dead where the new one belonged among them. Ansel's hands moved without hurry. Twenty years of this. They had been younger than the drowned girl when the voices started, and there were days — more of them lately, if Ansel was honest, and honesty was a habit the dead enforced — when they could not remember what silence felt like.

Not that they wanted silence. That was the strange truth of it, the truth they'd stopped examining years ago. The voices were a wound, yes. But they were also a kind of company no living person could offer. The dead of Thornfell were petty and cold and repetitive and endlessly, heartbreakingly human, and they had never once lied to Ansel. Not in twenty years. Living people lied the way they breathed. The dead had nothing left to gain from it.

*Deeper on the L,* the mule driver advised. *She was a deep child. Watched everything.*

Ansel cut deeper.

Outside, the light came up grey through the workshop's single window, and the mountain showed itself in pieces — the black spine of Thornfell proper, the white scar of the high pass where the smugglers' road threaded between the witch-finders' watchtowers, and below, far below, the thin silver thread of the lowland road where, sooner or later, trouble always came walking.

Trouble, in fact, was already climbing toward them. Ansel just didn't know it yet.

* * *

The peddler arrived mid-morning, when the name was finished and waxed and wrapped in linen, waiting for whoever would come to claim it — or, if no one came, for the niche in the memorial wall where Ansel kept the unclaimed, each one named, none of them forgotten.

Ansel heard him before they saw him, because the dead announced him.

*Peddler,* said three voices at once, overlapping, the way the dead spoke when something interested them. *Peddler, peddler, he has a thing, he has a thing that hums.*

Ansel set down the graver. In twenty years, the dead had never described an object as humming.

The man who came up the goat track to the workshop door was small and round and sweating despite the cold, leading a mule burdened with pots and ribbons and tin mirrors, the usual glittering nothing of a mountain peddler's trade. His coat was good wool gone shabby at the cuffs. His smile arrived a moment before the rest of his face, as though it had been sent ahead to negotiate.

"Master carver! Or mistress. Or — forgive me, the mountains make a man cautious." He doffed a hat that had seen better decades. "Tam Vetch, licensed trader, and I've got needles, ribbon, salt, a mirror that flatters—"

"I don't need anything."

"Everyone needs something. Salt, at least. Everyone needs salt." His eyes, quick as a magpie's, went past Ansel's shoulder into the workshop, to the bench, to the cabinets. Peddlers always looked. Most of them saw only bone-work and looked away, politely bored. This one's gaze snagged and stayed.

"The dead keep you busy," he said.

Ansel didn't move. "The dead keep everyone busy. They're just politer about it here."

"Mm." Tam Vetch scratched under his hat. Then, with the sudden decisiveness of a man jumping off a ledge, he turned to the mule and dug deep in the packs — deeper than ribbon, deeper than salt — and came out with a book.

Even from the doorway Ansel could see it was ruined. Water-stained, swollen, its leather cover bloomed white with mold along the spine, pages furred at the edges where the damp had gotten in and done its slow work. It looked like something pulled from a river. Or a grave.

"This," Vetch said, "I cannot sell in the lowlands. I've tried. Books frighten people down there unless the Crown printed them, and this one—" he patted it gingerly, "—this one the Crown very much did not print. But you're a bone-carver. You deal in remembering. And I found it in a drowned house on the Whitemere, and I'll be honest with you, master carver, because you strike me as a person honesty works on: I don't like having it in my pack. It hums."

The dead, pressed close behind Ansel now, a crowd at a doorway, agreed.

"What do you want for it?"

"For you? Nothing." Vetch pushed it into Ansel's hands before they could refuse. "For me, the pleasure of being rid of it. Consider it payment for the story you'll let me tell in the next valley — how the strange carver of Thornfell bought a cursed book without haggling. That's worth more than coin. Stories are."

He was gone before Ansel could ask anything else, mule trotting, hat bobbing down the track, and the last thing the dead said of him was *he lies about small things so you'll believe the big one*, which was, Ansel reflected, the most honest thing anyone had said all morning.

* * *

The workshop was quiet — the ordinary quiet, the murmur-quiet — while Ansel cleared a space on the bench and set the book down in the lamplight.

Up close it was worse. The water damage wasn't random; it had a pattern, like the water had been looking for something. The cover bore no title, only a symbol stamped deep enough to survive the soaking: a crown, and beneath the crown, broken in half, a key.

Ansel opened it.

The pages were vellum, expensive once, handwritten in an iron-gall ink that had gone brown with age. The hand changed several times through the book — different scribes, different generations, each copying and adding to the last, the way parish records grew, the way scars grow. Margins crowded with notes in perhaps six different scripts. Some entries were prayers. Some were accounts, dry as dust: sums paid, prisoners transferred, quantities of something measured in *vessels*. Ansel's eye snagged on those and slid off, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

And threaded through all of it, recurring every few pages like a refrain, were the prophecies.

Most were the usual mountain-madness. *When the twin stars marry, a red harvest.* *Beware the king who counts backward.* The sort of thing every hedge-witch scribbled and every wise woman sold. Ansel skimmed them with the mild contempt of someone who heard actual ghosts daily and remained unimpressed by paper predictions.

Until the page fell open — fell, as though the binding had been waiting — to a page near the end.

A date was circled there. Circled twice, the ink pressed hard enough to tear the vellum, by a hand that must have been shaking.

Ansel knew the date the way every soul in the mountains knew it: by the almanac nailed inside the workshop door, by the smugglers' tallies, by the feast-days marked in charcoal on the lintel. It was not tomorrow. It was not next week.

It was tonight.

Below the circle, in that same shaking hand, two names.

*Freya.*

And beside it, in ink of a different color — added later, added recently, the brown-black of fresh iron-gall over old — a second name that stopped Ansel's breath in their chest the way the river stops at the falls.

*Oyelaran.*

Their own name. Their own surname, spelled correctly, spelled the way no one outside the valley spelled it, the way only Ansel wrote it in the corner of every memorial bone before sealing the wax.

The workshop went silent.

Not quiet — silent. For the first time in twenty years, every voice, every grievance, every cold complaint and lost wandering whisper, stopped at once, the way birds stop before a storm comes off the peaks. Ansel stood in a silence so total they could hear their own blood, their own heartbeat, the lamp flame eating its oil, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling:

The dead had never lied to them.

Neither did paper.

*Read it,* said the old queen's voice — the one voice among the hundreds that Ansel had never been able to place, deep and imperious and rare, surfacing perhaps once a year like a whale coming up to breathe. *Read it aloud, bone-carver. All of it. Before nightfall.*

Ansel's hands were steady. That surprised them. Twenty years of the dead had taught their hands to be steady at exactly the moments steadiness made no sense.

They turned back to the beginning of the circled page, and read the passage above the date, aloud, into the silence, their voice sounding enormous and strange in a room that had never once been truly empty:

*"In the hour when the crown forgets its king, an Unbinding shall come out of the high places: one who carries the names of the dead, and one who carries the truth of the written word. Together they shall unmake the thing that wears the king's face —"*

The lamp guttered. Outside, far down the valley, on the lowland road, a bell began to ring — not the smugglers' signal, not any bell Ansel knew. A warning bell. And under it, faint but rising, the sound of many hooves taking the switchbacks fast.

The dead came back all at once, a tide, a roar, dozens of voices speaking over one another, and for the first time in twenty years Ansel couldn't sort them, couldn't pen them, because they were all saying the same thing:

*They're coming. They're coming for you. Run, bone-carver — run —*

Ansel looked down at the book, at their own name sitting beside a stranger's, at tonight's date circled twice in a shaking hand, and reached for their cloak.
