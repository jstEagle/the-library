## 2. Freya Falls Out of the Sky

The pages were going to get her killed.

Freya had known that for eleven days — since the night she'd knelt in the dark of the Royal Archive's restricted vault with a letter-knife in her teeth and her mentor's blood still tacky under her fingernails, and cut six leaves out of a book older than the kingdom. She'd known it when she sewed them into the lining of her coat. She'd known it every morning since, waking in ditches and haylofts with the coat buttoned to her throat like armor, feeling the paper against her ribs the way other people felt a heartbeat.

Knowing it didn't make the Thornfell road any less long, or any less empty, or any less full of the sound of hoofbeats behind her.

She counted them without turning around. Four horses, maybe five, moving at a canter — not a charge. Witch-finders didn't charge. Charging was for soldiers, and soldiers wanted you dead or gone. Witch-finders wanted you *kept*. They moved through the world the way a spider moves through its own web, patient, certain that everything tangled eventually.

Freya ran anyway. She'd been running for eleven days and her legs had stopped being legs somewhere around dawn and become two hot wires of pain attached to her hips, but she ran, because the alternative was standing on this rutted mountain road with her hands up while men in grey iron asked her questions about what she carried.

The road bent along the shoulder of the first true peak of the Thornfells, and the trees closed overhead — black pines, old ones, the kind that made even noon feel like a held breath. The light went green and dim. Her breath came in rags. Somewhere below her, a river said something cold and continuous to the stones.

*They'll search the coat,* she thought. *They'll find the pages. And then they won't kill me, because killing is quick, and the Crown has never once in its history chosen the quick thing.*

She stumbled on a root, caught herself against a pine trunk, and in the half-second her palm was flat against the bark she heard it — not the hoofbeats, not the river, but a voice, thin as thread:

*—left side, girl. Left.*

Freya froze. The voice had come from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices come in dreams. It had sounded, absurdly, like an old woman who found her disappointing.

Then the world went white at the edges, because the hoofbeats had stopped, and a man's voice called down the road behind her, pleasant as a market-day greeting:

"Traveler! A moment, if you please."

She turned. Three riders sat their horses twenty paces back, and a fourth was dismounting. They wore the grey lacquered iron of the Crown's Inquisitorial Office, faces shadowed under wide brims, and the one walking toward her had his visor raised to show a face like a well-fed priest: soft, smiling, and utterly without warmth. Captain Serah Voss rode at the rear, and even from here Freya could see the difference — Voss sat her horse like the horse was furniture, and her eyes did the work her captain's smile pretended at.

"You look tired," the smiling one said. "And lost. And carrying rather more than a traveler usually carries." He extended a gloved hand, palm up. "Coat, if you would. Just a formality. The mountains are full of smugglers and worse, and we're sworn to keep the King's roads clean."

Every story Freya had ever read about people in her exact position ended the same way, and none of them ended with handing over the coat.

"I'm expected," she said. "In Thornfell village. My uncle's a factor there — wool."

"Mm," said the witch-finder, and smiled wider, and drew a slim iron rod from his belt. It began, very softly, to hum. Freya had read about those rods in confiscated reports she'd been forbidden to read and had read anyway. They sang when they came near oath-magic, grave-gifts, things done in the names of the dead. Things like a book that had spent a century in a sealed vault absorbing whatever it was books absorbed.

The rod hummed louder as he stepped toward her.

That was when the sky fell on her.

Not the sky. A rope. It came out of the green dark above the road like a striking snake, looped twice around her waist before she understood she was being grabbed, and hauled. Freya left the ground. The witch-finder lunged for her ankle and got a fistful of air; the humming rod shrieked and went silent as its owner staggered back into his fellows. Above her, branches whipped past, and someone above her — *above her*, in the trees, which was impossible, which was the kind of thing that happened in ballads about smugglers and not in real life — swore creatively in a mountain accent and hauled hand over hand.

"Lighter than you look," muttered the voice belonging to the rope, and then the canopy swallowed her, and the road, and the three grey riders, and Captain Voss's narrowed eyes, and Freya was swinging through darkness that smelled of pine sap and cold stone.

* * *

They put her down in a place that should not have existed.

One moment she was being dragged through branches; the next, the branches parted around a cleft in the cliff face no wider than a coffin stood on end, and then she was through it, and it opened into a corridor of stone — a natural seam in the mountain, roofed with interlocking slabs, floored with gravel, running back into the dark like the throat of something enormous. Behind her, the cleft was already invisible; the branches had sprung back over it as if the mountain were closing its mouth.

Her rescuer dropped down beside her without a sound.

He — she corrected herself after a second look; the figure was narrow-shouldered, sharp-faced, androgynous in the way of people built by hard living rather than by any plan — wore oiled leather the color of wet slate, a hood pushed back from close-cropped dark hair, and an expression of profound irritation. Around their neck, on a cord, hung a single carved bone the size of a thumb joint.

"Don't talk," the rescuer said. "Sound carries funny in here. Comes out the far side sometimes." They cocked their head, listening to something Freya couldn't hear. "Two of them are dismounting. They'll walk the road past us. Stay still, stay quiet, and don't touch anything."

"Who—" Freya started.

The rescuer's glare could have curdled milk at forty paces. Freya shut her mouth.

So she waited, in the dark, chest heaving, while somewhere beyond the stone the witch-finders walked the road beneath her. She heard their boots on gravel, muffled, distant, as though through water. She heard the smiling one say something she couldn't make out, and Captain Voss answer in a voice like a drawn blade being sheathed again — unhurried, because she knew she could redraw it whenever she liked. Then the sounds faded, and faded, and were gone.

Only then did the rescuer relax, all at once, like a bowstring unstrung.

"They'll circle back within the hour," they said. "Voss doesn't give up; I've watched her hunt for three years. So we move." They turned and looked at Freya properly for the first time, and their eyes — pale grey, the color of the sky before snow — went straight to her coat. "You're the archivist."

Freya's blood went cold in a way the mountain chill hadn't managed. "I'm sorry?"

"The capital's been plastered with your face for a week and a half. *Disgraced royal archivist, Freya Solenne, wanted for theft of Crown property and crimes against the realm.* They've got your likeness in every waystation from here to the sea, and it's a good likeness, which must be flattering." The rescuer's mouth twisted. "You're also, according to every rumor that comes up the smuggler roads, carrying pages out of a book that officially does not exist. So." They spread their hands. "You're either the most dangerous person to enter these mountains in a hundred years, or the most foolish. I haven't decided which."

"And you are?" Freya said, because her mother had raised her to be polite even to kidnappers, and because asking questions was the only weapon she'd ever been any good with.

"Ansel Oyelaran. Bone-carver. Guide." A pause. "Reluctantly, in your case."

"You pulled me off a road with a rope."

"I pulled you off a road because Marta's caravan is camped two miles north and witch-finders crawling all over our road is bad for everyone in it, including sixty people who've never heard your name." Ansel shouldered a coil of rope and turned deeper into the seam. "You falling out of the sky onto it was an accident. My saving you was arithmetic. Don't mistake it for kindness."

"That's the least convincing speech I've ever heard," Freya said, "and last month I listened to the Lord Treasurer explain grain tariffs."

Ansel stopped. For a moment Freya thought she'd be left in the dark entirely. Then, over their shoulder, those snow-grey eyes regarded her with something that wasn't quite amusement and wasn't quite anger.

"Keep up," they said, "and maybe I'll decide you're dangerous after all."

* * *

The seam in the mountain went on for the better part of an hour, and Freya spent all of it cataloguing impossibilities.

First: the passage itself. It wasn't on any map she'd ever copied, and she had copied maps of the Thornfells — the Archive held four surveys of the range, commissioned across two centuries, and none of them showed so much as a goat track through this face of the mountain. Yet Ansel moved through it like a person walking their own hallway, never hesitating at forks, never needing light beyond what leaked through cracks overhead.

Second: the bones. Twice, Ansel paused at alcoves in the rock where small offerings sat — carved bone figures, weathered smooth, arranged with care. Each time, Ansel touched two fingers to their own pendant and murmured something too low for Freya to catch. Each time, Freya felt the hair rise on her arms, because the air in the alcoves was *warm*, in a mountain whose breath should have been ice.

Third, and worst: Ansel kept talking to nobody.

It wasn't madness, exactly. It was more like a person conversing with a room full of colleagues only they could see — little pauses, tilted head, a snort of laughter at some private joke, a muttered "*yes, I know*" or "*that's not helpful*". Once, passing a shelf of stone where water dripped, Ansel stopped dead and said, sharply, "Because she's *Crown*, that's why," to the empty air, and then glanced back at Freya with an expression of such naked guilt that Freya felt she'd interrupted something intimate.

By the time the passage opened onto a ledge overlooking a hidden valley — terraced slopes, a stream, smoke rising from a cluster of turf-roofed buildings that appeared on no survey ever commissioned — Freya had assembled the pieces, because assembling pieces was the one thing she had ever been great at.

"You hear them," she said. "The dead."

Ansel went very still, halfway through coiling the rope. The valley wind moved their cropped hair. When they spoke, their voice had lost every ounce of its earlier bite and gained something rawer underneath.

"Say that again and I'll leave you on this ledge."

"The alcoves are grave-offerings. You talk to someone between the words. Your pendant is a memorial bone — I've seen the style in funerary records, northern highland tradition, pre-Conquest. And just now, in the passage, you answered a question nobody asked." Freya hugged her stolen coat around herself, feeling the pages shift against her ribs like a second heart. "I'm not going to report you. I tore six pages out of the most protected book in the kingdom and ran. We're both criminals. I'm just — I need to know what you are, because I think you might be the only person alive who can help me."

For a long moment Ansel said nothing. Below them, in the settlement, someone laughed; a dog barked; ordinary life went on, oblivious.

"The dead of Thornfell talk to me," Ansel said finally, flatly, as if reciting a confession extracted under duress. "Have done since I was nine years old. It's not a gift, whatever the ballads say. It's a job. I carry their names. I carve them into bone so they're not forgotten, and I guide smugglers through passes the dead show me, because the dead of this mountain hate the Crown, and everything that hurts the Crown pleases them." Their jaw tightened. "Which brings us to you. Because when I laid hands on that rope, every voice I know started screaming at once, and they have not shut up since. They want me to keep you. They want it *badly*. And in my experience, when the dead want something badly, it's because it's going to cost somebody everything."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "Why? What do they think I am?"

"I was hoping you'd tell me." Ansel turned fully to face her, and the afternoon light caught the bone pendant at their throat, and Freya saw, for the first time, that it was carved with a ring of tiny names too small to read. "Eleven days ago, a peddler sold me a water-stained book for the price of a decent dinner. Prophecy. Old. Most of it rotted nonsense. But one page had a date circled on it — tonight's date — and beside the date, two names written in a hand I didn't recognize." Ansel's eyes didn't waver. "*Ansel Oyelaran,* it said. And underneath, in letters that looked burned into the page rather than inked: *Freya Solenne.*"

The valley seemed to tilt. Freya gripped the rock beside her.

"That's impossible," she whispered. "Those pages — the ones I took — the prophecy of the Unbinding — it names a date and it names *the Unbinding*, and I memorized every word before I ran, and neither name was in it. I would remember. I *always* remember."

"Then either your book and mine disagree," Ansel said, "or yours is missing pages too." They stepped closer, close enough that Freya could see the exhaustion under the defiance, the fear under the exhaustion. This was not a fearless person, Freya realized. This was a person who had been afraid for years and had simply gotten very good at carrying it. "So here's how this goes, archivist. You're coming down to Marta's camp, because leaving you on a ledge for Voss to find is arithmetic too, and the numbers are worse. Tonight, by firelight, you're going to tell me exactly what you stole, exactly why, and exactly what the Crown thinks it's worth killing a whole mountain to bury. And I'm going to listen to what the dead say about it. And if the two stories match—" Ansel exhaled slowly. "Then we're both in a great deal more trouble than either of us can survive alone."

"And if they don't match?"

Ansel looked out over the valley, at the smoke and the turf roofs and the small stubborn life hidden from every map in the kingdom, and when they answered, Freya heard the dead in it somehow — a hundred voices folded into one tired human throat.

"Then I throw you off this ledge myself," they said, "and apologize to you later."

They started down the switchback trail. After a moment, Freya followed.

Behind them, far below and impossibly distant, she thought she heard — though it might have been the wind in the pines, though it might have been nothing at all — the sound of grey riders entering the valley's upper pass, and a woman's voice, calm as a closing ledger, giving orders in the name of a king who, Freya now knew with absolute certainty, was not a king at all.
