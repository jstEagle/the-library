## 3. What the Dead Remember of Kings

The smuggler's cave had a name among those who used it — the Kettle, because the mountain's breath came through a crack in the far wall and made the whole hollow whistle in cold weather like water on the boil. Tonight it whistled low and steady, and the fire Odile's people had left stacked in the old ring burned down to a red heart that threw shadows up the walls like slow birds.

Ansel sat with their back against the stone and the prophecy book open across their knees.

They had not wanted to open it. They had carried it for two days since the peddler, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of their pack like something venomous, and every time their hand drifted toward it one of the dead would clear its throat — they all did this, the dead, as if politeness survived the body — and say *not yet* or *you'll wish you hadn't* or, from old Tam the drover, who had died face-down in a snowdrift and never lost his sense of humor, *go on then, it's only your life.*

But Freya was asleep. Actually asleep, finally, curled against her pack with her torn cloak pulled to her chin and her hands still half-curled as if around pages that weren't there anymore. She'd fought sleep the way she seemed to fight everything — with argument, with stubbornness, with a scholar's conviction that if she just kept working the problem it would yield before she did. It hadn't yielded. Somewhere between the second watch and the third her eyes had closed mid-sentence, and now her breathing went slow and even, and the firelight moved on her face, and Ansel found themselves watching longer than was strictly necessary before they looked away and unwrapped the book.

*Not yet,* said the dead, in chorus, and then, quieter, almost tender: *well. Now, then.*

The book was worse by firelight. Water had swollen some pages fat and others thin as skin; the ink had run in places so that whole passages wept into grey ghosts of themselves. But the page Ansel needed — the page the peddler's finger had found, the page with tonight's date circled twice in a hand that pressed hard enough to score the paper — that page had been protected somehow, tucked inside the swell of the binding, dry as bone.

Ansel read it once silently. Then, because the dead had always heard better when words were given air, because some part of them needed to hear how it sounded outside their own skull, they read it aloud. Low. Careful. The way they spoke names into carved bone.

"*In the hundredth year of the hollow crown,*" they read, "*when the mountain forgets its dead and the dead forget their king, there shall come an Unbinding — one who owes the Crown nothing and loves its hostage wholly. By their hand the thing wearing his crown shall be put off him, and the true king shall wake, and the debt of Thornfell shall come due.*"

The fire popped.

The dead did not murmur. That was the first wrong thing. The dead always murmured — a low tide-sound of grievance and gossip and grief that Ansel had lived inside since childhood the way fish live inside water. Silence from them was like silence from the mountain itself: a held breath.

Then the voice came.

It came from everywhere in the cave at once and nowhere in particular, the way the loudest of them always did, and it was old — old the way bedrock is old, old the way winter is old — and it was furious.

"*Stolen.*"

Ansel's spine went rigid. Their hands tightened on the book until the swollen pages creaked.

"*Twice stolen,*" the voice said, and it climbed as it spoke, gathering itself like a wave, "*first from the living and then from the dead, and you sit in a hole in my mountain reading thief's scripture by thief's fire—*"

"Who are you?" Ansel whispered. It was a foolish question. They knew the register of it, the weight. In thirty years of listening they had heard perhaps four voices with that much age in them, and each had belonged to someone who had ruled something, or been ruined trying.

"*I am the queen your histories were not permitted to remember,*" the voice said, and the whistle in the crack of the wall bent around her words, so that the whole cave seemed to speak with her. "*I am the grandmother of the boy on the throne. And I am telling you, bone-carver, oath-keeper, little listener — that prophecy is a cage built out of truth. Read it again. Read what it does not say.*"

Freya stirred. Ansel looked over — her brow furrowed, her lips moving around some dream-word — but she did not wake, and something in Ansel both relaxed and regretted it. They wanted her awake. They wanted witnesses to this who were alive, whose pulses they could check against their own to prove the world still worked.

They read it again. Aloud, slower, hunting the gaps.

"*One who owes the Crown nothing and loves its hostage wholly.*" Ansel frowned. "That's plain enough."

"*Is it?*" The queen's voice dropped, and fury cooled into something worse — precision. "*Who wrote it? Who keeps it? Who circled the date in a book that should have burned a century ago and let it fall into a peddler's pack, of all the packs on all the roads? Prophecies do not wander, bone-carver. They are planted. And things that are planted are meant to grow in a particular direction.*"

"You're saying it's false."

"*I'm saying it is bait.*" A pause, and when she spoke again the fury had gone out of her entirely, and what remained was so tired and so human that Ansel's throat closed. "*The thing under the crown is older than your Crown, child. It tried for me, in my time. It failed, because I had people who loved me more than they feared it. So it learned. It waited. It learned to write, too.*"

The fire had burned low. Ansel fed it a split of pine without looking away from the page, and the light leapt, and the shadows on the wall leapt with it, and for one vertiginous moment Ansel could have sworn the shadows arranged themselves — briefly, deliberately — into the shape of a crowned head, before collapsing back into ordinary dark.

"The true king shall wake," Ansel read aloud, testing it. "Aldric."

"*Say his name again,*" the queen said, softer.

"Aldric."

"*Again. He cannot hear himself, in there. Whatever it is he is kept in — a cell, a dream, a coffin of his own skull — he has forgotten the sound of his own name. Say it until it wears a groove.*"

"Aldric," Ansel said. "Aldric. Aldric."

And the dead of Thornfell — the hundreds of them, the drowned and the snowed-under and the fever-taken and the massacred, the whole murmuring congregation Ansel had carried since childhood — rose together into something that was not quite speech and not quite song, a sound like wind through a forest of standing stones, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, Ansel heard — or thought they heard, or desperately hoped they heard — something answer. Something small and far away and *alive.*

Then it was gone, and the ordinary tide of the dead closed back over it, and Ansel sat shaking by a dying fire with a book in their lap and no idea whether they had touched a miracle or a trap.

* * *

Freya woke to the smell of pine smoke and the sound of Ansel talking to nobody.

She came up out of sleep the way she did everything — abruptly, defensively, one hand already finding the knife in her boot before her eyes finished opening. Then she registered where she was: the Kettle, the fire, the guide from the pass sitting cross-legged with the forbidden book open, speaking aloud into empty air with an expression she had last seen on novices attempting their first translation of the Old Rite — concentration stretched over terror.

"You're reading it," she said. Her voice came out rough with sleep. "You weren't going to read it. You said—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Sat up properly, dragging her cloak around her shoulders. "What did it say?"

Ansel didn't answer at first. They were looking at her — really looking, the way they'd been doing since the pass, cataloguing her the way they catalogued everything, except that with her the catalogue kept refusing to close. Finally they turned the book around and held it out.

"Read the third line," they said. "Out loud. Please."

Freya took the book. The paper felt warm from Ansel's hands, which was absurd, and she noticed that she noticed, and decided not to think about why. She found the line.

"*By their hand the thing wearing his crown shall be put off him, and the true king shall wake.*"

"And after that?"

"*And the debt of Thornfell shall come due.*" She looked up. "That's standard prophetic structure. Condition, agent, action, consequence. Whoever composed this knew the formal grammar — this isn't folk prophecy, it's archive work. Trained hand." She paused, hearing herself. "Which means it came from somewhere like the place I stole it from."

"It means," Ansel said quietly, "that either your archivists can see the future, or your archivists can write it. And I've spent thirty years learning the difference between voices that remember and voices that want things. This book wants things."

Freya set the book down carefully, as if it might bite. "You talk about the dead like they're witnesses."

"They are witnesses. It's the only thing they are. They can't want anymore — wanting needs a body. They remember, and they grieve, and occasionally—" Ansel hesitated. It cost them something; Freya watched it cost them. "Occasionally they warn me. There's a voice in here with us right now. Old. Royal. She says the crown was stolen twice."

Freya went very still.

"Once from the living," Ansel continued, watching her face, "and once from the dead. She says she was Aldric's grandmother. She says the thing under the crown tried for *her*, generations ago, and failed, and learned, and waited. She says the prophecy is bait."

For a long moment the only sounds were the fire and the mountain's low whistle through the wall-crack. Freya's mind — Ansel could see it working, could see the archivist take hold of the panic and start sorting it into shelves — moved visibly behind her eyes.

"That's consistent," she said at last, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone handling fragile things. "That's — gods help me, that's *consistent*. Because here's what I know, Ansel Oyelaran, and I've been circling it for two days without saying it because saying it makes it real." She drew a breath. "No one has seen King Aldric in three years."

"What do you mean, no one has seen him? He holds court. There are decrees. There are portraits commissioned last spring—"

"I *made* the spring portrait," Freya said. "Or rather, I catalogued it. Court painter's study, His Majesty seated, and I stood three feet from it with my gloves on, and I will tell you what I told no one, because telling it would have ended my career and possibly my life." Her hands had started moving as she talked, the way they did when the knowledge got too big for her mouth alone. "The face is perfect. The face is *too* perfect — Aldric is fifty-one years old and the portrait shows a man of forty with a painter's idealization, fine, that happens, flattery is a court language. But the hands. Archivists learn hands, Ansel, because seals and signatures live in hands. The hands in that portrait are wrong. Too smooth. No sword callus. Aldric won the Vale campaign personally; his sword hand should look like braided leather. And the signature on the accompanying decree—" she laughed, short and awful, "—the signature is *beautiful*. Aldric's real hand was a scrawl. I've handled two hundred of his documents. He signed like a man being chased."

The fire cracked. Neither of them moved.

"So either the king changed his entire hand at fifty," Freya said, "or someone else signs his name. And here is the part I have said to no one, not even in my own head, until this cave, until you:" She leaned forward, and the firelight made her eyes very bright and very frightened. "Three years ago Aldric went on progress to the northern reaches. He visited Thornfell — there are records, I filed them myself. He came back thinner. Quieter. He cancelled the autumn hunt, which he loved. He stopped eating in public. And within a year, everyone who had known him longest — his old tutor, his chamberlain, the captain of his personal guard — had been retired, promoted to distant posts, or dead of sudden fevers. I know because I catalogued the succession of appointments. I thought it was politics. Everyone thought it was politics." She swallowed. "It wasn't politics. It was *husbandry*. Someone was clearing the field around him."

"*Yes,*" said the queen's voice, and Ansel flinched — but Freya didn't, couldn't, heard nothing but the fire and Ansel's sharp intake of breath. "*Yes, little archivist. You read the ledgers well. Better than the ones who buried me ever read my face.*"

"She agrees with you," Ansel said hoarsely.

Freya stared at them. "You can hear her. Right now."

"She's been shouting since I opened the book. She says—" Ansel closed their eyes, listening, translating, and their face did something complicated, grief and awe fighting for the same ground. "She says you read the ledgers well. She says—" A pause. The dead's tide-sound swelled and receded. "She says the thing doesn't wear the king like a mask. It wears him like a *coat*, and it has been letting the coat out at the seams for three years, feeding, growing comfortable, and the courtiers don't notice because it has learned to smile exactly the way power wants to be smiled at."

"Feeding," Freya repeated. "Feeding on what?"

And Ansel, who had grown up among the dead of Thornfell, who had listened to the massacred whisper their last moments into their ear for thirty years, who knew precisely what kind of hunger leaves a village full of bodies and takes nothing else — Ansel opened their mouth to say *I don't know* and found, instead, that the queen was answering, and that her answer arrived with the weight of a stone dropped down a well.

"*Ask her what the Crown feeds its prisoners,*" the old voice said. "*Ask her what the gaols are for, in a kingdom with so few crimes. Ask her why the condemned wagons go north in winter and come back empty in spring.*"

Ansel's eyes opened. They looked at Freya — at this stranger who had fallen out of the sky two nights ago with torn pages in her coat and witch-finders at her heels — and asked, slowly, the question they suddenly understood they had been brought into the mountains to ask:

"When prisoners are transported from the capital. Where do they go?"

Freya blinked. "The northern facilities. Why—"

"Why?" Ansel laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Because the dead of Thornfell have been complaining about wagon-roads for years. Roads that carry loads north and come back light. Because there are graves up there that the dead won't go near, even the dead, even *we* won't—"

They stopped. The cave whistled. The fire settled into embers.

"Your king isn't missing," Ansel said. "He's *housed*. And something in that palace has been keeping him the way farmers keep a beast — fed, and calm, and penned. And whatever the feed is—" they watched Freya's face go white as the ash on the fire's edge, watched her arrive at the same arithmetic they had just finished, "—your archives know. Don't they. That's what you tore out of the books. That's why they're burning the country looking for you."

Freya didn't answer. She didn't need to. She sat with her arms wrapped around her knees and her jaw tight and her eyes fixed on the coals, and she looked, for the first time since the pass, less like a fugitive than like a woman doing long division with lives.

"We can't go south," she said finally. "We can't stay here — Voss will sweep these passes within the week, she's methodical, it's how she hunts. And we can't outrun this, because—" she gestured at the book, at Ansel, at the whole impossible shape of the last two days, "—because apparently the two of us are *in* it. One who owes the Crown nothing." She looked at Ansel. "That's you. Smuggler's guide, oath-sworn against the Crown, if half of what Marta's people mutter about you is true. And loves its hostage wholly." Her voice caught slightly on the word, and she covered it by pushing on too fast. "That's — whoever that ends up being. That's the mechanism. That's how it works. Not armies. Not rites. Just — someone who owes nothing and loves anyway, walking up to the thing wearing the crown and taking it off."

"Prophecies are bait," Ansel reminded her. "Your majesty-in-the-walls said so herself."

"Fine. Then we treat it as bait and walk in anyway, because the alternative is letting it keep feeding." Freya unfolded herself, restless now, the scholar replaced by something harder and younger. "I spent six years believing the archive was the conscience of the kingdom. That somebody, somewhere behind all that rot, was writing down the truth so that eventually, someday, it would matter. And the truth is the archive is the pantry. We're not escaping anything anymore, Ansel." She said it like a verdict, and her chin came up as she said it, and despite everything — despite the dead screaming in the stone and the trap coiled in the paper and the witch-finders surely already climbing — despite all of it, Ansel felt something in their chest turn over and settle, warm and terrible, that was almost certainly the beginning of love.

"We're going to Carrowmere," Freya said. "We're going to see this thing wearing the king with our own eyes. And then we're going to figure out how to take it off him."

The dead of Thornfell roared — approval or alarm, Ansel genuinely could not tell — and above the roar, thin and clear and old, the queen's voice rang out one last time before the tide of the others swallowed her:

"*Then rest, children. And when you reach the city — count the birds. Count what will not land.*"

Outside the cave mouth, far below in the dark valley, a nightbird called once and fell silent, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep.

Ansel banked the fire, and did not sleep at all.
