## 4. The Oath at Thornfell Bridge

The cave breathed cold.

Ansel sat apart from the others, at the mouth where the firelight thinned to nothing, turning the prophecy book over in their hands as if it might explain itself. It didn't. Books never did. The dead were more honest — they only ever said what they had seen, and they had seen everything, and they never once pretended it meant anything kind.

*Read it again,* said the drowned child's mother, from somewhere behind Ansel's left shoulder. She had followed them up out of the valley two days ago and would not go back. *Read the part about the crown.*

"I've read it," Ansel murmured. "I don't like it."

*Liking is not a requirement,* she said, which was something her son used to say, back when he was alive and seven years old and wise about porridge. The dead borrowed each other's phrases the way travelers borrowed fires. After enough years, Ansel had stopped being able to tell whose grief was whose.

Across the cave, Freya slept sitting up, her back against the stone, the torn pages of her archive copy spread across her knees like a skirt of dead leaves. Even asleep her hands curled around them. She had not let them out of her reach since the pass. Ansel understood that. When everything else is taken, you hold what's left until your knuckles whiten and your fingers ache and holding becomes its own kind of wound.

Ansel knew about wounds that held on.

They looked down at the book again. The water stain had bloomed across the page like a bruise, but beneath it the words stood clear enough, inked by some hand now eighty years dust:

*And there shall come an Unbinding, who owes the hollow thing nothing and loves its host wholly, and they shall walk into the seat of the Crown—*

The seat of the Crown.

Ansel closed the book.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, the dead began to murmur. Not words yet. Not yet. Just the sound they made when something was coming that they did not like — a tide going out, a wind through ribs, a thousand small voices drawing breath at once.

Ansel knew that sound. They had known it since they were nine years old, kneeling in the mud at Thornfell Bridge with blood soaking through their trousers and an old woman's hand heavy on their head, and they had sworn.

* * *

The village had been called Hallowmere, though no one called it anything anymore, because there was no one left to call it anything. Ansel's mother had taken them over the mountain to trade — salt for wool, the long way around Thornfell because the low road flooded in spring — and they had come down into the valley on the third morning of the third month to find the smoke still standing in the air like a thing too tired to move.

Ansel remembered the silence first. Not true silence. A silence full of voices, thin as threads, all speaking at once, none of them loud. Their mother heard nothing. Their mother walked into the ruin calling names — hers, the miller's, the smith's twin daughters — and heard nothing, and wept, and heard nothing.

Ansel heard everything.

Nine years old, standing in the ash of a well with their hands over their ears, and the voices came through anyway, through bone, through the place behind the eyes where a person keeps their own name. *We are here. We are here. We are here.* Hundreds of them. All the village. Piled in a pit beyond the bridge, soldiers' work, quick and thorough, the Crown's black-and-gold still visible on a scrap of banner snagged in the hawthorn.

Their mother found the mass grave before the soldiers found them. That was the mercy of it, if you could call anything that week merciful: she got Ansel away over the western scree with soldiers' torches behind them, and she did not stop walking for two days, and when she finally stopped she looked at her child — at the child who had not slept, who flinched from every shadow because shadows were crowded now, who whispered answers to questions no one had asked — and she did not say *you're mad*. She said, "There's an old woman on the bridge. She says she's been waiting for you."

Ansel had never told her about the voices. But mothers know. Mothers always know first.

The old woman on Thornfell Bridge had been dead forty years. She had been the village's bone-carver before them — Ansel learned this later, much later, when they understood what they had inherited — and she stood at the center of the crossing where the massacre had begun, and she was solid as lamplight to Ansel's eyes, gray and patient and terribly sad.

"You hear us," she said.

"Yes," Ansel whispered.

"Then listen once, and choose." She knelt, and took Ansel's small hands in hers, and her grip was cold the way river stones are cold, cold all the way through, cold that did not bite because it had forgotten how. "The Crown did this. The Crown will do it again — next year, or in fifty years, it does not matter; crowns forget, that is what crowns are *for*. So swear, child. Swear to carry our names so we are not erased twice. Swear never to serve the Crown that made this pit. Swear it at our grave, with our dead as witness, and we will give you what you need to keep the oath — the hearing, the sight, the knowing. It will hurt. It will never stop hurting. But you will not be alone in the dark ever again, and neither will we."

Ansel looked past her, down into the pit, at the hundreds of pale shapes standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark like reeds in a frozen marsh. Waiting. All of them waiting. Some of them were children. One of them was smaller than Ansel was.

"I swear," Ansel said. "Carry your names. Never serve the Crown. As long as I live."

The old woman smiled, and it broke Ansel's heart even then, even at nine, because it was the smile of someone setting down a weight she had carried alone for forty years and handing it, piece by piece, to a child.

"So be it," she said. And the cold went into Ansel's hands and up their arms and settled behind their eyes like a second set of eyelids, and the world opened, and it never closed again.

* * *

"Ansel."

Freya's voice. Ansel surfaced from the memory like a diver coming up through dark water, gasping at the present tense of things: the fire burned lower, the smugglers Breck and Ilsa snoring in their bedrolls, the rain starting against the cave mouth in long silver threads.

"You were talking," Freya said quietly. "In your sleep, or near it. To someone."

"To several someones." Ansel turned. Firelight moved on Freya's face and made her look younger and more frightened than she probably wanted anyone to see. "How long was I—"

"An hour. Maybe more." Freya hugged the torn pages to her chest. "You said a name. *Maren.* You said, *I swore, Maren, I swore.*"

Ansel's hands went still on the book's cover. "The old woman. On the bridge. Maren." They had not spoken that name aloud in twenty years. It tasted the same. Ash and river water. "She gave me the hearing. In exchange for an oath."

"What oath?"

"Two parts." Ansel held up one finger. "Carry the names of Hallowmere's dead, so the Crown's erasure isn't the end of them." A second finger. "Never serve the Crown. Not its soldiers, not its courts, not its roads, not its wars. Nothing. Ever." They lowered the hand. "I was nine years old. I'd watched soldiers fill a pit with everyone I'd ever met. I would have sworn anything. I *did* swear anything. And the dead kept their half. Twenty years, they've kept their half."

"And now the prophecy asks you to break yours."

"Now the prophecy asks me to walk into the seat of the Crown," Ansel corrected, "which is either breaking the oath or keeping it, depending on how you read it, and depending on how *they* read it." They nodded backward, toward the deep of the cave, where the murmur had not stopped. If anything it had grown. "And they have been reading it aloud to each other for the last hour. In the dark. Where I can hear every word."

Freya came closer. She moved carefully, the way people moved around Ansel sometimes when the listening got bad — as though grief were a sleeping animal. "What are they saying?"

"That I'm forswearing myself." Ansel laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Some of them. The older ones, the ones who died hating the Crown most — they say the throne room is exactly where a bone-carver should stand, that oaths aren't walls, they're compasses. But the others—" Ansel's throat tightened. "The others remember the pit, Freya. The others remember the boots. And they say if I carry their names into that palace, I'm carrying them into the mouth of the thing that ate them. They say I promised. They say promises are the only law the dead have left."

"And what do you say?"

Ansel looked at her. Really looked — at the ink stains on her fingers, at the pages clutched like a shield, at the exhaustion carved under her eyes and the stubbornness carved under *that*. Three days ago she had been a stranger falling out of the sky. Now she was the reason Ansel had a hole in their coat from a witch-finder's crossbow bolt, and the reason the hole wasn't in their chest, and Ansel could not find the line between those two facts no matter how they squinted at it.

"I say," Ansel said slowly, "that when I swore, I swore to protect the powerless from the powerful. And there is no one in these mountains more powerless than a king screaming inside his own skull, wearing his own body like a coat someone else buttoned." They turned the prophecy book over again, around and around. "But I also say that oaths aren't weather, Freya. You don't get to wait them out. I built my whole life on that promise. Every road I've walked, every name I've carved, every smuggler I've guided past the Crown's tolls — all of it was the oath, wearing my shape. If I walk into Carrowmere, I don't know who walks out. Someone who kept faith. Or someone who broke the only thing the dead ever asked of me. And I won't know which until it's done, and neither will they."

The fire popped. Rain threaded down. Somewhere below them, far off, a wolf sang one long note and got no answer.

"The dead have never lied to you," Freya said. It wasn't quite a question.

"No."

"And paper?"

"Paper doesn't lie either. It just doesn't tell you everything." Ansel opened the book to the marked page one more time, and read the line beneath the prophecy, the small cramped annotation some long-dead archivist had squeezed into the margin:

*The Unbinding owes the hollow nothing — beware the guide who owes the dead everything.*

Ansel felt Freya read it over their shoulder. Felt her go very still.

"That's you," she said. "That's describing you."

"It's describing a trap," Ansel said. "Or a warning. With paper, those are usually the same document." They closed the book and pressed it into her hands, folding her fingers over it, and was distantly surprised at how natural that felt — handing her the weight instead of carrying it alone. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me whether I'll take you to Carrowmere. Tonight I can't answer, because tonight the dead are arguing in my skull and half of them are right."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are also right. That's the trouble with the dead. They've had time to get good at it."

Freya almost smiled. It was a small thing, worn at the edges, but real. "Then sleep," she said. "I'll watch."

"You don't have to—"

"I know." She settled against the wall beside the fire, pages in her lap, eyes on the rain. "I want to. Consider it the first payment on a very large debt."

Ansel lay down on their bedroll and did not expect to sleep, and did — briefly, badly, down in the old country of the dream where the bridge stood whole and the village smoked and a gray woman waited in the middle of the crossing with her hand outstretched, saying, as she had said twenty years ago, *choose, child, choose* —

— and woke to Freya's hand hard on their shoulder and her other hand clapped over their mouth, and her eyes wide and white-rimmed in the dark.

"Torches," she breathed. "Down the valley road. A lot of them. And they're not moving like patrols."

Ansel listened past her — past the rain, past the muttering dead, out to where the night itself had gone tense.

"They're moving like a net," Ansel said. "Someone's told Captain Voss exactly which road we're on."

* * *

They broke camp in the dark, dousing the fire with snow-melt, and the dead rose around Ansel like frost coming up through ground — not voices now but pressure, hundreds of them leaning close, all saying the same thing in a hundred different registers of fear and fury and grief:

*Not the Crown's road. Never the Crown's road. You swore.*

"I know," Ansel whispered, shouldering their pack. "I know. Help me anyway."

And behind them, faint as a lamp in a far window, old Maren's voice — the first voice, the coldest, the one that lived deepest behind Ansel's eyes — said something Ansel had never heard her say in twenty years of haunting.

She said, *"Hurry."*
