## 5. Witch-Finders at the Waystation

The waystation crouched in the fold of the mountain like something trying not to be seen, which, Freya thought, was exactly what it was. A squat stone building with a turf roof gone brown at the edges, a stable leaning against it for company, and a single chimney breathing thin smoke into a sky the color of old pewter. If you didn't know to look for it, you'd walk past. The smugglers had built it that way on purpose.

"Stonehouse," Marta said, patting the doorframe as she shouldered it open. "Been standing since before the Crown decided it owned these mountains. Older than me, even, and I'm older than sin."

"You're not that old," Freya said.

Marta gave her a look over one shoulder — the flat, unimpressed look of a woman who had buried three husbands and outlived two kings' worth of tax collectors. "Girl, I remember when Aldric's father was crowned. I remember when his grandmother was buried, and what a strange quiet day that was." She sniffed. "I'm old enough."

Inside, the waystation smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, and dried herbs hung from the rafters in bundles that swayed when the wind found the gaps in the walls. There was a long table scarred by decades of knives and tankards, a hearth big enough to roast a goat whole, and bunks along the far wall with blankets that looked — and smelled — like they hadn't been washed since the last reign. Freya had slept in the royal archive's climate-controlled scriptorium for eleven years. She had never once been warm there. She was warm now, and she distrusted how much she liked it.

Ansel set their pack down by the hearth and went immediately to the window slit, the way they always did, checking the approaches with the automatic vigilance of someone who had spent years keeping other people alive by knowing where the danger wasn't yet. In the grey light their face was all planes and shadow, the bone-carver's hands hanging loose at their sides. Freya had learned to read those hands the way she'd once read marginalia: the way Ansel's fingers curled slightly inward when the dead were talking.

They were talking now. She could tell because Ansel's jaw tightened.

"What is it?" she asked quietly.

"The dead don't like this place tonight," Ansel said. Their voice was low, pitched under the clatter of Marta putting out bread and hard cheese. "There's an old woman here. Died in her sleep forty years back. She keeps saying *they came asking*. Over and over. *They came asking.*"

"Asking about what?"

"I don't know. She doesn't know anymore. She only remembers the fear." Ansel turned from the window. "It might be nothing. Old ghosts repeat themselves. But we shouldn't stay past dawn."

"We're staying till dawn regardless," Marta said firmly, setting down a wheel of cheese with a thump. "You two look like something a wolf chewed up and spat out. Nobody crosses the high pass without sleep and hot food unless they're running from something specific, and if you're running from something specific, you'll run better fed." She pointed a thick finger at Freya. "Sit. Eat. You've got the eyes of a woman who hasn't slept in a week."

Freya sat. It was true; she hadn't. Since the archive, since tearing the pages free and running, sleep had become a country she could see from a distance but never enter. Every time she closed her eyes she saw Master Corvane's face the morning he told her to burn the file — no, not burn it, *lose* it, lose it so thoroughly that even its absence would be deniable — and then, three days later, saw his name on the death register instead of his signature on her letters of recommendation.

She ate the bread. It was dense and dark and better than anything she'd eaten in the palace, and she hated herself a little for noticing.

* * *

Dawn did not come gently.

Freya woke to a sound she couldn't immediately name — a wrongness in the mountain's silence, a note missing from a song she'd only just learned. Then she identified it: hooves. Many hooves, moving at a walk, disciplined, spread across the road below the waystation in the loose crescent formation that said *we are searching, not traveling*.

She was on her feet before she was fully awake, hand finding the knife at her belt — a smuggler's knife, given to her two days ago by a boy named Pip who couldn't have been more than fifteen and who had shown her how to hold it with the gravity of a fencing master.

Ansel was already at the window slit. The light through it had gone strange, gold-shot grey, and it caught the tension in their shoulders.

"How many?" Freya whispered.

"Twelve riders. Maybe more in the trees." Ansel's voice was flat, controlled, but their hands had gone still — completely still, which Freya had learned was worse than any fidgeting. "Iron-shod. And there's—" They stopped. Swallowed. "There's a hound. Dead hound. Been dead a hundred years. They use them for finding people like me."

"People like you?"

"People who hear things they shouldn't."

Below, a voice rang out, clear and cold as a bell struck in winter: "In the name of His Majesty King Aldric, second of that name, protector of the realm: come out, be counted, and be judged fairly."

Marta appeared in the doorway of the back room, already dressed, already awake, her silver hair braided tight against her skull. She looked at the window, then at the two of them, and Freya watched her do the arithmetic in about half a second.

"That's Voss," Marta said. "Serah Voss. Witch-finder captain. She's been working the Thornfell roads for three years. Never caught anyone worth catching, but she's patient. Patience is worse than cleverness." She was already moving as she spoke, pulling a loose floorboard near the hearth, revealing a cache beneath: a coil of rope, a hooded lantern, a bundle of cloaks. "Back wall. There's a goat track behind the stable that drops into the ravine. The dead woman's grave is up the slope — Ansel, your friends will know the path even if you don't, yes?"

"They'll know it," Ansel said slowly. "But Marta—"

"Marta," Freya started, understanding arriving cold and complete, like ice water down the spine. "No. No, you're not staying."

"Somebody has to answer the door, girl." Marta was stuffing bread and cheese into a cloth with brisk, unceremonious hands. "If the station's empty, she searches it. If she searches it, she finds the cold ashes, the three bowls, the second bedroll. Three travelers, no travelers. Then she tracks. But if there's one old woman here, alone, doing what old women do — mending, muttering, minding her own business — then there's a reason nobody else is here, and it's a boring reason, and bored captains move on."

"She'll take you in for questioning anyway," Ansel said. Their voice had gone rough. "Voss questions everyone on the road. You know what her questioning looks like."

"I know what it looks like," Marta said. "And I know what my answers look like. Forty years I've been answering Crown men on this road, dear. I'm very good at being dull." She pressed the cloth bundle into Freya's hands, and her grip was surprisingly strong, calloused as tree bark. "Listen to me now. You're going to feel guilty about this. Don't waste the feeling. Guilt's only good for one thing, and that's making sure you live long enough to make it mean something. So live. Both of you. That's the price of my morning, and I charge fair."

Below, the voice again, closer now: "The house by the ravine road. We know it's occupied. Come out or we come in."

Marta smiled — a real smile, crooked and unafraid — and pushed them both toward the back wall.

* * *

The goat track was everything its name promised: narrow, crumbling, and vertical in the places where it bothered to be horizontal at all. Freya climbed down it with her back against cold stone and her heart hammering against her ribs, Ansel above and behind her, calling directions in a whisper that sometimes slipped into another voice entirely — the dead woman on the slope, guiding them past the loose shelf, around the juniper root, down to where the ravine floor opened into scree and shadow.

Halfway down, they heard the door of the waystation bang open above them.

They froze against the rock, pressed into a crevice barely deep enough for one body, let alone two, and Freya became intimately aware of every sound she made: her breath, the shift of her cloak, the tick of her boot sole against stone. Below them, maybe thirty feet, the ravine floor waited. Above, voices drifted down like stones dropped into water.

"—alone, Captain. Old woman. Says she minds the station for her son, who's got the flocks in the high pasture."

A pause. Then another voice, and Freya felt it before she understood it — a voice like a drawn blade being slowly sheathed, controlled and precise and utterly without warmth.

"Her son. Name?"

"Hollis, Captain. Hollis Stonehouse."

"And where is Hollis Stonehouse now?"

"High pasture, she says."

"Take me to her."

Boots on stone. The murmur of conversation too low to parse. Freya closed her eyes and listened to Marta's voice rising and falling in the easy cadence of a woman complaining about wool prices and bad knees and the insolence of the young, and she thought: *she's good, she's so good, she's done this her whole life, she'll be fine.*

Then the blade-voice said, "Search the house. Thoroughly."

And after a long moment: "Captain, there's a cold hearth with three bowls washed and set to dry. And a bedroll. Just the one, but it's been slept in recently, and there's ash from a fire big enough for more than one."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"Old woman," Serah Voss said, and now the sheathed-blade quality of her voice was fully drawn, "who cooks for three and sleeps for one. Bring her."

Freya moved before she knew she was moving. Ansel's hand shot out and caught her wrist, iron-tight, and pulled her back into the crevice, and she fought them for one horrible silent moment — two seconds, maybe three, her elbow swinging, her breath sobbing — before Ansel put their mouth against her ear and whispered with terrible gentleness:

"If you go up there, she died for nothing. If you stay, she gets to have meant it. Those are the only two choices, Freya. Choose the one she'd choose."

Freya stopped fighting.

Above them, Marta's voice rose one last time, indignant, sharp with the authentic fury of a woman wrongly accused: "Three bowls! My son's bowl and mine and my sister's when she visits, you great louts, do you wash and dry six bowls for two people? Ask anyone on the ravine road, ask—"

The rest was lost as they were dragged away, or as Freya dragged herself away, she was never afterward sure which. She only knew that she descended the last thirty feet of the goat track with Ansel's hand locked around her arm, and that her tears fell silently onto the scree, and that she made no sound at all, because Marta had taught her the first lesson of the mountains without meaning to:

*Live. Make it mean something.*

* * *

They walked until the light failed, and then walked further, because stopping felt like betrayal and movement felt like penance, and neither of them said Marta's name aloud until full dark had settled and the smuggler's road had delivered them to a hollow beneath an overhang where a fire wouldn't show.

Then Freya sat down on the cold stone, held the cloth bundle Marta had packed — bread, cheese, a twist of salt, and, absurdly, a small carved wooden bird that must have lived on Marta's windowsill — and finally broke.

It wasn't graceful. It was ugly, gulping, furious weeping, the kind that hurts the throat, and Ansel sat beside her and said nothing, which was the right thing, because there was nothing to say. Freya had spent eleven years in the archive learning that knowledge was power, that information carefully gathered and secretly kept was a weapon, and she had wielded it — torn pages, stolen files, run — and every step of it had seemed clean, necessary, *hers*. She had never once considered the collateral. Why would she? Collateral was a word for battlefields. She was an archivist.

But Marta had a windowsill, and a wooden bird on it, and a sister who visited, and a son named Hollis in the high pasture who would come home to an empty station and a story the neighbors would tell him in hushed voices, and all of it — all of it — because Freya had run through Thornfell carrying pages with a king's secret written on them.

"It's me," she said, when she could speak. "They're not tracking you, Ansel. Or — they are, but they started with me. The archive theft went up the chain within hours. Corvane's death went up faster. There will be a description of me in every garrison from here to the coast. Everyone who helps me becomes visible. Everyone who shelters me becomes a target." She stared at the little wooden bird. "I did this. I brought this to her door."

"Yes," Ansel said.

Freya turned to stare at them. Of all the responses she'd braced for — comfort, denial, *it's not your fault* — she hadn't braced for agreement.

"You did," Ansel continued, calmly, feeding a twig into the tiny fire they'd allowed themselves. "And Voss chose to swing the net, and the Crown chose to write the warrant, and whatever hollow thing sits on the throne chose all of it first. You hold one thread, Freya. It's a real thread. But it isn't the whole web, and if you start believing it is, you'll do what guilty people always do — you'll run alone, and get caught alone, and everyone who helped you will have bled for nothing." They looked up, firelight moving in their eyes. "Marta didn't cover your escape because she was tricked into it. She did the arithmetic in half a second and chose. Respect the choice. That's all guilt is good for — remembering that somebody chose."

Freya looked at them for a long moment. "You sound like you've done this arithmetic before."

"I've been doing it since I was nine years old," Ansel said quietly, and there was something in their voice Freya had never heard before — an old door opening a crack onto a room full of dark. "Everyone who helps me bleeds. I told myself the same things you're going to want to tell yourself. None of them help. Only one thing helps."

"What?"

"Making it count." Ansel passed her the wooden bird. "So we make it count."

Freya turned the carving over in her hands. It was crude work, a wren or a sparrow, done by someone patient with a small knife — and then she saw the maker's mark on the base, a stylized *A* cut into the wood, and understood that Marta had carried a piece of Ansel's early work on her windowsill for years, perhaps decades. This family went back further than two days on a road. She was the newcomer here. She was the stranger they had decided, instantly and irrationally, to bleed for.

"Why?" she asked. "Why would she do that? Why would any of them? Odile's crew, Pip, Marta — they don't know me. I'm a liability with a knife and a stolen book. Rational people turn strangers over to witch-finders. There's usually a reward."

Ansel was quiet for a while. The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, an owl called, and Ansel's head tilted toward it, listening, and Freya realized with a small chill that they might be listening to something else entirely.

"Because the Crown took something from every one of us," they said at last. "Odile's parents. Pip's whole village. Mine—" They stopped. Started again. "When you've lost enough to the law, the law stops being the thing that protects people and starts being the thing you protect people *from*. That's all the network is, really. People protecting people from the thing that's supposed to protect them." They glanced at her, and the ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed their face. "You came out of the palace carrying the proof that the thing on the throne isn't a man. You think we'd trade that for a reward?"

Freya looked down at the bird. "I think," she said slowly, "that I've spent my whole life believing institutions keep their promises. The archive. The Crown. The law. And in four days I've watched outlaws keep promises better than any of them."

"Welcome to Thornfell," Ansel said.

They divided Marta's bread and ate it in silence, and it tasted like a debt.

* * *

Freya slept in fragments, and in the grey hour before dawn she woke to find Ansel sitting upright, rigid, staring into the dead coals of the fire.

"What is it?" she whispered. "What are they saying?"

Ansel didn't answer at first. When they did, their voice was stripped down to bare wire.

"The dead hound lost us. Voss knows it. But the old queen — the one who screamed about the crown — she's been following us since the cave, and she just said something new." Ansel's hands had begun, unconsciously, to curl inward. "She says the woman they took isn't in a cell. Voss doesn't keep prisoners in cells on the mountain road. She's taking Marta to Carrowmere. To the palace."

"To the palace," Freya repeated. "Why would a witch-finder take a smuggler to the palace?"

"Because," Ansel said, and now their voice dropped so low Freya had to lean close to catch it, "the queen says the thing on the throne is hungry. And it eats best when the meal is brought to it willingly — someone loyal, someone stubborn, someone who won't break." They looked at her, and their eyes in the pre-dawn dark were wide and bleak. "Freya. The Crown isn't just hunting us. It's *feeding*. And we just handed it its next meal."

The wooden bird lay between them in the ashes, and somewhere east, beyond the passes, the road ran down and down toward Carrowmere, and the lights of a hungry court.
