## 6. The Hollow Court

The road to Carrowmere unspooled beneath them like a ribbon someone had wept on.

Freya had spent eleven years in the Royal Archive, and in all that time she had never once thought about what the capital's roads looked like from below — from the ditches, from the treelines, from the places where people walked who had no business walking anywhere. Now she knew. She knew the exact shade of mud that clung to smuggler boots, and she knew how to walk with her shoulders down and her eyes forward, and she knew that Ansel Oyelaran could read a landscape the way she once read catalogues: instantly, hungrily, with a fluency that looked like magic and probably was.

"Two miles," Ansel said, not looking at her. They stood at the crest of a hill where the Thornfell foothills finally gave up their argument and surrendered to the lowlands. Below them, Carrowmere spread along its river like a spilled purse — walls, spires, smoke, the grey-brown sprawl of a city that believed itself the center of the world because it had never bothered to look outward. "We go in as wool-buyers. You don't speak unless I speak first. And you don't look at the palace."

"I'm an archivist," Freya said. "Looking is the entire profession."

"You're a fugitive now. Looking gets you hanged." Ansel adjusted the pack straps across their shoulders — they carried everything they owned, which was very little, and carried it like a person who had practiced losing things. "Besides. You'll see it whether you want to or not. It sits over the whole city like a bruise."

They came down through the western gate in the long amber light of late afternoon, and Freya learned that Ansel had understated it.

She had expected the palace to be visible. What she had not expected was for it to be *present* — a pale mass of towers and buttresses rising above the eastern quarter, white limestone gone slightly yellow with age, so large that it seemed less built than grown, the way a tumor grows. The banners of House Aldric hung from every tower, crimson and gold, and even at this distance Freya could see that they hung wrong. Dead still. The wind was coming off the river hard enough to bend the market awnings, to send grit hissing along the cobbles, to fill the sails of the ferry barges — and not one banner moved.

"Birds," Ansel said quietly.

"What?"

"Look at the palace. Look for birds."

Freya looked. Carrowmere was loud with birds — gulls screaming over the fish market, pigeons detonating in grey clouds from the eaves of the counting houses, jackdaws riding the thermals above the cathedral. The sky around the palace, by contrast, was empty. Not merely empty but *curated*, the way an empty chair at a funeral is curated. A single crow flew toward the eastern towers, banked sharply forty feet short of the nearest battlement, and fled back the way it had come as though it had struck glass.

"They won't land on it," Ansel said. "Any of it. Not the roofs, not the walls, not the trees in the outer court. Marta told me years ago. I thought it was smuggler superstition."

"And now?"

"Now I think the birds know something we don't."

* * *

Odile's people had a safehouse in the Tanner's Quarter, three rooms above a dye-works that stank so ferociously of urine and alum that no watchman would voluntarily come within fifty yards of it. The landlady was a one-eyed ex-soldier named Brakka who took their coins without counting them and asked no questions, which Freya was learning was the local currency of trust: not questions answered, but questions never asked.

They reached the rooms as the bells rang vespers, and that was when Ansel stopped in the doorway and went very still.

Freya had seen them do this before — on the road, twice, when the dead pressed close enough to interrupt whatever they were doing. It looked like a man listening to a door. But this was different. This time Ansel's face did something she had never seen it do. It went *afraid*. Not wary, not grim — afraid, the way a child's face goes afraid, all the architecture of adulthood falling away at once.

"Ansel?" She touched their arm. Their sleeve was damp with river fog and cold as a grave-cloth. "What is it? What do you hear?"

For a long moment they didn't answer. The dye-works clanked and slopped below them. Somewhere a cart driver cursed his mule with real artistry.

"It's quiet," Ansel said at last, and their voice sounded scraped. "The dead are quiet. All of them. Every voice I've carried since I was nine years old, and right now they've all gone silent at once, like—" They stopped. Started again. "Like a room full of people who have just heard something terrible in the next room and are all holding their breath."

"The palace."

"The palace." Ansel looked east, through the wall, through the rooftops, at something Freya couldn't see. "Two miles away and I can feel it pressing on them. Whatever sits on that throne, it isn't just wearing a crown. It's *loud*, Freya. Loud in a way that has nothing to do with sound."

And then Ansel swayed, put a hand against the doorframe, and said, in a voice that had dropped to almost nothing:

"There's someone inside it."

"Inside the palace?"

"Inside the *crown*." Ansel's eyes had gone somewhere else entirely. "A living voice. Faint — gods, faint, like a shout heard through three feet of stone. I've never heard a living voice before. The dead are my country; the living I hear the same as anyone. But this—" Their hand closed into a fist against the frame. "It's screaming. It's been screaming for a long time. So long it's forgotten how to do anything else."

Freya felt the floor tilt under her, or perhaps that was only her own pulse. Three years. Three years since King Aldric had last been seen in public unmasked, three years of decrees issued behind curtains and appearances conducted at distance, three years of the Archive quietly reclassifying every record that might explain why. She had found those reclassifications. That was how this whole catastrophe had started — a clerk's marginal note, a file pulled and never returned, a page torn out in the dark by a woman who had told herself she was being careful and had been, in fact, being brave.

"He's alive," she said. "Aldric is alive in there."

"I don't know if that's mercy or horror," Ansel said. "But he's alive. And he knows someone can hear him." Their eyes focused on her at last, and there was something raw in them. "He's been screaming a name. Over and over. For years, maybe."

"What name?"

Ansel's jaw worked. When they spoke, the name came out carefully, like something fragile being carried across ice.

"*Elspeth.*"

Freya went cold from the scalp down. Elspeth Aldric. The old queen-mother. The grandmother whose death records were sealed, whose portrait had been removed from the Long Gallery in the same year the king had stopped appearing — she had found the removal order herself, filed under *Aesthetic Renovation*, which was the Crown's favorite word for erasure.

"We rest tonight," Ansel said, closing the door. "Tomorrow we see him."

* * *

You could not simply walk up to the palace of Carrowmere. But you could stand in the Grand Processional on a feast-day eve, among ten thousand subjects, and watch the king hold open court from the Sun Balcony — and Odile's network had already procured them places in the crowd, two wool-buyers from the hill country come down to see the spectacle, standing third row back behind a family of onion merchants who smelled strongly enough to guarantee their privacy.

Freya had attended court functions before, in another life. She had watched Aldric's grandfather review troops from that same balcony. She knew the choreography: the heralds, the trumpets, the chamberlain reading petitions while the king nodded his gracious nods. She expected pomp. She expected theater.

She was not prepared for the wrongness.

King Aldric emerged at the tenth bell, and the crowd roared, and Freya did not roar, because the thing that walked onto the balcony was wearing the king like a coat.

He looked right. That was the worst of it. Tall, broad, mid-forties, the famous dark hair going silver at the temples exactly as the portraits promised, the strong jaw, the warm brown eyes. He smiled and waved and the crowd loved him, and every individual piece of him matched the man Freya had seen in paintings and archive daguerreotypes a hundred times.

But the pieces did not agree with each other.

His smile arrived a half-second after his eyes suggested it should. His wave was graceful but *looped*, the same gesture repeated with small variations, like a puppeteer testing range of motion. He turned his head to follow a falcon crossing the sky, and the turn went too far, a degree past natural, a degree past possible, and then corrected itself so smoothly that only someone watching for it would ever have caught it. He signed a decree held up to him by the chamberlain — a pardon, by the herald's announcement, granting clemency to a dozen debtors — and his signature flowed beautifully, effortlessly, and Freya realized with a lurch that it was *too* beautiful, that no human hand produced identical letters thirty times a day for three years, that the signature was a forgery performed by the hand rather than written by the man.

And then the sun shifted out from behind a cloud, and she saw the shadow.

The balcony faced west. The low afternoon light threw the king's shadow long across the marble floor of the audience hall, visible through the great arched windows to everyone standing below — a courtesy of architecture, some old king's vanity, letting the commons literally see their ruler cast his shadow over the law. Ten thousand people saw a shadow fall across the marble.

Freya saw a shadow that did not match the man.

It was *larger*. That was the first thing — larger than any man's shadow at that angle, sprawling, reaching, its edges moving when the king stood still. Its head was wrong: elongated, crowned not with the circlet of Aldric but with something branching, antlered, hungry. Where the king's arms ended in graceful sleeves, the shadow's arms dissolved into tendrils that crept along the marble toward the petitioners standing below, and when the shadow's reach touched the spot where a young woman curtsied with her petition raised, the woman swayed, blinked slowly, and straightened with a smile that was a half-second late.

Freya grabbed Ansel's arm hard enough to leave bruises.

"You see it," she whispered. "Tell me you see it."

"I see him." Ansel's face was grey. "I can't see shadows. I hear—"

And then Ansel made a sound Freya would remember for the rest of her life. Not a word. A small, involuntary noise, high and wounded, the sound a person makes when they touch a stove. Their knees buckled. Freya caught them, hauled them upright, wrapped an arm around their waist and laughed loudly, convincingly, the laugh of a wife steadying a husband who'd had too much festival wine, while around them the crowd cheered the king's mercy and nobody looked at the two wool-buyers in the third row.

"Talk to me," she breathed into Ansel's ear. "Quietly. What's happening?"

"He heard me." Ansel's lips barely moved. Sweat stood on their forehead despite the cool air. "The voice in the crown — when I listened last night it was distant, muffled, screaming at nothing. Just now I opened my hearing toward it and it *heard me listening.*" Their hand found hers and gripped it, crushing. "It stopped screaming. It said — Freya, it said, *who is there? Who can hear me? Is someone out there? Please. Please, whoever you are—*" Ansel shut their eyes. "Three years in that dark and the first thing it does is beg."

Below them, the false king turned.

Not gradually. Not the way a man turns when he hears his name. The head came around with the smooth mechanical precision of a weathervane finding wind, and the warm brown eyes swept the crowd — past the onion merchants, past the young woman with her belated smile, past ten thousand upturned loving faces —

— and stopped on Freya.

On her specifically. On her and Ansel both. And for one suspended heartbeat the smile remained perfectly in place while the eyes emptied completely, became two windows into a room where nothing lived, and Freya understood, with the calm total clarity of a woman watching the axe rise, that the thing on the balcony knew exactly what had just brushed against its prize. It knew someone could hear. It was going to find out who.

Then the chamberlain stepped forward with the next petition, the king's gaze slid onward with a courtier's smoothness, the trumpets blared, and the moment passed — but Freya's heart kept hammering as though it hadn't.

"Back away slowly," Ansel murmured. "Smile. We're drunk and happy and leaving."

They melted backward through the crowd, two more bodies among thousands, and the crowd closed behind them like water. At the mouth of the Processional, Freya risked one last glance back at the balcony.

The king was waving. The banners hung dead in the wind. And high overhead, a line of migrating geese crossed the sky toward the eastern towers — and split, every bird, all at once, flowing around the palace in two clean rivers of wings, as though the building were a stone in a stream.

Neither of them spoke until they were four streets deep in the Tanner's Quarter, behind Brakka's locked door, with the dye-works stink sealing them in like a moat.

"It's not a man," Freya said. Her voice shook and she let it; there was no one left to perform strength for. "It's not a possession, it's not madness, it's not a curse on the poor man. It's a *thing*. It's been feeding on him for three years and it's started feeding on the courtiers, did you see the girl with the petition, did you see her *eyes*—"

"I saw." Ansel sat down heavily on the room's single cot and put their head in their hands. "And it felt me. Freya, I have hidden what I am from witch-finders for twenty years, and in one afternoon I showed myself to the oldest hunger in this kingdom. It will be looking for me now. For us. Voss will have new orders by morning, and the orders will not say *capture*."

Freya stood at the window, looking east through the gap between rooftops at the distant pale mass of the palace, at the dead banners, at the sky the birds refused to touch. Somewhere in there, a man named Aldric was screaming a dead woman's name into the dark, and had been for three years, and the thing wearing his smile had just learned that someone outside could hear him.

"We can't run," she said. "If we run, it finds us anyway, and Marta rots in a cell, and he screams forever."

"No," Ansel agreed quietly. "We can't run."

Outside, the vespers bells began again, and Freya noticed — for the first time, with a chill that settled into her bones and stayed — that Carrowmere's great cathedral bell had cracked. Everyone said it had cracked years ago. Everyone said it was scheduled for recasting.

No one ever seemed to schedule it.

From the broken bell, the note that rolled out over the city was flat and wounded and wrong, and it sounded, Freya thought, exactly like a scream heard through three feet of stone.

Tomorrow, they would need the smugglers. Tomorrow, they would need papers, roads, names — everything Odile's outlaw family could give, at whatever price the Crown would make them pay.

Tonight, in the dark above the dye-works, Freya sat beside Ansel and listened to them breathe, and did not sleep at all.
