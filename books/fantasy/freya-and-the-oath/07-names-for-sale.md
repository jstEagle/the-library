## 7. Names for Sale

Carrowmere smelled of river mud and roasting chestnuts and, beneath it all, of fear — a thin sour note that Ansel had learned to pick out the way other people picked out rot in fruit. They walked through the Fishgate market with their hood up and Freya's hand tucked in the crook of their arm, two travelers come down from the mountains to sell wool, and let the city's noise wash over them like a river over stones.

The dead came with them, of course. The dead always came.

*Cold,* murmured a voice near the fish stalls — a woman who had drowned in the Carrowmere canal forty years before Ansel was born and had never once stopped complaining about it. *Cold water, cold stone, and nobody pulled me out.*

*I know,* Ansel said inwardly, the way they did a hundred times a day. *I'm sorry.*

*You're always sorry.* But there was no venom in it. Grief without teeth.

Freya's fingers tightened on their arm. "The palace," she said quietly, eyes forward, lips barely moving. "Third spire from the left. You can see it from here."

Ansel looked. Above the tiled roofs, above the smoke, the royal palace of Carrowmere rose pale as a knuckle of bone against the grey sky. Even at this distance Ansel could feel it — not hear it exactly, but feel it, the way you felt a storm building behind a mountain. A pressure behind the ears. Something vast and patient coiled inside those walls, wearing a dead man's face and signing decrees with his living hand.

And underneath it, faint as a thread pulled taut across a valley, a human voice screaming.

Ansel had heard it since the ridge above the city. Not words — not yet — just sound, raw and endless, like someone calling for help from the bottom of a well so deep the world had forgotten the well existed. It made their teeth ache. It made the dead restless too; even the drowning woman had gone quiet when they crossed the bridge, as if she remembered something older than her own death.

"We can't walk in the front gate," Freya said. "We need papers. Real ones, with real seals. Archive clerks, I said — the Feast of the Founding brings clerks from every province to update the rolls. If we look boring enough, we vanish into the woodwork."

"And where does one buy *boring* in this city?"

Freya almost smiled. Almost. Since the waystation — since Marta — she had worn grief like a coat two sizes too small, and Ansel had watched her refuse to take it off. "There used to be a woman," she said. "When I worked the archive, contraband came through my office sometimes. Books with pages missing, seals that weren't quite right. Everything traced back to a fence called Odile. Nobody ever caught her. Nobody ever even saw her."

"Then how do we find her?"

"You don't." Freya stopped at a chestnut cart, bought a twist of paper full of hot nuts, and handed them over without being asked. "You make yourself findable. Odile finds people who need things. That's how she works — she doesn't sell, she *answers*. You just have to ask loudly enough, in the right quarter, and hope she likes your question."

"Which quarter?"

"The one where orphans go missing and exiles turn up fed." Freya nodded toward the warren of alleys south of the Dyer's Canal, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. "The Rookery."

*

The dead did not like the Rookery.

That was the first thing Ansel noticed. In most places the dead clung to the living like burrs — grievances, warnings, the occasional song — but in these narrow streets the voices thinned and hushed, and Ansel realized why within an hour: the living here took care of their dead. Every doorway had its little shrine, every corner its candle stub, every name spoken aloud on the anniversary of its losing. A city within the city, built by people who had nothing, who gave away what little they had because they understood that memory was the only inheritance that couldn't be stolen.

Orphans ran the gutters like minnows. An old soldier with one arm sold skewers outside a shuttered temple. A woman mended sails in a doorway and sang a mountain tune that Ansel knew from Thornfell, three hundred miles from any sea.

"They're watching us already," Freya murmured. "They've been watching since Fishgate."

"I know." Ansel had counted four pairs of eyes. Children, mostly. Good children. Children who would report everything they saw to someone who would feed them for it.

They asked at the sail-mender's stall. They asked at the temple. They asked, finally, at a tavern called the Drowned Bell, where Ansel laid a carved bone on the bar — a small thing, a whorl of sheep bone polished smooth, worked with a pattern any smuggler would recognize as a road-mark — and said to the barkeep, "I'm looking for answers."

The barkeep looked at the bone, then at Ansel, then at Freya, and poured three cups of cider instead of two.

"Drink," he said. "She'll come when she comes."

She came before the cups were empty.

Odile entered the Drowned Bell the way weather enters a valley — suddenly, and with everyone aware of it. She was perhaps fifty, broad and silver-streaked, dressed in merchant's wool worth more than the whole tavern, and she moved like a woman who had never once in her life been where she wasn't meant to be. Two of the gutter-orphans flanked her, aged maybe ten and twelve, walking with the gravity of bodyguards.

"Well," Odile said, sliding onto the stool beside Ansel uninvited. She picked up the road-mark bone, turned it in her thick ringed fingers, and smiled. "Thornfell work. Old school. Who taught you — the Hermit of Cold Pass? No, don't tell me, let me have my mysteries." She set it down. "You want papers. Palace-grade. During feast week. Do you have any idea what that costs?"

"Name it."

"Oh, I will." Her smile sharpened. "But first, names. Mine you have. Yours?"

Ansel hesitated. Beside them, Freya went very still.

"Names are dearer than papers in this city," Odile said, reading them easily. "But here's what you don't understand yet, mountain-child: in the Rookery, a name given is a name kept. Give me yours and it goes in my book and my book doesn't leave my person and nobody tortures it out of me because nobody gets past Pip and Wren here, and if they do, I've already eaten the page." She tapped her temple. "So. Names."

"Ansel Oyelaran."

"Oyelaran." Odile tasted it. "Bone-carver. Thornfell guide. The one the witch-finders have been sniffing after since spring." She didn't seem surprised. She never seemed surprised. "And your friend?"

Freya's jaw worked. Then, quietly: "Freya. Formerly of the Royal Archive."

"*Formerly* of the Royal Archive." Odile laughed, delighted, terrible. "Oh, you're the one who tore pages out of the prophecy book. Half the capital's looking for you. The other half is pretending not to be." She leaned in, and her voice dropped to something warm and serious at once. "Do you know what I do here? Everyone thinks I sell things. Papers, silks, secrets. No. I keep people alive. Every orphan in this quarter eats because of this network. Every exile has a bed. We are the only government anyone down here recognizes, and our only law is that we don't abandon each other. So understand what you're asking. Papers into that palace — during the feast, with witch-finders crawling the gates — that's not a sale. That's me spending my children's safety. That's me defying the Crown *openly*, in ink, in wax, in ways that can't be walked back."

The tavern seemed quieter. Ansel was aware of the dead pressing close now, listening — the drowned woman, the old queen's distant thunder, all of them drawn tight as wire.

"Then why haven't you thrown us out?" Ansel asked.

"Because Marta vouched for you." Odile's face changed — something moving behind it like a fish under ice. "Marta, who carried my Wren eight miles through a snowstorm with a broken fever. Marta, who is sitting in a witch-finder cell right now because she stood between Voss's iron and two strangers she'd known four days." She let that sit. "If Marta bled for you, then you're family, and family gets answered. But hear the cost, Ansel Oyelaran, and hear it plain: the moment those papers exist, the Crown knows Odile chose a side. There's no more neutral ground after that. No more *we're just merchants*. Everything I've built — every child, every roof — becomes a target."

"Then don't do it for us," Ansel said. "Do it for Marta. Freeing her means getting inside that garrison, and getting inside anything in this city starts with papers."

Odile studied them for a long moment. Somewhere behind her, the boy — Pip — shifted his weight, and Ansel realized the child had been cataloguing every exit, every blade, every patron since the moment they sat down.

"You argue like a smuggler," Odile said at last. "Fine. Here's my price, and it isn't coin. Coin I have. What I want is a favor, unspecified, callable at a time of my choosing, redeemable by you personally, Ansel Oyelaran, bone-carver of Thornfell. One favor. Could be carrying a package. Could be forging a document. Could be standing somewhere you'd rather not stand." Her eyes were steady and dark and kind and merciless, all at once. "That's the trade. A blank page with your name on it. Given what the Crown wants from you, that might cost you everything. So think."

Ansel thought of the screaming voice under the palace. Thought of Marta's cell. Thought of an oath sworn on a mass grave thirty years ago — *never serve the Crown* — and of how many small betrayals the prophecy had already asked of them, each one reasonable, each one a step.

The dead were silent. For once, they offered no counsel at all.

"One favor," Ansel said. "Callable once. Not against Freya's life, not against the children of the Rookery. Those are my limits."

"A limit on a blank page." Odile grinned and spat in her palm. "Done, and done. Pip — the book."

The boy produced a ledger from inside his coat, absurdly formal, and Odile wrote with a flourish: *One favor, owed by Ansel Oyelaran, to Odile of the Rookery, terms as spoken.* Then she signed it, had Ansel sign it, and had Wren witness it, and just like that Ansel's name existed in a second book, bound to a second oath, and they felt the weight of it settle onto their shoulders like wet snow.

"Papers in three days," Odile said, standing. "Archive clerk credentials, provincial seals, the whole dull costume. Wear boredom like armor, both of you. And—" she paused at the door, backlit by the lamplight of the alley, surrounded by her ragged honor guard, "—when you see the false king, look at his shadow. Everyone looks at his face. Look at the shadow. That's how I lost two of mine last year. They got close enough to see, and not close enough to run."

She was gone. The tavern noise rushed back in like water filling a hole.

Freya stared at the space where she'd stood. "You just sold a piece of yourself."

"I sold a promise," Ansel said. "There's a difference. Small. But there."

Outside, the Rookery's candles were being lit one by one, name by name, and the dead of Carrowmere settled around Ansel like a congregation taking their pews. And far off, beneath the pale spire, the voice in the crown screamed on — and tonight, for the first time, Ansel thought they caught a word inside the screaming.

Not *help*.

A name.

Their own.
