## 9. What Freya Tore From the Archive

The safehouse was a smuggler's loft above a tannery in Carrowmere's lower ward, and it smelled of oak bark, tallow, and old secrets. Freya had been awake for two days when she finally took the pages from the lining of her coat.

She had carried them through three hundred miles of mountain road. Through witch-finder checkpoints where soldiers turned out carts with iron-tipped staves, prodding at flour sacks and wool bales, hunting for heresy. Through the pass where Ansel had pulled her off the road by the wrist without a word, into a crack in the rock that no living map showed. Through nights in caves where Ansel spoke softly to people who weren't there, and Freya had pretended not to listen, because listening meant admitting what she'd become.

Now Ansel sat across the tallow-lamp from her, mending a strap on their pack with a bone needle, their hands moving with the patience of someone who had learned to wait for things — stone to soften, wood to cure, strangers to decide whether they could be trusted. The dead murmured somewhere behind Ansel's eyes; Freya could tell by the way they paused sometimes mid-stitch, head tilting, like a person hearing their name called from another room.

"You've been circling it for three days," Ansel said, not looking up. "Like a moth around a lamp. Whatever you tore out of that archive, it's eating you alive. So either show me, or stop looking at me like I'm going to burn it."

Freya's hands were shaking. She hated that. She had been a royal archivist of the Third Vault for eleven years. She had handled documents older than the kingdom itself, catalogued the correspondence of dead queens, touched vellum so fragile it breathed apart under glass. Her hands did not shake.

She pulled the oilcloth packet from inside her coat lining, where she'd sewn it with waxed thread the night before she ran.

* * *

The Third Vault sat beneath the Hall of Records in the capital, three floors down, behind a door that required two keys and a countersignature. Most archivists worked their whole careers and never saw it. Freya had been given access because of her mentor, Magister Elowen Hart — because Elowen had requested her specifically, seven years ago, with a note that read only: *She reads what isn't written down.*

It was true. Freya had a gift for gaps. For the letter that referenced an earlier letter no longer filed. For the ledger whose columns didn't sum. For the history with a chapter missing and the binding re-sewn by a different hand. Where other scholars saw holes, Freya saw fingerprints — someone had been here, removing things, and left traces the way a thief leaves scuff-marks on a sill.

For six years she found nothing worth the gift. Census revisions. A prince's debts quietly expunged. Then, fourteen months ago, in a box of tax rolls from the reign of Aldric's grandfather, she found a ledger that shouldn't have existed.

It was bound in grey leather, unmarked, and its entries were not taxes.

They were prisoners.

Names. Dates of transfer from the dungeons of Carrowmere to a destination recorded only as *the Founding Chamber*. And beside each name, in a clerk's careful hand, a number: how long the prisoner had lasted.

Some entries said *hours*. Some said *days*. One, near the end, said only: *still feeding.*

"I thought it was a cult," Freya said now, in the lamplight of the tannery loft. "Some secret rite. Old kings doing blood sacrifice, the way old kings do. Horrifying, but — human. Explainable." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I actually felt relieved. Isn't that obscene? I found evidence of decades of murder and I felt *relieved*, because murder has a logic to it. Murder is something people do."

Ansel had stopped sewing. The bone needle rested in the seam. They were watching her with those still grey eyes, and Freya realized she had never once seen them look away from anyone while the person was speaking. It was unnerving, being attended to like that. Like being heard by more than one listener.

"Go on," Ansel said.

"So I cross-referenced. That's my whole talent, really — I follow the paper. Transfers from the dungeon spiked every year in the week before the Feast of the Founding. Every reign. Every king. Aldric's grandfather, his father, and—" her voice caught, steadied— "Aldric himself. Three years ago. The last entry in the ledger is dated nine days after the Feast. Nine days after, the transfers stop. The ledger stops. And the same week, a new scribe takes over the court chronicle, and the chronicle's hand changes, and the king stops appearing in public except at feasts and window appearances."

She laid the oilcloth packet on the table between them but didn't open it yet.

"I brought it to Elowen. She read it once, all the way through, and then she sat very still for a long time, and then she asked me if I knew what the Feast of the Founding celebrated." Freya shook her head. "I said the founding of the dynasty. The first king's coronation. She said — everyone thinks that. But the oldest version of the rite, the one in the sealed liturgies, doesn't celebrate a coronation. It celebrates a *feeding*. The founding of the dynasty wasn't a crowning. It was a bargain."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below them, the tannery vats breathed their sour breath into the night.

"Elowen told me about the entity. Not what the smugglers call it, not campfire versions — she told me what the Crown's own records call it. In the earliest documents it has a name. They stopped writing the name down after the second century, because — this is the part that made me sick, Ansel, this is the part I still can't make sit still in my head — because writing the name helped it. Knowing it was known made it stronger. So the Crown's solution, for four hundred years, was to know everything about it and write nothing. To keep the knowledge alive in a vault three floors underground and starve it of ink."

"And the prisoners?" Ansel's voice was very quiet.

"The prisoners were the other half of the arrangement." Freya finally untied the oilcloth. Inside were eleven pages, torn raggedly from their bindings, covered in her own cramped annotations in the margins. "You don't keep something like that docile with ignorance alone. Ignorance protects *it*. Hunger controls it. Once a year, at the Feast, it surfaces fully — it has to, to renew its hold on the host, on the crown, on whatever bond ties it to the throne — and once a year, the Crown gives it a meal so the rest of the year it stays quiet. Stays *wearing* instead of *eating*."

She spread the pages out. Ledgers, transfer orders, a fragment of liturgy, and one page that was different from the rest — older, in a hand that slanted hard to the left, signed with a cipher Freya had spent four months decoding.

"What am I looking at?" Ansel asked.

"A confession." She touched the old page. "This is from two hundred years ago. A Lord Privy Seal named Corvane. He tried to end the practice. He wrote a full account — what the entity is, where it came from, why the crown can't just be set aside. Because that's the trap, you see. You can't abdicate your way out of it. The bond is to the *line*, not the man. Set the crown down and it takes whoever picks it up. Corvane proposed starving it instead — cutting the feedings, letting it weaken over generations until it could be pried loose. His king agreed. They cut the feedings for six years."

"What happened?"

"He happened." She tapped the confession's final lines. "The entity got hungry enough to reach past the host. It started taking people in their sleep — courtiers first, then servants, then a whole wing of the palace found empty one morning, beds still warm. Two hundred souls in a season. So the king restored the feedings, doubled them, and had Corvane executed for treason, and ordered every copy of his account burned. This is the only surviving page. Elowen found it thirty years ago and hid it, and she spent thirty years looking for the rest of the answer — some way to break the bond that didn't require either feeding it or unleashing it."

Freya's hands had gone cold. She folded them in her lap to hide it.

"Fourteen months ago I finished cross-referencing the transfer ledgers against the dungeon rolls. I identified every prisoner who went to the Founding Chamber during Aldric's reign. Eleven people. Debtors, a poacher, two foreign sailors, a woman who'd spoken against a grain tax." She looked up. "None of them were criminals, Ansel. They were *quiet*. People no one would miss. The Crown didn't feed it justice. It fed it silence."

The words hung there. Ansel's face had gone very still — not blank, Freya realized, but *listening*, the way they listened to the dead, as though her words were arriving from a great distance and deserved the same care.

"Three weeks ago," she went on, "I requested the transfer orders for the most recent batch. Standard archival procedure. My signature, my seal, my section. Two days later, Elowen was summoned to the Lord Protector's office. She came back to the vault that evening, calm as anything, and she gave me her keys and told me to take the Corvane page and the ledger leaves and go home by the river route, and that she would follow at dawn." Freya's voice frayed. "She didn't follow at dawn. At dawn the vault was sealed, the guards were doubled, and the official record says Magister Elowen Hart died of a fever in her rooms. There was no body released for burial. There was no burial. I checked. I'm the one who keeps the death registers, and I checked every register in the city, and Elowen Hart is nowhere in any of them."

The lamp hissed. Ansel hadn't moved.

"They fed her to it," Freya said. "My mentor, who taught me to read gaps, who pulled me out of the copying-rooms and gave me the Third Vault — they fed her to the thing in the crown because she'd spent thirty years trying to find a way to kill it, and because I'd found the ledgers, and they knew she wouldn't give me up. So she made sure I ran first." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "And the prophecy book — the one the peddler sold you, the one with tonight circled — I tore pages from its twin. There are copies, Ansel. The prophecy exists in at least three archives, and the Crown knows about all of them, and they've been *hunting the Unbinding* for longer than I've been alive. Not to use it. To prevent it. Because an Unbinding means the bond breaks, and a broken bond means a hungry thing with no host, and they would rather rule beside a monster than survive without one."

Silence. Long enough that the tallow flame bent and straightened twice.

Then Ansel said, "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because we're close enough to the city that Voss will find us soon, and if they take me, you deserve to know what you're dying next to." Freya met their eyes. "And because you swore an oath to the dead of Thornfell never to serve the Crown, and I need you to understand that I'm not asking you to serve it. I'm asking you to help me burn down the lie it's built on. Those are different things. I need them to be different things, or I can't ask."

"That's not what I asked." Ansel's voice stayed gentle. "I asked why *now*. You've had these pages for three hundred miles. What changed?"

Freya looked down at her hands. The honest answer sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Last night, in the cave. When the old queen screamed about the crown being stolen twice — you talked to her. You answered her. You treated a voice in the dark like a person owed respect." She swallowed. "And I realized I've been treating you the same way the Crown treats everything. As a resource. A guide with a useful affliction. I let you walk toward the palace without knowing what's waiting there, because knowing might make you turn back, and I need you not to turn back." Her eyes were burning now, and she let them. "That's what Elowen taught me and that's what got her killed — that knowledge is a debt you owe to whoever you share it with. I've been withholding payment. So. Paid."

Ansel was quiet a moment. Then they reached across the table — slowly, giving her time to pull back — and turned one of the ledger pages toward themselves. Their finger, stained faintly with bone-dust, traced the column of names.

"These two," they said. "The sailors. Where were they taken from?"

"A merchant brig out of Carrowmere harbor. Smuggling run, actually." Freya blinked. "Why?"

"Because Odile's crew still sings about them. Two brothers, lost with their ship eight years back — the story was that the sea took them." Ansel's jaw tightened. "The sea didn't take them. The Crown did. And Odile has spent eight years pouring wine into the harbor for drowned men who aren't in it." They looked up, and something in their face had shifted — the wariness banked down to embers, and underneath it, kindling. "You said you're not the prophesied hero. That you chose this."

"I chose it. In a reading room, over a ledger, with ink on my fingers. No sky opened. No voice named me." Freya laughed, wet and unsteady. "The book names me and I don't even know why. Maybe the prophecy is wrong. Maybe it grabbed the nearest name. All I know is what the Crown did to eleven quiet people and one old woman who loved me, and that nobody else is going to say it out loud. So I say it. That's the whole of my destiny. I *say it*."

Ansel nodded slowly. Then, unexpectedly, they smiled — small and crooked and real, the first Freya had seen from them.

"My whole life," they said, "the dead have told me the truth because they have nothing left to lose by it. The living lie for a hundred reasons. And here you are, handing me the worst truth you own, free of charge, when lying would have kept me walking beside you." They began rolling the pages back into the oilcloth, carefully, the way they handled bone. "Elowen trained you well."

"She trained me to die politely in an archive. Everything else I've improvised."

"Improvised well." Ansel tied the cloth and slid it back across the table to her — back into her keeping, not theirs, and Freya understood the gesture and had to look away for a moment because of it. "We'll need Odile for the papers. And Odile will want to hear about her brothers."

"It wasn't her brothers."

"No," Ansel agreed, rising, taking the lamp. "But grief keeps better books than any archive. She'll come for the principle." At the stair they paused, silhouetted against the tannery's dim glow, and looked back at Freya with an expression she couldn't yet read — softer than trust, heavier than gratitude. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go shopping for a forgery. And Freya—"

"Yes?"

"When we get inside that palace — when you see it, whatever it is — you won't be saying it alone anymore."

She lay down after they'd gone, on a straw pallet that smelled of leather and bark, and slept without dreams for the first time in fourteen months.

She woke to shouting in the street below, and to Ansel's hand clamped over her mouth, and to the sound of iron-shod boots on the tannery stairs.
