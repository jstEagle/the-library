## 10. Voss Closes the Net

The undercity of Carrowmere smelled of tallow smoke and river rot, and Captain Serah Voss had learned, over three days of hunting, to read it the way other men read scripture.

She moved without a lantern. The witch-finders' iron badges stayed wrapped in her pocket; down here, the badge was not authority but a target. Above her, the lawful city went about its evening — lamplighters on the Ringway, the bells of the Chancery counting out the hour in their flat bronze voices — but beneath the cobbles, Carrowmere became another country entirely. A country with no king, no law, no names that could be trusted. She hated it the way one hates a fever: intimately, and with a certain grudging respect for how long it could survive.

*They came down through the Salt Stair,* she thought, crouched at the mouth of an alley where the drainage channel ran black and slow. *Which means they know someone here. Which means they have a door.*

Everyone in the undercity had a door. That was the disease of it.

Behind her, at a respectful distance, came her two remaining finders — Brannic, broad and slow and loyal as a hound, and young Pell, who still flinched at shadows and would either die in this work or be made by it. Voss had requested no more than three. Crowds made noise, and noise was death in these streets. Also — though she would never have said so aloud — she did not fully trust what the others might do if they found what she suspected they were going to find.

The order from the palace had been precise. *The archivist Freya Aldenmar is to be taken alive. The mountain guide is to be taken alive if possible.* The word *possible* had been doing strange work in Voss's mind for three days now. In her experience, when the Crown said *if possible*, it meant they had already decided the answer, and were merely leaving her room to pretend otherwise.

She touched the iron ring beneath her glove — the old ring, her grandmother's, worn smooth as a river stone — and began to walk.

* * *

Her grandmother's name had been Maren Voss, and Serah had been eleven years old the first time she heard it spoken aloud outside the family.

It happened in the training hall at the garrison, from the mouth of an instructor who had pulled a yellowed ledger from a locked case and read it out like a curse: *Maren Voss, finder, third rank, year 212 of the Founding. Removed from rolls. Cause: failure of nerve at the Unbinding of Corvess Hall. Line struck.*

Thirty children in that hall, and every one of them had turned to look at her. She remembered the heat in her face. She remembered deciding, in that single burning moment, that she would spend her whole life making the name mean something else.

She had done it, too. Twenty years of service. Every oath renewed on schedule, every mark taken clean, every execution warrant countersigned without trembling hands. The instructors used her as an example — *this is what the Voss line becomes when it remembers itself* — and she had believed, right up until this week, that the story was finished.

Then a water-stained book had crossed her desk in the capital, recovered from a dead peddler's cart, and she had opened it to the genealogies at the back because that was habit, and there, in a spidery archivist's hand, was a list.

*Aldric, King. Unbound, failed.*
*Maren Voss. Unbinding attempted, failed. Executed.*
*Tomas Voss. Attempted, failed. Executed.*
*Serah Voss. —*

The dash after her own name had been written in fresh ink. Someone in the archive had been keeping her place.

She had burned that page. She had told no one. And she had volunteered for the hunt herself, before anyone could wonder why the name Voss appeared twice in a prophecy's graveyard — because if she did not take this commission, some other finder eventually would, and they would ask questions she could not afford to have asked.

But there was a second reason, one she kept folded away like a knife in a boot lining, and it was this: she had read the whole book. She knew what an Unbinding was. And she knew, with the cold certainty of twenty years' service, that if the thing in the crown was real, then everything she had ever sworn her life to protect was already dead, and had been dead for years, and the law she served was only the thing's digestion.

She did not believe it. She refused to believe it. But refusal, she was learning, took work.

* * *

The runner was fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and quick eyes, and he nearly walked into her.

Voss had spent two days doing nothing but watching doors. The smuggler roads through Thornfell emptied into the undercity at perhaps six points, and of those, three were watched by the Crown and therefore useless to anyone clever. That left the Salt Stair, the Drowned Chapel, and the Weep. She had put Pell on the Weep and Brannic on the Drowned Chapel and taken the Salt Stair herself, because her instinct said the guide — the bone-carver, Oyelaran — would come down through the salt caves, where the mineral ate torchlight and even the rats kept quiet.

On the third evening, her patience paid, though not in the coin she expected.

The boy came up out of the stair two hours past dusk, moving fast, a satchel bouncing against his hip. He was good — better than most adults she chased. He checked his backtrail twice, varied his pace, kept to the wall side where the shadows pooled deepest. But he was a child running an errand, and children run errands with their whole face. She saw the direction of his want before she ever saw him clearly: left at the tannery row, toward the warehouse district by the old customs house, where the Crown's writ ran thin as winter ice.

She let him pass her once. Then she stepped out of the doorway behind him and said, quietly, "Don't run."

He ran, of course. They always ran. But she had chosen the alley, and the alley chose the ending: forty feet of brick with a drainage grate at the far end that hadn't opened since the flood year. He hit the grate, spun, and there was nowhere left in the world to go.

Brannic took his arms. The boy fought like a wet cat, spat, cursed with a fluency that would have made a dockhand blush, and then — when Pell raised the lantern and the light caught the iron at Voss's belt — went suddenly, completely still.

"You're them," he said. "Witch-finders."

"I'm the Crown," Voss corrected. "The difference matters."

"It doesn't down here."

"No," she agreed. "I suppose it doesn't."

She crouched to his level, which was a technique and also, lately, something less comfortable than a technique. Up close he was thinner than he'd seemed, with a scab on his knuckles and the particular hollow-cheeked look of a child who gave his bread to younger mouths. His eyes went past her shoulder, quick as a bird's, cataloguing exits that didn't exist.

"What's your name?"

He pressed his lips shut.

"You'll give it to me eventually. They always do." She held out her hand, palm up. "The satchel."

He looked at her hand for a long moment. Then, with the fatalism of the very young, he shrugged it off his shoulder and dropped it into her palm.

Inside: bread, hard cheese, a twist of paper holding dried plums, and — wrapped in oilcloth with the care of something sacred — a small carved figure. Bone, she realized, turning it in the light. A little bird, wings folded, worked so finely she could see the individual feathers. It was beautiful. It was also, unmistakably, the work of Ansel Oyelaran, whose carvings hung in half the shrine-niches of Thornfell and whose name was on a royal warrant written in the same ink as hers.

"Who sent you?" she asked.

Silence.

"Where were you taking this?"

More silence, but of a different texture now. Frightened silence. Protective silence. She had interrogated smugglers, murderers, and one memorable cultist who had tried to eat his own confession, and she knew the sound of a person guarding something larger than themselves.

"The guide," she said. "Oyelaran. And the woman with him." She watched his face the way she watched candle flames — for the flicker. There. A twitch at the corner of his mouth, quickly killed. "You know them."

"They're kind," the boy said, before he could stop himself, and then looked horrified at his own mouth.

Something in Voss's chest shifted, half a degree, like ice beginning to move on a river.

"Yes," she said. "I imagine they are."

* * *

They took him to the safehouse above the cooperage — one of six bolt-holes the garrison maintained in the undercity, this one disguised as a tally-clerk's lodging. Brannic tied him to a chair with proper knots, not cruel ones, because Voss had given the order, and Pell sat by the window with his crossbow across his knees looking like a boy playing at soldiers, which, Voss reflected, was exactly what he was.

The prisoner's name, extracted with nothing worse than patience, was Pip. He claimed it was short for Pippin, which was a lie, and claimed he was sixteen, which was also probably a lie, and claimed he had no idea who Odile was, which was such an obvious lie that Voss almost admired it.

"Odile," she said, pacing the narrow room. "Fence. Receiver of stolen goods. Runs maybe thirty children as runners and lookouts. Keeps her ledgers in her head because she knows paper can be seized." She stopped in front of him. "Am I close?"

Pip said nothing. But his chin came up, and there was something new in his face — not fear anymore. Defiance, bright and brittle as spun glass.

"You can sit there being brave," Voss said, "and I'll sit here being patient, and we'll both grow old. Or you can listen for one minute, and then decide." She dragged the room's second chair around and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that he had to meet her eyes or study the floorboards. "Here is what I know. Two people came down off Thornfell three nights ago. One is a fugitive archivist who stole pages from a sealed prophecy. One is a bone-carver who hears the dead — yes, we know, we've known for years, the Crown is slower than rumor but it is not blind. They are hiding somewhere in this city, and someone is feeding them, and I am going to find them whether you help me or not. Do you understand that part? Whether. You. Help. Me. Or. Not."

His jaw tightened. But he was listening. Children always listened; it was the grown ones who stopped.

"Now here is what I know about Odile's crew." She let the pause stretch. "There's a girl called Wren, nine years old, who works the fish market. There's twins, Hallow and Sef, who sleep in the chapel loft. There's a boy called Martim who lost three fingers to a trap on the Weep stairs last winter and cried for a week, and Odile held him the whole time, and nobody reported it because down here nobody reports anything." She watched the color leave his face with each name. "I know your family, Pip. All of it. Because that is my job, and I am very good at my job."

"You wouldn't touch them," he whispered. "They're just kids."

"So were you, once. So was I." She leaned back. "Here is my offer, and I will make it once. You send word to your people — a note, a knock, whatever sign you use — saying you're held, and that the price of you is the archivist. Freya Aldenmar, delivered to me, alive and unharmed. When I have her, you walk out of this room, and I burn every file I hold on Odile's crew. Every name I just said to you goes into a fire, and no finder touches any of them again. That is not a trick. I don't have time for tricks, and I don't lie about children."

"And her?" Pip's voice cracked. "The archivist. What happens to her?"

Voss was quiet for a moment.

"That," she said, "is above my pay, and below my conscience, and I have learned to live between the two. But she is a grown woman who made her choices, and you are a child who was caught carrying bread. Those are different weights, and I know the difference even if the Crown doesn't."

"She won't come," Pip said. "Freya. She won't trade herself for me."

"Then Odile will make her," Voss said. "Because Odile is practical, and practical people do arithmetic. One adult against one child." She stood, and gathered the little bone bird from the table, and set it in his bound hands — gently, she noticed herself doing it gently, and did not examine why. "Think about the arithmetic, Pip. It's the only honest thing anyone will offer you this week."

At the door she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "the carving is very fine. He must care about you, whoever he is. People carve badly for strangers."

* * *

She walked the rooftops afterward, alone, because she could not bear the safehouse and could not yet bear the garrison either.

Carrowmere spread below her, the lawful city and the lawless one stitched together by lamplight. Somewhere down there, in a warehouse smelling of hemp and lamp oil, a fence and her orphans were deciding whether to sell a woman's life for a child's freedom. Somewhere beyond that, in a rented room, a bone-carver and a runaway archivist were planning to walk into the palace and unmake the throne she had sworn her knees to.

And somewhere above it all — or below, the theology was unclear — a king wore a crown that did not belong to him, and signed decrees in a steady hand, and smiled at his court, and the birds would not land on his roof.

Voss had seen the birds. Three days ago, from the Ringway, she had watched the palace eaves and counted: not one sparrow, not one starling, not one pigeon on miles of perfect stone ledge. The city's birds flowed around the palace like water around a deadfall. She had stood there until dark, telling herself it was coincidence, weather, migration, anything.

Her grandmother had died screaming, according to the old records. *Failure of nerve at the Unbinding of Corvess Hall.* But Voss had read the original report once, in a locked drawer, in a year she did not talk about — and the original report said Maren Voss had stood in front of the entity with an iron blade in each hand and refused to lower them, and had been cut down by her own fellow finders on the direct order of the Crown, because the Crown had decided the entity was more useful fed than fought.

*Failure of nerve.* Her family had carried that lie for three generations, and the lie had been load-bearing, and now she had gone and read the truth, and the whole architecture of her life groaned like a ship in a storm.

She stopped at the roof's edge, above the customs house, and looked down into the warehouse district. Lamplit windows. Voices. A world that kept faith without oaths, outlaws who bled for strangers while the lawful city fed its king to a hunger and called it peace.

*If they refuse the trade,* she thought, *then they are better than the law, and I am the fool my grandmother was.*

*And if they accept it—*

She didn't finish the thought. Below her, in the dark between warehouses, a shutter opened, and a scrap of paper went out on a wire to a waiting hand, and a signal lamp blinked twice in a window and went dark.

The net was closing. She only no longer knew, with any certainty, on whom.

Voss drew her cloak tight and went down the fire stair to write her terms — and to wait, with a soldier's terrible patience, for the answer that would tell her what she had become.
