## 11. The Trade That Wasn't

The dead did not like the millpond.

They told me so all night while I sat on the cold stone lip of the old gristmill and watched the water go silver and then black. *Too still,* they said. *Water that doesn't move is water that's listening.* I had learned long ago not to argue with the dead about listening. They were experts.

Below us, in the hollow where the millstream used to run before the Crown dammed it for their garrison sluices, Odile's people were arranging the trade ground the way a jeweler arranges a theft. Lanterns hung at deliberate heights. Crates stood where crates had no reason to stand. Every shadow on that flat had been placed by hand, and every one of them was a lie.

"You're favoring your left side," Freya said behind me.

"I'm favoring nothing. I'm sitting."

"You're sitting like a man guarding a wound he hasn't admitted to." She came around the millstone and lowered herself beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which I suspected was the point. She'd been doing that for two days — small contacts, incidental-seeming, as if she were checking that I was still solid. As if she'd watched an iron blade pass close to me once already and had decided proximity was a thing you could stockpile against worse.

"That blade missed," I said.

"Blades don't miss. People miss. Blades just wait." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Voss will bring more of them tonight. She'll bring iron."

I didn't answer, because the dead were answering for me, and what they were saying was: *yes.*

* * *

The trade was simple, which should have been my first warning. Simple things in Carrowmere are never simple; they are only things whose complications haven't arrived yet.

Voss had taken Pip — Odile's youngest runner, eleven years old, quick as spilled mercury — off a rooftop in the Tanner's Quarter three nights ago. And Voss had sent her terms by way of a clerk with shaking hands: the archivist, Freya Aldenmark, delivered alive to the millpond flat at moonrise, in exchange for the child, unharmed. No soldiers within a hundred paces. Her oath on it.

"She'll keep the letter of it," Odile had said, pacing her warehouse among the crates and candle stubs and the quiet industry of orphans who moved like they'd been born to it. "That's the trouble with witch-finders. They're honest the way a trap is honest. The jaw does exactly what it says it will do." She'd stopped pacing and looked at me with those bright, ledger-keeping eyes. "So we don't stand where the jaw closes."

Now, watching the flat from the mill, I saw the shape of her plan assembling itself in the dark. Two of her crew — Rell and the twins — crouched in the dry culvert beneath the sluice gate, where the water should have been and wasn't. Marta's cousin Sefa had taken the high window of the ruined tannery with a crossbow she handled like a grandmother handling knitting. And Odile herself would walk Freya down onto the flat personally, because Odile believed — correctly, I think — that Captain Serah Voss was a woman who respected only what she could see, and what she could see needed to be exactly one fence, one prisoner, and no surprises.

Which meant the surprise had to be mine.

"The dead," Freya said quietly. "What are they saying?"

I almost laughed. Three weeks ago she'd flinched when I spoke to empty air. Now she asked after them like neighbors.

"They don't like the water," I said. "And there's one of them I don't know. New. Been circling since dusk. A soldier's voice, by the cadence. He keeps saying *the third lantern.*"

Freya went very still. "There are only two lanterns on the flat."

"Yes," I said. "I noticed."

* * *

Moonrise came up red behind Thornfell, the way it does when there's dust in the air, or ash, or something the mountain has exhaled that it shouldn't have. We came down from the mill in single file, and I felt the dead fall in around us like an honor guard — the drowned child whose memorial bone still lay unfinished on my workbench, the old queen with her voice like frost forming, the hundreds of Thornfell names I carried in my skull because I had promised, at nine years old, kneeling in a pit of the massacred, that I would carry them.

*Carry our names,* they had said. *Never serve the Crown.*

And here I was, walking toward the Crown's captain to buy back a stolen child with a stolen archivist, and every step felt like a coin turning over in a palm, showing first its face and then its other face.

Voss was already on the flat when we reached it. She stood alone, as promised, hands visible, sword peace-knotted — but I could hear the men she'd sworn weren't there. Not voices. Heartbeats. No — not heartbeats either. It took me a moment to understand what I was hearing, because I had never heard it before: breath. Living breath, held and released, held and released, from the reed beds east of the flat and the culvert mouth west of it. The dead sounded like wind through doorways. The living sounded like this — wet, rhythmic, afraid.

"There are twelve of them," I murmured to Odile as we stopped at the agreed mark. "Reeds and culvert. She kept her hundred paces. She just made the paces wider than you'd think."

"Twelve," Odile repeated, pleasant as a woman discussing fish prices. "How industrious."

Pip stood beside Voss, small and pale and — I'll remember this until I die — glaring at the witch-finders with pure furious contempt, as though they were the ones who should be embarrassed. When Pip saw Odile, something crumpled and reformed in the child's face, and Voss's hand settled on Pip's shoulder, light as a bird, heavy as a stone.

"The archivist," Voss said. Her voice carried across the flat without effort. "Unharmed. That was the term."

"And the child walks to us first," Odile said. "That was ours."

For a moment nobody moved. The red moon laid everyone's shadow out long and wrong across the stones, and I thought — absurdly, in the last calm moment I would have for hours — that shadows looked like souls stretched thin, like things being pulled out of their owners.

Then Voss nodded, once, and pushed Pip forward.

Pip ran. Small boots slapping stone, breath sobbing, straight across the flat toward us — and I saw, with the terrible clarity the dead lend me at moments like this, everything at once. I saw Rell begin to rise in the culvert to cover the child's crossing. I saw the reeds shift as Voss's hidden twelve drew their blades in unison, iron catching the red moon. I saw the third lantern — the one that didn't exist, the one the new dead soldier kept naming — swing suddenly alight in the tannery window above Sefa's position, and I understood in one cold flash that Voss had known about the tannery, had let Sefa take it, had put her own signalman in the room above.

It wasn't a trade. It had never been a trade. It was a net drawn closed, and we were all inside the draw.

"*Down!*" I shouted, and the world came apart.

* * *

Bolts first. Two of them, from the tannery, meant for Odile and Freya — and both deflected low by something I would learn later was Rell's counter-team loosing a weighted line through the culvert grate to snag the ladder, dropping the signalman's aim at the worst instant. The smugglers had planned better. Of course they had. But Voss had planned best of all, because her twelve came out of the reeds and the culvert *both*, and half of them came out of the culvert on top of Rell and the twins, and the flat became a single screaming knot of iron and lantern-light and red-shadowed bodies.

I lost Freya in the first three seconds. That was how fast it happened — one moment her hand was in mine, the next the crowd of fighting bodies poured between us like floodwater, and I heard her shout my name and then heard nothing of her at all except through the dead, who see everything and tell you nothing useful.

*Left,* they said. *Iron, left. Run, left, the child, left—*

Pip. The child had frozen in the open, mid-flat, between the two lines, too frightened to run either direction. And a witch-finder — young, helmeted, his breath coming fast and wet the way the living breathe — had broken from the melee and was running at the child with his blade already moving into the guard position that meant *take, subdue, carry off,* and I understood that Voss's real plan had never been Freya at all. You take the smugglers' child twice. You teach them what defiance costs. You make the second price higher than the first, forever.

My body decided before I did. I would like to report that I weighed it, that the oath rose in me and argued, that I chose. I didn't choose. Nine-year-old knees in a mass grave chose for me. I crossed the flat faster than I have ever moved, and I put myself between the blade and the child, and the world narrowed to a single bright line of iron.

It went into me below the ribs, on the left, angled up, the way witch-finders are trained to skewer the things they hunt. Cold — that was the miracle of it, how cold iron is, colder than the night, colder than the dead — and then hot, unbelievably hot, and then I was on my knees on the stones with Pip screaming somewhere very close and very far away, and the young witch-finder was staring at me over his own hilt with the blank horror of a man who has stabbed someone who isn't what he was told he was stabbing.

He was still holding the blade.

I put my hand around his wrist, and I looked at him, and I did the thing I had spent my whole life never doing in front of the living. I listened — fully, openly, with my whole self — and I *spoke back* to what I heard, and it wasn't the dead.

It was the man. His name was Tam. He had a mother in the lowland villages who thought he collected taxes. He was twenty-two years old and he had never stabbed anyone before tonight and he was going to be sick.

"Let go," I told him, in his own mother's village accent, and he let go, and fell backward, and was sick.

* * *

After that, the night went strange.

I've been wounded before. Smuggler's work collects wounds the way rivers collect rain. But this was different, and I knew it was different even as Freya's arms came around me from nowhere — she'd fought her way *to* me, gods help her, with a stolen knife and no technique whatsoever — and pressed her cloak against the wound, and the blood came through it warm and endless.

Because the dead came closer.

Not metaphorically. I felt them arrive, the whole gray congregation of Thornfell, pressing in around the wound like warmth around a fire, and their voices — usually a murmur, usually a crowd heard through a wall — became distinct. Every one of them. Individually. The drowned child telling me the water under the mill was safe, actually, if I needed to lie in it. The old queen reciting names in order, as if reading me a ledger of who I was. And underneath them, closer than any of them, the living sounds of the flat: Tam weeping, Pip sobbing into Odile's coat, steel ringing and going quiet, ring and quiet, ring and quiet, as the smugglers' better plan finished swallowing Voss's good one.

"We're withdrawing," Odile was saying somewhere above me, brisk and fierce. "All of us, now, before she rallies. Can he walk?"

"He can lean," Freya said, and her voice cracked down the middle on the word *lean,* and I felt her arm slide under mine, and I felt — this is the part I will never manage to explain — I felt her heartbeat through her ribs against my side, quick and stubborn, and it anchored me better than the dead did.

We went out along the millstream, away from the roads, the way the dead insisted, and the dead walked with us so thickly that the night itself seemed crowded, seemed *peopled,* and I staggered under the weight of hearing so much and bled and laughed, gods forgive me, laughed at the sheer impossible abundance of voices.

And then, beneath all of them — beneath the queen and the child and the massacre-names and the murmuring thousands — I heard something I had never heard in my life.

A living voice, far away, screaming.

Not with the ears. With whatever it is I hear with. It came up through the dead the way a scream comes up through the floor of a house, muffled and immense, and it was a man's voice, worn ragged, and it was saying words that repeated, that had clearly been repeating for years:

*"Please. Please, whoever hears — please, I'm in here. I'm still in here. My name is Aldric. My name is Aldric and I am still in here—"*

I stopped walking. Freya staggered with me. "Ansel? What is it? What do you hear?"

I looked at her, and the red moon was behind her head, and the blood was going through her cloak onto my hands, and I said the truest thing I have ever said:

"The king is alive."

She stared at me.

"He's alive, Freya. He's been alive the whole time. He's screaming." My knees went. She caught me. Behind us the millpond flat burned its lanterns at nothing, and ahead of us the mountain waited, and inside my skull a starved man in a golden cage begged a bone-carver bleeding out on a river path to please, please, tell someone he was still in here.

"Hold on," Freya whispered, and it wasn't clear whether she was talking to me or to him. She got her shoulder deeper under my arm. "Hold on, both of you. We're going home, and then we're going to get him out."

The dead, all of them, went silent — the first total silence of my life — as if even they needed to listen.

Then the queen's voice, very soft, right against my ear, cold as frost forming on a crown:

*"Now you know why the crown was stolen twice. Come to me, bone-carver. I have been waiting a hundred years to tell you how it ends."*

The dark took me before I could answer.
