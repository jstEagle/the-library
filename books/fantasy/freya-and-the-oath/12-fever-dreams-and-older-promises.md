## 12. Fever Dreams and Older Promises

The safehouse was a smuggler's attic above a shuttered dyeworks in Carrowmere's tanner quarter, where the smell of old lye and wet hides covered every other scent — including, Freya hoped, the scent of blood. Ansel lay on a straw pallet beneath the sloped roof, wrapped in two blankets and Marta's spare coat, and burned.

Not the way a fire burns. The way a forge does: heat held deep in the body, radiating out through skin gone grey as ash. Their lips moved constantly. Sometimes words came. Most often they did not, just the shape of speech without sound, as if Ansel were talking to someone only they could hear.

Freya knew exactly who that someone was. There were many of them.

"Drink," she said, for the dozenth time since midnight, and pressed the cup of willow-bark and honey to their mouth. Ansel swallowed some of it. The rest ran down their chin, and Freya wiped it away with her sleeve because there was no cloth clean enough to waste on it.

"You have to stop doing this," she told them quietly. "Taking blades meant for me. It's a terrible habit. I want you to break it."

Ansel's eyes opened a sliver. Fever-bright, unfocused, fixed somewhere past the ceiling beams. "They're loud," they whispered. "Tonight they're so loud."

"I know." She didn't know, not really. She couldn't hear the dead of Thornfell; she'd spent three days now wishing she could, if only to know whether the voices were cruel or kind, whether they were dragging Ansel under or holding them up. "Tell them to wait. Tell them you're busy."

A ghost of a smile crossed the cracked lips. Then Ansel's head turned sharply, toward the corner where the roof met the floor, and the smile died.

"No," they said. "No, I swore. I *swore*."

And then they were gone again, down into whatever country the fever had opened, and Freya sat back on her heels with the empty cup in her hands and felt dread settle over her like a second blanket.

She had read the prophecy book cover to water-stained cover. She had catalogued its marginalia, cross-referenced its verses against four different archive recensions, done everything an archivist does to make the unknowable knowable. And nowhere — nowhere — had any version said what the Unbinding would cost the one who anchored it. The book spoke of kings and crowns and things that hungered. It did not speak of bone-carvers bleeding out in attics while the dead argued over their bodies.

*Someone who owes it nothing and loves its victim,* the old queen had said, through Ansel's mouth, in the cave below Thornfell. Freya had understood then what she was to the prophecy. She was still learning what she was to Ansel, and the learning terrified her more than witch-finders ever had.

* * *

The fever broke its first crest near dawn, and in the strange lucidity that followed, Ansel talked.

Not to her, exactly. To the room. To the air. Their eyes stayed closed but their voice came steady and low, the storyteller's cadence Freya had heard from them on the mountain roads, when they'd narrate which passes were safe by naming who was buried in them.

"—I remember the bridge," Ansel murmured. "Mama, I remember it. The water under it was red that day and you told me it was clay, you told me it was clay washing down from the high fields, and I believed you because I was seven and you were my mother and mothers don't lie about rivers."

Freya went very still.

"There were so many of us in the pit," Ansel went on. "You held my hand and made me say it before they finished filling it, because you said the dead don't hear well once the earth is on them. *Carry our names. Never serve the Crown.* Say it back, little bird. Say it back or they'll take your ears too."

"Ansel." Freya touched their shoulder. The muscle beneath her hand was rigid as carved wood.

"They're asking me," Ansel whispered. "All of them, all at once. They're asking me to come home. They say the king is Crown business. They say the prophecy is Crown business. They say I promised, and promises to the dead are the only ones that can't be broken, because the dead have nothing else."

"Then tell them—" Freya stopped. What? Tell them the oath doesn't matter? She'd seen what happened to people who treated oaths lightly; she worked in an archive full of the wreckage of broken vows, whole provinces drowned in ink because someone signed something carelessly. "Tell them you're not serving the Crown. You're unmaking the thing that sits on it."

Ansel's eyes opened. For a moment they were clear — truly clear, the grey sharp gaze that could find a hidden pass in a snowstorm — and they looked directly at her.

"And when it's unmade," they said, "the Crown remains. And I'll have walked into the palace of the men who dug that pit, on behalf of a king, wearing papers forged by smugglers, and every one of them will be able to say I served the Crown after all. That's what they see, Freya. That's what they've been whispering since Carrowmere. Not the entity. The walls around it."

"Then they're wrong."

"They're *dead*," Ansel said, with terrible gentleness. "They don't get to be wrong anymore. They get to be right forever, because no one can argue with a grave." Their eyes drifted shut again. "Don't argue with a grave, little bird. Mama said that too."

Freya sat in the greying light and held the cup she had no more willow bark for, and understood, with a cold clarity that felt like reading her own name in a condemned ledger, that she was watching a war whose battlefield was a single body — and that both armies loved their soldier.

* * *

Odile came at midday with broth, bandages, and news, in descending order of quality.

"The broth is mutton," she said, setting the pot on the flat of the cold stove. "The bandages are boiled. The news is that Voss has doubled the watch on every gate out of the lowlands and put a price on your heads that would buy this whole quarter. Also Marta sends her love from wherever they're keeping her, which we believe is the garrison on Tanner's Row, which is, conveniently, four streets from here, and no, before you ask, we are not doing anything stupid about that yet."

"I wasn't going to ask," Freya said.

"You were going to ask with your whole face." Odile crouched by the pallet and studied Ansel with the frank appraisal of a woman who priced everything honestly. "How bad?"

"The wound's closing. The fever isn't."

"Mm." Odile peeled back the blanket edge and looked at the bandaged side, where the witch-finder's iron blade had opened Ansel from hip to ribs. The flesh around the dressing was angry red, but the red had stopped spreading. "Iron cuts leave fevers. Everyone knows that. But this—" she nodded at Ansel's moving lips, the constant silent argument— "this isn't iron. This is the other thing."

"Yes."

"My grandmother was from Thornfell stock," Odile said, conversationally, as she rewrapped the wound with hands far gentler than her voice. "Before the massacre drove what was left of the family into trade. She used to say the mountain dead didn't haunt. They *held*. Like a hand on your wrist. Kind, most times. But a hand is a hand, girl. It can hold you up or hold you down, and it doesn't always know the difference."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

"Good. Fear means you're paying attention." Odile tied off the bandage and stood, cracking her back. "Keep them drinking. Keep them arguing, if you can — a mind that argues is a mind that stays. And Freya." She paused at the ladder, and for once the charm dropped off her voice entirely. "Whatever they promise you in there, whatever the dead offer, remember that a bargain made in fever isn't binding until they wake up. Don't let them spend themselves while they're not looking."

Then she was gone down the ladder, and the attic was quiet except for the mutton broth ticking as it cooled, and Ansel's lips, moving, moving, always moving.

* * *

Night came, and with it the second fever, higher than the first.

Freya had nursed people before — archivists collapsed over candle flames, a mentor with a cough that turned out to be much worse than a cough — but she had never kept vigil like this, where the enemy was not in the body but speaking *through* it. Ansel thrashed. The blankets twisted. And the words that came out of them were not Ansel's words, or not only Ansel's; the pitch shifted, aged, multiplied, a woman's voice breaking through a child's, an old man's rasp underneath both, dozens of registers layered like sediment in stone.

"*Never serve the Crown.*"

"*Carry our names.*"

"*Little bird, come home.*"

Freya knelt beside the pallet and did the only thing she could think of, the archivist's instinct, the reflex of a woman who had spent her life believing that recorded truth was armor: she talked back.

"His name was Aldric," she said to the dark room, to the voices, to whoever was listening. "King Aldric, third of his name, crowned at nineteen, who reopened the granaries in the famine year and ended the tithe on salt. He has a sister in exile who still writes him letters he'll never answer. He has been prisoner inside his own skull for three years, screaming, and Ansel *heard him*, heard a living man begging from behind his own eyes, and if any of you remember what it is to be unheard — if any of you remember screaming into a pit while the earth went over you — then you know why Ansel can't walk away."

The room went very quiet.

Even Ansel's thrashing stilled. Their head turned toward her voice, eyes still closed, and for a long moment the only sound was the rain starting against the roof tiles.

Then, in a voice that was not Ansel's — older, colder, a woman who had worn a crown and been murdered for it — the dark answered.

*"You love them."*

It was not a question. Freya's mouth went dry. She thought of lying and discarded it; you didn't lie to the dead, everyone knew that, it was practically the first law of the world.

"Yes."

*"Love is how it keeps its crown,"* the queen's voice said. *"It feeds on kingdoms through the ones who love them. Wives. Mothers. Archivists. It will use you to open every door."*

"Then it and I have something in common, because I intend to open exactly one door, and it leads to the throne room."

A silence. Then — and Freya would swear this to the end of her life — something that might have been laughter, dry as pages turning.

*"You sound like me, girl. That should frighten you."*

"It does."

*"Good. Fear means you're paying attention."* A pause. When the voice came again it was quieter, and it was almost — almost — Ansel's own. *"We do not ask them to abandon the king lightly, archivist. We ask because we know what waits in that palace. We died in a pit dug by royal order. We know what crowns cost the small people who stand near them. If they go, they may not come back whole. If they stay, they stay safe, and the world burns without them, and we — we would rather burn with our little bird than keep them in a cage we built out of grief."*

Freya blinked hard. "Then what are you asking for?"

*"For them to choose,"* said the dead of Thornfell, all together, in a voice like wind through a mass grave. *"Not the fever. Not the fear. Not even the oath. Them. Freely. As we were never allowed to choose anything."*

And the presence lifted — Freya felt it go, a pressure off the air like a storm passing — and Ansel sighed, a long ragged exhale, and sank into true sleep for the first time in two days.

* * *

Freya did not sleep. She sat by the pallet with the single guttering candle and watched Ansel breathe, and let herself, finally, carefully, look at the thing she had been refusing to look at since the moment a stranger fell out of the sky onto a mountain road.

She loved them.

Not the way the prophecy needed her to — or maybe exactly the way the prophecy needed her to, which was worse, because it meant she could never be sure whether the feeling was hers or the book's, fate's fingerprint pressed into her chest. She loved the dry humor that survived even fever. She loved the stubbornness that carried smugglers' cargo through witch-finder checkpoints and carried the names of the massacred through twenty years of silence. She loved that Ansel took a blade meant for a woman they'd known for six days and called it a habit.

And loving them, she now understood, was a weapon anyone could pick up. The entity could use it. The dead could use it. The prophecy itself was built on it like a house on a foundation. *Someone who owes it nothing and loves its victim.* She owed the entity nothing, that was easy. But she owed Ansel everything already — her life on the road, her purpose, the first months in years when being Freya had felt like a choice instead of a sentence — and debts could be collected.

There was a version of the next weeks where she protected Ansel by ending this. She could see it clearly: the two of them walking north, back over Thornfell, back to the workshop that smelled of bone dust and beeswax, letting the king scream, letting the entity feed, keeping her person whole and hers. The dead had offered Ansel that bargain on the mountain — silence in exchange for surrender — and Freya had hated it, and now she caught herself understanding it, and hated herself a little for that.

And there was the other version. The throne room. The rite. The tearing-free, the screaming, the cost nobody had written down in any margin of any book. In that version she might save a kingdom and lose the specific person whose breathing she was counting right now, four in, five out, uneven but steady, steady.

*Marta bled for strangers,* she thought. *Odile defies the Crown for orphans. Voss hunts us because her law is the only wall she sees between order and the pit.* Everyone in this story was bound by something. The question the fever had asked her, the question she would carry into whatever came next, was not *what does the prophecy require.*

It was *what do I require of myself, when every path costs someone I love.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the rain — faint, rhythmic, wrong — came the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving up Tanner's Row, unhurried, in the ordered cadence of a garrison patrol.

Freya pinched out the candle and moved to the window slit.

Four streets away, torchlight bloomed at the door of the witch-finder garrison — and above it, hung from the garrison's upper window like a banner, like a message, like bait, a scrap of cloth she knew. Marta's scarf, the one with the faded blue thread.

Below it, chalked across the garrison door in a neat, precise hand, were two words:

**COME AND SEE.**

Down on the pallet, in sleep that was finally dreamless, Ansel murmured one word — "*soon*" — and Freya, with her heart hammering against her ribs, could not tell whether they had dreamed it or heard it too.
