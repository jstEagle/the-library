## 13. The Road Under the Mountain

The fever broke on the ninth day, and the first thing Ansel heard was silence.

Not true silence — never that — but a hush, as if the dead of Thornfell had drawn back to give them room to heal. They lay in the safehouse above Odile's warehouse in Carrowmere, sweat-soaked sheets twisted around their legs, and listened to the absence where the murmur should have been. It lasted perhaps a minute. Then old Halvard's voice came grumbling through the floorboards of the world — *the boy's awake, tell him he owes me a funeral song* — and Ansel laughed until the stitches in their side screamed.

Freya was asleep in the chair beside the bed, her head tipped against the wall, an open book sliding from her fingers. She'd cut her hair short with a smuggler's knife sometime during the fever; it made her look younger and harder at once, like a coin worn smooth. Ansel watched her breathe and thought: *I took iron for her. I would take it again. That is the whole of me now, isn't it.*

The dead murmured their agreement. The dead, annoyingly, always agreed with love. It was one of the few things they were sentimental about.

"You're staring," Freya said without opening her eyes.

"You're drooling on my grandmother's letters."

"I'm *resting my eyes* on your grandmother's letters." She sat up, wincing, and pressed her palm to Ansel's forehead the way she had done every few hours for nine days, as if fever were a door she could hold shut by force. Her hand was cool. "How do you feel?"

"Like I was run through by a witch-finder."

"You *were* run through by a witch-finder."

"And yet I feel worse than that sounds." Ansel flexed their fingers, tested the pull of the bandage across their ribs. The wound had closed clean — Marta's sister-in-exile, a herbwoman named Sefa, had packed it with moss and something bitter that smelled of the high passes. "I feel," Ansel said slowly, because it was true and strange, "*clear*. Clearer than I've felt since I was seven years old."

That was when the dead began, very softly, to sing.

Ansel went still. Freya saw them go still, and her hand found theirs on the blanket.

"What is it?"

"They're singing." Ansel tilted their head, listening past the walls, past the city, north and up into the mountains where Thornfell stood black against the stars. "They never sing. They complain. They grieve. They argue about whose grave is whose. But they don't—" The melody resolved, and Ansel's blood turned to snow water. "It's a road-song. A walking song. They're singing the deep road."

"The one under the mountain."

"The one under the mountain." Ansel met her eyes. "They want us to come home, Freya."

* * *

They left Carrowmere three days later, in the gray hour before dawn, dressed as wool-traders with papers Odile had forged so well that Ansel almost believed they were who the documents claimed. Odile herself saw them to the north gate, a small sharp woman wrapped in a fox-fur cloak despite the season, her crew of orphans arranged around her like ducklings with knives.

"The deep road," she said, and spat, though whether at the road or the dead Ansel couldn't say. "My grandmother ran it once, in the famine years. Came out the other side with her hair gone white and wouldn't speak of it except to say the mountain has a throat, and you don't walk down a throat unless something's swallowing you already."

"Something is," Ansel said.

"Mm." Odile looked at them for a long moment with her quick dark eyes — the same eyes she used to price stolen goods and weigh human hearts, and she was equally unsentimental about both. "You know what I think? I think the dead have been keeping you like a candle in a window. Useful, and theirs. And I think they know that if you reach the king, they lose you." She shrugged, fur rippling. "But then, I sell people things they shouldn't buy for a living. What do I know about bargains?"

She pressed a lantern into Ansel's hands — not oil but a slow-burning bone-tallow that gave light the color of moonrise — and kissed them once on each cheek, hard, like sealing a letter.

"Come back," she said. "All of you. Even the parts I find tiresome."

Then the gate opened, and the mountain took them.

* * *

Three days they climbed, following smuggler marks that only Ansel could read — a cairn built wrong, a blaze carved to look like lightning damage, a stream crossed at the one stone that didn't shift. The dead walked with them, louder now with every mile, and Ansel understood why the living avoided these slopes even before the massacres. Grief soaked into stone here the way rain soaked into everything else. By the second night Freya had stopped asking what the voices were saying. She simply walked closer, shoulder brushing Ansel's shoulder, and let the noise break against both of them together.

On the third evening they reached the door.

It was set into the root of the mountain itself, beneath an overhang where the rock folded like a curtain, and it was not a door so much as the memory of one — a slab of granite carved with spirals older than any kingdom, older than Aldric's line, older maybe than the idea of kings. The smugglers called it the Cold Gate. The dead called it nothing. The dead, for the first time in Ansel's life, had gone quiet.

"They're waiting," Ansel whispered.

"For what?"

"For us to choose to hear them." Ansel's hand hovered over the spiral. The stone was cold enough to burn. "Everything since the bridge, everything since the fever — it's all been voices. Words. But past this door they're not words anymore. Past this door they're *present*. Marta told me once, when I was young and stupid and asked too many questions. She said the deep road doesn't pass the grave, Freya. It passes *through* it. And the dead down there don't talk to you." She swallowed. "They look at you."

Freya put her hand over Ansel's, pressing it flat against the cold stone. "Then we look back," she said. "Together."

Ansel pushed, and the mountain swallowed them.

* * *

The deep road was exactly what its name promised: a passage bored through the roots of Thornfell, wide enough for two abreast, descending in long patient spirals toward the heart of the range. The bone-tallow lantern burned without flickering, throwing light the color of drowned moons along walls carved with spirals upon spirals upon spirals, thousands of years of them, some worn to whispers, some still sharp as the day a chisel bit stone.

And the dead were there.

Not voices. Not yet. *Presence.* Ansel felt them the way you feel weather coming — pressure behind the eyes, weight on the chest, the sense of a great crowd standing just outside the circle of light, patient as stone. Freya felt it too; her breathing went shallow and quick, and she gripped Ansel's arm hard enough to bruise.

"How many?" she breathed.

"All of them." Ansel's voice came out thin. "Every soul Thornfell ever buried. They've been walking beside us for days. Now they're—"

The road opened.

It opened into the grave.

Ansel knew it instantly, the way you know your own face in a mirror — the shallow bowl of earth ringed with standing stones, the mass pit beneath where the soldiers of the Crown had dumped two hundred bodies of a village that had refused a tax and paid in fire. Ansel had been seven. Ansel had been the only one left, hiding in a hollow tree while the smoke did its work, and when the soldiers rode away a voice had risen from the pit — a woman's voice, cracked and kind — and said: *Little one. You hear us? Then swear.*

The bowl lay silent under the lantern light. No wind reached this deep. And rising out of the darkness all around it, the dead came into being.

They did not glow. They did not drift. They simply *were*, suddenly, the way a face emerges from a crowd — hundreds of them, standing in the rings between the stones, farmers and shepherds and children and the old queen from the prophecy's margins, and every one of them looking at Ansel with expressions of such terrible tenderness that Ansel's knees nearly went.

"There you are," said the woman with the cracked kind voice — Mara, she had been in life, the miller's wife. "There you are, little one. All grown."

"Mara." Ansel's voice broke on it. Twenty years of hearing her, and only now seeing her. "Mara, I carried the names. Every one. I never—"

"We know." She smiled, and it was the smile from the worst night of Ansel's life, the last kindness in a burned world. "We know, child. You carried us beautifully."

Freya stood close, her hand a vise on Ansel's wrist, her eyes wide and wet. She could not see them — or perhaps she could see something, some shimmer, some disturbance in the lantern light — but she could feel the weight of them, and she held her ground like a woman holding a door against a flood.

"Why here?" Ansel managed. "Why now?"

"Because you're going to the king," said another voice — Halvard, the old shepherd, stepping forward, his crook in his hands. "Because you mean to unbind the crown. And because we love you, little carver, and we are so tired."

"Tired?"

"So tired." The word moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. "Two hundred years, Ansel Oyelaran. Two hundred years of whispering through your ears. Of borrowing your life because we lost ours. Do you know what we hear, inside you? Everything. Your griefs, your hungers, your—" a ripple of something like laughter, gentle, sad, "—your archivist. We hear you love her. We have known since the bridge. Before the bridge."

Heat crawled up Ansel's neck. Freya's grip tightened, but she said nothing.

"We are not angry," Mara said. "Hear us. We are not angry, and we do not accuse. But we offer you a bargain, and we need you to listen with the whole of that clever heart."

The crowd parted. Beyond them, at the far edge of the bowl, the standing stones framed a view of the road continuing downward — deeper, toward the lowlands, toward Carrowmere, toward the palace and the thing wearing Aldric's face.

"Walk away," Halvard said simply. "Turn south tomorrow. Leave the king to his hunger and the court to its moths. Go home to your workshop. Marry your archivist. Grow old carving bones for children who drown, and grow old *with* her, and when you die we will be waiting, and it will be soft."

"And in exchange," Ansel said, and their mouth had gone dry, "you'll stop."

"In exchange," said the old queen, stepping forward at last — tall, imperious even in death, her crown a ghost of ghost-light, "we will fall silent. Truly silent. Not hushed. Not sleeping. *Gone*, little carver. Unbound from you as surely as the thing must be unbound from the boy on the throne. You will wake tomorrow and hear nothing but wind and water and her voice across the breakfast table, and it will be the sweetest silence any soul ever bought."

The silence after that was absolute. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

Ansel looked at the dead. At Mara's cracked kind smile. At Halvard's patient stoop. At two hundred faces that had lived in their skull for twenty years, that had been their only family through the lean years, their chorus and their council and their curse. Voices that had never lied. Voices that had kept them company in the dark when no living person would sit with the bone-carver who talked to graves.

And the bargain was *good*. That was the horror of it. It wasn't a devil's trade, twisted and cruel. It was rest offered kindly. It was the wound offering to close.

"I could sleep," Ansel whispered. "I could actually—"

"Yes," said the dead, all of them, gently.

"But the king can't." Ansel looked up, and their eyes were burning. "There's a man screaming inside his own skull. I heard him. Through a fever, through iron in my ribs, I heard him begging. And if I turn south, nobody hears him again. Nobody *can*." Their voice steadied, hardened, became the voice that guided smugglers through blind passes. "You taught me to carry names. All of you. That was the whole oath — carry our names, never serve the Crown. But there's a name in that crown, and it's drowning, and you're asking me to let it drown so I can have a quiet kitchen."

Mara's smile faltered. "Child—"

"No." Ansel stepped forward, into the ring of stones, alone, the lantern held high. "No. Hear *me*. I love you. I have loved you since I was seven years old and you were the only warmth left in a burned world. If there were any other way to give you rest, I would carve it out of my own ribs. But I will not buy my silence with his screaming. I won't. So either curse me, or bless me, or get out of my road — but I am going to the king."

For a long moment, nothing. The dead stood in their hundreds, still as carvings, and Ansel stood among them with their heart hammering and Freya's absence at their back like a missing limb.

Then Mara began to laugh.

It was a small sound at first, cracked and kind, and then it spread — Halvard chuckling, the children giggling, the whole grey crowd rippling with laughter that had no cruelty in it at all, only an enormous, exhausted relief.

"Oh, little one," Mara said, wiping at eyes that were only memory. "Oh, you stubborn, mule-hearted *carver*. Did you think we didn't know? Did you think we hoped?" She spread her hands. "We had to ask. We owed you the asking. Because here is the truth we could never say while we lived in your ears, and it is the reason we brought you here:

"The bargain was never ours to give. It was yours. Silence or the king — we could only show you the door. And you—" her voice caught, frayed, went bright, "—you chose the way we always knew you would. You chose the drowning stranger. You always did. It's why we picked you, that night in the pit. Out of all the ones who hid and all the ones who ran, we picked the child who climbed *down* into the smoke to pull a body out."

Ansel's legs gave. They went down into the cold grass of the grave-bowl, into the dirt that held everyone they had ever loved, and wept.

Freya was there in a heartbeat, kneeling, gathering them in, her chin hooked over their shoulder, her arms locked tight. Above them the dead drew close — not crowding, never crowding, but near, the way family stands near a sickbed — and the old queen's voice came down like a benediction:

"Go, then. Both of you. And know this, bone-carver: when you stand in that throne room and call us through the bone — we will come. All of us. Louder than we have ever been. Not because you owe us." Her ghost-crown flared. "Because you *freed* us to follow you."

The lantern guttered. When the light steadied, the bowl stood empty, the stones bare, the road ahead open and dark and waiting.

Only the spirals on the nearest standing stone had changed. Fresh-cut, sharp-edged, impossible: two names, side by side, carved in a hand Ansel recognized as their own.

*Freya. Ansel.*

Bound together, the way oaths are written. Or promises.

Freya helped them up. Neither of them spoke. They shouldered the packs, lifted the lantern, and walked on down the throat of the mountain — toward the lowlands, toward the Feast of the Founding, toward the throne room where a hungry thing waited wearing a dead man's smile.

Behind them, faint as breath, the dead of Thornfell began to sing the walking song again.

This time, Ansel sang back.
