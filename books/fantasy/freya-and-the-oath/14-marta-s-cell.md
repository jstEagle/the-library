## 14. Marta's Cell

The witch-finder garrison at Carrowmere had been a granary once. Freya could still see it in the bones of the building — the great arched doors widened for carts, the ventilation slits near the roofline that had let grain breathe and now let screams drift out on cold air. She crouched in the shadow of a tanner's shed across the yard and told herself, for the third time, that she had done worse things than this.

She hadn't, of course. That was the lie of it. She had stolen papers, fled her post, led soldiers to a mountain pass by her own incompetence. But she had never walked into the place where people were unmade.

*You owe her,* said the small voice that sounded like her mentor, Edvard, dead three years now and still the loudest thing in her head. *Marta stood in a doorway for you. Old woman, bad hip, iron chains, and she stood there and lied to Voss's face with a smuggler's fluency you couldn't buy.*

Freya checked the sky. Overcast, low, the kind of grey ceiling that made shadows soft and faces indistinct. Ansel had wanted to come with her — had argued for it in their quiet, stubborn way, hands moving as if shaping an argument out of bone — but Freya had won, and winning had felt less like victory and more like handing someone you loved a knife and asking them not to use it.

*Because they'd hear them,* she thought. *Ansel would hear every soul in that building. The ones Voss has broken. The ones still waiting to be broken.* She had seen what the garrison did to Ansel from two streets away: the way their step faltered, their jaw tightened, their eyes went somewhere else entirely. Freya could walk through horror with her eyes open. Ansel would have to hear it with theirs shut.

So she came alone, wearing the grey cloak of a laundress, carrying a basket of linens she had actually purchased from an actual washerwoman who would be paid double when this was over, if there was an *over*.

The gate guard was young. That was the first mercy of the evening. He looked at her basket, at her lowered eyes, at the laundry mark stitched on her sleeve — Odile's forger had done it in under an hour, along with the forged duty roster that would get a laundress waved into the east wing twice a week — and he waved her through without touching the linens.

Inside, the granary's old architecture betrayed its new owners. The central floor had been raised into offices and interrogation rooms, but the original grain chutes remained, running down through the building like veins. And the ventilation system — Freya had spent eleven years in the royal archives; she knew buildings the way Ansel knew bone — the ventilation ran the whole height of the structure, shaft within shaft, and if the maps Odile's runner had copied were true, there was a maintenance gallery behind the east wall that no one had entered since the building changed hands.

She found the gallery door behind a rack of drying uniforms. It opened with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The gallery was narrow, dark, and smelled of dust and pigeon droppings and, beneath that, something sweeter and worse — lamp oil, hot metal, the particular scorched smell of the witch-finders' instruments. Freya moved by touch, one hand on the wall, counting doors by the light that leaked through the seams. Most were dark. Offices, empty at this hour. One glowed amber and hummed with voices — clerks, arguing about requisitions, the banality of it almost obscene.

Then she heard Marta's voice, and stopped so fast she nearly dropped the basket.

It came through a seam in the wall paneling to her left, thin as thread but unmistakable: dry, unhurried, threaded with the particular patience of a woman who had survived forty years of mountains and intended to survive this too.

"—told you, dearie. I sell wool. I mended a hem for a girl who passed through. I don't ask my customers where they're going. Only fools and tax collectors ask that."

"And the girl," said another voice, and Freya's blood went to ice water, "had eyes the color of wet slate. And she traveled with a bone-carver."

Captain Serah Voss. Freya had heard her speak exactly once, across a courtyard, giving orders — and even then the voice had lodged somewhere under her ribs like a splinter. It wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing about it. It was *precise*, the voice of a woman who believed every word she said, and Freya had spent enough years among fanatics to know that precision was more dangerous than cruelty ever was.

There was a pause. Then the creak of a chair, the clink of metal — chains, or manacles, or both.

"Mrs. Marta," Voss said, and something in her tone shifted, softened by a degree, which frightened Freya more than shouting would have. "I am going to tell you something I do not tell prisoners. I read your file. Forty-one years old. Widowed. A son who died at Harrow Ford fighting for the Crown. You have given this kingdom everything it asked of you. So understand that when I say I take no pleasure in this room, I mean it as fact, not courtesy."

"Then leave the room, dearie. Save us both the lamp oil."

A breath of laughter — Voss's, short and genuine, and Freya pressed her palm flat against the cold wall because the world had tilted slightly sideways. Witch-finders did not laugh at their prisoners' jokes. Witch-finders were not *people*, in the stories, in the archive reports she had catalogued by the hundred. They were instruments. She had filed them under instruments.

"I'm going to ask you once more about the bone-carver," Voss said. "Not because I doubt your answers. Because I need you to understand what happens tomorrow if those answers don't change."

Silence. Then Marta again, and her voice had changed too — lower, older, carrying something Freya hadn't heard in it before. Not fear. Recognition.

"You've done this before," Marta said. "This speech. This kindness. You learned it somewhere."

Another silence, longer. When Voss spoke, the precision had cracked down the middle.

"My mother was a witch-finder," she said. "And her mother before her. We are not recruited, Mrs. Marta. We are *raised*. There is a book we learn from before we can write our own names." A chair scraped. "You are going to tell me where the bone-carver is, because the alternative is that I stop being the person in this room who takes no pleasure in it, and neither of us wants to meet her."

Freya should have moved on. Every instinct honed by three years of flight said *move, move now*. But her feet had rooted, because Voss had said *book*, and Freya had spent the last month sleeping with a water-stained prophecy book under her head like a pillow and a weapon at once.

Through the seam, she heard pages turning. Heard Voss say, almost to herself: "Read it back to me. All of it. The passage about the Unbinding."

Marta's voice, weary: "I can't read, Captain."

A pause. Then Voss began to recite, and Freya's mouth went dry, because she knew the words. Knew them the way you know a scar.

*"When the crown lies hollow and the throne eats its own, an Unbinding shall come: one who owes the hunger nothing and loves its host, and they shall speak the king free or die in the speaking. Thrice has it been attempted. Thrice has it failed. The first was burned. The second drowned. And the third—"*

Voss stopped.

"The third," Freya whispered against the wall, finishing it from memory, her lips barely moving, "*the third bore the name Voss.*"

She had read the line a dozen times in Ansel's workshop, by candlelight, while the dead murmured at the edges of the room. She had assumed it was a common name, a scribal error, a coincidence — the archive-trained part of her had built three separate explanations and filed them all. Now, listening to a witch-finder captain's breathing go ragged on the other side of an inch of wood, every explanation caught fire at once.

"That's enough," Voss said sharply. Chains rattled — Marta shifting, or being made to shift. "Guard. Take her back to holding."

Footsteps. A door. Then silence, except for one sound Freya had to lean close to catch: the sound of a woman sitting alone in a lamplit room, breathing like someone learning the skill anew.

* * *

Freya waited in the dark of the gallery for the length of four bells — she counted them by the watch-crier in the street outside, muffled through stone — until the garrison settled into its night rhythm. Two guards on the main floor, rotating hourly. One on the cell block stairs. The interrogation wing emptied after the last session, per the duty roster Odile's forger had copied, and per the truth she confirmed with her own ears.

Getting Marta out was the easy part, in theory. Cells meant locks, and locks meant keys, and keys meant either theft or a forgery good enough to pass in lamplight. Odile had provided both: a copied key blank, ground to fit by a locksmith who owed the network a favor, and a story — a transfer order, signed by a magistrate whose signature Odile could reproduce in her sleep. Prisoners got moved between holdings all the time. Paper made the world go around; Freya had built her whole life on that principle, first honestly and then otherwise.

The hard part was that Marta was chained, and Freya had never struck a shackle off a living person, and her hands shook so badly on the first attempt that Marta took the hammer from her gently, the way you'd take scissors from a child.

"Breathe, love. You'll wake the dead."

"Don't say that," Freya whispered. "You have no idea."

Marta worked the pin loose with three efficient blows, wrapped in cloth to muffle the sound, and then they were moving — laundress and prisoner, through the service corridor, toward the coal chute that opened onto the alley behind the tannery. Twenty feet from freedom, Marta stopped.

"Girl." Her hand closed on Freya's wrist, iron-strong despite everything. "The captain. Before they took me back to holding, she asked me one question off the record. Off the paper. She asked—" Marta's eyes, adjusted to the dark, searched Freya's face. "She asked if the bone-carver hears them. The dead. And when I didn't answer, she said, *'My grandmother heard them too.'*"

The coal chute was right there. The alley beyond it. Odile's runner waiting with a cart.

"What did you tell her?" Freya managed.

"Nothing. But she knew I was lying, and she let me lie." Marta shook her head slowly. "That's not a woman hunting you, love. That's a woman standing at a crossroads. And crossroads women are more dangerous than hunters, but they're also the only kind who can change direction."

They went down the chute together, into the dark and the coal dust and the waiting cart, and Freya lay flat among the sacking with Marta's hand gripping hers and thought about inherited wounds, and oaths, and the exact wording of a prophecy line she could recite in her sleep.

* * *

The safehouse was a dyer's loft above the river, reeking of mordant and wool, where Odile's people kept a cot and a kettle and a view of nothing but water. Marta was installed downstairs with a hot meal and three smugglers who treated her like a returning general. Freya sat by the window with the prophecy book open on her knees and read the passage until the words blurred.

*Thrice attempted. Thrice failed. The first was burned. The second drowned. And the third, Voss by name, broke upon the threshold of the throne and was unmade, and her line was bound thereafter to the finding of witches, lest the hunger call to her blood again.*

*Lest the hunger call to her blood again.*

Freya had assumed the last clause was a curse laid on the family. Sitting here, she understood it differently. It was a leash. The Crown hadn't punished the Voss line — it had *employed* it, generation after generation, pointed at other people's magic so they'd never look at their own. Oaths could be inherited. Wounds could be inherited. Serah Voss had been born into a failure she'd never committed, handed an instrument, and told it was a sword.

The door below creaked. Footsteps on the stairs — uneven, careful, a weight favoring one side. Ansel. Freya knew their tread now the way she knew the weight of a well-made book, and something in her chest loosened and tightened at once.

They came up pale, snow-dusted, smelling of pine and road. Their eyes found her immediately, then the open book, then her face, and whatever they saw there made them cross the room without a word and sit beside her, close enough that their shoulder touched hers.

"Marta's out," Freya said. "Safe. Eating. Insulting everyone's cooking."

"Good." A pause. Ansel's gaze dropped to the page. Freya watched them read the line — watched their lips move slightly, the way they did when the dead were quiet enough to let them think. "You found something."

"She knows." Freya turned the book toward them, finger under the name. "Voss. Her grandmother tried the Unbinding and failed, and the Crown bound the whole family to witch-finding to keep it from happening again. Ansel — she's not hunting us because she believes we're guilty. She's hunting us because if she stops, she becomes the thing her whole life was built to prevent."

Ansel was silent a long moment. Outside, the river muttered against the pilings. Somewhere below, Marta laughed at something, rough and alive.

"The dead told me something similar," Ansel said quietly. "Weeks ago. I didn't understand it. One of them kept saying, *the hunter is also caught.*" They looked up, and their eyes held that faraway sheen Freya had learned to read — the look of someone translating between worlds. "I thought they meant the false king. Catching his own court. But they meant her."

"So what do we do?" Freya asked. "She'll have doubled the search by morning. She has Marta's description, the laundress disguise, half the city—"

"No." Ansel shook their head slowly. "No, I don't think she will. Not yet." They tapped the page, the name, the failed Unbinding. "Someone who finds out their whole inheritance is a wound doesn't tighten the net, Freya. First they stand very still. First they ask themselves whether everything they've sworn to protect was worth what it cost." A ghost of a smile, tired and strange. "I know how that goes."

Freya closed the book. Held it against her chest like a shield, or a heart.

"And if we're wrong? If she tightens the net anyway?"

"Then we run again." Ansel's shoulder pressed warmer against hers. "But Freya — the Feast of the Founding is in nine days. After that, there is no running anywhere. Whatever we're going to do about the thing on the throne, we decide soon. And I think—" they hesitated, choosing words with a carver's care, "I think the fastest way to decide is to find out what Serah Voss decides. Because right now, she's the only person in this city who's read that book *and* served the Crown *and* has reason to doubt both."

Downstairs, a knock at the street door — three quick, one slow, Odile's signal. Freya was on her feet before the echo faded, heart hammering, and Ansel beside her, and together they went down to hear what the night had brought.

Odile stood in the doorway, snow melting in her dark hair, and her face wore an expression Freya had never seen on it: not charm, not calculation, but something raw underneath both.

"It's Voss," she said. "She's sent word. Alone, no escort, no warrant." Odile stepped aside, and behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, stood a tall woman in a plain grey cloak with her captain's insignia torn away, snow in her close-cropped hair, and eyes like a woman who had just set fire to the only map she'd ever trusted.

"I need to talk to the bone-carver," said Serah Voss. "About my grandmother."
