## 15. Assembly of the Broken

Odile had hosted funerals in her warehouse before — the quiet kind, where you drank to a name and burned the debts of the dead — but never a war council. She surveyed the room from the top of the loading stairs with a tankard in one hand and a ledger of favors in the other, and decided that if this went wrong, at least she'd die among interesting company.

The warehouse smelled of tallow, river damp, and the pepper oil they rubbed into crates to fool the customs hounds. Below, on the floor where silk bales usually slept, her people had dragged every chair in the building into a rough crescent around a table made of two doors laid across barrels. On those doors lay a map of Carrowmere's palace district, stolen three years ago by a boy who was now a man who was currently bleeding in the corner from a knife wound Odile hadn't asked about.

She counted heads the way she counted coin: twice, and expecting disappointment both times.

Ansel Oyelaran sat nearest the map, bandaged shoulder stiff, face still carrying the gray bloom of fever that had nearly taken them a week past. Beside them, Freya — the archivist, the torn book, the reason half the witch-finders in the province were awake at odd hours — sat with her elbows on her knees like a woman waiting for a verdict she already knew.

Marta was there, which still made Odile's chest do something complicated every time she looked at her. Three weeks in a garrison cell, and the old woman came out thinner, grayer, missing two fingernails, and complaining about the tea as though that were the true atrocity. She'd refused the physician. She'd refused rest. She'd said, *I'm not dying until I've seen it done*, and Odile knew better than to argue with Marta about anything except card games.

And then there was the clerk.

Tobias Wren sat apart from everyone, as clerks did, spine straight even on a crate, his witch-finder gray coat folded neatly beside him like a skin he intended to shed properly rather than tear off. He was twenty-three, maybe, with ink-stained fingers and the look of a man who had spent his whole life being the most honest person in a dishonest room and had finally, catastrophically, decided to stop apologizing for it.

He'd walked into Odile's front room four days ago with Voss's own seal lifted from her desk and a list of garrison rotations written out in his beautiful clerk's hand, and he'd said, "I want to help. I don't know how. Teach me."

Odile had fed him first. That was policy. People who defected on an empty stomach defected back again by morning.

"Right," she said, coming down the stairs, boots loud on the boards so everyone would shut up without her having to ask. "You all know why you're here. Half of you shouldn't be. All of you are anyway. That's the family business."

* * *

They gathered at the doors-table, and Odile let Ansel speak first, because it was their funeral, technically.

"The Feast of the Founding," Ansel said. Their voice had changed since the wound — rougher, slower, as though they were listening to something between each word. Maybe they were. Odile had seen them flinch twice tonight at nothing, head tilting toward empty air. "Nine days from now. The palace opens its gates wider than any other night of the year — processions, foreign envoys, ten thousand people in the square. And the thing wearing Aldric's crown has to surface."

"It renews its hold," Freya said. She'd spread pages beside the map — the ones she'd torn from the prophecy book, soft as cloth now from handling. "The rite is old. Older than the archive's copies. It has to take the king's consent fresh, or what's left of him spoils, and then it needs a new host, and finding one costs it years it doesn't have. So on the night of the feast, it comes up. Fully. Into his eyes, his voice, his shadow." Her finger traced the palace plan. "That's when it can be unbound. Not before. Not after."

"And the unbinding needs—" Odile waved her tankard at the pair of them. "You. Both of you. The bone-carver who owes the crown nothing and loves its victim—"

"I don't love the king," Ansel said.

"You love what's under the crown. His grandmother won't shut up about it."

Ansel's mouth twitched. Even now, even fevered, they could almost smile at the dead queen's nagging. "I carry her voice. In the throne room, I anchor what's left of Aldric while Freya performs the rite. That's the shape of it."

"That's the shape of a noose," Marta said flatly. She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the ruin of her hands. "Two of you inside. The rest of us outside hoping. I've been in their cells, girl and boy. I know what they do to caught smugglers, and that's when all they want is names. What will they do to the two who tried to cut a god out of their king?"

Silence, of the honest kind. Odile let it sit. Hope without teeth was just wishing, and wishing got her people killed.

It was Tobias Wren who broke it, in his careful clerk's voice, like a man reading minutes he couldn't believe he was taking.

"There's a detail in the rotation schedules," he said. "For the feast night. The inner guard doubles — but the archive escort doesn't. Archive clerks move through the palace that night because the Founding records must be displayed in the great hall. Two clerks, always two, with sealed cases, escorted only as far as the hall door." He looked up, and his ears had gone red. "I wrote last year's roster. I could write this year's."

Freya stared at him. "You're proposing we walk in as archive clerks."

"I'm proposing you walk in as *me*. As people like me. Nobody looks at a clerk. That's the entire point of clerks." A ghost of bitterness crossed his young face. "I was invisible for six years. It's a skill."

Odile laughed — one short bark, surprising herself. "Oh, I like him. Marta, we keep him."

"We're keeping everyone," Marta said. "That's the problem."

* * *

The plan grew the way Odile's best plans always did: messily, argumentatively, each person throwing up a wall and someone else finding the door in it.

Pell, her youngest runner — sixteen, quick, still bearing the bruise Voss's men had left on his jaw from the trade that wasn't — knew the storm drains under the processional square better than the city engineers did. He drew them freehand on the back of a debt notice: a route from the river grate to a maintenance stair that came up inside the palace's lower court, dry nine months of the year and flooded on the tenth.

"And the feast falls in the wet month?" Odile asked.

"Falls right in it," Pell said cheerfully. "But there's a sluice gate. Old. Rusted. Two hours of work with the right tools and a strong back."

"You'll have my strong back," said Brannic, who was not strong so much as enormous, and who had once carried a piano across three rooftops to win a bet. He said little else. He never needed to.

Marta took the outside. That was non-negotiable, she informed the room, in the voice she used on dockhands and duchesses alike. If the children didn't come out of the palace by the third bell after midnight, she would raise such a fire in the tannery district that every watchman in Carrowmere would come running, and the garrison would be too busy drowning flames to drown fugitives.

"They'll hang you for arson," Tobias said, horrified.

"They'll *try*," Marta said. "I've been hanged twice already in rumor. It's good practice."

Odile watched the clerk absorb this — watched him realize, slowly, that these people joked the way soldiers did, over graves, and that he had joined something with a body count and a shared table and no way out except forward. He didn't run. To his credit, he didn't run. He just pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward himself and began drafting forged archive credentials in a hand so perfect it made Freya make a small involuntary sound of professional grief.

"That's better than the real seals," she whispered.

"I should hope so," Tobias said. "I practiced on yours."

* * *

Later, when the map was rolled and the runners sent to bed and the candles burned to thumbs, Odile found Ansel alone at the high window, looking out over the black roofs toward the palace hill, where the lights of the false court burned all night like a wound that wouldn't close.

She brought two cups of the good brandy, because some conversations required it, and handed one over.

"Do you hear it from here?" she asked. "The screaming."

Ansel wrapped both hands around the cup without drinking. Fever or not, their eyes were clear, and tired in the deep way Odile recognized from mirrors. "Every night since Carrowmere. Fainter here than in the low streets. But it knows I'm coming. I think... I think it's started talking *to* me instead of about me."

"What does it say?"

"Whatever I most want to hear." Ansel smiled without any joy in it. "Tonight it offered me my mother's voice. I haven't heard my mother since I was seven."

Odile drank. Outside, a patrol lantern swung along the far quay, and she tracked it out of habit — left, left, gone behind the salt warehouses, no stops. "You know what I was, before all this? Before the fences and the orphans and the warehouse?"

"A thief."

"A clerk," Odile said, and enjoyed the flicker of surprise. "Customs house, eleven years. I watched the Crown tax fishermen into starvation and call it law, and I kept perfect books of it, because I was *good*, Ansel, I was so damned good. Then one day I altered a single number. One. To keep a widow's boat from seizure. And I thought — the world didn't end. The sun rose. Nobody even noticed." She turned her cup slowly. "So I noticed everything else. Every number that was a person. And I walked out with the ledger under my coat, and I've been correcting entries ever since."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because tomorrow you go into that palace as a clerk, and you'll be tempted to believe the lie underneath the disguise — that the papers matter, that the law matters, that the thing on the throne has any right to be obeyed because it sits where kings sat." She tapped Ansel's sternum with one finger. "The only law in that building worth serving is the one screaming inside the crown. Remember whose side the numbers are on."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "The dead say something similar. Less brandy involved."

"The dead never bring the good bottle. That's why I outrank them."

Something cracked in Ansel's face — not quite a laugh, closer to relief — and Odile counted it a victory and left them at the window with the brandy and the distant burning court.

* * *

She found Freya in the office, asleep sitting up over the prophecy pages, and woke her with a hand on the shoulder and a cup of water, because archivists ran on worse fuel than smugglers.

Freya surfaced guilty, the way she always did — as though resting while others worked were theft. "I was cross-referencing the rite against the archive liturgies Tobias copied. There's a discrepancy in the third invocation. If I'm wrong about the order of the words—"

"If you're wrong, we'll know quickly," Odile said, "and loudly."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." Odile sat on the edge of the desk, which was hers to sit on. "Question for you, archivist. You've read more prophecy than anyone alive. Does it say we win?"

Freya was quiet. Lamplight moved on the water-stained pages. "Prophecies don't say win. They say *when*. The winning part is always blank space." She looked up, and there was iron under the exhaustion, the kind Odile had learned to trust more than confidence. "But I chose this. Nobody wrote me into the book until I'd already torn it open. Whatever happens on the feast night, it happens because we decided, not because it was fated. I find that—" she searched, "—cleaner."

"Cleaner," Odile repeated. "You sound like my accountant."

"He learned from me."

Odile grinned, drained her cup, and stood. At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and Odile Vane believed in saying hard things plainly; it saved time at the trial.

"Tomorrow night, when you two walk through that gate, my family walks into the fire with you. Pell in the drains. Brannic at the sluice. Marta with her matches. A boy who betrayed his uniform and a woman who betrayed her archive and a bone-carver who owes the dead an impossible debt." She shrugged. "Nobody here was drafted. But you should walk in knowing what you're carrying. It isn't just your lives in those forged credentials. It's ours too. Spend them well."

Freya stood, and to Odile's surprise, took her hand — the archivist's ink-stained fingers closing over the fence's scarred ones, brief and fierce.

"I know," Freya said. "That's why we're going to win. Not because the book says so. Because you're all coming with us."

* * *

Odile climbed to her loft above the warehouse floor and lay down without undressing, as she had every night for thirty years, one ear cocked for the door.

Below her, the family slept in drifts: Brannic's snore like a tide, Pell talking in his sleep about sluice gates, Marta's silence that was somehow louder than all of it, the clerk breathing carefully in unfamiliar blankets, and near the window, the two of them — the bone-carver and the archivist — close enough to touch, awake, whispering about invocation orders and grandmother's voices and the shape of a cage built from a crown.

On the table below, the forged credentials lay finished under a paperweight. Two archive clerks. Names invented, seals perfected, faces that no one ever looked at.

Nine days.

Odile closed her eyes and corrected one last entry in the ledger of the world: *Feast of the Founding — expected losses, unknown. Expected gains — a king, a silence, a family intact.*

She fell asleep before she could decide whether the arithmetic balanced, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of the customs house — except in the dream, every number in every column had gotten up and walked out the door, following her home.
