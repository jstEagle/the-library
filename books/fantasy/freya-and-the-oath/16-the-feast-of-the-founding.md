## 16. The Feast of the Founding

The papers Odile forged were beautiful. That was the first thing Freya noticed — the way the wax seal caught the lamplight, the way the ink of the Crown's archive cipher sat slightly raised on the page, exactly as it did on documents pulled fresh from the presses below the Hall of Records. She had spent eleven years in that hall. She had filed a thousand seals like this one. Holding the forgery in her hand, she felt something between grief and admiration.

"Where did you learn to do this?" she asked.

Odile didn't look up from her workbench, where she was pressing a second seal into cooling wax with the practiced boredom of a woman buttering bread. "I was an archivist's daughter in Carrowmere before I was anything else. The Crown burned our district for unpaid levies when I was nine. I kept the only surviving ledger." She blew across the wax. "Everything I do is bookkeeping, love. Some people just call it crime."

Freya tucked the papers into the inner pocket of her clerk's robe — grey wool, deliberately shabby at the cuffs, the uniform of the low-ranked record-keepers who moved through the palace like furniture. Ansel stood by the warehouse door in their own robe, hands folded, bone-pale and still. They had been quiet all day. Too quiet. Freya had learned to read Ansel's silences the way sailors read weather, and this one had a storm's weight behind it.

"The Feast of the Founding," Ansel said now, to no one in particular. "Three hundred years ago Aldric's ancestor broke ground on the first wall of Carrowmere. Every year they eat until dawn to remember it."

"And every year," Odile said, "the whole court is too drunk and too stuffed to count its clerks. You'll walk in with the evening records delivery, you'll be ignored magnificently, and you'll have until the toast at midnight before anyone wonders where you've gone." She finally looked up, and her charm dropped away like a mask set down on a table. Underneath it her face was older, and tired, and serious. "I want you both to understand what you're carrying in with you. If Voss has doubled the garrison—"

"She hasn't," Freya said. "We'd know."

"We would," Ansel agreed softly, and Freya saw Odile suppress a shudder. Even after weeks among them, the smugglers still flinched sometimes at what Ansel could hear.

Odile recovered, and the charm came back up, but slower. "Then go. And come back. That's not a request. I don't lose people twice in one season." Her eyes flicked to Marta, who sat by the fire with a blanket around her shoulders and a cup of broth she wasn't drinking, freed three days ago from a cell that had left new lines around her mouth. Marta raised the cup an inch. *Go on, then,* the gesture said. *I bled for this. Make it worth it.*

* * *

The palace of Carrowmere at night was a lit thing, a lantern of white stone glowing against the dark river, and Freya hated how beautiful it was. She had loved it once. She had been nineteen the first time she walked these steps as a junior archivist, awed by the banners and the marble kings, believing — genuinely, stupidly believing — that an institution could be good if enough honest people served inside it.

Now she climbed those same steps beside Ansel, both of them bent under the humble weight of record-satchels, past guards who looked through them as though they were smoke.

"Names?" the gate sergeant asked, bored, holding out his hand.

"Corven and Ilse," Freya said, the names rolling off her tongue without a hitch. "Evening ledgers from the Hall of Records. Harvest tallies and grain requisitions."

He barely glanced at Odile's perfect forgery. "Feast night. Leave the satchels at the records alcove and stay out of the great hall unless summoned."

"Of course, sir."

They passed under the portcullis, and Freya felt the change immediately — a pressure, faint but unmistakable, like walking into deep water. The air inside the palace was wrong. Not stale; palaces smelled of tallow and roast meat and lilies meant to cover both. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has died in it and no one has opened a window.

Beside her, Ansel made a small sound, low in the throat.

"What is it?"

"So many," Ansel whispered. Their face had gone grey in the torchlight. "Freya — there are so many voices here. Faded ones. Like — like embers. Like voices heard through a wall, through years." Their hand found hers under the sleeve of the robe, gripping hard. "They're being *drunk*."

* * *

The records alcove was a narrow stone room off the eastern gallery, stacked with ledgers and smelling of dust. From it, a service corridor ran the length of the east wing toward the great hall — the route clerks took when summoned to read proclamations aloud at feasts, which happened rarely, which meant the corridor was empty, which meant they could move.

They reached the gallery above the great hall just as the second course was carried in.

Freya had seen the Feast of the Founding once, from the clerks' benches at the hall's far end, when she was young enough to be dazzled. Now she looked down from the shadowed gallery and understood that she had never actually *seen* it at all. She had seen the surface: the gold plate, the boar glazed with honey, the fiddlers in the gallery opposite, the hundred candles in their iron wheel above the high table.

She saw beneath the surface now because she knew what to look for. Because Ansel's hand was shaking in hers.

The courtiers nearest the high table were wrong.

It was subtle — that was the horror of it. A woman in sea-green silk laughing at the king's jest, and her laugh going on two beats too long, hollow as a struck bell. A lord raising his cup, and the candlelight passing through his face as though his skin were oiled paper. They orbited him. That was the word Freya's mind supplied and refused to take back: *orbited*, slowly, in their seats, turning by degrees toward the dais the way flowers turn toward sun — except nothing about it was warm. It was the turning of moths. It was the leaning-in of things that feed.

And at the center, King Aldric — the thing wearing Aldric — lifted his head and smiled at his court.

Up close, rumor failed entirely. Rumor said the king was strange, reclusive, given to long silences. Rumor did not say that his smile reached his eyes and stopped there, as though his face were a painted mask over a well. Rumor did not say that his shadow, thrown huge by the candle-wheel behind him, moved a half-second *after* he did — lagging, catching up, like a reflection in water that has been disturbed.

Freya's breath went shallow. Beside her, Ansel had gone rigid.

"I can hear him," Ansel breathed. "Underneath. There's a voice underneath the crown, Freya, it's — gods, it's so *small* now. It's been screaming for three years and it's worn down to almost nothing, like a man shouting at the bottom of a well, and the thing drinks the sound—" Their knees buckled. Freya caught them, dragged them back from the gallery rail into the dark of the alcove, held their weight against her shoulder.

"Ansel. Look at me. Breathe."

"They're all fading." Ansel's voice cracked apart. "The courtiers. He's been feeding on them for years, slow, a sip at a time, and they don't even know, they think they're *happy*, they think the warmth they feel near him is favor — it's not favor, it's being *eaten* — and the dead in the walls, the ones he's finished with, they're still here, they can't leave, they're stuck in the stone like—"

"Ansel." She took their face in both hands. Their eyes were unfocused, seeing through the wall, through the floor, through everything. "You are not in Thornfell. You are not in the cave. You are here, with me, and we have a job, and the job is to *watch*, nothing more tonight. Can you watch? Just watch. Let me hold the rest."

For a long moment Ansel trembled against her hands. Then, slowly, their breathing evened, and their eyes came back from wherever they had gone.

"Watch," they repeated. "Yes. Watch." A ghost of their dry humor surfaced. "I'm better at carving than watching."

"You're terrible at both when you're frightened. Come on."

* * *

They watched in shifts after that — one at the rail, one keeping anchor on the other's shoulder — and what they watched turned Freya's stomach from dread into something colder and harder.

The entity did not merely sit at the feast. It *worked*. When a nervous young baron was presented to present the year's accounts, the false king leaned close and spoke to him at length, kindly, warmly — and Freya watched the color leave the young man's face like wine draining from a tipped glass. By the time the baron bowed and staggered back to his seat, he was smiling, blissful, emptied. Two of the king's silent guards — and Freya noted that their shadows matched them perfectly, which somehow was worse — eased him out a side door. No one else seemed to notice he was gone. Or no one else was capable of noticing.

"He's culling," Freya whispered. "The strong ones. The ones with the most life in them. He's taking the strongest first."

"To renew himself," Ansel murmured. "The queen told us. On the Founding he must feed deepest — it's how he holds the shape. Three hundred years of Foundings." Their jaw tightened. "Freya. The rite. Tell me again why midnight. Why the toast."

"Because the archive rite requires the true name spoken in the presence of the binding, and the binding surfaces fully at the renewal — grandmother Queen Isolde wrote it that way, she *designed* it that way, so that any Unbinding would happen when the thing was most exposed and least able to hide." Freya swallowed. "Also because it will be at its strongest. She was honest about that part."

"Wonderful woman," Ansel said faintly. "Terrible planner."

Below, the fiddlers swung into the processional air, and the great doors at the far end opened for the ceremony of the First Loaf — three centuries of ritual, priests in grey, a loaf borne on a cushion of cloth-of-gold, commemorating the breaking of ground and the breaking of bread. The whole court rose. The candle-wheel blazed. And the false king stood, and his shadow stood a half-second late, and he began to walk the length of the hall to receive the loaf, and every eye in Carrowmere's court followed him with that glazed, moth-stupid devotion—

Ansel made a sound.

Not words. Something smaller and worse than words — a catch, a hitch, the sound of a person whose body has understood danger before their mind has. Freya turned. In the torchlight of the gallery Ansel's face had gone white as the bones they carved, and their lips were moving, shaping names, dozens of names, hundreds, the way they shaped them when the dead pressed close.

"Ansel—"

"They're all calling for help." The whisper came out shredded. "All of them, everyone he's ever taken, they're all in the walls and under the floor and they can feel me listening and they're all calling at once, they think I've come to free them, they're *begging*—" Ansel's hand shot out and gripped the gallery rail hard enough that the knuckles went bloodless. "I can't — Freya, I can't hold all of them, I can't—"

"Don't hold them." Freya got her arms around them, pulled them back from the rail, anchored them against her chest the way she had in the cave, in the safehouse, in every dark hour since the road. "Don't hold them. Hold *me*. One voice. Mine. That's all you have to carry tonight."

Their heartbeat slammed against her collarbone, wild, slowing by degrees. Below, the ceremony rolled on, the loaf presented, the blessing chanted, the false king's hands closing around the gold-embroidered cloth. Nobody looked up. Nobody ever looked up at clerks' galleries.

Ansel's breathing steadied. Their grip on Freya's arms loosened from crushing to merely desperate. "One voice," they managed. "Yours. All right. All right."

And that was when the music stopped.

Not faded. Not concluded. *Stopped* — mid-phrase, every fiddle cut off at once, the way a room goes silent when a door opens. The chanting priests faltered. A hundred conversations died. The entire court stood in a silence so complete that Freya could hear the candles burning.

At the foot of the dais, mid-stride, the false king had paused.

Slowly — terribly slowly, with none of the lagging hesitation of his shadow, which had already finished turning — the thing wearing King Aldric's crown lifted its head. Past the priests. Past the courtiers frozen in their moth-orbits. Up, along the vaulted ceiling, along the banner-hung walls, to the shadowed clerks' gallery in the east wing.

To them.

Its eyes found them across sixty feet of torchlit air, and they were not glazed. They were not hungry in the way of the courtiers' hunger. They were *lucid*, and ancient, and amused, and Freya felt its attention land on her like a physical weight, like a hand laid flat against her sternum, pressing.

It smiled. Aldric's handsome face arranged itself into a smile of perfect, courteous recognition — the smile of a host greeting guests who have arrived early to a dinner prepared for them.

Then it inclined its head, very slightly, the smallest bow etiquette allowed, and turned back to receive the First Loaf, and the music started again as though nothing had happened, and the court exhaled as one creature and resumed eating.

Freya realized she had stopped breathing. She realized her legs would not hold much longer. She realized Ansel was saying her name.

"It knows," Ansel whispered. Their voice was calm now, which frightened her more than the shaking had. Calm the way the dead were calm. "Freya. It's known since the bridge. Maybe since the book. Tonight wasn't a risk we took." Their eyes, when they turned to her, held no fear at all — only a terrible, steady clarity. "Tonight was an invitation."

Below them, the false king raised the First Loaf high in both hands, and the candle-wheel flared, and his shadow on the wall behind him rose with him — and did not raise a loaf at all. His shadow's hands were open, reaching upward, toward the gallery.

Toward them.
