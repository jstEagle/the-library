## 19. The Unbinding

**Ansel**

The throne room doors opened inward, and the sound that came through them was not music.

It was breathing. A vast, wet, patient breathing, as if the hall itself were a lung and the Feast of the Founding merely the space between one breath and the next. Ansel stood in the doorway in a clerk's grey robe, Freya's shoulder pressed to theirs, and felt the dead of Thornfell rise up inside their skull like water finding its level.

*There,* said the old queen's voice, and for once it was not a scream. It was almost gentle. *There he is. My boy.*

King Aldric sat on the throne of his grandfathers beneath the great iron crown, and he was smiling.

That was the worst of it. Ansel had imagined a monster — something twisted, something that would make the horror legible at a distance. But the man on the throne was handsome in a worn, tired way, forty years old or so, with laugh lines around eyes that did not laugh. He raised a goblet. He toasted the Founding. The courtiers roared their approval, and every single one of them had the same glazed shine to their faces, the same faint blue at the lips, like people standing too long in cold water.

And above him, where his shadow should have stretched long across the marble in the candlelight, the shadow moved wrong. It lagged. It reached. When Aldric lifted his right hand, the shadow's left hand rose a half-second later, fingers spread wide, grasping at nothing.

"Ansel." Freya's voice was very quiet. "The rite needs the center of the room. Once I start, I can't stop."

"I know."

"And when I start, it will know."

Ansel looked at her. In the feast-light she looked younger than she had on the mountain road, and older too — all the fear burned off her now, leaving only the thing underneath, which was not bravery exactly but something more stubborn than bravery. She had chosen this. No prophecy had made her; no oath had bound her. She had read the truth in a dusty archive and walked out of her whole life carrying pages torn from a book, and she had never once looked back.

"I'll be listening," Ansel said. "For him. For all of them."

She squeezed their hand once, hard, and then they moved.

They crossed the throne room the way Odile's crew had drilled them: separately, unhurried, two clerks among forty, carrying an archive chest between them as if it weighed nothing. It did not weigh nothing. Inside it lay the bones Ansel had spent three sleepless nights carving — the queen's voice caught in whale-ivory and river-stone, every groove cut to the pitch of a scream Ansel had heard since childhood. And beneath the bones, wrapped in oilcloth, the rite itself: Freya's own hand copying out the Unbinding from the torn pages, ink still sharp enough to smell.

The entity turned its head before they were halfway there.

Not Aldric. *It.* The shadow peeled up off the floor like smoke poured backward, and though the king's body kept smiling and toasting and laughing at some courtier's joke, the shadow's face swung toward Ansel across thirty feet of marble, and it *looked* at them, and Ansel's knees nearly went.

*Bone-carver,* it said, and the voice did not come through the ears. It came through the teeth, through the marrow, through every hollow place in the body. *I know what you carry. I have eaten better listeners than you.*

"I don't doubt it," Ansel said aloud, and set down the chest, and knelt, and began unwrapping the bones.

Freya stepped forward into the center of the room and unrolled the rite.

* * *

**Freya**

The words of the Unbinding were older than the archive, older than the Crown, older than the language they'd been written down in. Freya had spent six years in the Royal Archive learning dead tongues, and she had thought that knowledge was the dullest thing in the world until the night she found what the living tongues were being used to hide.

Now she stood in the throne room of Carrowmere with the court staring at her — some confused, some amused, a handful with the slow animal dread of people watching something they had seen in dreams — and she spoke the first line of the rite in the voice of the First Archive, and the candles all bent sideways at once, as if a door had opened somewhere.

*"By the record and by the witness,"* she said. *"By the name written and the name erased—"*

The entity screamed.

Not the king. The shadow. It went up like a black banner snapping in wind, and the temperature of the room dropped so fast that wine froze in cups, and the courtiers — the drained ones, the ones with blue lips — simply folded to the floor like laundry, released. Others fled. Doors slammed. Somewhere a guard captain was shouting orders that no one obeyed, because there was no order left to give.

Aldric stayed on the throne. His smile was gone. His hands gripped the armrests, and his mouth was open, and what came out of it was a thin, continuous sound that Freya recognized with a lurch because she had made it herself once, alone, in the archive stacks, the night her mentor died: the sound of a person trying to come back to the surface after a long drowning.

*"—by the oath broken and the oath remade,"* Freya said, louder, over the screaming, *"I call the thief from the house it stole—"*

The shadow struck at her.

She saw it coming — a wall of dark, of cold, of a hunger so old it had forgotten what it wanted and remembered only wanting — and she did not flinch, because behind her, kneeling at the archive chest, Ansel lifted the first carved bone to their lips and blew across it, and the bone sang.

* * *

**Ansel**

The note that came out of the bone was the old queen's voice, and the old queen's voice was furious.

Ansel had carried her scream for twenty years without knowing whose it was. Now they knew: Queen Isolde Vane-Aldric, grandmother of kings, murdered in this same room three generations ago by the thing that had tried then, as it tried now, to wear the crown. Her voice filled the throne room like sunlight fills a well, and the shadow recoiled from it, writhing, because there is one thing a thief cannot abide and that is the true owner coming home.

*I am here,* the queen said through the bone, through Ansel, through every carved groove. *Aldric. Little one. I am here. Listen to my voice and come toward it.*

The king's head turned. His eyes — his own eyes, finally, glassy and terrified and *present* — found the source of the sound.

"Grandmother?" he whispered.

"Come toward my voice," Ansel said, and their own voice cracked down the middle, because they knew what it was to be called back from somewhere cold. "Don't look at it. Don't fight it. Just walk. We'll hold the door."

Aldric stood. It cost him everything — Ansel could see that, could see the way the shadow clung to him like tar, threads of darkness tearing and re-knitting between his spine and the crown — but he stood, and he took one step down from the dais, and then another, following the bone-song the way a child follows a lamp through the dark.

The entity understood, then, what was happening. Ansel felt its attention swing away from Freya — who was deep in the rite now, third stanza, sweat freezing on her brow — and settle entirely on them.

*I can give you silence,* it said. Sweet as honey down the marrow. *No more voices. No more dead murmuring in your skull at midnight. No more names, carver. You could carve wood again instead of grief. Walk away. Take your archivist. I will forget you both.*

And Ansel, kneeling on the marble of the throne room with a dead queen singing through their hands, thought: *it has been talking to Voss.* Because this was the offer, word for word, that Serah Voss's grandmother must have taken, and her grandmother before her, the whole failed line of Unbindings bought off one by one with the same coin.

"No," Ansel said.

*Everyone sells,* the entity said. *I have bought kings.*

"Not this one." Ansel lifted the second bone. "We're not for sale. We're *oath-sworn* — and unlike you, we keep ours."

They blew across the second bone, and every dead soul Ansel had ever carried came through it at once.

* * *

**Freya**

The sound was indescribable. Later, trying to write it down — because Freya wrote everything down, that was her nature and her penance — she would fill eleven pages and cross out ten. It was a village. It was a massacre. It was four hundred voices saying their own names at the same time, not in terror but in *witness*, and the shadow that wore a king's crown shrank from the sound of names the way rot shrinks from salt.

*"—by the record and by the witness,"* Freya cried, fifth stanza, final movement, her voice raw, *"I unbind thee from the house thou hast stolen—"*

The entity tore free.

There was no other word. It ripped out of Aldric like a splinter drawn from a wound three years infected, and the king collapsed, and the shadow rose — all of it, the whole vast hungry length of it, taller than the throne, taller than the windows, a shape like a crown worn by nothing — and it screamed with a hundred stolen voices, and it lunged not for Freya, not for Ansel, but for the doorway.

For escape. For another host. For the world.

And Serah Voss stepped into its path.

* * *

**Ansel**

Ansel saw her come through the far doors — armor stripped to shirt and sword-belt, hair loose, moving like someone who had stopped calculating and started choosing. Behind her, limping, came Marta with an iron bar and half of Odile's crew, holding the corridor against the witch-finders Voss had ordered to stand down with three words Ansel would spend the rest of their life wondering about: *"This one's mine."*

Voss drew her blade — the iron blade, the one that had opened Ansel's side in the undercity — and planted herself between the entity and the open doors.

"My family served it," she said, and her voice did not shake, which Ansel thought was the bravest sound in the room, braver even than the dead. "Four generations. My great-grandmother took its bargain and called it duty. My mother took its bargain and called it law. I took its bargain and called it order." She rolled her shoulders once, like a woman settling a yoke. "Someone in this bloodline is going to pay the bill."

*Move,* the entity said. *I will give you what I gave them. Silence. Rest. Peace.*

"That's the trouble with you," Voss said. "You think peace is a thing you can give." And she smiled — a real smile, tired and crooked, the first Ansel had ever seen on her — and drove the iron blade into the marble at her feet, and spoke a word that had been written in the margin of the prophecy book in a hand four generations dead: the sealing word. The Voss word. The failure, inherited so long it had become a key.

The floor opened.

Not dramatically — that was the horror of it. The marble simply became stone again, honest stone, mountain stone, and split along a seam older than the palace, and the entity fell toward the dark beneath Thornfell like water down a drain, screaming with every voice it had ever stolen, and Voss went with it, blade first, arms spread, a last daughter of a failed line doing the one thing her line had never done.

She looked at Ansel as she fell.

"Tell them," she said — and Ansel would never know whether she meant the dead or the living or both — "the law was supposed to protect people. Start there."

Then the seam closed, and the mountain swallowed its hunger, and the throne room of Carrowmere was silent except for one man weeping on the steps of his own throne, and one archivist lowering a scroll with shaking hands, and one bone-carver kneeling in the wreckage of everything, hearing — for the first time since childhood — nothing at all.

* * *

**Freya**

Freya reached Ansel before she reached the king. She would feel no guilt about that later, and decided not to pretend otherwise.

They were on their knees where the entity's attention had held them, and they were pressing both hands flat against the floor, and their face was the face of someone listening to a room where no one is speaking. Freya had seen that expression once before, on the mountain, the night Ansel had told her about the oath — the look of a person checking, always checking, for voices.

"Ansel." She knelt, took their face in her hands. Their skin was ice. "Look at me. Is it done? Can you hear—"

"Nothing." Ansel's voice came out strange, scraped hollow. "Freya, I hear nothing. Not the queen. Not the village. Not—" Their breath hitched. "Not anyone. It's quiet. It's *quiet*—"

And then Ansel Oyelaran, who had not wept in front of another living soul since the age of nine, put their forehead against Freya's shoulder and broke.

She held them. Around them, the throne room stirred back to life — guards finding their courage, courtiers waking on the marble, Marta's gravel voice organizing the chaos with the brisk contempt of a woman who had smuggled worse things than a rescued king. Aldric knelt on the dais steps, gaunt and gray and unmistakably, pitifully himself, whispering his grandmother's name over and over like a prayer he was only just learning.

But Freya held Ansel, and Ansel held her back hard enough to bruise, and outside the shattered windows the dawn was coming up over Carrowmere, and the birds — the birds that had refused the palace for three years — came down out of the gray sky in their hundreds and landed on the roof like rain.

Later — much later, when the king had been wrapped in blankets and the dead of the feast carried out and Odile's crew had vanished back into the city's veins with the professional discretion of people who preferred legends to thanks — Freya found Ansel sitting alone on the dais steps, turning the queen's bone over in their fingers.

"It doesn't sing anymore," Ansel said quietly. "None of them do. Whatever the Unbinding took from him—" they nodded at Aldric, sleeping at last under Marta's watch, "—it took the road back, too. The dead aren't waiting for me anywhere. They're just... gone. Wherever the dead go."

Freya sat down beside them. She didn't say *I'm sorry*, because she wasn't sure she was, and Ansel would have heard the lie — or rather, would once have heard it, and the absence of that certainty seemed to strike them both at the same moment.

"The queen's voice," Freya said instead. "Before it went. Did she say anything? At the end?"

Ansel was quiet a long time. When they answered, their thumb was moving slowly over the carved grooves, reading them by touch, the way they'd read every bone they'd ever made.

"She said: *You kept our names. That was always the whole of it. Now keep each other.*"

Below them, the great doors of the throne room stood open to the morning, and through them came the sound of a city waking up to discover it had a real king again — and, somewhere in the crowd already gathering, the sound of Odile's youngest runner shouting that the warehouse had started brewing celebration cider at dawn and there wouldn't be any left if the heroes dawdled.

Ansel laughed — wet, cracked, real — and got to their feet, and pulled Freya up with them.

"Well," they said. "We wouldn't want to disappoint the family."

Hand in hand, they walked out of the palace of Carrowmere together, toward the mountain, toward home, toward whatever an oath remade was going to ask of them next.
