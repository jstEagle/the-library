## 20. A King, Quietly Restored

He woke on the third morning after the Unbinding, and the first thing he asked for was not water, not a priest, not even his own name.

He asked who had died.

Freya was sitting in the chair beside the bed when it happened — she had barely left it in three days, sleeping in snatches with her boots on, one hand always near Ansel's bandaged shoulder where they dozed in the other chair. The king's chamber had been stripped of everything that could be used as a weapon or a symbol: no crown on the stand by the mirror, no scepter, no banners. Odile's people had seen to that before anyone thought to object. What remained was a bed, a window, and a man.

Aldric's eyes opened — gray, unfocused, human — and fixed on the ceiling as though he were reading something written there in a language he'd forgotten. Then he turned his head, slowly, the way a man turns when every joint remembers pain, and looked at Freya.

"Who died," he said again. Not *what happened*. Not *where am I*. Not even *who are you*.

Freya set down the cup of willow-bark tea she'd been holding for hours without drinking. "Your Majesty—"

"Don't." His voice cracked on the word like old ice. "Don't call me that until you've answered me. I remember the feast. I remember—" He stopped. Swallowed. Freya watched him try to find the memory and watched him fail to hold it, his face going through grief the way a hand goes through smoke. "I remember being very far away and very loud. I remember screaming for years and no one hearing me. And I remember the thing wearing my hands doing things, and I don't know which things."

Ansel stirred awake beside her. They didn't speak — three days of silence had settled into them like snow into a valley, and Freya had learned that Ansel did not fill quiet anymore; they simply inhabited it. But they sat up, wincing at the pull of the wound along their ribs, and met the king's eyes with the level look of someone who had carried dead voices their whole life and knew exactly what weight this man was asking about.

"Voss," Ansel said. "Captain Serah Voss. She sealed the entity beneath Thornfell. It took her."

The king closed his eyes. "Her line served mine for six generations," he said quietly. "Failed Unbindings, the archive called them. She must have known what she was walking toward better than any of us." A long breath. "Who else?"

"Marta of the waystation is alive — wounded, but alive. Odile's youngest runner, Pip, is safe. The courtiers who were drained at the feast — most will recover. Some won't." Freya kept her voice steady because someone in the room had to. "And there are names we're still counting. Prisoners. People your crown fed to it to keep it quiet."

At that, Aldric opened his eyes, and Freya saw the difference between the man and the hollow thing that had worn him — saw it in the way his face broke. The entity had never broken. It had only ever *hungered*, smooth and patient as deep water. This man shattered like dropped glass, and then, visibly, began gathering the pieces.

"Tell me all of it," he said. "Not the version the chancellors will write. All of it."

So they told him. It took most of the day.

* * *

By evening he could sit up, propped against pillows, wrapped in a plain wool robe that Odile had procured from somewhere — Freya suspected deliberately, so that the first sight the palace got of its returning king would be a man in wool, not gold. Word had spread through Carrowmere the way fire spreads through dry grass. Crowds had gathered in the square below the palace windows, not cheering but *waiting*, the way people wait outside a sickroom. Freya had heard them all day: a low murmur, occasionally a hymn, once — chillingly — a chant of the king's name that rose and fell like breathing.

Chancellors had come and been turned away. Generals had come and been turned away. A delegation from the Temple of the Founding had arrived with incense and been turned away so firmly that one of Odile's runners reported the high priest had gone pale. Aldric wanted no one at his bedside but two smugglers, a disgraced archivist, and the bone-carver who had unbound him.

"You should know," he said, when the last of the story had been told and the candles burned low, "that I remember some of it. Fragments. Like watching through frosted glass." His hands lay open on the blanket, palms up, as if showing they held nothing. "I remember signing decrees and feeling my mouth move around words I hadn't chosen. I remember the prisoners being brought in — I remember *knowing*, somewhere underneath, and being unable to scream loud enough to stop it. Three years." His voice frayed. "Three years inside my own skull, listening to myself rule."

Ansel shifted. When they spoke, their voice was rougher than usual — the wound, the fever, the silence where a hundred voices used to live. "We heard you. At the end. From miles away."

The king looked at them for a long moment. "Then you are the only ones who ever did," he said, "and I will not insult you by pretending a title can repay that."

"It wouldn't," Ansel said simply.

"No," Aldric agreed. "It wouldn't. But watch me try anyway."

* * *

He tried, as it turned out, the following morning, in the lesser council chamber with the windows open to the spring air because Aldric had refused to be shut in since waking — "I have spent three years in a small dark place," he'd said, "I will conduct the business of a kingdom in sunlight if I please" — and because the guards had learned quickly that the restored king's mild requests were not mild at all.

He came down to the chamber dressed plainly, walked past the throne at the head of the room without looking at it, and took a chair at the table like any other man. Freya noticed that detail and understood it was a message, and noticed that the chancellors noticed too, and that several of them went the color of porridge.

The audience was formal, but the king made it strange from the first minute.

"Before anything else," he said, standing — refusing the chair offered him — "the record." He nodded to Freya. "You were royal archivist. You know how records are kept, and how they are *unkept*. I want a full accounting written into the permanent rolls: every prisoner delivered to the palace between the third year of my reign and now. Every name. Every village they came from. Every official who signed the transfer orders." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Including the signatures of men standing in this room."

Silence, of the kind that has weight and temperature. One chancellor — Lord Cassel, Freya remembered from her archive days, a man whose signature appeared on eleven separate prisoner transfers — began, "Your Majesty, surely in the circumstances, the stability of the realm requires that we look forward rather than—"

"I was fed," Aldric said, "like a hearth is fed. For three years. By the government I am apparently expected to resume running this afternoon." His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "There will be no looking forward until we have looked directly at what we did. That is not a request, Lord Cassel. It is the first decree of my restoration, and I am writing it myself, in my own hand, so that no clerk can lose it in a drawer the way inconvenient truths are lost in drawers."

Freya caught Ansel's eye across the chamber. Something passed between them — not quite surprise. Recognition. Here was a man who had learned, in the worst classroom imaginable, exactly what power does when no one watches it.

Then the king turned to them, and the formality dropped away like a cloak.

"Now," he said. "You two. And before you refuse — because I can see you preparing to refuse, both of you, you have the faces of people rehearsing refusals — understand what I am offering and why."

He drew a folded paper from his robe. Wax seal, ribbon, the works. "Titles. Land. The Order of the Thorn, which hasn't been conferred in eighty years and comes with a pension that would embarrass a bishop. Full pardons — for the smuggling, for the archive theft, for the assault on witch-finders, for everything Voss spent three chapters of her report charging you with before she—" He stopped. Cleared his throat. "For everything. And places at court. Real ones. Advisor to the Crown, both of you. There is no one alive who understands what happened to me better than you do, and no one I would rather have standing between this kingdom and whatever comes next."

He set the paper on the table between them and stepped back.

Freya looked at it. She looked at Ansel. And she felt — clearly, calmly, the way she had felt standing in the archive the night she decided to steal the pages — that she already knew the answer, and that knowing it cost her something anyway.

Because it was a good offer. That was the trap of it. A year ago — half a year ago — she would have crawled across broken glass for reinstatement, for pardon, for her name cleared in the rolls. And part of her, the tired part, the part that had slept in ditches and bled in waystations, whispered *take it, take the warm rooms and the safety and let someone else carry the next burden.*

But she had seen the inside of this court. She had seen what it fed and what it buried and how smoothly the machinery ran while decent clerks filed the paperwork. Power didn't corrupt in this place so much as it *digested*, slowly, comfortably, with excellent wine.

Ansel reached out — not for the paper, but for the king's hand, and shook it once, formally, the way one seals a different kind of bargain.

"Keep the titles," they said. "Keep the land. We'll take the pardons — Marta shouldn't hang for harboring us, and neither should Odile's crew, and if your gratitude needs somewhere to go, it should go there first."

"Done," Aldric said immediately. "All of it. Pardons for every soul who helped you — I'll sign them today."

"And the rest," Ansel went on, "we decline. Not because we don't trust you, Majesty. Because courts aren't where this work lives." They tapped the table, once. "You'll write your accounting of the prisoners. Good. We intend to read it. Every line. And then we intend to walk to the villages those prisoners came from, and the mass grave under Thornfell, and every place your crown's shadow fell, and we intend to make sure the accounting doesn't stop at ink. Names carved. Families told. Reparations paid from the estates of the men who signed the orders — starting with Lord Cassel's."

Lord Cassel made a sound like a stepped-on boot.

"The oversight will be ours," Freya said, stepping forward, "and it will be public. Quarterly reports, posted in every market square in the lowlands, written plainly enough that a farmer can read them. If your chancellors stall, the reports say so. If they lie, the reports say that too. You'll have your reform, Your Majesty — but it will happen in daylight, where we can see it, not behind these walls where things get fed."

The king regarded them both for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — tired, crooked, entirely human.

"You know," he said, "for three years I watched that thing rule in my name, and I used to think: if I ever get free, I will surround myself with people who cannot be bought. And here you are, refusing the largest bribe in the kingdom's history before I've finished offering it." He slid the paper back into his robe. "Very well. No titles. But I keep the right to call on you. When the accounting turns ugly — and it will turn ugly, Cassel has cousins everywhere — I want the bone-carver who hears the truth and the archivist who steals it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Freya said.

"Agreed," said Ansel. Then, quieter: "Though I should tell you — I don't hear anymore. The Unbinding took it. The dead are silent to me now."

Something moved through the king's face — grief, recognition, guilt, all at once. "Was that the price?"

"That was the price," Ansel said. "And it was fair."

Freya reached over and took their hand. Ansel's fingers were steady. They had been steady for three days, ever since the silence settled in, and Freya had stopped trying to decide whether that steadiness was peace or shock. Probably both. Probably forever both.

* * *

They left Carrowmere four days later, on a gray bright morning with the mountain still wearing snow above the tree line.

The city had changed in ways that were small and enormous at once. Birds sat on the palace roof — Freya stood in the courtyard a full minute just watching them, a whole noisy congregation of starlings and pigeons, and felt her throat tighten over something as ordinary as birds. The crowds in the square had thinned into ordinary market noise. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and no one flinched.

Odile met them at the south gate with a cart, six of her crew, and a grin like a knife being sheathed.

"Well," she said, leaning on the rail. "The pardons came through at dawn. Marta's already halfway home — she sends her love and a demand that you both come fix her roof before summer. Pip wants to know if you're really going to teach them the Thornfell passes." She cocked her head at Ansel. "Are you? The passes only the dead knew?"

Ansel was quiet a moment. Freya watched them look north, toward the mountain, toward the grave and the bridge and the workshop with its silent benches, and she watched them arrive at something — not an answer exactly, more a settling, like sediment finding the bottom of a clear glass.

"There are other roads," Ansel said at last. "Ones I found with my ears, sure. But I walked every one of them with my feet first. I can teach the feet." A pause. "And maybe the dead left maps. Dead are thorough like that."

Odile laughed and swung up onto the cart bench. "Good enough. Get in, heroes. The road's long and Marta's roof won't fix itself."

Freya climbed up beside her. Ansel took the other side, and the cart rolled out through the gate, past the last of the city walls, onto the road that climbed green and slow toward Thornfell.

Halfway to the tree line, Freya realized the king's steward had pressed a final packet into her hands at the gate — she'd forgotten it in the bustle. She opened it now, tilting it against the wind.

Inside: the signed pardons, as promised. Beneath them, a single sheet in the king's own hand, no seal, private.

*I have begun the accounting,* it read. *The first true list of names goes to the engravers this week. But I write to tell you what the archivists found yesterday, cataloguing the entity's effects beneath Thornfell, and which I thought you should know before anyone else: among the bones it hoarded, there is a box. Old iron, sealed with wax older than my grandmother. Inside, a book — water-stained, familiar, I'm told. The prophecy. Not the copy. The original. And on its final page, in a hand none of us recognize, a new entry has appeared.*

*It is dated for midsummer.*

Freya read it twice. Then she folded it away, and looked up, and found Ansel already watching her — reading her face the way they had once read voices, easy and unerring, no gift required.

"What is it?" they asked.

Freya looked north, where the mountain waited under its melting snow, and handed them the letter.

"Midsummer," she said. "The book isn't finished with us."
