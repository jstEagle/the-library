## 22. Freya and the Oath

Spring came to Thornfell the way forgiveness comes — slowly, then all at once.

One week the passes were still choked with grey ice, the pines bent under a winter that seemed determined to outlast them all. The next, meltwater roared down every gully, crocuses split the frozen mud outside the waystation door, and the mountain smelled of wet stone and green things waking. Freya stood on the rebuilt porch with a mug of Marta's terrible nettle tea and watched the thaw happen in real time, as if the whole range had been holding its breath for a year and had finally decided to exhale.

The waystation itself was new timber over old bones. They had raised it on the same foundations — Ansel had insisted, and no one argued with Ansel about foundations anymore — but the walls were straighter now, the roof steeper, the windows larger than any smuggler's station had a right to. Odile had called it *ostentatious*. Marta had called it *home* before the last beam went up. The dead, of course, said nothing at all, and never would again, and some mornings Freya still caught Ansel tilting their head toward an empty corner, listening to a silence only they could feel.

"You're staring at the road again," Marta said from behind her, hands busy with a basket of early greens. "Road's not going anywhere."

"The road's exactly where I left it," Freya said. "I'm watching for the others."

"Odile's crew can find this place blindfolded in a snowstorm. They'll come when they come." Marta set the basket down and squinted up at her. "You've got ink on your face."

Freya wiped at her cheek and came away with blue fingers. She'd been copying all winter — the prophecy book, restored page by page from memory and from the fragments she'd torn free of the archive, now annotated in her own hand. Not a weapon anymore. A record. Someday, she thought, it might sit in a library where anyone could read it, where knowledge belonged to everyone instead of being fed to a hunger in a crown. But that library was in Carrowmere, and Carrowmere was still sorting out what it meant to have a king who wept openly at the funerals of prisoners he hadn't known he'd condemned.

Aldric had insisted on coming to the funeral himself. Incognito, he'd said, though a king incognito is a contradiction that fools no one; half the smugglers in the valley had recognized him within an hour and pretended magnificently otherwise. He had stood at the mass grave with his bare head bowed while Ansel read the names aloud — all of them, every name carved into bone over years of listening, spoken now into open air because there was no one left to whisper them. When it was done, Aldric had knelt in the mud like any mourner, and no one had helped him up, and he hadn't asked them to.

That had been three weeks ago. Today he was due back, traveling light with two guards who were very bad at looking like merchants.

"There." Freya pointed. Two figures on the lower switchback, one broad-shouldered and moving with a limp that was mostly gone now, the other tall and unmistakable even at distance, gesturing at the mountainside as if lecturing it.

"That's not a merchant's walk," Marta observed.

"No," Freya said, smiling. "It isn't."

* * *

Ansel felt the spring the way they felt everything now — plainly, without translation.

It was strange, learning the world at their own age. For twenty years the dead had narrated everything: which bridge plank was rotten (a drowned carter knew), which pass held wolves (a shepherd girl knew), what the weather would do by evening (half the graveyard knew). Now Ansel read the mountain the ordinary way, with eyes and feet and the accumulated cunning of a life spent walking it, and found — to their quiet astonishment — that they had always known how. The gift had been a second language laid over the first, and beneath it, all along, they had been fluent.

They were teaching Freya the roads. Not the smuggler roads exactly — those changed with the seasons and the patrols and Odile's shifting arrangements — but the grammar underneath them. How to read a scree slope for the line a body could hold. How water told you where stone was hollow. Why you never camped in a fold that faced the wind, and why you always, always greeted a shrine even if you didn't believe in what lived there.

"This one," Ansel said, crouching at the mouth of a side pass above the waystation, "is the lie. See how it looks easier than the main trail? Wide, gentle grade, goes straight through." They traced the route with one finger. "It floods in April. Three travelers drowned in it the year I was born. Nobody marked it, because marking it would mean admitting the Crown's maps were wrong, and the Crown doesn't do that."

"So how do people know?"

"They don't. That's why I'm telling you." Ansel stood, knees cracking, and looked at Freya — flushed from the climb, hair escaping its braid, a smudge of charcoal still at her jaw that she kept missing. "You're going to be the one who knows, after me. Someone has to carry it forward or the mountain eats strangers forever."

"I thought you were immortal," Freya said. "Marta said so. Very firmly."

"Marta also says her tea is medicinal. We all tell comforting lies." Ansel started back down the switchback, and Freya fell in beside them, close enough that their shoulders brushed on the narrow path — a thing that had stopped being accidental sometime in the winter and had never once been mentioned since, because neither of them wanted to name it and risk making it smaller.

Halfway down, Ansel stopped.

"What?" Freya said, hand going to her knife out of habit.

"Nothing. I just—" Ansel laughed, short and wondering. "I used to walk these paths with a hundred voices in my ears. Every step, someone commenting. Dead carter complaining about the bridge. Dead queen telling me I walked too fast. And now it's just—" they gestured at the world, the roar of meltwater, the wind in the pines, the birdsong that had returned with the thaw, "—this. Just the living. And I keep expecting to miss it more."

"Do you?"

Ansel considered it honestly, the way they considered everything. "Some days I grieve it. Some days I wake up and reach for them like a limb that's gone. But most days?" They looked at Freya. "Most days it's the quietest my head has ever been, and I finally understand what everyone else was doing all those years. Just... living in one world at a time."

"And the oath?" Freya asked carefully. It was the first time either of them had said the word outright in weeks. "Does it feel broken? Still?"

Ansel turned the question over. Below them, smoke rose from the waystation chimney; somewhere on the road, Odile's crew would be arriving with wine and stolen cheese and opinions. The oath — *carry our names, never serve the Crown* — had been sworn by a terrified child kneeling in blood at a mass grave, and it had kept them alive, and it had nearly cost them everything, and in a dungeon cell they had set it down like a stone carried too long.

"It feels finished," Ansel said at last. "Like a book I read all the way through. I don't regret it. I wouldn't be me without it. But I'm done rereading." They glanced sideways at Freya, mouth curving. "Time for something new to swear."

"Oh?" Freya said, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "What kind of something?"

But Ansel only shook their head and kept walking, and the rest of the descent passed in the comfortable silence of two people who had learned that not every important thing needed saying immediately. Some things needed a kitchen, and good bread, and witnesses.

* * *

By late afternoon the waystation was full.

Odile arrived first, as she always did, sweeping in with six of her crew and a cart of goods whose provenance she declined to discuss. The youngest runner — Pip, twelve now, taller, still bearing the faded scar where Voss's men had grabbed him a lifetime ago in Carrowmere — vaulted off the cart and into Marta's arms before the wheels stopped turning. Behind Odile came the clerk, Tam, the witch-finder defector who had spent the winter compiling lists: names of the fed prisoners, names of the guards who'd processed them, names that would take years to answer for. He worked constantly, quietly, like a man paying down a debt he could never finish owing, and perhaps that was exactly what he was.

Marta came out of the kitchen wiping flour from her hands and pointed a wooden spoon at the assembly. "Wash first. All of you. This is a home, not a barn."

"Yes, Marta," said six smugglers, a fence, a former archivist, a bone-carver, and a king in disguise.

Aldric had come up the last stretch on foot, having sent his "merchants'" cart around the long way, and if anyone noticed that the plain-clothes traveler moved like a soldier and spoke like a court, they had the good grace not to mention it. He sat at the far end of the table, content to be nobody, passing bread, laughing at Pip's impressions of Carrowmere officials. Only once did the mask slip — when Marta set down the stew and he looked at it a moment too long, and Freya saw him remember, briefly and visibly, all the tables in the palace where food had been served to a thing wearing his face. Then he breathed, and came back, and asked Marta for the recipe.

Later, when the dishes were cleared and Pip had fallen asleep against Odile's shoulder, Aldric rose and stood by the fire.

"I won't make speeches," he said. "You've suffered enough of those from my chair." A ripple of careful laughter. "I came to say what I said at the grave, and to say it here, where you live, so it holds. What was done in my name will be answered in my name. The trials begin at midsummer. I want your testimony — all of yours. And I want you to know that when it's done, the door to my court stands open to every one of you. Titles. Land. Whatever reparation I can give."

He looked at Ansel and Freya last, and there was something old and tired and grateful in his face.

"And you two most of all. Name anything."

Ansel and Freya exchanged a glance — the whole table watched them do it, and grinned.

"Justice for the fed," Ansel said. "Tam's lists, seen through. Every name."

"And the massacre answered," Freya added. "A marker on the grave that says what the Crown did. Not hidden. In official record, in official stone."

"Done," Aldric said simply. "And for yourselves?"

"We'll oversee it," Ansel said. "From out here. You'll find us hard to miss — we're keeping the roads."

Something eased in the king's shoulders. Perhaps he had feared they'd ask for nothing at all; perhaps he understood that this — the work, the witness, the refusal of thrones — was its own kind of answer. He inclined his head, sat back down, and let Marta pour him another cup of nettle tea, which he drank with the expression of a man accepting a penance and finding it, unexpectedly, bearable.

* * *

Night settled soft over the mountain. Most of the company had rolled into blankets and bedrolls; Odile's crew claimed the loft, snoring in a heap like puppies. Freya and Ansel ended up on the porch with a blanket and the last of the wine, watching stars come out over the pass.

The door stood open behind them. It always stood open now — Marta's rule, though she claimed it was for the cat. Anyone could walk in. Anyone was welcome. That was the whole architecture of the place, more than timber and stone: a building with an unlocked door, on a road that belonged to no crown.

"Teaching you the roads," Ansel said eventually, "isn't really why I took you up the mountain today."

"No?"

"No." Ansel turned the cup in their hands. "I've been thinking about the prophecy. About what it actually said. *An Unbinding will come, who owes the crown nothing and loves its bearer.* Everyone read that as destiny. Chosen ones. Fate written in water-stained paper." They shook their head. "But I was there, Freya. I heard the dead scream. I broke an oath. You tore pages out of an empire. None of it was fate. Every single part of it was a choice somebody made when choosing was hard."

Freya was quiet a moment. "My mentor used to say prophecy is just history that hasn't apologized yet."

"Your mentor sounds like she'd have liked Odile."

"She'd have hated Odile. They'd have been inseparable." Freya laughed softly, then sobered. "So what are you saying? That the book was wrong?"

"That the book was a mirror. It didn't make us anything. It just showed us what we'd already decided to be, if we were brave enough to look." Ansel set down the cup and turned to face her fully. In the starlight their scarred hands were steady. "Which brings me to the something new to swear."

"Here it comes," Freya murmured, heart going strange.

"No altar. No grave. No god listening and no dead taking notes — just you and me and a mountain that's heard worse." Ansel took both her hands. Their palms were rough as bark and warm as bread. "I swore an oath once to protect the powerless, and it became a chain because I gave it away — gave my judgment, my future, my self, to voices that needed it more than I did. So this time I'm swearing differently. Freya. I choose you. Not because a book said so. Because you stole the truth out of a burning archive and ran with it until your lungs bled. Because you covered for Marta and mourned her like family before she was even lost. Because you looked at the worst thing in the world and chose to fight it with paperwork and stubbornness and love. I choose you on the good days and the fever days and whatever days come after. That's the whole oath. That's all of it."

Freya discovered she was crying, which was undignified, and found she didn't care.

"Then hear mine," she said, voice unsteady and clear. "I spent my whole life believing knowledge was something you earned permission to touch. Locked archives, sealed records, truth rationed out by rank. And then I met a bone-carver who gave names to the nameless for free, and I understood what knowledge is actually *for*." She squeezed their hands. "So: I choose you, Ansel Oyelaran. Not the guide, not the Unbinding, not the prophesied stranger's counterpart — you. The one who walks too fast and greets empty shrines and cries at other people's funerals. I choose you in the silence and I'd have chosen you in the noise. No crown required. No book required. Just this."

"Just this," Ansel agreed, and pulled her in.

Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient, and the mountain breathed its slow spring breath, and behind them the door of the waystation stood open on lamplight and sleeping friends — an open door, a lit room, a road that went everywhere.

Somewhere below, faint on the night air, came the sound of the meltwater river, running free down off the mountain toward the lowlands, carrying the winter away.

Neither of them made a wish on it. They didn't need to. Everything the prophecies had promised and everything they hadn't dared to hope for was already here, warm against them in the dark, breathing.

And in the morning there would be roads to teach, and lists to answer, and a king's promise to hold to account, and bread, and work, and each other.

The oath, remade, held.
