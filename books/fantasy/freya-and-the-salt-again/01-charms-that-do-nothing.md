## 1. Charms That Do Nothing

The dead in the walls of Lena Nakamura's shop had opinions about everything, and this morning they were discussing her tea.

*Too strong,* murmured old Sefa, who had died of a bad heart forty years before Lena was born and had complained about beverages ever since. *She steeps it like she's punishing it.*

*Lena punishes everything,* said another voice, thinner, older, one Lena did not let herself name. *It's how she shows affection.*

Lena poured the tea down the sink and started again with less leaf, and told herself — as she told herself every morning — that she had not heard that. That the murmur under the floorboards was the tide. That Verrin was built on salt marsh and old bones and the whole city groaned and settled at night like an old man in a chair, and none of it meant anything, and certainly not words.

Twenty years of practice, and she still almost believed herself some mornings.

The bell above her door rang at half past eight, which meant it was Tam Broadstairs, who came every Tuesday to buy a charm against drowning for his fishing boat, though he'd bought eleven already and his boat was barely seaworthy enough to drown in. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with hands like hams and the anxious eyes of a man who had once seen something in deep water and never told anyone what.

"Morning, Mistress Nakamura." He ducked under the lintel — the shop had been built for smaller people, or for people who stooped out of habit, which in Verrin amounted to the same thing. "You've got the, ah. The boat charms?"

"I always have the boat charms, Tam."

"Aye, but last month's ones." He shifted from foot to foot. "My wife says they're not working."

Lena kept her face pleasant and empty, the way you keep a room you don't want anyone to look into. "Not working how?"

"They're not—" He lowered his voice as if the walls might be listening, which, unfortunately, they were. *Ask him about the eels,* whispered Sefa gleefully. *He knows about the eels.* "They're not doing anything. A charm ought to do something, shouldn't it? You ought to feel it working. Like a poultice. My mother's charms used to hum."

His mother's charms had hummed because his mother had been carved by Maren Solace, and Maren's charms were the only ones in three generations of Verrin carvers that had ever done anything at all, and Maren Solace had been hanged from the Salt Gate for it twenty years ago while Lena stood in the crowd and memorized every face.

"Charms aren't supposed to hum," Lena said mildly. She took the little bone fish from its drawer — she'd carved it yesterday from a gull's wing-bone, smooth and pale, with a single hole bored through the tail for the cord. It was beautiful, if she said so herself, and it did absolutely nothing, and that was the entire point. "A charm is a reminder. Like hanging a horseshoe. It doesn't pull luck; it reminds you luck might exist. Very calming."

Tam turned the fish over in his enormous fingers. "It's very small."

"It's very portable."

"My wife wants one that hums."

*Tell him the wife can come carve her own,* said Sefa. *No — tell him about the eels—*

"Your wife," Lena said, "wants to hang."

The word dropped into the shop like a stone into a well. Tam went pale above his beard. Outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles, and someone laughed, ordinary and alive, and the sound made everything worse.

"I didn't mean—" Tam began.

"You didn't mean anything," Lena said, gentler now, taking the fish back and threading it on a cord with quick, practiced movements. "Nobody means anything in Verrin. That's why we're all still here. Twelve coppers, and I'll throw in a knot-charm for your nets."

He paid without counting, which was either trust or terror, and in Verrin the two looked identical. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said, quietly, not looking at her: "You carve like her, you know. The Solace woman. Same way with the tails. Same little notch on the left side."

The dead went silent. All of them, all at once, the whole murmuring city holding its breath, and the silence was worse than any accusation.

"That's a common style," Lena said. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later. "Salt-carving. Everyone notches the tail. It keeps the cord from slipping."

"Mm," said Tam. And then, terribly, kindly: "My mother bought from her, once. Before. Said her charms were the only honest ones in the city." He ducked out under the lintel, and the bell rang, and he was gone into the grey morning, and Lena stood in her shop with her heart going like a trapped bird and the dead slowly, cautiously, resuming their chatter around her like a household after a slammed door.

*Well,* said Sefa. *That went well.*

"Shut up," Lena said to the empty room, and then flinched, because she'd said it aloud, and the neighbor's boy was passing the window, and he gave her a look.

*

The trouble with hearing the dead — and there were many troubles, but this was the foundational one — was that they never stopped talking.

Verrin was two thousand years old and built on its own graveyards; the salt marsh had swallowed generations of the drowned, the plague-dead, the executed, and all of them had soaked into the foundations like brine into timber. Every wall in the old town held somebody. The cellar under Lena's shop held nine, by her count, including a smuggler named Brackish Pete who had been murdered over rum in 1147 and considered his death a fascinating topic of ongoing conversation. The church next door held hundreds, layered like sediment. The harbor pilings held so many drowned sailors that walking along the quay at night was, for Lena, like wading through a crowded market.

And through it all ran the First Tongue — the old language, the soul-language, the one the dead spoke when they forgot themselves and drifted back toward the way all souls speak before they learn to wear a body. Lena had been born understanding it, the way some children are born knowing how to whistle. Nobody had taught her. Nobody could have. When she was four years old she had asked her grandmother why the lady in the pantry was crying, and her grandmother had gone white as bleached bone, and after that Lena learned the second lesson of her life: that some things you hear, and some things you admit to hearing, and the distance between those two things is where people like Maren Solace end up swinging from gates.

So she carved. Bone, mostly — gull-wing, sheep-horn, the occasional legally acquired whale tooth — because bone was traditional, bone was harmless, bone was what every charm-seller in Verrin had worked since the Purge stripped the craft down to superstition and knickknacks. Her charms did nothing. She tested each one personally. She held them up to the walls and listened, and the dead confirmed it: inert, inert, inert. *Lovely workmanship,* Sefa would say grudgingly. *Doesn't do a thing. You've really outdone yourself.*

It was, Lena reflected, sweeping the floor at midday, a strange life. She was arguably the most skilled carver in a city of carvers, practicing a craft whose entire purpose was to accomplish nothing, while possessing a gift that would get her killed, spending her days listening to a chorus of the dead complain about tea and eels and the price of candles, and her nights lying awake memorizing the sound of a voice she refused to name, coming up through the floorboards, patient as tide.

*You could answer me,* the voice said sometimes. Not often. It was careful, that voice. Careful even dead. *Just once. Just to know you're well.*

Lena swept harder.

There was a knock at the door — the real kind, living hands, three raps — and she called "Closed!" and the door opened anyway, because in Verrin "closed" meant "open, but I'm annoyed about it," and in walked Widow Marsh, who wanted a charm against nightmares, and behind her a young clerk from the magistrate's office wanting a love token, and behind him a dockhand with a cough he was convinced was spiritual. The afternoon assembled itself. Lena sold them all beautiful, useless things. The widow wept a little over a carved moth and paid too much. The clerk blushed. The dockhand got a bone bead to suck on, which would do nothing for the cough but occupied the mouth, and an occupied mouth couldn't tell the magistrate what ailed it.

*You're good at this,* said the thin voice from the floorboards, wry and warm, and Lena's broom stilled.

*I'm good at hiding,* she thought back, furiously, silently, the way she'd been thinking back for twenty years without ever once admitting it. *There's a difference.*

*Is there?* said Maren Solace, dead these twenty years, hanged these twenty years, present these twenty years, and there was laughter in it, and underneath the laughter something older and sadder that Lena refused, on principle, to examine. *I taught you carving, girl. I know the difference better than anyone. I just never managed to teach you that hiding well is still hiding.*

"I'm busy," Lena said aloud, to no one, and the widow Marsh looked up startled, and Lena smiled and said, "For the moth, madam — a blessing over it, folk custom, nothing more," and pressed her thumb briefly to the carved wings, and felt nothing pass between her hand and the bone, because nothing lived in the bone, nothing ever lived in the bone, and that was the arrangement, that was the bargain, that was the whole shape of her careful, cowardly, survivable life.

*

It was near dusk when the second customer came, and Lena would think about him afterward — would take him apart and put him together a hundred times, looking for the moment she should have known — and never find it, because there wasn't one. He was simply a man, wet from the rain starting outside, ordinary as bread, buying a charm against bad roads for a journey inland.

"Inland?" Lena said, wrapping the little carved wheel in cloth. "There's nothing inland but mountain."

"That's where I'm going." He smiled. He had grey eyes and a forgettable face, the kind of face you'd struggle to describe to a magistrate, and something about the smile snagged on her attention like a fishhook in wool. "To the court. Under the mountain."

"There's no court under the mountain," Lena said. "That's a story for children. There's nothing under the mountain but rock."

"Mm," he said. And then, turning the wrapped charm over in his hands, looking down at it with an expression of mild scholarly interest: "This is fine work. The tail-notch on the left side. The way the grain follows the curve." He glanced up, and his grey eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "You carve like Maren Solace."

The rain ticked against the window. In the walls, the dead had gone quiet again — all of them, everywhere, the whole buried city listening.

"Common style," Lena said. "Salt-carving."

"Of course," he agreed easily. "Everyone notches the tail." He set his coins on the counter, exact to the copper, and picked up his charm, and paused at the door with the bell silent above him, and said, without turning around:

"She had a student, you know. Back before. A girl. The records say the girl was never found after the trial." He tilted his head, considering the rain. "Records are interesting things. They say so little, and cost so much to correct."

When the door closed behind him, Lena stood a long time in the fading light, her hands flat on the counter, breathing the way Maren had taught her — slow, low, invisible — while the dead murmured around her like a sea deciding whether to be calm.

Then, from under the floorboards, faint and clear, in the First Tongue that no living throat in Verrin dared speak:

*Girl. Tonight. Come to the harbor wall. There's something on your doorstep you need to see — and it isn't dead.*

Outside, the storm rolled in off the salt marsh, and somewhere in the white noise of wind and rain, beneath the sound of the rising curfew bell, Lena heard — small, high, unmistakable — a child crying.
