## 2. The Child at the Door

The storm came in off the salt flats an hour before curfew, dragging its grey skirts across Verrin like a widow who had outlived everyone worth mourning. Lena watched it from behind her shutters, counting the gap between lightning and thunder the way her teacher had taught her—*one, two, three, four*—and thinking that a storm this slow was a storm with patience. Storms with patience were the ones that got inside you.

She closed the shop early. There would be no customers tonight; even the frightened stayed home when the sky itself seemed to be enforcing the curfew. She banked the fire, set her carving knives in their oiled leather roll, and climbed the narrow stair to the room above, where she kept the things she could not sell: Maren's whetstone, worn to a crescent; three letters she had never dared burn, because burning them felt like answering them; and a small bone fish, half-finished, that Maren had been working on the morning the wardens came.

Twenty years. Twenty years of hearing the dead murmur in the walls of Verrin and never once letting her face change. She had grown so good at not-hearing that sometimes she fooled herself, right up until nightfall, when the city's dead grew restless and their voices rose like tide-water through the mortar of every building on Saltrow, and there was no fooling anyone then, least of all herself.

Tonight they were louder than usual. Storms did that—she had never learned why. Perhaps the salt in the air carried them better, or perhaps the dead simply hated storms as much as the living did and huddled closer to whatever warmth they could find. She lay on her cot listening to them seep through the floorboards: an old woman reciting the names of grandchildren who no longer visited her grave; a drowned boy from the harbor district repeating the last thing he ever said, which was *wait for me*; and beneath it all, as always, the low grinding hum of the mountain, far to the north, where something vast and old breathed very slowly, or did not breathe at all.

Lena pulled the blanket over her head. This was the arrangement. The dead spoke; she did not answer. In twenty years she had answered exactly once, and the price of that answer still hung from the gallows-beam in the square by the harbor, weathered to the color of driftwood, and every child in Verrin knew whose hands had carved the charms found in Maren Oduya's pockets.

A sound woke her past midnight.

Not thunder. Thunder rolled long and low across the flats, but this was short, sharp, and close—a knock. Three knocks, spaced evenly, struck with the flat of a small hand rather than the knuckles. She knew this because she had heard that particular rhythm before, years ago, in another life: *flat palm, three beats*, the way children knocked when they had been taught that knuckles were for adults and doors were for begging.

No one knocked at midnight in a curfew storm. No one knocked at midnight in Verrin at all unless they were wardens, and wardens did not use the flat of the hand.

Lena sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and listened. The dead had gone quiet—not silent, *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it. Even the drowned boy had stopped his *wait for me*. The whole city's dead seemed to be holding its breath, and that frightened her more than the knocking, because in twenty years the dead had never once shown interest in anything that happened above ground.

The knock came again. Three beats. Flat palm.

She should not open the door. Every survival instinct she had honed over two decades said *do not open the door*. Curfew-breakers were flogged. Harborers of curfew-breakers were hanged. And whoever stood on her step in this storm was either mad, desperate, or sent—and all three categories ended at the same gallows-beam.

But the dead were quiet, and quiet dead meant watching dead, and watching dead meant this mattered.

She took her heaviest knife from the roll—more habit than plan—and went down the stairs with the candle unlit, feeling her way by memory. The storm hammered the shutters. Water had gotten under the door somehow; she could feel it seeping cold around her bare feet.

She lifted the bar.

The girl on her doorstep could not have been more than six. She stood perfectly straight in the rain, which was remarkable in itself, because six-year-olds in storms usually folded themselves small against the wet. This one stood like a soldier awaiting inspection, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat against a thin brown face, wearing a smock that had once been white and was now the grey of old salt. Her eyes were enormous and dark and utterly dry—the rain slid around her face without touching her eyes, and Lena would think about that later, much later, and shiver.

"Child." Lena kept her voice low and hard, the voice she used on customers who lingered too long. "Do you know where you are? Do you know what hour it is? There are wardens on Saltrow tonight—they sweep the row when the storm breaks. If they find you—"

The girl looked up at her. She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

She tried again—Lena could see the effort of it, the small throat working, the lips shaping around some word that would not come—and what emerged instead was a sound like wind through a keyhole, thin and reedy and wrong. Not a cry. A cry has weight to it. This was air shaped into the ghost of a word and failing.

Mute. The girl was mute, or near enough, and standing on a charm-seller's doorstep in a curfew storm as if she had walked out of the sea itself.

"You can't stay here," Lena whispered, and even as she said it her eyes darted up and down Saltrow, empty and silvered with rain, and her hands—traitorous, soft-hearted things—were already reaching out to haul the girl inside.

The girl resisted. It was the strangest thing: she planted her feet on the wet stone and would not cross the threshold, and she raised one small hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, and then, deliberately, she pointed at Lena.

Then at the doorframe.

Then at herself.

And Lena, who had spent twenty years pretending not to understand the language written in the world's margins, understood anyway. *You. This place. Me.* The girl was saying: *I was sent here. To you. To this door.*

"No," Lena said aloud, to the girl, to the storm, to the listening dead. "No. Whatever you've been told, whoever told you—I don't take apprentices. I don't take strays. I sell souvenirs to gulls and fools, that's all I am, that's all I've ever—"

The girl's mouth worked again. That keyhole-wind sound, harder now, more frustrated, her whole small body trembling with the effort of forcing something through a passage that had been closed to it—and then—

The dead spoke first.

It came up through the floorboards, through the threshold stone, through twenty years of silence, in a voice that was not the girl's and yet was entirely hers: high, clear, and articulated with the strange doubled cadence that only the First Tongue carried, each word spoken twice, once forward and once backward, like a wave going out and coming home.

*They sold me twice.*

Lena's knees nearly went. She caught herself on the doorframe, and the knife clattered to the stone floor, and for one long moment she could do nothing but stand there while the words settled into her like cold water settling into a well.

The First Tongue. The language of the dead. The language Maren had bled out on a scaffold rather than teach the wardens, the language Lena had heard every day of her life and spoken exactly never, the language that had gotten her teacher killed and gotten her own name written in no ledger anywhere, because she had learned, above all things, to be invisible.

And a six-year-old child was standing in the rain speaking it—speaking it *through* her muteness, as if the tongue lived somewhere below speech, in the marrow, and only surfaced when the ordinary voice failed.

"They sold me twice," the dead-voice said again, patient, terrible, and this time Lena heard what she had missed the first time: the backward echo of the phrase was not identical to the forward. The First Tongue always carried two meanings, the surface and the undertow, and the undertow of those words said something else entirely, something that made the fine hairs on Lena's arms rise:

*I am owed.*

The girl swayed. Whatever effort it cost her to channel the words had emptied her; her eyes rolled back, and Lena lunged forward and caught her before she hit the stone. The child weighed almost nothing. She was ice-cold except at the throat, where a pulse fluttered fast and shallow as a bird's, and against her sternum, under the sodden smock, Lena felt something hard and small press against her palm.

A charm.

Carved bone, no bigger than a thumbnail, strung on a frayed cord. Lena's fingers knew it before her mind did—the specific grain of river-bone, the double-score line at the base, the way the carver had cut *against* the grain instead of with it, a technique so distinctive it might as well have been a signature.

Maren's technique. Maren's exact, unmistakable, hanging-offense technique.

The storm roared. Somewhere down Saltrow a shutter banged. And in the walls, in the floor, in the very salt of the air, the dead began to murmur again—but differently now, urgently, dozens of voices overlapping, and though Lena could not parse them all, one thread ran through them like wire through cloth:

*She is marked. She is marked. They will come for what is marked.*

Lena dragged the girl inside and barred the door, and then stood in her dark shop with a freezing, unconscious child in her arms, listening to the rain and the voices and her own heart, and understood with perfect clarity that the careful, hidden, twenty-year life she had built was over. You did not harbor a curfew-breaking mute child who spoke the forbidden tongue of the dead and expect to see morning as the same person who had barred the door.

The question—the only question that mattered now—was what had replaced it.

She carried the girl upstairs, stripped off the wet smock, rubbed her limbs until feeling came back into them, and wrapped her in every blanket Lena owned. The child did not wake. She breathed in a strange rhythm, slow and deep, as if she were sleeping somewhere much larger than Lena's cot, and once, in the space between two breaths, her lips moved and the walls whispered a single word in translation: *soon*.

Lena sat by the banked fire and held the little bone charm up to the light. Against the grain. Double-scored. Maren's hand, or the hand of someone Maren had taught—and Maren had taught exactly one person, and that person was looking at it.

Which meant either the dead had reached across twenty years to carve a charm for a living child, or someone else alive knew Maren's work well enough to forge it.

Both possibilities were unbearable. Both possibilities meant the past had not finished with her.

She thought of the girl's dry eyes in the rain. She thought of the doubled cadence, forward and back, wave going out and coming home. She thought of how the dead had gone silent when the knocking started—as if they had been waiting for the child, or escorting her, or afraid of her. Six years old. Mute. Speaking the tongue of the dead through a mouth that could not speak at all.

Children did not learn the First Tongue. That was the whole horror of it, the reason the Purge had been so thorough, the reason Maren was driftwood-colored bone in a pauper's grave: the First Tongue was not learned. It was *kept*. Passed down like an heirloom, like a debt, from the dying to the newly born, and no one alive in Verrin had kept it in twenty years.

No one alive.

So where had this child been keeping it? Who had given it to her—and what had they taken in exchange?

Below, the storm began to slacken, and in the new quiet Lena heard the mountain breathe—one long, slow exhalation from far to the north, so deep it was less a sound than a pressure, a change in the weight of the air. The dead fell silent after it, all of them, every voice in Verrin, as if called to attention. Then, softer, in a murmur that was not one voice but many, they began to speak again, and what they said was not for the girl and not for the storm but for Lena alone, patient as tide:

*The mountain holds what was sold. The mountain holds what was sold. Ask us, carver. We remember what the living paid.*

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened—dark, ancient, dry—and fixed on Lena with an expression no six-year-old should be able to make: recognition, and apology, and underneath both, fear so old it had gone smooth as sea glass.

Her lips moved. No sound came. But the dead, gently, from the walls, translated:

*You knew my mother.*

Lena sat down slowly on the edge of the cot. Outside, dawn was still hours away, and the mountain breathed again, and somewhere between the storm and the silence a door had closed behind her that she did not remember opening.

*They sold me twice,* the child had said. *I am owed.*

And the dead, all of them together, waited for her to ask.
