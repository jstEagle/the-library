## 3. What the Dead Remember

The child slept in the good chair.

Lena had given her the good chair because it was the only one that didn't creak, and because the girl had looked at the stool by the fire as though it might bite her. Now Isla—she'd learned the name from a scrap of embroidery on the child's collar, the only common thing about her—was curled up like a comma, breathing in the small, even rhythm of someone who had finally stopped running.

Lena did not sleep. She sat at her workbench with a half-carved luck-knot under her hands and listened to the walls.

The dead of Verrin were never quiet, but they had manners. They murmured. They gossiped the way water dripped—one word, then another, then a long pause in which you could almost convince yourself you'd imagined it. Old Tammas, who'd died in the flood year, was complaining about the damp as he had complained about the damp for two decades. The woman who had once owned this shop before the Purge renamed everything was humming a lullaby in a key no living throat could reach.

And beneath all of it, threaded through the plaster and the salt-rotted beams, came the child's language. The First Tongue. It rose off Isla's sleeping body like heat off a stove, faint but unmistakable, and every ghost in the building had gone quiet to listen.

That was the thing that frightened Lena most. Not the words themselves. The *silence* around them. The dead of Verrin hadn't gone quiet for anything in twenty years—not the hangings, not the fires, not the night the wardens dragged Maren Voss down Saltmarket Row.

Maren. Lena's thumb found the groove in the workbench where she'd carved her teacher's initials when she was nineteen and stupid and certain that carving a name made it safe. *MV*, worn nearly smooth now by her own restless fingers.

"Tell me what she is," Lena whispered to the room. "You've all heard her. Tell me."

The lullaby stopped. Tammas said something about the damp. And then, close—so close it was as if someone had leaned against her shoulder—a voice that was not a wall-voice at all said, in the accent of a woman who had spent thirty years telling students to hold their chisels properly:

*You're asking the wrong question, little wren.*

Lena closed her eyes. "Hello, Maren."

*You've let a soul-bonded child sleep in your good chair,* Maren said. *In your shop. In this city. Have you entirely lost the survival instincts I beat into you?*

"You never beat anything into me. You lectured. Endlessly." But Lena's voice cracked on the last word, because it had been twenty years since anyone had lectured her, and grief is a strange animal that comes back to life at the sound of an old scold. "She knocked. During curfew. What was I supposed to do, leave her in the storm?"

*A locked door would have been a start.*

"She speaks the First Tongue, Maren."

Silence. Even the dead, it seemed, needed a moment.

*I know,* Maren said at last, and her voice had changed—gone careful, the way it went when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. *I heard her from the moment she crossed the threshold. That's why I'm here instead of resting, which, incidentally, is very difficult to do in this city, the acoustics are appalling.*

"Maren."

*The child isn't haunted, Lena. She's bonded. There's a difference, and the difference will get you both hanged if you don't learn it.*

Lena set down the luck-knot. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was moving like water finding cracks. "Bonded to what?"

*To the court beneath the mountain.*

"I don't know what that means."

*No,* said Maren, and there was something underneath her dryness now, something old and tired and afraid. *But the dead do. Ask us properly. Not 'tell me what she is.' Ask us what we remember. We're witnesses, wren. It's the one job we can't be executed out of.*

* * *

Maren Voss had been dead for twenty years, and in that time she had developed several strong opinions about being dead, none of which she could share with anyone except the occasional cat and, once a decade or so, her former student.

Being dead, in Maren's professional assessment, was mostly administrative. You drifted. You overheard. You accumulated grievances the way a gutter accumulates leaves. The living walked through you without noticing, which was rude, and the other dead talked constantly, which was worse, because the dead of Verrin were, without exception, terrible gossips.

But they remembered. That was the bargain nobody had told them about. Flesh forgot; souls kept records. Maren had spent two decades listening to the city's dead recite their memories to each other like merchants comparing inventories—the flood year, the plague years, the Purge—and somewhere in those recitations, repeated across a hundred cold rooms, was the answer to the question Lena had just asked.

So Maren gathered them. It took three nights, which was fast; the dead moved slowly unless motivated, and nothing motivated like fear.

They met in the cellar of the old dye-house, eleven ghosts standing in a circle around a stain on the floor that only they could see: the place where, during the plague years, a broker had set up his scale.

"This is a waste of time," said Tammas, who had drowned and therefore considered himself an expert on all matters of depth. "Nobody sold souls *here*. This is a story grandmothers tell."

"A grandmother told it to me," said the lullaby-woman, whose name had been Sef and who had died of the grey cough in the second plague winter. "I thought it was a story too. Until my daughter couldn't remember me."

The circle went quiet. Dead quiet, which among the dead meant something specific: the silence of people deciding whether to say the true thing.

Sef said it anyway. "I died in the second winter of the grey cough. My daughter lived. I have watched her for fifteen years, and she walks past my grave, and she feels nothing, because she cannot feel it. They took it out of her. The memory of me. Sold it, my grandmother's story says, to the court beneath the mountain, where souls are kept like coin in a vault." She looked around the circle. "I am not a story. I am collateral. And so is everyone in this city who survived the plague too easily."

"The child," Maren said. "Tell me about the child."

It was Tammas who answered, reluctantly, because Tammas had been a ferryman and ferryman's ghosts knew things that happened on water. "There were barges," he said. "During the third winter. Taking the sick children upriver, toward the mountain. The parents were told it was a sanatorium." His voice, which had been complaining about damp for twenty years, went flat. "It wasn't a sanatorium. I carried one such barge myself, the week before I drowned. I heard the brokers talking. They said the mountain court pays best for souls that are *young and unspoken*—souls that haven't finished settling into their bodies yet. Easier to lift out whole."

"And the bond?" Maren asked. "When they take a soul that way, what holds?"

"The contract holds," said Sef. "A soul sold isn't stolen—it's *owed*. The child carries the debt in her blood. She'll always hear the court calling its property home. And the court—" Sef paused, and Maren, who had been a teacher for thirty years and knew the anatomy of dread when she heard it, felt the cellar grow colder. "The court keeps its treasury burning. There's a fire under the mountain, an old one, older than the city. As long as it burns, the held souls persist. When it goes out—"

"When it goes out," Maren said quietly, "they go out with it."

"All of them," said Sef. "Every soul in the vault. Extinguished like a lamp. And the bonds break backward, into whatever's left of the child."

Maren stood in the circle of the dead and thought about a six-year-old girl asleep in a good chair above them, carrying a debt written in a language older than law, and thought about her student sitting vigil below, stubborn as a barnacle, and thought about the fire.

"How long?" she asked. "How long does the fire have?"

Tammas shrugged, the shrug of a man who had spent eternity not knowing things. "The dead can't count that high. But the mountain's been groaning all winter. Ask any ghost near the north road—they say the ground hums at night, low, like a kettle coming off the boil."

Like a kettle, Maren thought, running out of water.

* * *

Lena woke at dawn with her cheek on the workbench and Maren's voice still echoing in the hollow behind her ears, the way a bell keeps ringing after the rope stops.

*Soul-bonded. Sold twice, the child said. Once by her parents to the brokers, once by the brokers to the court. The debt lives in her blood. And there is a fire under the mountain keeping every stolen soul alight, and it is going out.*

She looked at Isla, still asleep in the good chair, small fists curled under her chin. In the morning light the girl looked ordinary—chapped lips, a healing scrape on one knee, the universal grime of childhood. Nothing about her said *collateral*. Nothing about her said *the last dragon-fire in the world is the only thing between her soul and darkness*.

Except, of course, everything did.

"Isla," Lena said softly.

The girl woke the way hunted things wake—all at once, upright, eyes wide, scanning the room for exits. Then she found Lena's face and something in her unclenched. She pointed at herself. Then at the floor. Then, with terrible clarity, she pressed her small hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, and spoke.

The First Tongue poured out of her, three syllables, urgent and repeating, and Lena didn't need a translation because she'd spent twenty years hearing the dead say versions of the same thing:

*It's pulling. It's pulling. It won't stop pulling.*

"The court," Lena said. "Something under the mountain. It's calling you home."

Isla stared at her. Then, slowly, wonderingly, she nodded—and reached out and touched Lena's wrist, right over the pulse, as if to ask how Lena could possibly know.

Because I hear the dead, Lena thought. Because the language you speak in your sleep is the language that's been keeping me company for twenty years. Because I am apparently the only person in this cursed city who can understand you, which means the universe has a sense of humor, and it's Maren's.

The knock came at the door.

Three raps, measured, official. The kind of knock that came with paperwork. Lena was on her feet before the third rap finished, one hand sweeping Isla toward the back room, the other already reaching for the curtain over the window.

Through the gap she saw the salt-grey coat, the magistrate's sash, the face beneath the hat brim—Corvan Hale, Chief Magistrate of Verrin, the man who had signed the order that put Maren Voss on the scaffold. He stood on her step in the early light with a document in his hand, and he was looking directly at her window, directly at the gap in the curtain, directly at her.

Behind him, faint but persistent, the dead of Saltmarket Row had begun to murmur—and beneath the murmur, rising like smoke, Maren's voice, dry as chalk:

*Well, wren. I'd say hide her. But I suspect the magistrate isn't here for the child at all.* A pause, and then, with the grim satisfaction of a teacher proven right: *He's dreaming, Lena. I've heard him dream. He hears us too.*
