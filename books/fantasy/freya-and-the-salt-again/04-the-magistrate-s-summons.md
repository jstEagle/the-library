## 4. The Magistrate's Summons

Magistrate Corvan Hale had hanged eleven people in his twenty years of service to Verrin, and he could name every one of them.

This was not a boast. He had never once been tempted to make it a boast, though there were men in the garrison who treated the number like a score in a dice game, something to be improved upon. Hale kept the names instead the way other men kept debts — written small on a slip of paper folded into his boot, carried against his skin, consulted at odd hours. *Maren Voss, carver. Tam Ollery, hedge-healer. The sisters Brannic, singers.* Eleven names, and every morning he laced his boot over them and went out to enforce the law of a city that had decided, twenty years ago, that magic was a rope-shaped problem with a rope-shaped solution.

He was fifty-three years old. He had gray at his temples that he pretended not to notice, a wife who had stopped asking him about his dreams around year six of their marriage, and a dog named Bristle who was afraid of thunder and nothing else, which Hale privately considered the correct way to live.

And for the past three months, Corvan Hale had been dreaming of voices.

* * *

The dreams were always the same shape, if never quite the same content. A room he did not recognize, lit the color of candle-wax through a curtain. Voices murmuring behind the walls — not muffled, exactly, but *filtered*, as though the plaster itself were doing the talking, choosing which words to pass through. They were not threatening. That was the strange part. They sounded patient. They sounded like people waiting in a very long queue who had made their peace with the wait.

In last night's dream, one of the voices had said, clearly enough that he woke with it still in his ear: *"Ask her about the child."*

He had sat up in bed in the dark, heart going like a fist on a door, and thought: *I don't know any child.*

Then he thought: *Except I do. Or I've seen one.*

Because three days ago, during the curfew storm — the kind of salt-squall that emptied Verrin's streets faster than any patrol — a witness had come to him. A fishwife named Dree Sallow, who ran a lamp-oil stall two doors down from the bone-carver's shop on Tidewrack Lane, and who had the sort of memory that should have been employed by the census office and was instead employed by gossip.

"A little girl," Dree had said, leaning across his desk with the confidence of a woman delivering a verdict. "Six years old, maybe seven. Barefoot. No coat in that weather, Magistrate, no coat. Walked straight up Salt Row like she owned it, turned down Tidewrack Lane, and knocked on the carver's door. And the Nakamura woman let her in."

"Children knock on doors," Hale said. "It's one of the things they're known for."

"Not in a curfew storm they don't." Dree's eyes narrowed with professional pride. "And here's the thing. Here's the thing, Magistrate. When the girl spoke — and I couldn't hear what she said, mind, the wind was howling like a debt collector — but when she spoke, the Nakamura woman's face changed. Went white as gull-bone. Like she'd heard a ghost."

Hale had thanked her, given her the standard coin for information, and watched her go with the expression of a man filing something in a drawer he intended to open later.

Now, three mornings later, standing at his window overlooking the gray rooftops of Verrin while Bristle snored under his desk, he admitted the drawer was open.

*"Ask her about the child."*

He did not believe in ghosts. This was a matter of some professional necessity. A magistrate in Verrin who believed in ghosts would have to believe in the voices the ghosts sometimes left behind in walls and bones and carved charms, and then he would have to hang half the city, or believe in nothing at all. Hale had chosen, long ago, to believe in paperwork. Paperwork could be read, filed, and burned. It asked nothing of him at three in the morning.

But the dreams did not care what he believed. The dreams kept talking.

He took the slip of paper from his boot, unfolded it, and read the eleven names, as he did most mornings, the way another man might say grace. Then he refolded it, laced his boot, and called for his clerk.

"Pell," he said. "Draft me a summons."

* * *

Pell was nineteen, earnest, and possessed of handwriting so beautiful it was nearly a religious experience, and he drafted the summons in triplicate before asking the obvious question.

"What's the charge, sir? Unlicensed carving?"

"Suspicion of unlicensed carving," Hale said. "Which is to say, no charge at all yet. It's an invitation."

"An invitation," Pell repeated, in the tone of a young man who had seen what happened to people who accepted the magistrate's invitations. "Shall I word it gently?"

"Word it precisely. Gently is how you get people running." Hale pulled on his coat. "And Pell — when you deliver it, take a look at the shop. Count the charms in the window. Note anything unusual."

"Unusual how, sir?"

Hale considered this. "You'll know it when you see it," he said, which was a lie, because he didn't know it himself yet, and then felt the particular shame of a man who has just lied to someone who admires him.

* * *

Tidewrack Lane smelled of brine and tallow and the particular damp that got into everything in Verrin and never fully left, the way grief gets into a house. Hale walked it slowly, hands clasped behind his back, nodding to the shopkeepers who nodded back with the careful blankness of people greeting a man who could end them. He had learned, over twenty years, to read that blankness the way sailors read clouds. Most of it was fear. Some of it was contempt. Occasionally — rarely, and always from the oldest residents — there was something else underneath, something almost like pity, though he could never have said why.

The carver's shop sat where Dree Sallow had said it would sit: narrow-fronted, salt-stained, its sign a wooden hand holding a carved bird. The window display was modest. Whalebone combs. A few amulets on twine. Little tablets of bone etched with patterns — waves, birds, the coiled shapes people liked to think meant protection.

Hale stood before the glass a long moment.

There was nothing illegal in the window. That was the first thing. Every charm visible was inert — decorative, superstitious, harmless as a knitted sock. He knew inert work when he saw it; he had spent two decades learning the difference between a charm that held a voice and a charm that held nothing but the carver's hope. The Purge had taught Verrin's carvers caution, and the good ones had gotten very good indeed at making beautiful nothing.

But.

He leaned closer, breath fogging the glass.

The etching style. The fine double-line border, the way the wave-motifs broke into small deliberate gaps — *breathing spaces*, his memory supplied, from a lifetime ago, from a classroom above a harbor, from a woman with ink-stained fingers who had said, *the pattern must breathe, Corvan, or it's only decoration* —

Maren Voss's style. Executed twenty years ago by his own order, and here it was, alive in a stranger's window, worn smooth by repetition into something almost but not quite its own.

Hale straightened slowly. His heart was doing something he declined to name.

"Well," he said quietly, to nobody. "Hello."

Bristle, tied outside, sneezed at a passing gull.

* * *

Lena Nakamura received him in the shop itself, because the back room was apparently full of curing resin and she claimed the fumes made magistrates worse than usual. She was perhaps forty, sharp-eyed, with the callused hands of her trade and the posture of a woman who had rehearsed this moment so many times it had gone smooth as river stone.

"Magistrate." She set down the comb she was working on. "I received your summons. I confess I'm curious what I've done. My license is current." She produced it before he asked, which told him she'd expected the question, which told him nothing new — everyone expected the question.

"It is," Hale agreed, taking the proffered paper and examining it with more attention than it needed. "Your work is... consistent."

"I try to be."

"Mm." He set the license down and picked up one of the window charms instead, turning it in the light. "Tell me about your training, Mistress Nakamura."

"Apprenticed in Sallowmouth, as a girl. Bonework mostly. Decorative." The word *decorative* arrived with a small, deliberate emphasis, a coin placed precisely on a table.

"And your master? In Sallowmouth."

"A man called Ferren. He's dead now." Her face did absolutely nothing, which was, Hale reflected, its own kind of eloquence.

"Ferren," he repeated. "I don't know the name."

"No," she said pleasantly. "I wouldn't imagine you would."

They regarded each other across the counter, two professionals conducting a transaction neither had named. Hale had interrogated smugglers, poisoners, one memorable arsonist, and a man who had stolen an entire church bell for reasons never adequately explained, and he knew the texture of a lie being maintained. This was not quite that. This was a truth being *withheld*, which was a different craft entirely, and finer.

He set the charm down.

"Mistress Nakamura. Three nights ago, during the storm, a child came to your door."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "Did one? I sleep heavily in storms."

"A fishwife saw her enter. Barefoot. Alone. Six or seven years old." He paused. "She says the child spoke to you, and that your face — her words — 'went white as gull-bone.'"

"The fishwife," Lena said, "has a remarkable imagination and a stall that sells lamp oil. Perhaps the child wanted light."

"Perhaps." Hale let the silence stretch. Outside, Bristle whined once at a cart. "A barefoot child alone in a curfew storm is a matter for the wardens, Mistress Nakamura. If such a child exists, she should be found. For her own safety."

"Of course," Lena said. "If she exists."

"And if she doesn't exist," Hale said, "then I'll thank you to explain why your window display changed overnight. This morning there are four fewer charms in that case than there were yesterday. Four. All of them in a style I happen to recognize." He leaned forward slightly. "You hid them when you heard I was coming. Which suggests that whatever they are, you believe they're worth hiding. From me."

For a moment — just a moment — the shopkeeper's pleasant mask slipped, and beneath it he saw something that startled him. Not guilt. Not fear, exactly.

*Recognition.* As though she were looking at him and seeing, all at once, the whole shape of him — the boot, the names, the dreams he hadn't told anyone about. As though she'd already measured him and found the measurement interesting.

"You dream of voices," she said quietly.

The words landed in the shop like a dropped glass.

Hale did not move. Twenty years of discipline held his face in place, but inside, something enormous shifted its weight. "That," he said at last, "is a strange thing to say to a magistrate."

"Is it?" She began tidying the counter — combs, twine, the small domestic motions of a person buying time to think. "You look like a man who hasn't slept well. And you ask questions like a man who's been answered already, somewhere else, and didn't like the answer."

"The child," Hale said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "What did she say to you?"

"Why does it matter to you? You hang people for less than what I suspect she said."

"I haven't hanged anyone in four years," he said, and was surprised to find it true, and more surprised to find it mattered to him that she know it.

Lena stopped tidying. She looked at him for a long moment, and whatever calculation she was running, it finished. "No," she said. "I don't suppose you have. You've looked tired for years, Magistrate. The hanging kind of tired."

"That's not —"

"She said," Lena interrupted, and now her voice was different, stripped of the shopkeeper's varnish, quiet and level and afraid, "*they sold me twice.*"

The words went through Hale like cold water through cloth.

He did not know why. That was the terrible part — he did not know *why*, and yet some door far back in him, some door he had painted over and furnished around and lived with for twenty years, rattled on its hinges. *Sold twice.* Somewhere beneath the mountain. Somewhere in the dark under Verrin, in the old stories, the ones mothers told and magistrates ignored —

"Dreams," he said hoarsely. "I've been having dreams. Voices in the walls. Last night one of them told me to ask you about the child." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "I don't believe in ghosts, Mistress Nakamura. It's a professional requirement. And yet here I am, telling a suspected felon that the dead are giving me errands."

"They do that," Lena said. "Give errands. They're very fond of it." She glanced toward the back room, then at the window, then at him — checking sightlines, he realized, the way a woman checks them when there is something in her house that cannot be allowed to be seen. "Why did you really come here, Magistrate? Not for the charms. You knew about the charms before you wrote the summons. You recognized the style. Whose style was it?"

The question hung between them.

Hale thought of the slip of paper in his boot. Of Maren Voss's name, second from the top, worn soft at the folds from twenty years of unfolding. Of ink-stained fingers and a voice saying *the pattern must breathe*, and of the order he had signed because the order came from above and he was a man who enforced the law, that was the whole of him, that had been the whole of him —

"Someone I executed," he said.

Lena Nakamura went very still.

"And I think," Hale continued, each word costing more than the last, "that whoever taught her is standing in front of me pretending to sell combs. And I think the child who knocked on your door said something that no living child should know how to say. And I think we are looking for the same thing, you and I, and I have spent twenty years on the wrong side of the finding of it."

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the salt-gray roofs. Bristle sighed and put his head on his paws.

Lena studied him the way she might study a piece of bone before committing the knife to it — turning him over, testing the grain, deciding what he could bear.

"If I tell you," she said finally, "there's no walking away after. Not for you. Not with your boot and your paper and your eleven names." Her eyes flicked down, briefly, to his left boot, and back up, and Hale felt the floor tilt a degree. "Yes. I know about the names. The dead talk, Magistrate. It's the one thing they can't stop doing."

His mouth was dry. "And what do they say about me?"

"Nothing yet," Lena said. "That's what frightens me. They say plenty about everyone. But about you—" she hesitated, and for the first time since he'd entered, she looked genuinely uncertain, "—they've gone quiet. Like they're waiting to see what you do next."

Hale stood in the little shop that smelled of bone dust and brine, a man who had built his life on believing in nothing he could not file, and understood that the file had just been opened on him.

"Then tell me about the child," he said. "All of it. And then tell me what's under the mountain."

Lena reached beneath the counter and drew out a charm — one of the four missing from the window, its double-lined border breathing in small deliberate gaps — and set it on the wood between them like a key laid on a table.

"Lock the door first," she said. "And send your dog inside. What I have to tell you, even the walls shouldn't overhear."

* * *

Behind them, unnoticed, the little carved bird on the shop's sign turned slowly on its nail — though there was no wind on Tidewrack Lane that day, and none of the dead, for once, said a word.
