## 5. Salt and Ash

The storm had spent itself by morning, but the salt it left behind glittered on every sill and doorstep in Verrin like the city had been dusted by a very thorough, very indifferent god. Lena swept her step before dawn, as she did every morning, because a clean step said *nothing to see here* better than any sign could.

Isla slept in the back room, curled in the chair where customers usually sat while Lena pretended to carve charms that worked. The girl slept the way soldiers slept—lightly, one hand closed around the little bone sparrow Lena had given her, ready to run even in dreams. Twice in the night she had made small sounds, not words, not quite, and each time the dead in the walls had stirred and murmured toward her like moths toward a lamp, and Lena had hissed at them—actually hissed, out loud, alone in her shop at three in the morning—to be quiet, for pity's sake, some of us are trying to hide a child from an empire.

The dead, to their credit, had settled. They were mostly considerate that way. It was the living who were the problem.

She thought about Ode while she swept. She thought about whether there was anyone else, and there wasn't, not really, and that was its own kind of indictment of twenty years of careful living. A life built on hiding left you with very few doors you could knock on.

Ode's door was behind the fishmonger's on Brine Street, up a staircase that smelled of tar and onions, past a door with no nameplate because names attracted attention and Ode had built a whole philosophy on avoiding attention. Lena knocked twice, paused, then once more—the old signal, older than the Purge, passed between people who moved things across borders that laws said shouldn't be crossed. Not smugglers of goods, exactly. Smugglers of *exceptions*.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye appeared, brown and bloodshot and deeply skeptical.

"No," said Ode.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You have the face. You get the face when you're about to ruin my life." The door opened wider anyway, because that was Ode: protest first, surrender second, complaint throughout. "Come in before Mrs. Halloway sees you. She reports foot traffic to the wardens now. For the ration credit."

Inside was the organized chaos of a woman who moved contraband for a living: crates labeled FISH OIL that did not contain fish oil, rope coiled with the precision of a temple offering, and a cat the size of a loaf of bread asleep in the one clean spot on the floor, radiating the smugness of all cats everywhere.

"Is that still Mackerel?" Lena said.

"He's called Admiral now. He earned it." Ode shut the door and turned, arms crossed, and Lena watched her take inventory the way she always did—one glance, head to boots, cataloguing everything wrong. Her gaze snagged on the bundle of blankets in Lena's arms and stayed there. "No," she said again, with more feeling this time.

"She has nowhere else."

"Everyone has somewhere else. That's what 'else' is *for*."

"Ode."

"Don't 'Ode' me. I know what that voice means. That's the voice you used when you wanted me to move a crate of unlicensed ink through the tide-gates, and I still have the scar from the tide-gates." But she was already clearing a space on the bench, pulling down a blanket that smelled of cedar, arranging things with the brisk tenderness she'd deny to her dying day. "Put her down. Is she sick? She looks half-starved. What happened to her?"

Lena laid Isla on the bench. The girl woke halfway, blinked at the strange room, the enormous cat, the stranger with the tar-stained hands—and then looked at Lena, and something in the look asked a question in a language with no common words: *is this safe?*

"As safe as anywhere," Lena said, which was true, and sad, and enough.

Isla settled. The Admiral relocated from the floor to the bench's far end, tucked himself against the girl's feet with the air of a soldier taking up a post he hadn't been asked to guard, and began to purr like distant thunder.

Ode watched this with narrowed eyes. "Animals don't do that," she said. "Not for strangers. Not that fast."

"I know."

"And you're not going to tell me why."

"I'm going to tell you as little as possible, so that if anyone asks, you can honestly say you don't know anything, and they'll believe you, because you've never once in your life been able to hide that you know everything."

"That's fair," Ode admitted. "That is devastatingly fair." She sat down heavily on a crate marked FISH OIL, which creaked in protest. "How long?"

"A week. Maybe two."

"Lena—"

"A week, Ode. Feed her. Don't let her talk to anyone. If she makes sounds that aren't words—" Lena hesitated. How to explain? *She speaks the language of the dead; the walls will answer her.* "If she seems frightened for no reason, open the windows. Fresh air helps."

"Fresh air helps," Ode repeated flatly. "Right. And if wardens come?"

"Then she's your niece. From the eastern villages. Plague orphan. You took her in out of the goodness of your heart, which everyone knows you don't have, so it must be true."

Something flickered across Ode's face—not quite pain, but its neighbor. "Plague orphan," she said quietly. "There are more of those than anyone says out loud."

"I know," Lena said again, and this time the word carried twenty years of knowing in it.

Ode looked at the sleeping girl for a long moment. Then she reached over and tucked the blanket higher, rough-handed and gentle at once. "A week," she said. "And you'll come back for her yourself. Not send word. Come."

"I'll come."

"And Lena?" Ode's voice dropped. "Whatever this is—you're doing that thing where you go quiet and start planning something that will end with me burying you. Don't. I've gotten fond of you. It's inconvenient and I resent it, but there it is."

Lena managed a smile. "I'm always careful."

"You're always *careful*, yes. That's what frightens me. Careful people only break once."

* * *

Finding the Tallowman took four days, and it took the dead.

This was the part of her life Lena never spoke of, even to herself, in the clear honest voice of daylight: that the dead were useful. That grief, kept long enough and tended carefully, became something like a network. Maren had taught her that, in the years before the Purge, when hearing the First Tongue was merely eccentric instead of fatal. *The dead remember everything, little wren,* Maren used to say, hands steady on the carving knife. *They have nothing but time and grudges. Be polite to them and they will work for you.*

So Lena went down into the salt-cellars under her shop, where the oldest dead of Verrin pooled like groundwater, and she asked her questions politely.

The dead did not know the Tallowman's face. Faces, they said—or rather, the pressure and chill that stood in for saying—were for the living. But they knew his smell. Tallow and cloves. They knew his footsteps, soft-soled, unhurried. And they knew his ledger, because ledgers were the one thing the dead could read, being written in the same currency they were paid in.

*A contract,* the dead pressed against her, cold as a wet stone against her mind. *Sold twice over. Once by the parents, once by the broker. The parents signed for grain and medicine. The broker signed for the court. Two signatures, one child.*

"Where does he keep the contracts?" Lena asked aloud, her voice echoing strangely off the salt-crusted brick.

*Below the counting-house on Ledger Street. Below, below. He keeps them where the living won't look and the dead can't reach. Iron boxes. Iron is rude to us.*

Iron was rude to the dead. It was also, Lena noted, extremely effective at keeping secrets from them—which meant the Tallowman knew about the dead. Knew about listeners. Which meant he was careful, and careful men were hard men, and hard men were dangerous in ways that required a different kind of planning entirely.

She climbed back up into the shop with salt dust in her hair and fury sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not stop repeating itself, no matter how she tried to sleep past it: two parents, desperate, plague in the house, three children already buried. They had gone to a broker because there was nothing else. The plague that had come through the eastern villages eleven years ago—the one Lena remembered, the one whose name she couldn't say without tasting ash—had been survivable with certain charms. Charms Maren had designed. Charms that worked.

Charms that had stopped working, in the villages, right when they were needed most.

Lena had told herself for twenty years that it was chance. That magic guttered sometimes, like any flame. That Maren's last great working had simply failed, the way works sometimes failed, and the Purge had taken Maren before she could fix it.

But the dead had been murmuring for four days, and murmurs had a way of becoming patterns, and patterns had a way of becoming truths, and the truth assembling itself in the dark of Lena's mind had edges like a knife: *the charms didn't fail. The charms were made to fail. Fear drives the desperate to brokers. Despair fills ledgers.*

Someone had engineered the desperation. Someone had fed children to the mountain court one plague-season at a time, and dressed it as mercy, and called it trade.

Lena sat at her workbench with a half-carved charm in her hands—a protection knot, inert, beautiful, useless—and realized she had been holding the carving knife hard enough to hurt, and that she had no memory of picking it up.

"Well," she said to the empty shop, and her voice came out low and even and stranger to her than the First Tongue. "That's enough of that."

* * *

Ledger Street was the respectable spine of Verrin's merchant quarter: pale stone facades, brass numbers, clerks hurrying with their collars up against the salt wind. The counting-house at number nine was gray and dignified and utterly forgettable, which was of course the point. Nobody robbed the counting-house. Nobody even looked at it too long. Looking too long at respectable buildings was how you got reported.

Lena looked at it for exactly as long as a woman waiting for someone might look, then walked past, then bought chestnuts from a cart at the corner and ate them slowly, watching reflections in the cart's polished brass kettle rather than turning her head.

The Tallowman came out at midday.

She knew him instantly, though she'd never seen him—a testament to the dead's descriptive powers. He was a soft-bodied man in a good coat the color of weak tea, with a round, mild face and small, patient eyes, the sort of man who might sell you candles or insurance or absolution. He smelled, even at forty paces on the wind, faintly of tallow and cloves. He paused on the steps of the counting-house to exchange courtesies with a magistrate's clerk, laughing at something, a warm domestic sound.

And walking two steps behind him, carrying his case, was a boy of perhaps fourteen who did not laugh, did not blink, did not look at anything at all—whose eyes had the flat shine of a house with all its lamps put out.

Lena's chestnut went cold in her hand.

*Hollowed,* whispered a dead woman near her shoulder, one of the cellar-dwellers who had followed her up out of habit and loyalty. *Not sold whole. Sold in pieces. They leave just enough to carry the case.*

The Tallowman descended the steps, nodded to his clerk friend, and set off along the street at his unhurried pace, the hollow boy gliding after him like a shadow with shoes.

Lena followed.

She told herself she was only following to learn his route, his habits, his hours—reconnaissance, caution, the careful work of a careful woman. And for six streets that was true. She window-shopped. She lingered at a ribbon stall. She let a funeral procession pass between them, heads bowed, and slipped through the mourners like smoke.

But then the Tallowman stopped outside a tenement door, and a woman came out—a thin woman with a baby on her hip and another child hiding behind her skirts—and the Tallowman smiled his mild smile and opened his case, and even across the street Lena could see what lay inside on the velvet: medicine. Good medicine. The real kind, the kind that worked, in amber bottles with wax seals.

And a contract, unfolded on the tenement's doorstep, and a pen offered, handle-first, courteous as a dance.

The woman looked at the medicine. She looked at the child behind her skirts. She looked at the contract for a long, long time.

Lena was moving before she decided to move. Three steps into the street, actually in the open, actually about to do something catastrophic and public and unforgivably stupid—

—and a hand closed on her arm, firm as a shackle, and pulled her back into the ribbon stall's shadow.

"Are you *insane*?" Ode breathed, hauling her against the stall's slatted side, her face gray under its freckles. "In broad daylight? On Ledger Street?"

"How did you—" 

"The fishmonger's boy saw you heading this way looking like weather coming on. I know what you look like when you're about to do something unforgivable, Lena. You get *serene*. It's terrifying." Ode glanced across the street. The transaction was concluding. The woman held an amber bottle in one hand and her pen-stained fingers trembled; the child behind her skirts stared at nothing, at something, at the space where the Tallowman stood, and Lena saw the exact moment the boy-mind behind those young eyes registered the contract being signed—saw recognition, or its ghost, flicker and drown.

The Tallowman folded the paper, wished the mother health and long life, and moved on, unhurried, his hollow boy behind him.

Across the street, the mother went inside and closed the door, and did not come back out, and did not look at the sky, and did not weep where anyone could see. Survival, Verrin-style: conducted indoors, in silence, at market rates.

Lena found she was shaking. Not with fear—with something hotter and less familiar. Twenty years of fear she knew how to carry; this was new. This had teeth.

"They sign," she said. Her voice sounded wrong. Flat and quiet and very calm, and she heard herself the way Ode had described her—serene, terrifying. "They always sign. Because the alternative is a grave, and the brokers make sure the alternative is always a grave."

"Yes," Ode said simply. She didn't argue. That was the thing about Ode; she was a coward in every way that mattered for self-preservation and brave in every way that mattered for anything else. "And you can't save them all by tackling brokers in the street. So. What's the plan, the actual plan, the plan that ends with you alive?"

Lena watched the Tallowman's round, mild back recede down Ledger Street, tallow and cloves trailing behind him like a signature.

"He keeps his contracts in iron boxes beneath the counting-house," she said. "He buys children's souls for the court under the mountain. And somewhere in that ledger is a page with Isla's name on it, and probably mine, and I intend to read every word."

Ode was silent a moment. "That's not a plan. That's a declaration of war."

"Yes," Lena said. "It is."

She had spent twenty years being careful, and careful had saved her life and cost her everything else, and it had not saved Maren, and it had not saved the eastern villages, and it had not saved a six-year-old girl from being sold twice. Careful was a currency too, she understood suddenly, and the Tallowman's kind had cornered that market decades ago. Every careful person in Verrin was collateral in someone's ledger.

Survival meant becoming dangerous. Not reckless—dangerous. There was a difference, and the difference was aim.

"Go back to Isla," Lena said. "Lock the door. Keep the Admiral close."

"Why? What are you going to do?"

Lena turned toward home, toward the salt-cellars, toward the polite, patient dead who owed her favors and remembered everything. "I'm going to find out what happens," she said, "when the ghosts go to war."

Behind her, on Ledger Street, the counting-house door opened once more, and a clerk stepped out and looked directly at her—looked, and frowned, and reached inside his coat for something—and Lena walked faster, heart hammering, already composing the lie she'd need if he followed.

He did follow.

He was calling her name when she rounded the corner—and it was not her name he called.
