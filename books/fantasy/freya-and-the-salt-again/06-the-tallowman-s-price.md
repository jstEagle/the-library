## 6. The Tallowman's Price

The Tallowman kept his shop in the one street in Verrin that smelled worse than the tanneries, which was saying something, because the tanneries could peel the varnish off your memories at forty paces. The street was called Candle Row, though no candles had been made there in a generation. What was rendered there now was subtler and considerably more illegal, if anyone had bothered to write it down.

Lena had been walking past his door for twenty years without going in. She knew the building the way you know a dog that bites—by giving it a wide berth and not making eye contact. But contracts left traces, even soul-contracts. Especially soul-contracts. And the dead who clustered around Candle Row like moths around a lamp that ate them had told her, in their murmuring way, that the paper she needed lived behind that door.

She stood outside it now, hood up, hands in her sleeves, feeling like a woman about to knock on the mouth of something.

*Don't*, said a voice from the gutter-grate at her feet. It was an old dead man, a cooper by trade, dead sixty years and still fussing. *He'll want more than coin. He always wants more than coin.*

"I know," Lena murmured.

*You're talking to a grate,* said the cooper.

"I'm aware of that too."

The door opened before she knocked. Of course it did. The Tallowman had never once, in all the stories the dead told of him, been caught unprepared. He was a long, pale man, the color of something kept out of the sun on purpose, with small wet eyes and hands so clean they looked newly boiled. He wore grey, head to foot, the grey of tallow and ash and old snow.

"Master Nakamura," he said. His voice was soft as fat melting. "I wondered when you'd come. Do come in. Mind the step. It's not a step, actually. It's a threshold, and thresholds are load-bearing in my trade."

The shop inside was nothing like hers. Her shop was bone-dust and wood shavings and the honest smell of work. This place was warm—warm the way a hand is warm—and full of shelves, and every shelf held jars, and every jar held something that caught the light wrong. Not preserved things. Preserved *moments*. A jar of someone's wedding morning. A jar of a soldier's last sunset. A jar, near the window, that seemed to hold nothing but the smell of bread, and Lena's chest ached looking at it until she made herself stop.

"You keep them alive," she said. "The memories."

"Kept is the word." He gestured to a chair. She didn't sit. He sat anyway, folding himself down like tallow poured into a mold. "A memory in a jar is a candle, Master Nakamura. Burn it, and for a little while you are there again—the whole of it, every sense, every heartbeat. My clients burn grief when grief gets too heavy. They burn joy when joy runs short. Very humane, really. I've often thought I should be knighted."

"The dead say otherwise."

Something flickered across his face—not offense. Interest. "Do they? And what do the dead say?"

"That the jars don't empty. That whoever burns them feels the memory once, but the memory itself goes dimmer each time. That you're not selling candles. You're selling the candle's death, slowly."

He smiled. It was a good smile, in the way that a well-made trap is well-made. "You've been listening to the walls again. Careful. In this city, hearing things is a hobby with a body count."

"I carve charms," Lena said flatly. "Charms that do nothing. Everyone knows this. I'm the least magical woman in Verrin. Ask anyone."

"Oh, I have. That's rather the point of you." He laced his boiled-clean fingers. "But you didn't come here to discuss your reputation. You came about a contract. A sale of record, twenty years old, involving a child. You want to see the paper."

Lena kept her face still. Twenty years of practice, and still her heart tried to climb out through her ribs. "If you have it."

"I have everything. That is my entire business model." He rose, drifted to a cabinet, and drew out a slim folder bound in grey leather. He set it on the table between them but kept two fingers resting on it, lightly, the way a man rests fingers on a bird he intends to keep. "Before we discuss terms—a courtesy. I admire your work. Your teacher's work, I should say. Maren Voss carved like no one else in three generations. And you—" his wet eyes traveled over Lena's hands, "—you inherited the style and buried it under mediocrity. Deliberately, I'd guess. Which took discipline. I respect discipline."

"Say what you want."

"What I want is simple. The contract is yours—for a price. Not coin." He turned his hand palm-up on the table. "I want a charm. Carved from your own bone. A finger-joint will do. Fresh."

The room got very quiet. Even the jars seemed to lean in.

"No," Lena said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard the terms. Bone freely given carries the giver in it—that's first-year knowledge, back when there were first years. A charm of my own bone would be a charm of *me*. Whatever you burned it for, whatever you sold it as, it would work. Really work. For the first time in my life I'd have made something true, and it would belong to you." She stepped back from the table. "No."

The Tallowman sighed, and for just a moment the salesman's polish slipped and something older looked out—something tired and transactional all the way down. "You know what I find remarkable about people like you? You think refusal costs nothing. Refusal is a purchase too, Master Nakamura. You're buying your wholeness. Everything has a price; refusing to pay simply means paying later, at interest." He slid the folder back into the cabinet. "The offer stands for six days. After that, the price changes. Prices only ever go up in my shop. It's the one honest thing about me."

"Then I'll find another way."

"There is no other way. There's me, and there's the mountain, and the mountain doesn't negotiate—it collects." He showed her to the door with terrible courtesy. "Give my regards to the cooper. He was always a worrier."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the color of dishwater, and the cooper was still muttering in the grate.

*Told you,* he said.

"You told me."

*What will you do?*

Lena walked fast, hood up, past the tanneries, past the shrine where nobody prayed anymore except with their eyes down. "I don't know yet. Ode might know someone. There are other brokers."

*There aren't. There's him and there's the ones who feed him.* The cooper's voice went strange and careful, the way voices did when the dead were choosing words around something they could see and she couldn't. *Girl. There's a woman following you. Two streets back. Grey coat.*

Lena didn't turn. She cut left down Cooper's Lane—his lane, ironically, he'd been pleased about that once—then right, then through the fish market where the smell covered everything and faces were all turned away from the wind. The footsteps stayed with her. Patient. Unhurried. Not trying to hide.

At the mouth of Saltcutter Stair she stopped and let them come.

It was a boy. Sixteen maybe, grey-coated, hollow-cheeked, with the particular blankness of someone being paid to have no opinions. He stopped three paces off and held out a folded paper.

"He says you'll want to see this before you decide," the boy recited, toneless. "He says it's free. Free things are his favorite advertisement."

She took it. The boy was already walking away.

Inside the paper, in the Tallowman's neat, greasy hand: an address in the Weft, the poor quarter, and a time—tonight, the ninth bell. Beneath it, one line:

*Come watch a professional at work. Then decide whose bone is worth more.*

*Don't go,* said the cooper, who had apparently followed her the whole way, which was unlike him; the dead hated leaving their haunts.

"I have to know what he does," Lena said. "Knowing is the whole job now."

*Knowing is how he gets you.*

"Yes," she agreed. "That's why he's offering it for free."

* * *

The Weft at night was a country unto itself, with its own weather—smoke instead of fog, its own laws, which were mostly the law of numbers: how many in a room, how many coins in a fist, how many days since the wardens last bothered. The address was a tenement with a green door and a queue outside it. Not a bread queue. A quieter thing. People standing alone or in pairs, not speaking, shuffling forward one at a time into the green doorway and coming out the side door minutes later, lighter somehow. Visibly lighter. Lena watched a man come out rubbing his temples like a man stepping out of loud noise into quiet, and she understood, with a slow cold turn of her stomach, what she was looking at.

A shop. An ordinary shop, with ordinary customers, selling ordinary things—except the stock was people's insides.

She joined the line. Nobody questioned it. That was the horror of it; nobody questioned anything. A woman ahead of her held a sleeping baby and stared at the wall. When Lena offered to hold the baby so the woman could rest her arms, the woman flinched as if offered a knife, then shook her head, then whispered, "Not tonight. Tonight I'm selling. Don't be kind to me tonight, I'll lose my nerve."

"What are you selling?" Lena asked, though some part of her screamed not to.

The woman looked down at the baby. "Doesn't matter. It's mine. That's what they tell you. It's yours, and you can sell what's yours, and isn't that freedom?" Her laugh cracked in the middle. "Nine years I had it. Nine years I carried it. They weigh it while you wait, did you know that? Like meat. Mine weighs—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—mine weighs heavy. Heavy ones fetch better."

The line moved. Lena's blood moved slower than the line, thick and unwilling.

Inside the green door was a room lit by actual candles, dozens of them, and Lena understood suddenly why the street was called Candle Row, and understood it so completely she wished she could un-understand it. Every candle was a memory burning. The room was full of other people's best days, burning down to light the business of selling more of them.

The Tallowman sat behind a table like any respectable tradesman, weighing small glass jars on a brass scale, writing in a ledger. He looked up when Lena entered and inclined his head, gracious as a host.

"Master Nakamura. You came. Sit anywhere. Mind the candle on the left—it's somebody's mother singing, and it spits."

Lena didn't sit. She stood against the wall and watched, because watching was the job now, and the job was going to hurt.

The woman with the baby was called forward. The Tallowman was gentle with her—Lena had to give him that, and giving him that made her angrier, because gentleness used as a tool is worse than cruelty, cruelty at least knows what it is. He spoke softly. He let her hold the baby while she signed. He weighed the memory—she'd been right, they weighed it, a small clay weight against a small glass jar—and named a figure in coin that would feed her family for a season.

"It's a fair price," the Tallowman said, and meant it, and that was the worst sentence Lena had ever heard in Verrin.

The woman signed. They took her into the back room, and there was a pause, and a sound like a long exhale, and when she came out she was smiling. That was the part Lena hadn't braced for. She came out *smiling*, relieved, unburdened, nine years gone and the space where they'd been swept clean, and she kissed her baby's head and the kiss landed on skin she no longer had history with, and she said, "What a beautiful child. Whose is this?" and laughed, and everyone in the room pretended that was normal.

Nobody in the room pretended harder than the Tallowman. He met Lena's eyes across the candles, and his expression was almost apologetic.

*This*, his look said, *is what desperation makes legal. I don't make the desperation. I just make change.*

Lena walked home through the Weft with her fists in her sleeves and the cooper's ghost keeping pace beside her, unusually silent, and when she finally spoke her voice came out low and level and stranger than she'd ever heard it.

"He wants my bone. One joint of my finger. And after tonight I understand—he'll take it gently, and fairly, and I'll walk out smiling, and somewhere down the line some frightened person will burn a piece of me to get through a hard night, and I'll never know what I did for them, and they'll never know it was me." She breathed the salt air. "Twenty years I've hidden. Hidden, and called it survival, and told myself hiding was harmless."

*And now?* asked the cooper.

"Now there's a child who was sold twice, and a contract with my name somewhere in the margins, and a dragon under a mountain going out like a candle." Lena turned toward the harbor, toward Ode's warehouse, toward the smuggler who owed her and the plan she didn't have yet. "The Tallowman said prices only go up in his shop. Fine. Then I'd better start shopping somewhere else."

Behind her, far off in the dark, the green door opened and closed, opened and closed, patient as a pulse.

And in her sleeve, against her wrist, the little bone charm she'd carved for Isla—the one that did nothing, that had never done anything—was, for the first time in twenty years, warm.
