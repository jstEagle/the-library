## 7. Dragon-Fire Guttering

The dead do not sleep, which Maren had always considered one of the great oversights of the afterlife. She had been a woman who treasured her sleep—she used to tell her apprentices that half of good carving happened in dreams, that the hands learned while the mind was off duty—and now she had nothing but waking, and waking, and waking, in a dark beneath the world that smelled of sulfur and old law.

She drifted down the long throat of the mountain, past galleries of bone and ledger-halls where clerks with no faces scratched at contracts written in a script that hurt to read even secondhand. The dead of Verrin called this place the Court Beneath, when they spoke of it at all, which was rarely, because speaking of it drew attention, and attention down here was a currency worse than coin.

Maren had been dead twenty years and she still thought of herself as a carver first. It was how she navigated: by grain and grain-structure, by the way stone remembered chisels, by the fact that everything—everything—was carved by something, given shape by pressure, and could in principle be given a different shape.

The dragon's chamber was carved too. That was the joke of it, if you could call it a joke. The court had hollowed out the mountain's heart to make a room for the thing they'd chained there, and the room was beautiful, vaulted and ribbed like the inside of some enormous instrument, and every surface bore tool-marks from masons three hundred years dead whose souls were probably also in ledgers somewhere, filed under *labor, uncompensated*.

Maren settled into the gallery above the fire-pit—the dead didn't sit so much as agree to stay put—and looked down at what remained of the last dragon in the world.

Its name, once, had been a word in the First Tongue that meant something like *the long warmth*. Nobody used it now. The clerks called it Asset Nine. The wardens called it the Furnace. The court's accountants, in their endless gray arithmetic, had reduced a creature older than their whole civilization to a line item: *heat source, diminishing; projected exhaustion, soon.*

Soon. That was the word that had brought Maren all the way down here, past wards that would have unraveled a younger ghost. *Soon* meant weeks. Maybe days. The dragon lay coiled around its own chains in the center of the pit, and it was—there was no kind way to say it—going out. Its scales, bronze-black once, had gone the color of cold ash. Its breathing came slow as tide. And its fire, the fire that burned in a hearth at the heart of it, the fire the court had spent three centuries siphoning to light their halls and seal their contracts and keep their stolen souls from simply drifting home—

That fire was guttering.

Maren watched it the way you watch a candle in a draft. One moment it stood tall and gold, throwing heat across the pit in waves that even the dead could feel, a warmth like being remembered. The next it shrank, shivered, bent nearly flat, and the whole chamber went dim and cold enough that the frost on the far walls crept a hand-span closer.

And every time the flame dipped, something in the walls cried out.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. The souls held here—the thousands upon thousands bought and brokered and bound into the mountain's stonework as living mortar—they didn't scream. They had screamed, once, in the early years. Now they made a sound like a congregation very far away, murmuring through a wall, and every time the fire guttered the murmur thinned, the way a choir thins when voices drop out one by one.

When the fire died, the murmur would stop entirely. Not pass on. Not go home. Just—stop. A soul held past its anchor's death didn't get to move on; it got to be *extinguished*, unmade, subtracted. The court's oldest contracts depended on it. Fear, after all, was the only collateral that never lost value.

Maren had come down to count.

It was grim work, counting. She moved along the gallery walls, ghost-fingers hovering over the soul-stones set into the rock like teeth, reading each one the way she'd once read grain in a piece of bone. Every stone held a name, if you knew how to look. Most were worn smooth by centuries of clerks' handling. Some were fresh.

The fresh ones were children's.

She found Isla's on her third circuit, and stopped, and stayed a long time.

It was a small stone, no bigger than a plum, set low in the wall near the floor where the frost reached first. It should have glowed warm amber, the color of a six-year-old's soul—which is a color Maren could not describe to the living except as *the way bread smells when someone you love is baking it*. Instead it flickered. Dim. Syncopated with the dragon-fire above, brightening when the flame stood tall, guttering when it guttered, because the child's soul was bonded to the court's reserve, and the reserve was bonded to the fire, and the fire was going out.

*They sold me twice.*

The girl had said those words to Lena Nakamura in a curfew storm, and the dead had carried them down the corpse-roads faster than any living messenger, and Maren had heard them and felt them land in whatever she had instead of a chest. Sold twice. Once by parents desperate through a plague-winter, once again by the Tallowman, who had bought the contract cheap and resold the soul to the court at a markup that would have embarrassed a slaver. Two sales. Two signatures. And somewhere in between, a small girl had stopped speaking—not because her voice was taken, but because the First Tongue was the only language left in her that was truly hers, and the First Tongue was the language of the dead, and saying things aloud in it felt like shouting into a grave.

Maren knew something about that. She'd spent her whole life teaching a dying language to people who were afraid to be caught alive using it.

"You're staring," said a voice behind her. "It's rude to stare at Asset Nine."

Maren turned. The clerk drifting at her shoulder had no face, strictly speaking—just a smooth stretch of gray where a face should be, with a quill tucked behind where an ear would go—but she recognized the posture. Everyone down here eventually developed the same stoop. Three hundred years of hunching over ledgers will do that, even to the dead.

"I'm staring at a child," Maren said.

"The child is an entry." The clerk consulted a slate that wrote on itself. "Lot 4471, bonded reserve, minor denomination. Frankly, I don't know why you haunt this gallery. There are eleven thousand lots in this wing alone."

"Eleven thousand two hundred and six," Maren said. "I counted."

Something passed over the place where the clerk's face wasn't—a flicker, almost like discomfort. "Counting doesn't change the total."

"No," Maren agreed. "But it changes the counter." She drifted back toward the pit's edge. Below, the dragon shifted in its sleep—or what passed for sleep—and a scale the size of a barn door sloughed off and fell into the ash at the bottom of the pit with a sound like a door closing. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't make me ask it in the First Tongue. You hate when I do that. It makes your ledgers smudge."

The clerk's quill twitched. Down here, the First Tongue wasn't outlawed—it was *load-bearing*, which was somehow worse—but the clerks still flinched from it, the way men flinch from the names of their own crimes. "The projections say—" It consulted the self-writing slate, which was, Maren noticed, writing more slowly than usual. Everything down here was slower than usual. Even the dark seemed to be thickening. "The projections say nine days. Eleven if we ration the municipal lighting. Four if the warden-captains keep requisitioning heat for their quarters, which they will, because warden-captains are the same in every century."

"Nine days," Maren repeated.

"Perhaps eight. The fire has opinions about schedules."

Everything has opinions, Maren thought. That was the thing the court had never understood, in three hundred years of treating souls as currency and dragons as furnaces: everything has opinions. Stone has opinions about how it's cut. Bone has opinions about the hand that shapes it. Fire has opinions about what it's asked to burn for. And a chained dragon, asked to heat the halls of the institution that murdered its kin, had exactly one opinion left, and it was expressing that opinion by dying.

* * *

She went closer to the fire than the clerks liked. The dead aren't burned by flame, but they can be *disturbed* by it, the way a reflection is disturbed by a stone dropped in water, and the court's rules forbade unauthorized proximity to Asset Nine on the grounds that ghosts near the fire sometimes *remembered things aloud*, and remembering aloud was contagious.

Maren had been contagious her whole life. It was practically her profession.

Up close, the dragon was less a creature than a landscape. She drifted along the vast curve of its skull, past nostrils wide enough to drive a cart through, each exhale carrying the smell of every fire that had ever mattered to anyone—forge and hearth and funeral pyre and birthday candle, all of it layered, all of it *familiar*, because dragon-fire was the original fire and every other fire was a copy of a copy of a copy.

One ancient eye opened as she passed.

It did not open all the way. It opened the way old men open their eyes at the end of a long afternoon: partially, wearily, with the full knowledge that opening them all the way would change nothing worth changing.

"You're the one who counts," the dragon said. Its voice was not sound so much as heat with grammar. "The little ghost with the list."

"I'm the one who taught," Maren said. "The counting came later."

"Teaching. Yes." The eye's lid dragged upward another fraction. In the depths of it, the guttering fire reflected, tiny and precious, like a coal cupped in two hands. "You taught the tongue. I heard it, before the chains. Children singing it in the valleys. It sounded like—" The great head shifted, and a chain the thickness of a cathedral took up the slack with a groan. "It sounded like weather. Like weather that loved you."

"It still exists," Maren said. "One speaker left that I know of. A child."

"A child." The word came out as smoke. "Then it isn't weather anymore. It's a seed. Those are different problems." The eye found her, and there was something in it that Maren recognized from mirrors, from the last year of her life, from every teacher who has ever watched the thing they built begin to fall. "You've come about the lot. 4471. The little one in the north wall, low, where the frost gets her."

Maren hadn't told him which stone. She hadn't needed to. "You know them all."

"I know everyone the fire keeps alive." A plume of breath, hot and sad. "Eleven thousand two hundred and six. Your number was right. I check it against my own heartbeats. We have approximately the same count, the fire and I, though mine is shrinking faster." The eye closed, then opened again, annoyed at itself. "Nine days. Perhaps eight. The clerks think I don't hear their projections. I hear everything. Hearing everything is the entire tragedy of being a furnace."

"What happens to them?" Maren asked, though she knew. "When you go out. Tell me what happens to them."

The dragon was quiet a long time. The fire in the pit sank until it was barely a golden thread running through the dark, and the murmur in the walls thinned, and somewhere in the eleventh thousand, a voice dropped out of the choir.

"When I die," the dragon said at last, "the bonds break. That part is true, and the clerks know it, and it is the one honest sentence in all their contracts. But a bond breaking is not a soul going free. A bond is a rope. If the rope burns through while you're holding the weight—" The fire guttered, hard, and the whole chamber went black for a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Maren heard the walls clearly, eleven thousand voices mid-word, mid-*name*, suspended. Then the flame caught again, and the voices resumed, thinner. "They fall. And there is nothing below the fall. I have watched it happen twice before, in the small die-offs, when sections of the reserve failed early. They do not scatter. They do not return to their bodies or their gods or their grandmothers' kitchens. They simply are not, afterward, and the universe does not notice, and that—" the chains groaned as the dragon pulled itself slightly straighter, an old animal making room for dignity, "—that is the part I cannot forgive. Not the chaining. Chaining happens. The not-noticing. That."

"Then we have eight days to make the universe notice," Maren said.

"We?" The eye gleamed, and for the first time there was something in it other than weariness—something dry, almost amused. "You're a ghost, teacher. Ghosts can't lift chains."

"No," Maren agreed. "But ghosts can talk. And I know a carver in Verrin who listens to ghosts, and a magistrate who's started dreaming of voices he executed, and a child who speaks the First Tongue like she was born knowing it, because she was, because it's the language of everyone your fire kept warm." She drifted closer to the terrible gentle eye. "And I know something else. I know that everything down here is carved, and everything carved can be re-carved. Including contracts. Including chains. Especially chains."

The dragon regarded her for a long moment. Above them, the fire stood tall for one glorious instant—gold, roaring, the whole vaulted chamber lit like dawn—and then sank again, lower than before, and the frost on the north wall crept another hand-span, and Isla's small stone flickered like a moth against glass.

"Eight days," the dragon said. "Perhaps seven. Go and count somewhere useful, teacher. And when you talk to your carver—" the eye began to close, but the words came anyway, smoke curling up through the dark, "—tell her the fire remembers her hands. Tell her the fire has been waiting twenty years for someone to pick up the chisel."

Maren went still, in the way the dead go still, which is completely.

"You know her," she said. "You know Lena."

"I know everyone the fire keeps warm," the dragon said, and closed his eye, and began, very slowly, in the First Tongue, to sing.

The song was old. Older than the court, older than the chains, older than the mountain's name. It was a counting song—Maren realized that after a moment, astonished—a song for keeping track, verse by verse, soul by soul, so that none of them would fall unwitnessed. He had been singing it for three hundred years. He would sing it until the fire failed.

Maren listened to exactly one verse. Then she turned and rose through the mountain's dark as fast as a ghost can rise, which is faster than fear, because eight days was not a schedule—it was a debt coming due, and somewhere above her, in a salt-city full of frightened people buying charms that did nothing, there was a woman with a chisel and a child with a seed-language and a magistrate dreaming of ropes, and not one of them yet knew how little time the fire had left them.

Seven days, perhaps. Maybe fewer.

The dead began carrying the message before she'd finished thinking it.
