## 8. An Alliance of Enemies

Corvan Hale had signed one hundred and fourteen execution orders in his twenty years as magistrate of Verrin, and he could recite the names of every one of them in the dark, which was a thing he did more often than he admitted to anyone, including himself.

He had never once dreamed of the faces. Until lately.

Now they came nightly: a tanner with salt-cracked hands, an old woman who had sold "luck-knots" at the fish market, and—most often, most unwelcome—a lean woman with ink-stained fingers and a mouth that had smiled at him from the scaffold steps as if she knew something about him he didn't yet know himself. Maren Ostrova. Carver. Teacher. Heretic, according to the writ. He had read the charges aloud himself, in his best courtroom voice, the one that made grief sound like procedure.

He told himself for three weeks that the dreams were indigestion. Magistrates were not haunted. Magistrates *did the haunting*, professionally speaking, and it would be a gross dereliction of dignity to be outdone by his own caseload.

Then the child appeared.

* * *

The report came from Warden Tull, who had the observational skills of a damp brick but excellent penmanship. *Subject: female child, approx. six years, unregistered. Seen entering the premises of L. Nakamura, bone-carver, Saltrow Lane, during curfew storm. Subject did not appear to walk so much as arrive.*

Hale read it four times. Lena Nakamura. The name sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He had pulled her file twice over the years—once on routine suspicion, once because her teacher's name was Maren Ostrova and he'd wanted, absurdly, to know what became of the students. The file was thin. Too thin. A woman who carved charms in a city where magic was a hanging offense should have accumulated paper like driftwood after a storm. Complaints, informants' notes, at least one nervous neighbor. Nothing. As though someone had swept the road clean ahead of her.

He had assumed luck. He no longer believed in luck. Luck was just a ledger you hadn't been shown.

So on a gray morning with rain coming down like the sky was trying to confess something, Corvan Hale put on his magistrate's coat—the good one, with the silver chain of office that clinked when he walked, announcing him to half the street before he arrived—and went to Saltrow Lane to arrest a bone-carver.

Or so he told the two wardens trailing him.

* * *

The shop smelled of tallow and shavings and something older underneath, like a church that had been converted into a pantry. Charms hung in rows from the ceiling beams on twine: knucklebones wrapped in copper wire, slivers of antler, little carved fish that were supposed to keep drowning away and almost certainly kept nothing away except the buyer's money.

Lena Nakamura stood behind the counter with a carving knife in her hand and an expression of profound, weary courtesy, as though he were the fourth tax collector of the day.

"Magistrate." She set the knife down with exaggerated care, the way one does when one wants very much to be seen setting it down. "To what do I owe the honor? If it's about Mrs. Pell's charm, I already told her the fish works better if she stops naming it."

Behind her, in the doorway to the back room, a small face appeared and vanished. Dark hair. Enormous eyes. Gone like a minnow.

Hale felt the wardens shift behind him. He raised a hand—not quite a halt, not quite a blessing—and said, "Wait outside."

Tull opened his mouth. Hale looked at him. Tull, to his credit, closed it, and the two of them clanked out into the rain, where they would stand under the awning of the chandler's shop and discuss lunch at length.

The door shut. The shop went quiet enough that Hale could hear the rain finding new ways to be miserable against the roof.

"You have an unregistered child in your home," he said.

"I have a niece," said Lena Nakamura, "visiting."

"Your file lists no family."

"My file," she said pleasantly, "lists nothing at all. Which has always struck me as either very good news or very bad news, depending on who's reading it."

She wasn't afraid. That was the first thing that unsettled him. Everyone in Verrin was afraid of him—it was practically his job description—but this woman looked at him the way a schoolmistress looks at a boy caught writing on desks: disappointed, but willing to hear his side.

"The child was seen entering during curfew," he said. "Alone. In a storm. Unregistered children are remanded to the Foundling Office. You know this."

"I know lots of things I know."

"And yet you took her in."

"She was knocking," Lena said. "It seemed rude."

Something in Hale's chest did a complicated maneuver. He had rehearsed this arrest on the walk over—cuffs, caution, the walk to the Hall, the paperwork, the tidy machinery of law doing what law did. Instead he heard himself say:

"What did she say to you?"

Lena blinked. It was the first honest expression he'd seen on her. "What?"

"The child. When she spoke. What did she say?" His voice came out lower than he intended. "Because Warden Tull says she doesn't speak. And I've spent three weeks dreaming of voices, Mistress Nakamura, and I have begun to suspect that the dead of this city are trying to tell me something, and I would very much like to know if a six-year-old is delivering their mail."

Silence. Rain. Somewhere in the back room, a floorboard creaked with the specific care of a small person listening hard.

Then Lena Nakamura did something no citizen of Verrin had ever done in his presence. She laughed—one short, disbelieving huff—and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"Twenty years," she said. "Twenty years I keep my head down, carve my nothing-charms, sell my nothing-charms, and the magistrate walks into my shop and asks me if I'm receiving correspondence from the dead." She looked up at him. "Who told you?"

"No one told me anything. That's rather the problem." He unclasped the chain of office from around his neck—heavy, familiar, the weight of it part of his skeleton by now—and set it on the counter between them with a clink that sounded, in the quiet, obscenely loud. "I'm going to tell you something now, and then you're going to decide whether to call your wardens back in and have me arrested for insanity, which would be fair."

Her hand drifted toward the carving knife. He pretended not to see. Courtesy between professionals.

"Maren Ostrova," he said. "I executed her. Twenty-two years ago. I read the charges myself." He stopped. Started again, because the prepared version of this speech had dissolved somewhere around the door. "The orders came down from the Purge Commission. Sealed writ. Her trial records were destroyed before I even saw them—I was told that was standard. I believed it was standard. I believed—" He exhaled through his nose. "Last month I requested the original writ from the Commission archive. For reasons I won't bore you with. Dreams. Indigestion. Whatever a man calls his conscience when he's too proud to call it a conscience."

"And?"

"And there is no writ. There never was a writ. The seal on my copy was forged, Mistress Nakamura. By someone inside the Commission. Maren Ostrova was executed on false orders, and I was the instrument, and I have spent twenty-two years being very good at my job so I wouldn't have to think about why that sentence makes my hands cold."

The knife-hand had gone still. Lena was staring at him with an expression he couldn't parse—grief and fury and something that might have been relief, all fighting for the same chair.

"You're lying," she said quietly. "This is a trap. You say her name, you watch my face, and everything I do next goes in your report."

"If I wanted to trap you, I'd have brought more than Tull, whose idea of subtlety is whispering." He turned his palms up—an old gesture, surrender, older than any office he held. "I don't want absolution, is the thing. I came here thinking I did. I've thought about it every night since the dreams started. But absolution requires someone qualified to grant it, and I've decided there isn't anyone left. So I'll take ruin instead, if that's what's on offer. I genuinely no longer care which. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am a man standing in your shop offering you either forgiveness or my head on a plate, and I will accept the second without complaint, but I would prefer the first, and I am *ashamed* of preferring the first, and there it is. All of it. On your counter, next to the drowning-fish."

For a long moment, Lena Nakamura said nothing. Then, from the back room, in a voice like a bell rung underwater—in a language Hale did not know and yet felt in his teeth, the way you feel thunder—a child spoke.

Both of them turned.

Isla stood in the doorway, small and grave, holding a carved wooden bird in both hands. She looked at Hale the way children look at large animals: assessing whether he was the kind that ate people. Apparently deciding the jury was still out, she said something else in that ringing tongue, and this time Hale caught a shape in it—a rhythm, a cadence—and the hair rose on his arms, because he had heard that cadence before. From the scaffold. From Maren Ostrova's lips, in her last minute, murmuring words the record-keepers couldn't transcribe and he'd pretended not to hear.

"What did she say?" he whispered.

Lena's jaw worked. When she answered, her voice was rough. "She said—" A breath. "She said, 'The tall sad one remembers her.' That's what she said. Word for word."

Hale sat down. Not gracefully; his knees simply resigned. He ended up on the customer's stool among the hanging charms, a magistrate of Verrin, fifty-one years old, with rain dripping off his coat onto a clean floor, and he put his face in his hands.

"I remember her every day," he said, to his own palms. "Every single day. Is that what she wanted? To be remembered by the man who killed her? Because if so, it's a cruel wish, and she didn't strike me as cruel."

When he looked up, Lena had come around the counter. She crouched to his eye level, which should have been humiliating and somehow wasn't, and studied his face with the focus of a carver reading grain.

"You want to fix it," she said. "That's what this is. You didn't come to arrest me or confess. You came because you found out the machine you served for twenty years chewed up an innocent woman, and you can't stand being a cog anymore, and fixing things is the only prayer you know."

"That's—" He considered lying. It lasted half a heartbeat. "Yes. That's exactly it. God help me."

"There's no god down here. Just salt." She stood, and offered him her hand—ink-stained, scarred, steady. "Here's what I know, Magistrate, and you're going to wish you'd stayed home. The child's soul was sold. Sold twice, she says—to a broker called the Tallowman, and through him to a court beneath the mountain, where souls are kept like coin. And the coin is going to burn, because the fire that keeps that court running is dying. When it goes out, everything held there goes with it. Including her."

Hale stared at her. Any other day, any other year, he would have reached for his cuffs and his courtroom voice. Instead he heard himself ask the only question that mattered:

"How long?"

"The dead aren't sure. Weeks. Maybe less. They argue about it." She smiled without humor. "You get used to that. Dead people argue constantly. It's like living above a tavern."

"And you intend to go down there. Into the mountain. With a child. To steal her back from a court of the dead."

"Yes."

"Which is treason, heresy, and suicide, in ascending order of certainty."

"I did mention you'd wish you'd stayed home."

Hale looked at the chain of office lying on the counter. Twenty years of silver. One hundred and fourteen names. One forged writ. He thought about the archive clerk who had gone pale when he asked his questions, and the letter he'd received that morning—unsigned, official-smelling—advising him that further inquiry into Purge-era records would be treated as sedition. They knew he was digging. Which meant that soon, whether he dug or not, he'd be buried.

Ruin was coming regardless. It was merely a question of pointing it at something useful first.

He picked up the chain, weighed it once—and set it back down on the counter, deliberately, like a man leaving a tip he couldn't afford.

"I have access," he said. "Warden rotations, the mountain toll-gate schedules, the Commission's sealed routes under the ridge. And I have a name you'll want: the forged writ was authorized with a countersignature I finally traced last week. Initials C.V. I don't know who that is yet. But someone high up murdered your teacher with my hands, Mistress Nakamura, and I find that I want to return the favor."

Lena looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, warmly, she laughed again—that same startled huff—and shook her head.

"An alliance of enemies," she said. "Maren would have loved this. She always said the law was just magic that forgot it was magic—words that make things happen because everyone agrees to pretend." She stuck out her hand. "One condition. You don't lie to me. Not even kindly. I've had twenty years of kindly lies and I'm full."

"Then I'll start as I mean to continue." He took her hand. Her grip was stronger than his. "I'm frightened out of my mind, I have no plan beyond the front door, and I haven't slept properly in a month."

"Wonderful," said Lena Nakamura. "You'll fit right in with the dead."

From the doorway, Isla held up the wooden bird and said something in the First Tongue, solemn as a verdict.

Lena translated, and her smile went crooked. "She says, 'Good. Now we need a boat.'"

"A boat," Hale repeated. "Why would we possibly need a—"

And that was when the knocking started at the shop's front door—three slow knocks, patient, wet, and wrong, in a rhythm Hale had heard only once before, on a sealed writ he would never forget.

Someone outside knew the Commission's private knock.
