## 9. The Road Under the World

The corpse-roads began where the salt-roads ended, which was to say: at the edge of everything Verrin's magistrates pretended existed.

Lena had walked the first mile in silence, Isla's small hand swallowed by hers, Hale a half-step behind with his magistrate's coat turned inside out so the grey lining showed instead of the black. He looked like a man wearing his own shame as a garment, which Lena supposed was accurate.

"You're walking too loudly," she said, without turning around.

"I'm walking like a person."

"You're walking like a magistrate who has never once worried about being heard." She stopped and crouched beside Isla, tucking the girl's collar tighter against the wind off the flats. "Wardens walk heel-to-toe. They count their steps. You can hear them a quarter-mile off if you know what to listen for."

"And you know what to listen for," Hale said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've spent twenty years listening to things other people can't hear. Footsteps are easy."

What she did not say — what she could not say, not yet, not while his hands were still capable of reaching for his irons out of habit — was that the dead were already talking. They had been since the moment they'd left the last lamp of Verrin behind. The corpse-roads were old beyond old, laid down before the Purge, before the mountain court, back when the people who buried their dead here had believed the road itself should carry them home. Every stone under her boots hummed faintly with the memory of feet. And threaded through it all, thin as wire, came the guidance she'd been promised: *left at the split stone. Down through the hollow. Do not take the high path; the high path is watched.*

She couldn't tell if the voice was Maren's. That frightened her more than the wardens did.

Isla tugged her hand and pointed ahead, to where the flats gave way to a sunken lane walled in packed earth older than any wall had a right to be. The girl's face did something complicated — fear and recognition fighting for the same ground — and then she made a sound. Not the First Tongue. Not words at all, exactly. A low, urgent note, like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

"She says we're close to the first shelter," Lena translated, though translation wasn't really the word for it. Isla's meanings arrived whole, weather-systems of feeling that Lena simply understood, the way she understood the murmur in her shop walls. "And she says—" Lena paused. "She says there are bad men on the high path already."

Hale went very still. "How many?"

Lena listened. The dead were imprecise about numbers; they'd never cared much for counting. "Three. Maybe four. Ahead of us, moving toward the ford."

"Then we don't go to the ford." Hale was already unbuttoning his coat the rest of the way, shrugging out of it, folding it with the crisp economy of a man who had folded a thousand official documents and could not stop folding things now. Beneath it he wore a plain wool shirt gone soft with age, and without the magistrate's black he looked suddenly, startlingly ordinary. A tired uncle. A man who fixed roofs. "We cut down through the hollow and travel dark until the moon sets."

"The hollow is a grave-pit," Lena said.

"Yes," said Hale. "I know. I signed the order that closed it."

* * *

They made camp in the hollow because there was nowhere else, and because the wardens would sooner walk into the sea than sleep among the dead. That was the thing about the Purge's children, Lena thought as she coaxed a flame no bigger than a thumbnail out of damp gorse-root: they had won so thoroughly that they had made themselves cowards. A generation raised to believe the dead were nothing but superstition, and now superstition kept them off the roads at night, and superstition was keeping three hired wardens up on the high path, huddled around a fire of their own, jumping at every owl.

"Small mercies," she murmured.

"What?" Hale looked up from the strip of dried fish he was pretending to eat.

"Nothing. Talking to myself." An old habit, and a useful one. No one questions a woman who mutters.

Isla had curled against Lena's side the way she had done every night since the shop, since the storm, since *they sold me twice*. But tonight she didn't sleep. Tonight she sat watching Hale with the frank, unnerving stare of a child cataloguing something dangerous, and after a long while she reached over, picked up one of Lena's practice-bones — a scrap of sheep rib Lena whittled when her hands needed occupation — and held it out to him.

"He doesn't carve," Lena said gently.

Isla didn't withdraw the bone. She just waited, patient as tide.

Hale looked at the bone. Then at the child. Then, briefly, at Lena, with an expression she might have called *help* if she'd been feeling generous. He took the rib scrap in two fingers, turned it over, and said, "I don't know what to do with this."

Isla pointed at his pocket. At the folded square of paper he'd been reading by firelight when he thought they slept.

"That's not the same thing," Hale said.

Isla continued pointing.

"It's a confession," he said finally, and his voice did something strange on the second word, fraying at the edges. "Twenty years of names. Everyone I sentenced. Everyone I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It's a list of the dead, and I put most of them there. Your teacher is third from the top."

Isla took the bone back. And then, with the terrible practicality of six-year-olds everywhere, she pressed it into his hand again and closed his fingers around it, and patted his knuckles twice, and Lena felt the meaning arrive before the gesture finished: *you hold your list. I'll hold mine. We'll carry them together.*

Hale stared at his closed fist for a long time.

"She does that," Lena said softly. "Makes you carry things you didn't volunteer for."

"When did she—"

"First night. She handed me a cold coal from the hearth. Just set it in my palm and looked at me." Lena watched the fire. "I think it meant *this is what grief feels like, so you know I understand yours.* Or maybe it meant *don't forget what burned.* With her it's hard to say. The First Tongue isn't really words. It's more like... weather."

"And you speak weather."

"I grew up in it."

Hale was quiet for a moment. Then, awkwardly, as though the motion had rusted in its hinge, he tucked the bone into his breast pocket, over his heart, next to the folded confession. Isla nodded once, satisfied, and burrowed into Lena's side, and within minutes her breathing went slow and deep.

"She's decided to keep you," Lena said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Children decide these things. You don't get a vote. Congratulations."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. In the firelight, with the magistrate stripped away and only the tired man remaining, he looked — Lena searched for the word and disliked all the candidates — *approachable*. It was an unsettling quality in a man who had executed her teacher. She resolved to trust it exactly as far as she could throw him.

Above them, at the rim of the hollow, the night wind carried voices. Not wardens. Older. *Left at the split stone,* they reminded her. *Down through the hollow. The child must reach the gate before the fire fails.*

"How long?" she asked the dark, barely breathing it.

The answer came back the way answers always came from the dead: not in days or hours but in weight. *Less than you have. More than you fear. Walk.*

* * *

They walked for three days.

On the first day the corpse-road dipped beneath the roots of a forest that had no business growing in salt-country, trees white as bone themselves, and Isla rode on Hale's shoulders and pulled his hair with the cheerful cruelty of the very young, and he bore it with the expression of a man discovering that being kept by a child involved taxes.

On the second day it rained, and they sheltered in the ribs of some enormous structure the Purge had erased from every map — a temple, maybe, or a school, though the difference had probably mattered to someone once — and Hale built the fire this time, competently, and Lena caught herself almost complimenting him and swallowed it like a stone.

On the third day, Isla spoke.

They had stopped at noon in a fold of hills where the road ran beside a stream, and Lena was showing her how to skip stones — a skill with no survival value whatsoever, taught purely because the child had watched her do it twice with an intensity usually reserved for miracles — when Isla picked up a flat grey pebble, weighed it in her small hand, and said:

"Home?"

One word. Common tongue. Cracked and rusty as an unused gate, pitched wrong, vowels in the wrong places, the word of someone assembling speech from wreckage. But a word. A real, ordinary, living word, in the language of people who were not dead.

Lena's hands went still around the stone she'd been about to throw.

Isla looked up at her, brow furrowed, and tried again, patiently, the way she did everything: "Home?" And then she pointed — back the way they'd come, toward Verrin, toward the shop with its shelves of charms that did nothing, toward the narrow bed and the cold hearth and the door with the crooked latch — and then forward, down the road, toward the mountain, and her face crumpled with the effort of holding both directions in one small heart.

"Oh," Lena said. Her throat had gone tight. "Oh, sweetheart."

She gathered the girl in, pebble and all, and Isla clung to her coat and asked again into her shoulder, smaller now, *home?*, and Lena understood that the question wasn't really a question about geography. It was a question about whether a person could belong somewhere after being sold out of it twice. Whether a soul that had been traded like coin could ever be a *where* again instead of a *what*.

"Home is wherever you're not for sale," Lena said into her hair. It wasn't a good answer. It was possibly a terrible answer. But Isla went quiet against her, considering it with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a petition, and then nodded once, filing it away.

Behind them, Hale had turned ostentatiously away, studying the stream as though it might contain suspects. His ears, however, were visibly red.

"You heard all that," Lena said.

"I heard nothing. I'm a magistrate. We're trained not to hear things." He cleared his throat. "There's a village ahead. Half a day. The maps call it Hollowmere."

"The maps are wrong. The dead call it something else." She stood, brushing off her knees, and hoisted her pack. "They call it the village that forgot. We'll want to be careful there. Careful and kind. The people aren't well."

"Not well how?"

Lena looked down the road, where the hills folded green and innocent around whatever waited in them, and felt the dead press close at her shoulders like a congregation leaning forward in their pews.

"They bought their lives from the mountain," she said. "And paid in memories. They'll feed us and shelter us and smile at us, and none of them will know why their eyes are empty." She adjusted Isla on her hip. "So we'll be kind anyway. That's the whole trick of it, Magistrate. Kindness doesn't require remembering why."

They walked. The road bent down toward the valley, and the light went gold and long across the grass, and Isla — riding Hale's shoulders now, her small fists knotted in his hair, humming a tuneless little song that was half First Tongue and half the new cracked word she'd found — pointed at the smoke rising from Hollowmere's chimneys and said it again, wondering, testing, like a coin bitten to see if it was true:

"Home?"

And far behind them, at the crest of the ridge they'd left that morning, four figures in warden-grey crested the hill and began, unhurried, to follow.
