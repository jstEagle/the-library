## 10. The Village That Forgot

The rain found them an hour before the village did.

It came down the mountainside in gray sheets, turning the corpse-road to soup and Isla's hood into a small waterfall that she bore with the dignity of a duchess. Lena had long since stopped trying to keep the child dry. Some battles were for other people's pride.

"There," Hale said, pointing through the murk. Lights. A scatter of them, low and yellow, huddled in a valley fold like coins someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up. "Hollowmere."

"You know it?" Lena asked.

"I signed its tax rolls for eleven years." He said it flatly, the way he said most things now, as though every fact about his old life were a stone he was carrying in his mouth and refusing to spit out. "Grain, wool, and one very litigious goat farmer."

"One very what?"

"You'll meet him. His name is Doss. Everything is a dispute."

Isla tugged Lena's sleeve and made a sound—not a word, not quite, but a question shaped like a bird taking off. *Warm?*

"Yes, little fish," Lena said. "We'll find warm."

The girl had taken to calling nothing anything yet; she spoke her First Tongue like water running under ice, and once—only once, on the second night of the road—the common word *home?*, which had sat in Lena's chest ever since like a swallowed coal. Mostly she communicated by sleeve-tugs and expressions of imperial judgment. She was six years old and already better at silence than Lena had been at twenty.

The dead, for their part, were quiet tonight. They often went quiet near villages. Lena had never asked herself why until now, walking down a road the ghosts refused to comment on, and the not-asking sat strangely against her ribs.

*They don't like coming here,* said a voice she hadn't heard in days—a woman's voice, old and dry as seed husks. One of the road-dead, the ones who haunted the corpse-roads themselves rather than any particular grave. *Or maybe they can't.*

"Can't what?" Lena murmured, too low for Hale to hear over the rain.

*Remember where they are.* The voice sounded puzzled, which was unusual for the dead, who were generally the most certain creatures Lena knew. *Something eats remembering here. Something ate it all.*

The village gate was a rope across two posts, which struck Lena as either very trusting or very sad. Beyond it, Hollowmere arranged itself around a single cobbled square: a well, a shrine with no saint in it—just an empty niche, scrubbed clean—and houses with good timber and good roofs and windows that glowed the way windows glow when people inside are trying very hard to seem normal.

A man met them in the square before they'd crossed half of it. Big, weathered, holding a lantern up as if light were an argument he intended to win.

"Travelers," he said. "In this weather. Well." He looked them over: a bone-carver with a satchel, a magistrate in a coat too fine for the mud, a child. His eyes snagged on Hale's coat and stayed there a moment too long, and something moved behind his face—something reaching for a thought and finding the shelf empty. "Well," he said again, having apparently lost whatever came next.

"We need shelter for the night," Hale said. "We can pay."

"Pay. Yes. We take coin." The man frowned, as though coin were a concept he'd heard described but never seen demonstrated. "I'm Tam Vetch. I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm the headman. I think I'm the headman. Someone has to be."

"You think?"

"It's written down somewhere," Vetch said, with the tone of a man defending himself against an accusation. "There's a paper. I'm nearly sure there's a paper."

Lena felt the hair rise on her arms, and it wasn't the cold.

* * *

The inn was called the Hearthstone, or possibly the Wayfarer's Rest, or possibly both; the sign above the door had been repainted so many times the names lay over each other like sediment. Inside it was warm and smelled of stew and wet wool, and the innkeeper, a round cheerful woman named Brey, took their coin and their order and then stood holding the menu board upside down for a full ten seconds before noticing.

"Long day," she said, laughing, and everyone in the common room laughed too, a beat too late, the way you laugh when laughter is expected but the joke has slipped out of reach.

Lena watched them while she ate. That was the thing about twenty years of hiding: you learned to watch. And what she saw made her skin crawl in a way the Tallowman's shop never had.

They were healthy, these people. Well-fed, well-housed, mended clothes, good boots. Children chased a dog between the tables. An old man played a fiddle with more enthusiasm than accuracy. And every single one of them, sooner or later, did the same thing: reached for something that wasn't there.

The fiddler lost his place mid-song and stared at his hands as if they'd betrayed him. A young woman knitting dropped a stitch, frowned, and said, to no one, "I almost had it. Why do I almost have it?" Two men at the corner table argued cheerfully about whose turn it was to buy the next round, and the argument circled the same three sentences like a dog chasing its tail, because neither of them could remember who'd bought last, or whether anyone had, or how the game even worked.

And in the middle of it all, Brey brought them a fourth bowl of stew nobody had ordered, set it down in front of an empty chair, and stood there smiling at it.

"Brey," said her husband gently, from the kitchen doorway. "Love."

"Oh." She laughed, picked the bowl back up. "Habit. Silly me. Whose was—I've forgotten whose—" Her smile held, but her eyes went somewhere else for a moment, someplace with no furniture in it. Then she bustled off, and the moment closed over her like water.

"They sold it," Lena said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd known since the gate. She'd known since the empty niche in the shrine, maybe. "To the court. Their memories."

Hale didn't answer at once. He was watching the room too, and his face had gone the color of old tallow himself. "The plague years," he said finally. "Forty-one, forty-two. This valley was supposed to burn. I remember the orders." He stopped. Corrected himself, jaw tight. "No. I remember reading the orders. I don't remember deciding anything about them. There's a difference, and I'm only now learning what it is." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Someone here must have brokered a trade. Souls' worth of memory, whole village, in exchange for the court looking away. It would have been legal. It was always legal. That was the trick of it."

"And they got to live," Lena said. "And they got to forget they'd paid."

"Not forget." Hale shook his head. "Worse. Look at them. They know something's missing the way an amputee knows. They keep reaching for limbs that aren't there. They just can't say what shape the absence is."

Isla, curled against Lena's side with a bowl of her own, looked up at the two adults with the weary patience of a child surrounded by slow learners. Then she pointed her spoon at the empty niche visible through the window across the square, and said something soft in the First Tongue.

"What did she say?" Hale asked.

Lena hesitated. Translating the First Tongue aloud was dangerous in Verrin; here, in this hollow-hearted place, it felt almost obscene. But the words had been simple, simple the way children's truths are simple.

"She said: *the house is swept, but nobody lives in it.*"

* * *

Later, when Isla slept in the room's one bed and Hale kept first watch by the shuttered window, Lena crept down to the common room, drawn by something she couldn't name—which was itself becoming a familiar sensation, and one she liked less each day.

Brey was still up, banking the fire. She looked up, unsurprised. "Couldn't sleep either? Nobody sleeps right in Hollowmere. Never have." A pause. "As far as I know. Which isn't far, probably."

"May I sit?"

"Fire's yours as much as mine. More. You paid."

Lena sat. For a while neither spoke. The fire ticked and settled. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard announced Isla rolling over, and both women glanced at the ceiling out of the same instinct, and smiled at each other for having done it.

"My daughter would be about your guest's age," Brey said suddenly. "If I had one." She frowned at her own sentence, tested it, found it wanting. "That's not right. I mean—if I'd had one. If I'd ever—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "You see? There's a hole there. Right there, in the middle of me, shaped like something. And I go to touch it and my hand goes straight through, and I laugh, because what else do you do? And then I set out four bowls of stew."

"Brey." Lena kept her voice very gentle. "Do you remember the sickness? The plague years?"

"Sickness." Brey said the word like a foreign dish she'd been told she enjoyed once. "Everyone talks about the sickness. The priest used to—no. There's no priest. There's a niche with nothing in it." Her breath caught. "Why is there a niche with nothing in it? Who takes a saint out of a wall and leaves the hole?"

"I don't know," Lena lied, because the truth—that the villagers had likely sold the memory of their own patron along with everything else, that grief had been itemized and traded by weight—was not hers to give at midnight to a kind woman with hollow hands.

Brey wiped her eyes briskly, businesslike, the way people do when crying has become a chore rather than a relief. "You're the carver. The bone-woman. Tam said." She leaned in, and her voice dropped, and underneath the cheer there was something raw and pleading. "Could you make me something? To hold? Everyone says your charms are only wood and string, that they do nothing, that it's foolishness—but I'd like a thing that does nothing. I'd like to hold nothing in my hand and pretend it's the shape of what's gone. Do you understand?"

Lena understood so completely it frightened her.

She thought of her shop in Verrin, the rows of inert charms, the frightened customers who needed objects more than magic. She had always told herself she was selling comfort, harmless comfort, a kindness. Sitting in Hollowmere, she saw the other side of the ledger for the first time: that a charm that does nothing is also a monument to everything that's been taken. That she had spent twenty years carving little empty niches for people to carry in their pockets.

"I'll make you something," she said. "Tomorrow. Before we go."

"Before you go." Brey nodded, and then, abruptly, gripped Lena's wrist. Her hand was trembling. "Where are you going? All of you, with the child, and the fine coat—where does anyone go, anymore, that's worth going to?"

"Away," Lena said. "And toward something. Both at once."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Lena agreed. "But it's an honest one."

* * *

She woke before dawn to the feeling of being watched, and lay still, cataloguing: Isla's breath beside her, slow and even. Hale's silhouette at the window, unmoving. Rain stopped. And beneath it all—

Voices. Faint ones. The dead, come back from wherever they'd gone, murmuring at the edge of hearing like guests arriving at a party after the host has died.

She slid out of bed, wrapped her cloak, and followed the sound down the stairs and out into the blue-gray cold of the square. The mist was thick enough to drink. And standing in it, at the edge of the empty shrine, was a figure she knew.

Maren looked exactly as she had at the end: gray-robed, sharp-eyed, one hand forever half-raised as if about to correct someone's grip on a chisel. Twenty years dead and still radiating disapproval. Lena's throat tightened with a love that had nowhere left to land.

"You're quiet here," Lena whispered. "All of you. Why?"

Maren's ghost drifted closer to the empty niche. When she spoke, her voice came thin, stretched, like a thread pulled too tight. *Because there's nothing here for us to stand on, child. The dead walk on memory. This village sold theirs, and the memories went below—to the mountain, to the court, into the vaults. We can't haunt a place that's been emptied. We slip off it, like rain off oilcloth.*

"Their memories are in the court. With the souls."

*Everything they took goes to the same vault. Souls. Years. Names. Grief.* Maren's image flickered, steadied. *Including, if it comes to that, yours.*

Lena went cold in a way the morning couldn't explain. "Mine?"

*Ask yourself why the wardens never found you, Lena. Twenty years of illegal carving, of hearing us, of teaching the Tongue to a ghost's granddaughter—and not one warden's knock. Ask what that safety cost, and who paid it.* The ghost's face did something complicated, grief and guilt braided together, and then the mist stirred, and Maren's voice thinned to almost nothing. *Not here. Not now. But soon, and you will hate me, and I need you to forgive me anyway—*

"Maren—"

But the mist swallowed her, and the square was empty, and the only sound was the well rope creaking in the wind like something learning to speak.

Behind her, the inn door opened. Hale stepped out, sword-less, watchful, his breath smoking. "You feel it too," he said. It wasn't a question. "The road east. Since midnight."

Lena listened. Beneath the dead's faint returning murmur there was another sound now, carried on the wind off the ford road: hooves. Many. Moving fast. And threaded among them, unmistakable to ears trained by twenty years of listening for it—the dry rattle of warden chains, and under that, thinner than everything, a whistled tune she'd hoped never to hear again.

The Tallowman's men. Three days behind them on the corpse-road, no longer behind at all.

"Hollowmere's the only crossing before the ford," Hale said grimly. "If they hold the bridge—"

"Then we cross before they reach it." Lena was already moving toward the inn, toward Isla, toward the small warm life she'd somehow become responsible for. Behind her the village slept on, dreaming around its holes, and she carried the sight of it away with her like a stone in her boot: *this is what survival costs. This is the receipt.*

She only prayed, climbing the stairs two at a time, that when her own bill came due, she'd have the courage to read it.
