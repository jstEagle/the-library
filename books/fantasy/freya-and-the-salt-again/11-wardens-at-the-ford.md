## 11. Wardens at the Ford

Corvan Hale had spent thirty years learning to read a river, and the Greywater told him everything he needed to know and none of it was good.

It ran high with the spring melt, brown-green and muscular, dragging branches past the ford like it had somewhere to be. The crossing itself was honest enough—waist-deep at the center, cobbles worn smooth by centuries of carts—but the banks were wrong. Too quiet. No herons. No midges bothering the horses. Even the reeds stood still, as if they were listening.

"Something's off," he said.

Lena looked up from tightening Isla's bootlaces. "You say that every morning."

"And every morning I've been right."

"You said that about the mushroom stew on Tuesday," Lena said. "Isla ate three bowls of it."

Isla, who had been drawing shapes in the mud with a stick—the looping, curling shapes of the First Tongue, which she did absently now, the way other children hummed—looked up and offered Hale a solemn nod of agreement. Then she pointed at him, then at the stew, then made an expression of profound disappointment that no six-year-old should be able to execute so precisely.

"I'm being ganged up on," Hale muttered. "By a charm-seller and a child who communicates primarily in judgment."

He dismounted anyway, because his gut had been whispering to him since dawn, and thirty years of enforcing the Purge had taught him that his gut was the one informant who never lied to him. Not because it was clever. Because it was afraid, and fear kept better books than ambition ever had.

The ford was empty. That was the problem. The Greywater sat on the old corpse-road between Verrin and the mountain, and in the years before the Purge, this crossing had seen pilgrims, traders, funeral processions going the long way around to bury their dead where the salt couldn't reach them. Now it saw nothing, and nothing, Hale had learned, was rarely natural. Nothing was usually arranged.

He walked the near bank while Lena watered the horses and Isla collected smooth stones with the fierce concentration of someone building something important. He found what he expected to find on the third pass: hoofprints in the soft ground upstream, half a dozen horses, shod in the military pattern, and pressed into the mud beside them the clear wax seal-scars of warden dispatch markers. They'd been here within the hour. They'd watered their horses and moved into the trees.

Waiting.

Hale stood very still for a moment, holding the evidence in his palm like a stone he wasn't sure whether to throw or swallow.

Six wardens. Maybe eight. Led by someone competent, because there'd been no cookfire, no noise, no careless horse left picketed where travelers could see it. And they would have his description. They would have *all* their descriptions, because the Tallowman's hired wardens didn't send sketches—they sent men who had seen your face and could describe the way you held your shoulders.

There was a version of Corvan Hale who crossed that ford alone, walked into the trees, and talked his way out. Thirty years of that man existed. That man knew the wardens' hand-signs, knew their patrol rotations, knew which captain owed him favors and which ones simply feared him. That man could stand in front of six armed wardens with a magistrate's seal in his pocket and say *stand down* and be obeyed, mostly, and if it went badly he could claim he'd been tracking fugitives all along and produce Isla as evidence and walk away with his pension intact and his name clean.

That man had died somewhere on the corpse-road, Hale realized. Somewhere between the village of hollow people and the night Isla fell asleep against his shoulder on watch and he had sat unmoving for four hours rather than wake her. He couldn't point to the exact spot. Grief and change were like that—no dramatic moment, just a road, and then you looked back and the man you'd been was over the horizon.

"Hale?" Lena called softly. "You've got your thinking face on. It looks like constipation."

"It's called deliberation."

"It looks like constipation with ambitions."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Wardens. Upstream, in the trees. Six or more. They're waiting for us to commit to the crossing—middle of the river is when you take people. Can't run in waist-deep water."

Lena went still in the way she went still when the dead spoke to her, though he knew—she'd told him, reluctantly, on the fourth night, staring into the fire—that the voices were quieter now, farther away, as if the mountain's gravity pulled at them too. She looked at Isla, who was still stacking stones, oblivious, golden in the morning light, and Hale watched the same arithmetic happen behind Lena's eyes that was happening behind his own.

"We go back," Lena said. "Around through the hills. Adds two days."

"They'll have riders watching our backtrail. The Tallowman doesn't leave doors open." He turned the seal over in his mind. "But there's another way across."

"I hate when you say that."

"There's a warden protocol for contested crossings. I wrote half of it." He took a breath. "I walk out there in full magistrate's colors, announce myself, demand their authority papers. They'll show them. While they're showing them, you take Isla across the low route downstream—there's a shelf of rock under the waterline, knee-deep if you know where it is. By the time they realize, you're on the far bank and I'm—"

"No," Lena said.

"—I'm negotiating," he finished. "Extensively."

"Hale."

"Lena. Listen to me." He kept his voice level, the voice he'd used in courtrooms for decades, and hated how easily it came. "If we all run, they chase, and chasing is what wardens are *for*. If I stand, they hesitate, because I'm still Corvan Hale, and Corvan Hale has signed more execution orders than any man alive, and men like that don't get arrested by their own dogs without a great deal of paperwork." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "My reputation is finally good for something."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's a plan with a eulogy-shaped contingency. Very fashionable among magistrates."

Isla had stopped stacking stones. She was looking at Hale with those too-old eyes, and she said something in the First Tongue—soft, questioning—and Lena translated, her voice gone careful: "She asks if you're going to do something brave and stupid."

"Tell her brave and stupid are the same thing viewed from different distances."

Lena translated. Isla considered this with the gravity of a judge reviewing a petition, then walked over and pressed one of her smooth stones into Hale's hand. It was grey, river-worn, unremarkable, except that she'd scratched a single First Tongue character into it with another, sharper stone. Hale turned it over.

"What does it say?"

Lena looked at it a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone rough. "It says *come back*."

* * *

They gave him twenty minutes' head start, which was generous, since he'd given them five minutes of instructions and eleven minutes of Lena arguing with him in a fierce whisper while pretending to check harness buckles.

Hale rode out onto the ford road in his magistrate's grays, brushed clean, seal-chain visible, sitting his horse like the last thirty years hadn't happened. The trees ahead were perfectly ordinary trees. He let his mount amble toward the crossing, and when he was forty feet from the treeline, he said, clearly, in the carrying voice of the courtroom:

"Wardens. You're standing in my investigation. Show yourselves or be reported for dereliction."

A pause. Then they came, the way wardens always came—not from where you looked but from where you'd stopped looking. Six of them, fanning out of the trees with crossbows relaxed but loaded, and at their center a woman Hale recognized with a sensation like stepping into cold water: Captain Vess Rook. Fifteen years under his command structure. Good officer. Thorough. The kind of thorough that got people hanged correctly, according to the law, which was the only kind of correct the Purge had ever allowed.

"Magistrate Hale." Her eyebrows rose, genuine surprise, quickly mastered. "Sir. We weren't told you were operating in this district."

"I don't report my movements to contract labor." He dismounted slowly, letting them see his hands, letting them read the scene the way he wanted it read: a magistrate, alone, in pursuit. "Who holds your commission?"

Rook hesitated a fraction too long. There it was. "Private contract. Broker's authority." The Tallowman. Of course. "We're pursuing a child, sir. Soul-bonded property, stolen from lawful custody. And two accomplices." Her eyes flicked over him, cataloguing. "One of them may be known to you."

"Describe them."

"A woman. Carver by trade. Nakamura. And—" Rook's jaw tightened, and Hale watched fifteen years of discipline fight something older and truer, "—a man matching your description, sir. Traveling with them. Armed."

The crossbows didn't quite rise, but they stopped being relaxed.

"Well," Hale said. "That's awkward."

"Sir, step aside. This doesn't have to concern you."

"Captain Rook." He said it gently, almost kindly, and saw her flinch, because kindness from Corvan Hale had historically preceded paperwork. "Fifteen years ago you brought me a carver out of Tanner's Row. A boy, seventeen, who'd scratched a luck-mark on his doorframe. Do you remember what I told you?"

"You told me—" Her voice caught. "You told me the law was the law."

"The law was the law." He nodded slowly. "And I believed it. I built my whole life on it. I executed a woman named Maren Ashgrave on the strength of it, and I have recently learned that the orders were false, and that the law I served was a ledger with a hangman attached." He began, deliberately, to unbuckle his sword belt. "So let me tell you what the law is today, Captain, on this riverbank, in front of your witnesses."

He dropped the sword belt in the mud. Then the seal-chain, the gray-and-silver weight of thirty years, landing with a sound like a small bell ringing for a funeral.

"Today the law says a six-year-old girl belongs to whoever paid for her soul. Today the law says I should help you take her." He spread his arms. "I am telling you, formally, publicly, with witnesses: I will not. Arrest me. Report me. Hang me. But you will go through me to reach that child, and I am done being the law's hands. From this moment I am its criminal, and glad of it."

Silence. River sounds. Somewhere a bird remembered it was spring and complained about it.

Rook stared at him, and Hale watched the war behind her eyes—the career, the orders, the private broker's pay that doubled a warden's wage, and underneath all of it, the seventeen-year-old carver from Tanner's Row who had never stopped weighing on her either. He'd put that weight there himself. He'd put it on all of them.

"Sir," she said quietly, "if you stand aside, I can report you were deceived. Coerced. I can—"

"Captain. You can't." He smiled at her, and this time it was entirely his own. "That's rather the point."

One of the younger wardens shifted, crossbow rising an inch, and Rook snapped, "Steady!" without looking away from Hale. Then, lower, only for him: "Why? Thirty years, sir. Why throw it away for—"

"Because I dreamed of voices," Hale said simply. "For a year. Every night. And when I finally listened, do you know what they wanted? Not vengeance. *Correction.*" He glanced upstream, toward the rock shelf where, if the gods were kind, a charm-seller and a small stubborn ghost-tongued child were even now picking their way through knee-deep water. "And I find I'd rather be corrected than comfortable."

Rook closed her eyes. Opened them. Made a decision that would follow her to her grave, whichever grave it was.

"Form line," she said to her wardens, loud, official. "Magistrate Hale is taken into protective custody pending review of his mental fitness for duty. He resisted. Non-lethal measures authorized." And then, in a murmur meant only for him, as they moved to bind his hands: "Downstream shelf. Knee-deep. My father fished this ford; I've known it since I was six." A breath. "Go quickly, sir."

They took his arms, gently, for an arrest. And Corvan Hale, traitor, felon, free man at last, laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, echoing off the water—as the cold river wind came down the valley carrying, faintly, from the far bank, a child's voice calling something in the First Tongue that needed no translation at all.

Because some words are the same in every language.

*Isla*, it said. *Come back.*

Wait—no. Correction, as the dead would insist: it said *come home*.

And home, for the first time in thirty years, was wherever they were.
