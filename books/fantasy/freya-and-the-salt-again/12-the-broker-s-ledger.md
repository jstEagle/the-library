## 12. The Broker's Ledger

The trap was, Lena had to admit later, elegantly simple. She should have respected it more at the time.

She had spent three days following the Tallowman's runners through the salt-fog of Verrin's lower wards, and on the fourth day she followed one straight into a room with no back door.

The runner was a boy of perhaps fifteen, all elbows and nervous glances, carrying a ledger-satchel the way other children carried bread—close to the chest, protectively, hungrily. He led her down a stair that smelled of tallow and old wax, past a door marked with the sign of the scales (the honest kind, grain-weighing, deliberately boring), into a counting room where forty candles burned in perfect rows and not one of them flickered.

The door shut behind her with the soft click of a well-made thing doing its job well.

"Sit," said the Tallowman.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the first insult. Twenty years of whispered horror stories had built him tall—a giant of rendered fat and coin-greed, some ogre of the trade routes—and instead there was this: a neat, grey man in a neat, grey coat, with the mild face of someone's favorite uncle and hands so clean they looked boiled. He sat behind a desk of black saltwood, and before him lay an open ledger, and beside the ledger lay a cup of tea, steaming, with a second cup waiting across from him.

He had prepared for two. That was the second insult.

"I didn't come to be served," Lena said.

"No one ever does." He gestured again, patient as weather. "But you came, which is rarer. Sit, carver. The tea is only tea. If I wanted to drug you I'd have done it on the stairs, and far more cheaply."

She looked at the door. The boy was gone from beyond it; she heard nothing in the walls but the ordinary murmur of pipes and settling stone, and beneath that—the dead were quieter here, she realized. Muffled. As though the room itself had been padded against them.

That frightened her more than the candles did.

She sat.

* * *

"You've been following my people," the Tallowman said, pouring. "Three days. You're better at it than most, worse than you think. On the second day you stood under the eave of the chandler's shop for the length of a full bell while Rennet counted coins inside. He saw your reflection in the brass scale. He thought about telling the wardens."

"Why didn't he?"

"Because wardens ask questions, and questions cost time, and time is the only inventory I cannot replace." He slid the cup toward her. "Also because I told him you'd come eventually, and that when you did, he was to lose himself in a crowd and let you follow. Which he did. Which brings us to now."

Lena did not touch the tea. "You wanted me here."

"I wanted to meet the woman who has been asking after soul-contracts in every gin-house and back-alley chapel between here and the Ford. Asking loudly. Asking badly." He folded his boiled hands. "Do you know what happens to people who ask those questions loudly, carver?"

"They get invited to tea."

"They disappear," he said pleasantly, "usually within the week, usually into the mountain, occasionally into a river if the mountain is full. It is a remarkably efficient system, because it requires almost no effort on my part. Fear does the sorting. I merely keep the books." He tilted his head. "And yet here you sit, asking away for days, and no warden has touched you, and no river has taken you, and I confess that puzzled me enough to spend a candle or two on it. So I looked you up."

Something cold walked down Lena's spine and settled between her shoulder blades.

"In the ledger," the Tallowman said, and turned it around.

* * *

It was a beautiful object. That was the third insult—that something so monstrous should be so lovely. Saltwood boards, vellum pages gone cream with age, ink in two colors: black for the living entries, red for the settled ones. Columns ruled straight as a hangman's drop. And down the margins, in a small precise hand, names. Hundreds of names. Thousands.

"Every contract I hold," the Tallowman said. "Or held. The court beneath keeps the originals; I keep the working copy. A broker who doesn't know his own book is a broker about to be somebody else's entry." He tapped a page near the middle. "Yours is twenty years back. Go on. Read it. I find people prefer to read their own ruin themselves. Secondhand ruin never sits right."

Lena's hands were steady. She noted that with distant surprise, the way one notes rain beginning—something happening to the world, not to her.

She read.

**Contract of Surety — Trial of Maren Voss, convicted of Unlicensed Working, seventh day of the Purge Year.**

**Collateral pledged: one (1) soul, minor, Nakamura, L., age eleven (11), ward of the condemned.**

**Terms: Should the condemned attempt escape, appeal, or working of any art during trial or execution, the collateral soul passes in full to the Court Beneath, there to be held as currency, redeemed or extinguished at the court's discretion.**

**Should the condemned proceed quietly to judgment, the collateral is released unclaimed.**

**Status:** —and here the ink changed color, red as the inside of a mouth— **Settled. Released unclaimed. Note: surety renewed annually by same hand, terms identical, twenty consecutive years. Renewal current.**

Lena read it twice. Three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"Renewed," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's, someone older. "Every year. By whose hand?"

"The condemned's." The Tallowman refilled his own cup, unhurried. "Maren Voss came to me herself, the week before her arrest. Sat where you're sitting. Drank the tea, actually—you'd have liked her, she had opinions about it. She asked what it would take to make one specific child invisible to the court. Not protected—invisible. No contract, no claim, no entry, no interest. Nothing for anyone to want." He smiled faintly. "I told her the truth, which is that invisibility isn't free. It's collateral. The court doesn't give discounts; it gives deferrals. Her life for yours, year over year, so long as she walked quietly to the block and made no fuss and taught no one anything dangerous in the meantime."

"She taught," Lena said. "She taught me everything."

"Yes. Quietly. In a cellar. With chalk that wiped away clean." He shrugged, a small economical motion, a man balancing figures. "The court allowed it. Curious, isn't it? A woman convicted of teaching forbidden things, permitted to teach one student in secret for years. You never wondered why the wardens never found you? Why the raids passed your street? Why, in twenty years of carving charms in an outlaw city, no one official ever once looked twice at you?"

"I was careful," Lena said. "I was good at hiding."

"You were purchased," the Tallowman said, gently, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "There is a difference, and the difference is in this book. Every year on the anniversary of her arrest, Maren Voss walked down those stairs and signed her name and renewed your surety, and every year the court agreed to go on not seeing you. You weren't hidden, carver. You were *held*. There's a word for property nobody's claimed yet, and it isn't 'free.'"

The candles burned in their perfect rows. Somewhere above, muffled through floors of stone and salt, Verrin went on shouting and selling and surviving, and none of it reached this room, and neither did the dead, and Lena understood suddenly why the silence here felt padded: because rooms like this one were where the dead were *made*. Of course they couldn't get in. This was the workshop.

"Maren let them execute her," Lena said slowly, "to keep me off the books."

"Maren traded her death for your obscurity. Whether that's the same thing depends on how sentimental you are." The Tallowman drank his tea. "She also, I'll note since you'll learn it anyway, bargained harder than anyone I've dealt with before or since. She got the renewal clause written in blood-ink so it couldn't be forged. She got a rider that your name could never be sold onward without her signature—which died with her, technically, but the court honors dead signatures; they're sentimental about permanence. She got—" he paused, and for the first time something flickered across the uncle-face, something that might have been respect, "—she got twenty years for you out of a single execution. I have kings in this book who bought less with whole provinces."

"And now she's dead," Lena said, "and the renewal—"

"—is current through this season. After that, the surety lapses, and Nakamura, L., becomes an unencumbered asset in an open market." He set down his cup. "Which brings us, at last, to the interesting question. Because here is what puzzles me, carver. You've spent weeks pulling at threads—my runners, my contracts, the child. Every thread you pull frays toward the mountain. Any sensible person would stop. But you're not sensible, are you? You're the student of Maren Voss, and Maren Voss was the least sensible person ever to sit in that chair. So before I decide whether you leave this room as a customer, a corpse, or a complication—I'd like to know what you think you're doing."

Lena looked at the ledger. At her own name, small and neat in its column, eleven years old forever on the page. At the red word *Settled*, which was supposed to mean safe and actually meant *deferred*, which meant *not yet*, which meant that her whole careful, hidden, vigilant life had been a debt someone else was paying down in installments of blood.

Twenty years of watching doorways. Twenty years of carving charms that did nothing, hearing voices she could never admit to, flinching from boots on stairs. She had built her whole self on the belief that she had survived by being small and quick and clever—that her freedom was her own work, hard-won, hers.

It was rented. It had always been rented, and the rent had been paid by a woman walking up these stairs every year to sign away her ghost.

The grief that rose in her then wasn't sharp. It was enormous and slow, like a tide coming in over ground she'd thought was solid.

"I'm going to the mountain," she heard herself say. "For the child. Isla. Her soul was sold twice, and I'm going to bring it back."

The Tallowman regarded her for a long moment. Then he did something she had not expected: he laughed, softly, genuinely, shaking his head like a man hearing a familiar song.

"Of course you are. She would have too." He closed the ledger with a sound like a kiss. "Well. Here is my problem, carver. Everything you intend to do is catastrophic for my business. The court falls, my contracts void, twenty years of inventory walks out the gate as smoke. I ought to drown you in the harbor tonight and sleep beautifully." He laced his fingers. "But."

"But."

"But the dragon is guttering. You may not know that yet—the court keeps it quiet, even from its brokers. When it goes dark, every soul in that vault extinguishes. Permanently. No redemption, no resale, no recovery. Including mine." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "People assume I keep my own soul somewhere safe. Where? I'm the bookkeeper. The book stays with the books." He pushed back his chair and stood, and up close his eyes were tired in a way that no uncle's face should be. "So. You want to break into the mountain. I know the mountain. I know the gates, the schedules, which clerks can be bribed and which can only be robbed. And unlike you, I can walk in the front door."

"Why would you help me?"

"Because if the court dies, I die with it—and if it lives, it will eventually come collecting on every deferred account in this book, including yours, including, when the renewals lapse, everyone you love. Either way the ledger ends. The only question left is whether it ends *closed* or ends *burned*." He extended his boiled-clean hand across the desk. "Help me burn it, carver, and I'll tear your page out myself. Free and clear. The only honest transaction I'll ever have made."

Lena stared at the hand. Behind her, the padded door. Above her, the oblivious city. In her chest, twenty years of borrowed safety settling over her like ash.

Somewhere in the walls—or maybe only in memory, or maybe in the place where Maren had lived and now lived differently—a voice she could not quite hear seemed to be saying something urgent, something that sounded terribly like *don't*, or possibly like *go*, and she would never know which, and that was its own kind of wound.

She did not take his hand.

"I don't trust you," she said.

"Correct," said the Tallowman. "Neither do I. It's the strongest foundation I know." He withdrew the hand, unoffended, and began snuffing candles with a brass snuffer, one by one, each flame dying with a tiny civilized sigh. "You have until the season turns before your surety lapses, and rather less than that before the dragon does. My runner will show you the corpse-road gate at low tide tomorrow. After that we're partners, not friends—do try to keep the distinction clear."

At the last candle, in the half-dark, Lena found her voice again, small and level:

"When Maren came here. That last time. Did she know? About the dragon? About how it would end?"

The Tallowman paused, snuffer in hand, and for a moment the uncle's face was gone entirely and something much older looked out of it, something that had watched twenty years of desperate people sign themselves away and kept the books balanced anyway.

"She asked me that exact question," he said. "First thing, before she even sat down. And I told her the fire had maybe thirty years in it, and she said—" he set the snuffer down with great care, "—she said, 'Then it will outlive me, and that's all I need. Let it be someone else's arithmetic.'"

He snuffed the final candle, and the dark came down like a page turning, and in it his voice, dry as dust in a ledger's spine:

"Congratulations, carver. It's someone else's arithmetic. It's yours."
