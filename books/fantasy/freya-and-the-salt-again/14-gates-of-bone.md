## 14. Gates of Bone

The mountain announced itself long before we saw it.

We had been walking the corpse-roads for six days—old paths that ran beneath the salt-flats like veins beneath skin, paved with stones that no one would admit to quarrying from graves—and on the sixth morning the air changed. It went thin and sweet, the way air goes in a room where someone has just died, and Isla stopped in the middle of the road and pointed with her whole arm, the way small children point, as if her finger alone were not enough to hold the thing she was seeing.

The mountain did not look like a mountain. It looked like a tooth. A single vast molar rising out of the ash-plain, white at the crown where snow should have been but wasn't—white because it was bone, all of it bone, weathered and pitted and faintly translucent in the morning light so that when the sun hit its western face I could swear I saw shapes moving inside it, slow as fish under ice.

"Well," said Hale.

He had not said much in six days. He was a man who rationed his words the way a miser rations coin, and the road had spent him. His magistrate's coat was gone—he'd burned it at the ford, along with everything else he'd been—and what remained of him wore a smuggler's oilskin three sizes too big and an expression I can only describe as a man discovering that the floor of his life had been painted on and there was nothing underneath.

"That's not stone," he said, when neither of us answered.

"No," I said.

"It's—"

"Bone. Yes."

Isla tugged my sleeve. She looked up at me and then at the mountain and then back at me, and her mouth worked around a word that wouldn't come. Six days of travel had loosened something in her; she spoke now in fragments, half First Tongue and half the common speech she was only beginning to remember how to use, and every fragment landed somewhere in my chest and stayed there, warm and heavy as a stone from a sun-warmed wall.

"Big," she managed finally.

"Very big," I agreed.

"Scary?"

"A bit, yes."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge reviewing a sentence, then took my hand anyway, which I understood to mean: *a bit scary is acceptable if you are coming too.*

I had been hearing the dead for twenty years, and in all that time they had never once been quiet. They murmured in walls. They muttered in doorframes. They followed me down streets complaining about the cold, the living, the price of candles, the ingratitude of grandchildren. The corpse-roads, though—the corpse-roads were loud in a way I had never experienced. The dead here did not murmur. They *sang*. Low and wordless and endless, a sound like wind through a reed bed, and the closer we came to the mountain the more the song resolved into something with structure, with verses, with—

With a warning.

"They know we're coming," I said.

Hale's hand went to the knife at his belt, which was a habit I found almost touching, since a knife had never once helped anyone against the dead. "Wardens?"

"Worse. Hosts." I listened harder, tilting my head the way Maren used to tilt hers when she was parsing a difficult text. "There are things between us and the gate that were people once. The court keeps them. Guards them. They're—" I searched for a word that wouldn't frighten Isla and failed to find one. "They're the gate's teeth."

Isla squeezed my hand very hard.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pillar—a pillar, mind you, thicker than Ode's entire warehouse, lying on its side in the ash like a felled tree, carved end to end with names. Not decorative carving. Names. Thousands upon thousands of them, cut deep and filled with something dark, and I caught myself reading them by the light of our small fire before I remembered that reading the names of the dead aloud, even silently, even to yourself, was a kind of invitation.

I read them anyway. Some habits are older than caution.

Hale sat across the fire sharpening a knife he would never use against anything that mattered, and watched me do it, and after a while he said, "You knew my teacher."

It was not a question. He had been circling toward it for six days the way a dog circles a wound.

"Maren Voss," I said. "Yes."

"I executed her."

"You've mentioned." Twice. Once at the ford, once at the village where the hollow people smiled at us with their empty faces and offered us soup they couldn't remember why they'd made. Hale confessed the way other men bled: involuntarily, repeatedly, and with visible distress.

"It doesn't weigh less for mentioning it," he said.

"No," I said. "It won't. That's rather the point of it."

He looked at me across the fire. In the firelight he looked older than he had in his office, older than he had on the road, older perhaps than he actually was, which I suspect is the truest way any of us ever look. "Do you want me to say I'm sorry? Properly? On my knees, with the right words in the right order?"

"I want you to mean whatever you say," I told him. "The order of the words has never once saved anybody."

That shut him up for a while. Good. Silence, on the whole, was Hale's best quality.

Isla slept curled against my side with her thumb in her mouth, which she did only when she thought no one could see, and in her sleep she spoke—not common words, not yet, but the First Tongue, running through it the way other children run through their letters. *Ama. Sess. Vaelin-dur.* Small syllables. Door-sounds. Window-sounds. The sounds the dead made when they were talking to each other about ordinary things, about bread and weather and who had failed to visit whom.

Listening to her, I felt the grief come up in me the way groundwater comes up in a cellar—slowly, from everywhere at once, unstoppable. Because I knew what was coming. I had known since the Tallowman's cellar, since the ledger, since Maren's confession in the dark of the road. To get Isla's soul back, I would have to pay in the only coin the court accepted. And the coin was *me*.

Twenty years of voices. Twenty years of the dead keeping me company in a city that would have hanged me for them. Maren's voice, dry and exact, correcting my grammar in the First Tongue even in death. My mother's voice, humming. The old man from the shop next door who'd died in the Purge's first winter and had never once stopped telling me where the good clay was.

All of it, traded away. And afterward I would walk out of that mountain hollow as a woman who had never heard anything at all.

I looked at Isla's sleeping face and found, to my mild surprise, that the arithmetic still came out the same. It came out the same every time I checked. That was the terrible thing about love, I was learning. It didn't make the price smaller. It just made you willing.

* * *

The gate took the whole next day to reach, though it was never more than a mile away. That is the sort of thing a mountain made of bone can do to distance. We walked toward it and the road bent and the ash shifted and the gate receded politely, the way a host recedes ahead of a guest, letting you arrive at your own pace, and by midday I understood that the mountain was deciding whether to let us approach at all.

The dead sang louder. And among the singing, individual voices began to separate out, the way instruments separate from an orchestra when you stand close enough, and some of those voices were saying my name.

*Nakamura. Nakamura-of-the-carvings. Nakamura-who-hears.*

"They're talking about you," Isla said, startled, in her broken common. Then, catching herself, in the First Tongue: *They know your name.*

"The dead know everyone's name," I said. "It's knowing anything else about them that's hard."

But that wasn't quite true, and Isla's look told me she knew it wasn't quite true, and I sighed and gave her the better answer. "My teacher's name is woven through this road. Everything she touched, the dead remember. I think—I think part of why we've gotten this far is that they remember her walking it. And they remember me walking behind her. Twenty years ago. When I was barely older than you."

Isla absorbed this. Then, with the devastating practicality of the young: *Was she nice?*

"She was infuriating," I said, and found I was smiling. "She was brilliant and stubborn and she hid kindness inside criticism the way other people hide medicine inside honey. She once made me re-carve the same charm forty times because my lines were 'morally sloppy.' I hated her for a month. I would give—" I stopped. Careful, Lena. "She was the best person I ever knew."

*I miss her,* Isla said simply, in the First Tongue, and I had to stop walking for a moment and pretend to adjust my boot, because a child who had never met Maren Voss missed her, carried her in the language Maren had died protecting, and I was not going to be able to explain to anyone, least of all myself, why that undid me so thoroughly.

* * *

We reached the gate at dusk.

I had imagined doors. I don't know why—habit, probably. Every important thing in Verrin came behind doors, locked and warded and legal. But the gate of the mountain court was not a door. It was a *mouth*.

An archway, yes, technically—an archway sixty feet high, carved from a single curve of femur that must have belonged to something no honest history had ever recorded—but the archway opened onto darkness, and the darkness breathed. Slowly. In and out. The whole mountain breathed around it, a tide of air that smelled of ash and candle-tallow and, underneath, faintly, of the sea.

And the surface of the gate—every inch of it, floor to impossible height—was carved with faces.

Not names this time. Faces. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, overlapping, interlocking, each face no bigger than my palm, each one distinct, each one *watching*. Old faces, young faces, faces mid-laugh and mid-grief and mid-word. Souls, I realized. Rendered in bone. The court kept its accounts on its own front door.

Hale stood beside me and did not reach for his knife, which I counted as growth. "Those are—"

"People," I said. "Or what's left of the ones the court holds. It's a ledger. It's showing off."

"And the riddle?" Hale had heard the stories too. Everyone had. The mountain gate asks, and only the First Tongue may answer, and a hundred smugglers' tales explained why no army had ever breached it, why the Purge's soldiers had stood before it for a year and gone home.

As if the gate had been waiting for us to ask, the singing stopped.

All of it. At once. The silence was so total I could hear Isla's breath, quick and shallow, and my own heartbeat, and far below us—or perhaps far within us—the slow guttering pulse of the dragon-fire, weaker than a candle in a draft.

Then a voice came out of the gate. It did not boom. That was the worst of it. It was a reasonable voice, a clerk's voice, dry and courteous, and it spoke in the common tongue first, so that Hale and I would understand, and then repeated itself in the First Tongue, so that Isla would understand, and the two versions were identical:

**WHAT IS THE WEIGHT OF A SOUL?**

Hale exhaled slowly. "That's it? That's the famous riddle?"

"It's not a riddle," I said quietly. "It's a question. There's a difference. Riddles have tricks. Questions have truths." I stared up at the ten thousand watching faces. "And it's asked in both tongues, which means it wants both kinds of answer. The dead answer in the First Tongue. The living answer in common. And the gate knows the difference."

"What do we do?"

"We let Isla answer."

Hale turned to stare at me. "She's six."

"She's the only one of us whose soul has actually been weighed on their scales," I said. "She's the only one who knows the answer from the inside. And she's the only one who can speak the First Tongue without—" I hesitated. "Without borrowing it. Mine comes through the dead. Hers is native. The gate will hear the difference."

*Isla.* I crouched down to her height, which put her eye level with roughly four hundred carved souls, none of whom blinked, because they couldn't. *You hear the question?*

She nodded. Her chin was trembling. She pressed herself against my legs and buried her face in my coat, and for a long moment I thought she simply wouldn't be able to do it, and I was already turning over alternatives—there were no alternatives, but I was turning them over anyway, the way you do—when she lifted her head.

*Will it hurt me?*

The question went through me like a hook. *No, little one. It's a door, not a trap. Doors only want to know who's knocking.*

*The bad men knocked,* she said. *The men who sold me. They knocked and it opened for them.*

*Because they brought money,* I said. *You're bringing yourself. That's different. That's the whole difference, Isla. That's maybe the only difference that has ever mattered.*

She looked at the gate for a long time. The breathing darkness breathed. Somewhere far above, ash sifted down through the windless air, settling on our shoulders like a blessing or a burial.

Then she stepped forward—one step, out of the shelter of my coat, into the open, alone, six years old and smaller than the smallest carved face on that enormous gate—and she lifted her chin the way Maren used to lift hers before delivering an opinion she knew would be unpopular, and she answered.

In the First Tongue, her voice clear and high and carrying, she said:

*A soul weighs what it loves.*

Silence.

Then, in her new, clumsy, brave common words, looking up at the gate as if it were merely a very large adult who ought to be able to follow along: "A soul weighs what it loves. So it's heavy. Mine is heavy. You have to give it back."

Nothing happened.

For three heartbeats, four, five, nothing happened, and I felt Hale go rigid beside me, and I felt my own hope begin the familiar slide toward ruin—

And then every carved face on the gate closed its eyes.

Ten thousand souls, all at once, in a wave that rolled up the archway from the ground like wind through wheat, and in the darkness beyond the gate a sound began—not the clerk's voice, not the singing, but something older, a groan of recognition, of *acknowledgment*, the sound of a lock that has waited a very long time to be turned by the right key.

The darkness breathed out.

And the gate opened—not swinging, but *unclenching*, the archway widening, the carved faces parting to either side like a crowd making way, and the path beyond was lit with a dim gold light that flickered unsteadily, the way fire flickers when it is nearly out of things to burn.

Isla backed up quickly and grabbed my hand again, job done, key delivered, terrifying errand complete, and looked up at me with an expression of enormous dignity undermined entirely by the fact that she was shaking.

*Did I do it right?*

"You did it perfectly," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I decided not to care.

Hale drew a breath, squared his shoulders inside his borrowed oilskin, and looked into the gold-lit dark with the expression of a man walking into his own trial—which, I supposed, in every way that mattered, he was.

"After you," he said.

"No," I said, taking Isla's hand tighter. "After *her*. She's the key. Keys go first."

And Isla, the smallest of us, the sold-twice child, the last speaker of a dying tongue, walked into the mountain that held her soul hostage—and the gate of bone breathed us in behind her, and began, softly, to close.
