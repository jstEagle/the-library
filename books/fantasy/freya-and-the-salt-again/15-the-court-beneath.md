## 15. The Court Beneath

*Lena*

The gate closed behind them like a book shutting.

Lena felt it in her teeth first—a soft, enormous click—and then in the air, which changed from mountain-cold to something warmer and older, the temperature of a room that had been occupied for a very long time by someone who never opened a window. The corridor beyond the gate was lit by veins of pale light running through the walls themselves, soul-light, she realized, the glow of held things. It made her skin look the color of candle wax.

Isla's hand found hers without either of them looking. That was the thing about the child: she did not grab in panic. She simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and you adjusted.

"Stay close," Hale said. He had his magistrate's voice on—the one he probably used to tell families their petitions were denied—but underneath it Lena could hear the other voice, the one from the ford, the one that had shouted *she's mine to protect* at men with crossbows. "And whatever you see down here, whatever they offer you—don't sign anything."

"You think I'd sign something?" Lena said.

"I've watched you haggle with smugglers, Nakamura. You'd sign something."

"That was one time, and Ode was robbing me."

"He was giving you a fair price," Hale said, "and you talked him down anyway. I respect it. I also fear it."

Despite everything—the bone gate at their backs, the light of stolen souls in the walls, the fact that somewhere below them a dragon was burning down to its last embers—Lena almost laughed. That was the strange arithmetic of terror, she was learning. It left room for small absurdities the way a flood leaves furniture floating.

They descended. The corridor spiraled, and the dead walked beside them—not visibly, not the way Maren had been visible to Lena alone, but as a pressure, a crowd-sense, hundreds upon hundreds of presences pressed into the stone like flowers between the pages of an enormous ledger. And for the first time since the auction of herself that she didn't yet know she'd lost, Lena heard them clearly. Not murmurs. Words.

*They came down,* the dead said, in the First Tongue, in Isla's language, in the language of everyone who had ever died with something unfinished. *They came down the wrong way, but they came.*

"The wrong way?" Lena whispered.

"There's a proper entrance," said a voice that was not Hale's and not Isla's. It came from the wall itself, dry as paper. "Ambassadors use the Stair of Petition. You've come through the Bone Gate, which is how debtors arrive." A pause, with the quality of someone adjusting spectacles. "You are not ambassadors, are you."

"No," Lena said.

"Debtors, then. How refreshing. Everyone who comes through the Gate is a debtor. It saves so much paperwork."

* * *

*Hale*

Corvan Hale had spent twenty years believing he understood what law was. Law was ink. Law was procedure, forms filed in triplicate, a gallows built to specification, a hood, a drumroll, a body cut down after the customary hour. Law was the Purge, and the Purge had been signed, and signatures made things real. He had told himself this for twenty years because the alternative was to lie down in the street and never get up.

Then he had walked into the court beneath the mountain, and Corvan Hale learned that he had never once seen law in his life. He had seen its shadow on a cave wall. This—this was law itself, awake, and it was worse than anything he had ever done in its name.

The chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell. It was vast beyond sense, tiered in descending rings, and every ring was filled with desks. Not thrones—desks. Thousands of desks, arranged in concentric circles going down and down toward a central well of darkness, and at every desk sat a figure that had perhaps once been a person. They were gray and translucent, worn smooth like river stones, and they moved with the terrible efficiency of things that had been doing one task for centuries. Some stamped documents. Some weighed coins that were not coins. Some wrote, endlessly, with pens that scratched like insects.

And everywhere, being traded, being counted, being stacked in little clay jars with labels tied on with hair: souls.

Hale had seen the slave markets before the Purge, in his youth. He had told himself those were abolished, that the Purge had been about magic, about safety, about order. He saw now where the trade had gone. Down. Inward. The market hadn't died; it had gotten a bureaucracy.

"They're all clerks," Lena said beside him, hushed. "The whole court. It's all just—" She gestured helplessly at the tiers. "Filing."

"The dead are patient," said their guide, materializing out of the wall-light: a gray figure with the remnant of a clerk's green sash still faintly visible across its chest, like a stain of color remembered rather than seen. "Patience is our chief qualification. That, and we no longer require wages."

"And the dragon?" Hale asked. His voice came out flat. He made it flat on purpose. If he let it shake, he would hear everything he was feeling in it, and he could not afford that yet.

The clerk tilted its head. "Ah. You've come about the furnace."

"The furnace," Lena repeated dangerously.

"What else would you call it? A fire must be fed or banked. Ours is fed." The clerk said it the way a man reports rainfall. "This way, please. The Registrar will want to see you. Or will want a form filled out stating that you saw him. The distinction has never mattered to anyone but him."

* * *

*Isla*

Isla did not have words for most of what she saw, in either language. But she had been to this place before—not with her feet, with the other part of her, the part her mother and father had sold when the plague came and the charms failed and the Tallowman had sat in their kitchen counting out coins that would buy medicine. She had been here in dreams, every night since, standing in the dark well at the center of everything while gray hands measured her.

She knew the sound the desks made. She knew the smell—old paper and cold smoke. She knew that if you stood too long in one place, a clerk would notice you and begin writing your name, and once your name was written here, you became easier to hold.

So she did the thing she had learned in dreams. She pulled Lena's hand, and then Hale's, and made herself small and quick, threading between the desk-rows while the two tall people argued in whispers about whether to follow the clerk.

A gray figure looked up from its ledger as she passed. Its face was kind, in the way of faces that have forgotten why kindness matters but remember the shape. It looked at her, and then at the jar on its desk, and Isla saw—with the sight the mountain had given her—that the jar had her name on it. ISLA. SOLD TWICE. PENDING.

Pending. That word again. Pending meant not-yet. Pending meant there was still a door.

She squeezed Lena's hand twice, hard, the signal they had made on the road. *Run.*

* * *

*Lena*

They ran—or walked very fast with dignity, which in Verrin amounted to the same thing—following Isla's tugs down through the tiers while the clerk-guide drifted behind them making sounds of mild administrative distress. "You are missing your appointment," it called. "There will be a notation. There is always a notation."

The tiers ended at the well. And the well—

Lena stopped, and forgot, briefly, how to be afraid, because some things are too large for fear and leave only awe.

The last dragon lay coiled at the bottom of the world.

It was smaller than the stories. Everything true is smaller than the stories, and sadder. It was the color of ash over embers, and along the whole length of its chained body ran lines of dull red light, pulsing slow, like breath, like a heartbeat counting down. Chains the size of ships' cables ran from its limbs up into the rock, and where each chain met the dragon's skin, the light dimmed, as though the chains were drinking it.

Every pulse of that light, Lena understood without knowing how she understood, was a soul being burned as fuel. The court above lived on the light. The light lived on the held dead. When the dragon went dark, everything the court held would go dark with it.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor old thing."

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of cooling iron, and it found her across the distance with the unhurried attention of something that had watched empires be born and filed away. Lena felt it look *through* her—felt it find the place where twenty years of voices used to live and found, to its evident surprise, that the shelves were empty.

Its nostril flared. Smoke curled out, thin and gray.

*Empty,* said a voice like a landslide deciding to be polite. *They took your ghosts, little carver. And still you came down here for a child's soul. Interesting. The court will find that interesting. The court finds everything interesting except mercy.*

"Can you hear me?" Lena whispered.

*I can hear everything. It is my curse and my employment.* The great eye slid past her, to Isla, and something in the dragon's ancient face changed—the smallest softening, a mountain deciding not to avalanche today. *Hello, twice-sold one. You should not have come back. And yet I am glad. It has been four hundred years since anyone came here glad to see me.*

"We're going to free you," Hale said. He had come up beside them, and his voice had that flatness again, but his hands, Lena noticed, were fists. "Or die trying. Whichever the paperwork allows."

*Paperwork,* the dragon rumbled, and the sound might have been laughter. *Oh, the magistrates never change. Four hundred years, and they still think the chains are the problem.*

"Aren't they?"

*The chains are the symptom. The ledger is the disease. Break the chains while the ledger stands, and the court writes new ones. Burn the ledger—* the eye gleamed, ember-bright— *and even a dying fire can make its own light, for a moment. Long enough.*

"Long enough for what?" Lena asked.

But the dragon's eye was closing again, its pulse slowing, and the clerk-guide had caught up, and behind it came others—a dozen gray figures descending the tiers in formation, and among them, borne on a litter of black wood, something that had once been grander than a clerk.

* * *

*Hale*

The Registrar was the oldest dead thing Hale had ever seen, and Hale had once shaken hands with a lich-mayor at a municipal conference, so the bar was high.

Where the clerks were worn smooth, the Registrar was worn *deep*—a figure like a carving left out in rain for centuries, all hollows and suggestion, draped in robes that might have been cloth or might have been condensed shadow. On its lap rested a ledger. The ledger was the only truly solid object in the entire cavern. Hale felt its solidity the way you feel a magnet near iron: the whole court oriented around that book, every desk, every chain, every jar of souls. The ledger was the mountain's heart, and the dragon was only its clock.

"Petitioners," the Registrar said. Its voice was dry and vast, like wind through a courthouse. "Through the Bone Gate. Unregistered. Unannounced." A hollow pause. "Delightful. We so rarely get irregularities. Regularity is, after all, our entire function."

Hale stepped forward. Every instinct trained into him over twenty years said: bow, state your office, establish hierarchy. He let the instinct pass through him and kept walking.

"My name is Corvan Hale. Magistrate of Verrin. Formerly." He let *formerly* sit in the air. "I'm here to enter a claim."

The Registrar's hollow head tilted. Around the tiers, thousands of gray clerks paused their pens at once, and the silence was enormous.

"A claim," the Registrar said.

"On behalf of the souls held in this court. All of them. Their detention is unlawful under statutes older than your ledger—I've read them. The Concord of Ash. The Rite of Quiet Passing. The First Covenant, if it comes to that." Hale's voice did not shake. He had spent twenty years perfecting the voice that does not shake while signing death warrants; it turned out the same voice worked for this. "I am the ranking officer of living law present. I formally surrender myself to this court as a prisoner, in exchange for a stay of proceedings against the child Isla, pending review of her contract."

Lena made a small strangled sound beside him. He did not look at her. If he looked at her, he would lose the thread.

"You offer yourself," the Registrar said slowly, "as collateral."

"As a prisoner. Collateral implies I expect to be redeemed." Now he did glance sideways—at Lena, at the small gray-eyed girl clutching her hand, at the ember-gutted god below them all. "I don't. I know exactly what I'm worth. I executed an innocent woman twenty years ago and called it law. Whatever this court does with me, it cannot be worse than what I've done with myself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, faintly, the dragon's pulse beat slower.

"The court accepts," said the Registrar, and a dozen clerks began writing at once. "Prisoner Hale. Your confession is noted, timestamped, and—forgive us—filed. Proceedings regarding the child's contract will convene at the next session." The hollow eyes turned, and Hale felt them like cold water. "Which is soon. The furnace dims, petitioners. When the last light gutters, all accounts must be settled. Debts forgiven—or collected."

"And if the debts are collected?" Lena asked.

The Registrar smiled, or performed the memory of smiling.

"Then everything the court holds," it said, "is extinguished. Beginning, I should think, with the lot currently marked *pending*."

On the nearest desk, in its little labeled jar, the soul called ISLA flickered like a candle in a draft.
