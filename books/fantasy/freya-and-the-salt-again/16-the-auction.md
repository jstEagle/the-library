## 16. The Auction

The court beneath the mountain smelled of tallow and old paper, which Lena had not expected. She had expected brimstone, or at least something grander than the smell of a clerk's office left too long in the sun. But the Court Beneath was, above all things, a bureaucracy, and bureaucracies everywhere smelled the same: candle grease, dust, ink gone brown with age, and beneath it all a faint sweetness like fruit rotting in a locked room.

The sweetness was the souls.

They were kept in jars. That was the detail that undid her—not the scale of it, not the cold grandeur of the hall with its pillars of soul-bone polished to a wet shine, but the sheer domesticity of the shelves. Row upon row of them, rising into dark she couldn't measure, each jar holding a curl of light no bigger than a moth. Some of the lights moved. Some pressed against the glass the way a cat presses against a window, watching weather it cannot touch.

Lena made herself stop looking at the shelves. If she looked too long she would start reading names off the labels, and if she started reading names she would start weeping, and if she started weeping the clerks would notice, and clerks noticed everything. It was their whole nature. They had been dead so long that noticing was all they had left.

"Lot forty-one," said the crier.

His voice was dry as a page turning. He stood on a dais of black salt before a lectern carved from a single vertebra—dragon vertebra, Lena realized, from the size of it, from the way her teeth ached when she looked at it—and behind him, on a pedestal of glass, burned a small light the color of dawn through sea-ice.

Isla's soul.

The girl herself sat between Lena and Hale on a bench of bone, very still, very small, her hands folded in her lap the way Maren used to fold hers when waiting for a customer to make up his mind. Isla had not spoken since they came through the gate. She didn't need to. Her light was up there on the pedestal, and every time the crier's gavel tapped the lectern, the light flinched, and Isla flinched with it, a small synchronized shiver, and Lena's chest did something structural and painful each time.

"Forty-one," the crier repeated. "One soul, juvenile, recently bonded. Provenance: plague-year contract, Verrin district, brokered by the Tallowman." A pause, the dry rustle of a ledger leaf. "Condition: twice sold. Title disputed."

"Title is *not* disputed," hissed the Tallowman.

He stood three lots down the bench, wrapped in his coat of rendered fat and yellow wax, and he had been objecting in a low furious monotone for an hour. Twice sold meant the court's own rules gave him only half-claim, and half-claims auctioned poorly, and the Tallowman had come down the mountain expecting profit and found instead a courtroom full of dead accountants preparing to nickel-and-dime him into eternity.

"The first sale lapsed," the crier said, without inflection. "The bond was never sealed. The second sale is valid but contested by present claim of kin."

"I'm not her kin," snapped the Tallowman.

"No," agreed the crier. "But she is present, and she has not consented to you."

Lena felt Hale go rigid beside her. She understood why. *Present* and *has not consented* were words with weight here, words that belonged to a justice older than any law, older than the Purge, older than Verrin itself. In this court, a child who could speak—even in a tongue the clerks pretended not to know—was not property. She was a party to the dispute.

Which meant there would be a hearing. And hearings took time. And time was the one thing the mountain did not have.

Through the high ribs of the cavern ceiling, far above, Lena could feel it: a warmth fading. Not heat exactly—more like standing near a hearth in another room, knowing the fire was dying without seeing it. The dragon. Chapter after chapter of her life had led her down corpse-roads and across black rivers to this bench, and now she could feel the last dragon's fire guttering like a candle in a draft, and every soul on those shelves dimmed a little when it did.

"How long," she murmured to Hale.

He didn't look at her. He had spent the descent transforming himself, shedding the magistrate layer by layer—the seal, the posture, the voice that ended arguments—and what remained was a tired man in a good coat who kept touching the empty scabbard at his hip as though checking whether his old life still weighed anything. "The fire fails within a day," he said quietly. "Perhaps less. When it goes—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. When it went, everything held by its light went with it. Every jar. Every moth-winged soul. Isla's dawn-colored flicker among them, extinguished not even cruelly, just incidentally, the way a lamp going out takes the whole room's shadow with it.

On the dais, the crier raised his gavel.

"Lot forty-one will be heard," he said. "Claims and bids. The court accepts coin, service, memory, or bone. The court does not accept promises."

*The court does not accept promises.* Lena turned the phrase over. It sounded like something Maren would have said about customers—a little dry humor worn smooth by repetition. Twenty years of hearing the dead murmur in walls, and she had never once imagined she'd miss them in advance.

She had planned for this. She had planned on the corpse-road, in Ode's smuggler's loft, in the grey hours before dawn while Isla slept against her shoulder. She had run the arithmetic a hundred times, and every time it came out the same, and every time she'd hoped she was wrong.

Coin: she had none worth naming. Service: she was a charm-carver whose charms did nothing; the dead had no use for nothing. Bone: the Tallowman had asked for a piece of her once, and she had refused, and she refused still—not out of courage but because a piece of herself felt survivable to lose, and this court deserved better than survivable.

That left memory.

* * *

The bidding opened with the Tallowman, naturally. He bid in grief-memory, a widow's last night with her husband, twenty years aged and mellowing nicely, and the clerks along the walls stirred like moths and wrote it down. He bid a farmer's childhood summers. He bid a mother's face. Each lot he offered had been pried out of some desperate living soul above, and each one gleamed on the scales the clerks brought forth—actual scales, brass and bone, weighing light against light—and the needle swung toward him a little further with every lot.

Hale leaned close. "I can offer myself," he whispered. "Imprisonment. Enforcement. They're always short of hands that can walk in the sun."

"They'll take you and keep the auction running anyway," Lena said. "You heard the crier. This lot gets heard today. They don't want the girl, Corvan. They want the *precedent*. A living claimant walking in here and winning—that's a crack in the floor they've been polishing for centuries."

"Then what do we—"

"I know what I have," Lena said. "I've known since the ford."

And she had. She had known, and she had hated knowing, and she had carried the knowledge down the mountain road like a stone sewn into her coat, taking it out at night to look at it, putting it away again, telling herself there would be another way. There wasn't. There was never another way. That was the lesson of the Purge, of the plague, of the charms that did nothing: you could carve all the protection in the world into a piece of bone and it would not stop one fevered child from being sold by parents who loved her and could not feed her.

"Lena Nakamura." The crier's voice cut across the hall, dry as salt. "You are named in the dispute. Will you state your claim?"

Every dead clerk in the hall turned. Not their bodies—their bodies stayed perfectly still, quills poised—but something in them turned, the way a field turns toward rain. Word had traveled, as word does among the dead. The woman who hears. The woman the First Tongue follows through gates of bone.

Lena stood. Her knees objected. She let them.

"I claim the soul of Isla," she said, "on grounds of keeping. She has been in my care since the storm-night she came to my door. I fed her. I hid her. I taught her three common words and she taught me eleven in the First Tongue, and I'll thank the court not to ask me to pronounce them because I'll do it wrong and embarrass us both."

A ripple went through the benches. Somewhere, a clerk made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter hadn't been taxed out of them centuries ago.

"The court recognizes grounds of keeping," the crier said. "Grounds are weighed, not counted. State your bid."

Here it was, then. The arithmetic. She had done it a hundred times on the road and it always came out the same, and she had hoped, right up until this moment, that hope might count for something in a court of the dead.

It didn't. Nothing counted here but weight.

"I bid," Lena said, and her voice held, which surprised her, "twenty years of memory. All of it. Every voice I ever heard in the walls of Verrin. Every name in my ledger. Every conversation with the dead since the night of the Purge."

The hall went very quiet. Even the Tallowman stopped fidgeting.

"The court weighs memories by their living weight," the crier said. "Grief outweighs comfort. Love outweighs grief. Name the principal."

And there it was. The terrible arithmetic, laid out on the brass scales for everyone to see. She could not bid *the dead*, plural, vague, a category. The court wanted a name. One name, the heaviest one, the load-bearing wall of her entire haunted life.

"Maren," Lena said.

Saying it aloud in this place was like opening a door she had kept locked for twenty years. Maren Voss. Carver. Teacher. The woman who had taught her that a charm's power was in the giving, not the carving; who had hidden her students' names inside knotwork patterns when the wardens came; who had been hanged from the Salt Gate on a false order signed by the shaking hand of a young magistrate who now sat beside her on a bench of bone, wearing his guilt like a coat that no longer fit.

Maren, who had bargained away Lena's safety to save the First Tongue, and whom Lena had forgiven somewhere on the corpse-road, in the dark, without ceremony, the way forgiveness actually happens.

The scales were brought. A clerk—an old one, older than the others, his robes gone grey as ash—took a crystal vessel from the lectern and held it out to her.

"You understand the terms," he said. His voice, for the first time, was not dry. It was almost gentle. "When the trade completes, you will not remember her voice. You will not remember her face. You will not remember that there was ever a hole where she had been. You will walk out of this mountain lighter than you have been in twenty years, and you will not know why the lightness hurts."

"Yes," Lena said.

"You may find you regret—"

"I won't get the chance," she said. "That's rather the point."

The old clerk regarded her for a long moment. Then he inclined his head, a small precise motion, the first courtesy anyone in this court had shown the living, and held out the vessel.

Lena took it. It was warm. That was the worst part—it was warm, like a hand, like a kitchen, like every ordinary morning she had ever survived.

* * *

The Tallowman protested, of course. He was on his feet, wax cracking at the seams, voice climbing into registers that made the jar-lights tremble on their shelves. "Sentiment! The court weighs *value*, not sentiment! Twenty years of ghost-whispering from a provincial charlatan against certified, brokered, *documented* lots—"

"The court," said the crier, "weighs love above grief, and grief above comfort, and finds the lot before it consists principally of love." A pause. The gavel rose. "Also, broker, your title remains half-claimed and your paperwork is, frankly, an embarrassment."

The sound that went through the hall then was unmistakably laughter—dry, rustling, dead laughter, but laughter—and the Tallowman sat down as though his strings had been cut.

The scales were set. The vessel of Maren was placed on one pan. On the other, gently, the clerk lifted Isla's light from its pedestal, and the moment it left the glass the girl on the bench gasped—a small, whole, human sound, her first breath in hours—and pressed both hands over her heart as though catching something falling.

The needle swung.

It hung between them for a moment—love against love, a teacher's twenty years against a child's one dawn-colored soul—and Lena watched it hang and thought, absurdly, of her shop, of the frightened customers who bought charms that did nothing because they needed something to hold. *This,* she thought. *This is what holding looks like.*

The needle settled. Toward Isla. Toward the living.

"It is weighed," the crier said. "It is sufficient. Lot forty-one passes to the keeper, Lena Nakamura, by trade of principal memory, complete and irrevocable upon completion."

He raised the gavel to strike.

And the whole mountain shuddered.

Dust sifted from the rib-vaults overhead. The jar-lights flickered in unison, ten thousand moths dimming at once, and somewhere far above them all, deeper than the shelves, deeper than the dais, a sound rolled through the rock like the last log collapsing in a great hearth.

The dragon-fire, guttering.

The gavel did not fall. The crier looked up, and for the first time in what might have been centuries, the dry old face showed something alive: fear.

"The fire fails," he said. "Completion must be sealed *now*, or the lot extinguishes unsold, and the court—" his voice caught on the word, a dead man's horror— "*loses everything.*"

"Then seal it," Lena said.

The old clerk raised the vessel. Lena looked at it one last time—warm gold light swirling, and inside it, impossibly, briefly, a shape like a woman's silhouette raising a hand, the way Maren used to wave her off at the end of a lesson, *go on, go home, you've earned your supper*—

"Don't be afraid, girl," the light seemed to say, in a voice Lena was already forgetting. "You were never the haunted one. You were the *hearth*."

The vessel touched her brow.

Cold went through her like winter water. And in the space of a single heartbeat, twenty years began to pour out of her—names first, then faces, then voices, then the shape of the silence they left behind, dissolving, dissolving, a ledger burning page by page from the back—

—and on the bench beside her, Isla's soul sank home into her chest like a bird returning to a lit window, and the girl cried out, and the dragon-fire above them roared back to sudden blazing life as ten thousand freed souls poured their gratitude into it like oil on flame—

—and the doors at the far end of the hall slammed open, and the Tallowman was on his feet with a warden's iron chain in his wax-cracked hands, screaming that the trade was fraud, that the court owed him, that *no one leaves*—

Lena turned toward the noise, her mind suddenly, strangely quiet, like a house after everyone has gone to bed.

She was crying. She didn't know why.
