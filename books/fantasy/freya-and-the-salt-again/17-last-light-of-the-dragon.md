## 17. Last Light of the Dragon

The auction hall was a cavern of bone and ledger-light, and the dragon was dying above it.

Lena felt the fire's failing the way you feel a fever breaking in someone else—wrongly, helplessly, as a warmth withdrawing from the world. Far overhead, past galleries of seated dead and their brass counting-frames, the great chain ran up through a shaft of smoke to where the last dragon lay coiled around its own heart. Its light came down the shaft like honey gone thin, pooling on the floor in shapes that almost meant something.

Isla stood on the auction block with her chin up, which was the bravest thing Lena had ever seen, because the child was shaking so hard her teeth clicked.

"Lot forty-one," intoned the auctioneer, a dead man whose voice had been polished smooth by centuries of use. "One soul, juvenile, First Tongue speaker. Bonded twice over. The court values rarity."

The bidding was conducted not in words but in memories. Dead hands rose holding small lights—glimmers of first snowfall, of a mother's laugh, of a name spoken kindly—and each time one rose, the bidder dimmed slightly, like a candle trimmed. That was how the court spent its citizens: not all at once, but in installments, until nothing was left but the ledger-entry.

Lena stepped forward before she could talk herself out of it, which was the only way she had ever done anything brave.

"I bid," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, so she started again. "I bid twenty years of voices."

The hall went quiet in the particular way that only rooms full of the dead can go quiet—a silence with weight, with attention, with a thousand hollow faces turning at once.

The auctioneer tilted his head. "Clarify your lot, living woman."

"The dead," Lena said. "All of them. Every voice I have heard since I was nine years old. Maren Voss who taught me the First Tongue. The grandmother in the wall behind my shop who hummed when it rained. The drowned boy from the canal. The plague-dead of the Salt Quarter, four hundred souls, every one of them a name." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "Twenty years of listening. Take it. Take all of it. In exchange for the girl goes free, whole, and walks out of this mountain with her soul in her own keeping."

Somewhere in the galleries, a counting-frame clattered. Somewhere else, something that might have been laughter, or weeping; among the dead they sounded the same.

"You understand what you offer," the auctioneer said. It was not quite a question. "Memories of the dead cannot be returned. They are consumed in the settling of accounts. You will walk out of here unable to remember why you came."

"I know why I came," Lena said. "That part I get to keep. It hasn't happened yet."

* * *

Hale stood at the edge of the light with his magistrate's seal still hanging from his belt—he had refused to take it off, saying that if he was going to die a traitor he would die an identifiable one—and he watched Lena Nakamura do the arithmetic of love out loud, and he thought: *this is what the law was supposed to protect, and never once did.*

He had executed Maren Voss on a forged order twenty years ago. He had told himself for two decades that he had merely followed procedure. Procedure. He could hear the word now in the auctioneer's voice, in the court's dry rustle of ledgers, and he understood at last that procedure was just mercy with the mercy scraped out.

The court's clerks were converging on Lena, brass styluses out, ready to carve the bargain into her the way they carved everything—permanently, painlessly, without asking twice.

And the court's enforcers were converging on him.

They had noticed, of course. The court always noticed. Three figures in ash-grey robes detached themselves from the gallery, carrying not weapons but warrants—actual paper warrants, issued by a government of the dead, for the arrest of one Corvan Hale, magistrate of Verrin, on charges of trespass, sedition, and *theft of a bonded asset*. Isla. They had called her an asset. He had signed documents using that word himself, once, about people.

"Hale," Lena said, without looking away from the clerks. "Whatever you're planning, plan it faster."

"I'm working on it," he said.

* * *

Above them all, the dragon guttered.

Maren had been climbing toward it for what felt like a century and was probably an hour, up the inside of the great smoke-shaft, hand over hand along the chain links, each one big as a rowboat. Being dead made the climb easier. Being dead also made it pointless-feeling, because what did a ghost care whether a fire went out?

She cared. That was the whole surprise of dying, really—you kept caring. Nobody warned you.

She reached the last turn of the shaft and saw the dragon at last: vast beyond sense, coiled in the dark like a mountain that had decided to be sad, its fire burned down to a bed of embers no bigger than a village. Its eye opened when she arrived. Old. Amber. Unutterably tired.

"You're the teacher," the dragon said. Its voice was the sound a hearth makes at three in the morning. "The one they hanged. I felt your soul pass through the court's books. They never spent you. Too stubborn a memory, the clerk said."

"They said that?" Maren managed. "About me?"

"Stubbornness is hard currency down there." The ember-bed pulsed, dimmed. "You've come to watch me die. Kind of you. Most of the dead look away."

"I've come to tell you that a woman named Lena is trading everything she has to free the souls you've been keeping warm." Maren knelt at the edge of the fire-pit, close enough that the last heat washed over her like a tide going out. "And when those souls go free, they're going to be grateful. And gratitude, old one, is fuel. You taught the court that yourself, didn't you? Gratitude burns hotter than fear. They just never believed it, because they'd forgotten what it looked like."

The dragon's eye brightened by a fraction. "A theory."

"A hope with good posture."

Below, faint through the shaft, Maren heard the auctioneer's voice rise to the formal cadence of settlement. *Sold. To the living woman, against all reason.* And then a sound she had not heard in twenty years and would not be able to describe afterward even if she'd been asked: four hundred voices, released all at once, rising up the shaft like birds off water.

The embers caught.

* * *

Lena felt them leave her.

It did not hurt, exactly. It was more like setting down a pack she had carried so long she'd grown around it—the sudden lightness was its own kind of injury. The grandmother's humming went first, then the drowned boy, then the plague-dead in a long silver ribbon, name after name after name, each one a small warm weight lifted out of her chest by clerks with gentle hands and brass instruments, and she stood there letting them go because Isla was watching her, and Isla needed to see that it could be done.

Then Maren.

The last voice was the loudest and the quietest at once. For one heartbeat Lena held her teacher's whole presence—the smell of bone-dust and cedar oil, the exact pitch of *again, from the top, Nakamura*, the terrible private tenderness under all that discipline—and then the clerks drew it out like a splinter, and it was gone, and Lena Nakamura stood in the auction hall of the dead knowing that someone had loved her very much and being unable to remember who.

She was crying. She noted it distantly, the way you note weather. *Someone,* she thought. *Someone mattered.*

Isla came off the block at a run and hit her at hip height, arms locked around her legs, and said—in the First Tongue, in the language of the dead, the last time she would ever speak it fluently—something short and fierce that Lena, horrifyingly, could no longer translate. But she didn't need to. Some things are the same in every tongue.

"Yeah," Lena said, hoarse, gathering the girl up. "Me too."

And then the whole hall lit up gold.

It came down the shaft like sunrise deciding to happen indoors: the freed souls' gratitude, four hundred strong, pouring into the dying dragon's ember-bed, and the ember-bed catching it the way dry grass catches a struck flint. The fire roared back up the chain. Light flooded the galleries, and the dead raised their faces into it, and for one moment the court beneath the mountain was not a bank or a prison but simply a room full of people standing in the sun.

Lena laughed. She couldn't help it. It was either that or come apart entirely.

Then the ash-grey robes moved, and the moment ended.

"Enforcement," said the auctioneer, and his polished voice had gone cold as a ledger's spine. "The trade is settled. The girl is freed. But these two—" a stylus indicated Hale, then swept toward Lena "—entered under false names, consorted with a rogue spirit, and defamed this court before witnesses. Warrants stand. Enforcers, take them."

The three grey figures advanced, warrants held high, and behind them came more, dozens, the court's whole machinery of quiet violence grinding into motion, and Lena backed toward the wall with Isla in her arms and thought, with strange clarity: *we got her out, and that was always going to be the easy part.*

Hale stepped forward to meet them.

* * *

Corvan Hale had spent his life believing that law was the thing that stood between people and the dark. Standing now between the dark and two people he had come, inconveniently and late, to care about, he finally understood that law was only ever a tool, and tools don't stand anywhere. People stand. Or they don't.

"Magistrate Hale," said the lead enforcer. "Surrender your seal."

"My seal," Hale said, drawing it off his belt, "has been surrendered for some time. I've just been wearing it out of habit." He weighed it in his palm—brass, heavy, stamped with the scales of Verrin justice. Then he threw it, hard, into the rekindled light below the shaft, where it rang once on the stone and spun to rest, and every dead clerk in the gallery watched it lie there, meaningless.

Then he turned and looked up the smoke-shaft, at the chain.

It was an old chain, older than the court, older than the Purge—forged in the first conquest to bind the dragon's fire to the court's service, link by link, so that the beast's burning would warm their ledgers and light their auctions and keep their souls from dispersing. Every soul in the mountain hung, one way or another, from that chain. Including, now, the ones he loved.

"Hale," Lena said sharply. "Hale, whatever you're thinking—"

"Somebody has to enforce something down here," he said. "Might as well be me. I have experience."

He crossed the hall at a run, shouldering through the first enforcer—who staggered, surprised, because living men were not supposed to fight back—and reached the base of the shaft where the chain anchored into the rock, and put his hands on the coldest link, and pulled.

Nothing. Of course nothing. It was a chain that had held a dragon for a thousand years.

But the dragon was awake now, and fed on gratitude, and its fire was running down the links like blood returning to a numb limb, and when Hale set his shoulder against the anchor-stone and heaved with everything a guilty man has left, the dragon pulled too—from the other end, upward, for the first time in a millennium pulling *up*—

The first link cracked like a glacier calving.

The gallery screamed—not in pain, in alarm, in outrage, in the panic of an institution discovering it can die. Clerks scattered. Ledgers burst open, spilling loose memories across the floor like fish from a net. The enforcers' warrants caught fire in the updraft and burned green.

"Hale!" Lena was running toward him, Isla screaming, the whole hall tilting into chaos. "Let go! *Let go!*"

"One more," he grunted, and grinned at her over his shoulder, and it was the first honest grin she had ever seen on him. "For Maren. Tell her—I mean. Tell whoever remembers her. Tell them I'm sorry."

The second link broke.

The backlash came off the chain like a wave off a harbor wall. It took Hale off his feet and lifted him, and the light that poured out of the broken binding was not fire exactly, nor forgiveness exactly, but something between—the raw unmediated burning of a freedom a thousand years deferred—and it went through him the way dawn goes through a window, completely.

He landed at the base of the shaft, and Lena reached him, and knelt, and knew.

"It worked," Hale said. His voice was already thinner, already further away. Above them the chain fell link by link, ringing down the shaft like enormous bells, and the dragon's roar shook dust from a ceiling no one had ever seen. "Listen to it. Listen. Worth it. Do you know—I dreamed of voices, all these years. Thought it was guilt." His eyes found hers, and they were clear, clearer than she had ever seen them. "It was instructions."

"Don't," Lena said. "Don't do the dying-with-a-speech thing. I hate the dying-with-a-speech thing."

"Too late." He tried to smile. "The girl. Keep her close. And Nakamura—when you can't remember why you're crying, someday, on some road—cry anyway. It counts. Grief doesn't need its object. It just needs—" his breath caught, held, "—the crying."

His hand, which had been gripping hers, went slack, and the light took the rest of him gently, the way the tide takes a footprint, and Corvan Hale—magistrate, executioner, traitor, friend, in whatever order history cares to list it—was done.

* * *

Above, the dragon rose.

Maren rode the updraft of its waking, laughing, actually laughing, dead twenty years and finally, *finally* witnessing something worth the wait. The great wings spread in the dark of the mountain's heart; the last chain-links rained away like shed scales; and the dragon turned its amber eye on the hole its own fire had spent a millennium polishing in the roof of the world, and flew at it.

Stone split. Night poured in—real night, salt wind and stars, the sea-smell of Verrin far below—and through it the dragon climbed, trailing fire like a banner, and every soul the court had ever shelved rose after it, hundreds, thousands, streaming up through the broken mountain like sparks off a bonfire, going wherever it is that the unowned go.

Maren paused at the lip of the breach. Below, tiny, she could see a woman kneeling beside a fallen man, and a small girl with her arms around the woman's shoulders, and the woman's mouth moving—crying, or laughing, or both; from this height they were the same.

*Memento,* Maren thought at her, uselessly, lovingly, knowing the word would fall on ears that could no longer hear it. *Remember me. Oh—but you can't. Well then.* She gathered herself into the wind. *Remember that you loved me. That will do. That will more than do.*

Then the last dragon went over the edge of the sky, and its dead went with it, and the mountain was only a mountain.

* * *

In the ruins of the auction hall, amid drifting ash and the slow rain of settling dust, Lena sat with Isla in her lap and Hale's cooling hand in hers, and she cried and cried and could not have said for the life of her why.

"Come on," she whispered finally, to the child, to herself, to no one. "We should go. Before anything else in this mountain decides to make a point."

Isla nodded, wiped her nose on her sleeve, and tugged her toward the shattered gate—toward the long road home, the salt air, the shop with its broken door and its walls that had, for the first time in twenty years, gone silent.

Behind them, deep in the rubble, something stirred: a single ledger, unburned, its pages fluttering open in the draft—as if the court, even dying, had one last entry to make.

And on its final page, in a clerk's neat brass-cut hand, a name was still legible:

*Lena Nakamura — collateral — status: unsettled.*
