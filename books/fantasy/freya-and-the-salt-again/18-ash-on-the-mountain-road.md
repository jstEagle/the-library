## 18. Ash on the Mountain Road

The mountain began to die at dawn, and it died beautifully, which Lena would think about for a long time afterward without knowing why.

She was halfway up the switchback road when the first tremor came—a long shudder through the stone, like a great animal turning over in its sleep. She had Isla on her hip, the girl's legs wrapped around her waist and her arms around her neck, and when the mountain shook Isla only held tighter. She did not cry. She had not cried since the gate.

Behind them, far below and deep within, something vast exhaled.

Lena turned to look, because you cannot not look. The peak of the mountain split along a seam no eye could see, and light came out—not firelight, not sunlight, but something older and warmer, the color of the inside of a word. It rose in columns. It rose in ribbons. It rose in shapes that might have been people if people were made of morning, hundreds of them, thousands, streaming upward out of the wound in the world like sparks off a bonfire, wheeling once—she would swear forever that they wheeled once, all together, as if bowing—and then scattering into the grey sky until there was nothing left of them but a brightness behind the clouds that might have been weather.

Her eyes were wet. She noticed this with mild surprise, the way you notice your hands are cold.

*I'm crying,* she thought. *How strange.*

She could not remember why. There was a grief in her chest, enormous and specific, shaped exactly like something she had lost—and yet when she reached for the shape, it slipped through her fingers like water through a charm-carver's grip. She knew she had lost something. She did not know what. It was like standing in an empty room and feeling certain the room had furniture yesterday.

"Up," Isla said, tugging her hair. "Keep walking, Lena."

Isla's voice was different now. That was another thing Lena noticed without understanding: the child had spoken in two languages on the road beneath the world, the old tongue and the common one, sliding between them the way children slide between games. Now there was only the one voice, small and ordinary and six years old, and the other language—the one Lena had known better than her own name, the one she had heard in walls and wells and the spaces between heartbeats—was gone from the girl entirely.

Gone from everywhere, maybe. Gone from Lena too. When she tried to recall even a single syllable of it, her mind came back smooth and blank as river stone.

*You paid for her,* said a thought that arrived from nowhere, or from somewhere very deep. *That's what the emptiness is. You paid.*

But that made no sense. You couldn't pay for a child with nothing, and Lena felt very much like nothing just then—like a purse turned out, like a shop swept clean, like a bone carved down past the marrow.

She kept walking. The mountain shook again, gentler now, settling into its ruin.

* * *

They found Hale's body where the chain had been, near the mouth of the old dragon-road, laid out neat as a magistrate on review day. The backlash had burned him from the inside; there was hardly a mark on him, which seemed unfair, somehow, after everything. His face was calm. His hand was still closed around a link of broken chain, blackened iron gone soft as wax, and he looked less like a man who had died than a man who had finally put down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

Lena knelt beside him. Isla slid off her hip and stood close, solemn.

"I knew him," Lena said slowly, testing the words. "Didn't I? I knew him."

"You knew him," Isla agreed. "He was our friend." The girl frowned, working at it. "Wasn't he?"

"He must have been." Lena touched the man's cold forehead, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure was locked. "Why else would we be crying?"

And they were, both of them, tears running freely down their faces in the ash-fall, weeping over a dead man whose name Lena could not remember and whose face she could barely hold onto—there had been something tired about his eyes, something that wanted to be kind—but she could not, for the life of her, say what he had done or why it had mattered or why her chest hurt this badly looking at him.

Grief without its object. That was the shape of it. A bell with no clapper, rung anyway.

"We should bury him," Isla said.

"There's no ground soft enough up here." Lena looked around at the scree and the salt-grey stone. "We'll cover him. With stones. Travelers do that, on high roads. So the crows don't—" She stopped. Started again. "It's respectful. People do it out of respect."

They built the cairn together, Isla handing her stones smaller than her fist, some of them still warm from the mountain's dying heat. When they set the last one, Lena found herself saying words she didn't know she knew—a few lines of something, rhythmic, formal, in a cadence meant for graves. She had no idea where the words came from. They felt worn smooth by use, like steps in an old house. By the time she reached the end of them she was crying harder, and she still didn't know why, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound, a tooth-ache of the soul.

"What was that?" Isla whispered.

"A burial rite," Lena said. Her voice cracked. "Someone taught me. I don't remember who."

* * *

The descent took two days. The corpse-roads, Ode had called them, back when—back whenever that was, before, in the part of her life that had doors in it instead of holes. The roads were quiet now. That was the thing Lena could not stop noticing, the way you can't stop probing a missing tooth: the roads were *quiet*. Once—they must have been—these paths had been full of voices. She knew it the way you know a forest has birds even in winter, from the shape of the silence itself. The silence here had structure. It was the silence of a place where something used to sing.

At night Isla slept pressed against her side, and Lena lay awake under the indifferent stars doing arithmetic she couldn't finish.

She knew facts. That was the maddening part. She knew she had lived twenty years in Verrin. She knew she carved charms, good ones, honest ones that did nothing. She knew she'd had a teacher, because her hands remembered the teacher's cuts, the particular way of finishing a spiral—but the name wouldn't come, nor the face, nor the voice. She knew she had walked into a mountain with a magistrate and a child, though the reasons had burned away like fog in sun. She knew she had loved someone deeply enough that losing them had hollowed her out, and she knew she had lost them *on purpose*, traded them away, and even that knowledge brought no clarity—only a fresh wave of that objectless grief, rolling through her like weather.

Once, on the second night, she caught herself talking to the dark.

"—and then we take the coast road, it's safer in the wet season, I've told you a hundred times—"

She stopped. Listened. Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But her body had been mid-conversation, comfortable, easy, the way you talk to someone who has heard your stories so often they finish them for you. She had been talking to someone. Someone who used to walk beside her on nights exactly like this one.

She sat in the ashes and the starlight and cried until Isla woke up and patted her arm with a small, sticky hand, and said, with terrible six-year-old gravity:

"It's all right, Lena. We're going home."

Home. Verrin, then. Salt and curfews and customers who flinched at shadows. It should have sounded bleak. Instead the word opened something warm in her chest, and she realized she had been afraid—not of the mountain, not of wardens, not of anything with a name—but simply, formlessly afraid, the way a child is afraid of a room where the lamp has gone out. And Isla had just relit it.

"All right," Lena said. "Home."

* * *

They came down out of the hills on the third morning, and Verrin rose out of the salt-haze ahead of them, crooked and grey and smelling of brine and fish-oil and woodsmoke, and Lena's heart did something complicated that she chose to call relief.

The city didn't know what had happened under the mountain. Of course it didn't. Cities never know. The fishmongers were shouting prices. The curfew bells rang their dull noon count. A woman argued with a cart-driver about turnip tariffs. Somewhere a dog was having opinions about a cat. Life went on with the magnificent indifference of life everywhere, and Lena stood at the gate with ash still in her hair and a child on her hip and a dead friend's name dissolving on her tongue, and understood that no one would ever know what they had done. There would be no songs. The court beneath the world had kept its ledger for centuries and now the ledger was ash, and the only witnesses left were a carver who couldn't remember and a child who was learning to forget.

Maybe that was mercy. Maybe that was the whole point.

Ode found them before they'd gone ten streets—which was Ode all over; the woman had a nose for trouble the way sharks have a nose for blood, except her response was always tea. She came barreling out of a side alley in a coat that had clearly lost several arguments, took one look at the two of them—ash-grimed, hollow-eyed, trailing grief like a torn hem—and said nothing at all, which for Ode was practically a eulogy. She just gathered them both under one arm each and steered them toward her warehouse, barking at gawking bystanders with a fluency of profanity that could have stripped barnacles.

Inside, over tea strong enough to trot a horse across, Ode studied Lena with those quick dark eyes and asked, carefully, "Do you remember me?"

"Of course I remember you," Lena said. "You're Ode. You smuggle things. You cheat at cards and deny it while holding the evidence." Then she paused, frowning. "Don't I? I do. Why are you looking at me like that?"

"No reason," Ode said, too fast, and refilled her cup, and shot a glance at Isla that Lena couldn't read and didn't try to.

Later, when Isla slept in a nest of sacking by the stove, Ode sat down across from Lena and said, quietly, "There was someone. Before. Wasn't there. Someone you're not remembering."

Lena looked into her tea. The surface trembled with her hands.

"Yes," she said. "I think—I think there was more than one. More than one someone. I think my whole life had... company. And now it doesn't." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I keep turning around to tell someone something. Every hour. Something happens and I turn around and there's no one there, and I don't even know who I'm missing. How do you mourn someone you can't remember? How do you know how big the hole is when you can't see the edges of it?"

Ode reached across the table and gripped her wrist, rough and warm.

"You don't," she said. "So you let other people carry the edges for you till you can stand up straight. That's what friends are for. Now eat something, you look like a drowned rat with a mortgage."

And that—that ridiculous, profane, ordinary kindness—did more than any rite or revelation could have. Lena ate. The stew was terrible. She cried a little into it, quietly, and Ode pretended not to notice, and outside the window Verrin went on being Verrin, bells and gulls and the eternal argument between the sea and the seawall.

* * *

On the fourth day, Lena went back to her shop.

The street looked the same. The door stuck the same way, needing the hip-lift she'd taught her hands a decade ago. Inside, dust had settled over everything in fine grey veils, and her tools lay where she'd left them—or where someone had left them, arranged with a care that made her pause. Her carving knives were clean. Her stock of finished charms hung in rows, little spirals and knots and interlocking rings, bone-white and patient.

She picked one up. A simple piece, a guard-charm, the kind she'd sold a hundred times to frightened people who needed something to hold. Her thumb found the finishing cut on the spiral automatically, that particular way of ending the line—

—and her chest seized, sudden and total, with a grief so sharp she had to grip the bench.

Because she knew that cut. Her hands knew it. Some part of her had learned it from someone, had been shown it patiently, corrected gently, praised—praised!—by a voice she could not hear anymore, attached to a face she could not see, belonging to a person she had loved so much she had given up every memory of them to save a child she'd known for a week.

She stood in her dusty shop and wept for Maren—wait.

*Maren.*

The name surfaced like a bubble through deep water, one syllable, two, breaking against the surface of her mind and popping into nothing. She grabbed for it. Gone. But for one white-hot instant she had *had* it—a name, a warmth, a flash of someone laughing at something Lena had said, someone with clever hands and a stubborn jaw and a way of saying *girl* that made the word sound like a blessing—

Then it was smoke. Then it was nothing. Then it was just a name-shaped absence, ringing like the empty bell.

"Maren," Lena said aloud, to the dusty shop, to nobody. Testing whether saying it would bring anything back.

Nothing came. Only the silence, structured and birdless, the silence of a place where something used to sing.

She wiped her face with her sleeve. She hung the charm back on its hook. Outside, the afternoon light was going gold over the salt-roofs, and somewhere below a customer was knocking at her door—she could hear the knock, hesitant, hopeful, the knock of someone who needed something to hold.

Lena looked at the door for a long moment. Then she lifted her chin, scrubbed the last of the ash from her hair, and went to answer it, carrying her grief the only way left to her: without knowing its name, without knowing its face, knowing only—down in the place the trade could not reach, deeper than memory, older than language—that she had loved the dead, and the dead had loved her, and love like that does not vanish when the remembering does.

It just gets very, very quiet.

She opened the door. The woman on the step clutched a scrap of paper in shaking fingers, and the first words out of her mouth were ones Lena would chew on for weeks:

"You're the carver," the woman said. "They said—you're the one who helps people the law won't."
