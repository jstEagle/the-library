## 19. The Empty Shop

The door of the shop had been broken in a way that was almost polite.

Lena stood on the threshold with her key still in her hand, looking at the splintered frame, the hinges hanging like dropped jaws, and thought: *someone wanted me to know they'd been here, but they didn't want to hurt the shop.* The glass in the display window was cracked but not shattered. The counter was overturned but not burned. Even the sawdust on the floor seemed swept into neat little drifts, as if a very tidy storm had passed through.

Inside, it was another matter entirely.

Drawers pulled out and upended. Bone blanks scattered across the floorboards like a spill of teeth. Her good carving knives—Maren's old knives, the ones with the whale-ivory handles—gone. And there, in the middle of the room, in the exact spot where she'd kept it under a loose floorboard for twenty years, a black circle of ash where her ledger of names had been.

She knelt down in it before she could stop herself. The ash was cold. It got into the creases of her knuckles and stayed there, gray as old snow. She rubbed her thumb against her forefinger and felt nothing at all—not the whisper of a name, not the ghost of a voice, not even the ordinary human ache of remembering that you used to remember.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. She knew the ledger had held names. She could see the shape of the knowledge, the way you can see the shape of a keyhole without the key. *I wrote names in that book. I kept them safe. There were so many.* But when she reached for a single one—her teacher's name, the first one, the one written in a hand steadier than hers—the reaching came back empty, over and over, like a bucket lowered into a dry well.

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, to no one, because saying sorry to no one was apparently what she did now.

The silence answered the way silence always answers: by being complete.

* * *

It took her two days to put the shop to rights, mostly because Isla insisted on helping and Isla's idea of helping was to sort every scrap of bone into piles according to a system known only to Isla. By the end of the first day there were seven piles. By the end of the second day there were eleven, and Lena had stopped asking.

"You're making a mess," Isla said, watching Lena sweep.

"I'm unmaking one. It's different."

"It looks the same."

"That," said Lena, leaning on the broom, "is the deepest thing anyone's said to me all week."

The girl grinned. Her front tooth had come in crooked, and Lena had decided privately that this was the finest tooth in Verrin. Since they'd come back from the mountain, Isla had been talking more—not the First Tongue, never that; that language was draining out of her day by day like water through cupped hands—but ordinary words, blunt little words, words about bread and weather and whether the cat that sometimes slept on their step belonged to anyone or was simply a public cat. Her soul had settled back into her the way a bird settles after a fright, feathers ruffled, eyes bright, checking that the world still held.

Lena envied her a little. Isla had lost something too, but she'd lost it forward, into a future. Lena's loss sat behind her, facing backward, an empty chair at every table.

On the third morning, she unlocked the door—new lock, new frame, Ode had grumbled through the whole installation about how if she'd just listened to him twenty years ago she'd have had a back exit and a false wall—and opened for business.

The first customer came within the hour. Mrs. Anselm, widow, three streets over, who bought a charm every season against drowning though she hadn't been on water since her husband's funeral boat.

"The usual?" Lena asked, already reaching for the drawer.

Mrs. Anselm hesitated. Something flickered across her face—she'd heard things, then. Everyone had heard things. The mountain's fall had been visible from the harbor, a red glow and then a great exhale of light, and the stories had multiplied in the weeks since like flies on a carcass.

"They say," Mrs. Anselm said carefully, "that your charms don't work."

Lena set the charm on the counter between them. It was a small piece of rib bone, carved with the knot pattern Maren had taught her, sanded smooth as a river stone. It did nothing. It had always done nothing. That had been the whole point, once—the perfect crime, magic-shaped objects with no magic in them, sold to frightened people who needed to hold something.

"They're right," Lena said. "They don't work. They never worked."

Mrs. Anselm stared at her. Then, to Lena's astonishment, her chin began to tremble.

"Then what have I been paying for?"

"For this." Lena turned the charm over in her palm. "A thing to hold. When my hands are frightened, they want a task. Your hands are frightened too—I've watched them for six years, Mrs. Anselm, every season, same purchase, same knot. You don't buy protection from drowning. You buy something to do while you wait for the fear to pass." She slid it across the counter. "Same price as ever. Or free, if you'd rather know the truth and keep your coin."

Mrs. Anselm looked at the charm for a long moment. Then she paid, wrapped it in her handkerchief, and left with her spine straighter than she'd come in.

Lena stood there feeling like she'd been turned inside out and found, surprisingly, to be made of something useful.

* * *

By the end of the week, word had done what word does in a salt-city—it had gone everywhere and changed in every telling. Some versions had Lena confessing to the magistrate himself. Some had her striking the chains off the last dragon with her bare hands. One particularly ambitious version, retailed to her by a fish-boy who clearly hoped for a discount, had her riding the dragon out of the mountain and into the sea.

"There wasn't any dragon riding," she told him.

"That's what someone would say," said the fish-boy, "if they didn't want people knowing about the dragon riding."

She gave him the discount.

But the customers kept coming. That was the part she couldn't get straight in her head. She'd expected the truth to empty the shop—that was the logic of her whole life, the logic of hiding: *once they see what I am, they'll go.* Instead they came more. Old customers, new customers, customers she'd never seen before, standing at her counter with their hats in their hands like mourners.

A dockhand who wanted a charm for his brother's grave, and when Lena said *it won't do anything*, he said, "I know. I want it anyway. He liked holding things."

An old man who'd traded his memories to the court decades ago—one of the hollow ones, one of the village kind, though he lived in Verrin now—and who stood at her counter turning a blank piece of bone over and over, unable to say what he wanted, only that he felt he'd come in for something and couldn't recall what. Lena carved him a small round stone-smooth bead with no pattern at all, and he wept over it, and neither of them knew why, and both of them let it be true.

And once—a woman with a burn-scarred jaw and the flat stare of someone who has been sold and knows it—who laid a coin on the counter and said, "I heard you can hear the dead."

"No," Lena said. It came out steady. It surprised her. "I can't. Not anymore. I gave that away."

"Gave it? Or lost it?"

Lena considered the question honestly, which was more than she'd managed in the first weeks. "Both. It was a trade. What I got in exchange is standing in my kitchen eating the crusts off the bread loaf when she thinks I'm not looking."

The woman looked toward the kitchen doorway, where Isla was indeed committing crust-theft with great concentration, and something in her flat stare loosened. She took a plain charm off the rack, paid double, and left without another word.

After she'd gone, Lena stood a while with her hand flat on the counter, in the spot where the ledger's ash had been, before she'd scrubbed the boards pale.

*I loved them,* she thought. *Whoever they were. I know the shape of loving them. I just can't hear them anymore.*

It was like living next door to a house she'd once inhabited. She knew which windows had been hers. She could stand in the street and point to them. But the rooms were dark, and no amount of knocking brought an answer, and slowly—this was the part nobody warned you about—slowly the knocking became a habit rather than a hope, and then the habit faded too, until some mornings she walked past the house without pointing at all.

Those were the mornings that scared her most. Not the grief. The *not*-grief.

* * *

Ode came by on the ninth day, ostensibly to inspect his lock, actually to eat her bread and judge her curtains.

"You look terrible," he said fondly, dropping onto the stool across the counter. He was a broad, salt-cracked man with a beard like ship's rope, and he had carried Isla four miles on his shoulders coming down off the mountain road without once complaining, which in Ode's language was a sonnet.

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

"Business?"

"Booming. That's the joke, isn't it. Twenty years selling nothing quietly, and the moment I admit it's nothing, everyone wants some."

Ode chewed, considering. "People don't want charms, Lena. They never did. They want permission to hope with their hands full." He pointed the bread at her. "You've got honest hands. Turns out that's rarer than magic."

"And what do you want? You never come in without wanting something. Last time it was my good sandpaper and a favor involving a boat I don't ask questions about."

He set the bread down, and his face did the thing it did rarely, the thing where the humor drained out of it and left the older, harder man underneath—the smuggler who'd hidden a soul-bonded child through a curfew storm without blinking.

"There's a woman," he said. "Came off a coastal trader Tuesday. Young. Got that look—you know the look. Hollowed-out around the eyes, like something was taken and the taking was recent." He paused. "She's asking after you. Not for a charm. She says—" and here Ode's voice went careful, the way it did when he was quoting exactly, "—she says she heard the mountain fell, and she heard you were there, and she wants to know if what was taken can be taken *back*."

Lena's hands went still on the counter.

"She has a name?"

"Says her name's Wren. Says her sister's soul is still owed somewhere, in whatever's left of the courts below. Says—" Ode winced slightly, "—says she heard you help people the law won't help."

"I said that?"

"You said it to the fish-boy. He tells everyone."

Lena looked past Ode, through the cracked window, out at the street. Evening was coming on, the salt-light going amber, the lamps being lit one by one down the lane. Somewhere out there was a woman named Wren with empty hands and a debt the law would never acknowledge, walking toward a shop that sold nothing, believing—against all evidence, against everything Lena knew about her own diminished self—that something could be done.

"I can't hear the dead anymore, Ode," she said quietly. "Whatever I did under that mountain, I spent it. The voices, the names, the knowing—gone. I'm just a carver now. My carvings do nothing."

Ode shrugged, vast and unhurried, like a hill deciding to move.

"So carve," he said.

He left the bread crumbs and took his coat, and at the door he paused, not turning around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the ones you helped—they're not quiet anymore either. Word travels. Doors open. And somebody's going to need to be standing in the doorway when they do."

Then he was gone, and the shop was quiet, truly quiet, the way it had never been quiet in twenty years—no murmur in the walls, no whisper under the floor, just Lena and the lamplight and the small sounds of Isla practicing letters at the kitchen table, sounding out each word like a climber testing footholds.

Lena sat down at her bench. She picked up a blank of bone, smooth and white and silent. She picked up her knife.

And she began, without quite deciding to, to carve—not a charm, not a knot, not any pattern Maren had taught her, but something new, something the knife seemed to already know: a small figure, a woman, arms open, standing in a doorway.

Outside, footsteps on the cobbles, stopping at her door.

A knock—polite, hesitant, the knock of someone who has knocked on many doors and been refused most of them.

Lena set down the knife. She looked at the little carved woman in her palm, arms open, holding nothing, offering everything.

"Coming," she called, and went to answer the door.
