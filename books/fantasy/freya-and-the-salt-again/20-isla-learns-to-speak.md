## 20. Isla Learns to Speak

The first word Isla lost was *ashen*. She said it one morning over breakfast—*ashen*, in the First Tongue, the way the dead say it when they mean *gone but warm*—and then she frowned at her porridge like it had stolen something from her.

"What did you say?" Lena asked, though she'd understood perfectly. That was the trouble with understanding perfectly. It meant you were still listening for ghosts in a house that had finally gone quiet.

Isla opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again and said, with tremendous dignity, "Porridge."

"That's not what you said."

"Porridge," Isla repeated, louder, as if volume could substitute for vocabulary. Then, after a pause, in a smaller voice: "It was a different word."

"I know."

"It's gone." The girl looked genuinely alarmed, the way children look when they lose a tooth and realize the gap is permanent—or at least feels that way. "It was right there and now it's gone."

Lena reached across the table and tapped the girl's bowl with her spoon. "Words are like that sometimes. They come back when they're needed. Or they don't, and you make new ones." She hesitated, then added, because lying to this particular child felt like carving a charm that lied: "The old ones weren't yours anyway. Not really. They were borrowed."

Isla considered this with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing evidence. "Whose were they?"

"The dead's." Lena said it plainly. There was no point dressing it up; the child had spent months as a vessel for a language older than Verrin's walls. "You were carrying their words the way you'd carry water. Now the bucket's empty and it's just you in there."

"Is that bad?"

"No," Lena said, and found, to her surprise, that she meant it without any hollow places behind it. "That's the best news I've had in twenty years."

* * *

The losing of words happened slowly, the way ice leaves a window—not all at once but in patches, clear spots spreading where the frost had been. Some mornings Isla would wake speaking three or four phrases of the First Tongue, murmuring them over her boots while she laced them, and by evening they'd be gone, replaced by ordinary Verrin-salt common speech, clumsy and child-shaped.

She learned *no* first among the useful words, which Lena privately considered the most honest possible beginning. Then *mine*. Then, catastrophically, *why*, deployed with the relentless precision of a siege engine.

"Why do you carve bones?"

"Because it's my trade."

"But why?"

"Because people pay me."

"But why do they pay you?"

"Because they're frightened and frightened people like to hold something."

"But why are they frightened?"

"Because—" Lena put down her knife. "Because the world is large and they are small, and somewhere between those two facts everyone decides to be scared."

Isla chewed on this for a full minute, swinging her legs off the workbench stool. "I'm small," she announced at last, "and I'm not scared anymore."

"No?"

"No. I was scared under the mountain. And I was scared on the road. But now I'm here and the floor doesn't hum and nobody is counting me." She held up her hands, turned them over, examined the palms as though checking for tally marks. "Nobody is counting me," she repeated, wonderingly, like a person reading good news twice to make sure.

Lena's chest did something complicated. She picked the knife back up. "Well. Don't tell the tax collectors."

"What's a tax collector?"

"A kind of warden with better handwriting."

* * *

Teaching her to carve had been Ode's idea, delivered over smuggled tea in the back room of the shop, while Ode's boots dripped harbor-water onto Lena's cleanest floor.

"You can't have her sitting about watching you work all day," Ode said. "Children who watch go strange. Children who *do* stay ordinary, more or less. Ordinary's the whole prize, isn't it? That's what we bought."

"We didn't buy it," Lena said. "I paid for it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Ode had the grace to look abashed, which lasted approximately four seconds. "Fine. You *paid*. Either way, give the girl a bone and a blunt knife before she takes up something worse, like asking questions of strangers."

So Lena gave her a bone—a knuckle joint from an ox, boiled clean, smooth as river stone—and a knife with an edge dulled past usefulness, and showed her how to hold both.

"Now," Lena said, "you're going to make nothing. Do you understand? This isn't a charm. It will never be a charm. It's a shape. A duck, maybe, if your hands are better than mine were at six."

"What did you make at six?"

"A duck."

"Was it good?"

"It was so bad my teacher kept it on her shelf for twenty years to laugh at."

Isla's eyes went wide. "Your teacher. The one whose voice you—" She stopped herself, which was new. Weeks ago she'd have barreled straight through such a sentence without noticing the cliff edge. Now she noticed. Her soul settling into her, Lena thought, was teaching her more than language. It was teaching her weight—the weight of things said aloud.

"Yes," Lena said. "That one."

"Do you miss her?"

And here was the strange country Lena lived in now: the honest answer was *I don't know*, because missing requires a shape to miss, and Maren's voice was gone from her the way a word is gone from Isla—not dead, exactly, just no longer anywhere she could reach. She remembered that she had loved her teacher. She remembered it the way you remember a sum you once did correctly: the answer remained, the working was lost.

"I remember that I loved her," Lena said. "That's enough. That has to be enough."

Isla nodded solemnly, then ruined it entirely by asking, "Can I make two ducks?"

"You can make forty ducks."

* * *

The ducks multiplied. Ducks became fish, fish became a lopsided horse that Isla insisted was a dragon, and the dragon became a small industry, because within a fortnight the customers started buying them.

This was the part Lena hadn't predicted. Her charms sold, as always, to the frightened and the hopeful—little carved tokens that did nothing, that had never done anything, that she sold with a straight face and a clean conscience because hope does its own work regardless of the medium. But the dragons sold differently. People bought them for children. People bought them for themselves. A dockhand bought three and lined them up on his windowsill facing the sea. An old woman bought one and held it in her palm the entire time she talked about her late husband, turning it over and over, and left without ever saying why.

"They're not magic," Lena told each customer, because she always did, because the habit of honesty was the last thing Maren had left her besides the memory of having loved her.

"We know," they said. "We don't care."

Ode found this hilarious. "Twenty years selling nothing to scared people, and now you're rich off a six-year-old's hobby. There's a lesson in commerce there."

"There's a lesson in grief there," Lena said. "People need something to hold. It turns out it barely matters what."

"And the girl?"

Lena watched through the shop door as Isla sat cross-legged on the floor, tongue poking out the corner of her mouth, carving a dragon with fierce concentration and, lately, actual skill. Her hands had steadied. Her cuts followed the grain instead of fighting it. Somewhere in the weeks since the mountain, the trembling had gone out of her—not all at once, but the way the words had gone: in patches, in thaw.

"She sleeps through the night now," Lena said. "She hasn't spoken the First Tongue in nine days. Yesterday she laughed at Ode falling off a crate, which is the healthiest thing a child can do." She paused. "Yesterday she called me—"

She stopped. Ode waited, uncharacteristically patient.

"She called me *Lena*," Lena finished. "Not the dead-word for keeper. Not the mountain-word for vessel. Just my name, said wrong, the way children say names, with too many vowels. It was the best thing anyone's said to me since—" She waved a hand. "Ever, probably. Don't tell anyone I said that. I have a reputation as a flint-hearted carve-witch to maintain."

"Your secret dies with me," Ode said, "or possibly tonight at the Anchor, depending on how the ale goes."

* * *

But healing, Lena learned, is not a straight road, and some evenings the frost came back.

On one such evening Isla woke screaming—not words, not any language, just sound, the raw animal noise of a body remembering what the mind had set down. Lena was in the room before she was fully awake herself, lamp in hand, heart hammering against twenty years of trained composure.

Isla was sitting up, knees to chest, eyes open and fixed on nothing.

"They're counting again," she whispered. "In my dream they're counting and I don't know the number and if I don't know the number they won't let me out."

Lena sat on the edge of the bed. She did not touch the girl yet—some terrors you had to approach like a wild bird, slowly, sideways. "There's no counting now," she said. "Listen. What do you hear?"

Isla listened. The shop creaked. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere down the street a drunk sang three notes of a song and forgot the rest.

"Creaking," Isla said. "Rain. A man singing badly."

"That's the whole world right now. That's everything there is. No mountain. No ledger. No numbers." Lena held out her hand, palm up, the way she'd once seen Maren do with frightened apprentices. "Count what's real, if you need to count. One: rain. Two: bad singing. Three: me."

Isla took her hand. "Three: me," she repeated, and her grip was a child's grip, small and strong and entirely her own. "Four: the duck I made. Five: breakfast."

"Breakfast counts double."

A wet, hiccupping almost-laugh. "Six: Lena."

"See? Rich already." Lena tucked the blanket up. "The dreams get smaller. I promise. They don't leave all at once either—they leave the way the words left. In patches. You'll wake up one morning and the dream will just be a dream, and you won't even remember it was ever anything else."

"Did your dreams do that?"

Lena thought about the dreams she no longer had—the ones that used to come every night, full of voices, full of Maren's dry commentary on the state of the world, full of the dead murmuring in the walls of her sleep. She couldn't remember the last time one came. She couldn't remember the voices at all. She remembered only that they had been there, the way you remember a room you grew up in after the house is torn down.

"Yes," she said. "They did."

* * *

Spring came early to Verrin that year, or what passed for spring in a city built on salt—thaw-water running in the gutters, gulls arguing over the fish market, light lasting a few minutes longer each evening until suddenly it was staying for supper.

Isla's speech bloomed with it. Sentences now, whole paragraphs, arguments, negotiations, demands. She had opinions about everything: the quality of Ode's boots (disgraceful), the correct number of sugar lumps in tea (three, non-negotiable), whether dragons could swim (yes, obviously, and Lena was a fool for suggesting otherwise).

And the First Tongue faded from her like a tide going out. Sometimes she'd reach for a phrase and find only the shape of it, the feeling, and she'd frown and translate badly: *"It's the word for when something ends but keeps being true."*

"Home?" she'd asked on the corpse-road, her first common word, and now home was a word she used casually, constantly, weaponized: "Home is boring. Home smells like bone dust. Can we go to the harbor?"

"Boring," Lena said, "is the entire point. Boring is what we fought for. Boring is expensive."

"How much did it cost?"

Lena kept her eyes on her carving. "More than you'll ever owe me."

"That's not an answer."

"When did you become a magistrate?"

"Mama says I ask questions like one."

The word dropped into the room and lay there. Lena's knife stopped moving. Isla, sensing the shift the way animals sense weather, went still.

"She talks about them," Isla said quietly. "In my head. Not the dead-words anymore. Just... remembering. They sold me because the sickness was killing everyone and the Tallowman said the mountain would save the town if someone paid. They thought—I think they thought—it wasn't really *me* they were selling. Just a piece. Just the part that would grow back." Her voice wobbled. "Do pieces grow back?"

Lena set down the knife. Crossed the room. Sat down beside the girl on the floor amid the drift of bone shavings and half-finished dragons.

"Some do," she said. "Some don't. The ones that don't, you build around. You build so well that someday the hole is just... part of the shape. Like the space in a whistle. The space is what makes the sound."

Isla leaned into her side, small and warm and solid, and after a long moment said, "Will you teach me the whistle-space kind of building?"

"I'm doing it right now."

* * *

It was nearly dusk, weeks into the thaw, when the knock came.

Not Ode's knock—Ode knocked like she was breaking the door down on principle. Not a customer's knock, tentative and guilty. This was hesitant, exhausted, the knock of someone who had walked a long way and wasn't sure of their welcome.

Lena opened the door on a woman perhaps thirty, salt-stained travel cloak, a boy of maybe ten half-hidden behind her skirts. The woman's eyes had the look Lena knew from mirrors: the look of someone who had done arithmetic with their soul and was still checking the sums.

"You're the carver," the woman said. "They said—you're the one the mountain fell for. They said you got a child back."

Lena heard Isla behind her, setting down her knife, going quiet.

"They said," the woman continued, and her voice cracked clean through, "that maybe you could help us. The law won't. The law says the sale was legal. The law says what's sold stays sold." She pulled something from her cloak—a scrap of paper, a contract, a ledger line. "But the mountain's gone. So who owns him now? Who owns any of us?"

Behind Lena, small footsteps crossed the floor. Isla appeared at her hip, looked up at the stranger's boy, and held out a dragon—bone-white, ash-pale, freshly carved, wings still rough at the edges.

"For you," Isla said. "It's not magic. But it's good for holding."

The boy took it. The woman looked at Lena, and Lena looked at the contract in the woman's hand, and somewhere in the walls of the shop—in the silence where the dead used to murmur—Lena felt the unmistakable shape of a beginning.

"Come in," she said. "Both of you. Tell me everything."
