## 21. Justice Outside the Law

Ode had smuggled contraband through three customs regimes, a plague quarantine, and one memorable winter when the harbor froze and he'd walked crates of unlicensed tea across the ice at midnight, whistling to keep his courage up. He liked to think nothing surprised him anymore.

Then, on a gray morning in the month after the mountain fell, he came up from the salt-docks to find a queue outside Lena Nakamura's shop.

Not a queue of customers. A queue of *grievers*.

They stood in the drizzle without umbrellas, patient as posts: a fishwife with her hands folded like she was waiting for a verdict; two brothers who kept looking at each other as if checking the other was still there; an old man holding a hat that had clearly been made for someone smaller. When Ode reached the door, the fishwife looked up and said, "Are you the advocate?"

"I'm the smuggler," Ode said. "The advocate is inside, arguing with a teapot."

* * *

He'd known something was wrong—right wrong, but wrong—for weeks before that. Word had come down the corpse-roads ahead of them, the way word always did, faster than feet: *the mountain is open, the court is ash, the dragon burns free.* In Verrin, people said it quietly, in the tone reserved for miracles and tax audits. Then the first letters arrived.

Ode ran letters. It was half his trade. Most were love notes or debts or apologies twenty years overdue. But after the mountain fell, the letters changed. They came from villages along the salt roads, from Verrin's own back lanes, and they all said versions of the same thing:

*I cannot remember my daughter's face.*

*I know I had a brother. There is a bed in my house that no one sleeps in and I do not know whose it is.*

*The court took my grief-memory in payment. I signed. I would sign again. But now I want it back and I do not know how to want a thing I cannot name.*

Ode read forty of them before he understood what they were. Not pleas. Inventories. The court beneath the mountain had kept ledgers, and ledgers outlive courts. Somewhere in the ash and collapse, those ledgers had scattered—or been carried out—and every soul-sale ever recorded was now a matter of record. People knew what had been taken from them. They knew the price they'd paid. What they didn't know was how to get any of it back, because the law of Verrin had a simple answer for all questions involving magic, which was: hang first, ask never.

So they came to Lena. Of course they came to Lena. She'd walked into a mountain with a magistrate and a child and come out with the child and no magistrate and no explanation anyone found satisfying, which in Verrin was as good as a confession of sainthood.

The trouble, Ode thought, watching her through the shop window that morning, was that Lena had the warmth of a salt-flat and the hospitality instincts of a locked door.

"—I carve charms," she was saying through the glass, to the fishwife, in the voice of a woman explaining tides to a cat. "They do nothing. I have said this for twenty years. It is even true now."

"They say you brought a girl back from the dead," the fishwife said.

"I brought a girl back from a *ledger*. Different thing. Legally worse."

"My son's laugh." The fishwife's hands twisted. "They took the memory of my son's laugh. I have his face—I wrote it down, after, everyone wrote things down—but I can't hear him anymore. You got your girl back. Tell me how."

Lena opened her mouth. Closed it. Ode could see the exact moment the argument died, because there was no argument. There was only a woman who had paid twenty years of voices to buy one child's soul, standing in front of another mother who wanted the same arithmetic done for her.

"Come back tomorrow," Lena said finally. "Bring your letter."

"I don't have a letter."

"Then bring your memory of not having a letter. It'll be the same size."

The fishwife blinked. Then—Ode saw it happen—the corner of her mouth moved. "That's a terrible thing to say to a grieving woman."

"Yes," Lena agreed. "It's why I work alone."

But she took the woman's name anyway, carving it into a scrap of bone with quick sure strokes, and hung it on a nail by the door where a dozen other scraps already dangled like strange pale fish.

Ode let himself in through the back, because front doors were for customers and he'd never been either.

"You're collecting them," he said.

"I'm writing them down."

"That's collecting, Lena. With extra steps."

She set down her knife and gave him the look she reserved for weather, children, and him—the three great uncontrollable forces. "There are sixty-three names on that nail. Sixty-three people who sold pieces of themselves to stay alive during the plague, or to buy bread, or because the Tallowman told them their grief was worth more than their grief was worth. The law says none of this happened. The law says if it happened, they're criminals. And they're standing in my shop asking me to fix it because I once did the impossible and am therefore legally required to do it forever."

"Well," Ode said, "when you put it like that it sounds unreasonable."

"It *is* unreasonable."

"Mm." He helped himself to her tea, which was terrible, which was why he always helped himself to it. "You know what I did last night? I read eleven more letters. A woman in Harrow-lane sold her memory of her wedding day to feed her sister. She wants it back. A dockhand sold the sound of his father's voice. An entire village upcoast sold—" he checked the letter, savoring it, "—'the reason we feel sad.' Which, frankly, sounds like a bargain, but they disagree."

"Stop."

"There's a man who sold his sense of smell and now complains constantly about how much better everything used to smell—"

"*Stop.*" But she was laughing, or doing the thing she did instead of laughing, which was pressing her lips flat while her eyes betrayed her completely. "Sixty-three names, Ode. Maybe six hundred by spring. What exactly am I supposed to do? March into the magistrate's hall and demand restitution from a legal system built on pretending souls don't exist?"

"No," Ode said. "Obviously not. You're supposed to do what you always do. Carve something that does nothing, sell it to frightened people, and let them believe whatever keeps them upright. Except this time—" he tapped the nail, the little bones clicking, "—this time it might actually be true. That's new for you. Try to seem pleased."

* * *

It went the way these things go, which is badly, then sideways, then somehow forward.

The first problem was the magistrate's hall. Corvan Hale was dead—that much had leaked, though the official story involved a tragic accident and the unofficial story, the one Ode traded in, involved a chain and a dragon and a man who chose his moment. Either way, the Purge laws hadn't died with him. The new magistrates were younger and hungrier and had built careers on the assumption that magic was a hanging offense, and here was the woman who'd toppled a mountain court, sitting in plain view with a wall full of names.

The second problem was that Lena refused to be quiet about it.

"The law says the sales were voluntary," she told the hearing room, on the gray afternoon when the fishwife's case came up—because of course it came up; Ode had made sure of it, greasing palms and planting letters the way he planted anything, generously and slightly illegally. "Voluntary. As if a starving mother negotiating with a broker is a fair contract. As if fear is consent."

"Magic is outlawed," said Magistrate Vess, who was young and had a voice like a filing cabinet. "Whatever was done was done illegally, by criminals, and the remedy for crime is prosecution."

"The remedy," Lena said, "for a woman who cannot remember her son laughing is not prosecution. I've read your statutes. All of them. Do you know what they call a soul-sale in the old codes? Before the Purge?" She let the silence stretch. "Neither do I. Because the Purge burned the codes too. You aren't enforcing law, Magistrate. You're enforcing a bonfire."

Ode, sitting in the gallery between a smuggler and a nun, thought: *she's going to hang.* And then he thought: *no, look at the room.* Because the room was full. Packed. Fishwives and dockhands and hollow-eyed villagers from upcoast, all of them silent, all of them watching Vess the way a crowd watches a man juggling knives—who wants him to fail, but more than that, wants to see what happens next.

Vess dismissed the case. "Pending further review," he said, which in magistrate language meant *I have no idea and I'm terrified*, and the crowd filed out past Lena in a silence so thick Ode could have carved it.

In the corridor afterward, the fishwife caught Lena's sleeve. "What do I do now?"

"Now?" Lena looked at the scrap of bone she still carried, the woman's name on it. "Now you write down everything you remember about not remembering. Every gap. Every place where something should be and isn't. Gaps have shapes. Shapes can be matched." She paused. "And come back Thursday. I'm starting a list."

"A list of what?"

"People," Lena said, with the air of a woman discovering the word for the first time. "Just... people."

* * *

By midwinter, the shop had changed.

The charms still hung in the window—inert, decorative, honestly labeled—but the back room had filled with shelves, and the shelves had filled with ledgers, real ones, kept in Ode's best hand because Lena's handwriting looked like a spider's funeral. Names. Dates. What was taken, what was paid, what remained. Cross-referenced against the Tallowman's recovered ledger pages, which had surfaced in six separate smuggling runs and which Ode had acquired through means he described, professionally, as "archaeology."

Lena held sessions three evenings a week. She taught people to map their own absences—to find the edges of a missing memory the way you find the edge of a missing tooth, with the tongue, patiently, until the shape of the hole tells you something about the shape of what was lost. She couldn't restore anything. She said so a hundred times a week. But she could *name* the loss, and put it in a ledger, and file it under a claim against a court that no longer existed—which meant, eventually, against whatever grew in its place.

And the strangest thing, Ode thought, watching a Tuesday session from the doorway, was that she'd gotten good at it. Not gentle, exactly. Lena would never be gentle; gentleness required a certain tolerance for nonsense. But she'd gotten *precise* in her kindness, the way a good knife is kind. When the old man with the small hat wept over his granddaughter's name, Lena didn't comfort him. She asked him what the hat smelled like, and when he said he didn't remember, she asked what he remembered *about* not remembering, and thirty minutes later he was laughing—wetly, raggedly, but laughing—at some absurd detail of his own grief, and Ode understood that this was the trick. She couldn't give people back their dead. She could give them back the *shape* of them, and sometimes the shape was enough to stand in.

"You're staring," Lena said, without turning around.

"I'm admiring."

"Don't. It'll ruin me." She blew out a candle. The session had run long; the last of the grievers had gone home into the snow. "Sixty-three became two hundred and eleven, by the way. Since you keep count."

"I keep count of everything. It's my whole personality." Ode came in and sat on her workbench, which she hated, which was why he sat there. "Two hundred and eleven claims against a dead court. Who's going to pay them?"

"Somebody will have to build something to pay them with." She turned the scrap of bone in her fingers—the fishwife's name, worn soft now from handling. "A court, maybe. Or whatever comes after a court. Something with ledgers that give back instead of take."

"That's not a shop, Lena. That's an institution."

"Don't say that word."

"Institution," Ode said sweetly. "Advocate. Founder. Oh—*foundress*. People love a foundress."

"I will drown you in the harbor."

"You'd miss me. I'm the only person who knows where the Tallowman's second ledger is buried."

That stopped her. She looked up sharply. "Second ledger?"

Ode had been saving this for three days, chewing on it like gristle, deciding. But the shop was warm, and the snow was coming down soft past the window, and Isla was asleep upstairs in the room that used to be storage, and some things you couldn't carry alone—not because they were heavy, but because they were *true*, and truths needed witnesses or they curdled.

"The Tallowman kept two books," Ode said. "Sales and purchases—we have the purchases, the pages that washed out of the mountain. But the sales book, the original..." He leaned forward. "It doesn't record what people sold. It records *who bought*. Every soul-fragment the court ever purchased has a destination. And Lena—" he watched her face go still, "—some of those destinations aren't in the mountain. Some of them are addresses. In Verrin. Living houses. Living *people* who've been carrying other people's grief-memories for twenty years, maybe without knowing why their dreams were never their own."

The candle smoke curled between them. Outside, the snow kept falling, patient as a queue.

"How many addresses?" Lena asked quietly.

Ode smiled, and it wasn't entirely a happy smile, but it was the honest one. "Enough to fill a second nail," he said. "Get your knife."
