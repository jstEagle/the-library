## 22. Freya and the Salt Again

The morning of the anniversary, Isla woke before the gulls did.

Lena knew this because she woke to the sound of small feet on the stairs, the particular careful thump of a child trying very hard to be quiet, and then the unmistakable clatter of her good carving knife being lifted from the bench. Lena lay in the dark of the loft above the shop and listened to the knife being set down again, picked up again, set down again — the moral struggle of a six-year-old conducted entirely in metal-on-wood — until finally it was taken for good, along with, by the sound of it, three apples and the whetstone.

She smiled at the ceiling beams. A year ago she would have gone down and scolded. A year ago she had still been learning how to be a person who slept through noises instead of cataloguing them as threats. Now she simply lay there and let the shop be robbed of its breakfast, because some thefts were a kind of blessing.

By the time Lena came downstairs, dressed and shawled against the salt wind, Isla was sitting on the front step with the knife across her knees like a sword she'd been knighted with, waiting with the terrible patience of children who have decided today is important and are prepared to make everyone else agree.

"You took my knife," Lena said.

"I was going to bring it back."

"Before or after you carved something?"

Isla considered this with great seriousness. Her hair had grown long over the year, dark as wet kelp, and she wore it in two braids that Ode had taught her to tie herself, badly, so that they always looked like ropes that had given up halfway. "After," she said. "Maybe."

Lena held out her hand. Isla put the knife in it, handle first, which was one of the rules, and then ruined the gravity of the moment entirely by handing her an apple too, also handle-first, which was not a rule but was very funny at that hour of the morning.

"We're going up," Isla said. It wasn't a question. On this day, every year from now on, they were going up. They had agreed on it last winter, sitting by the stove, when Isla had asked what the day was called and Lena had said she didn't know yet, and Isla had said, with the flat authority of a magistrate, "It's called Going Up," and so it was.

"The cliffs," Lena said. "I know."

"And we're bringing the wood." Isla pointed at the bundle of driftwood leaning against the doorframe, bone-white and sun-bleached, gathered over months from the tideline below. Lena had been saving the best pieces without quite admitting why. Some part of her hands remembered what they were for even when the rest of her didn't.

"I know," Lena said again. "Eat your apple. We'll go while the tide's out."

* * *

The path up from Verrin to the sea cliffs was old before anyone could remember how old, cut into the chalk in switchbacks worn smooth by generations of feet. Lena climbed it slowly now, not from age — she was not old, whatever Ode said about her knees — but because she had learned, this past year, the value of going slowly. Hurrying had been a way of life once. Hurrying and hiding and listening. She did less of all three.

Verrin fell away beneath them as they climbed: the grey terraces, the salt-stained roofs, the market square where the fish-cries started before dawn. The city looked smaller every time she saw it from above, and kinder. From street level it was all debts and curfews and neighbors who watched each other too carefully. From up here it was just a place where people lived, which was more than most places could say.

Halfway up, Isla stopped at the flat stone they always stopped at, because it was shaped like a chair if you squinted, and announced, "Rest point."

"Rest point," Lena agreed, and sat.

Below them the harbor glittered. Above them the sky did its enormous empty thing. And between — nothing. No murmur in the stones. No whisper under the wind. Lena had spent twenty years hearing the dead in every wall and gutter of Verrin, and now she heard only the sea, only the wind, only a child crunching an apple beside her with the focus of a woodcutter.

People who had never lost anything thought silence was peaceful. Lena knew better. Silence was a room with the furniture taken out. You kept reaching for the arm of a chair that wasn't there. You kept turning your head to answer someone who hadn't spoken.

But she was getting better at living in the empty room. That was the whole work of the year, really: getting better at it.

"Do you remember them?" Isla asked. She asked sometimes, always directly, always without warning, the way children ask the questions adults spend decades constructing polite detours around.

"No," Lena said. It didn't hurt the way it used to. "I don't remember any of them. Not their faces, not their voices, not their names." She paused. "Except I know there were names. I know I loved them. It's strange — it's like knowing there was a fire in a room after the fire's out. There's the warmth shape left on everything."

Isla chewed on this, literally, swallowing the last of her apple core. "Like the stove in the morning."

"Exactly like the stove in the morning."

"So you loved them the way the stove loves the kitchen."

Lena laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her. "That is either very wise or completely backwards, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Wise," Isla said firmly, and stood up. "Come on. Rest point's finished."

* * *

The cliffs at the top were grass and gorse and wind, and beyond them the sea went on being the sea, grey-green and enormous, throwing itself against the rocks below with the patience of something that had been doing it forever and intended to continue. Freya's Teeth, the locals called the rocks — black spires offshore where the water broke white. There was an old story about a woman named Freya who had walked into the sea rather than let the mountain take her soul, and the rocks were what remained of her stubbornness. Lena had never known if the story was true. These days she suspected most true stories were like that: a person, a choice, and a lot of rock standing in for the rest.

They found their spot — the hollow behind the gorse, out of the worst of the wind, with a flat shelf of stone that made a fine workbench. Lena laid out the driftwood pieces while Isla arranged them by size with the ruthless taxonomy of childhood, smallest to largest, and then rearranged them by color, and then by "which ones look friendliest."

"This one," Isla said at last, holding up a piece about the length of her forearm, bleached almost pure white, curved like something that had once meant to fly. "This one wants to be the dragon."

Lena took it. Turned it in her hands. The wood was smooth where the sea had worried it, rough where it had broken, and there was a knot near one end that would make a fine eye if she carved around it right.

"All right," she said. "But you're doing the tail."

"My tails are bad."

"Your tails are improving. Last month your dragon looked like it was escaping from its own back end. This month it'll only look mildly alarmed."

Isla giggled and settled cross-legged on the grass with her own piece of wood, and for a long while there was no sound but the wind, the sea, and the small industrious scrape of knives.

Lena carved slowly. This was another thing the year had taught her: slowness. When she had carved charms for sale — charms that did nothing, that had always done nothing, that were only shapes to hold onto in the dark — she had worked fast, hundreds of them, a factory of false comfort. But this was different. This was not for sale. This was for remembering, and you cannot hurry remembering, especially when the memory itself has been sold away and all you have left is the fact of it.

Because that was the arithmetic of it, the terrible sum she had paid at the auction beneath the mountain, and she would not pretend otherwise even now, even on a bright morning with a child beside her: she had traded twenty years of voices for one girl's soul. Every ghost. Every murmur in the walls. Maren — and she knew the name the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak; she knew it mattered, knew it had been the center of everything, and felt nothing but the warm shape where the feeling used to be. All the dead who had sustained her through the Purge years, who had kept her company in the long nights, whose whispers she had answered in secret for two decades — gone, spent, spent gladly, and the ledger balanced.

Some days she woke furious about it. Most days she woke grateful. Both were true. She had learned to hold both, the way you hold a hot kettle: carefully, and with both hands.

"Lena." Isla held up her wood. "Is this a tail or a wing?"

Lena looked. "That's a wing."

"It was supposed to be a tail."

"Then it's a dragon flying toward us instead of away. Wings forward. Very dramatic."

Isla studied her carving, then Lena, deciding whether this was kindness or truth. With Isla you could see the decision happening, the little furrow between her brows. Finally she nodded. "Forward wings," she said, satisfied, and went back to work.

* * *

The sun climbed. The tide turned below them, the water crawling back up the sand. Lena's dragon began to emerge from the driftwood: the arched neck, the folded wings, the long body tapering toward the tail Isla would finish. She carved the eye last, around the knot, leaving the dark wood showing through — and when it was done, the little dragon seemed to look at her, and she looked back, and something in her chest ached in a way that had no name and needed none.

She had never seen a dragon. She couldn't remember whether she'd ever seen one. But her hands knew the shape, the way her hands knew things her mind had lost, and she trusted the hands.

"Tell me the story again," Isla said. She always asked, on this day. "About the mountain."

"You know the story."

"I want to hear you tell it."

So Lena told it, the way she'd told it a dozen times, the version suitable for a child and mostly true. There had been a court under the mountain where souls were kept like coins, and a dragon whose fire kept those souls alive, and the fire was going out. There had been a girl whose soul had been sold twice — once by frightened parents, once by a broker with a soft voice — and a carver who went down to buy her back. There had been a magistrate who broke his own law to break a chain. There had been a trade, and the trade had cost the carver everything she carried of the past, and she had paid it, and she would pay it again.

"What happened to the magistrate?" Isla asked, as she always asked.

"He stayed," Lena said. "He held the chain-breaker in place while the fire came back, and the backlash—" She stopped, as she always stopped. "He stayed. He chose to stay."

"Was he sorry? For the bad things he did before?"

"Yes," Lena said. "I think he was sorry all the way down. I think sorry was the biggest thing about him, in the end. Bigger than the bad."

Isla absorbed this. Then: "Did he have a family?"

"I don't know." And she didn't — couldn't — and the not-knowing sat in her like a stone she had decided to stop trying to spit out. There were so many things she didn't know anymore. Whether Maren had forgiven her, or needed forgiving. What her teacher's face had looked like. Whether the voices in the walls had ever, in all those years, been saying anything kind. She had built a life on listening and now the listening was done, and the strangest thing, the thing she could hardly explain to anyone, was that she missed it without missing *it* — missed the shape of the companionship while being unable to summon a single companion.

But she remembered that she had loved them. That was the mercy buried in the bargain, or maybe just the nature of love: it outlasted its evidence. The stove remembers the fire. The kitchen stays warm.

"Done," Isla announced.

Lena looked over. Isla's dragon was smaller than hers, lumpier, with forward-pointing wings and a tail that had clearly lost an argument with the knife, and it was, objectively, magnificent. "It's looking at me," Isla said.

"They both are."

"Yours looks sad."

"Mine looks like it's thinking about something," Lena corrected. "There's a difference."

"What's it thinking about?"

Lena turned her dragon over in her hands. Bone-white as ash. Warm from the sun and from her grip. "Probably about how far it flew to get here," she said. "And how it's glad it did."

* * *

They ate lunch on the cliff — bread, hard cheese, the remaining two apples, one of which Isla claimed had been unfairly confiscated that morning and was therefore legally hers. Lena conceded the point. Below them, Verrin smoked gently with cook-fires; the fishing boats came back in a straggling line; somewhere down there Ode was probably arguing with a customs officer about the contents of a crate that definitely contained smuggled tea and definitely did not, and winning, the way Ode always won, by making the officer laugh at exactly the wrong moment.

Word had spread, over the year, quietly, the way word spreads in cities built on watching. People came to the shop now — not many, a trickle, a few a week — men and women with hollowed-out eyes who had signed things during the plague years, who had sold grief-memories or sold their children's futures or sold pieces of themselves they couldn't name, and who wanted help the law would never give. Lena couldn't give them back what they'd lost. Nobody could; the court was rubble and the ledger was ash. But she could listen — she was, it turned out, still good at listening, even to the living — and she could write letters, and she could stand in rooms where decisions got made and refuse to leave, and she could carve them charms that did nothing, which was somehow exactly what they needed. Something to hold. Something that proved a stranger believed their loss was real.

She had fought becoming important. She had lost, gracefully.

"Next year," Isla said, lying back in the grass with her hands behind her head, "we should carve two dragons."

"We carved two dragons today."

"Three dragons. One for the shop window. So people see it and think about the mountain."

"People will think we're selling them."

"We're not selling them."

"No," Lena agreed. "We're not."

Isla rolled onto her side and propped her chin on her hand and looked at Lena with that directness that still, after a year, could knock the breath out of her — the look of a child who had been to the bottom of the world and come back up carrying her own soul in her own two hands. "Lena. Are you sad today?"

Lena considered lying. She had been a liar for twenty years; it was a professional skill, like carving. But the whole point of the price she'd paid was that she didn't have to lie anymore, not even the small kind lies, not even the kind ones.

"A little," she said. "Sad and glad at the same time. Like rain on a sunny day."

"Sunshower," Isla said immediately, pleased with the word.

"Sunshower," Lena agreed.

Isla nodded, satisfied, and lay back down, and after a while she started humming — a tuneless, wandering hum, a child's noise, meaning nothing, signifying nothing except that she was safe and full and bored in the best way. Lena listened to it and thought: this is what the quiet sounds like now. Not an empty room. Just a room, with a child in it, humming.

She picked up her dragon and set it on the stone shelf facing the sea, where the wind off the water could get at it, and beside it Isla's, forward wings and all. Two small white shapes against the green and the grey. Somewhere under the mountain, if anything remained of anything, a fire had burned low and then blazed and then gone out forever, and the souls it had warmed had scattered like embers on the wind, and one of them — she was nearly sure, though she could not say why she was sure, though the sureness lived in her hands and her chest and nowhere else — one of them had once taught a girl to carve.

"Thank you," Lena said to the sea, quietly, not knowing exactly to whom, and meaning it completely.

Isla, without opening her eyes: "Who are you talking to?"

"The stove," Lena said.

Isla laughed so hard she had to sit up.

They stayed until the light went gold and long, and then they packed up the bread crumbs and the knife — handle first — and started down the path toward home, the shop, the ordinary evening ahead. Behind them the two little dragons kept their watch on the cliff, white as bone, white as ash, facing the sea.

And if, some nights afterward, Lena woke in the dark and reached for a voice that wasn't there — if she lay listening to the walls of the shop and heard, or thought she heard, or wished she heard, the faintest murmur like a teacher saying *well done, child, well done* — she didn't tell anyone. She just smiled at the ceiling beams, and held the warm shape of it, and went back to sleep.
