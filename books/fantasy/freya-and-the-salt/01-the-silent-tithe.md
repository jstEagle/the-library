## 1. The Silent Tithe

There are three rules to guiding smugglers through the Thornfell passes, and I have broken all of them at least once, which is why I'm still alive.

Rule one: never take a client who asks too many questions. Rule two: never sleep facing downhill. Rule three — and this is the one that matters, the one the mountain itself enforces — pay your tithe at every shrine you pass, even if the god behind it has been dead to you for twenty years.

Especially then.

The shrine on Kestrel Saddle was the usual sort: a cairn of black stones stacked hip-high by hands long gone stiff in their graves, a shallow bowl at its crown for offerings, and nothing else. No idol. Vess had never wanted an idol. "I am not a thing you can look at," my mother used to say, mimicking the old liturgy while she kneaded bread, flour to her elbows. "I am the thing that looks back." She'd thought it was funny. She'd been dead eleven years now, and I still couldn't decide whether it was funny or the most frightening sentence ever spoken over a loaf.

I dug in my pack and came out with the salt. Always salt. Salt was what you gave when you had nothing else — it preserved, it stung, it remembered being part of the sea. The old rites said salt was grief made useful, and I'd always suspected whoever wrote them down had been having a bad decade.

"Tithe," I said, because you're supposed to say something, and poured a thin white line into the bowl.

Nothing happened. Nothing had happened at any of the forty-one shrines between here and Lowmark, and nothing would happen at any of the others. Twenty years of salt and silence. Twenty years of me standing in the wind like an idiot, talking to a hole in the air, waiting for the thing that looks back to look back.

Behind me, someone snorted.

I didn't turn around. Turning around rewards snorting. That's rule four, the one I invented myself, and unlike the other three I've never once broken it.

"You actually believe in this," Tam said. He was nineteen, the youngest son of a wine merchant with more sons than sense, and he'd hired me to walk him and two mules' worth of unlicensed brandy over the pass ahead of the excise men. He had the particular confidence of a boy who had never yet lost anything he couldn't buy another of. "The shrines. The salt. You do it like it's *real*."

"It's real enough that you paid me extra to know where they are." I set the bowl straight. Wind had knocked it crooked, or maybe the mountain had; with shrines it was hard to tell neglect from displeasure. "Clients who mock the local customs tend to arrive at their destinations as stories. I prefer my clients arriving as clients."

"I'm not mocking." He wasn't, quite. He had that hungry look young men got when they smelled a story they could carry home. "I just mean — everyone knows the old gods stopped answering. My grandmother prayed her whole life and her knees still went. So why bother? What's the point of paying a toll to a tollhouse with no keeper?"

*What's the point.* As if I hadn't asked myself that question every morning for twenty years, kneeling in the dark with my palms open and empty, hating the habit and hating more the fact that I couldn't kill it. Habit is just grief that learned to dress itself and go outside. Nobody tells you that. They tell you grief is waves, grief is stages, grief is a country you eventually emigrate from. Nobody tells you it can shrink down small enough to fit inside a ritual, so that every time you pour salt into a stone bowl you're really saying *come back, come back, come back* to something that left without leaving a note.

"The point," I said, "is that nobody's confirmed the keeper's dead. Absent isn't dead. There's a difference, and the difference will kill you if you bet wrong."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He grinned, because nineteen-year-olds think catching you out is a game with prizes. "You *do* believe. The great Coral Nakamura, terror of the excise office, scourge of the Thornfell tolls — praying to a god that ghosted them."

"Ghosted." I let the word sit there a moment. Somewhere below us a hawk turned a slow circle over the valley, riding the thermals off stone that had been warm since before anyone's grandmother. "Walk, Tam."

We walked.

* * *

The high road through Thornfell isn't a road so much as an argument the mountain is having with itself, and smugglers are the punctuation. It climbs through pine and then through pine's bad dreams — twisted, wind-flayed things that grow sideways out of rock like they're trying to leave — and then above the treeline into a country of grey scree and sky, where the only sounds are your own boots and the wind reading aloud from a book no one wrote down.

I like it up there. That's the truth I don't put in my sales pitch. Down in the towns there are faces, and faces want things, and most days I don't have the surplus. Up on the passes there's just work: read the snow, read the cloud, count the switchbacks, keep the mules off the cornices. A problem with a shape to it. I could solve problems all day. It's everything else I've never gotten the hang of.

Tam talked the whole first day, which is standard. Clients talk to fill the silence because the silence up there is enormous and it makes them feel like they're disappearing into it. I let him. I answered maybe one question in ten, and the ones I answered were about the route, because route questions have answers.

By the second morning we'd made the saddle below the Widow's Teeth — two granite spires like a jaw mid-sentence — and camped in the lee of a shelf that some forgotten shepherd had walled against the wind a hundred years before I was born. The brandy mules were content. The sky was doing its evening trick, going bruise-colored at the edges while the peaks held the last light like coals under ash.

That was when Tam found the second shrine.

It sat fifty paces from camp, half-swallowed by the slope: another cairn, older than the first, its bowl furred with lichen the color of old blood. Someone had tried to build a windbreak around it once. The stones of the windbreak had fallen inward, as if whatever they were protecting had finally decided it wanted the weather.

"You're doing it again," Tam said, appearing at my shoulder with the timing of a cat. "Salt. Bowl. Little words. Come on. Give me something. My grandmother swore Vess answered her sister once — healed a fever in a night. Was that true? Did it ever answer *you*?"

I weighed the salt in my palm. Coarse grey sea-salt, bought in Lowmark from a woman who charged me double because she knew I'd pay it. Twenty years of double-priced salt. If I'd saved the money I could have retired to a coast somewhere and become a different kind of problem.

"Once," I said.

He went very still, the way people do when they realize the story is load-bearing.

"I was nine," I said. "My mother took me up to the high shrine at Midwinter, the one above the glacier. You don't go there anymore — the way's gone. She taught me the prayer. Not the formal one, the *home* one, the one families kept secret, passed mouth to mouth. Every family had a different word for Vess. A private name. Mine was—" I stopped. Some doors you don't open for clients. Some doors you barely open for yourself, and only at night, and only after the third drink. "Mine was mine. I said it. And the fire in the shrine bowl — there was a fire then, shrines kept fire then — it stood up. Straightened. Like a dog hearing its name."

"And?" Tam breathed.

"And it answered." I tipped the salt into the lichen-furred bowl. It hissed faintly, though there was no moisture to hiss against. "Not in words. In — weight. You know how a room feels when someone walks in behind you? Like that. The whole mountain felt *attended*. For about as long as it takes to blink twice. And then it was gone, and my mother cried, and I didn't understand why until much later."

"Why did she cry?"

"Because it had heard us the whole time," I said. "Every prayer. All those years of silence — it had been listening the entire time and choosing not to speak. She said—" I heard my mother's voice come out of my mouth, and hated it, and let it finish anyway. "She said, *it knows we're here and it doesn't care, and that's worse than being alone.*"

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere far off, snow let go of a slope with a sound like a held breath released.

"So it abandoned you," Tam said. "And you still pay it. Still pray. Why?"

Because the alternative is admitting those twenty years were wasted, and I am not brave enough for that arithmetic. Because my mother died still whispering that private name, and throwing away the name feels like burying her twice. Because hope is a debt you can't stop servicing even after the creditor stops answering letters. Because—

"Insurance," I said.

"That's a lie."

"It's a very good lie. I've been polishing it for years." I stood, brushed the scree dust off my knee. "Get some sleep. We cross the Teeth at first light, and the light comes late in the gap. You want to be moving when it does, not admiring it."

He went. He was young; he could be ordered to bed and still feel like he'd won the argument. I stayed by the shrine a while longer, because that was the other part of the ritual, the part I'd never said out loud: after the salt, you waited. The old liturgy said you waited to see if the offering was accepted. Twenty years of waiting. Twenty years of watching a bowl of salt do absolutely nothing while the wind erased it grain by grain, and every single time, some traitorous chamber behind my ribs leaning forward anyway. *Maybe this one. Maybe now.* I despised that chamber. I fed it daily.

"Come back," I said to the bowl, very quietly, in the word that was mine and my mother's and no one else's. Then, because the mountain was listening even if the god wasn't — mountains always listen — I added, "Or don't. But send a sign either way, you coward, so I can stop."

The salt sat in the bowl. The wind took its share. The dark came down like a lid.

* * *

First light in the gap was a rumor, then a promise, then a slow gold bleed along the eastern teeth of the range. We broke camp in the blue hour, mules grumbling, Tam wrapped in enough wool to be mistaken for cargo himself. The crossing was routine — I'd done it thirty times, knew where the path cut behind the northern spire, knew the stretch of ice you crossed at a trot so your weight never settled, knew the cairn halfway across where travelers had left ribbons and buttons and, once, a full set of dentures, offerings to a god or to luck or to nothing at all.

We were a hundred paces from the far side when Tam stopped walking.

"Coral." His voice had changed. All the nineteen years had dropped out of it. "Coral, the shrine. Back there. Look."

I looked back across the ice field, back along the way we'd come, to the little cairn with its bowl of lichen and its windbreak collapsed inward like cupped hands.

The salt was burning.

No flame touched it. No ember, no spark, no source. The salt itself glowed — a low, deep gold, the exact color of that fire in the high shrine twenty years ago, the fire that had stood up when it heard its name. It burned without heat and without smoke, steady as a held note, and the light of it lay across the ice in a long stripe that pointed — I checked, because my trade is checking — directly at us.

At *me*.

The mules felt it before I understood it. Both of them swung their heads toward the burning bowl and went silent, ears forward, the way animals go silent when something vast changes its breathing nearby. The wind, which had been worrying at our backs all morning, stopped. Not gentled. *Stopped*, the whole pass holding its lungs.

And beneath my boots — beneath the ice, beneath the stone, beneath twenty years of silence — I felt the mountain lean in.

Tam's hand found my sleeve. "Is that—" he started, and couldn't finish, because there was no word for it that a wine merchant's son would know, and only three or four that I knew, and none of them were *insurance*.

Twenty years I'd asked for a sign. Any sign. A flicker, a warmth, a dream, a coincidence I could squint at sideways.

Be careful what you ask a god for. Sometimes the answer is *yes*, and sometimes yes is the sound a trap makes when it closes.

The salt burned on, patient as a debt, and far below us — so far below that no map I owned admitted there was anything there at all — something ancient cleared its throat and began, in a voice like a landslide remembering how to speak, to say my name.
