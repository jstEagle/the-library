## 2. Smoke from a Cold Altar

The storm found us an hour before the shrine did, which was typical of Thornfell. The mountains never gave you what you wanted when you wanted it. They waited until you were tired and wet and your patience had worn down to the nub, and then they offered you exactly enough shelter to make you grateful, which is how you learned to need them.

"Shrine ahead," I called back to the caravan. "We'll hole up there till this passes."

Tam Voss rode up beside me, hood plastered to his skull, looking like a drowned nobleman pretending he wasn't one. He was the youngest of the three clients on this run — silk and salt-cured fish, nothing that would hang us if we were caught, nothing worth dying for either. He'd been asking questions since Lowmark. Young men always ask questions until someone teaches them not to.

"You said there was a shrine at the last pass too," he shouted over the wind.

"There is."

"And you left an offering."

"I did."

"Why?" His horse slipped on scree and he cursed it with the particular fluency of someone who'd only recently learned to curse well. "The god of these passes hasn't answered a prayer in twenty years. Everyone knows it. My father says the old gods are dead and the shrines are just rocks now."

"Your father sounds like a man who's never needed anything he couldn't buy."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

I spurred ahead before he could follow. It wasn't cruelty. Well — it wasn't *only* cruelty. But there are questions I've spent twenty years learning not to answer, mostly because I don't have answers that would survive daylight. Why does a mercenary who sells her sword to smugglers still carry salt and barley up frozen switchbacks for a god who stopped listening? Why does she do it in the dark, alone, so no one sees? Why does she hate herself for it every single time?

Because habit is just grief wearing work clothes. That's why. And grief doesn't care whether anyone's watching.

* * *

The shrine sat in a fold of the mountain like something the rock had swallowed and half-digested. It had been a proper waystation once — I knew because I'd known it whole, or near enough. A roof of slate, walls of fitted stone, a fire-pit where travelers warmed themselves and paid their tolls in grain and song. Now the roof had fallen in on one side, the walls wore beards of lichen, and the altar stood open to the sky like a mouth that had forgotten how to close.

I got the clients settled under the surviving stretch of roof, saw to the horses, built a small fire from wet wood and stubbornness. Tam watched me the whole time with those questions still stacked behind his eyes. The other two — the Ferro brothers, professional and incurious, the best kind of clients — rolled into their bedrolls and were asleep inside ten minutes. Smugglers sleep the way soldiers do: whenever the road allows, because it won't allow long.

Then I took my offering and went out into the rain.

Salt, first. A pinch from the little horn I keep against my ribs, scattered across the altar stone the way my mother taught me, the way her mother taught her, all the way back to whoever first looked at a mountain and thought *something lives in there and it is watching me.* Then barley, a handful, from the pouch that never ran empty no matter how lean the winters got. I've told myself for years that I keep it stocked out of professionalism — guides pay tolls, that's the trade — but I restock it in every town we pass through, carefully, secretly, the way other people maintain vices.

And then the prayer. The old words. The ones in the language nobody speaks anymore except at altars, and barely there.

I won't write them here. Some things stay between me and the stone.

Twenty years ago, the god of Thornfell — Vess, the Quiet One, the Listener Under Stone — answered prayers the way a hearth gives heat: constantly, unremarkably, without being asked twice. Travelers swore by the passes. Lost children walked home. My mother said Vess once held a collapsing mine-shaft shut for three days while they dug out forty miners, and that when the shaft finally came down it came down soft, like a hand opening.

Then, one winter, silence. No catastrophe. No omen. Just — nothing. Prayers went up and fell back down like stones dropped into a well with no bottom. The priests drifted away. The shrines crumbled. The world moved on to gods who answered, or decided it didn't need gods at all.

Everyone grieved and got over it. I did everything but get over it.

So I said the words into the rain, kneeling on wet stone, feeling like a fool and a fraud and something worse than both, which is sincere. I felt the familiar nothing rise up to meet the prayer. The familiar absence. The shape of a silence I'd memorized the way you memorize a dead person's face — precisely, painfully, knowing it will never change again.

I was about to stand up when the salt caught fire.

Not the barley. Not the altar wood, which was sodden through anyway. The salt — a thin line of white crystals on black stone — lit with a flame the color of deep water, blue-green and slow, and it burned *upward*, the flames falling toward the sky instead of rising, and the rain around the altar stopped falling as if the storm itself had flinched.

I did not move. Twenty years of mountain work teaches you that sudden things are usually dangerous things, and that the wise response to the impossible is stillness while you figure out which kind of impossible has found you.

"Coral Nakamura." 

The voice came from everywhere the rain wasn't. From under the stone. From under the stone under the stone. It was not loud. It didn't need to be. It was the voice you hear in the pause after an avalanche starts, quiet and total, the sound of something very large deciding something.

It knew my name. Not Coral — everyone knows Coral, Coral's on the manifests and the bounty boards. It used the full name. The one given at a hearth-altar thirty-odd years ago, in a village that doesn't exist anymore, spoken by a priestess who poured the salt herself. A name I hadn't heard aloud since she died. A name I'd half-forgotten I had.

"Vess," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Inside, something twenty years old was tearing along seams I'd thought were scars. "You're late."

"*I am never late. I am exact. You mistook my patience for absence.*"

"Twenty years of silence reads as absence from down here."

"*Silence is also an answer. You never asked what question it answered.*"

The blue-green flame climbed higher, feeding on nothing, and I became aware — slowly, the way you become aware of a hand on the back of your neck — that the storm had gone utterly silent around the shrine. Rain hung in the air beyond some invisible boundary, suspended, glittering in the altar-light like a cage of beads. The horses, back under the roof, weren't screaming. They should have been screaming. That frightened me more than the voice did.

"What do you want?" I asked. "If this is about the offerings — I kept them. Every pass, every year. Even when—"

"*I know what you kept. I counted. Do you know what else I counted, Coral Nakamura? Debts. Oaths. The small print of devotion, which mortals sign in blood and forget in a season.*"

My stomach went cold in a way the storm couldn't account for. "I don't follow."

"*When you were nine years old, your mother prayed at this altar. She prayed for your life. You had the fever — the grey fever, the one that empties villages. Do you remember?*"

I remembered. Of course I remembered. I remembered the fever dreams most of all: the walls breathing, the ceiling raining ash, a woman made of smoke sitting at the foot of my bed singing in a language I didn't know. I'd assumed the smoke-woman was the fever talking.

"She prayed," Vess said, "*and I answered. I pulled you back from the grey country. And the price was written into the answering, as prices always are, as your mother knew, as she accepted, as she wept, and as she never told you.*"

The rain stayed hanging in the air. The flame burned upward. Somewhere behind my ribs my heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like the answer to.

"What price?"

"*A soul, promised to the court beneath the mountain. Promised forty years past. Kept in trust. And now the trust is called due, because the fire below is going out, Coral Nakamura, and when it goes out, everything owed must be collected — everything, from everyone, all at once. Your god included.*"

"The court beneath—" I'd heard the stories. Every guide had. The undercourt, the lords of the deep places, the ones smugglers paid extra to avoid angering and extra again to avoid mentioning. Stories for frightening apprentices. "That's a story."

"*So was I, until tonight.*"

"And what soul? Whose soul?"

The flame guttered. For a moment — and I would spend years turning this moment over, trying to decide if I'd imagined it — the voice sounded almost gentle. Almost sorry.

"*A child's. Sold decades ago, before your birth or near it. Small. Bright. Kept beside the last dragon-fire to feed it, because a soul freely loved burns longer than any fuel. When the fire dies, the debt comes due, and the collection falls to the oath-bearer. To you. You were bound the night your mother prayed. You have been mine since you were nine years old, Coral. You simply never read the contract.*"

I stood up. My knees were shaking and I made them stop, the way you make hands stop shaking before you draw a blade, because shaking hands get people killed.

"No," I said.

"*No?*"

"No. Whatever you think I owe, whatever contract my mother signed — I'm not delivering a child's soul to anyone. Not to a court, not to a god, not to you. Especially not to you. Find another collector."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The suspended rain trembled in its invisible cage.

"*You misunderstand,*" Vess said, and now there was nothing gentle anywhere in it. "*This is not a request I am making. This is a debt I am calling. You may refuse. Refusal has consequences. Everything has consequences; that is the oldest law under stone. But hear this before you choose: the fire is guttering even now. And when it dies — every prayer ever answered becomes a leash. Every miracle ever granted becomes a hook. Every mortal who ever knelt at an altar and was heard belongs to whatever holds the ashes. Including you. Especially you.*"

"So refuse the leash," I said. "You're a god. Unmake the debt."

A sound then, from under the world — I can't call it laughter, though it laughed. More like the mountain shifting its weight.

"*Gods cannot unmake debts. Gods ARE debts, Coral. We are the promises mortals made and could not take back. I am the promise that someone listens. And I am telling you, oath-bearer, collector, child-of-the-fever: go down. Go under. Bring back what was sold, or bring it where it was sold to go — the choice of direction is yours. The choice of obedience is not. You have until the last flame gutters out.*"

"How long is that?"

"*Less time than you hope. More than you fear. Now run, little keeper. Run, and tell yourself on the road that you refused. See how far the lie carries you.*"

The flame snapped out. The rain came down all at once, every suspended drop released together, hitting the shrine-roof and the scree and my shoulders like applause, like a verdict, like the sky closing a book.

* * *

I don't remember walking back under the roof. I remember standing there afterward, soaked, watching the clients sleep, listening to the storm hammer the fallen half of the shrine. Tam woke at some point — young men sleep shallow — and looked at me, and for once in his pampered life asked nothing. Maybe it was my face. Maybe it was that the horses still wouldn't settle, circling their pickets with their ears flat, staring down the mountain the way animals stare at things people can't see.

I should have slept. Guides sleep when the road allows. Instead I sat by our little fire and turned it over, the way you turn a blade over to check for flaws.

Here's what I knew. Something had spoken with Vess's voice, and it had known my secret name, and it had moved a storm like a curtain. Here's what I didn't know: whether it was really Vess, or something wearing Vess the way a poacher wears a uniform. Because that was the thing, wasn't it — the thing I kept circling and refusing to land on. Twenty years of silence, and the moment prayers start getting answered again — the moment, I'd heard whispers in Lowmark, the river-towns' little shrines flickering back to life, sick children waking whole, lost boats finding harbor — the moment the old gods wake, one of them wakes straight into *my* altar and calls in a debt I never signed.

Coincidence. Probably coincidence. Mountains are full of worse.

But underneath the fear, underneath the fury, there was something else, and it shamed me more than either. Because when the voice said *you have been mine since you were nine years old*, some small, starved, stupid part of me had *warmed*. Had thought: *it knows me. It counted my offerings. It was listening all along.* Twenty years of praying into a well, and the well finally spoke, and my first feeling wasn't terror.

It was relief.

I fed that feeling to the fire and watched it burn and hated myself with the clean, efficient hatred of long practice. Grief makes idiots of us. Starved dogs don't ask who's holding the leash.

Toward dawn the storm broke apart the way storms do in Thornfell — suddenly, grudgingly, leaving the air scrubbed raw and the peaks dripping. I got the clients up, got them moving, got us onto the high track east while the light was still grey. Tam rode beside me for a while without talking, which I was grateful for, and then talked anyway, which I wasn't.

"You look like you saw a ghost," he said.

"Worse. A client with opinions."

He almost smiled. Then his eyes went past my shoulder, up the flank of the peak we were skirting, and his face changed. "Coral. What's that?"

I turned. Below us, maybe two hundred feet down the slope, the morning mist lay in the valleys like spilled milk. And it was moving. Not drifting — moving. All of it, every valley, every fold of the range as far as I could see, flowing slowly, steadily, in the same direction. Downhill, yes. But also *inward*. Toward the deep heart of Thornfell, toward the roots of the range, the way water moves toward a drain. The way breath moves toward a listening ear.

The horses felt it too. So did I, in my teeth, in the scar tissue of an old oath I hadn't known I carried. The whole mountain range — stone and mist and the things that live under both — leaning in. Listening. Waiting to see what the small stubborn creature on the high track would do next.

"Keep riding," I said, and my voice didn't shake, and I counted that as the day's single victory. "Don't stop for anything. And Tam?"

"Yeah?"

"If anyone asks — we took the eastern pass because of the weather."

He nodded, solemn as a funeral, and I put my heels to my horse and led us down into a world that had started, quietly and without permission, to listen back.
