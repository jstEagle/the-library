## 3. The Priestess Who Drowned Her God

There are three reliable ways to learn that your god has abandoned you. The first is the slow way: the prayers go unanswered one by one until you realize you've been talking to a wall for years and the wall has better things to do. The second is the official way: a tribunal of men in gray robes reads out your crimes in a voice like wet paper while they take your vestments, your name from the rolls, and — this is the part nobody warns you about — your chair. You don't appreciate a chair until someone else is sitting in it.

I got both of those. The third way is the fast way, and I don't recommend it.

But let me start where all my troubles start, which is at a river, drinking.

Lowmark sits where the Vell slows down and gets ideas above its station, widening into something almost sea-like, all mudflats and gull noise and warehouses that lean on each other like old drunks. It's a smuggler's town, a pilgrim's town, a town for people going somewhere else who ran out of road money. I had been there four months, which by my standards was practically settling down. I poured ale at the Drowned Bell, I read fortunes with a deck missing two cards (the Tower and the Sun, which tells you everything about how my luck ran), and every morning before dawn I went down to the water and did the thing I was no longer permitted to do.

I prayed.

Not properly. Not with the forms. You can't do the forms without the vestments, and anyway the forms were the crime — that was the whole ugly business. No, I prayed the way a drowning woman prays: quickly, quietly, and with no expectation of rescue. *Vess, who walks the high passes, who keeps the salt and the snow-line, hear me.* Twenty years of silence from every god worth the name, but I'd been raised on Vess since before I could walk, and you don't unlearn a god like you unlearn a language. You just stop speaking it out loud and hope nobody hears the accent.

That morning, somebody answered.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened, because afterward everyone — Coral especially — wanted me to have hallucinated it, and I refuse.

The river was gray. The gulls were being their usual selves, which is to say screaming about nothing. I knelt on the mud-slick stones below the fish-market stairs, said the words, and the water stopped.

Not froze. Stopped. The current simply quit, mid-flow, the way a conversation quits when someone important enters the room. And under the surface — under, not in — there was a light moving upstream against the current, patient as a finger tracing a line of text, and a voice that did not so much speak as occur:

*The debt is called due.*

Then the river remembered itself and went on flowing, and I sat down very hard on a stone and laughed until the fishwives upstairs came out to look at me.

Here is what you need to understand about me, so you can calibrate your sympathy accordingly: I am not a good woman. I was a priestess of Vess for eleven years, and I was good at it, which is worse. I could make a grieving widow feel held. I could make a warlord weep into his offering bowl. And when the order needed certain problems handled — heresies, schisms, a splinter congregation upriver that had started saying Vess spoke *to* them instead of *through* us — I was the one they sent, because I believed, absolutely, that the silence of the god was holy and anyone claiming otherwise was selling something.

Then I found out the silence wasn't holy. It was purchased. But that's later. That's the part with the knife and the well, and I'm not ready to tell you that part yet, and honestly you're not ready either.

The tribunal called it apostasy. I called it — well. For three years I called it nothing, because naming it meant thinking about it. I drowned my god the only way a defrocked priestess can: I stopped listening, moved to a river town, and poured ale for smugglers.

And then the god spoke through a river, and the first word out of its mouth after twenty years was *debt*, and I thought: *of course*. Of course it wakes for an accounting. Gods are like creditors that way. They never write when they owe *you*.

* * *

I saw them that same evening, which I choose to interpret as fate rather than coincidence, because the alternative is that Lowmark is simply small.

The Drowned Bell fills up around sundown with the kind of clientele who pay in coin of uncertain parentage. I was working the long table, refilling cups and dodging hands, when the door opened and the room did that thing rooms do — a ripple of attention, quick and then carefully suppressed — and I looked up and felt my stomach drop through the floorboards into the cellar.

Coral Nakamura.

You have to understand: I knew that face the way you know a scar on your own hand. Three years ago, before the tribunal, before the well, my order had hired a guide to track a runaway priestess through the Thornfell passes. They'd chosen the mercenary carefully — someone who knew the shrines, someone the mountain roads trusted. I'd seen them exactly once, across a shrine courtyard at dusk, and I'd run, and I'd gotten away, and I'd told myself for three years that the person hunting me was a shape, a silhouette, a job.

They were not a shape. They were a person with a tired face and salt-gray streaks coming in at the temples and the particular posture of someone carrying weight they've stopped noticing. And they had just walked into my tavern with a caravan client in tow, and they were looking around the room the way professionals look around rooms, and I had perhaps four seconds to decide what kind of woman I was.

Option one: turn them in. There was still a price, I assumed; there's always a price. I could send a boy to the harbormaster's office, collect whatever bounty survived my defrocking, and be gone by morning with actual money for once.

Option two: finish pouring this ale.

I finished pouring the ale. I'd like to tell you it was principle. It was mostly that my hands were full and my feet hadn't caught up with my panic.

Coral took a corner table, ordered without ceremony, and ate like someone who'd learned to eat fast in company and never unlearned it. I made myself walk over. Refill. Smile. The smile of a tavern worker, which is armor-grade if you forge it right.

"Passing through?" I asked. My voice held. Small mercies.

"Guiding," Coral said. "Client wants the high pass north of Thornfell inside a week." A pause, and those eyes came up — pale, flat, the eyes of someone who has looked at a lot of horizons and found most of them wanting. "You're staring."

"You look familiar," I said, which was true in the way a knife wound looks familiar.

"People say that." They went back to their food. Dismissed. And I should have walked away, I genuinely should have, because whatever I was about to do, doing it in front of the whole taproom was idiotic.

But here's the thing about shame: it doesn't make you careful. It makes you *hungry*. Three years of silence, and then a river speaks, and then the one person alive who knows what I did walks into the room — and some starved animal in my chest stood up and said *tell someone*.

So I leaned down, close enough that only they could hear, and I said:

"The river stopped this morning. At the fish-market stairs. Something moved upstream against the current and said the debt was called due."

Coral went very still. Not dramatically — no dropped cup, no wide eyes. Just a stillness like a held breath, like a door closing softly somewhere inside them. When they spoke, their voice was low and even. "Taverns water the ale. Maybe it watered you too."

"I don't drink on shift."

"Everyone drinks somewhere."

"Coral Nakamura," I said, and watched the name land like a thrown stone, "hired by the Order of the Quiet Veil three years ago to bring back a runaway priestess. Dead or alive, though alive paid better. I know because I'm the priestess."

And there it was — the flicker. Not guilt, exactly. Recognition of debt, maybe, or just the professional's discomfort at seeing a job walk in wearing an apron. "You got away," Coral said.

"I got away." I set down the pitcher. My hands were not steady anymore; I let them not be. "And now prayers are being answered, and mine was one of them, and I need to get to Thornfell, and you are the only person I know of who can get me there alive. So I'm asking. Not turning you in. Asking."

* * *

We talked in the alley behind the Bell, because Coral wouldn't do it indoors — "walls with ears, and these ones drink" — and because apparently being hunted by gods makes people cautious in ways that look like paranoia until you remember what woke up yesterday.

"You heard it answer," Coral said. "Your prayer. What did you pray?"

I hesitated. This is the part where I confess something embarrassing, so bear with me. "I didn't pray for forgiveness. Or absolution, or anything useful. I prayed—" I looked at the muddy cobbles. "*Show me it wasn't a lie.* That's it. That's the whole prayer. Every morning for three years. Show me the silence meant something, show me I didn't drown a god that was actually listening, show me—"

"That you weren't wrong."

"That I wasn't wrong." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "And it answers after twenty years and the first thing it says is *the debt is called due*. Which means either I was wrong, or I was right and it doesn't matter, and I genuinely cannot tell which would be worse."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere behind us a cat knocked over a crate with the confidence of a much larger animal. When they finally spoke, it wasn't what I expected.

"What did you do? To get defrocked. The real version, not the tribunal version."

Ah. There it was. The toll on the bridge, and fair enough — I'd named their sin; they wanted mine.

So I told them. About the splinter congregation upriver, the ones who claimed Vess spoke to them directly. About how my superiors sent me to quiet them, and how I found a village of farmers and fishermen kneeling in a barn saying they'd heard the god's voice in the wind off the fields, gentle, ordinary, *theirs*. About how I reported it as heresy because that was my job, and how the order's response came back sealed in black wax, and what the response required.

"There was a well," I said. "Behind their meetinghouse. The order's instruction was that the source of the false prophecy be silenced permanently. I was the instrument. That's the phrase they used. *Instrument.*" My voice did something I hated. "It was a girl. Sixteen. She'd been hearing the god since she was nine. I held her head under the water myself, because I believed the silence was sacred, and a god that talks to farm girls in barns couldn't possibly be our god, and if it wasn't our god then it was a demon, and demons drown."

Coral said nothing. The cat did it again, louder, offended by our solemnity.

"Afterward," I went on, because stopping was impossible now, the same way it's impossible to stop a cart halfway down a scree slope, "afterward I went back to my cell and I prayed, and for the first time in eleven years I prayed *angry*, and I said: if that was you in the wind, if that girl was yours, then I want the truth or I want nothing. And I got nothing. Twenty years of nothing. And I thanked the nothing, because nothing meant I hadn't murdered a messenger. Do you see? I built my whole penance on the silence. The silence was my alibi."

"And now the silence ended," Coral said quietly.

"And now the silence ended, and the first thing it says is *debt*, and I don't know if the debt is the girl's life or my lie or something older than both, but I know I can't stand in Lowmark pouring ale while the sky starts talking. If the gods are waking, I want to be standing in front of one when it does. Even if it's to be judged. *Especially* if it's to be judged. Judgment would at least be an answer."

I ran out of words there, which was probably for the best, and stood in the alley breathing the smell of river mud and spilled beer, waiting for the mercenary to tell me no.

Coral looked at me for a long time. And I saw something pass over their face that I recognized, because I'd been wearing it for three years in every reflective surface: the look of a person deciding whether to hand a stranger a piece of themselves and risk never getting it back.

"Twenty years," Coral said at last. "I've paid tribute at every shrine on every pass I've crossed. Every season. Never missed. And it never once answered." A pause. "Until yesterday. At a ruined altar above the Kettle Gap, my offering lit without kindling, and it said the same thing yours said. *The debt is called due.*"

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the river air. "The same debt?"

"I don't know. I ran. That's the honest answer — twenty years of faithful service and when it finally spoke, I ran into a storm like a child." Their mouth twisted. "So. You want passage to Thornfell. I'm going to Thornfell, because I don't believe in coincidence anymore, and because if a god calls in a debt, I want to know the amount before it takes it out of my hide. You can come. On conditions."

"Name them."

"One: you don't preach. Whatever's left of your priesthood stays in Lowmark. Two: you pull your weight on the road — cook, haul, stand watch, whatever I say. Three—" and here they paused, and the lamplight from the alley mouth caught their face, and for one unguarded second they looked less like a hunter and more like what they probably were underneath: someone else the god had been silent to, all those years, while they kept setting out offerings anyway. "Three: you tell me everything. When you remember it. The order, the girl, all of it. Because if the gods are waking up and calling debts, I want to know what kind of god we're walking toward. And you're the closest thing to an expert I've got."

"An expert," I repeated. "On drowning them, you mean."

"On what they cost," Coral said. "Same thing, maybe."

We shook on it there in the alley, a defrocked priestess and a mercenary with a god-shaped hole where their faith used to be, two people bound together by the oldest covenant there is: shared disgrace. It was not friendship. It was not trust. It was two drowning people agreeing to hold each other under slightly more slowly, and I think we both knew it, and shook anyway.

* * *

I went back inside to settle my tab and collect my things — a bag, a knife, a fortune-telling deck with two holes in its heart. Tam, the young client traveling with Coral, watched me pack with open curiosity, the way the young watch anything that might be a story.

"Where's she going?" he asked Coral, not quietly enough.

"North," Coral said.

"To the shrines?"

Coral glanced at me, and something passed between us that wasn't quite warmth but was adjacent to it, the way a fever is adjacent to health. "To ask one a question," they said.

I slung my bag. And as we stepped out into the Lowmark night — gulls screaming, river running, the whole indifferent world going about its business — I felt it again, faint, far off, upstream against the current: that patient light, moving.

It was heading for the mountains.

So were we.
