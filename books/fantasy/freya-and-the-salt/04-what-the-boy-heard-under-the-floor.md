## 4. What the Boy Heard Under the Floor

The job came to me the way most jobs do in Lowmark: through a door I should have kept shut.

I was sitting in the Salt Cellar — which is not a cellar at all but a tavern that smells like brine and bad decisions — counting what remained of my coin after the storm cost me two mules and most of my dignity. The innkeep's boy set down a cup I hadn't ordered and said, "Gentleman over there wants a word."

There were three gentlemen. One was fat, one was nervous, and one was twelve years old, wearing clothes worth more than my entire outfit of mules, alive or dead. The fat one did the talking. The nervous one did the sweating. The boy did something worse: he stared at me like he already knew how this conversation would end.

"Coral Nakamura," the fat man said. "Lord Aldric Vane sends his regards."

"Lord Aldric can keep them. Regards are heavy, and I'm traveling light."

"He wants his son found."

"Then he wants a tracker, not a smuggler. Try the guild."

"The guild tried. The boy doesn't want to be found." The fat man leaned in, lowering his voice as if the tavern full of drunks were listening, which, to be fair, they always were. "He ran three weeks ago. We've had him twice. Both times he walked out of locked rooms. Both times he said the same thing when they caught him."

"Let me guess. He heard a voice."

The fat man's face did something complicated. "He said the mountain told him where the doors were."

I looked at the boy properly then. Perrin Vane had his father's jaw and none of his father's certainty. He sat with his hands flat on the table, very still, the way you sit when you're trying not to hear something. I knew that posture. I'd worn it for twenty years.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the price."

"I've heard the job. That's enough."

"Five hundred crowns."

I drank my unordered cup slowly, because it gave me time to do arithmetic I didn't want to do. Five hundred crowns was a new caravan. Five hundred crowns was never taking another job from men who smelled of tallow and fear. Five hundred crowns was also, I suspected, blood money with extra steps, because rich men don't pay five hundred crowns for a runaway; they pay it for silence about *why* he ran.

"Four hundred," I said, "and he walks out of Thornfell on his own feet or not at all. I don't carry children over passes. They're heavier than they look and they complain more than mules."

The fat man exhaled like I'd agreed to something reasonable instead of something stupid. The nervous man paid. And the boy — Perrin — finally spoke, and his voice was quiet and level and wrong in a way I couldn't name yet.

"You're Coral," he said. "You pray at the shrines even though nothing answers."

* * *

We left Lowmark two days later, and I'll be honest: I spent both of those days trying to find a reason to renege. Not because of the money. Because of the way he'd said it. *You pray at the shrines even though nothing answers.* Nobody knows that. My clients know I stop at shrines — it's part of the service, tribute for safe passage, standard practice on the high roads. What they don't know is that I say words at those shrines. Old words. Words my mother taught me before she taught me anything else, in a language so old the priests call it *pre-liturgical*, which is a fancy way of saying nobody's supposed to remember it.

Perrin knew it. Or he knew *something*. And a boy who knows things he shouldn't is either a miracle or a liability, and in my experience miracles are just liabilities with better publicity.

So we went up into Thornfell, the boy and I, with my remaining mule and my remaining patience, and by the third day I had catalogued his strangenesses the way I catalogue switchbacks.

One: he slept facing downhill. Always. You put a child on a mountainside and instinct says curl toward the drop, away from the rock, because the rock feels like a wall and walls feel safe. Perrin slept with his back to the valley, pressed to the stone, like he was listening through it.

Two: he counted in his sleep. Under his breath, all night, numbers ticking down from some number I never caught the start of. When I asked him what he was counting, he said, "It's not me counting," and wouldn't elaborate, and I decided I respected that, because there are questions I don't answer either.

Three: he talked to the mule. Not the way children talk to animals — no baby voices, no confidences. He'd lean against her flank and go silent, eyes half-closed, and afterward the mule would behave strangely. Calmer. Once, she refused a trail I'd chosen, planted all four hooves, and Perrin said, "She's right, the bridge is out below," and it was. Forty feet of it, gone into a gorge I'd have ridden us straight over in the fog.

"So," I said that evening, over a fire small enough to hide, "you want to tell me why your father thinks the mountain talks to you?"

Perrin turned a stick in the coals. He had the particular stillness of a kid deciding whether an adult can be trusted, and I recognized that too, because I'd been that kid, except my version involved deciding whether the adult was going to sell me.

"It's not talking," he said finally. "Talking is words. It's more like..." He frowned, hunting for it. "You know when you're falling asleep and the house makes noises? Settling sounds? It's like that, but it's the whole mountain, and it's not settling. It's *dreaming*. And dreams have... weight. You can feel where they're thick."

"Where they're thick," I repeated.

"Under the floor of everything. Under the passes. There are places where it pools." He pointed his stick east, deeper into the range, toward the high country where even smugglers go carefully. "It's loudest there. And it's getting louder. It used to be like hearing someone talk in another room. Now it's like they're in the hall."

"What are they saying?"

His shoulders came up around his ears. "Counting."

"Counting what?"

"Down." He looked at me across the fire, and his eyes in the firelight were too old for his face, and I hated that, because I knew exactly what put that look on a person and none of the causes were survivable. "Something under there is counting down, Coral. And everyone else has stopped being able to hear it, and I don't know why I still can, and I'm scared that when it reaches zero, I'll be the only one who does."

I fed the fire another stick because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Here's what happens now," I said, in my best professional voice, the one that means *this conversation is over*. "Tomorrow we cross the Saddle and come down the west side, and I hand you to whoever your father sends, and you get to live in a big house where the floors don't dream. That's the whole job. That's the beginning, middle, and end."

"That's not what happens," Perrin said.

"And why's that?"

"Because the mountain knows your name." He said it gently, the way you deliver a diagnosis. "It says it when you sleep. *Coral. Coral.* Like it's checking you're still there."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat up with my back against a boulder and watched the stars wheel and told myself a dozen sensible lies: the boy was a fraud, a trained little spy, that Aldric Vane had researched me, that servants gossip and someone had seen me at the shrines mouthing words. All plausible. All comforting.

None of them explained the prayer.

It happened on the fifth morning, at the shrine below Grey Col — one of the old ones, pre-liturgical, a hollowed stone with a basin worn smooth by offerings older than any kingdom. I stopped there because stopping there is what I do, the way a man scratches an itch he hates having. I set out the salt cake. I knelt. And I said the words my mother gave me, low, under my breath, in the old tongue:

*Vess-ka-lumen, torrath vey.*
*Sleeping one, salt of the first fire,*
*I am small and I am yours until you wake.*

That last line was mine. Added when I was seven, kneeling in a shrine like this one, bargaining with a god who had never once answered, promising myself away in exchange for — I don't even remember what. Rain, maybe. My mother's cough going away. Something small. Children bargain small.

I finished. I stood. I turned around, and Perrin was standing three paces behind me, and his face was white as the salt cake.

"You added a line," he said. "The others are old. Carved. But the last line — *I am small and I am yours until you wake* — that one's yours. It's got your shape on it. Everything the mountain hears from you has your shape on it, and that line is—" His breath hitched. "Coral, that line is *loud*."

"How." My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "How do you know those words."

"I don't. I mean — I didn't. Before." He wrapped his arms around himself. "They're just *there*, the way the counting is there. I hear what the mountain hears. And the mountain has been hearing you say that prayer your whole life. Every shrine, every pass, every year. Twenty years of it. It's worn a groove, the way water wears stone. I could recite it in my sleep. I *have* recited it in my sleep, that's what the counting is sometimes, it's your prayer mixed up with the other counting, and Coral—"

"Stop."

"—it answers back. It's started answering back. Not words. More like a hand moving under a blanket. And it moves *toward* you. Every time you pray, it turns toward you, and I can feel it turning, and I think—"

"*Stop.*"

He stopped. Good kid. Smart kid. Because I was standing there with twenty years of weary habit cracking open down the middle like river ice, and underneath the habit was the thing I'd been not-thinking since the night my offering lit itself without kindling and a voice I'd begged to hear for two decades finally spoke, and it hadn't said *at last, my faithful one*. It had said *a debt is called due*.

A god wakes up after twenty years and its first word to its most devoted servant is *pay me*.

And here was a child standing in front of me telling me that every prayer I'd ever whispered had been heard — not by a distant sleeping god, but by something underneath, something that had been *collecting*, something that had worn a groove in the world with my voice, my specific voice, my mother's tongue, my stupid seven-year-old promise — and that something was now turning toward me like a flower toward sun.

I had spent my whole life believing my prayers fell into silence. It had never once occurred to me that they might have been falling into an ear.

"Perrin," I said, and I made myself kneel so we were eye to eye, because the next thing I was going to say deserved eye level. "When the mountain turns toward me. Does it turn toward anyone else?"

He shook his head slowly. "Just you. And—" He hesitated.

"And?"

"There's a woman. In the river towns. I heard her once, in Lowmark, when we were leaving — a prayer with the same shape as yours. Same old words. Hers is angry, though. Yours is sad." He tilted his head, listening to something I couldn't hear, and his expression flickered through several things too fast to name. "Coral, the counting changed."

"Changed how?"

"It was at forty-one yesterday. This morning it's at thirty-nine." He swallowed. "It skips when you pray. Like your prayers are... pulling it forward. Like it's excited."

The wind came down off Grey Col and moved through the pass, and I swear on every god I no longer trusted that the mountain leaned. Just slightly. Just the way a listener leans.

Here is the truth I arrived at in that moment, standing in the cold with a child who could hear my whole life echoing off the inside of the world: I could not give this boy back. Not to Lord Aldric, whose hired men sweated when they said their son's name. Not to whatever waited for him in a house with dreaming floors. Because the court beneath the mountain — and I knew, in the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds form, that there *was* a court beneath the mountain — had spent twenty years listening to me, and now it had found a boy who could hear it back, and things that listen always want the ears that hear them.

Every instinct I had said *walk away*. Take the four hundred crowns, report the boy lost to weather, start over under a different name in a different range. That was the smart play. That was the play I'd have advised any client to take.

Instead I heard myself say: "New plan. We don't go down the west side."

Perrin blinked. "But the job—"

"The job's done. I'm keeping the money anyway; your father can afford it." I kicked dirt over the fire and started saddling the mule, mostly so I wouldn't have to look at the kid while I did the dumbest thing of my career. "We go east. Deep. If something under there is counting down to zero, I want to know what it's counting *to* — and I want to be the one standing between it and you when it gets there."

"Why?" Perrin asked. And it wasn't an ungrateful why. It was the genuine question of a child who had learned that adults' kindnesses always had invoices attached.

I tightened the last strap harder than it needed.

"Because somebody prayed me awake too," I said. "Might as well return the favor."

Behind us, far below in the stone, faint as a pulse, the counting went on. Thirty-eight. Thirty-seven.

And for the first time in twenty years, I prayed on purpose — not the old words, not the bargain, just a plain thought flung down into the dark like a stone into a well:

*I'm coming. Leave the boy alone. Take me instead.*

Under the floor of the world, something laughed.
