## 5. Tolls on the Smuggler's Road

The trouble with ambushes is that they're always announced, if you know how to listen. The birds had gone quiet an hour before we ever reached the Narrows — not fled, mind you, but quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone's listening at the door. Birds don't flee from bandits. They flee from things that eat birds. Bandits they just gossip about.

I'd been running the Thornfell passes for nineteen years, and in that time I'd learned three things worth passing on: never trust a pass with no wind in it, never camp where the moss grows only on one side of the stones, and never, ever take a job from a man who says *it'll be easy*. Lord Aldric's steward had said *it'll be easy* twice, which is how I knew it wouldn't be, and why I'd doubled my fee and tripled my provisions.

So when the silence came down over the Narrows like a dropped curtain, I did what I always do. I stopped walking.

"Keep moving," Tam said behind me. He was one of my regulars — a salt-runner with a face like wet leather and the nerves of a man who'd been robbed so many times he'd achieved a kind of peace about it. "Coral. Why are we stopping?"

"Because we're being robbed," I said. "And I'd rather be robbed standing up."

Behind him, Sable pulled Perrin close against the cliff wall. The boy had gone pale as the limestone around us, his head tilted in that way he did lately — listening to something under the world. He'd been doing it more each day since Lowmark. Listening, and sometimes mouthing numbers I didn't want to hear.

"How many?" Sable asked. She'd learned fast, my disgraced priestess. Three days on the road and she already asked the right questions instead of the frightened ones.

"Twelve," I said. "Maybe fourteen. Two archers on the shelf above us — see how the scree hasn't settled there? Someone's been sitting. Six in the rocks ahead, four behind to close the trap." I shrugged. "Standard toll-gang arithmetic."

"And the way out?"

"There isn't one." I smiled at her, because that's what guides do. We smile at people while telling them the worst news of their lives. It's in the guild charter somewhere. "There's only the way through, and the way through is mine."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about smuggler's roads: the official ones are for carts and corpses. Everything valuable moves along paths that don't exist. Not hidden paths — *unwritten* ones. Paths that are only true if you walk them correctly, at the right hour, humming the right tune your mother taught you, stepping left-right-left where every instinct says right-left-right. My grandmother called them the Old Man's Steps. She said the mountain let certain people through because it liked the songs.

I hadn't hummed those songs since Vess woke up. Since the offering ignited on a cold altar and a voice I'd spent twenty years praying into silence finally answered back: *a debt is called due.* I didn't know yet what the debt was. I only knew that everything old in these mountains had started paying attention to me, and attention, in Thornfell, is rarely free.

But the Narrows don't care about your theological crises. The Narrows only care whether you can walk a path that doesn't exist while fourteen men with crossbows decide whether you're cargo or customers.

"Everyone stay exactly where I put you," I said. "Tam, hand on the mule's halter, don't let her shift. Sable — Perrin stays touching you at all times. If we get separated, count to two hundred and walk toward the sound of water. Don't run. Running tells them who to chase first."

"What are you going to do?" Perrin whispered. His voice cracked in the middle, the way twelve-year-old voices do, and something in my chest did a thing I decided not to examine.

"I'm going to pay a toll," I said. "Just not the one they're expecting."

I walked out into the open stretch of the Narrows alone, hands loose at my sides, and I began to sing.

It wasn't much of a song. A counting song, really, the kind grandmothers use to teach children which stones hold and which stones lie. *One for the door, two for the sill, three for the stone that stands stock-still...* My voice bounced off the canyon walls, and I watched the scree above me shift as the archers leaned out to look, and I stepped left where the song told me to step, and the mountain opened.

That's the only way I can describe it. There was no gap in the rock a moment before, and then there was, a slot of shadow barely wider than my shoulders, and I walked sideways into it, still singing, and the Narrows became a place I was remembering rather than standing in.

The bandits, to their credit, reacted quickly. Crossbows snapped — two bolts buried themselves in stone where my head had been half a heartbeat earlier. Shouts echoed. Men poured out of the rocks ahead, and men came up from behind, and Tam made a noise like a kettle giving up.

Then the mountain closed behind me, and the shouting went strange and far away, like weather happening to someone else.

* * *

The Old Man's Steps aren't a tunnel. They're a promise. You move through slots and shelves and chimneys of stone, always within earshot of the party you left behind, always able to come back out — but only where the song allows. I came out on a ledge ten feet above the lead bandit, a scarred woman with a wolf-pelt cloak and a sword worn down to a whittler's blade, and I looked down at the top of her head for a long moment while she shouted orders at empty air.

"Looking for someone?" I said.

She spun. To her credit, she didn't gape long. She just squinted up at me, at the ledge that had no business holding a person, and said, "You're the guide. Nakamura."

"Fame at last."

"The court wants a word." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *the tide wants a word* or *winter wants a word.* No fear in it. Just the tired respect of someone who knows better than to argue with geography.

"Which court?" I asked, though I knew. There's only one court under Thornfell, and everyone who works these mountains pays it lip service and pretends it's a story. Toll-gangs, smugglers, shrine-keepers — we all leave a coin at the deep shrines now and then, not because we believe, but because believing costs nothing and not-believing might cost everything.

"You know which." She sheathed the whittler's sword with exaggerated slowness, showing me her empty hands. "Come down. Nobody touches you or yours. That's the terms. I have a message, and then we're done, and you can go back to pretending you're nobody."

I should have refused. Let the record show that some part of me, the part that had survived nineteen years by refusing exactly this kind of invitation, screamed *no* loud enough to taste. But the other part — the part that had lit offerings on cold altars for twenty years, the part that still prayed and hated itself for it — that part wanted to know who else was watching. Who else had heard Vess wake.

I climbed down.

* * *

Her name was Brannoc, and her gang stood in a loose ring around us while my own people clustered near the mule, watching. Perrin had his face pressed against Sable's shoulder, but his eyes were open, and they weren't looking at Brannoc. They were looking past her, down the Narrows, toward the deep end of the mountains, and his lips were moving again. Counting.

"Message first," Brannoc said. "Then questions, if you've got them, though I'm told the answers cost."

"Message," I said.

She reached into her cloak and drew out a thing wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a snake, and held it out flat on her palm. It was a coin. Old work, older than any mint I knew — black metal, soft-edged, with a hole through the center. Through the hole, impossibly, I could smell brine. Sea-smell, in the heart of the mountains.

"The court knows about the debt," Brannoc said. "They know the god woke. They know what it wants collected." Her eyes flicked, just once, to Perrin — and away. "They want you to collect it. For them."

"For them," I repeated.

"The soul's been theirs forty years. Bought and paid, kept warm beside the fire." She turned the coin over; the hole caught the light and held nothing. "But the fire's guttering, Nakamura. When it dies, everything bought and kept goes dark with it. The court would rather the soul be *delivered* — carried out by oath, handed over clean — than lost. An oath-delivery binds the debt forever. A death loses it. So they want their property moved, and they want it moved by the one mortal alive who's bound to carry it."

The ring of bandits was very quiet. Somewhere above us, wind found the Narrows again, and the birds started up their gossiping, and I understood that the whole ambush had been theater — a stage, an audience, a message delivered with crossbow punctuation.

"And if I refuse?"

Brannoc almost smiled. "Then the god takes it anyway, and the court takes it from the god, and you're in the middle of two things eating the same meal. Or—" she paused, weighing how much to spend, "—or you bring it out yourself, and the court owes *you*. Court-debt, Nakamura. Names written in the deep ledger. There are worse things to hold than a favor from under the mountain."

She pressed the coin into my hand. It was warm. Nothing in this world should have made it warm.

"They said you'd understand," she added, quieter. "They said: *tell the guide the board was set before the god woke. Tell the guide they were placed, not chosen.*"

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow off the high trail, a smuggler's cache I've used a dozen times, with good stone on three sides and one bad side I could watch. Tam drank himself stupid and passed out by full dark. Sable sat up with me at the fire, turning the black coin over and over without touching it — she used a stick, like it might bite.

"It's a tithe-coin," she said at last. "Pre-schism. My order used them before the Drowning. Payment between gods and courts, back when the gods still negotiated." She set the stick down. "Coral. This is court-minted. This came from *under* the mountain."

"I noticed."

"Do you understand what they're saying? That you were *placed?*" Her voice had an edge I hadn't heard before — not fear, something sharper. "That your whole life, your oath, your guiding, all of it — arranged? Set on the board like—"

"Like a piece," I finished. "Yes, Sable. I got the sermon."

She flinched. Good. Misery loves company, and I was feeling miserly.

Perrin appeared at the edge of the firelight. Neither of us had heard him wake, which should have been impossible — the boy slept like a sack of turnips — but there he was, barefoot on cold stone, staring at the coin in its circle of firelight.

"That's why the mountain counts," he said.

Sable went still. "Perrin—"

"It counts because it's keeping books." His voice was steady, too steady, the voice of a boy reporting weather from a country he can't leave. "Every prayer answered, every debt called, every piece moved. It's been counting since before I was born. And last night—" he swallowed, and for a second he was just a scared kid again, "—last night the number changed. It counted *me*. It said—" his eyes came up to mine, wide and terrible, "—it said *the guide's piece has begun to move.*

I looked at the coin in my hand. Warm as a living thing. Smelling of the sea, forty leagues from any sea.

Nineteen years I'd believed I chose these roads. Chose the passes, chose the clients, chose the shrines I still fed out of habit and grief. And now a bandit queen delivers a message in crossbow theater, a boy hears my name in the mountain's ledger, and a god I loved is calling in a debt I never knew I'd signed.

"Get some sleep," I told them both, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Tomorrow we go down. All the way down."

Sable stared at me. "Down where?"

I turned the coin once more, watching the firelight drown in its little hole of brine-dark, and gave the only honest answer left on the board.

"To collect."
