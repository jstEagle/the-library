## 6. Vess Speaks in Three Tongues

I had not performed the Rite of the Opened Door in eleven years, and I had hoped the muscle memory would have rotted out of my hands by now. It hadn't. That was the first insult of the evening.

The second was that it worked.

We were camped in a hollow below the third ridge of Thornfell, where the smuggler's road bent like a guilty conscience and Coral had declared — with the particular flatness they used for things they did not want argued with — that we would go no further until morning. Tam's caravan was two days behind us and three days ahead of its own doom, or so Coral claimed; I had stopped asking how they knew things. Asking Coral how they knew things was like asking a river why it flowed downhill. You got wet and no wiser.

Perrin was asleep by the fire, curled around his own knees like a comma, and even asleep he was muttering numbers. He'd been doing it since the pass. *Four hundred and six. Four hundred and five.* I didn't want to know what he was counting down to. Knowing things had been my profession once, and look what it had gotten me: defrocked, disgraced, and eating hardtack beside a mercenary who prayed at empty shrines out of spite.

Or not out of spite. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. I had watched Coral at the ruined shrine on the high trail, watched them set down salt and honey with hands that shook, watched them hate themselves for shaking. I knew that gesture. I had invented that gesture. There is a specific posture of grief reserved for people who keep loving something that has stopped loving them back, and Coral wore it like an old coat, worn shiny at the elbows.

So when the fire guttered sideways — toward me, against the wind, which is not how fire behaves — I told myself it was mountain weather.

When the smoke began to move in spirals, I told myself I was tired.

And when the smoke stood up, I stopped telling myself anything, because I had learned long ago that lying to yourself is a skill, and mine had always been mediocre.

* * *

The shape it made was almost polite. A tall figure, featureless, the way smoke is featureless, but with a suggestion of shoulders and a suggestion of a head tilted slightly, as if listening. Gods, in my training, were described as vast presences, oceanic, overwhelming. Nobody had warned me that one might arrive looking apologetic.

"Sable of Lowmark," it said, and its voice came from three directions at once — from the smoke, from the coals, and from somewhere behind my own sternum, which I resented enormously. "Once of the Order of the Tidewife."

"Formerly," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The Order revoked me. You'll want to check your records."

"I do not keep records." The smoke rippled, which I chose to interpret as amusement. "I keep debts."

Coral was awake. Of course Coral was awake; I suspect Coral slept the way cats do, with one ear rotating. They were on their feet with a knife already drawn, standing between the god-shape and Perrin's sleeping form, and there was something in the set of their shoulders that made my chest ache — the posture of a bodyguard who has decided, without consulting anyone, that this child is theirs now.

"Vess," Coral said. The name came out of them like a stone coughed up after twenty years. "You're speaking through her rites."

"I am borrowing," Vess said. "She left the door open. Doors are such generous things." The smoke-head turned toward me, and though it had no eyes, I felt examined, catalogued, filed under a heading I would not enjoy reading. "You drowned your god, little priestess. And yet you still build altars in your sleep. Your hands remember what your mouth renounces."

"That's slander," I said. "My hands and I have an agreement."

"They pray every night. You simply refuse to listen to them."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue. What came out instead was nothing, because the terrible truth was that my hands *did* pray. Every night, half-asleep, fingers moving through the old mudra of the Tidewife's blessing on whatever surface was nearest — a bedroll, a table, once, humiliatingly, a barmaid's shoulder. Eleven years defrocked and my body still kept the office hours.

"You heard prayers being answered," Vess said, and now the voice dropped low, intimate, the register priests use for confessions. "You told the mercenary as much. You begged passage because you heard the silence break. Do you know why it broke?"

"No," I said honestly.

"But you hope." The smoke leaned closer. "You hope it was your prayers that woke us. Yours, Sable of Lowmark, the heretic, the god-drowner, kneeling in a river town whispering into water that hadn't answered you in years. You hope you mattered."

Gods are supposed to be above cruelty. This one was not even above pettiness. I folded my arms. "Get to the terms. You didn't borrow a dead woman's rites to flatter me."

"Flattery is a tool like any other." The smoke straightened. "Very well. The terms."

What follows, I will set down as plainly as I can, because someone should record it accurately and Coral records nothing and Perrin was mercifully asleep.

Vess spoke in three tongues, as its title promises — or rather, it spoke once and I heard it three ways, which I am told is worse. In the tongue of smoke it said: *A debt is called due.* In the tongue of ember it said: *A soul was sold, and sold souls burn slow.* And in the tongue behind my sternum — the worst tongue, the one that spoke in my mother's cadence — it said: *Forty years ago, a child was given to the court beneath the mountain. That child's soul keeps the last dragon-fire alive. The fire gutters. When it dies, the court will extinguish flame forever, and every prayer ever answered becomes a leash.*

"A leash," Coral repeated. Their knife hand had not moved. "Explain that word."

"Every miracle binds," Vess said. "This is the oldest law and the least loved. When a god answers a prayer, the answer wraps a thread around the asker. One thread is nothing. A lifetime of answered prayers is a net. For twenty years I slept, and the threads went slack, and mortals forgot they were caught." The smoke spread wide, almost luxuriant. "Now I wake, and I answer, and each answer tightens. Already the farmers whose rains returned feel lighter on their feet. Already the sick who woke healed dream of walking where I send them. Deliver the child's soul to me before the last flame dies, and I will hold all those threads gently. Fail, and the court beneath takes them instead — and the court does not hold gently. The court *pulls*."

"And if we just say no?" Coral asked. "If we walk away and let the fire die?"

"Then every prayer answered anywhere in these mountains becomes a chain, and I become either your master or your mourner, and neither of us will enjoy it." A pause. Smoke does not sigh, but this came close. "There is no path out of this that does not run through the mountain. I have looked. I have had twenty years to look."

"How much time?" I asked.

"The flame gutters. Weeks. Perhaps less. The court means to smother it deliberately — they have been starving it for forty years, feeding it nothing, letting the keeper-soul thin. They believe a world without dragon-fire is a world without gods, and they intend to rule the quiet that follows."

"And the child," Coral said. "The soul. Is it—" They stopped. Recalibrated. I watched them choose the question they could bear. "Is it aware?"

"It dreams," Vess said. "It has dreamed for forty years beside the fire it feeds. It does not know it is a prisoner. It believes the vault is home." The smoke tilted. "That is the cruelest mercy the court offers. They make the cage beloved."

Coral said nothing. But I saw their jaw work, and I thought: *there it is.* Whatever Coral had been telling themselves about this job — that it was a delivery, a contract, a toll on a road they happened to be walking — it had just curdled into something else. You cannot hear *it believes the cage is home* and stay a courier. Somewhere in that scarred professional heart, Coral had just been conscripted by pity.

I, meanwhile, was doing arithmetic I had sworn off eleven years ago.

*Forty years ago. A child sold to the court beneath the mountain.*

I was fifty-one years old. Forty-one years ago, my brother Joren had stood in our mother's kitchen in Lowmark and told her he'd found a way to pay father's debts. He was nineteen. He went up the Thornfell road in autumn with a merchant caravan and never came down again, and our mother told everyone he'd died of fever in the passes, and she lit a candle for him every Tidewife's Eve until the year she died, and I — I was fifteen, and angry at him for leaving, and I did not light candles, and I did not ask questions, because asking questions meant learning that my family had done the unforgivable arithmetic that so many poor families do: that one child weighed less than the whole house.

He hadn't died of fever. He had been *sold*. Sold to the court beneath the mountain, where he had fathered — no. No, that thread I wasn't pulling yet, not here, not in front of a god that collected secrets the way other entities collected souls. Later. Later, when I could scream about it privately.

But the dates. The dates fit like a key I'd spent four decades pretending wasn't shaped like my brother.

"Sable." Coral's voice, quiet. "You've gone white as the salt trade."

"I'm always this color," I said. "It's the order's old habit. We bleached ourselves with prayer. Very fashionable."

Vess laughed. It was not a nice sound. It sounded like wind through a chimney, like a hymn sung backwards. "You hide something from me, priestess."

"I hide many things from you. It's called dignity."

"You hide a name." The smoke drifted nearer, and the voice behind my sternum grew warm, coaxing, unbearable. "A small name. A boy's name. Say it, and I will tell you whether—"

"*No.*"

It came out of me like a slap. Even Coral flinched. Even the fire recoiled. And Vess — Vess went very still, the way large things go still, and I understood that I had just done something either very brave or very stupid, and that the difference would be determined entirely by whether the god before me found defiance charming or nourishing.

"You refuse a god," it said.

"I refuse a *creditor*," I said. "You've made it very clear this is a transaction. Fine. Then hear my terms: you get the soul delivered, if it can be delivered. You don't get my memories. You don't get my grief. Grief is the last property I own outright, and I'm not pawning it at your bazaar." I was shaking. I let it shake my voice too; let the god think it was fear. Some of it was. "You want a priestess? I'm defrocked. You want a believer? I drowned yours. You want an instrument? Instruments don't negotiate. So take the deal as offered or find another fool with working hands."

Silence. Long enough that I heard Perrin murmur numbers in his sleep. *Three hundred ninety-eight. Three hundred ninety-seven.*

Then the smoke bowed — actually bowed, a deep ironic sweep — and said, in the tongue behind my sternum, in my mother's cadence, softly enough that only I could hear:

*"Joren's sister. Just as stubborn as him."*

And it was gone. The smoke collapsed into the fire, the fire burned ordinary and orange, the wind resumed its proper direction, and the night became merely a night, cold and enormous and indifferent, which after a god's attention feels almost like an insult.

* * *

Coral didn't speak for a while. They banked the fire, checked Perrin — tucked his bedroll higher around his shoulders with a roughness that fooled absolutely no one — and then sat across from me with their knife across their knees, which was apparently how Coral relaxed.

"You refused it," they said finally.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because it knows my brother's name. Because it said it in her voice. Because if I give a god one true thing it will take the rest of me as interest. Because I have spent eleven years being nobody's instrument and I will not start again at fifty-one.

"Because someone has to model good behavior for the boy," I said.

Coral studied me. Coral had a way of studying people that felt like being appraised for smuggling capacity — weight, volume, whether you'd rattle. "You know something about the child," they said. "About who was sold."

"The whole realm knows a child was sold. It's in the terms."

"You knew before tonight."

I fed a stick into the fire. Watched it catch. Bought myself three seconds, which was two more than I needed and one fewer than I wanted.

"Coral," I said, "when you prayed at those empty shrines for twenty years — did you ever wonder what you'd do if it answered? Really answered? Not comfort. Answer. With terms, and deadlines, and a bill?"

"Yes," Coral said, with no hesitation at all. "I wondered every single day. And now I know the answer." They turned the knife over. "I'd do exactly what you're doing. Lie badly and keep walking."

I laughed. It escaped before I could vet it, a short ugly sound, and Perrin stirred and muttered *three hundred ninety-five* and settled again.

"Weeks," I said. "It said weeks. Maybe less."

"I heard."

"The court wants the fire dead. Vess wants the soul delivered. And we're supposed to steal a prisoner out of a vault beneath a mountain full of things that buy souls with kindness first." I shook my head. "Do you know what the worst part is? Forty years ago, somebody stood exactly where we're standing and decided it was worth it. Somebody walked down there. Or tried."

Coral looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes — the flicker of a smuggler recalculating a route, noticing a passenger knows the way better than advertised.

"Whoever it was," Coral said slowly, "they must have had a reason worth dying for. Family, maybe. Blood."

"Or guilt," I said, and stood, and brushed off my skirts, and announced that I would take first watch, and that further theological discussions could wait for daylight, and that if the smoke came back it could leave a message with my hands, who were apparently still devout.

Coral let it go. That was the thing about Coral nobody expected, given the profession: they knew when a package was sealed and didn't pry at the wrapping. They lay back down beside Perrin, one arm over the boy like a drawbar across a gate, and within minutes their breathing went long and even.

I sat watch. The mountains leaned in around our little fire, black against a sky full of indifferent stars, and somewhere far below us — beneath stone, beneath salt, beneath forty years of my family's lies — a small soul dreamed beside a dying flame and believed itself home.

*Joren,* I thought, testing the name in the privacy of my skull the way you test a frozen river with one foot. *You idiot. You brave, stupid, sold idiot. Did you volunteer? Did they trick you? Were you trying to save us?*

Because that was the shape the arithmetic made, now that I finally let myself do it. Father's debts. Mother's tears. A nineteen-year-old boy who carried the family temper and the family tenderness in equal, disastrous measure. He wouldn't have gone screaming. He'd have gone *smiling*, telling our mother it was temporary, telling me — fifteen and furious — that he'd come back rich, that he'd fix everything.

He had gone to feed a fire. And the fire was dying anyway, and the court that bought him meant to snuff it, and the only people between that small dreaming soul and oblivion were a defrocked priestess, a god-haunted smuggler, and a child who counted down in his sleep.

Eleven years ago I had drowned my god because it demanded obedience and gave nothing back. Tonight a different god had offered me everything back — answers, purpose, my brother's name in my mother's voice — and I had refused it, because the price was the same coin either way: myself, rendered down, spent.

Fine. Then I would spend myself my own way.

I looked at Coral's sleeping shape, at the arm around the boy, and made the kind of vow that needs no shrine: *We get the child out. Not delivered — out. Whatever Vess thinks it's buying, whatever the court thinks it's selling, that soul goes free or we all stay down there and haunt the place together.*

Behind my sternum, faint as a tide turning, I felt something note the vow, and file it, and I did not care.

Let the gods keep ledgers. I had a brother to bury properly, and I intended to do it breathing.

Above us, far up the pass, a night bird cried once — and beneath us, so faint I might have imagined it, something answered.
