## 7. Salt and Ash

The village of Brinehollow announced itself the way grief does — gradually, then all at once. First the smell: salt and woodsmoke braided together, sharp enough to make your eyes water after three days of mountain air that tasted like nothing but cold. Then the terraces cut into the valley wall, pale as old scars, where miners had clawed salt out of the rock for longer than anyone remembered. And then the lights.

That was the part that made Sable stop walking.

"Coral," she said quietly, in the tone she used when she wanted me to confirm she wasn't mad. "Those windows are lit."

"I can see that."

"There's no one in those houses. I felt it from the ridge. No heartbeats. Not one."

Perrin, who had spent most of the day with his hood up against the wind and his ears full of whatever the mountain was muttering to him, lifted his head like a hound catching a scent. "They're home," he said. "They're just not *in* themselves anymore." He said it the way you'd say the soup's on, no drama at all, and then he started down the slope toward the lights before either of us could stop him.

Which is how we came to shelter for two nights in a village where every hearth was tended by the dead.

* * *

I want to be clear about something before I go on, because smugglers have a reputation and mercenaries have a worse one, and neither reputation prepares anyone for what Brinehollow actually was. I've walked into haunted places before. Haunted places are supposed to be cold. They're supposed to whisper, and drip, and make the hair on your arms stand up like a warning written in a language your body reads faster than your mind.

Brinehollow was warm.

The first door that opened for us opened onto a woman — or what had been a woman, once, her outline gone soft at the edges like a drawing left out in the rain, her eyes two gentle smudges of light where eyes used to be. She looked at Perrin first. They always looked at Perrin first, in those days; children are a magnet for anything that remembers being young.

"Travelers," she said, and her voice was like wind through a keyhole, thin and sweet. "Cold travelers. Come by the fire."

"We don't—" Sable started.

"You do," the woman said, with the mild certainty of someone stating the weather. "Everyone does. The fire's been waiting."

And here's the thing I still can't fully explain, even now, even after everything: I believed her. Not because ghosts are trustworthy — they're not, generally, and I'd have staked my life on that rule more than once — but because there was no hunger in her. Every spirit I'd ever met wanted something. These ones just wanted the fire fed and guests sat down beside it, and if that sounds like a small thing, you've never been cold and hunted and tired in the high passes of Thornfell.

We ate stew that tasted of barley and thyme and something underneath both, faintly mineral, like licking a stone in summer. A man with a miner's crooked shoulders ladled it out and hummed while he worked, an old working song, the kind with no words left in it because the words wore away generations ago and only the tune kept going, stubborn as water finding its way downhill.

"They keep the fires," Perrin said, around a mouthful. He'd already made friends with a ghost-child who didn't so much sit next to him as hover near him, radiating the particular shyness of the dead young. "All of them. Every house. That's the whole job."

"Somebody has to," said the miner, not looking up from his ladle.

"Why?" Sable asked. She asked it gently, which surprised me. Sable's gentleness was a rationed supply, doled out in thimblefuls, and I was starting to learn what bought it: honesty, mostly. She couldn't bear performance.

The miner considered the question the way you'd consider a stone you were about to skip. "Because the undercourt buys warmth," he said finally. "And we decided to stop selling."

* * *

Later, when Perrin had fallen asleep sitting up by the biggest hearth, mid-sentence, mid-laugh, with the ghost-child keeping watch over him like a small dim lantern, the elders of Brinehollow gathered us close to the fire and told us how their village died without dying.

It went like this: forty years ago — and I felt Sable go very still beside me at the number, though she said nothing — the court beneath Thornfell sent traders up into the mining towns. Not soldiers. Traders. They came with soft voices and soft hands and they bought things nobody thought were for sale. A widow's loneliness, weighed out in silver. A father's guilt over a son lost in a collapse, purchased outright and carried away in a jar. Grief, regret, longing — the undercourt dealt in all of it, and paid well, and the people of the salt towns sold and sold until there wasn't much left of them but hands that knew how to work and hearts that had forgotten why.

"And then they came for the rest," said the woman who had first opened her door to us. Her name had been Maren. She offered it the way you offer bread — freely, expecting nothing back. "The last thing anyone has. Their names. Their selves. The court calls it kindness, at first. It always does. They find what hurts and they hold it, and holding feels like help, right up until you realize you can't take it back."

"The dead of Brinehollow refused," Sable said slowly. "You gave up your names before they could buy them."

Maren's smile was a soft thing, worn smooth. "We burned them in our own hearths instead. Salt keeps everything, priestess. Salt keeps names too, if you ask it nicely." She gestured at the fire, and I swear the flames bent toward her hand like a plant toward light. "So we stayed. Nameless, but ours. Tending fires. Keeping warm the places the court wants cold."

I sat with my bowl cooling in my hands and thought about a god named Vess who had answered my prayers exactly twice in twenty years, both times to demand payment. I thought about prayers as leashes, though I hadn't heard that word yet — that would come later, underground, in a cell — and I thought about how the difference between a god and a merchant might be smaller than any of us had ever wanted to believe.

"What do they want with warmth?" I asked. "The undercourt. Why buy it?"

Maren turned her smudged, gentle eyes on me, and for a moment the softness in her face went somewhere else — not cruel, never cruel, but old. Old the way ice is old.

"Because warmth is the only thing that burns, dear. You can't light a fire with stone. You need living stuff. Wood, fat, feeling, soul." She poked the hearth with an iron rod that had no business moving through her fingers, but did anyway. "The dragon-fire under the mountain is guttering. Everyone knows it, even if nobody says it. And a dying flame gets hungry. And hungry things get *bought* things."

Sable's voice came out flat. "You're saying the court is stocking a larder."

"I'm saying," Maren said, "that if someone has told you a bargain will save you, count your fingers afterward. Count your *name* afterward. The court is kind the way winter is kind — it puts you to sleep first."

* * *

I slept badly, which was normal, but I slept badly in a new way, which wasn't. I kept waking to check on Perrin, and every time I woke he was fine — asleep, breathing, the ghost-child still stationed beside him like a sentry who'd appointed herself to the post and would brook no relief. And every time I found myself standing there in the doorway of some dead family's guest room, watching a boy sleep, and feeling something in my chest pull tight as a knot.

Here's what I knew about myself by then, though I hadn't said it out loud to anyone, including me: I had spent twenty years paying tribute to a silence. Twenty years of leaving offerings at shrines that never answered, telling myself it was habit, professionalism, respect for tradition — and underneath all of it, the ugly truth, which was that I couldn't stop praying because I couldn't stop hoping. Hope is the cruelest habit there is. It doesn't even give you the mercy of being wrong quickly.

And now, in a village of the nameless dead, I watched a boy who wasn't mine sleep safe by a fire somebody's grandmother was keeping lit for no payment and no promise, and I understood — with the specific, sickening clarity that only comes at three in the morning — that this was what I'd actually been praying for. Not Vess. Not answers. This. A hearth. People in it. Someone keeping the fire so you don't have to.

Found family, Sable would have called it, if Sable ever used words that soft, which she didn't. But she was downstairs, I later learned, sitting up with Maren, asking questions about rites and relics in a voice that shook only slightly, and I think she was learning the same lesson on her own schedule, the way we all were.

The ache of it was the worst part. Not fear — I could carry fear; fear was cargo like anything else. The ache. Watching something you want so badly you'd deny wanting it at knifepoint.

* * *

Morning in Brinehollow arrived without a sun worth mentioning — Thornfell clouded over, grey on grey — but the village woke anyway, or did whatever the dead do instead of waking. Fires were fed. Bread was baked, though I never saw anyone eat it; the loaves went out onto windowsills, an offering to nobody, or to everybody, or maybe just because the shape of the day demanded it. Habit, again. I recognized it. Weary habit masking grief — I'd invented that trick, or thought I had, and here were a hundred dead people doing it better than me, turning their mourning into warm bread and clean floors and a child's bed aired and ready.

Perrin spent the morning in the salt yards.

I watched him from the terrace above, ostensibly checking the road south, actually just watching. The ghost-keepers had taken to him completely. The miner — Harl, his name had been — walked him through the terraces and showed him how salt came out of the rock, how you read the white veins in the grey stone, and Perrin listened with his whole body the way he listened to everything, leaning in, mouth open, and then he put his hands on the rock face and closed his eyes and said something I couldn't hear, and the vein he'd been looking at seemed to brighten. Just slightly. Just enough.

Harl laughed — a sound like a bell wrapped in cloth — and clapped the boy's shoulder, and Perrin glowed like he'd been handed a title.

"He hears it," Maren said, materializing beside me the way the dead do, no footsteps, no warning. "The mountain. We heard it too, once, before we gave up our names. It dreams, you know. All that stone, all that dark, dreaming. Most folk just feel it as a weight in the chest. Your boy hears the actual dream."

"He's not my boy."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "Of course not."

I let that lie there between us, glaring at the terraces, and Maren let it lie there too, radiating the patience of someone who had been dead for decades and had learned that most arguments resolve themselves if you simply outlast them.

"He'll break your heart or mend it," she said at last. "In my experience it's usually both, and in that order."

"Is that what happened to you?"

Something moved through her then, a flicker in the smudge of her face, like weather crossing water. "My daughter sold her grief over me to the court," she said. "Thought it would help her sleep. And it did, for a while. That's the trap, Coral Nakamura. It *does* help, at first. Kindness first. Cruelty after. Always in that order, and never the other way round, because cruelty after works — you're already too soft to fight it." She looked down at the boy in the salt yard, laughing at something Harl said. "Keep him close. And when someone offers to take his nightmares away — and someone will, down there, someone kind with kind eyes — remember which order it goes in."

* * *

We left on the second evening, because roads don't wait and debts less so, and the whole village came out to see us off, which is to say the whole village stood in their doorways glowing softly, which is somehow more of a crowd than a crowd.

Maren pressed a small cloth bundle into my hands. Inside: a lump of grey-white salt, veined pink, warm as a held stone.

"For the road. Salt keeps everything, if you ask it nicely. Names. Promises. People." Her light-eyes held mine. "When you get where you're going — and you're going *down*, aren't you, I can smell the deep-road dust on your boots — when you get there, and they offer you kindness, hold this and ask yourself what comes second."

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, which frightened me more than the ghosts had.

Perrin hugged the ghost-child, who had no body to hug and managed it anyway; I saw his arms close around a shimmer and a warmth and a sound like a small laugh. Sable bowed to Maren, priest-fashion, an old reflex she clearly hated having, and Maren bowed back, deeper.

"One more thing," Maren called as we reached the edge of the lamplight. "The flame below — the last dragon-fire. When you see it, don't kneel."

Sable stopped. "What?"

"It doesn't want kneeling." Maren's voice followed us out into the dark, thin and sweet and certain. "It never did. It wants *witnessing*. There's a difference, and the whole world turns on it. Kneeling is what you do to owners. Witnessing is what you do to—"

The wind took the rest. Or she let it. Dead folk know when to stop talking; it's one of their few advantages.

We climbed out of Brinehollow with salt in my pocket and an ache in my chest and Perrin chattering about veins of light in stone, and behind us the little village glowed in its hollow like a coal cupped in a grey palm, tending its fires for no one, waiting for no answer, asking nothing.

I caught myself looking back four times. On the fifth, I made myself stop.

Two days later, at the mouth of a pass that shouldn't have existed, we met a traveler coming the other way — robed, courteous, smiling, with kind eyes and a voice like warmed honey — who told us he'd been sent to guide us safely down, that the court beneath had heard of our journey and wished us every comfort, that all our fears could be set down like heavy packs, that he would carry them for us, gladly, kindly, asking nothing in return.

His smile was perfect. His hands were open. And every hair on my body stood up like a warning written in a language older than words.

Kindness first, Maren had said. I put my hand in my pocket, closed it around the warm salt, and asked myself what came second.
