## 8. The Court Beneath Sends an Invitation

I want it on the record that I did not invite her in.

She arrived the way bad news always arrives — politely, at dusk, when the fire is low and everyone's guard is down and the tea is just this side of drinkable. One moment the camp was three tired travelers and a mule named Duchess who had opinions about everything; the next there was a woman standing at the edge of the light wearing my mother's face.

Not a likeness. Not an approximation. Her face. The exact architecture of it — the crooked left eyebrow that never quite recovered from the scar she got falling out of a pear tree at nineteen, the small mole beneath the jaw like a misplaced period, the way her mouth turned up at one corner as though perpetually about to share a joke with someone standing just behind you. I had spent eleven years memorizing that face and twenty-three years trying to forget it, and here it was, asking to sit down.

"Hello, little wren," she said, in her voice, with her lilt, using the name only she had ever used for me.

My hands did something clever then. They kept stirring the pot. I have found that when the world tears along its seams, the body wants a task, any task, and mine chose soup. Stir the soup, Sable. The dead woman at the edge of the firelight can wait until the barley softens.

"You're not her," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But I'm wearing her well, don't you think? We were very careful. The court keeps excellent records of the things people can't bear to remember."

Coral had gone still by the pack-mule, one hand resting on Duchess's neck, the other nowhere near their knife, which was its own kind of alarm. Coral's stillness is louder than most people's shouting. Perrin had stopped mid-sentence — he'd been telling us some elaborate nonsense about a dream where the mountain played dice with the moon — and now he sat with his bowl forgotten in his lap, staring at the visitor with the particular horror of a boy who can hear things the rest of us are spared.

"She sounds wrong," he whispered. "Underneath. Like a bell full of bees."

"Hush, darling," said my mother's face kindly. "Grown-ups are talking."

And that — *that* — was the moment I knew exactly what we were dealing with, because my mother had never once in her life told a child to hush. She believed children were the only honest audience an adult ever got. She'd have let Perrin talk until the stars burned out if he'd wanted to.

The thing wearing her sat down without being asked. That was wrong too. My mother waited to be invited into rooms; she said a welcome given freely was worth ten taken. The court beneath the mountain does not wait for welcomes. The court takes doors the way water takes cracks.

* * *

Let me tell you about my mother, since apparently the universe has decided I'm going to spend this chapter doing it whether I like it or not.

Her name was Maren. She sold herbs and blessings out of a cart in Lowmark, and she was the sort of woman who could make a fever break with a look and a cup of willow-bark, and who sang off-key on purpose because she said the gods distrusted pretty voices. When I was small I thought she was the most powerful person alive. When I was older I learned she was simply stubborn in the specific way that looks like power from the outside. She died of a winter cough when I was sixteen, two years before I took my vows, and I have spent every year since both grieving her and being quietly furious at myself for how much of my faith was really just missing her.

Which is, I now understand, precisely the weakness the undercourt trades in. They don't buy souls with cruelty first. They buy them with kindness. Coral saw the ghost-villagers tending hearths in the salt town and understood it before I did: the court's whole economy runs on longing. You show a person the shape of what they've lost, and they'll sign anything to hold it for five minutes.

"Your brother," said the thing in my mother's face, folding its hands the way Maren used to fold hers over needlework, "is cold."

The word went through me like a fishhook.

"He has been cold for forty years," it continued, gentle as snowfall. "He was sold young, you know. A desperate father, a failing farm, a god who promised rain and delivered debt. The court paid well. Children keep better than adults — less of them has hardened yet. He has been in the vaults a long time, little wren, beside the last warm thing in the world, and he is so very cold, and no one has come for him."

"I didn't know about him," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Until Vess spoke. I didn't know my brother existed."

"No. Your order made sure of that. Erasure is a service we respect." It smiled with my mother's mouth. "But you know now. And knowing changes the arithmetic, doesn't it? Here is our offer, priestess, and it is a simple one, because we despise complexity — it breeds lawyers."

It leaned forward into the firelight, and for one horrible heartbeat the light moved across her face and I saw, underneath, something vast and patient and old, the way you sometimes see the bones of a hill through thin grass. Then it was Maren again, warm and tired and kind.

"Deliver the soul the god Vess demands. Walk it to the vault yourself, place it where it belongs, and your brother walks free. Whole. Warm. Alive, even — the court can do that; we bought him breathing and we can return him breathing. Safe passage guaranteed, sworn on the deep names, which cannot be broken. No tolls, no traps, no more bandits in the passes. And when it's done, you go home, Sable. Home to Lowmark, home to a hearth, home to a brother who remembers being loved. All you have to do is give them one child's soul."

"One child," I repeated.

"One child," it agreed. "Who has been theirs for forty years already. Who doesn't remember sunlight. Who would not know what to do with freedom if you handed it to him wrapped in ribbon. Truly, little wren — and I say this with admiration — you would be returning stolen goods."

* * *

Here is the shameful part, the part I'll deny under torture and confess at funerals:

For about ninety seconds, I wanted to say yes.

Ninety seconds. I counted them afterward, the way you count the seconds between lightning and thunder, measuring how close the disaster came. Ninety seconds in which I stood inside my mother's face looking at my mother's face, and the arithmetic did its terrible work. One soul, already owned, already forgotten, against a brother restored. Against *home*. Do you know what home means to someone like me? I drowned my own god, I was stripped of my orders, my name is mud in three dioceses and a cautionary tale in a fourth. I have not had a home since I was sixteen years old and coughing season took everything warm out of the world.

The court offered me one back. With my brother in it. With my mother's laugh in it, probably — they have her records too, they said so themselves, they keep excellent files on the things people can't bear to remember.

And the truly insidious part, the part I keep turning over like a stone with something venomous under it, is that the offer wasn't even a lie. I looked for the lie. I'm good at finding lies; I spent six years hunting heretics and the rest of my life hunting my own hypocrisy, and I know all the places lies hide. But the bargain was clean. Deliver the soul, get the brother. Sworn on the deep names. The court keeps its contracts — that's what makes it monstrous. Cruelty breaks promises; the undercourt honors them to the letter while the letter hollows you out.

Because here's what the arithmetic leaves out: the soul in the vault isn't an object. It's a child. My nephew — though I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know until later, and oh, how that knowledge would rearrange me. But even ignorant, I knew enough. I'd heard Perrin describe the flickering light that called him *brother*. Souls don't stop being souls because someone filed paperwork on them. And the flame beside which that soul is kept — the last dragon-fire in the world — gutters a little more each day, and Vess wants it fed, and feeding it that soul feeds Vess, and Vess, whatever else it is, is not a god I owe anything but grief.

So the deal was real, and the deal was honest, and the deal was a knife held handle-first, waiting for me to take it by the blade and call it a handshake.

"Ninety seconds," I said aloud, finally, because saying it felt like driving a stake through it. "You gave me ninety seconds of wanting that. Well done. Genuinely. I hope whoever crafted her face got a bonus."

"We craft nothing," said my mother's face, and there was something almost wounded in it, which was the most convincing performance of the night. "We only remember. Your mother is in us, Sable. Every part of her you couldn't bury. Would you like to hear her sing?"

"No," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated myself for the crack, and I said it again, harder. "No."

* * *

That was when Coral stepped into the firelight.

They hadn't spoken the whole time. They have this way of going so quiet you forget they're armed, which I suspect is deliberate, and which I am beginning to suspect is also, in its strange mercenary fashion, protective — Coral stands between people and things the way walls stand between houses and weather, and pretends it's just a job.

"The answer's no," Coral said.

"It wasn't offered to you," said the envoy.

"Doesn't matter. The answer's no for both of them." Coral crouched down to the envoy's level, which is either insolence or intimacy depending on how you take it, and looked into my mother's face with an expression I can only call unimpressed. Twenty years of guiding liars through mountain passes will do that to a person. "I've heard this pitch before. Different face, different road, same song. 'Give us the thing you're carrying and we'll give you back the thing you lost.' Everyone who takes that deal arrives with empty hands and discovers the thing they lost was never in the seller's inventory. You don't have her brother. You have a hostage and a ledger entry."

"We have forty years of receipts," the envoy said coolly.

"And we have a boy who hears the mountain dreaming," Coral said, nodding at Perrin, "and a priestess who drowned her god, and between the three of us we have absolutely nothing left to lose, which makes us the worst customers you'll ever rob. So here's my counteroffer. You walk away. You tell your masters the delivery is cancelled, the account is closed, and the next time they want to negotiate, they can send someone wearing their own face. It might be instructive for them."

The silence that followed had texture. Perrin later swore the fire itself leaned away from the envoy, flames bending like grass in wind, and I believe him, because I felt it too — the whole night holding its breath around that borrowed face.

"You refuse," it said slowly, "on behalf of another."

"Somebody has to," Coral said. "Refusing for yourself is easy. Refusing for someone else is the whole job."

And I want to be clear about what that sentence did to me, because I was still standing in the wreckage of my ninety seconds, still smelling home in the smoke, still hearing my brother described as *cold*, and Coral — who prays to a silent god out of habit and hates themselves for it, who trusts nothing and no one, least of all gods — planted themselves between me and the shape of my own longing and refused it on my behalf. Not because they knew what was right. Because they decided I shouldn't carry the weight of choosing alone. I have been offered many things in my life — absolution, ordination, exile, death threats, once a genuinely lovely cheese — and none of them undid me quite like being refused-for, by a smuggler, in front of my dead mother's face.

The envoy rose. The warmth drained out of Maren's features the way color drains from a sunset, and for a moment it wore her the way a coat hangs on a hook — empty, waiting, patient.

"Pity," it said. "The vaults are cold, priestess. Cold things remember who declined to warm them." Its eyes — her eyes, brown, crinkled at the corners — slid to Perrin. "And the mountain knows the boy's footsteps now. Dreams travel faster than mules."

Then it stepped backward past the edge of the firelight and was simply not there anymore, the way a word is not there after you swallow it, and the camp was ordinary again, and Duchess the mule snorted as though she'd been holding her breath too.

* * *

Perrin cried. I'll say that plainly, because he'd want it said plainly and not dressed up as bravery. He put his head on his knees and shook, and said, "She sounded so nice, and underneath she was bees, and nobody else could hear the bees, and I thought maybe I was making it up, I thought maybe I'm just — wrong, all the time, about everything—" and I knelt down and held onto him and told him that hearing the bees is not a curse, it's a warning system, and warning systems save lives, and that he was right, he had been right, and being right about monsters is a horrible gift and I was sorry he had it.

Coral banked the fire and didn't say anything for a long time. Then, without looking at me:

"You wanted to say yes."

"For ninety seconds."

"I've wanted worse for longer." They poked the coals. "The deal was real, wasn't it. They'd have honored it."

"They'd have honored it to the letter."

"That's what scares me." Coral finally looked up, and their face in the firelight was the face of someone doing arithmetic of their own, and not liking the sum. "If they're offering deals this clean, this early, it means they think we can still be bought. Which means they haven't shown us their real price yet. Envoys don't lead with the threat. They lead with the gift."

"So the next thing they send won't wear a friendly face."

"No," Coral said. "The next thing they send will wear ours."

I laughed, because it was absurd, and then I stopped laughing, because it wasn't.

We broke camp before dawn. As I rolled my bedroll, I found a small object sitting on top of it that had not been there when I'd slept — a sprig of dried rowan, tied with red thread, the exact charm my mother used to tuck into my coat pocket every autumn against bad dreams.

I stood there a long moment, holding it, hating how much my hand wanted to keep it.

Behind us, far below in the dark, the mountain made a sound I had no name for. Perrin did. He went white and said, "It's counting," and refused to say what number it had reached.
