## 9. Descent at the Weeping Gate

I have spent twenty years learning the ways of Thornfell, and I want it on the record that I hated every step of what came next.

The Weeping Gate sits in a fold of the mountain that doesn't appear on any map worth trusting. I'd passed within half a mile of it maybe forty times in my life and never seen it, which should have been my first warning. Smugglers live and die by noticing things — a scuffed stone, a fire burned down wrong, a bird gone quiet. The Gate had been hiding from me my whole career, and when Sable finally stood in front of a blank cliff face and said, "Here," with the certainty of a woman reading something written on the inside of her own eyelids, I felt like a man who'd just learned his house had a thirteenth room.

"Here," I said. "Here is rock."

"It's listening," Perrin said.

The boy had been strange for three days — stranger than usual, which was saying something. He'd stopped sleeping properly. He'd sit up in the dark of our camp with his head cocked like a dog hearing a whistle nobody else could hear, and once, at dawn, he'd said, "It's counting again," in a voice so flat it made Sable drop her tea.

Now he pressed both palms flat against the cliff, and the cliff — I swear this on whatever's left of my reputation — *leaned into him.*

"There," Sable breathed.

Where the boy touched, the rock went translucent. Not glass-clear; more like sea ice, all shadow and depth, and beneath it something moved. A seam of darkness opened downward, wide as a cart road, and cold air breathed out of it smelling of brine and old iron and — this is the part I still don't like saying out loud — *tears.* Not metaphorically. The air off that passage tasted salted the way skin tastes after crying.

"The Weeping Gate," Sable said. "In the old rites they called it the mouth that mourns. Pilgrims used to come here to grieve. You'd descend carrying your sorrow and leave it with the mountain."

"And the mountain did what with it?"

"Kept it. That was the point. Nothing down there wastes anything."

Perrin pulled his hands back and looked at them like they'd betrayed him. "It knows we're coming," he said. "It's been waiting for us to knock."

"Wonderful," I said. "A door with manners." I checked my gear out of habit — rope, flint, the long knife I've kept since before either of these two could walk, a week's rations wrapped in oilcloth. Then I stood there like an idiot, because everything I knew about entering a place was useless. You scout a ruin for structural rot. You case a fortress for guard rotations. What do you check on a doorway that *mourns?*

Sable caught me doing it. "You're counting exits."

"I'm always counting exits."

"How many are there?"

"One." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile, but it was an honest one, and from Sable those were the only kind she had left. "Welcome to my old life, Coral. Everything has one exit, and it's usually faith."

"I hate your old life."

"So do I. Come on."

* * *

The first hour underground, I was fine. Genuinely fine. The passage was broad and sloped gently, worn smooth by feet that hadn't walked it in centuries, and there were niches cut into the walls every hundred paces where pilgrims had once set candles. Some of the niches still held stubs of wax fused to the stone like fossils. I know tunnels. Tunnels are honest. They go where they go, and if you count your steps and mark your turns, you can always find your way back.

That's what I told myself, anyway, right up until the moment I realized the tunnel had turned.

Not us. *It.*

We'd been walking straight — I'd been counting paces, keeping my left hand on the wall the way you're supposed to, maintaining a dead reckoning that has never once failed me in twenty years of moving contraband through places that wanted me dead. And the wall under my hand curved. Slowly, politely, the way a river changes course, but unmistakably: we were no longer heading down-and-east, which was where the Gate had led. We were heading down-and-*north*, toward the deep roots of Thornfell, and behind us the passage we'd come through had — 

I turned around. The passage behind us was not the same passage.

Same width. Same candle-niches. Same fossil wax. But the slope was different, and the brine-smell was stronger, and when I held up my lamp the light fell on a wall that had not been there when we entered.

"Stop," I said. "Everyone stop. Don't move."

They stopped. Good kids. Even terrified, they listened, and I'll admit that warmed something in me that I didn't have time to examine.

"Coral?" Sable said.

"The road moved."

"What?"

"The *road moved.*" My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the one professional skill I've never lost. "This isn't the way back. It looks like the way back. It's wearing the way back like a coat. But we've been turned, and I don't—" I stopped myself before I said *I don't know where we are,* because I am a professional, and professionals say *we've deviated from the known route* even when the truth is that the ground itself is lying to us.

Perrin was looking at me with an expression I recognized too late as pity.

"You can't read it," he said. Not mocking. Almost gentle. "Because it's not a road. It's a dream. Roads stay put. Dreams don't."

"Perrin—"

"You have to listen instead of look." He crouched, laid his palm against the floor, and closed his eyes. His lips moved. For a moment nothing. Then the boy shivered all over, like a horse shaking off flies, and said: "It's dreaming about water. There's a river under us that remembers being rain. If we follow the memory of falling, we go down. If we follow the memory of the sea, we go deeper than we want."

"That means nothing to me."

"It means everything to *it*," Sable said quietly. She'd gone pale. I watched her make herself kneel next to the boy, watch her lay her hand beside his on the stone, and I saw the exact moment the old rites woke up in her body — her breathing changed, her spine straightened, and for three seconds she looked like the priestess her order had drowned rather than defrocked. "Coral. He's right. This is how the deep pilgrims traveled. You don't navigate the mountain. You *ask* it."

"I don't ask anything anymore," I said. "Asking is how gods get their hooks in you."

"This isn't a god."

"No. It's worse. Gods lie to get what they want. Mountains don't even notice you're there." I looked down the wrong-shaped passage, at the candle-fossils and the brine-dark, and admitted the thing I'd been choking on since the Gate: "I'm useless down here. Twenty years of knowing every goat track in Thornfell, and the first time it actually matters, the ground won't hold still."

Nobody said anything comforting, which I appreciated. Comfort would have made it worse.

Then Perrin stood up, wiped his palms on his trousers, and said, "Follow me. The falling-memory goes left here. And Coral — hold my hand if you need to. It likes you. I think it's curious why you smell like promises."

* * *

I took his hand. Write it down somewhere. Coral Nakamura, who once talked a border garrison into believing a mule train was a funeral procession, held a twelve-year-old's sweaty palm in a haunted tunnel because the alternative was being lost forever inside a thing that dreamed.

And here's the humiliating truth: it worked. Better than it should have. The boy would stop, touch stone, murmur, tilt his head, and then choose — left, right, down, wait — and every choice was right. Twice the passage forked into near-identical corridors and twice he picked without hesitating. Once he made us stand perfectly still for the better part of ten minutes while something vast shifted overhead, a grinding like a millstone the size of a cathedral, and afterward the corridor we'd been standing in simply wasn't there anymore; the wall had closed over it smooth as scar tissue, exactly where we'd have been walking if I'd been leading.

After that, I stopped pretending. I gave him the lead and I gave him my full attention, and I started doing the only thing I was still good for: watching him. Learning him. Which dreams made him flinch (the ones about teeth), which ones made him smile (a recurring one about a woman singing — I chose not to ask), which ones he lied about ("it's nothing" meant it was very much something).

Somewhere in the fourth hour, Sable fell into step beside me and murmured, "You're doing it again."

"Doing what."

"Collecting us. You hoard people the way other mercenaries hoard coin. You've decided he's yours to protect and you haven't told him yet."

"He's a client."

"He's a child who navigates nightmares, and you've already started planning how to feed him." She nodded ahead, where Perrin had stopped at a niche and was frowning at the wax fossil inside it. "Careful, Coral. In my order we had a word for someone who loves people by guarding them. We called it *priesthood.*"

"I'm not a priest."

"No," she agreed. "You're worse. You don't even believe in what you're devoted to."

Before I could answer, Perrin said, "Listen," and everything else stopped mattering.

* * *

The sound came up through the floor first. A rushing, a hush, a rhythm — and then, distantly, unmistakably, weeping. Not one voice. Hundreds. Thousands. A congregation of grief echoing up some shaft below us, rising through the stone like water through cloth.

"The Gate cries," Sable whispered. "But the Gate is miles above us now."

"No," Perrin said. His face had gone gray. "There's more than one gate. Every entrance to the deep is a mouth, and mouths—" He swallowed. "Mouths cry when they open. Something big is opening. Something's coming *up* to meet us, or we're coming down to meet it, and either way—"

He stopped. Went rigid. His hand found mine and gripped hard enough to hurt, and I let it, because the boy had earned the right to bruise me.

"It says," Perrin whispered, "that it knows about the debt. It says the court has lit lamps along the road for us. It says—" His breath hitched. "It says one of the lamps is burning with a face I'll recognize."

Sable made a sound low in her throat. I put myself between the boy and the dark, which was stupid, because the dark was everywhere, but you don't become a smuggler by being sensible.

"Which road?" I asked the floor, the walls, the whole dreaming weight of the mountain. "Tell me which road, and I'll keep him off it."

The mountain answered through the boy, and its answer was the worst thing yet, because it wasn't cruel. It was almost kind:

*"All roads are its road, little oath-keeper. But bring your lamp lit, and walk behind the dreamer. The flame remembers you were promised."*

Behind us, far above, faint as a memory of a memory, the Weeping Gate began to cry in earnest — and somewhere below, in the dark where the last dragon-fire guttered, something heard it, and answered with a sound like a bell tolling underwater.

Perrin turned to me. His eyes were wet, and not from the brine.

"It wants us to hurry," he said.
