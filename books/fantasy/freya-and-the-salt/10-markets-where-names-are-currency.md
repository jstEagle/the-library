## 10. Markets Where Names Are Currency

I want it on the record that I did not want to go into the bazaar. I said so, in fact, twice, in the flat priestess voice I keep for occasions when saying a thing plainly might still do some good, which is to say almost never.

"We can go around," I told Coral.

"There is no around." Coral stood at the mouth of the tunnel where the Weeping Gate's brine-trickle finally gave out and the air went warm and amber-lit, like the inside of a lantern. "There's the market or there's the dark, and the dark has teeth."

"Everything down here has teeth."

"Yes," Coral said, with the terrible calm of a person who has already done their grieving and come out the other side into logistics. "But the market's teeth are for sale."

That is the thing about Coral Nakamura that nobody who hires them ever understands. They think the mercenary calm is armor over a soft heart, and they are right, but they have the direction wrong. The heart isn't under the armor. The armor is under the heart. You have to get through all that love first — love of craft, love of route, love of every sorry soul they've ever walked across a mountain — before you reach anything as simple as fear. I was a priestess for eleven years. I know armor when I see it. I also know what it costs to wear it, and I watched the bill come due in the bazaar beneath Thornfell, and I have never forgiven myself for standing there and letting it be paid.

* * *

The bazaar does not announce itself. One moment you are walking through warm stone and the next you are simply *in* it — a cavern the size of a cathedral's ambition, terraced down in rings, hung with lamps that burn without flame, lit instead by what I can only describe as *remembered* light. Every lamp glows the color of somebody's best day. I passed one that was gold as harvest evening and felt, absurdly, my grandmother's kitchen; I passed another the blue-white of a specific winter morning I have never had and grieved it anyway.

The stalls sell everything a person can lose.

Not goods. Losses. That is the trade down here: memories, names, years, griefs, the sound of your own mother calling you home at dusk. The merchants are not people, exactly. Some were people once — you could tell by the way they flinched when touched by genuine feeling, like old burns. Others had never bothered with flesh at all. One stall-keeper was a swarm of moths arranged patiently in the shape of a woman, and she sold secondhand courage by the spoonful, and I am ashamed to say I priced it.

Perrin stayed between us, hood up, hands in his sleeves. The boy had been quiet since the Gate — quieter than a child should be, quiet the way a house is quiet after something breaks in another room. He kept pressing his palm flat against the stone as we walked, and I realized after the third time he wasn't steadying himself. He was listening. The mountain dreams even here, maybe especially here, and my guess was the dream was having a nightmare.

"Don't touch anything," Coral murmured. "Don't taste anything. Don't tell anyone your name."

"You've said that four times."

"And I'll say it until it works."

"It hasn't worked yet," Perrin said, because he was twelve and honest, and a moth-woman three stalls over turned her whole congregation toward him with an expression I can only call *appetite*.

We moved along.

Here is what I knew going in, from the rags of theology they left me when they stripped my orders: the undercourt runs on currency the surface world doesn't recognize because the surface world spends it without counting. Up top, a name is just a name. Down here, a name is a deed to a soul. A memory is a brick of yourself, and bricks build houses, and houses hold fires, and the last dragon-fire in the world was burning somewhere past all these rings of commerce, in a bowl of bone, guttering, and the court meant to let it die and rule the dark forever after.

We needed to find the vault. To find the vault we needed someone who knew the way. And knowledge, in this place, was not given. It was *sold*, and the price was always the same kind: you.

I hate markets. I want to say that plainly too, since I'm keeping a record. Not because of the haggling — I grew up in Lowmark, I can haggle fishwives under the table — but because a market is a machine for making need visible, and visible need is the raw ore of cruelty. Everyone in that cavern was missing something. That was the admission fee. And circling above them all, patient as tide, were the ones who sold back what you'd lost, at interest, in installments, forever.

* * *

The broker we found called herself Ninepence, which should have been our first warning, because nothing down here prices itself honestly. She had a stall of small glass bottles, each holding a curl of colored smoke, and she sat behind them like a spider who had read etiquette books.

"Surface folk," she said, tasting the word. "You smell like weather. How novel."

"We're looking for the fire vault," Coral said. No preamble. Coral bargains the way they walk — direct line, no switchbacks unless the direct line is watched.

Ninepence laughed. It was a nice laugh. That was the trap, of course. Everything down here that's nice is a trap; niceness is the local dialect of hunger. "Everyone who comes through wants the vault. The court wants it guarded, the ghosts want it freed, the fire wants it fed, and you—" she leaned forward, and her eyes did something eyes shouldn't, focusing on a point slightly *inside* Coral rather than on them— "you want it for reasons you haven't finished admitting even to yourself. Delicious. Yes, I can sell you the way."

"The price."

"A memory." She spread her hands over the little bottles like a farmer showing off a field. "One, freely surrendered. Not stolen — stolen memories spoil. Freely given, they keep beautifully. Something cherished. The vault road is expensive information, friend. Cheap memories buy cheap answers."

Coral went very still.

I have watched Coral face bandits, rockfall, a god waking after twenty years of silence, and I have never seen them go still the way they went still at that stall. Stillness, in Coral, is not calm. It is the body's way of standing guard over something while the rest of the person decides whether to surrender it.

"No," I said.

Nobody had asked me.

"No," I said again, stepping forward, putting myself half between them, which was stupid, because you cannot body-block a transaction. "Coral. Listen to me. I spent eleven years watching people trade pieces of themselves to feel whole. It doesn't work. It has never once worked. You give it a memory and it gives you a road and then you spend the rest of your life walking roads wondering why the landscape feels like it's missing a room."

"Sable." Coral didn't look at me. "How else are we paying?"

"With cleverness. With time. With—"

"We don't have time." And there it was, under the logistics, the thing Coral never says outright: the fire was dying. Perrin had heard it counting down in his sleep for weeks. Whatever number the mountain had reached, it was smaller today than yesterday. "And I'm not sending the boy in there alone, and I'm not asking you to pay, because you've already paid enough for one lifetime, and I—" Coral stopped. Recalibrated. The armor sliding back into place over the heart. "It's one memory. I'll choose a cheap one."

"Cheap ones buy cheap answers," Ninepence repeated sweetly.

"I know what I said," Coral told her. "Give me a moment."

* * *

They stepped aside, to the edge of the terrace, where the remembered light fell gold and they stood in it with their back to us, and I did not follow, because there is a privacy to surrender even when you're about to watch it happen, and I owed them that much.

I talked to Perrin instead, low, keeping my eyes anywhere but on Coral's shoulders.

"What are they thinking about?" he asked. Children ask the questions adults spend decades learning to dodge.

"Something they love," I said.

"How do you know?"

"Because that's the only kind worth stealing."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "When I was little, before I ran, my father had a steward who used to say you should never sell your birthright for a mess of pottage. Sunday lessons. I thought it was about soup." He scuffed his boot against the stone. "It's not about soup, is it."

"No, love. It's not about soup."

Across the terrace, Coral's hand rose and pressed flat against their own sternum, right over the heart, the way you do when something aches and you want it to know you've noticed. Then it dropped. Then they turned around and walked back, and their face was the smuggler's face, the professional face, the one clients trust with their contraband and their lives, and I understood with a sick lurch that they had chosen already, and that whatever they'd chosen, they had chosen it *for us*, and that this was somehow worse than if they'd chosen it for coin.

"One memory," Coral said to Ninepence. "Freely given. Cherished. Take it and show us the road."

"What will you give?" Ninepence asked, and her voice had gone gentle, gentler than any merchant's should be, the gentleness of a knife being warmed before use. "Name it. Let me hear what you've decided to lose."

Coral closed their eyes.

"My mother," they said. "Singing."

The words came out level and terrible, like a report of casualties.

"She used to sing while she mended nets. There was a song — I don't remember its name, I never knew its name, it was just *hers*. She'd sing it and the whole house would feel like it was floating on something. I've kept it forty years. Through the oath, through the silence, through every night Vess wouldn't answer. It's the one thing I own that was never part of any bargain." Coral opened their eyes. "Take that one."

"*No*," I said, and this time my voice cracked clean down the middle, priestess composure and all, because I knew — I *knew* — what Coral was doing. They weren't choosing the cheapest memory. They were choosing the most expensive one they could bear to lose, because Ninepence had said the vault road was expensive information, and Coral Nakamura does not underpay. Not for gods. Not for brokers. Not for anyone. If the price must be paid, pay it fully, pay it honestly, owe nothing — that is the whole rotten architecture of their honor, and it is magnificent, and it is going to kill them one day, and I stood there and let it start.

"Done," said Ninepence.

She didn't take it with claws or magic or any of the theater I'd been braced for. She held out a small glass bottle, no bigger than my thumb, and Coral breathed into it. That was all. One long exhale, the kind you make at the end of grief, and a thread of smoke curled out of them — pale gold, shot through with a color I don't have a word for, the color of lamplight on water at dusk, of nets drying, of a voice you'd know anywhere — and coiled into the glass, and Ninepence stoppered it, and it was done.

Coral swayed.

I caught their elbow. And here is the part I will carry to whatever grave takes me: Coral looked *fine*. That was the horror of it. They straightened, thanked the broker, asked the question about the vault road, received the answer — three turnings past the Bone Choir, follow the sound of the fire's hunger, don't answer if it calls your name — all crisp, all competent, all *Coral*.

But lighter. They came back lighter, the way a ship comes back lighter after jettisoning cargo in a storm, riding high and wrong, and when they smiled at me to reassure me the smile fit perfectly and meant nothing, the way a coat fits a mannequin.

"Coral," I said quietly, as we walked. "What did she sing?"

They stopped. I watched them reach for it — actually watched, in real time, the hand go out into the space where the song had lived for forty years and close on nothing. Their face did not crumple. That was the worst part. Their face did the opposite of crumple. It smoothed, politely, around the absence, the way water smooths around a stone that's been removed from a riverbed, leaving the shape of the hole and no hole at all.

"I don't know what you mean," they said.

"You told the broker. Your mother singing. Nets."

"Did I?" A small frown, genuine, searching. "I don't remember telling her that."

Perrin made a sound. A small one, a boy-sound, quickly swallowed, and he grabbed Coral's sleeve with both hands, and for one moment the conduit and the mercenary stood there in the amber light and neither of them knew what had been taken, and only I did, and I would have given anything — *anything*, and I mean that literally now, I understand the exchange rate of this place — to un-know it.

"It's fine," Coral said, patting the boy's hands. "It's fine. I feel fine. Better, actually. Lighter." And they believed it. That's the design. That's the genius of the theft: the wound doesn't hurt because the wound is gone, and the scar doesn't ache because the scar went with it, and all that remains is a person shaped slightly wrong, walking slightly high, smiling slightly too easily, carrying one less reason to survive.

* * *

We walked toward the Bone Choir. Coral led, because leading is breathing to them, and gave us the route in their crisp guide's cadence — three turnings, mind the drop, stay off the singing stones — and every word was correct and every word was hollow, and I trailed a step behind with Perrin's cold little hand in mine and did the arithmetic I had sworn off doing.

One memory bought a road. What would the next toll cost? And the one after? Coral would pay. Coral would pay and pay and smile lighter each time, and the person who broke their oath for a child's soul, the person who was going to stand in front of a god and refuse — how much of *them* would be left to do the refusing?

"Priestess," Perrin whispered.

"Hm?"

"The mountain says—" He stopped, frowning, listening to something under the floor of the world. "It says the fire knows what Coral paid. It says the fire keeps accounts too." His grip tightened on my hand. "It says *hurry*."

Ahead of us, in the dark past the third turning, something vast began to sing in bone voices, and beneath the singing, faint and getting fainter, I could hear the last dragon-fire counting down — and Coral walked toward it whistling a tune they did not know they had lost, and the tune was wrong, it was *wrong*, it was the shape of their mother's song with the heart scooped out of it, and I followed them into the dark knowing that before this mountain let us leave, I would have to find a way to give that song back.
