## 11. The Guttering Flame

Perrin walked like a boy who had been told a secret and couldn't stop grinning about it. That was the trouble with conduits — nobody warns you they're also children.

"Left here," he said, at a junction where left was a wall.

"That's a wall," I said.

"It's a wall *now*," Perrin said, with the patience of the very young explaining weather to the very old. "It's dreaming about being a corridor. If we go while it's still thinking about it, it'll let us."

I looked at Sable. Sable looked at me. Twenty years of guiding smugglers through Thornfell's passes, and I had never once in my life been asked to walk through a wall because a nine-year-old said it was dreaming about being somewhere else. There were days I missed bandits. Bandits made sense. Bandits wanted your purse and occasionally your throat, and both of those were things you could plan around.

"Trust him," Sable said quietly. "He hasn't been wrong yet."

"He was wrong about the stairs."

"The stairs came back," Perrin protested. "They just took the long way."

The stairs had, in fact, come back. We had descended a flight of steps into a chamber, turned around to leave, and found the staircase gone — replaced by smooth stone that hummed faintly, like a struck bell someone had muffled with their hand. Perrin had put his palm flat against it, closed his eyes, and said, "Come back, please," in the tone of a child calling a dog, and the stairs had unfolded out of the rock like a paper flower dropped in water. I had watched it happen and my hands had not stopped shaking for an hour afterward. I told them it was the cold. Underground, nobody can check.

So: the wall. I put my shoulder to it first, because if the mountain decided to eat anyone, it could start with the professional. The stone gave under me like the skin on warm milk, and then I was through, standing in a corridor that had not existed ten seconds earlier and would probably not exist ten seconds from now, and behind me I heard Sable murmur something that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse, and Perrin laugh — a small, bright, terrible sound, the laugh of a boy who has just discovered the world will bend for him and hasn't yet learned what bends back.

"Good lad," I said, because my voice needed something to do that wasn't screaming.

* * *

We had been underground for three days. I want to be precise about that, because time down there is a liar and I need at least one honest number to hold onto. Three days since the Weeping Gate had swallowed us, brine running down its carved faces like the mountain crying over what it was about to do to us. Three days of corridors that rearranged themselves when you weren't looking, of markets where merchants sold years of somebody's childhood in twist-paper packets, of the particular silence that falls when you realize every shadow in the room is watching you with professional interest.

Three days since Coral Nakamura, who once navigated the Salt Labyrinth blindfolded during a whiteout because a client paid double, had stood in a passage that had moved and felt — for one long, humiliating moment — completely, catastrophically lost.

You don't get over that. You carry it. It sits in your chest like a swallowed stone and reminds you, at odd hours, that everything you are good at is conditional, and the conditions do not apply here.

But Perrin could hear the mountain dreaming, and the mountain, it turned out, liked him. Liked him the way old people like the one grandchild who visits. So we followed a boy through the dark, and the dark, mostly, let us pass.

Now he stopped so suddenly I nearly trod on him.

"What is it?" Sable whispered.

Perrin didn't answer right away. His head was tilted, listening to something below hearing, and his face had gone strange — not scared, exactly. Awed. Which frightened me more than scared would have, because awe is what people feel right before they stop being careful.

"It's close," he said. "The fire. It's — " He frowned, hunting for the word. "It's *tired*. Like a fire that's been burning a long time and everyone's gone to bed and it doesn't know if it should keep going."

Sable made a small sound. I pretended not to hear it. She'd been making those sounds for three days, ever since Vess had spoken through her old rites and named the debt, and I had a suspicion about what she wasn't telling us — the way she flinched whenever the soul was mentioned, the way she'd gone white as salt when the market-slaves had asked whose name we carried. But secrets are currency down here, and I wasn't going to spend mine until I knew what hers bought.

"How much farther?" I asked.

"Not far." Perrin pointed down a corridor that sloped gently, its walls veined with something that glowed a dull, dying red — like embers seen through closed eyelids. "That way. But Coral?"

"What?"

"I think there are guards."

"There are always guards," I said. "Guards are how you know the treasure's real."

* * *

The vein-light thickened as we went, until the walls themselves seemed to pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. And maybe they were. I'd stopped assuming the mountain was dead rock around day two. Down here the stone breathed. You could feel it in the floor, a rhythm far too slow and far too large to be anything comfortable, and every step landed inside that rhythm whether you wanted it to or not.

Then the corridor opened, without warning, into the vault.

Oh.

I've seen things, in twenty years of this trade. I watched a glacier calve at midnight and heard the ice scream like a living thing. I saw the drowned bells of Lowmark rise in a drought year, still ringing under the air. None of it — none of it — prepared me for the bowl of bone.

It sat in the center of a cavern vast enough to hold a cathedral and its god. Carved from the skull of something that must have lived before mountains had names, it was big as a fishing boat, bleached ivory shot through with veins of gold, and in its hollow cradled —

Fire.

Not fire like a hearth. Not fire like a torch. Fire like a *creature*. It lay coiled in the bone bowl, wings folded, head tucked, no bigger than a campfire now — but every lick of it had shape and intention, and even guttering, even half-asleep, it burned with a light that made the whole cavern look like the inside of a lantern. The red glow of the walls came from it. The warmth on my face came from it. I realized I had been cold for three days and hadn't noticed until this moment, standing in the last dragon-fire in the world, feeling it look at me.

Because it did look. Fire shouldn't have eyes. This had something better than eyes — a regard, ancient and exhausted, that swept over the three of us and paused, briefly, on each. On Sable it lingered. On Perrin it softened. On me it stayed longest, and I got the distinct, absurd impression of being recognized. Of being *known*, all the way down, by something that had been alive since before my gods were ideas.

"Well," I said, because wry is armor and I was wearing all of mine. "That's that, then. The last dragon-fire. Smaller than the stories."

"Stories exaggerate," Sable whispered. Her voice was wrecked. She had both hands pressed over her mouth, and above them her eyes were streaming, and she wasn't looking at the fire at all.

She was looking at what floated beside it.

A foot away from the bone bowl, held in a cage of pale light that hung from nothing, was a small flickering thing. Soul-sized. Child-sized. A knot of soft gold-white radiance, dimmer than the fire, pulsing gently — and as we stepped closer it pulsed *faster*, straining against its cage like a moth against glass, and a voice came out of it.

Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words, the way you remember sunlight in winter. But it rang clear in the vault, and it said:

"*Brother?*"

Perrin made a noise I'll hear until I die.

He ran. No caution, no thought of guards or traps or thirty years of professional instinct screaming at him to stop — he ran straight at the cage of light and pressed his hands to it, and the light flared against his palms like a cat pushing its face into a familiar hand.

"You're real," Perrin was saying, laughing and crying at once. "You're real, you're real, I heard you, I heard you counting down every night, I thought I was making you up — "

"*Brother,*" the light said again, warmer now, and the cage brightened where he touched it. "*Brother, brother. You came. Nobody comes.*"

"Perrin." Sable had followed him, and her voice cracked apart on his name. She knelt beside the cage, and up close I could see what I hadn't seen before: the light wasn't formless. There was a shape inside it, small and curled, a child drawn in light instead of ink. A boy, maybe. Younger than Perrin. His knees pulled up, his head lifted, staring at us with eyes like two drops of the fire itself. "What's your name?" Sable whispered. "Sweetheart, what's your name?"

The light-child tilted his head. And something in his face — the set of the jaw, the way one eyebrow sat higher than the other — hit me sideways, because I'd spent three days watching Sable make that exact expression over maps and rations and my generally deplorable character.

"I had one," the child said. His voice was thin, worn, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. "Before. They keep it in a jar somewhere. They said I could have it back if I was good." He looked at Perrin. "Are you really my brother? I dreamed brothers. When it was quiet. I dreamed a brother who could hear me."

"Yes," Perrin said instantly, fiercely, in the voice of a boy deciding something permanent. "Yes. I'm your brother. I'm getting you out."

And that was when I understood why Sable had been making those small sounds for three days. Why she'd gone white in the market. Why she flinched at the word *soul* like it was a blade.

Forty years ago, a child was sold to the court beneath the mountain. Vess demanded that soul delivered before the flame died. And Sable — defrocked, disgraced, drowning-her-god Sable — had known, all along, whose soul it was. Her brother's son. Her own blood, sold before she could stop it, kept in a cage of light beside a dying fire for forty years while she prayed to gods who didn't answer.

I looked at her. She looked at me. Neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't break something we'd need intact later, and because the child in the cage was already talking again, and every word out of his mouth was a small knife.

"Is it almost time?" he asked. "The tall ones said when the fire goes out, I go home. They said I'd get to sleep." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I have ever seen, because it was a *grateful* smile. "I'm tired, brother. I want to sleep."

"No," Perrin said. "No, you're coming *with* us — "

The fire stirred.

It rose off the bone bowl like a snake uncoiling, and though it was small — though it was dying — the whole cavern shuddered with the motion, dust sifting from a ceiling miles overhead, and its regard fixed on the child in the cage with an intensity that raised the hair on my arms. When it spoke, it didn't use sound. It used heat. The words arrived as warmth against the skin, pressing meaning directly into the blood:

***KEEPER.***

The child turned toward it. "*Keeper?*"

***MY KEEPER.*** The fire settled back into its bowl, weary. ***THEY SOLD ME A KEEPER AND THEN LOCKED US BOTH AWAY TO DIE TOGETHER. FORTY YEARS, SMALL ONE. YOU HAVE KEPT ME COMPANY IN THE DARK. THAT WAS NOT THE BARGAIN. THAT WAS KINDNESS.***

"Is it time?" the child asked again, hopeful.

***NOT YET,*** the fire said, and its gaze slid to us — to me — and the weight of it was like a hand on the back of my neck. ***STRANGERS. SMUGGLER. PRIESTESS. DREAMER. I KNOW WHY YOU COME. THE GOD WHO SENT YOU DOES NOT WANT WHAT IT TOLD YOU IT WANTS.***

"Yeah," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only trick I've ever truly mastered. "We'd kind of figured that out."

***GOOD.*** A pause. The fire dimmed, and I watched — actually watched — one of its coils go grey at the edge, ash where flame had been, and something in my chest clenched hard enough to hurt. ***THEN LISTEN. I DO NOT HAVE LONG. WHEN I GO OUT, THE MOUNTAIN DIES. THE OLD ROADS CLOSE. EVERY PRAYER EVER ANSWERED BECOMES A DEBT COLLECTED IN FULL. AND THE CHILD — ***

The fire stopped. Not hesitated — *stopped*, the way a clock stops, and in that frozen instant the entire cavern went silent as a held breath, and I understood that the next words were being chosen with enormous care.

***— THE CHILD IS NOT WHAT THEY TOLD YOU EITHER.***

"What do you mean?" Sable whispered.

But I never got to hear the answer, because Perrin shouted — a raw, cracking shout, nothing like the confident boy who'd walked us through dreaming walls — "*Coral!*" and I turned, knife already out of my sleeve, and saw them.

Guards. A dozen of them, maybe more, pouring out of passages that had definitely not been there a moment ago. Tall ones — the child had said *the tall ones*, and now I knew what he meant. They were shaped like people the way a statue is shaped like a person: all the lines correct, none of the life. Bone-pale, jointed wrong, wearing armor of black salt that drank the firelight, and their faces were smooth as eggs except where mouths should be, and the mouths opened anyway.

The nearest one spoke, and its voice was the sound the mountain made when it dreamed — deep, slow, layered, a hundred voices saying one word:

"*Stay.*"

Another drew a blade of grey glass. Another. Another. The ring tightened. Perrin grabbed the cage of light with both hands and pulled, and the cage didn't budge, and the child cried out — not in pain, in fear, in the specific fear of a thing that has been locked up so long it knows exactly what recapture feels like.

Sable was chanting — her old rites, the drowned god's words, useless and desperate. I put myself between the guards and the boys, which was stupid, which was suicide, which was absolutely what twenty years of professional instinct told me to do anyway, because that's the job. You stand between the client and the bad thing. Even when the client is a conduit child and the bad thing is the mountain's own police and the treasure is a soul that calls your client *brother*.

The lead guard raised its glass blade.

And the fire roared.

Small as it was, guttering as it was, it roared like the end of the world, and the cavern lit white, and every guard froze mid-step — because whatever else the undercourt was, it was built around this flame, and you do not move against the hearth while you stand inside the house.

***RUN,*** the fire said, to me, directly, heat searing the words into my skin. ***TAKE THE DREAMER. TAKE THE KEEPER'S UNCLE-BLOOD. I CAN HOLD THEM — BRIEFLY. I HAVE HELD MANY THINGS BRIEFLY FOR TWENTY THOUSAND YEARS.***

"We're not leaving you," Perrin said.

***YOU ARE,*** the fire said gently. ***THAT IS THE ONLY WAY ANY OF THIS WORKS. GO. FOLLOW THE BOY. HE KNOWS THE WAY OUT BETTER THAN THE MOUNTAIN KNOWS THE WAY IN.***

The guards began to move again, slower, fighting the fire's command. The lead one's egg-smooth face turned toward me, and its mouth opened wider than a mouth should, and it said, in that layered dream-voice, in a tone of terrible courtesy:

"*The court thanks you for delivering the keeper's escort. Your debt may now be collected.*"

And every passage out of the vault filled, at once, with the sound of more of them coming.

I grabbed Perrin's collar. Sable grabbed Perrin's hand. Behind us the fire blazed and blazed, spending itself like a miser in his final hour, holding the door — and ahead of us, down a corridor that was already starting to dream itself shut, the boy who could hear the mountain said, in a small steady voice that made me prouder and more terrified than I have ever been in my life:

"This way. And Coral? Run *fast*. It's about to forget this hallway exists."
