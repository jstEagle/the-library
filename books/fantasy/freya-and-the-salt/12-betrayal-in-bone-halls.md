## 12. Betrayal in Bone Halls

I want to say, before anything else, that I was eleven years old and I had not slept properly in nine days.

That's not an excuse. Coral would tell you excuses are like smugglers' maps — everyone's got one, none of them hold up when the road moves. But it's true, and truth is supposed to count for something down here, in a place where they sell it by the ounce. So: eleven years old, nine days without real sleep, standing in a hall made of bones that were not arranged like a threat but like *architecture*, like someone had looked at a ribcage the size of a cathedral and thought, *yes, load-bearing.*

And the voice had been talking to me for three days.

* * *

It started polite. Everything down here starts polite — that's what the salt-dead told us, back in the village where the ghosts baked bread nobody ate just to keep their hands busy. *The court buys souls with kindness first,* the old dead woman had said, kneading dough that steamed though there was no oven lit. *Cruelty after. Kindness is cheaper.* I'd held her hand while she said it. Her hand felt like holding a handful of cold river stones that remembered being warm.

The kindness came to me at night, which is when I'm weakest, because night is when the mountain dreams loudest through me. Everyone thinks my gift is hearing things. It's not. It's being heard. The mountain pours itself into me the way water finds a crack, and I am mostly crack.

So on the first night in the bone halls, when the others slept and I lay staring up at vertebrae arched overhead like the inside of some enormous throat, the voice didn't come from outside. It came from the same place the dreams do. It sounded like my own thoughts, if my own thoughts had better manners.

*You haven't slept since Lowmark,* it observed.

"I sleep," I whispered. "Just badly."

*Nine days of badly. You dream of your father's house. Of the corridor outside your room. Of the door at the end of it that was locked every night for two years, and how you pressed your ear to it and heard nothing, and how the nothing was worse than shouting.*

"Stop."

*Of course.* A pause, courteous as a bowed head. *But I will point out that here, no one locks doors on children. Here, doors open for you. That is all we have ever offered.*

That's the thing about the undercourt's kindness. It's never a lie. It's just arithmetic. They offer you exactly the thing you're missing, weighed to the grain, and they never mention what it costs because they don't have to. You'll do the math yourself, eventually, at three in the morning, alone, in a language of wanting.

I did the math on the third night.

* * *

Here is what I want written down somewhere, in ink that doesn't fade: I did not betray them because I stopped loving them. I betrayed them because I was so tired that love felt like another weight to carry, and the voice offered to set me down.

The nightmares weren't even about the mountain. That's the joke of it. My gift means I don't get normal dreams — no flying, no teeth falling out. I get the mountain's dreams, secondhand, and the mountain has been dreaming of its own death for four hundred years. Every night: the last dragon-fire guttering in its bowl of bone, and beneath that dream a counting, patient as dripping water. *Nine hundred thousand and forty-one. Nine hundred thousand and forty.* The heartbeats left in the flame, maybe. Or the prayers. I never found out. I stopped wanting to know around the time the numbers started showing up when I was awake, ticking along under conversations like a debt collector humming in the next room.

Coral knew. Coral always knows, which is insufferable and also the only reason any of us were still alive. On the second day in the halls, while Sable argued theology with a market-priest whose face kept sliding between versions of itself, Coral crouched in front of me in the shadow of a femur-pillar and said, "Perrin. Look at me. Whatever's been talking to you — it wants something. Nothing down here talks for free. When it asks, you come find me. Wake me. Kick me awake. I sleep light."

"You don't sleep at all," I said.

"I sleep *light*," Coral repeated, with the particular ferocity of someone who has decided this is true. "Promise me."

And I promised. I want that on record too. I promised, and I meant it, and meaning it lasted almost exactly thirty hours.

Because on the third night the voice changed tactics, the way a good merchant does when the customer hesitates. It stopped selling and started giving.

*I've taken the counting away,* it said, softly, into the dark behind my eyes. And it had. For the first time in weeks, the silence under my skull was just silence. I lay there listening to it the way a man who's lived beside a waterfall lies listening to ordinary quiet, and I cried, quietly, into the crook of my arm, because I was so grateful and so ashamed of being grateful that I couldn't tell which feeling was bigger.

*You see?* the voice said, gentle as banked coals. *We are not monsters, Perrin Aldric. We are landlords. And we have one tenant who refuses to pay — a little flame in a bowl, hoarding warmth it stole from the world above. Your friends mean to take it. If they fail, the flame dies anyway, and everything it remembers dies with it — every mother's song, every first snow. If they succeed wrongly, the court falls, and the dark comes up the mountain like water into a lung. There is a third way. There is always a third way. Bring us to the flame before they reach it, and we will let them walk out. All three. Even the priestess, who is carrying more guilt than luggage. Even the mercenary, who swore a god's oath and hates it more each day — yes, we know about Coral Nakamura; we know everything that prays. No one has to be hurt. You just have to open a door.*

"It won't be locked," I said. "Coral doesn't lock doors. Coral says locks just tell people where to look."

*A gate, then,* the voice said, unbothered. *The vault has a gate. Bone answers to us, not to smugglers. One touch from your hand and it opens. That's all. A door, opened by a boy. Children have done worse and been forgiven for less.*

I lay there doing the math again. Three lives against one flame. Except the flame wasn't a life, exactly. Except it dreamed, and I'd felt its dreams, and they were warm the way bread is warm. Except the voice had lied to no one yet, as far as I knew, and Coral lied constantly, charmingly, out of habit, and I trusted Coral anyway.

Except I was eleven years old and I had not slept properly in nine days, and the voice had taken the counting away, and I would have sold my own teeth for ten minutes of that kind of quiet.

I got up. I remember the floor was warm. That's the detail that haunts me — not the bones, not the guards, not Coral's face. The floor was warm, like the whole mountain was a body and I was walking on the skin over its heart, and it felt *good*, and I hated that it felt good, and I walked toward the vault anyway.

* * *

The gate was exactly where the voice said it would be, at the end of a corridor of ribs, and it was beautiful, which I hadn't expected. Not grim. Beautiful — a lattice of interlocked finger-bones and knuckle-joints, fitted so finely you couldn't slip a hair between them, and through the gaps came a light like late afternoon in autumn, gold going amber going ember. The last dragon-fire. Close enough now that I could feel it the way you feel a fire across a room: that faint sweet pressure on your face.

And beside it, in a niche carved like a birdcage, hung the soul.

I'd imagined — I don't know what I'd imagined. Something grand. What hung there was small. A light the size of a plum, flickering off-rhythm, like a candle in a draft, like a heartbeat that couldn't decide. And when I got close it turned toward me — lights shouldn't be able to turn, but it did — and it said, in a voice like a kid much younger than me:

"Brother?"

My chest did something complicated and painful. Because I knew that voice. Not from meeting it. From the mountain's dreams. From four hundred years of the flame's memories pouring through a crack of a boy — and one of those memories, worn smooth from repetition, was of a woman singing to a baby she was about to give away, and the baby's name, and the way the song went up at the end like a question.

Sable's brother's child. The debt. The whole reason we'd come down here, wrapped in secrets Sable thought I didn't know. I know lots of things I'm not supposed to. That's the gift. That's the curse. Same thing, really.

"Brother," the little light said again, hopefully, the way you say it when you've asked a lot of strangers and none of them ever are.

"Not — I'm not—" My hand was already rising toward the gate. I watched it rise like it belonged to somebody else. "I'm sorry," I told the light, and I meant it for both of us, and I touched the bone.

The gate didn't groan or grind. That would have been honest. It simply *unlaced*, finger-bones loosening like hands opening, silent as snowfall, and the warm air of the vault breathed out past me smelling of woodsmoke and, absurdly, of my mother's kitchen, because the court knew exactly what it was doing, it had always known—

"Perrin."

Coral's voice. Behind me. Flat in a way I'd never heard it, flat as ice on a well.

I turned. Coral stood at the mouth of the corridor with a knife already drawn and no expression at all, which on Coral is worse than rage. Sable was behind, one hand pressed to her mouth. And beyond them, filling the corridor like water fills a drain, came the guards — tall, jointed wrong, faces smooth as eggs, moving with the patience of things that have never once needed to hurry.

They hadn't followed us. They'd been *waiting*. For the gate to open. For me.

"No," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "No, wait, the deal was—"

"The deal," Coral said, stepping forward, putting their body between me and the guards the way other people put up umbrellas, casual, automatic, "was that you'd wake me." And then, horribly, gently, over their shoulder, without looking away from the closing line of guards: "Get behind me, sprout. Both of you. We'll talk about this later, when I've decided whether to yell or cry."

There wasn't time for either. The guards moved — fast now, finally fast, the patience spent — and Coral did the thing Coral does, the impossible geometry thing, the thing I'd watched a dozen times on the passes and never gotten tired of: turned a narrow space into an advantage, made three bodies into a wall, made a knife into a sentence with a period at the end of it. Two guards went down. The corridor filled with a sound like wet rope snapping.

Then a third guard reached past Coral's guard — past, through, the way water reaches past a stone — and its hand closed on Coral's arm, and Coral's knife-hand, and then there was a sound I will hear until I die, a sound like a promise being folded up very small, and Coral went down on one knee and did not get up.

"CORAL!" Sable screamed it, and threw something — light, her old rites, whatever she had left — and it flared and guttered, because her god was gone and her rites were ash and the court had known that too.

I stood at the open gate with the little light calling *brother, brother* in a voice going thin with fear, and I did nothing. That's the part I can't forgive, and I've tried, believe me, I've tried every version of it in the dark since. I did nothing. Eleven years old, nine days without sleep, and I stood in the doorway of everything and did nothing while they took Coral, while Coral — pinned, arms wrenched back, blood in their mouth — looked at me.

Not angry. That was the worst possible outcome. I could have survived angry. Coral looked at me with something worse than anger, which was *understanding* — the quick, terrible read of a smuggler assessing damage — and said, around the guard's grip, calm as quoting a price:

"It's all right, sprout."

It was not all right. It was the opposite of all right. But Coral said it the way you say *it's all right* to a child who has just broken something irreplaceable, and the guards dragged them up and carried them away into the dark, and Sable caught my arm hard enough to bruise and pulled me back from the gate, and the little light in its cage was crying — souls can cry, it sounds like a kettle very far away — and the gate laced itself shut behind us, finger by finger, and the voice in my skull said, satisfied as a full belly:

*Thank you, Perrin. You may sleep now.*

And gods help me, I slept. Ten hours, dreamless, the first real rest in nine days, and I woke in a side alcove with Sable watching me from across the space, her brother's death-mask clutched to her chest like a shield, her eyes saying *I know what you did* and also *I know why* and also *neither of those things helps right now.*

"Coral's alive," she said. No comfort in it. Just inventory. "The court doesn't kill what it can trade."

"They said no one would be hurt."

"Yes," Sable said. "They say a great many things. It's how they buy." She looked at me a moment longer, and I waited for her to hate me, and I wanted her to, because hating would have been cleaner than what actually crossed her face, which was recognition — the look of a woman remembering every bargain she'd ever almost made. Then she stood, and slung her pack, and said, "Come on. We're getting them back. And Perrin—" she paused at the alcove's mouth, silhouetted against bone-lattice and ember-light, "—when we find them, you're the one who tells them why. Not me. You."

The mountain, beneath me, resumed its counting. *Eight hundred thousand and twelve,* it whispered, and for once I understood the numbers perfectly, because I could feel every single one of them draining out through the hole I'd opened, and the little light in the vault was still calling *brother* to no one, and somewhere below us Coral Nakamura was in chains because I had wanted to sleep.

I got up. I followed Sable into the dark. And I made myself a promise, there in the bone halls, that I have kept every day since, the way you keep a wound: clean, uncovered, looked at often.

Whatever it costs — a memory, a name, everything — I lead us out. Every wrong turn charged to me. Starting now.
