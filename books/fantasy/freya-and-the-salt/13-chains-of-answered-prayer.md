## 13. Chains of Answered Prayer

The cell they put me in was not a cell, exactly. It was a chapel.

I want to be clear about that, because it matters. It mattered to whoever built it. The room was long and low-ceilinged, carved from the same grey salt-stone as the rest of the undercourt, but someone had gone to the trouble of making it beautiful in the way that only places of worship are beautiful — with intent. Niches lined the walls where candles might have stood. A dais at the far end, worn smooth by knees. And set into the floor at the center, a ring of iron so old it had gone soft-edged, like a wedding band worn down by a lifetime of turning.

My ankle was locked in that ring. The chain was long enough to let me reach every niche, every wall, every corner of the room except the door.

Which meant I could touch everything except the way out. Someone had thought about that too. Someone had thought about it for a very long time.

"They gave you a chapel," Sable said. She'd been given the cell across the corridor — a proper cell, bare and honest — and she stood at my doorway now because the guards had decided we could talk. Or rather, they'd decided we couldn't *not* talk. Everything about this place was designed to let us see just enough of each other to despair properly. "They gave you a chapel and chained you to the altar."

"It's not an altar," I said. "There's no idol. No god's been worshipped here in a thousand years." I ran my thumb along the iron ring. Cold, even for a mountain. Cold the way things are cold when they've never once been warm. "It's just a room shaped like faith."

"Everything down here is," Sable said.

She didn't sit. She never sat when she was working something through; she paced her little cell like a dog circling before lying down, and when she talked to me through the bars she stood with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a door only she could see. Defrocked priestess. Hunted apostate. The order had hired me once to bring her back, years ago, before either of us knew what the other was. Now here we were, both of us caught, both of us chained, and the only thing between us and whatever the court wanted next was the fact that we were apparently more useful as bait than as examples.

Perrin was somewhere else entirely. They'd taken him the moment the gate came down behind us — taken him gently, which was worse, with soft voices and warm hands and a promise that the boy who heard the mountain dreaming would be *so well looked after*. He hadn't cried. That frightened me more than screaming would have. Screaming means a child still believes someone will come.

* * *

They fed us well. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing I should have understood sooner.

Meals arrived on lacquered trays, carried by servants whose faces I couldn't quite hold onto — I'd look away and lose them, the way you lose a dream. Roasted roots with real butter. Bread that steamed. Once, a small cup of honey wine that tasted of summer I'd actually lived through, some orchard near the coast, and I sat there afterward wondering how they'd known, and hating myself for being pleased.

"You notice what they're doing," Sable said on the third day, or what I decided was the third day, since time down here moved like syrup and lied about it constantly.

"I notice," I said.

"Say it anyway."

"They're being kind."

"They're being *precise*." She crouched at my threshold, forearms on her knees. In the corridor's dim light her face was all angles and shadow, the defrocking scar at her temple pale as a fish belly. "Kindness is easy. Any god can do kindness. This is kindness with measurements. Every comfort is calibrated. They're not trying to break us, Coral. Breaking is loud and it ruins the goods. They're trying to *convince* us."

"Convince me of what? I already know why I'm here. Debt's called due. Vess wants its soul delivered. Your court wants the same delivery, just with different paperwork."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the voice of a woman setting down something heavy so she can pick up something heavier: "Coral. What do you think prayer *is*?"

"A request. You ask, the god decides."

"And when the god always decides yes?"

"Then you're lucky."

"Or then you're leashed." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you report weather. "Every answered prayer leaves a mark. A thread. You ask for rain, and the rain comes, and part of you belongs to the hand that sent it. Ask enough times, get enough answers, and eventually you're not a person who prays anymore. You're a puppet who thinks the strings are blessings."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out ugly. "That's heresy. That's the exact heresy your order burned people for saying."

"My order burned people for saying it because it's *true*, Coral." She wasn't angry. That was the terrible part. She was tired in a way that went all the way down past her bones into whatever a person is underneath their bones. "I drowned my god, do you remember me telling you? Walked into Lowmark harbor with my holy symbol and let the sea take it. Everyone thought it was despair. It wasn't. It was surgery. I'd found out what answering costs, and I couldn't carry it anymore."

* * *

The warden came on the fifth day.

I say warden because that was his function, though he dressed like a scholar and spoke like a host. Grey robes, no ornament. A face like everyone's uncle. He unchained my ankle himself — knelt to do it, which I found obscene — and invited me to walk with him, and because the alternative was the chain, I walked.

We went up. That surprised me. I'd assumed the undercourt went only down, that below Thornfell there was nothing but deeper Thornfell, but the corridors climbed and widened until we came out into a gallery overlooking a vast dark space, and I stopped walking, and the warden politely pretended not to notice.

Below us, arranged in terraces like an amphitheater, were flames.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Small ones, mostly — candle-flames, hearth-flames, the blue ghost of a flame burning underwater somehow, the white needle of a flame in a sealed glass jar. Each one sat in its own vessel, each vessel on its own shelf, and each shelf bore a small plaque of bone with words cut into it that I was too far away to read and too afraid to ask about.

"The archive," the warden said, with the mild pride of a man showing off his library. "Every fire ever prayed beside. Every hearth where a child was tucked in. Every candle lit over a sickbed. When mortals pray to fire — warmth, safety, return home — the prayer has to go somewhere. We keep it. We keep *them*. They're quite docile, in captivity."

"That's not possible," I said. "Fire isn't — you can't shelve a hearth."

"No?" He gestured, and one of the thousands of small flames leaned toward him, the way a plant leans toward sun. "You've spent twenty years leaving offerings at shrines to a god who never answered. Tell me, mercenary. Did you ever wonder where the offerings *went*?"

And there it was. The question I'd spent two decades not asking, laid out on a tray beside the honey wine.

"I wondered," I admitted.

"You *hoped*," he corrected, gently, horribly. "Hoping is what we harvest. Come. There's someone who's been asking to meet you."

* * *

He took me down again, deeper, through halls where the walls were veined with threads of gold so fine they might have been hair, and into a round chamber with a single chair and a single table, and on the table, a bowl of bone.

I knew the bowl. I'd seen it in dreams I'd told no one about, dreams that had started the week Vess woke and spoke its first word in twenty years. Inside the bowl burned the last dragon-fire — smaller than I'd imagined, no bigger than a fist, guttering like a lamp left too long, and around its base the bone was scorched black and crumbling.

But it wasn't the fire that made my stomach drop.

It was the light beside it. A second flame, tiny, trembling, sitting in a dish of salt. It flickered in a rhythm I recognized without wanting to: quick-quick-slow, quick-quick-slow. Like breathing. Like a sleeping child's breathing.

"There it is," the warden said fondly. "Forty years in our keeping. Do you know what it is?"

"A soul," I said. "A child's soul. Sold. I know the terms. Vess told me."

"Vess told you a version." He drew out the chair for me. I stayed standing. He sat instead, folding his hands, patient as stone. "Sit or don't, it makes no difference. Here is the truth, since you've earned it by coming all this way. Forty years ago, a desperate woman sold her unborn brother's child to this court — no. Forgive me. The details are tangled even for us. A child was sold. The payment was protection. The court held the soul here, beside the dragon-fire, because souls kept near the last true flame don't fade. They *wait*. And a waiting soul, mercenary, is the most valuable object in any world. Because a waiting soul can be *given a purpose*."

"What purpose?"

"This one." He tapped the bowl of bone, once, lightly, the way you'd tap a keg. "The dragon-fire is dying. Not naturally — fires don't die naturally, they're *put out*, and ours has been smothered for centuries by neglect, by theft, by gods drinking deep of it and giving back ash. When it dies, everything dies with it that depends on it. Which is everything, though your surface theologians have gotten very good at not knowing that. So the court needed a keeper. A soul bound to the flame, feeding it, tending it, holding the last ember alive against all sense. And the perfect keeper is a soul that was *sold* — because a sold soul already knows how to belong to something that doesn't love it back."

The child-breathing rhythm of the little light stuttered. Or I imagined it did. I couldn't tell anymore what was real down here and what was staging.

"So deliver it," I said. My voice sounded fine. That felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside me. "Vess's debt. Bring the soul, hand it over, done. Why am I in a chapel instead of a grave?"

"Because Vess," the warden said, and here his mildness finally cracked, just at the edge, like ice over moving water, "does not intend to deliver anything. Vess intends to *consume*. The soul, the flame, all of it — swallowed whole, so that when every other fire in the world gutters out, one god remains, fed on the last true warmth, the only answer left to any prayer anywhere. Yours included. *Especially* yours." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has finally gotten to the interesting part of the lecture. "Twenty years of silence, mercenary. Twenty years of unanswered prayers at those shrines. You thought it was abandonment. It was *fasting*. Vess starved you on purpose. A devotee who has been denied for twenty years answers the first call like a starving dog answers a whistle. You were never being tested for faith. You were being *ripened*."

* * *

They put me back in the chapel after that. They didn't rechain me. They didn't need to.

I sat on the dais — the worn-smooth dais, knee-polished by dead worshippers — and I thought about twenty years of offerings.

Salt, always salt, because Vess had come out of the sea in the oldest stories. Blood, once a year, from the left palm, because the old rites said the left hand was the hand that holds what you can't put down. And prayers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Prayers for safe passage through the passes, for clients' lives, for rain on the lowland farms, for — I'll say it now, since shame is the one coin I have left to spend — prayers that Vess would simply speak to me. Anything. One word. A sign. Proof I hadn't spent my whole life talking to weather.

And Vess had said nothing. Twenty years. I'd built a whole personality around that silence. Weary, wry, faithful-anyway Coral, the mercenary who keeps paying tribute to an empty shrine because that's what devotion *is*, isn't it, paying when there's no return. I'd made my silence into a virtue. I'd made it into *myself*.

And it had been farming.

Every unanswered prayer had been a hook set in me. Every year of silence had been the line pulled tighter, not looser. Vess hadn't abandoned me; Vess had been *curing* me, the way you cure meat — hanging me in darkness and dry air until I was exactly the thing it needed: a guide who knew every hidden way through Thornfell, hardened by longing, primed to come running the instant the whistle blew. The debt was never a debt. There was no debt. There was a trap with a forty-year fuse, and I had walked into it carrying the torch myself, grateful for the light.

I laughed again, alone in the chapel, and it echoed wrong in all those empty niches.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about losing your faith: it doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like amputation. Twenty years of habit doesn't vanish; it stands there in the room with you, phantom-limbed, reaching for offerings I no longer had words for. My hands kept making the shape of prayer. I kept catching them doing it and forcing them flat against my knees, and they'd drift back up, patient, faithful hands, hands that had never once been answered and loved anyway, and I wanted to scream at them, *don't you understand, we were livestock, we were the crop—*

Somewhere beyond the door, faint as a memory of a memory, I heard singing. A child's voice. Perrin, or the soul-child, or the mountain itself, I couldn't tell, and maybe down here there was no difference. Quick-quick-slow. Quick-quick-slow.

And beneath the singing, closer, inside the stone, inside my own chest where twenty years of devotion had hollowed out a space exactly god-shaped:

*You are angry,* said Vess, gentle as ever, gentle as the day it fell silent. *Good. Anger is also an offering.*

I pressed my palms flat against the salt floor and did not answer.

Because that was the one prayer I had left, and I meant it with everything the trap hadn't managed to take: *no.* Not yes. Not silence. *No.*

And for the first time in twenty years, something in the dark listened — and did not like what it heard.

Down the corridor, iron scraped. Footsteps. Sable's voice, low and urgent, arguing with a guard — and under it, another sound I knew from a hundred smuggler camps: the particular whisper of a lock being picked by someone who'd learned it young and desperate.

Someone was coming to my door.
