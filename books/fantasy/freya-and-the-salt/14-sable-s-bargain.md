## 14. Sable's Bargain

I had spent eleven years telling myself that grief was a discipline. That if you folded it small enough — morning prayers, evening prayers, the little liturgies of washing and eating and not weeping — it would fit inside you without changing your shape.

Eleven years of bad theology, as it turned out.

The bone halls of the undercourt did not echo so much as *absorb*. Every footstep I took was swallowed whole, and the silence that followed felt less like absence than like attention. The mountain was listening. The mountain had always been listening; that was rather the point of gods, wasn't it? They listened, and then they invoiced you.

Coral was in a cell somewhere below me, chained to answered prayer — the court's own words, delivered by a guard whose face kept forgetting itself between one blink and the next. Perrin sat beside me on a ledge of black stone, knees drawn up, humming the way he did when the mountain dreamed too loudly. And in my hands, wrapped in a scrap of altar-cloth I'd carried for eleven years like a tumor I couldn't bring myself to cut out, was my brother's death-mask.

*You will have to give it up,* I told myself. *That is the entire shape of the thing.*

I am, by training and temperament, very good at knowing the shape of things and very bad at touching them.

* * *

Let me be precise about what a death-mask is, because the word does it no favors. It is not a likeness of the dead. It is what remains of them after the fire has taken everything flammable — the part that loved, pressed into clay and fired until it could hold nothing else. My order made them for every priest who died in service. We wore them on our sleeves at the funeral. We spoke into them once, formally, telling the dead whatever we had failed to tell them alive, and then we sealed them in the reliquary and never spoke of them again.

My brother's mask had never been sealed. Because my brother had never been a priest, and because his death had never been admitted to be a death at all.

Here is the confession I had been carrying up the mountain like ballast: forty years ago — well, thirty-nine; I was seven and I counted — my brother Tammas sold something to the court beneath Thornfell. Not gold. Not his voice, though the court would have taken that gladly. He sold a soul. His own unborn child's, traded before the child even existed, to a court that deals in futures the way other markets deal in grain. In exchange, the court paid our family's debts, healed our mother's lungs, and looked away from the small heresies that let our village keep its shrine open through a hard winter.

Tammas told me this the night before he walked into the mountain for the last time. He was laughing when he said it. That was the worst part. He thought he'd been clever. He thought a debt owed against a child who might never be born was a debt that could never come due.

Then I was born two years later, and the court adjusted its ledger, because courts do, and the child it claimed was me — except it wasn't, because my mother bargained, and mothers bargain beautifully, and the debt slid sideways onto a nephew born three years after that, a boy with Tammas's laugh who lived four days and died with a name no priest would speak, because speaking it would mark him as owned.

The court took him anyway. Souls don't need bodies. They just need to be *owed*.

And the flame they keep him beside — the last dragon-fire, the one guttering toward dark — has been keeping him warm ever since. Or so Perrin hears in the mountain's dreams. A small light that calls him brother. I heard Perrin say it and I had to walk to the edge of the passage and be sick over the dark, quietly, like a lady.

So now you know why I defrocked myself. Not doubt. Bookkeeping.

* * *

The envoy found us on the third landing above the cells, which I resented, because I had been trying to find *it* for two days and it had simply strolled up wearing my mother's face again, patient as weather.

"You're going down there," it said, in my mother's voice, with my mother's little crooked smile, the one she saved for moments when she was about to say something devastating and wanted to soften the blow. "To trade. I can smell the relic on you."

"Then you know what I want."

"Coral Kavanagh, released. Unchained, unmarked, unclaimed." The envoy tilted its head, and my mother's face did something my mother's face had never done — it *waited*, hungrily, the way creditors wait. "And all it costs you is the last piece of your brother you have left. You see how clean that is? The court admires symmetry."

"The court," I said, "would admire a knife going into someone's back, if the angle were elegant enough."

"Yes," it agreed pleasantly. "We would."

Perrin tugged my sleeve. "Sable." His voice was very small. "It's lying about something. The mountain says its mouth is full of hinges."

"I know, love."

"It's not lying about Coral, though. That part's true. It just wants you to think the true part is the whole price."

I looked at the boy — Lord Aldric's runaway son, ten years old, who navigated hell by listening to it snore — and felt the specific vertigo of realizing a child understands the negotiation better than you do.

"What's the rest of the price?" I asked the envoy.

"There isn't one," it said, in my mother's voice, and for half a heartbeat the face flickered — my mother, then something older and smoother beneath, like a stone under river water — and Perrin flinched hard enough that I grabbed his hand.

"There's always one," I said. "Your whole economy runs on it. But here's what you've misunderstood about me, and I want you to carry this back to whoever wears your face when it's not wearing hers: I'm not trading the mask to save Coral. I'm trading it because it was never mine to keep."

The envoy went still. Even the mountain seemed to lean closer, or perhaps that was Perrin pressing against my side.

"My order taught that relics are held in trust. That grief hoarded is theft from the dead." I unwound the altar-cloth as I spoke, slowly, because my hands were shaking and I wanted the shaking to look deliberate. "I have been committing theft for eleven years. This ends today. On my terms, not yours."

"And your terms are?"

"Coral walks free. The chains come off in front of me. And you tell me where the vault is — the real vault, not the one you showed the smuggler in your little theater of guards."

Something moved behind my mother's eyes. Calculation, or appetite; in the undercourt the two were probably the same organ.

"You drive a hard bargain for a woman with nothing left," it said.

"I have everything left," I said. "That's what none of you understand. You deal in what people own. I'm spending what I *owe*."

* * *

They brought Coral up in manacles of woven light — prayer-stuff, actual solidified devotion, which is the most obscene material I have ever seen used as hardware. Coral looked terrible. Coral also looked, in the particular way of mercenaries confronting their own mortality, extremely annoyed about it.

"Sable." Coral's voice came out scraped. "Tell me you didn't—"

"Trade the mask? I did. Don't make it a scene, you're not worth a scene."

"That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me in twenty years."

The guard unlocked the manacles with a gesture, and the woven light sloughed off Coral's wrists like snow off a branch, and I watched a person become un-owned in real time. It's an odd thing to witness. There's a moment — I'd seen it once before, at a manumission ceremony back when I still believed in ceremonies — where the freed party sways slightly, as if the world briefly weighs less than expected.

Coral swayed. Caught themselves on the wall. Looked at me.

"The mask," Coral said carefully. "What was it, really?"

"A death-mask. My brother's."

"And you gave it up. For me." Coral said it the way you'd say *and you set fire to your own house*. "Why?"

Because guilt is a debt and penance is payment and I have spent eleven years defaulting. Because you are a bad, stubborn, oath-bound fool who broke your vow for a child you'd known three weeks. Because somebody in this story has to do the arithmetic honestly.

"Because it was heavy," I said, "and I'm tired."

Which was true, and insufficient, and Coral knew it, and mercifully didn't press, because the mountain chose that moment to shudder.

* * *

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes are indifferent. This shudder had *intent*. The walls of the corridor flexed like the flank of a sleeping animal disturbed by a fly, and somewhere far below us — below the cells, below the bazaar where names are currency, below everything — something vast exhaled heat that rose up through the stone and touched my face like a hand.

Perrin gasped. "It's waking up. The fire — Sable, the fire heard you."

"Heard me what? I haven't prayed since Lowmark."

"You don't have to pray anymore." The boy's eyes were enormous. "Don't you get it? Everything's answering now. That's the whole problem. The mountain heard you give up the mask and it—" he pressed both hands flat against the wall, listening with his palms, "—it says *witness*. It keeps saying *witness*. What does that mean?"

I didn't answer, because I was busy having the floor drop out of my understanding of the universe.

Here is what my order taught, in the last doctrine I ever memorized before I burned my vestments: the old gods are hungry. All of them. Hunger is the engine of divinity; worship is the meal; prayer is the fork. We are eaten politely, and call it grace.

But the fire under the mountain wasn't saying *feed me*. It wasn't saying *worship* or *obey* or any of the thousand imperatives gods use to dress up appetite.

It was saying *witness*.

As in: *see me*. As in: *remember what I remember*. As in the oldest, most desperate request anything living can make of anything else.

"Oh," I said aloud, stupidly, to a corridor. "Oh, you *idiot*, Vess. You absolute fraud. You've been starving at a banquet."

Coral stared at me. "Care to share with the class?"

"Not yet. I need to be wrong first, properly, before I say it out loud." I was already moving, pulling Perrin along, heading down — down, toward the heat, toward the vault where a bowl of bone held the last dragon-fire and a small stolen light that called my dead brother's son *brother*. "But if I'm right, then everything the court told us about the debt was true and useless, and everything Vess told us was a lie with excellent posture, and the only thing that can rekindle that flame is—"

The mountain shuddered again, harder, and this time the heat that rose through the stone carried something with it. A sound. Thin, high, unmistakable.

A child, singing. Badly. With my brother's exact laugh caught in the middle of it, the way a laugh gets caught when the singer knows they're terrible and doesn't care.

Perrin stopped dead. "That's her," he whispered. "That's the little light. She's — Sable, she's *singing*. She hasn't sung since they put her in the dark."

I stood in a corridor of black stone in the guts of a dying god's mountain, holding empty hands where eleven years of grief used to be, and discovered that the space grief leaves behind is not emptiness at all. It's room. Room enough, suddenly, terrifyingly, for hope.

"Well," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because I was done performing composure for audiences of stone. "Then we'd better go meet my nephew's daughter before the world ends. Coral — you still remember how to run?"

Coral was already running.
