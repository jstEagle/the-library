## 15. The Long Way Through the Mountain's Dream

Here is what nobody tells you about betraying your friends: the guilt doesn't wait for a quiet moment. It doesn't knock. It moves into your chest like a relative with no other prospects and starts rearranging the furniture.

I had unlocked the gate maybe an hour ago. Maybe two. Time down here was a rumor — the passages didn't have sunrises, just a slow amber gloom that came from nowhere and went back to it when it felt like it. We'd been running since Sable got Coral out of the bone cells, running through corridors that were supposed to be behind us and weren't, and I was at the front because I was the one who could hear where the mountain wanted us to go.

Except now the mountain wasn't talking to me.

That was the thing. That was the whole horrible thing. For as long as I could remember, I'd heard it under everything — the dreaming, the counting, that vast slow voice like a grandmother murmuring in another room. In my father's house in Alderholt I'd thought I was mad. On the road I'd learned I was a conduit, which is a nicer word for the same condition. And in the court beneath Thornfell, when the guards had come for me with their offer — *no more nightmares, little listener, no more voices, just sleep* — I had believed them for exactly as long as it took to turn the key.

The moment the lock clicked, the dream had gone silent. Not quiet. Silent. The difference between someone leaving a room and someone deciding you don't exist.

"Perrin." Coral's voice, low, from behind me. "You've stopped walking."

"I'm listening."

"You've been listening for ten minutes and we haven't moved."

"Maybe I'm being thorough," I said, which would have landed better if my voice hadn't cracked on *thorough*.

Coral came up beside me. They didn't say anything else. They'd barely said anything to me since the cell block, and honestly I'd have preferred shouting. Shouting means someone still thinks you're worth the breath. What Coral gave me instead was this: they looked at me the way you look at a bridge you're not sure will hold, and then they looked past me, down the passage, and waited.

Sable caught up, her brother's death-mask wrapped in cloth against her chest like a sleeping cat. "The walls are wrong," she said. "They've changed since we passed."

"They do that," Coral said flatly.

"No, I mean they've changed *badly*. The carvings are facing inward now. Everything in this mountain is turning its face away from something." She paused. "Or toward it."

I knew what they were turning away from. Me. The whole mountain had felt me put the key in the lock, and the whole mountain had drawn a breath it hadn't let out yet.

*All right,* I thought. *Fine.* If the mountain wouldn't come to me —

"I need to stop," I said. "Not rest-stop. Stop-stop."

Coral's jaw tightened. "We don't have time for—"

"We don't have time for me to keep guessing either." My hands were shaking and I made fists of them so nobody would see, which was pointless, because everyone saw. "It won't talk to me. Since the gate. Since I—" I couldn't finish it. Saying *betrayed you all* out loud felt like swallowing a stone from the riverbed, all edges. "I have to apologize. To it. Directly."

Sable blinked. "To the mountain."

"To the mountain."

"And how exactly does one apologize to a mountain?" Coral asked, and there it was — not warmth, exactly, but the dry edge of a real question, which from Coral was practically a hug.

"I lie down," I said. "In the dream. The way you'd kneel in a temple, except the temple is everything."

* * *

We found a side chamber — or it found us; down here I'd stopped believing in the difference — a hollow off the main passage where the walls were smooth and warm, veined with threads of dim gold light like marrow showing through skin. The last dragon-fire's light, maybe, reaching even here. Or the mountain's own blood. I didn't ask. Asking questions of this place felt rude lately.

I lay down on my back in the center of the chamber. The stone was warmer than it should have been, like a bed someone had just left.

"What do we do?" Sable whispered.

"Nothing," Coral said. "Guard him. If anything comes out of the walls that isn't Perrin, kill it."

"That's very reassuring."

"It's not meant to be reassuring. It's meant to be accurate."

I closed my eyes and did what I'd been doing my whole life without knowing it was a skill: I fell sideways into the dream.

* * *

The inside of the mountain's dreaming is not a place. I want to be clear about that, because every story you've ever heard about the underworld makes it a place — halls, rivers, thrones, a ferryman with a boat and a bad attitude. It isn't. It's more like standing inside a memory that belongs to someone enormous. The sky was salt-white and slow. The ground was thought. Shapes moved in the distance the way shapes move in the corner of your eye: a village of hearth-ghosts tending fires that burned without fuel, a bazaar dissolving into arithmetic, a small bright mote of light that might have been a soul and might have been a firefly and might have been Freya, and I ached toward it before I could stop myself.

And everywhere, the silence. The great murmuring voice I'd heard my whole life was holding itself closed like a fist.

I walked — or did whatever the equivalent of walking is when the ground is thought — toward the center of it, toward the heaviest part of the silence, and I knelt.

"I'm sorry," I said.

The silence didn't move.

"I know that's not enough. I know 'sorry' is the cheapest coin there is. Believe me, I've done a lot of shopping down here lately, I know the exchange rates." My voice echoed strangely, coming back to me from directions I hadn't spoken in. "But I'm going to say it anyway, because it's true, and because I don't know what else to bring."

Still nothing. Somewhere far off, the little light flickered, and I felt it flicker in my own ribs.

"I turned the key," I said. "They told me they'd make the nightmares stop. Do you know what my nightmares are? They're this. They're you. Every night since I was six, I've dreamed of the counting — the voices under the floor counting down, getting closer, and I never knew what they were counting down *to*, and it turns out it was this, it was the fire going out, it was everything ending, and I was so tired of hearing it that when someone offered me a door out of my own head, I took it." I pressed my forehead to the thought-ground. "I didn't think about Coral. I didn't think about Sable. I didn't think about the child whose soul they're keeping next to a dying flame like a lamp-oil reserve. I thought about *me*, and I opened the door, and guards came through it, and Coral got chained because I couldn't stand one more night of being afraid."

The silence stretched. It had texture now, the way a held breath has texture.

Then, finally, the mountain spoke.

*Little listener.*

Its voice didn't come through my ears. It came up through the ground of the dream, through my knees, through my teeth. It sounded less like thunder than I'd expected and more like my grandmother, if my grandmother had been four hundred miles wide and mildly disappointed.

*You are sorry.*

"Yes."

*You are also sorry in the way children are sorry, which is to say: sorry that it happened, sorry that you were caught in it, sorry that the feeling of having done it sits in you like a swallowed stone. This is not nothing. But it is not yet the same as being trustworthy.*

I flinched. "I know."

*Do you?* Something shifted in the white distance — a shape too large to look at directly, the way you can't look directly at your own life. *Understand what you are asking. You want to lead us again. You want me to open the dream-paths for you, the quick ways, the ways that shift and fold, the only roads left now that the court has sealed the honest ones. But every path you choose, you choose with the same self that chose the key.*

"I know."

*So there is a price. There is always a price; the court below learned pricing from me, though they perverted it — they buy and sell, and I only weigh. You may lead. But each time you take a wrong turn — each time fear chooses for you instead of care — I will take from you a memory of home.*

My stomach dropped through the floor of the dream, which, being made of thought, offered no resistance whatsoever.

"A memory of home," I repeated. "Of Alderholt."

*Of home,* the mountain said, and I understood with cold clarity that it did not mean my father's drafty house and my father's cold table. It meant home. All of it. The smell of the kitchen garden after rain. My mother's hands — her actual hands, the specific way her thumb bent back too far, the way she'd taught me to whistle through a blade of grass. Home was not an address. Home was everything I carried that made me someone who could be missed.

"How many wrong turns are there likely to be?" I asked, trying for wry and landing somewhere near pleading.

*More than you hope,* said the mountain, with what I swear was gentle humor. *Fewer than you fear. Choose carefully, little listener. Care is the only compass I honor.*

"And if I run out of memories?"

*Then you will walk out of this mountain free and safe and alive,* it said, *and you will not know why the other two look at you the way they do. You will be a stranger who survived. Some people call that mercy.*

I thought about Coral pawning their mother's face in the bazaar. Coming back lighter and wrong. I thought about how they'd looked at me in the corridor since the cell block — like a bridge that might hold — and I realized the mountain was offering me the same trade Coral had already made, and that neither of us had been given a choice about whether the price was fair, only about whether we'd pay it.

"Yes," I said. "I accept. Lead-and-pay. Let's go."

*One more thing.* The voice softened, and the whole white dream seemed to lean closer, the way a big animal leans over a small hurt one. *The key you turned opened more than a gate. When you let the guards through, you let them taste your fear, and fear is a scent they can follow. There is one who follows it now. Older than the court. Hungrier than the court. It wears the shape of an answer to prayer, and it is coming for the flame, and it will reach you before you reach the vault unless you run the dream-paths faster than you have ever run anything.*

"Vess," I said.

*Vess,* the mountain agreed, and for the first time I heard something in its vast voice that I had never heard there before, and it took me a moment to name it, because I had only ever heard it in much smaller creatures.

Fear.

*Wake up, little listener. Wake up and lead.*

* * *

I came back to my body all at once, gasping, and Coral's knife was already out and leveled at my face, which — fair.

"It's me," I croaked.

"Prove it."

"The night before we left Lowmark you sat up talking in your sleep and you said, quote, 'the toll is paid in salt, you absolute turnip,' and then you went back to snoring."

Coral lowered the knife with the expression of someone who has been betrayed by their own unconscious and intends to file a complaint. "That stays between us."

"There are no secrets underground," Sable said solemnly, and then ruined it by grinning.

I sat up. The chamber was the same; I was not. Something in me had been weighed, and I could feel the weight sitting in me like ballast — steadier, heavier, honest. I stood, rolled my shoulders, and faced the passage.

"It'll talk to me again," I said. "Conditionally. I lead, but every wrong turn costs me a memory of home. That's the deal."

Sable's face did something complicated. "Perrin, that's — that's a terrible bargain."

"It's an honest one," I said. "Which around here makes it practically charitable." I started walking, and the walls leaned in, and the amber gloom ahead of us folded open like a hand. "Also, small update, and I want everyone to stay calm: Vess is following us. Right now. Through my fear. So we're going to run the dream-paths faster than anything has ever run them, and I am going to try very hard not to be afraid, and if I suddenly forget my mother's name, somebody remind me gently."

Behind me, Coral swore in three languages, one of which I was fairly sure was smugglers' cant and one of which might have been prayer.

Ahead, the mountain opened the first door, and I stepped through, and I did not look back to check whether it closed on something with wings.
