## 16. Vess Unmasked

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a god does something unforgivable.

I'd heard it once before, twenty years ago, the night Vess stopped answering. I'd knelt at a shrine with my palms bleeding from the tithe-cuts and felt the silence settle over Thornfell like snowfall — total, patient, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. I'd spent two decades telling myself that silence was absence.

It was not absence. It was a held breath.

We were three days out of the bone halls, running through passages that Perrin swore were dreaming us forward, when the silence came back. Sable felt it first. She stopped mid-stride with her hand raised, and Perrin walked straight into her back, and neither of them apologized, which told me everything before I understood any of it.

"The air's gone thick," Sable said.

"That's not air." I looked up. The passage ceiling was black salt, veined with something that might have been quartz or might have been veins. "That's attention."

Perrin pressed his palm flat against the wall. His face did the thing it always did when he listened to the mountain — eyes unfocused, mouth slightly open, like a boy hearing music through a floor. But this time his expression curdled.

"It's not the mountain," he whispered. "It's *in* the mountain. It's been walking beside us since the bazaar."

"How long have you known?" Sable asked me.

"I didn't." And that was the truth, though not the whole truth. The whole truth was that I'd felt lighter ever since I'd pawned my mother's memory — lighter and wrong, as Sable had said — and some mercenary instinct buried under all that wrongness had been screaming at me for three days that we were being herded. Hidden ways, dream-paths, shortcuts through the dark. Every route we'd taken had been *given* to us. I'd been so proud of navigating by Perrin's dreams that I hadn't asked who else could read them.

"Keep moving," I said. "If it wants to talk, it can talk while we walk."

Gods, it turns out, do not care for being made to walk.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into a cavern the size of a cathedral, and I mean that literally, because I've seen cathedrals and this one had clearly been copied from one — pillars of salt carved into saints, a vaulted dark overhead, even the suggestion of pews in rows of stone. Except every saint's face had been worn smooth, and the pews were cracked down the middle, and at the far end where an altar should have stood there was instead a shallow bowl of black stone, empty, waiting, shaped exactly like the one that held the last dragon-fire.

Vess was already seated on the altar steps.

I want to tell you it looked like something monstrous. It would be easier if it had. But Vess wore the shape it had always worn in my prayers: a tall figure in a traveler's cloak, hood back, face like weather — changeable, gray, impossible to hold in memory for more than a moment. It looked like every road I'd ever walked. It looked like home, if home had been a direction instead of a place.

"Coral," it said, and my name in its mouth sounded like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten I carried.

"Vess." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You've put on weight. Twenty years of silence, I figured you'd wasted away."

Sable made a small sound that might have been a prayer or a laugh strangled at birth. Perrin grabbed the back of my coat, whether to hold me back or hide behind me, I couldn't tell.

"You are angry," Vess said. It sounded genuinely puzzled, which was somehow worse than anger. "I don't understand angry. I gave you purpose. I gave you roads. Every smuggler you ever guided safely through Thornfell — that was my breath on your neck, my hand on your compass. You called it skill."

"It *was* skill."

"It was obedience wearing skill's clothes." Vess rose, and the cavern changed around it — the shadows leaning, the salt saints tilting their smooth faces toward us like a congregation craning to see. "But you're right to be angry, so let us stop pretending. You want the truth? Here is the truth, unmasked, as they say."

It spread its hands, and the traveler's shape began to come apart — not violently, but the way fog comes apart when the sun insists. Underneath there was no horror. There was only *hunger*, given a silhouette. A hunger so old and so vast that my knees tried to buckle just looking at it, and I have stared down avalanches.

"Every god you have ever prayed to," Vess said, "answers prayers for the same reason a fisherman baits a hook. An answered prayer is a debt. A debt is a leash. The old ones — the fire-drakes, the salt-mothers, the dreamers under the stone — they never learned this. They answered out of *love*, the fools, freely, asking nothing, and so they built nothing. No congregations. No chains. No power that endures." Its voice dropped, and the whole cavern dropped with it. "I learned. I let myself go silent for twenty years because silence makes mortals desperate, and desperate mortals overpay. Do you know what happens when the last dragon-fire dies, Coral?"

"Everyone says the world goes cold."

"Everyone says that. Everyone is wrong, because everyone repeats what priests tell them, and priests repeat what gods pay them to say." Vess smiled, and its smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. "When the fire dies, the old covenant dies with it. Every soul those foolish gods ever blessed — freely, without contract — becomes *unclaimed*. Orphaned grace, drifting loose. And I will gather it. All of it. Every miracle ever given away for free, I will call home, and I will be the only answer left in the world. Every prayer in every tongue will land on my doorstep, and every mortal who prays will owe me, forever, and their children after them."

The child's soul, I thought. The small flickering light in the vault that had called Perrin *brother*. "And the keeper-soul," I said. "The child sold to the court forty years ago. Where does it fit?"

"It *is* the fit." Vess stepped closer, and each step made the floor ring like a struck bell. "The dragon-fire needs a keeper — a soul bound to tend it, feed it, love it, or it gutters. That child has kept the flame alive for forty years on scraps of stolen kindness. When I consume the flame, I consume the keeper. Two debts settled with one swallow. Your oath, Coral — your precious oath, sworn at fifteen, blood on the shrine-stone — requires you to deliver that soul to me. You have delivered it. It waits in a bowl of bone not half a day's run from here. All you must do now is stand aside."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you break your oath." It said it gently, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "And no mortal has ever survived unbinding. The oath is woven into you, Coral. Into your name, your luck, your lungs. Break it, and the weaving pulls tight. I have watched men try. They do not die quickly and they do not die whole."

The silence after that was the cathedral kind. Even the mountain seemed to lean in and listen.

I thought about my mother singing. I couldn't remember it — I'd sold that memory in the bazaar for a handful of names, and the hole where it used to be ached like a pulled tooth. I thought about how I'd spent twenty years praying to this thing at roadside shrines, hating myself for it, unable to stop, and how the whole time it had been counting. Tallying my devotion like a moneylender. I thought about Tam, the young client who'd asked me why I bothered paying tribute to a god that never answered, and how I'd deflected, and how the honest answer — the answer I hadn't even admitted to myself — was *because I was afraid of what I owed.*

Well. Now I knew what I owed. And now I knew who was holding the ledger.

"Coral," Sable said quietly, at my shoulder. "Don't. Whatever you're about to do — think first. Please. I've watched a person defy a god before." Her voice caught. "It was my brother. It took him forty years to die of it."

"I'm not going to defy Vess," I said.

Relief flickered across Vess's weather-face, quick and greedy, and I confess I savored it.

"I'm going to do something much ruder." I turned to face it fully, and I made myself look at the hunger underneath, because you should always look at the thing you're refusing. "Twenty years ago I swore you an oath. Blood on the shrine-stone. My service, my roads, my tithe, and one delivery besides — the soul of a child, whenever you called it due. I meant it. I was fifteen and frightened and you were the only voice that had ever answered me, and I mistook being answered for being loved."

"Do not—"

"I'm talking." And here is the strange thing, the thing I still can't fully explain: it stopped. A god, stopped by a mercenary raising one hand. Perhaps it was surprise. Perhaps it was the mountain holding its breath so hard that even Vess had to wait. "An oath is a bargain. Both sides keep their terms or both sides are freed. You went silent for twenty years. You broke faith first — you left me kneeling at cold shrines with bleeding hands, begging, and you counted my desperation as interest. So the terms are already broken. From your side. Which means—"

"Which means nothing," Vess snarled, and the cathedral shook, and salt dust fell like gray snow. "The oath lives in *you*, not in me. Renounce it and the weaving pulls. Renounce it and I will watch you unravel, thread by thread, and then I will take the child's soul anyway, and the boy's dreams, and the priestess's relic, and everything you have built on this road, because you are *mine*, Coral Nakamura, you have been mine since you were fifteen years old and you will die mine—"

"I renounce it."

Three words. I'd rehearsed harder sentences to customs officers.

The world did exactly what Vess promised it would. The weaving pulled.

I won't lie to you about what it felt like, because lying is for smugglers dealing with clients, and you deserve better. It felt like being unmade. It felt like every promise I'd ever kept reaching down my throat to take back the shape they'd given me. My name went first — I felt it lift out of me like heat off a stone, *Coral Nakamura*, gone, and with it went my luck, the small bright coin-flips that had saved my life a hundred times in the passes. Then the deeper threads: the sureness of my feet on mountain paths, the instinct that had kept caravans alive, the very sense of who I was when nobody was watching. The oath had been the skeleton of me. I felt it being drawn out through my skin, slow as a splinter the length of a life.

I stayed standing. I don't know why. Stubbornness, probably. Stubbornness is the one material I've never run short of.

Somewhere very far away, Sable was saying words in an old liturgical tongue — not a prayer, I realized dimly, a *counting*, the rhythm priests use to time a dying heart. Somewhere closer, Perrin had his arms around my waist, holding me to the world like a child anchoring a kite in wind. And somewhere beneath everything, so deep I almost missed it, the mountain was *humming*.

Not Vess's bell-note. Something older. A low, warm tone, like a hearth heard through a wall, like a voice humming a lullaby it half-remembered. The humming rose up through the salt and the stone and into my emptied-out body, and where the oath-threads had been torn free, it filled the gaps — not replacing them, not binding me, just *filling* me, the way light fills a room without asking anything of the walls.

Vess felt it too. I watched its weather-face go still.

"What," it said slowly, "is that."

"That," said a voice from the tunnel mouth behind us — Perrin's voice, but pitched strange, doubled, the way it sounded when the mountain spoke through him — "is what you never learned, little hunger. That is what fire remembers."

Vess turned toward the tunnel. And for the first time in twenty years of silence and one week of answers, I saw something on a god's face that looked almost like fear.

Because down that passage, faint but unmistakable, carried on air that shouldn't have moved underground — I could smell smoke. Not the cold reek of the bone halls. Warm smoke. Pine and resin and the inside-of-a-hearth smell of a fire that has decided, against all odds and all debts, to keep burning.

The last dragon-fire had felt its keeper's freedom ripple through the stone. And it was coming to see.
