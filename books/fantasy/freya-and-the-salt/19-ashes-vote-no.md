## 19. Ashes Vote No

Here is the thing nobody tells you about the end of the world: it's mostly waiting.

Vess hung above the vault like a storm that had learned to want things, and Coral stood in front of it with their name held out in both hands — which is not a metaphor, by the way. I could *see* it. Names look different when they're being given away. They go bright, and then they go thin, like a candle flame deciding whether to be a candle or a memory.

I was crouched behind a pillar of bone with Sable, holding a dead man's mask against my chest, and I was terrified. Not of Vess. Of what I'd done. The gate I'd unlocked was still open behind us somewhere, back down the dream-passage, and every guard who had come through it had come through because of me.

"Perrin." Sable's voice was very quiet. "The mountain is listening to you right now. Do you feel it?"

I did. That was the horrible part. Ever since I'd apologized to the mountain — since it had forgiven me *conditionally*, the way adults forgive you, which means not at all until you've paid — I could feel it the way you feel a hand resting on the back of your neck. It had been waiting for me to ask for something. I hadn't asked for anything yet, because asking felt like spending.

But Coral was standing under a god made of hunger, offering up the only thing they had left, and Vess was hesitating, and hesitation doesn't last forever. Nothing lasts forever. Except maybe salt.

"Sable," I said. "If I call them — all of them, everyone the court ever bought — will they come?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes were wet and furious and certain, all three at once, which I have since learned is just what her face does.

"They were never sold," she said. "They were *lent*. There's a difference, and the difference has teeth."

* * *

Let me back up, because I'm getting ahead of myself, and if there's one thing I've learned from Coral it's that you tell a story in order or you lose your audience.

When Vess came down into the vault, the whole mountain changed key. You know how a room feels when someone important walks in? Multiply that by a mountain. Multiply it by twenty years of silence finally breaking its fast. The walls stopped being walls and started being *attention*.

And everything the attention touched woke up.

I heard them before I saw them. Down in the dark under the floor of the world — the same place I'd been hearing voices counting down since I was six years old, the same place my father said was nightmares and gave me medicine that tasted like pennies. But it was never nightmares. It was never counting down, either. It was counting *up*. It was a ledger, and ledgers don't count down; they wait for payment.

"Who keeps the books?" I whispered.

"The dead," Sable said. "The court bought their souls, but a soul on paper isn't a soul in a jar. There's always a remainder. Always a little left over that nobody owns." She pressed her brother's death-mask harder against her chest. "My order called it the residue of grace. We used to sweep it out of the temples every spring."

"You *swept* it?"

"We were very tidy people," she said, with the particular bitterness of someone describing their own funeral. "Tidiness is how you commit large crimes without noticing."

Above us, Vess spoke. Its voice wasn't sound exactly — it was more like the feeling of a door opening in a house you thought was empty. It said, in the tongue of the old rites, in the smuggler's cant, in plain market speech, all at once:

*A name freely given. How novel. How rare. How — insufficient.*

"It's sufficient," Coral said. Their voice didn't shake. I've heard Coral lie to bandits, haggle with priests, talk their way past three separate tolls, and I know every version of their voice. This was none of those. This was the voice underneath all of them, the one they probably didn't know they had. "You wanted a soul. A name freely given *is* a soul. That's the oldest law there is, older than you."

*Older than me,* Vess agreed, and something in its hunger flickered — doubt, maybe, or appetite pausing to savor. *And so I will take it, and the child, and the flame, and I will be the last answer left standing when every other prayer goes quiet. Kneel, oath-keeper. You have knelt your whole life. One more time costs you nothing.*

"That's where you're wrong," Coral said. "It costs me everything. That's why it counts."

And I felt the mountain hold its breath.

Not metaphorically. I mean the air stopped moving. The brine-gate far above us stopped weeping mid-drop. Every ghost in every wall went still as a held note. And then — because I was the conduit, because I was the boy who heard the dreaming, because apparently being a nightmare-ridden noble child was actually a job qualification this whole time — the mountain turned to me and asked, in the wordless way it asked everything:

*Well?*

I stood up from behind the bone pillar.

Sable grabbed my sleeve. "Perrin—"

"They're going to say no," I told her. "All of them. Everyone down here. I can hear them. They've been saying no for forty years and nobody was listening." My voice cracked, which was embarrassing, but honestly at this point the whole situation was embarrassing, so I let it crack. "Somebody just has to count the vote out loud."

* * *

Here's what I said. I'm writing it down now, later, by firelight, because I want to remember it exactly, and also because Freya asked, and I would do approximately anything that child asks, which Coral says makes two of us.

I said: *"Everyone the court ever bought. Everyone who prayed and got an answer and wondered why the answer felt like a hook. Everyone whose kindness was purchased and whose cruelty was scheduled. I hear you. I've heard you my whole life. You are not debts. You are people. And people get to vote."*

And the mountain answered.

It started small. A salt-dead villager stepped out of the vault wall — one of the gentle ones from the mining village, the woman who'd taught Perrin — no, taught *me* — to bake bread over a ghost-fire, the one who'd hummed while she worked. She looked at Vess the way you look at weather you've decided to walk through anyway.

Then another. And another. The walls of the undercourt, which I had thought were stone, turned out to have been people all along — packed earth and ash and patience, generations of the bought and the borrowed, standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark, waiting for someone to give them permission.

*I can't,* some of them whispered. *We're bound. The contracts—*

"Contracts need a counterparty," Sable said, stepping out beside me, and oh, she was magnificent, she was terrible and magnificent, the defrocked priestess with her stolen relic held up like a lantern. "And the counterparty," she said, "is *dead*."

She meant her brother. She meant the man whose face was on the mask — the one the court had bought forty years ago, the one whose signature anchored every contract downstream of his, the one whose child they'd taken next. She held the death-mask up to whatever passed for light down there, and she said the words her order used to unmake bargains, the ones they'd burned into her memory and then thrown her out for knowing too well:

*"What was lent returns. What was sold was never owned. What was sworn to a dead man dies with him. By the mask, by the grave, by the grief that outlasted both — I unmake it. I unmake it. I unmake it."*

Three times. Everything important happens three times. Salt, vows, knock on wood.

The mask cracked down the middle with a sound like a heart finally exhaling, and the crack ran outward — through the walls, through the floors, through forty years of paperwork written in bone-dust and bad faith. I felt it go through me, too, clean as a bell. Somewhere behind us, guards who had been closing in stopped closing in, looked at their own hands, and found them their own again.

Vess screamed.

I want to be honest: it was not a satisfying scream. It was awful. It sounded like every answered prayer un-answering at once, like twenty years of silence learning it had been wasted. The god thrashed against the ceiling of the world, and its hunger turned inward, and for one moment it was small — just a cold altar in a ruined shrine, smoke rising from nothing, waiting for someone, anyone, to feed it.

Coral looked up at it, and I watched them do the bravest thing I have ever seen, which was: nothing. They didn't gloat. They didn't curse. They just stood there, nameless and unbought, and let the god see what a person looks like when no one owns them.

Then the dead moved forward, and Vess fled upward, and the mountain closed behind it like a fist.

* * *

Which left the child.

Freya — though we didn't call her that yet; she was still just the small flickering light beside the bone bowl, the soul that had called me *brother* in a voice like a struck match. She drifted toward me, and I swear on my father's entire unpleasant household that she was smiling.

"Is it over?" she asked.

"Not quite," Sable said gently. "You have to go home now. Into the fire."

The last dragon-fire guttered in its bowl of bone, barely a blue thumbnail of flame, and I understood suddenly why it had been kept beside her all these years — not as fuel, not as hostage, but as company. Two small lights keeping each other alive in the dark. The court hadn't understood what it was guarding. Cruelty never does.

"What if it hurts?" Freya asked.

"It won't," said Coral, kneeling down to her level. "Do you know why? Because fire is just love that got old and forgot itself. And you—" their voice caught, just slightly, the mercenary, the oath-breaker, the person I would follow anywhere, "—you are going to remind it."

She went. Of course she went. Children are braver than us; they haven't learned to price things yet.

She stepped into the bowl of bone, and the flame rose up around her like arms, and for one heartbeat the whole vault was silent, and then—

Light.

Not the guttering blue thumbnail. Not the sullen embers the undercourt had rationed for forty years. Dragon-fire, real dragon-fire, gold at the core and green at the edges, roaring up the shaft of the vault and out through cracks I hadn't known existed, and everywhere it touched, the dead *warmed*. The salt-woman laughed. The packed-earth walls sighed. Forty years of cold unwound itself in about eleven seconds, and I cried, openly, undignified, a noble boy sobbing in a bone hall, and I regret nothing.

The mountain, above us and around us and *through* us, began — and I am aware of how this sounds — to sing. Low, at first, a rumble in the deep ways. Then voices joining it: ghosts, freed souls, the weeping gate itself changing its tears to something brighter. Every remembered prayer, rising at once, not asking for anything. Just saying thank you.

Sable put her hand on my head like a blessing, which technically it was, even though she'd been defrocked. "You counted the vote," she said.

"Somebody had to," I said. "Ashes vote no. Somebody just has to read it out loud."

Coral stood apart from all of it, watching the fire with an expression I couldn't read — relief and grief sharing a chair. Twenty years of devotion, broken open and found hollow, and still standing there anyway. I went and stood next to them. It seemed like the kind of thing you shouldn't do alone.

"So," I said. "No god anymore. How's that feel?"

Coral was quiet a long moment. The firelight moved on their face, and for once they weren't calculating exits.

"Lighter," they said finally. "Weirdly lighter." Then, wry as ever, because they couldn't help themselves: "Ask me again after we climb out. If the stairs are real stairs this time, I'll give it a glowing review."

Behind us, Freya — warm, breathing, *alive*, wearing a body the fire had woven her out of ash and morning — tugged on Sable's sleeve and pointed up the long shaft toward a distant coin of daylight.

"Can we go home now?" she asked.

And Coral, who belonged to no god, who had lost a name and gained a family, looked at the three of us — the disgraced priestess, the runaway, the child returned from the dead — and said the only prayer I ever heard them pray that got answered instantly:

"Yes. All of us. Let's go home."
