## 20. Rekindling

I have given my name to a god before. It felt like signing a contract — ink and iron, the small dry sound of a life becoming property.

Giving it to the fire was nothing like that.

I stood at the edge of the bone bowl with my hands open and my mouth full of a word I had carried since I was six years old, and when I finally said it — *Coral* — it did not feel like surrender. It felt like setting down something heavy I had been told all my life was precious, only to discover that what made it precious was that I could put it down.

The fire took it the way a mother takes a child: completely, and without keeping.

Here is what I expected: to be emptied. To feel Vess's claim burn out of me like a brand seared from flesh, leaving a hole shaped like twenty years of devotion. I braced for the grief of it. I had already started composing the eulogy — *here lies Coral's faith, survived by no one* — because that is what you do when you are me. You joke at funerals, even your own.

Instead, the flame went up through me like dawn through a window, and everything it touched, it asked permission first.

I felt it find Vess's mark — that old cold knot behind my sternum where the oath had lived, the leash-end I'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a collar — and I felt the fire consider it. Not tear at it. Consider it. The way you'd consider a splinter in someone you loved, deciding whether to pull it quick or slow.

Then it pulled it quick, and I screamed anyway, because even mercy has edges.

What came out of me was not light. It was smoke — grey and greasy and shaped, horribly, like a hand letting go. Twenty years of answered prayers unraveled out of my chest and burned: every shrine-tribute, every whispered tithe, every night I'd knelt on frozen stone talking to a silence and calling the silence holy. It went up black and then it went up gold, because that is the trick of the old fire, the one the undercourt never understood — it doesn't destroy what it burns. It *witnesses* it. All those years of my stupid, stubborn, humiliating faith turned gold as they burned, and for one moment the whole vault smelled like my mother's kitchen, bread and salt and woodsmoke, and I understood that even my wasted devotion had been worth something. Not to Vess. To the fire. Because it had been *given*, and giving is the only coin the divine ever truly honored.

Vess understood it too, in that moment. I heard it — not with ears; there are no ears down there, only the mountain's long attention — I heard the god realize, far too late, that it had spent forty years building a ledger when it should have been learning a language.

"You would give your *name*," Vess said, and its voice had lost the three-tongued grandeur. It sounded small now. It sounded like a merchant discovering the market has moved. "Names are power. Names are *collateral*. That is what names are *for*—"

"No," said Sable, beside me, her brother's death-mask cradled against her chest like a sleeping cat. "That's what names are for *you*. That's why you're starving."

And the fire rose.

I want to describe it properly, because I was the only one watching with eyes that still belonged entirely to them, and someone should remember it right. The last dragon-flame — the little guttering thing we'd found trembling in its bowl of bone, small enough to cup in two hands — took my offered name and did what fire does. It spread. But not like wildfire spreads, greedy and blind. Like hearthfire spreads, when you open the door between rooms and let the warmth decide where it's needed. It poured up out of the bowl and into the walls of the vault, and the walls were salt and bone and the compressed memory of ten thousand years of worship, and all of it caught. The whole mountain's underdark lit gold from the inside, every vein of it, every buried road Perrin had led us down by listening to its dreams — lit like a lantern held against a palm.

And in that light, the soul-child woke.

She had been a flicker beside the flame, a small stolen light the court kept like a jewel. Now she unfolded — I don't know how else to say it. She unfolded out of being an object and into being a girl, maybe seven years old, dark-haired, wearing the shape of a child who had been sold before she could walk and had spent forty years as a lamp-oil for other people's ambitions. She looked at the fire. The fire looked at her. And she laughed — a small cracked laugh, rusty from disuse, the worst and best sound I have ever heard — and stepped into the blaze like a child stepping into a warm bath.

The fire did not consume her. The fire *recognized* her. Keeper-soul, Sable had called her, back when she was confessing in the dark. The flame's heart, sold away so the court could hold the fire hostage. And now the heart came home, and the fire beat.

I felt it in my teeth. In the salt floor. In the dead.

Because the dead were already rising — but I'm ahead of myself. First, Vess fell.

* * *

Gods do not die the way mortals die. I know this now, and I wish I didn't. A mortal dies and leaves a body and a hole. A god dies and leaves *everything it ever took*, and all of it comes back at once.

Vess had taken prayers. Forty years of them, hoarded — and older ones too, centuries of tithes and offerings and frightened bargains struck at shrines across Thornfell, every one of them a thread running from some mortal's desperate hope into the god's hungry center. When the rekindled fire burned through the vault and touched the threads, they did not snap. They *reversed*. Every prayer Vess had ever swallowed came home.

I watched a god drown in gratitude.

It thrashed — a shape like smoke, like a hand, like the negative space of everything I'd ever wanted — and everywhere it thrashed, the returned prayers caught fire and burned gold, and Vess shrank each time, smaller and smaller, until it was the size of the offering I'd left at that ruined shrine in chapter one of my miserable life: a cup of wine and a copper coin, alone on cold stone, waiting for an answer that should never have come.

"Take it back," Vess said. Its voice was almost human now. Almost pitiable. "Take back your name. Be bound again. Anything is better than—"

"Than what?" I asked. "Than belonging to no one?"

I crouched by the last little smear of it, the god that had raised me, or at least collected me, and I did the one thing it never taught me to do. I was kind.

"I forgive you," I said. "Not because you deserve it. Because carrying the grudge would be one more thing I belong to."

And I left it there, a cup of wine and a copper coin's worth of divinity, guttering in the gold light, while around us the undercourt began to come down.

* * *

It came down gently, which surprised everyone. You'd expect rage from a place built on cruelty — screaming stone, collapsing galleries, the whole vindictive architecture of a kingdom of thieves burying its thieves. Instead the mountain simply... exhaled. Rooms that had been held upright by fear let themselves fold. The bazaar where I'd pawned my mother's face dissolved into salt drift, and I felt the memory settle back into me like a bird returning to a branch — lighter than I remembered it, the way things are lighter after you've missed them. The chains of answered prayer, the ones meant to leash every praying soul in the world, rusted through and sloughed off the walls like old shed skin.

Perrin stood in the middle of it with his arms out and his eyes shut, and I realized he wasn't bracing. He was *listening*.

"It's not dying," he said. "It's waking up. There's a difference." He opened his eyes and looked at me, and for the first time since the gate — since his betrayal, since the unlocked door and the guards and my capture — he looked at me straight on. "Coral. The dream says thank you. It says—" His voice wobbled. "It says nobody ever gave it anything before. They only ever took."

"Tell it," I said, and my voice was rougher than I intended, "that it's welcome. And tell it we need a way out that doesn't involve stairs, because I've had enough stairs for one lifetime."

Perrin grinned — a real grin, gap-toothed, twelve years old, absurdly alive — and pointed down a corridor that had definitely not existed a moment ago. "That way. It's dreaming us a road."

Sable snorted. Even crying, she managed to snort, which is the single most impressive thing I saw anyone do that day, including the resurrection of a primordial flame. "Mountains don't dream roads."

"This one does," said Perrin.

"This one," I said, stepping onto the impossible corridor, "has excellent taste in architects."

Behind us, the vault glowed steady and gold, and in it the child sat cross-legged inside the fire like it was a garden, and when Sable hesitated at the threshold — forty years of guilt standing in the doorway of the thing she'd come to save — the girl held out her hand and said, in a voice like a bell that hadn't been rung in decades:

"Auntie. You came."

Sable made a sound I will not repeat here, because some things belong to the people who say them. She crossed the threshold. The fire parted around her like a curtain. And when she came back out, she was carrying her nephew's daughter — the sold child, the keeper-soul, breathing, warm, solid as bread — in her arms, and the death-mask of her brother hung forgotten at her belt, its work finally done.

"Her name," Sable managed, "they never gave her—"

"The fire did," Perrin said. "It's been waiting to. Ask it."

But I think we all knew already. Some names arrive before you hear them. The girl had salt in her hair and ash on her cheeks and she looked up at the three of us — the mercenary, the priestess, the boy — with the expression of someone taking attendance, and I felt something in my chest click into place that no god had ever managed to fasten.

"Freya," she said. Trial balloon. Testing the weight of it.

"Freya," Sable repeated, and broke entirely, and we let her.

* * *

The climb out took a day, or a year, or the length of one very good dream — the mountain's timekeeping was sentimental, and none of us were in a position to argue. The dreaming road wound upward through halls where the salt-dead stood aside and bowed, and some of them — the villagers from the hearth-town, the gentle ghosts who'd fed us stew and warned us about kindness — walked with us a while, chattering like neighbors seeing off travelers.

One of them, the old woman who'd mended Perrin's coat, pressed a twist of cloth into my hand at a landing. Inside was salt. Just salt.

"For the road," she said. "You'll know the house."

I didn't ask what she meant. I'm learning.

The air changed before the light did — I felt it first as a smuggler feels weather, a pressure shift, a promise. Then grey daylight, actual daylight, filthy and freezing and the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, spilled down the dreaming road, and we climbed into it, four of us where three had gone down, and behind us the mountain settled into its new sleep with a sound like a dog circling twice before lying down.

We came out above the Weeping Gate, on a ledge where the brine-falls caught the morning and threw rainbows at us like the mountain was showing off. Freya had never seen the sky. She stopped walking, tipped her head all the way back, and stared at the clouds for so long I started to worry, and then she said:

"It's so *big*. Why did they tell me it was small?"

Nobody had an answer for that. Nobody ever does, for children asking about the sky.

Sable held her closer. Perrin stood at the ledge-edge with the wind wrecking his hair, grinning down at the world like it was a present he'd been told was too expensive to touch. And I stood a little apart, the way I always do, and checked myself over the way you check your pockets after a pickpocket works a crowd.

Name: gone. Given away, gladly, and the space where it used to sit was not empty — it was *open*, like a window opened in a stuffy room. Oath: burned, and good riddance. God: none. Debts: none. The ledger of my life, forty years in the keeping of powers great and small, balanced to zero for the first time since I was six years old.

I belonged to no one.

I turned around, and Freya was looking at me.

"Coral," she said — my old name, the one I'd given to the fire, the one that should have been ash somewhere in the vault below. She said it easily, the way you say a word you've always known. "That's yours. The fire gave it back. It said—" she frowned, translating something only she could hear, "—it said names aren't for keeping. They're for *calling*. So people can call you home."

I am not going to pretend I handled that well. I am a professional. I have walked through bandit ambushes and burning markets and the literal underworld with a flat face and steady hands. And I stood on a mountainside in the morning sun and cried like my name was being handed back to me in a paper cup, because it was, more or less, and because the hands it came from were small and warm and had waited forty years in the dark for someone to teach them that gifts go both ways.

"Well," I said, when I could talk again. "Someone has to teach you which berries are lies. And which passes the toll-keepers watch. And how to win at cards, though honestly you should learn from Perrin, he cheats better."

"I do not cheat," said Perrin.

"He cheats beautifully," I said. "It's a gift."

Sable laughed — wet, cracked, real. Freya laughed, that rusty bell-ringing laugh, already less rusty. And the four of us stood there on the ledge, a smuggler with no master, a priestess with no god, a boy with a mountain's forgiveness, and a child with a brand-new name and a brand-new sky, and if that isn't a family, then I don't know what the word has been for.

Below us, the Weeping Gate had gone quiet. Above us, the pass ran on toward Lowmark, toward Tam and the caravan and the ordinary crooked world of tolls and trails and dinners cooked over small fires.

Freya took my hand. Hers was warm.

"Home?" she asked.

"Not yet," I said, and squeezed back. "First, breakfast. Belonging keeps. Breakfast doesn't."

And we started walking — downhill, into the light, four shadows stretched long and free across the snow, going wherever we pleased, answerable to no one, called home by anyone who knew our names.
