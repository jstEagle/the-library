## 21. Belonging to No One

The sun came up over Thornfell like an apology it had been rehearsing for forty years.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that we climbed out of the Weeping Gate at dawn, filthy as grave-robbers and smelling of brine and old smoke, and the light hit us so gently — so *carefully* — that I nearly laughed. The mountain had spent a week trying to kill us, or marry us, or both; the sky, apparently, had decided to be kind about it.

Coral came out last, carrying Freya.

That is not her name yet. That is her name already. There is a difference, and I have stopped pretending I understand it. The child in Coral's arms was breathing — actually breathing, chest rising and falling like any ordinary sleeping thing — and she was warm in a way souls kept beside dragon-fire are never warm, and when Perrin asked what to call her, Coral looked at the light on the pass and said, "Freya," the way you say a word you've been carrying under your tongue since before you knew it belonged to someone.

Perrin repeated it. The mountain, far below us now, seemed to repeat it too. I felt it through my boots, faint as a heartbeat in a floorboard: *Freya. Freya.* Not a prayer. A greeting.

Sable, I told myself. You are Sable, defrocked priestess of a drowned order, and you are standing in daylight holding nothing but your own two hands, and one of them is shaking, and that is allowed.

We walked down out of the high passes for most of the day without speaking much. There is a particular silence that follows the end of a war or a rescue or a bargain — the good silences, the ones where nobody needs anything from anybody. I have stood vigil in temples full of chanting and never heard quiet like the quiet between the four of us on that road.

Freya woke once, near midday, and looked at all of us in turn with eyes the color of banked coals, and said nothing, because she was perhaps six years old and had been a small flame in a bowl of bone for longer than she'd been a girl, and there are things even children need time to say. But she reached up and put her hand flat against Coral's jaw, the way you check whether something is real, and Coral went very still, and then very soft, and I looked away because some moments are doors and you shouldn't stand in them.

* * *

We buried Tam's brother's mask — no. Forgive me. Old habit. My order trained me to speak of the dead in third person so the grief would stay at arm's length. It never worked, and I am done pretending it did.

We buried my brother's mask three days later, in the salt village, because that was where he would have wanted to be, if wanting were something the dead still got to do.

You should understand what the mask was. When a priest of the Salt Order dies, we press their face into white clay and keep the mask on the family altar until the mourning is finished. My brother sold his son to the court beneath the mountain when the boy was four years old — sold him for a debt, for mercy, for reasons I have spent this whole story refusing to judge him for and will continue to refuse, because he is dead and I loved him and love does not require a ledger. He died two winters after. His mask sat on my altar for eleven years while I told myself the mourning wasn't finished, because as long as the mask stayed, the debt was still open, and as long as the debt was open, there was still something I could *do*.

That is the priestess's disease, if you want to know. We don't pray because we believe. We pray because praying is a shape to put our hands in.

The ghost-keepers met us at the edge of the village. They walk gently, the dead here — I've described them before, the way they tend hearths and mend fences and leave bowls of water out for travelers who died centuries ago — and their eldest, a woman named Hesh who has been dead so long she's forgotten which century owes her an apology, took one look at the bundle in my arms and said, "Ah. Finally."

"You knew?" I said.

"Child, we're dead. We know everything except how to mind our own business." She led us to the hearth-house, where the salt-dead keep their warmest fire, and the villagers — living and otherwise — cleared a space by the eastern wall, where the ground was soft and the morning sun would reach it first.

I dug the grave myself. Coral offered. I said no. This was mine to do, the way the selling had been my brother's, the way the hunting-down of heretics had been mine for nine years before they stripped my vestments. You do not hand your penance to someone with stronger arms.

The mask went into the earth face-up. I know the rite says face-down, so the dead needn't watch the world go on without them, but I broke that rule along with every other one I ever swore, and I wanted him to see the sky. I wanted him to see the same sky his son walked under, alive and breathing and calling a stranger's name.

I didn't say the offices. I started to — the words were right there, worn smooth as river stones from thirty years of use — and then I stopped, knelt in the dirt with my hands full of grave-soil, and said instead:

"Marek. Your boy is free. I didn't save him the way the Order taught me to save anyone. I saved him the way people do. Badly, late, together." My voice cracked on *together*, which was fine, because everyone pretending not to hear included two people who had also cracked on that word recently. "The debt's paid. Not the way they wanted it paid. Better."

Hesh put her hand on my shoulder. Dead hands are cold, but hers was cold the way well-water is cold in summer — a kindness of temperature.

"He hears you," she said. "They always hear you. It's the answering back they're bad at."

I laughed. It came out wet and surprised, the way real laughter does after a long drought. And the strange thing — the thing I'll carry — is that no god leaned into that laugh. No presence stirred. The old rites used to feel like shouting down a well and waiting for the echo to wear your own face. This was just a woman laughing over her brother's grave, in a village of gentle ghosts, with the wind moving through it.

It was enough. Saints preserve me, it was *enough*.

* * *

Lord Aldric's men found us on the fourth day, which was faster than I'd hoped and slower than Perrin feared.

There were six of them, riding livery horses, with the look of household guards sent on an errand they'd rather not have — the particular misery of men ordered to retrieve a child they privately think is better off lost. Their captain was a broad, tired woman with grey coming in at her temples, and she dismounted before her horse had fully stopped, which told me she'd been Perrin's nurse or his swordmaster or both.

"Perrin Aldric," she said. "Your lord father requests—"

"No," said Perrin.

He said it very quietly. He'd been practicing, I think. I'd seen him mouthing it at the campfire the night before, testing the weight of it, a boy rehearsing the largest word he'd ever owned.

The captain blinked. "Young master—"

"I'm not going back." He stepped forward — in front of Coral, I noticed, in front of all of us, a child putting himself between armed men and the people he'd chosen, which is either foolishness or the whole point of being alive, and I have decided it's the second one. "Father sent me to the mountain's edge to listen to the dreams because he thought it was a gift he could use. He didn't ask if it hurt. Nobody in that house asks if things hurt. They ask if things *work*."

The captain's jaw tightened. I watched her weigh it — duty against the specific exhaustion of serving a man who counts his children among his instruments. I have stood in that exact place. I wore its uniform for nine years.

"And these?" she said, nodding at us. "Smugglers and a defrocked priestess. Fine company for the heir to—"

"The best company I've ever had," Perrin said, "and I've had a table with forty seats."

Silence. Somewhere behind us, Freya giggled — she'd been teaching herself to giggle that week, trying the sound on like a new coat, and it still startled her every time it worked.

The captain looked at Coral. Coral, who had been standing very still with their hands loose at their sides, wearing the expression of a person who has decided not to have an opinion unless invited. Coral shrugged — a small, eloquent shrug that said *the boy speaks for himself, and frankly he does it better than either of us.*

"His lordship will send for him again," the captain said at last, and there was something in her voice that might have been apology, or might have been envy, or might have been both wearing each other's clothes.

"Then he'll send for someone who isn't here," Perrin said.

She mounted up. Before she turned her horse, she looked down at him a long moment and said, quietly, "You get the stubbornness from his mother, thank the gods," and rode off, and Perrin stood watching the dust until it settled, and then turned around and burst into tears, and Coral caught him, and that was that.

He chose us. I want to write it plainly because everything else about it was plain: a boy, a road, a choice. No god demanded it. No bargain required it. He chose the people who fed him badly cooked fish and listened to his nightmares and forgave him — eventually, imperfectly, Coral slower than the rest of us — for the worst week of his life.

Found family is just a phrase until you watch a child pick it, deliberately, over a house with forty seats.

* * *

Coral built the shrine on the last evening of autumn, at the crossroads below the pass, and it was the strangest holy work I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a bishop consecrate a chapel built entirely of shipwreck wood.

There was no idol. That was the point, though it took me a day to understand it. Coral hauled stones for two days — actual labor, sleeves rolled, swearing at the big ones — and fitted them into a low cairn-shape with a hollow at its heart, and then stood looking at the hollow for a long time, and I realized they were waiting for a god to fill it, and no god came, and Coral smiled like that was the answer.

"What is it?" Perrin asked. "A shrine to who?"

"To no one," Coral said. "That's the entire theology. Come here."

They showed him: travelers leaving offerings in the hollow — not for a god, but *past* the hollow, for whoever came next. A heel of bread for the hungry traveler behind you. A dry cloak for the cold one. A song, if you had one, sung badly at the cairn because the cairn couldn't judge. Salt, always salt, because salt remembers.

"It's a shrine where the prayers get answered by people," Coral said. "Turns out that was the only kind of answering that ever worked. Took me twenty years and one extremely bad god to figure it out."

I stood there while they worked, this mercenary who had spent a lifetime paying tribute to a voice that only ever wanted to eat them, stacking stones for strangers, and I thought: *this is what my Order was reaching for and missed by the width of a heaven.* We built altars upward — to the sky, to the deeps, to anything that might deign to look down. Coral built one sideways. Across. The direction people actually travel.

Freya helped with the last stone. She insisted. She placed it crooked and Coral declared it load-bearing, and she laughed her new laugh, and the sound went up past the pass and nothing answered it, and that was fine.

That was finally, finally fine.

* * *

We camped that night around a fire Coral built too large, because Coral builds all fires too large now, as if personally compensating for one guttering flame in a bowl of bone. Freya slept between us like a coal in ash. Perrin was talking in his sleep — not nightmares anymore; he was narrating the mountain's dreams, softly, the way other children talk to stuffed animals, and the mountain, far above us, seemed to be listening kindly.

I took the last watch, because I like the last watch, because it belongs to no one.

And that's when I understood the chapter of my life I'd just closed. I had been a priestess, then a heretic, then a hunted thing, then a bargainer, then a penitent. Every one of those names was given to me by something larger than myself — an Order, a god, a court, a debt. I had spent my whole life belonging upward.

I looked at the fire. At Coral, asleep sitting up, one hand resting on Freya's blanket even in sleep, guarding by pure habit. At Perrin murmuring dreams. At the crooked stone shrine catching starlight at the crossroads, empty-hearted and full-handed.

I belong to no one, I thought. And then, correcting myself the way you learn to correct yourself after enough years on the road: I belong to *no one above*. Down here, at the level of fires and dinners and badly sung songs, I belong quite thoroughly — to a smuggler who breaks oaths for love, to a boy who leads the dead, to a child made of rekindled flame, to a brother's grave under salt earth with his face turned to the sky.

The wind moved through the pass. Far off, the first snow of the season began, unhurried, unthreatened, falling on a mountain that no longer dreamed of dying.

And at the edge of the firelight — just at the edge, where the dark starts — I saw the ghost-keeper Hesh standing, or something shaped like her, or maybe only the wind remembering her. She raised one cold hand. Not a blessing. Priests give blessings. This was simpler.

It was a wave goodbye. Or possibly a wave *hello* — because far down the road, past the crossroads shrine, a traveler's lantern was coming toward us through the snow, and someone would need bread, and a dry cloak, and a song sung badly.

I got up to put the kettle on.

Somebody has to answer.
