## 22. Freya and the Salt

The trouble with being a legend is that people keep asking you questions.

"Is it true," says the youngest of the wool merchants I'm guiding over the pass this morning, "that you broke an oath to a god?"

"It's true that I stopped paying rent to one," I say.

"But the songs say—"

"The songs also say I fought Vess with a sword forged from my own grief. What actually happened is considerably less dignified and involved more running."

He laughs the way young men laugh when they think I'm joking, which is fine. Let him have the version with the sword. The truth — that I stood in a vault of bone and offered up the only thing I owned outright, my name, like a fool laying his last coin on a table he didn't understand — doesn't make for good fireside material anyway. Truth rarely does. It's too quiet. It mostly sounds like a woman saying *take mine instead* in a voice that shook.

We crest the shoulder of the pass by midmorning, and there it is: the shrine.

It isn't much. That was always the point. A low cairn of grey stone at the bend where the Thornfell road drops toward the valley, no idol, no altar, no carved face staring down at travelers demanding their due. Just stones, and salt, and whatever strangers have left behind this season. Today there's a clay cup of coarse salt going damp in the drizzle, three copper coins nobody will ever collect, a child's mitten, and — new since last week — a small wooden bird, badly carved, its wings lopsided as a drunk's attempt at flight.

I stop, because I always stop. Habit, or something older than habit.

"You can go on ahead," I tell the merchants. "I'll catch up."

They pass the shrine without pausing, though the youngest glances back at me, curious. Most travelers do one of two things here. They hurry past, uneasy, or they leave something. A pinch of salt from their supper. A few notes of a song hummed under the breath, half-embarrassed. Once I watched a whole caravan of pilgrims take turns setting their palms flat on the cairn like it was the flank of a horse they were gentling.

Nobody prays here. That took some getting used to, in the early years. People would arrive, see the shrine, and start in on the old reflexes — kneeling, clasping hands, asking for safe roads and full bellies and their mother's arthritis to ease. And nothing would answer, because there was nothing left up there to do the answering, and for the first little while that silence used to sit on me like a wet cloak.

Then Freya taught me better.

* * *

She comes over the ridge now, ahead of the merchants' train, the way she always ranges ahead, because she has never once in her life met a horizon she didn't want to argue with. Eleven years old. Barefoot until the frost makes her wear boots, and even then only grudgingly. Her hair is the color of the flame she was born out of — or reborn into; the theology still gets fuzzy if I think about it too long, so mostly I don't — and when she runs, the mountain wind gets tangled in it and seems to carry her along like it's proud of her.

Which, honestly, it might be. The mountain likes her. The mountain likes her the way old soldiers like the children of the friends they couldn't save.

"Mama Coral!" she shouts, because she refuses to call me anything else, and because Sable gave up correcting her around year three. "There's a hawk! There's a hawk and it's *huge* and Perrin says it's a kestrel but it's obviously not a kestrel, it's enormous—"

"Perrin knows birds," I call back.

"Perrin knows *books about* birds."

This is true and fair. Perrin is twenty now, tall and quiet and still carrying the careful look of a boy who once unlocked a gate he shouldn't have and paid for it in memories of home. He walks with us most seasons, between stints at the university in Lowmark where they've decided — gods help them all — that he is the foremost living authority on the dreaming of mountains. He hates the title. He wears it anyway, because Sable pointed out that a man who can hear what the deep places are thinking might be able to warn people before the deep places get ideas.

He catches up to her at the bend, red-faced from the climb, and points at the hawk without breaking stride. "Kestrel."

"That bird could carry off a sheep."

"Then it's a very ambitious kestrel."

She laughs — that laugh, bright and unguarded, the one that took years to grow in a child who'd spent forty years as a flicker of light in a bowl of bone — and runs on ahead again, and I watch her go the way I watch every time, which is greedily, hoarding the sight of it.

Sable falls into step beside me. She's greyer now, her priestess's discipline long since softened into something more like weathered kindness, and she carries the same walking staff she's carried since Lowmark, though the relic it once concealed is gone. Buried, these six years, under a rowan tree above the salt village, next to a grave we marked with a name instead of a mask. Her brother. She visits when the passes are open. She comes back quieter each time, but it's a different quiet than the one she carried when I first met her. That quiet was a held breath. This one is an exhale.

"She left salt at the shrine again," Sable says.

"I saw the cup."

"She thinks I don't notice her taking it from the cooking stores."

"I think everyone notices. We let her have it because it matters to her."

Sable smiles at the road. "It matters to all of us. We just pretend ours is rationing."

* * *

Here is what happened to prayer, after everything. Nothing dramatic. Prayer didn't die. It just stopped getting answers, and discovered it had never needed them.

Oh, there are still priests. There are still temples in the lowland cities, still incense and liturgy and men in expensive robes explaining why the gods have withdrawn their faces. The temples have theories. The temples always have theories. Sable read one treatise that claimed the divine silence is a test of faith, and another that claimed the gods are sleeping, and a third so convoluted I stopped following somewhere around the footnote about the ontological status of smoke.

Freya's theory is better. Freya's theory, delivered at age eight with the absolute authority of a child eating soup, was: *"Maybe they're done pretending to live in the sky. Maybe they moved in with everybody."*

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. Because here's what we saw, the three of us and then the growing circle of us, in the years after the undercourt crumbled and the dragon-fire came back up through the roots of the world like spring through frozen ground: prayers went unanswered, and yet things got answered. Not by thunder. By neighbors. A widow's prayer for firewood answered by a woodcutter who "happened" to have extra. A lost child's prayer answered by a shepherd who heard crying on the wind. Nobody called it a miracle. Everybody quietly behaved like one.

The salt shrines spread the same way. No doctrine, no order, no tithes. Just stones at crossroads and passes, and salt — always salt, because salt was what the dead of the mining villages tended their hearths with, because salt preserves, because salt is what tears dry into — and small songs, hummed, whistled, sung badly. Travelers leaving a little of themselves at the edge of the road for whoever comes next. Belonging was never above. It was beside. I didn't coin that phrase; Sable did, one night over wine, and then pretended she hadn't said anything profound, which is the most profound thing about her.

Do I miss it? People ask me that sometimes, usually late, usually after enough drink to make the question brave. Do I miss having a god? Being bound? Knowing that something vast was listening when I spoke?

Here's the honest answer: I miss nothing, because I was never listening to something vast. I was talking to a wall and calling the echo a voice. Twenty years I paid tribute to Vess at cold shrines, hating myself for hoping, and the whole time the thing I was starving for was sitting across a campfire wearing a defrocked priestess's face, or asleep against my saddle roll in the form of a runaway boy, or waiting forty years in a bowl of bone for someone to come and witness it. Everything holy I ever encountered fit inside a human-sized space. Arms, mostly. Arms and dinner tables.

The answering happens at dinner tables now. That's not a metaphor. That's just the schedule.

* * *

We make camp that night in the lee of the old toll rocks — the same ones the bandits ambushed us behind, back when, though the bandits are long scattered and one of them, ironically, now runs a respectable inn at the foot of the pass and gives our family free rooms whenever we pass through, out of what he insists is business sense and what we all know is gratitude. Perrin builds the fire. He's good at fires. He says the wood tells him how it wants to burn, and none of us ask whether he means that literally anymore, because he does, and it's fine, it's all fine, the world is stranger and kinder than the maps admit.

Freya performs her evening ritual, which is: she takes a pinch of salt from the cook pouch, marches to the edge of the firelight, and scatters it in a thin arc facing the mountain.

"For the ones below?" Sable asks, though she asks every night, because Freya likes telling it.

"For the ones below," Freya confirms gravely. "So they remember the way up. And for the ones above—" she spins and throws a second pinch skyward, where it vanishes into the dark, "—so they remember the way down. In case anybody got lost."

"And does it work?"

"Obviously." She sits back down between us, radiating the smugness of the theologically correct. "Everything works if you mean it."

Perrin, poking the coals, says quietly: "The mountain agrees with her."

"The mountain would," I say. "It's soft on her."

"It's soft on all of us." He says it simply, without embarrassment, and that's the change in him, the healing of the wound he gave himself at that gate. For years he flinched whenever anyone trusted him with anything — a door, a secret, the lead position on a trail. Now he says things like *the mountain agrees with her* and lets the sentence stand there in the firelight, unguarded, and nobody laughs, and that's how you know a boy became a man: not by scars fading, but by him deciding what the scar means.

Later, when the others sleep, I sit up with my tea and do the thing I still do, the last habit of the old life, except changed.

I pray.

Not to anything. To no one. I put my hands around the warm cup and I say the names — Sable, Perrin, Freya, the innkeeper, the ghost-keepers of the salt village, Tam the smuggler who asked me why I bothered and who, I heard, now leaves salt at every shrine between here and the coast — and I hold them the way you hold something warm in cold weather. If anyone's listening, they don't need to answer. The answer already happened. It happens nightly, at a fire, with bad tea and good company and a child's salt thrown at the stars.

Once, a god demanded my name as payment. What it never understood — what none of the old powers understood, hungry and ancient and alone in their high cold places — is that a name given freely stops being a possession. It becomes a gift, and gifts travel. My name is in Sable's mouth when she calls me to supper. It's in Perrin's maps, inked in the corner of the good one: *surveyed by C.* It's in Freya's stories, told to other children at other fires, where I am apparently seven feet tall and fight with a sword made of grief.

Let the gods keep their registers. I'm listed elsewhere.

* * *

Morning comes up gold and cold. We break camp, and the merchants' train is waiting at the bend, and beyond them the road runs down and down toward the valley, toward Lowmark and the river and the ordinary miraculous world.

Freya is at the shrine again, of course. She's added something to it — I watch her set the lopsided wooden bird carefully atop the cairn, adjusting it twice until the wings face the wind.

"Who's that for?" I ask, coming up beside her.

"A traveler who hasn't come yet." She brushes off her knees, matter-of-fact. "Somebody's going to come over this pass scared, someday. And they'll see the bird and know somebody was glad they made it this far. Even before they did."

I look at her for a long moment. Forty years in a bowl of bone, kept beside a dying flame by creatures who thought souls were currency, and the court never managed to teach her scarcity. The fire remembered love, and it gave her all of it.

"Well," I say, when I can trust my voice. "Come on, then. Road's long and the tea's better at the bottom."

She takes my hand — still does, at eleven, and I intend to enjoy it while it lasts — and we start down together, the four of us strung out along the pass like beads on a thread: the disgraced priestess, the boy who hears mountains, the mercenary belonging to no one, and the child named by fire.

Behind us, the shrine stands in the morning light with its salt and its small songs and its badly carved bird, waiting, patient as stone, for the next stranger.

Above us, nothing watches. Nothing needs to.

Beside us — that's where everything is.
