## 1. The Apprentice Who Wasn't

The trouble with hiding a thing for three years, Oscar Oyelaran had learned, was that you got so good at it you forgot what it was you were hiding.

She discovered this on a Tuesday morning in the market town of Lowmarsh, when she caught herself knotting a frayed lead-rope with a pattern that would keep the mule calm through thunder — a pattern her hands knew better than her head did, a pattern with *magic* braided into every loop like a hair in the wool.

She stopped. Stared at her own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger. Then, glancing left and right down the empty lane behind Ferro's stable, she picked the knot apart and tied it again, plain and stupid, the way any honest carter would.

"There," she muttered to the mule, whose name was Duchess and who had opinions about everything and shared none of them aloud. "Boring. Safe. You'll spook at the first storm and we'll both be sorry, but sorry is cheap."

Duchess flicked one ear at her, which from Duchess constituted a lengthy philosophical rebuttal.

Three years since Thornfell. Three years since she'd run from Maren's cottage with nothing but a satchel, a scolding ringing in her ears, and the certain knowledge that she would never touch magic again — not real magic, not the kind that got noticed. And she'd kept that promise, mostly, if you didn't count the knots. And the trail-marks. And the small charm she worked into her bootlaces each morning so her feet never blistered, which wasn't really magic, more of a courtesy. And the way she could quiet a colicky horse with a hum in a key no one else could hear.

So: she'd kept the promise entirely, except for constantly.

The trick, she'd found, was to make the magic so small it disappeared into ordinary life. A knot that held. A mark scratched on a fencepost that said *safe road this way* in a language only other runaways might read, if they'd been taught by someone who'd been taught by someone back down the long chain of hedge-witches and herb-wives and midwives who had kept the old knowledge alive by keeping it *dull*. That was Maren's first lesson, though Oscar hadn't understood it then: *The crown doesn't fear power, girl. It fears power that can be seen. So be useful and be boring, and you'll live to be old and tedious.*

Oscar had taken to the boring part like a duck to a millpond.

She was twenty-two now, lean and sun-brown, with a guide's weather-eye and a reputation — modest, hard-earned, and exactly as large as she wanted it — as the best mule-train handler between Lowmarsh and the Thornfell passes. She could get a loaded train over the Salt Road in fog, talk toll-keepers down from robbery to mere insult, and mend a harness with twine and spite. Nobody asked where she'd learned it. Nobody asked anything about her at all, which was precisely the point, and which was also, some nights, alone in whatever loft or lean-to the road provided, a loneliness with teeth.

But that was Tuesday's problem, and Tuesday's problem was currently being solved by untying a knot.

* * *

Wednesday brought the proclamation.

She heard it before she saw it — the town crier's bell, and under it a strange hush moving through the market like a cold current through warm water. Hushes were bad. Markets were supposed to be loud; a quiet market meant either a death or a tax, and Oscar had learned to dread both equally.

The crier stood on the steps of the assize hall with a scroll and a voice trained to carry across battlefields, reading:

*"By order of His Majesty's Privy Council: all persons possessed of uncanny gifts — hedge-craft, spell-work, second sight, beast-speaking, or any art beyond common nature — shall present themselves for registration within thirty days, upon pain of law. Registration is not punishment. Registration is protection."*

He said that last part twice, which told Oscar everything. Things that are true once are enough. Things that are lies need hammering.

A copy of the proclamation was already nailed to the tavern door across the square, big black letters on good paper — *good* paper, crown paper, the kind that cost more than most folk in Lowmarsh saw in a month. Someone had hammered it up crooked. Someone else, an old woman selling apples, was looking at it the way you look at a snake in the woodpile: very still, very carefully, planning your route around it.

Oscar looked too. She made herself look properly, because running from a thing with your eyes shut is how you walk into its arms.

*Registration.* A list. Names, faces, places of dwelling, descriptions of gift. A list in the hands of the crown, and lists, in Oscar's experience, had a way of becoming kindling. Her mother — dead these ten years, gods rest her — used to say the last hunt had started with a list too, back before Oscar was born. Started with clerks and ink and polite questions, and ended with smoke on the north wind and whole families gone from the parish rolls like they'd never been.

"Protection," said the apple-seller beside her, softly, to nobody. "Aye. Sheep are protected too."

"Ma'am," Oscar said, because it seemed required.

"They'll come for the loud ones first," the woman went on, not looking at her, addressing her apples. "The ones what can't help showing off. Then the ones reported by neighbors. Then—" She stopped. Picked up her basket. "You've kind eyes, girl. Mind they stay in your head where they're safe."

And she shuffled off, leaving Oscar standing in the square with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

Because here was the thing, the thing she hadn't let herself think in three years: the proclamation wasn't aimed at her. Not exactly. It was aimed at *gifted persons*, plural, a category, a species — and Oscar had spent three years convincing herself she wasn't one. She was a mule-train guide. She was boring. The knots were just knots; the trail-marks were just trail-marks; the bootlace charm was practically superstition, and everyone had superstitions.

Except that was the lie, wasn't it. That was the whole rotten architecture of it. She hadn't stopped being gifted. She'd just gotten very, very good at being invisible, and invisibility, she realized now, staring at the crooked-nailed paper, was not the same thing as being safe. Invisibility was safety *on loan*, and somewhere out there was a clerk with a ledger who could call in the debt.

The cost of it landed all at once, the way debts do. Three years of small cowardices — every time she'd seen a runaway's mark on a fencepost and walked past without answering it, every time she'd heard whisper-talk of someone hunted and kept her mouth shut and her head down, every time she'd chosen the boring knot, the safe silence, the invisible life — all of it came due in that moment, standing in the market square with apple-stalks under her boots.

She had been so proud of her hiding. And hiding, it turned out, was just another word for letting it happen to somebody else first.

* * *

That evening she did what she always did when the world turned ugly: she went to work.

Ferro's yard smelled of straw and mule and honest sweat, and Brannic Ferro himself stood at the tack-room door with his ledger under his arm, watching the light die over the rooftops. He was a big man gone quietly grey, with a face like a closed strongbox and a voice he rationed like wartime sugar.

"You heard it, then," he said. Not a question. Everyone had heard it.

"Heard something," Oscar said. "Depends whether I believe criers."

Brannic grunted. It might have been a laugh. With him you learned to grade your grunts. "Got a run coming. North. High country."

"How high?"

"High enough." He opened the ledger, consulted it, closed it again without showing her anything, which was very Brannic. "Salt Road to the fork, then the smugglers' cut over the shoulder of Grey Crag. Nine days there, six back, weather permitting."

"Weather permitting," Oscar repeated. "It's autumn, Brannic. Weather permits nothing above four thousand feet. Weather does what it likes and sends apologies later, if it bothers."

"That's why I pay you what I pay you." He paused. Something moved behind the strongbox face — not quite hesitation. Brannic didn't hesitate; he *weighed*. "Cargo's delicate."

"Cargo's always delicate. Last time it was glass bells, time before it was a bishop's wine that I strongly suspect was not for the bishop."

"This cargo breathes."

Oscar's hands went still on the harness she was mending. Outside, dusk settled over the yard, and somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"People," she said.

"People who pay well and ask no questions and want to be somewhere the new law hasn't got to yet." Brannic's eyes came around to her, flat and unreadable. "I need a guide who knows the cut. I need a guide who doesn't talk. And I need a guide who—" the pause stretched, "—who understands why a person might want to be hard to find."

There it was. Laid on the tack-room floor between them like a dropped knife.

He knew. Of course he knew. Three years working for the man, and she'd thought herself invisible, and all along he'd been able to see her fine — maybe not the knots and the charms specifically, but the shape of her, the *what* of her, the reason a girl that young was that careful. You couldn't hide from a man who made his living knowing what people paid to conceal.

"I'm a mule-train guide," she said. It sounded thin even to her own ears.

"Aye," said Brannic. "And I'm a respectable merchant." He tucked the ledger away. "Think on it. We leave day after tomorrow, dawn. Pay's triple, and there's a bonus in it for you if the cargo arrives breathing and unbilled."

He walked off toward the house, boots crunching on the frozen mud, and left Oscar alone with the harness and the dark coming down.

* * *

She didn't sleep much. Lofts in autumn were cold, but that wasn't the reason. The reason was the proclamation, which she could still see nailed to the inside of her eyelids, crooked and black-lettered, *registration is protection*, and underneath it, in letters only she could see, all the names of everyone she'd ever failed to help.

Somewhere past midnight she gave up, lit the stub of candle she kept for emergencies, and dug into the bottom of her satchel.

Maren's map fragment was there. Had been there three years, folded and refolded along the same creases until the creases were going soft as cloth. It was a scrap of the high country north of Lowmarsh — passes, ridgelines, a stretch of the Salt Road — drawn in Maren's cramped, impatient hand, annotated in a cipher of little marks that Oscar had never fully cracked. A circle here. A crossed line there. And in one corner, half worn away, words in the old script that she'd never been able to read and had never shown anyone, because showing anyone would mean admitting she had it, and admitting she had it would mean admitting she'd stolen it off Maren's table the night she ran, and—

Well. Some thefts you carry like a stone in the shoe. You get used to the limp.

She traced the fragment by candlelight now, the way she did perhaps twice a year, on the bad nights. Maren's hand. Maren's sharp little annotations. Maren, who was probably dead by now, or worse than dead — registered, listed, filed away in some clerk's neat ledger. Three years of silence between them, and Oscar had told herself it was safety, hers and Maren's both. Cut the thread and neither end can be pulled.

But the thread didn't feel cut tonight. It felt like it had been pulled taut, all the way from wherever Maren was, straight through the dark, and hooked into Oscar's chest.

*Registration is not punishment.*

Her mother had a saying about proclamations: *When they tell you what a thing isn't, believe them the first time.* 

Oscar blew out the candle and lay in the straw-dark and made herself think it plainly, no dodging, no boring-knot evasions: the world had just changed shape. Thirty days. Lists. Hunters eventually — there were always hunters eventually; the crown's patience was a bowstring, drawn slow and loosed fast. Every gifted soul in the province had thirty days to decide what they were going to be: registered, or hidden, or gone.

And she, Oscar Oyelaran, apprentice who wasn't, witch who wasn't, invisible expert of three years' standing, had a choice dressed up as a job offer waiting for her at dawn.

Smuggler's cut. Breathing cargo. People paying to be somewhere the law hadn't got to yet.

Triple pay, said the sensible voice, the survival voice, the voice that had kept her alive and dull and safe for three years. Take the coin. Guide them over. Don't ask names. Don't learn faces. Be the road, not the rescue. Invisible.

But the other voice — quieter, older, wearing Maren's accent — said only: *Girl. You know what happens to sheep.*

Dawn came grey and early. Oscar was in the yard before Brannic, saddling Duchess, checking hooves, doing all the small busy work of a woman who has already decided and is furious about how long she pretended otherwise.

Brannic appeared with two cups of tea, handed her one, and watched her over the rim of his own.

"Well?" he said.

"The cut past Grey Crag floods in the narrows after rain," Oscar said. "We'll want tarps, rope, and nobody in that cargo who argues with my orders on the mountain, because on the mountain I'm not a servant, I'm the weather they have to live with."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of Brannic's mouth and was firmly escorted off the premises. "Tarps and rope I can do. The cargo—" He set down his cup. "Seven of them. And one of them's a child."

Oscar's hands paused on Duchess's girth strap.

"A child," she repeated.

"Mute, they tell me. Maybe eight, nine years. Keeps close to a woman called Wren." Brannic shrugged, a gesture that didn't quite manage indifference. "Breathing cargo, I said. Didn't say it'd be easy cargo."

Oscar finished cinching the strap, patted Duchess's neck, and looked north, where the Thornfell passes stood up against the pale sky like the backs of sleeping animals. Somewhere up there was the country she'd sworn off, the roads she'd memorized as a child and promised never to use, the whole buried geography of the life she'd run from.

Thirty days, said the proclamation. And lists. And after lists, always, sooner or later: smoke.

"Day after tomorrow, dawn," she said. "Have your cargo ready, Brannic. And Brannic—"

"Aye?"

"If anyone asks, I'm just your guide."

"That's all you ever were," said Brannic Ferro, deadpan, and went to shout at the farrier, and Oscar stood in the cold yard with her heart hammering and told herself the fear in her chest was just the altitude ahead.

She was lying, and she was fairly sure she'd get better at it on the road.
