## 2. Smoke on the North Pass

Oscar smelled the smoke before she saw the village, and for three long heartbeats she let herself believe it was nothing. A chimney fire. A baker's oven gone wrong. The ordinary accidents of ordinary people who had never once needed to hide a charm in their bootlaces.

Then she crested the ridge above Thornfell North and saw the black wings of crows turning over the green, and her stomach dropped through the mountain beneath her.

"No," she said aloud, to nobody, to the mule beside her. Buttercup—named ironically, because she was gray and mean and had once bitten a tax collector—flicked one ear back at Oscar's voice but did not break stride. Mules were honest that way. They didn't pretend your fear was unfounded just to be polite.

The village was burned. Not all of it—the mill still stood, and half the row of houses along the stream—but the parts that mattered were charcoaled skeletons. The meeting hall. The shrine with its carved lintel that old Tammas swore predated the crown itself. And at the end of the lane, set apart from everything else the way it always had been, the cottage.

Maren's cottage.

Oscar had told herself she wasn't going there. She'd told herself that for three years, walking roads that looped wide around the North Pass, taking contracts that kept her south and east and anywhere but home. She'd gotten very good at telling herself things. It was, she reflected now, the family trade: Maren had been telling herself things for decades, and look where it got her.

She tied Buttercup to a fencepost that was more ash than wood and walked down the lane like a woman wading into cold water—one deliberate step at a time, hating every one of them, unable to stop.

* * *

The door hung off one hinge. Inside, the smell of wet char and old herbs fought each other and lost to something sweeter and worse underneath, and Oscar knew that smell from a winter when she was nine and a fever took half the village children: blood gone bad.

"Maren?" Her voice came out small. She hated that too.

A sound from the back room. Not words. Something between a breath and a laugh, which was very Maren—finding even dying faintly funny, or wanting Oscar to think so.

Maren lay on the floor beside her overturned worktable, propped against the wall as though she'd arranged herself there on purpose, because even now she would not be caught looking undignified. She was seventy-some years of iron-gray braid and sharp brown eyes, and those eyes found Oscar in the doorway and lit up with an expression Oscar hadn't seen since she was small: pure, unguarded gladness.

"There she is," Maren said. "Three years, and you come when I'm flat on my back. Typical."

"What happened?" Oscar crossed the room and knelt, and then she saw the wounds and stopped breathing for a moment.

They weren't burns, exactly, though they looked like burns. They were lines—clean, deliberate lines drawn across Maren's forearms and chest, seared into the skin in patterns too regular to be accident. Oscar knew hunter-wrought when she saw it; every gifted child learned the shapes young, the way other children learned to recognize adders. Interrogation marks. Truth-burns. Someone had asked Maren questions with fire and geometry, and Maren had not answered.

"Who did this?"

"The crown sends such polite visitors these days." Maren's hand twitched toward a cup on the floor; Oscar fetched it and held it to her lips. Water, laced with willow and something bitterer. "Captain Varga's people. They came asking after a boy."

"A boy?"

"A child. Dark-haired, maybe eight years old. Doesn't speak." Maren's eyes held hers, steady and terrible. "They think he passed through here. I told them he didn't."

"You told them—" Oscar's throat tightened. "You can't lie to truth-burns. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone is wrong." Maren managed something like a smile. "Truth-burns punish lies. They don't compel truth. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is how much pain you're willing to carry. I've been carrying pain since before your mother was born. A little more won't kill me."

It would, though. That was written all over her, in the gray of her lips and the shallowness of her breath and the way the burn-lines had begun to spread like frost across a windowpane, reaching for her heart.

"You should have run," Oscar said. "You should have come south. You knew where I was—you always knew where I was, don't pretend otherwise, you had birds or charms or—"

"I had a cousin in Millbrook who writes letters." Maren's smile went crooked. "And yes, I could have run. But then they'd have burned the whole village asking after me, instead of just me. I did the arithmetic. I was always better at arithmetic than you."

"That's not true. I balance my ledgers fine."

"Your ledgers balance because you underpay yourself." Maren coughed, wet and long, and Oscar held her shoulders until it passed, and felt how light she was, how much of her had already gone somewhere else. When the coughing stilled, Maren gripped Oscar's wrist with fingers that still had surprising strength left in them. "Listen to me. We haven't time for grief yet. Grief keeps. This doesn't."

"Maren—"

"*Listen.*" And the old command in it, the schoolroom voice, made Oscar's spine straighten before her mind agreed. "Under the third floorboard by the hearth. Bring it."

Oscar pried up the board with numb fingers. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, was a piece of leather—stiff, cracked, and marked with ink that had faded to the color of dried blood. A map fragment. Just a corner of some larger chart, showing mountain contours she half-recognized and a valley she didn't, with a single symbol inked at its center: a circle broken by thirteen short rays, like a moon wearing a crown of knives.

"What is this?"

"A sanctuary," Maren said. "The Hollow of Thirteenth Moon. If the worst comes—if they sweep the passes the way I think they will—you take anyone you can gather and you go there. Do you understand? Anyone. Gifted, hunted, frightened. You go, and you take them with you."

"Why me? Why not go yourself, why not send word to whoever runs this place—"

"Because I'm dying, Oscar, and dead women make poor couriers." Maren said it flatly, without self-pity, the way she'd once announced that supper was turnips again. "And because the road to the Hollow isn't written anywhere whole. It's split. Half is on this fragment. The other half—" she tapped her own temple, "—is in here, and I have perhaps an hour to put it into yours."

Oscar's hands were shaking. She noticed distantly, as if the hands belonged to someone else. "An hour. You've known you were dying for longer than an hour. How long have you known they were coming?"

Maren looked at her then, and something in the look made Oscar's stomach turn over—not the pain in it, but the guilt.

"Long enough," Maren said quietly. "I've known what was coming since midwinter. I could have sent for you in spring. I chose not to."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. "You *chose*—"

"If I'd called you home, they'd have followed you here. Your trail leads straight to my door, girl. It always has. Every mile you stayed away was a mile of rope between us that they couldn't pull." Maren's grip tightened. "I hid you by losing you. I thought you understood that. I thought that's why you never wrote."

Oscar had never written. Three years of silence, and she'd dressed it up as safety, as wisdom, as the mature choice—and all the while she'd believed, in some unexamined corner of her heart, that Maren had let her go easily. That the leaving had cost the old woman nothing. That she alone had carried the weight of the parting.

And now here was Maren, dying on a scorched floor, admitting she'd carried it too—had chosen it, deliberately, coldly, lovingly—and Oscar couldn't tell if she wanted to weep or shake her.

"You hid things from me," Oscar said. "All those years. The sigil-craft, the battle-work you taught me at night and swore me to secrecy about, and now this. What else? What else didn't I know about you?"

Maren laughed—a real laugh, brief and rusty, that turned into another cough. "Everything, dear. You knew almost nothing about me. That was rather the point of me." She caught her breath. "But here's the thing about secrets. You'll learn it soon enough, if you haven't already. A secret isn't a wall. It's a roof. You build it over someone to keep the rain off them, and then one day you realize you built it so heavy they can't stand up under it, and you can't lift it alone anymore." Her eyes glistened. "I built a lot of roofs. I'm sorry for the weight of them."

Oscar bowed her head. Somewhere outside, a crow complained, and the wind moved through the burnt rafters with a low moan like the house itself grieving.

"I'm not ready," she said finally. "For any of this. For the map, for the Hollow, for—for you to—"

"Nobody's ready. Readiness is a luxury for people whose villages aren't burning." Maren shifted, winced, and pressed the map fragment into Oscar's hands, folding the girl's fingers closed over it with both of her own. Her hands were cool. That frightened Oscar more than the burns. "Now. The second half of the road. Attend."

What followed was an hour Oscar would remember for the rest of her life: Maren's voice growing thinner and thinner, reciting landmarks like a litany—the drowned mine shaft behind the white scree, the bridge that demanded truths, the cairn shaped like a sleeping man—and Oscar repeating them back, again and again, carving each one into memory the way Maren had once carved letters into her palm with a stick of charcoal when she was five and stubborn. Twice Maren drifted, and Oscar shook her gently awake, and both times Maren surfaced with the same irritated clarity, as though death were a dull student who kept missing the point.

On the third drift, she didn't come back all the way.

"Maren." Oscar cradled her mistress's head. "Maren, stay with me. Please. I came back. Doesn't that count for anything?"

Maren's eyes opened halfway. The sharpness in them had gone soft, and far away. "Counts for everything," she murmured. "Knew you would. That's why I never sent... never sent the letter..." Her hand rose, trembling, and touched Oscar's cheek—a gesture Oscar hadn't felt since she was small, since the nights of fever and nightmares when Maren would sit by her bed and hum songs with no words. "My girl. My best work. Don't you dare bury yourself with me. Bury me quick and walk fast."

"Maren—"

"And Oscar." The voice was nearly gone now, a thread of smoke itself. "The boy. If you find him... the dark-haired boy... he matters more than all of us. More than you know. Keep him close. Whatever he carries..." Her eyes focused on something past Oscar, past the ruined ceiling, past maybe everything. "...it sings, doesn't it..."

Her hand slipped from Oscar's cheek.

The room went very quiet. Outside, the wind kept moaning through the rafters, and the crows argued over the green, and the stream ran on indifferent and bright, and Oscar Oyelaran knelt on a scorched floor holding the last person who had ever loved her first, and understood that the silence where Maren's voice had been was going to be the loudest thing in her life for a very long time.

* * *

She buried her at dawn, because Maren had said quick, and because Oscar could not bear the thought of the crows deciding otherwise.

There was a hazel tree behind the cottage that Maren had planted the year Oscar arrived—a whip-thin sapling back then, a proper shade tree now. Oscar dug beneath it with a shovel she found in the ruined shed, working until her palms blistered, because blisters were easier to think about than anything else. She laid Maren in facing the mountains, wrapped in the good wool blanket from the bed, with her iron-gray braid coiled neatly—she would have wanted neatness—and a sprig of rowan on her breast, because even dead, a hedge-witch keeps her protections.

Oscar said the old words, the ones Maren had taught her for burying animals and once, gravely, for practicing on a beet. Then she added some new ones of her own, which were mostly apologies, and one promise, which was mostly anger:

"I'll find this Hollow. I'll do whatever you were doing. And I am going to be *furious* with you for years, so you'd better be somewhere you can hear it."

The hazel leaves stirred, though there was no wind. Oscar chose to believe that was agreement.

* * *

By full light she had done the arithmetic Maren had mocked her for, and the sum was ugly.

The proclamation was nailed to every tavern door from here to the coast—that's what the carter at the crossroads had said, grinning, relieved, one of the safe ones, one of the ones who'd never had to read a notice and feel it reading him back. *Gifted persons to present themselves for registration.* Registration today. Whatever came after registration tomorrow. And hunters sweeping the passes, hunting a mute boy whose absence Maren had died protecting, a boy who mattered "more than all of us," a boy who carried something that *sang.*

Sang. Like the sigil-marks in Maren's oldest books, the ones kept behind the false shelf-back, the ones Oscar had been forbidden to touch and had touched anyway, because she'd been born without the gene for obedience. Marks that hummed near power. Marks that meant chains.

Chains meant something chained.

Oscar stood in the middle of the ruined lane with Buttercup's reins in her hand and the map fragment heavy in her coat pocket, pressing against her hip like a second heartbeat, and looked north, where the passes ran up into cloud.

She could go south. South was sensible. South was new names and flat country and small magics hidden in knotwork, invisibility worn like a well-fitted coat. South was everything she'd spent three years building, and it suddenly looked very thin, like a coat with holes she'd never let herself notice.

Or she could go north, following a dead woman's map toward a sanctuary that might be a trap, hunting a boy she'd never met, carrying a secret that was already heavier than any roof Maren ever built.

"He'd better be worth it," she told the mountains.

The mountains, being mountains, declined to comment. But Buttercup turned her head and bumped Oscar's shoulder with her blunt gray nose—an act of affection the mule performed roughly once a decade—and Oscar laughed, wetly, and wiped her face with her sleeve, and swung up into the saddle.

Behind her, the hazel tree dropped a handful of early leaves onto the fresh-turned earth, like a blessing, or a wave goodbye.

Ahead, the North Pass waited, and somewhere in it, a boy who couldn't speak was carrying a song that could wake a god.

Oscar clicked her tongue, and Buttercup walked.
