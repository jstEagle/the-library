## 3. Cargo That Breathes

The note came tucked into a wheel of cheese, which was how all of Brannic Ferro's business arrived: wrapped in something edible, so that if the wrong hands opened it, they'd find only dairy.

Oscar read it twice by the light of the stable lantern, then a third time because she didn't want to believe it.

*One last run. North road, high passes, before the snows close. Cargo is delicate — breathing delicate. Triple your usual rate and a bonus I'll discuss in person. Come to the Drowned Ox at moonrise. Burn this.*

She did not burn it. She fed it to the goat, who had opinions about parchment but no ability to read, which made him the safest confidant in three valleys.

"Triple rate," she told him. The goat chewed thoughtfully. "Nobody offers triple rate for honest cargo. Honest cargo gets paid in turnips and gratitude." She scratched his ears. "Breathing delicate. That's either livestock somebody's embarrassed about, or people."

The goat blinked at her with the serene indifference of an animal who had never once been asked to smuggle anything across a border, and Oscar felt the old familiar pull in her chest — the one that sounded like coin counting itself into her palm and felt like a rope tightening around her ribs.

Because here was the arithmetic of her life, plain as a ledger: winter was coming, the proclamation was nailed to every tavern door from here to the coast — *all gifted persons to present themselves for registration, by order of the crown* — and registration, everyone now understood without anyone saying it aloud, was not a list. It was a net. And Oscar Oyelaran had spent three years teaching her fingers to hide what they knew, knotting small magics into bridle-work and trail-marks, folding charms flat as pressed flowers into the lining of her coat, and none of it would matter if a hunter decided her knots looked *knowing*.

A mule-train guide with no coin could not buy silence. A guide with coin could buy a great deal of it.

"I hate him," she told the goat, meaning Brannic, meaning herself, meaning the whole crooked economy of fear that made a woman weigh other people's lives against her own winter bread and call it prudence. The goat, having extracted all nutrition and moral guidance available from the note, butted her knee and wandered off.

She went to the Drowned Ox.

* * *

Brannic Ferro occupied a corner table the way a mountain occupies a valley: not by effort, but by the simple fact of having always been there. He was a broad, grey-bearded man with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had been weathered past expression sometime around his fortieth year. He drank small beer. He always drank small beer. "Clear head," he said once, when Oscar asked why a smuggler wouldn't drink like one. "Dead men have clear heads too. It's the living ones who need them."

"You look terrible," Oscar said, sitting down across from him. This was true and also a courtesy; between the two of them, pleasantries were considered a form of theft.

"You look like a woman who read my note," Brannic said. He slid a cup toward her. "Beer?"

"I read your note. I'm here to say no."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"The terms are triple rate and a bonus, and the cargo breathes, and you're asking me to take it north through Thornfell in the same season the crown's witch-hunters are sweeping every pass like lice through a wool shirt. Those aren't terms, Brannic. That's a eulogy with numbers attached."

Something moved behind his beard that might, on a younger man, have been a wince. "You've seen the proclamation."

"I've seen six of them. They nail them up crooked, which offends me more than the content."

"They're not hunting witches." He said it quietly, leaning in, and the lamplight caught the grey in his eyes. "Not really. You mark any knots lately? Any trail-signs?"

Oscar's spine went cold. "What kind of question is that?"

"The kind a man asks who's watched hunters take apart a hedgerow's fence-posts looking for charmwork. They're not burning hedge-witches, girl. Hedge-witches are beneath them this season. They're *collecting*. Something about the gifted being wanted whole." He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the table, a habit he had when he was saying things he hated. "Which makes your little knotted secrets worth more than you think, and worth less than you hope."

"And you know this how?"

"I know it the way I know most things. Somebody paid me to move something, and the something scared me." He met her eyes. "Seven of them. Fugitives off the coast road. Farmers' kids, a healer, an old soldier, twin girls, and—" he paused, and the pause was long enough that Oscar felt her stomach begin its slow descent, "—a boy."

"A boy."

"Mute. Ten years old, maybe eleven. Doesn't speak, doesn't write, doesn't cry, which is the part that keeps me up at night. Ten years old and he doesn't cry, Oscar. Children cry. It's the whole point of them."

"What's wrong with him?"

Brannic looked at her the way men look when they're deciding how much truth a person can carry without spilling. Then he pulled back his sleeve — no. Not his sleeve. He reached into the satchel beside his chair and drew out a strip of linen, folded small, and laid it on the table between them like a man laying down a card he already knew lost.

"Unfold it," he said. "Slowly."

Oscar unfolded it. Inside, wrapped in the linen, was a scrap of bandage, bloodied at one edge, and printed on the bloodied part — no, not printed, *drawn*, though no hand had drawn it — was a mark in black ink so deep it looked wet. A sigil. Lines within lines, a knot of strokes that seemed, when she looked away and looked back, to have rearranged themselves slightly, the way clouds do, the way nothing drawn should.

It hummed.

Not a sound, exactly. More a feeling in her back teeth, in the fillings of the room, in the lantern flame, which bent very slightly toward it, the way grass bends toward a stone rolled aside.

Oscar put the linen down and pushed it away with one finger, gently, the way you'd move a sleeping snake. "What in the nine hells is that."

"That's a piece of the boy's arm. He was cut — grazed, in a scuffle at the last safehouse, some fool panicked with a knife. We bandaged it. The bandage kept humming, so we stopped using bandages." Brannic took it back and folded it away with the tenderness most men reserve for letters from the dead. "The mark's on his forearm. It moves. I've watched it crawl two inches in a night, like it's looking for something. And when the hunters come near — and they've come near twice — it hums louder. Loudest thing I never heard."

The tavern noise went on around them — dice, argument, a fiddle missing half its strings — and Oscar sat inside a small cold silence of her own manufacture.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"No. Brannic. No. Do you know what that is? What it might be? Because I don't, and I've read Maren's whole library, or what's left of it after—" She stopped. The grief for Maren was still new enough to have edges. Three weeks since she'd found the village burned, since she'd held her mistress's hand while the life went out of it, since the map fragment and the dying words — *the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon, find it, keep it safe* — and she still couldn't say the old woman's name out loud without her throat doing something traitorous. "That mark is older than anything I know. Older than the crown, maybe. And you want to walk it through the mountains during a hunt, with seven fugitives and a mute child, on roads I swore I'd never use again."

"Yes," said Brannic.

"Why?"

He was quiet for a moment. Around them the fiddle sawed on, cheerfully out of tune. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he used for weights and distances — the voice of a man stating facts he wished were otherwise.

"Because the people who want that boy don't want to kill him. I told you — they collect. And because I owe a debt to someone who asked me to move him, and I pay my debts. And because—" he stopped himself, jaw working, and Oscar watched a man decide against a story. "And because triple rate," he finished, lamely, and both of them knew it wasn't the reason at all.

"Who do you owe?"

"Someone who saved my life a long time ago and has never once let me forget the interest." He pushed a purse across the table. It clinked with the heavy, honest sound of real silver, not clipped coppers. "Half now. Half at the far end. Bonus on delivery, as discussed."

Oscar didn't touch it. "I want to see them first. All seven. Before I say yes or no, I see them, and I talk to them, and if any of them says no to me, the answer's no."

"They can't exactly—"

"*Talk*, Brannic. Talk comes in more shapes than words. I've guided mules for six years; I know a creature that's willing from one that's merely obedient, and I will not be party to hauling human beings north like kegs of brandy." She heard her own voice rise and dragged it back down. "I'm not a slaver. Whatever else I am, whatever else I've done, I'm not that."

Brannic studied her for a long moment. Something in his face shifted — not softening, exactly. More like a door opening onto a room where a lamp was lit.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Dawn. The quarry road, old kiln above the millpond. Bring your own horse. Don't bring the goat."

"How do you know about the goat?"

"Everyone knows about the goat, Oscar. You tell the goat everything. He's the worst-kept secret in the county."

* * *

The old kiln above the millpond smelled of cold lime and older smoke, and the seven of them were waiting inside it like coins in a closed fist.

Oscar counted them before she let herself feel anything: seven, as promised. An old soldier with a soldier's posture ruined by a bad hip, standing anyway, because some men stand even when sitting is offered. A healer — young, freckled, with the capable hands of someone who'd set a hundred bones and apologized to each one. Twin sisters, nineteen or twenty, holding hands with the fierce unconscious grip of people who had decided, jointly and permanently, that separation was death. Two farm brothers, wide-shouldered and terrified, who flinched when she came through the kiln mouth and then hated themselves for flinching.

And the boy.

He sat apart from the others, the way objects sit apart, and Oscar understood immediately why nobody had grouped him in. He was small and dark-haired and ordinary in every feature except his eyes, which held the particular stillness of water over a deep place. His left sleeve was pinned and bound at the wrist. As she approached, the binding gave off a faint vibration she felt in her molars before she heard it — a hum, low and patient, like a struck bell that had forgotten to finish ringing.

The hum rose as she got closer. Rose, and steadied, and — she would swear this later, under oath if it ever came to that — softened. Like a dog deciding whether to growl.

Oscar stopped an arm's length away and crouched, putting her eyes level with his, the way you'd approach a frightened animal or a nobleman's daughter: slowly, and below their eyeline.

"Hello," she said. "My name's Oscar. I'm going to be honest with you, because I think you've had enough of people being otherwise."

The boy watched her. The hum held its note.

"There's a run north. High passes, hard roads, hunters on the low routes. I'd be guiding. Seven of you, mule-train pace, cold camps. I get paid for it, and I won't pretend otherwise, because pretending otherwise is how guides start thinking of passengers as cargo, and I refuse." She paused. "But here's the true thing underneath the true thing: I don't want to do this. I want to go home, bolt my door, and be a coward in comfort. So I need you to give me a reason. Can you do that?"

The boy tilted his head. Then, deliberately, he pulled the pin from his sleeve.

The twins gasped. One of the brothers said, "Boy, don't—" and the healer put a hand on his arm.

The boy unwound the binding, fold by fold, and showed Oscar his forearm.

The sigil lay along it from wrist to elbow, black as well-water, and it was moving — not crawling, not now, but shifting the way a shoal of fish shifts, a thousand tiny adjustments that added up to a pattern that refused to hold still. Up close, the hum resolved into something almost like language, almost like a song heard through a wall, and Oscar felt the hair on her arms lift and her grandmother's oldest superstition wake up in her chest and say *run*.

She didn't run. She looked at it. Really looked, the way Maren had taught her to look at anything strange: *fear closes the eye, Oscar; wonder opens it, and only the open eye learns.*

And in the shifting of the marks, for half a heartbeat, she saw — or thought she saw — a shape. Mountains. A hollow between them. Thirteen moons crowded into a sky that should hold one.

The map fragment in her coat pocket, the one Maren had died pressing into her hands, seemed suddenly warm against her ribs.

The boy watched her not-flinch, and something in his stillness changed — a loosening, barely visible, the way ice shifts at thaw. He raised his right hand, slow, and touched two fingers to his own chest. Then he pointed at her.

*I trust you,* said the gesture, in the wordless grammar children invent when the world takes their words away.

Or possibly: *you're the one.* With gestures, Oscar was learning, the freight mattered more than the phrasing.

She exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding since the Drowned Ox. "Well," she said, and stood, knees cracking. "Damn it all."

She walked back to Brannic, who waited by the horses wearing the expression of a man who has just watched a fisherman hook himself.

"Terms," she said. "Quadruple rate."

Brannic's eyebrows climbed. "You said triple was robbery."

"It was. Then I saw the boy's arm, and now I'm pricing in the nightmares." She held out her hand. "And one more thing. If it goes wrong — if we're cornered and it's the boy or the rest of us — I decide. Not you, not the soldiers among us, not anybody with a knife and an opinion. Me. I'm the guide. On my roads, I choose what gets carried and what gets dropped, and I'll tell you now so there's no surprise later: I don't drop children."

Brannic took her hand. His grip was mooring-rope all the way through. "Agreed," he said, and then, quieter, with something that in another man might have been shame: "I'd have agreed to that anyway. Just so you know. I lost—" He stopped. Started again. "There are things I don't talk about, Oscar. Ask me at the far end, if we get there."

"Everybody's got a far end," Oscar said. "That's the trouble with roads."

They shook once more, and it was done — the coin in her pocket, the seven souls in the kiln, the humming thing asleep against a ten-year-old's bone. Complicity, she thought, walking back to saddle her mare in the grey dawn light, turns out not to arrive like a thief in the night. It arrives like a customer. Polite. Counting out silver. Asking only that you hand over the thing you were always going to hand over, and thanking you kindly for your service.

Behind her, faint as a thought, the sigil hummed — and the sound, she noticed, was pointed north. Toward Thornfell. Toward the Hollow Maren had named with her last breath.

Toward home, if she was honest, though she'd sworn never to use that word for those passes again.

Oscar swung into the saddle and did not look back at the kiln, because looking back, Maren used to say, is how you find out whether your courage held — and some mornings it's kinder not to check.
