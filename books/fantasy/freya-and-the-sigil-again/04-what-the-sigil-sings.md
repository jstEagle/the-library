## 4. What the Sigil Sings

Tam counted the world in sounds.

The creak of the wagon's third axle, which was older than the other two and complained about it. The wet snore of Doss in the back corner, sleeping like a man who had made peace with every hard surface in the world. The whisper of Brannic Ferro's ledger pages turning up front, because the smuggler checked his numbers even when the numbers were people.

And underneath all of it, faint as a fly trapped behind a wall: the humming.

Tam pressed his palm flat against his forearm, where the black mark curled beneath his sleeve. The sigil hummed when it was happy, which it almost never was. Tonight it hummed low and slow, the way a kettle hums before it decides to scream.

*Soon*, it seemed to say. *Soon, soon.*

He didn't answer it. He never answered it. Answering was how you ended up talking to it all day, and then it wasn't a mark on your skin anymore, it was a roommate, and roommates wanted things.

The wagon jolted over a root. Wren, the healer, stirred beside him and pulled the blanket higher over his shoulders without waking. That was one of the good sounds too — the small rustle of someone choosing to be kind while unconscious. Tam filed it away with the others, in the place where he kept everything he couldn't say out loud.

Because that was the other thing about Tam. He had a whole voice. He'd had it once, anyway, back before the men with the white hoods came to his mother's door, back before the night he could not think about without the sigil flaring hot enough to blister. Somewhere between that door and this wagon, the words had gone into hiding. They were still in there. He could feel them, sitting in his chest like coins at the bottom of a well. But every time he reached for them, the water closed over his hand.

So he listened instead. And the sigil, being what it was, listened with him.

*Thirteen*, it murmured, in its wordless way. Not words — Tam got pictures, mostly, and feelings, and occasionally something so large it took him hours to understand he'd been shown it at all. *Thirteen, thirteen.* An image unfolded behind his eyes: thirteen moons in a ring around a black sky, and beneath them, far beneath, a shape like a man if a man were made of mountains and had been folded up wrong.

Tam squeezed his eyes shut. *No,* he told it. *Not tonight.*

It showed him anyway. It always showed him anyway.

A god, buried. Chains of oath-stone wrapped around it, each link carved with a name of someone who had sworn to keep it buried, and every name worn smooth now, half-erased, like steps trodden by too many feet. The chains did not look cruel. That was the worst part. They looked careful. They looked like bandages.

And threaded through every chain, thin as wire, bright as frost: the sigil. His sigil. The last knot left untied.

*I'm not a key,* Tam thought at it, fiercely. *I'm a boy. I like plums. I can whistle through my hands.*

The sigil pulsed, amused, and the image dissolved into ordinary dark.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, smelling of pine and woodsmoke. Tam climbed down from the wagon and stood in the frost with his breath steaming, doing his morning work: checking the edges of himself, making sure nothing had leaked in the night.

That was the thing nobody understood about carrying a living seal. It wasn't like having a scar or a secret. It was like holding a door shut from the inside, forever, with your whole body. Some mornings his arms shook with it before breakfast.

He heard the argument before he saw it.

They'd gathered around the cold fire pit — Brannic with his arms crossed, Wren standing the way she stood when she was about to be right at somebody, the twins huddled together under one cloak, old Doss sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. And Oscar Oyelaran, the guide, sitting on an overturned bucket with her boots apart and her face doing something complicated.

"—saying we should turn around," Brannic was saying. "I'm saying we should consider turning around."

"You're saying we should leave the boy," Wren said.

"I'm saying," Brannic said, with the patience of a man walking across ice he personally knew was thin, "that every hour that boy is with us, the hunters get a clearer line on us. You felt it at the toll bridge. The stones knew him. The stones *answered* him. Whatever he's carrying, it isn't quiet, and I move cargo for a living, and quiet is the entire business."

"He's not cargo, Brannic."

"No," Brannic agreed. "Cargo doesn't make my ledger sweat."

Tam stood very still at the edge of the camp. This was another thing he was good at: being furniture. He'd had three years of practice. People said things in front of furniture.

"Where would he even go?" one of the twins asked — Sefla, the bolder one, though Tam could never fully tell them apart and suspected that was part of their armor. "There's nothing behind us but burned villages."

"There's nothing ahead of us but worse," Brannic said. "You've seen his arm."

Everyone had seen his arm. In the night, sometimes, the black lines crept past his sleeve, up toward his elbow, delicate as frost ferns, and they moved, slowly, the way ivy moves if you're patient enough to watch. Wren had salved it. The salve helped the ache but not the spread.

"The hunters aren't chasing us," Doss said suddenly, from behind his knife. Everyone looked at him; Doss spoke maybe four times a week and hoarded his words like a miser. "They're *herding*. Think about it. Every road we've taken, they were already there first. Waiting. Funneling." He tapped the knife against the whetstone, once. "Somebody wants this boy somewhere specific. Question is whether we walk him there ourselves."

Silence. Even the fire pit smoke seemed to lean in.

"That's mad," Sefla said.

"That's tactics," Doss said. "Thirty years of them. I don't like the shape of this road any more than you do."

Brannic rubbed his jaw. "Then what's your recommendation, soldier? We've got seven souls, two wagons, and a boy who glows in the dark for anyone with the sense to look."

Doss looked at Tam. Directly at him, the way almost nobody did — most people looked near him, or through him, or at the arm. Doss looked at him like he was a person who might have an opinion.

"Well?" Doss said.

Tam's throat closed. The coins sat at the bottom of the well. He opened his mouth, and what came out was nothing, the same nothing as always, and he watched the sympathy move around the circle like a passed dish, and hated it.

But Oscar was watching him differently. She wasn't passing him sympathy. She was waiting. Like she genuinely believed something was coming.

*Later,* she mouthed, so small only he caught it. *Tell me later. Any way you like.*

* * *

She found him at noon, or he let her find him — by then the difference hardly mattered. She came down the trail from scouting the pass with frost in her red hair and sat herself on the rock beside his without asking, which was, Tam had decided, the single greatest talent a person could possess.

Oscar Oyelaran didn't talk at him the way people talked at mute children, all loud and slow, like deafness and silence were cousins. She just talked. About the trail. About a hawk she'd seen. About how Brannic's third axle was going to give out within the week and she was going to enjoy saying *told you so*.

Then she unrolled a strip of birch bark on the rock between them and set a piece of charcoal on it.

"For maps," she said. "In case you want to draw me something. Or write. Or throw it at my head. All acceptable."

Tam looked at the charcoal. Then at her.

Here was the thing about Oscar that Tam had figured out in the first two days and confirmed every day since: she wasn't afraid of him. Not of the arm, not of the silence, not of whatever the hunters wanted with him. Oh, she was afraid plenty — he could see it living in her shoulders, in the way her eyes kept moving to the ridgelines — but none of her fear pointed at him. It pointed outward, at the world, like a shield held in front of them both.

People flinched from Tam. His mother hadn't, at the end, but everyone else had — the neighbors, the priests, the kind woman who'd hidden him in a root cellar for a month and then cried the whole time she prayed over him. Flinching was the normal response. It was practically polite.

Oscar had looked at the sigil the first night, when his sleeve had slipped, and her face had done something Tam had never seen anyone's face do. It had gone soft. Not pity-soft. *Recognition*-soft. Like she'd seen a knot she knew how to tie.

Now she waited, patient as stone, while he picked up the charcoal.

His hand hovered. Where would he even start? *Hello, I am a door*? *There is a god under the mountains and it dreams in my direction*?

He started with the moons. Thirteen circles, in a ring. Then, inside the ring, the folded-up mountain shape. Then the chains — he drew them carefully, each link, and the worn-smooth names on them, or the idea of them, since he didn't know the names, only the feeling of them, like letters rubbed away by centuries of thumbs.

Oscar leaned over the bark. Her breath went shallow.

"That's the Thornfell range," she said quietly. "The shape. That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Tam. Is this a memory or a dream?"

He hesitated. Then he drew a small figure — stick arms, stick legs — standing inside the ring of moons, and put the sigil on its arm, and drew a line from the sigil to the chains, and then crossed the line out. Twice. Hard enough to nearly tear the bark.

*Not me. I'm not opening anything.*

Oscar read it the way she read trails, he realized — quickly, wholly, missing nothing. He watched her arrive at the understanding and watched her decide, visibly, not to be frightened of it. It was like watching someone swallow a large fish whole and keep smiling.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. So you're not a key. You're..." She frowned at the drawing. "You're the last knot. And knots can hold things together or they can hold things shut, and either way, everybody wants to own the hands that tied them."

Tam stared at her. Then he drew a small star next to her head, which was the closest his vocabulary of pictures came to applause.

She huffed a laugh. "Don't flatter me. I'm a mule-train guide with a criminal aptitude for knotwork, not a scholar." But she was pleased; he could hear it under the dryness. "Here's what matters. Doss is right — they're herding us. Which means the smartest thing we can do is stop being herdable. Which means I need to know everything that mark knows. Not because I'll use it against you." She held up her hand, solemn as an oath. "Because nobody should carry a map alone. Maps are heavy. Believe me. I'm hauling several."

Tam looked at her for a long moment.

Then, slowly, he set his bare forearm on the rock between them, sleeve pushed up, sigil exposed to the daylight where it shifted and breathed like something asleep that was not entirely asleep.

Most people, presented with the sigil, went pale. Went backward. Went for priests.

Oscar leaned in and squinted at it like a buyer judging cloth.

"Huh," she said. "It's warmer than the rest of you." And then, before he could react, she laid two fingers lightly against the outermost line — the way you'd touch a horse's neck to say *easy, easy* — and the sigil *hummed*.

Not the warning-hum. Not the kettle-scream. Something lower. Almost a purr. Tam's whole arm went loose with shock; it had never done that for anyone. It had barely ever done that for *him*.

"Oh," Oscar said softly. "Oh, hello."

The images came then — Tam felt them rise like bubbles through deep water, and he braced to be flooded, to be shown the buried god and the folding mountains and the thirteen moons all at once, the full weight of it—

But it didn't come to him. It went to *her*, gently, a thread of light between her fingertips and the mark, and Oscar's eyes went wide and distant for exactly three heartbeats, and then she blinked and sat back and looked slightly seasick and extremely thoughtful.

"Well," she said. "That's going to take some thinking about."

Tam grabbed the charcoal and wrote, fast and jagged: **WHAT DID IT SHOW YOU**

"A door," Oscar said. "Under a mountain. With thirteen locks. And—" she paused, and Tam watched her choose honesty over comfort, watched her do it deliberately, the way she chose everything, "—and it showed me that the locks are almost all open already. Not broken. *Worn*. Like they were meant to be tended, and nobody tended them, for a very long time." She met his eyes. "Except yours. Yours is still tied tight. You're not the key, Tam. You're the last person still doing the job."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere up the pass, a hawk screamed once and quit.

Tam looked down at his arm — at the strange, calm, purring dark of it — and felt something crack open in his chest that was not the seal. It was smaller than the seal and much older. Three years of root cellars and flinching strangers and passed dishes of sympathy, and here was one red-haired runaway guide with frost in her hair, touching the worst thing about him and calling it a job, calling it *his*, calling it worth sharing the weight of.

The coins rose in the well. He reached for them.

What came out was not a word. It was a sound — rough, cracked, rusty as a gate in winter — half syllable, half sob. But it was *out*. The first thing out of his chest in three years, offered to the gray sky and the cold rock and this woman who didn't flinch.

Oscar didn't make a fuss. That was the miracle of her. She just put her hand on his shoulder, brief and solid, and said, "There he is," as if she'd known all along he was in there and had simply been waiting politely by the door.

From up the slope, Brannic Ferro's voice came rolling down like weather: "Guide! If you're done communing with the merchandise, we've got a problem. Riders on the north ridge. Four of them. Gray cloaks."

Oscar was on her feet before the sentence finished, charcoal forgotten, birch-bark map snapped up and tucked into her coat. But she looked back at Tam once, and tapped her own forearm — *same place, same time tomorrow* — and the look she gave him was not the look people gave the boy who carried the god's door.

It was the look you gave family.

Tam pulled his sleeve down over the humming mark and followed her up the slope, and for the first time in three years, the silence inside him felt less like a prison and more like a held breath.

Something was coming. The sigil sang it low under his skin, thirteen times over, like a bell counting down:

*Soon. Soon. Soon.*
