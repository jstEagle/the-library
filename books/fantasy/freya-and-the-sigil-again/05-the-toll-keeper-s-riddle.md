## 5. The Toll-Keeper's Riddle

The bridge announced itself an hour before they saw it, the way bad weather and worse relatives do—by a change in the air.

Oscar felt it first in her boots: a faint tremor through the stone of the switchback trail, rhythmic as a heartbeat. Then Brannic Ferro, walking point with his ledger strapped to his back like a shield, held up one fist and said, without turning around, "Toll-bridge."

"You didn't mention a toll," said Wren, adjusting the strap of her healer's satchel. She had a way of speaking that made even complaints sound like diagnoses.

"I mentioned *costs*," Brannic said. "Costs is a broad category."

Oscar came up beside him at the bend where the gorge opened below them like a throat. The Greywater ran three hundred feet down, white and furious, and across it stretched the only crossing for forty miles in either direction: a span of grey mountain stone, old as anything, arched so high and thin it looked less built than grown. At its near end stood a hut of stacked slate with a chimney smoking cheerfully against all reason, and before the hut stood a figure wrapped in a coat made, apparently, of several other coats.

"That's Old Merrow," Oscar said. Her stomach did something complicated. "She's been here since before I was born. My mistress used to trade her dried fish for stories."

"Stories," Brannic repeated flatly.

"Maren called them tolls." Oscar watched the bundled figure raise one arm in greeting—or warning; with Merrow the two had always shared a border. "You pay in truths. True things, spoken aloud, that you'd rather keep. The bridge decides if they're worth enough."

"The bridge *decides*."

"It's not an ordinary bridge, Brannic."

"No," he agreed grimly. "Nothing on this road is ordinary. That's why I'm paid the big coin." He glanced back at the caravan strung out behind them on the narrow path: Wren; Doss the old soldier moving with his bad knee favoring the downhill side; the twins, Sef and Ilka, walking shoulder to shoulder like two halves of one thought; a mule named Duchess who had opinions; and Tam, small and silent at the middle of everything, his sleeve pulled down over his forearm out of habit that had never once worked.

Tam was looking at the bridge. His head was tilted, the way he tilted it when listening to something no one else could hear.

Oscar had learned to read that tilt over the past days, and she didn't like what it usually meant.

* * *

Merrow received them at the bridge-head with the ceremony of a queen receiving ambassadors, which is to say she looked them up and down, sniffed twice, and said, "Seven souls, one mule, one smuggler, and—" her clouded eyes found Tam and stayed there, "—one riddle wearing boots."

"He doesn't talk," Sef said quickly.

"Everything talks," Merrow said. "Some things just do it quietly." She turned to Oscar, and something flickered under the fog of her gaze—recognition, maybe, or its ghost. "Hazel Halloran's girl. Grown. I'd know those knotwork trail-marks anywhere. You've been leaving your little woven secrets along my road for three days. Sloppy ones. Guilt makes the hands hurry."

Oscar's face went hot. Three years of hiding, and the first person to see through her was a half-mad toll-keeper in a coat of coats.

"Rules are rules," Merrow went on, hobbling to the bridge's first stone and laying one mittened hand upon it fondly, the way you'd touch a horse. "One truth per soul, spoken standing on the span. The bridge weighs it. If it's light—if it's a pebble instead of a stone—the bridge knows. And the bridge," she added, patting the parapet, "gets *tetchy* about being cheated. Last fellow who lied to it is still down there somewhere. Fish talk about him. Politely, but still."

Doss snorted. "And if we refuse?"

"Then you climb the Serpent's Stair around the north face. Four days. In this season." Merrow smiled, showing three teeth and tremendous satisfaction. "Or you pay."

Brannic was already doing sums; Oscar could see the abacus working behind his eyes. Coin meant nothing here—that was the trap and the mercy of it. Truths were the one currency none of them had any surplus of.

"I'll go first," Oscar said. "Set the price fair."

She stepped onto the bridge. The stone was warm under her boots, absurdly warm, like standing on a sleeping animal. Somewhere beneath her feet she felt the slow pulse again, stronger now, attentive.

She had planned to give a true thing. A real one. But then she looked back and saw them all watching—Wren with her steady eyes, Doss with his soldier's patience, the twins holding hands without seeming to notice they were doing it—and the old reflex moved faster than honesty ever could.

"My name is Oscar Oyelaran," she said clearly, "and I am a mule-train guide."

It was true. It was also nothing. It was a coin rubbed smooth of every face but the profile.

For one heartbeat, the bridge seemed to consider it.

Then the warmth under her boots vanished, all at once, like a door closing, and the whole span *shuddered*—a long, muscular ripple running from bank to bank, stones grinding against stones, the parapet flexing under her hand like the rib of something breathing hard. Below, the Greywater roared louder, or perhaps the world simply got quieter around its noise.

"*Girl,*" Merrow said sadly, from the far shore of her own patience, "you tried to pay for a bridge with a button."

The second shudder threw Oscar to her knees. A crack spidered out from under her boot, fine as hair, then not fine at all. Behind her she heard shouting—Brannic bellowing for everyone to get *off*, get *back*—and the twins screaming, and Duchess the mule expressing herself at length.

And through it all, through the grinding and the roar, Oscar heard something else.

A hum. Low, rising, coming from behind her—from Tam.

He had stepped onto the bridge. Nobody had told him to. He walked past her kneeling form with his sleeve already pushed back, and there on his forearm the sigil was moving—not crawling, not spreading, just *turning*, slowly, like a lock remembering its key—and it was singing. Not sound exactly. More like the feeling of a struck bell heard through the floor, through the bones, through the soles of the boots. The black lines pulsed once, twice, in time with the bridge's own trembling heartbeat.

And the bridge *stilled*.

Not gradually—all at once, the way a startled animal goes quiet. The crack under Oscar's knee stopped growing. The stones settled against each other with a long sigh, like a dog lying back down. The warmth returned, deeper than before, and Oscar could have sworn the whole span leaned toward the boy, the way a crowd leans toward a fire.

Tam crouched, laid his bare palm flat on the stone, and stood there while the sigil hummed its low note into the mountain.

When he looked up at Oscar, his expression wasn't frightened. It was apologetic. As if the bridge's tantrum were somehow his manners to answer for.

"Oh," said Merrow softly, from her hut doorway. "*That's* what's been humming up my valley for a fortnight. I thought it was the mine workings." Her clouded eyes had gone strangely clear. "No wonder the stones like him. Old things know their kin."

* * *

They crossed in single file after that, and the bridge carried each of them gently, like a mother moving a sleeping child—but the mood on the far side was a different country entirely.

Oscar paid her truth properly, kneeling on the span with her palms flat where Tam's had been, and gave the bridge the thing she'd flinched from giving: *"I can do magic, and I have spent three years pretending I can't, and I am tired, and I don't know how to stop."* The stones warmed under her hands like approval. It felt awful and wonderful, like lancing a boil.

Wren paid with the name of the patient she'd lost and couldn't save. Doss paid with the order he'd obeyed and should have refused, thirty years ago, in a war nobody sang about. Sef paid with a confession about Ilka's registration papers that made Ilka go white and then, oddly, hold her sister tighter. Brannic paid last, grumbling, with a truth so curt the bridge actually rocked him once, reproachfully, until he sighed and added, quieter, a sentence about a daughter and a winter and a door he hadn't opened in time. After that the bridge let him pass like an honored guest, and Brannic stared straight ahead the entire way and dared anyone to comment.

Nobody commented. That was the strange grace of truths: once paid, they belonged to everyone, and everyone pretended they didn't.

But Tam paid nothing, because when his turn came, Merrow waved him across with her mittened hand and said, "His toll's already been collected. Bridges remember who holds them up." And the sigil hummed its low note the whole way over, and the stones sang back, faintly, like a choir finding a pitch.

Which left them all, that evening, in a hollow below the tree line with a fire going and supper bubbling, sitting in a circle that had a boy in the middle of it who could steady mountains—and everyone knowing it now, and nobody having said it yet.

Oscar couldn't stand it by the third hour.

"All right," she said, setting down her bowl. "We're going to talk about it, because not talking about it is how bridges nearly drop us into gorges. Secrets have weight. We all just watched the proof."

"Careful," Brannic murmured. "You're describing my profession."

"Your profession nearly died today because *I* lied to a rock." She looked around the fire, at each face. "So. Tam carries something. We've known since the moor that hunters want him specifically—we just didn't know why. Now we know it's bound up in whatever that mark is. And I think—I *think*—that it's why Varga's riders keep finding us when we've done everything right." She hesitated. "I think he hums loud enough for the wrong ears."

Silence. The fire cracked. Duchess, tied nearby, made a sound of profound agreement.

It was Doss who spoke first, in his gravel way, staring into the flames. "On the bridge. When it shook. I've stood on collapsing walls in a siege, and that was the same sound." He looked at Tam. "You stopped it. Didn't you, lad."

Tam nodded.

"And it cost you." Doss nodded at the boy's arm, where the sigil's lines had gone dull and greyish, like a bruise fading wrong. Tam's other hand was pressed over it, and there was a fine sweat on his forehead despite the cold.

Tam considered the question honestly, the way he considered everything. Then he shrugged—one shoulder, noncommittal—and reached for the stick Wren kept by the fire for exactly this purpose, and wrote in the ash-dust between them:

*A little.*

"Liar," Wren said gently, and Tam's mouth twitched, which for Tam was a full laugh.

"We're not giving him up," Sef said suddenly, fiercely, to the group at large, though her eyes were on her sister. "Whatever anyone's thinking. We're not."

"I wasn't thinking it," Ilka said quietly.

"I was thinking it," Brannic admitted, and the whole circle turned on him, and he raised both hands. "I was thinking it for about four seconds on the bridge, while the rocks were trying to kill us, and then the boy put his hand down and the rocks stopped, and I stopped too. That's the whole confession. I'm a smuggler, not a saint. But I keep my cargo." He jabbed a finger at Tam. "Especially cargo that holds up bridges."

Something in Oscar's chest unclenched a notch. She looked at Tam—at the sigil, dull and spent on his arm—and made herself say the harder thing.

"There's more, isn't there. On the bridge, when it was humming—you weren't scared. You were *listening*. Same look you get some nights, staring east." She kept her voice soft. "What does it tell you, Tam? What does the mark sing about?"

Tam looked into the fire for a long moment. Then he took the stick and wrote, slowly, pressing hard enough to carve:

*Thirteen moons.*

He paused. Wrote again:

*It's counting.*

Oscar felt the words land in her belly like swallowed stones. Thirteen moons. Maren's map fragment, tucked inside her shirt against her skin these many days, had a border of little crescents—thirteen of them, she'd counted once and thought nothing of it. Thirteenth Moon. The Hollow of—

She didn't finish the thought. Some thoughts you carry to the fire and burn.

"Counting to what?" Wren asked.

Tam looked at her. Looked at Oscar. And for the first time since she'd known him, the boy's face showed something close to fear—not for himself, she realized. For whoever the counting was *for*.

He wiped the ash smooth with his palm and wrote one word:

*Soon.*

Far off to the north, beyond the dark shoulders of the passes, thunder rolled—or something very like thunder, with a rhythm to it, a pulse, like a heartbeat three hundred miles wide.

Old Merrow had said the stones liked him. Old things knew their kin.

Oscar sat very still, and listened to the mountain listen back.
