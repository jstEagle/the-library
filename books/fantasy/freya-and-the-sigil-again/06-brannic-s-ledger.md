## 6. Brannic's Ledger

Brannic Ferro kept his accounts the way he kept his mules: honestly, in his own hand, and with a firm belief that anything that couldn't be written down was probably a lie.

The ledger lived in the breast pocket of his coat, bound in oiled leather gone soft as an old dog's ear. It had three columns. *Owed to me*, *owed by me*, and — squeezed into the margin, in smaller script, because he'd never found room for it anywhere else — *owed to no one, which is to say, owed to the dead*.

Most men's ledgers balanced. Brannic's never had. He'd made peace with that around the same time he'd made peace with most of the other disappointments of his fifty-one years: bad knees, worse weather, and a crown that had decided, sometime in the last season, that people like the ones he carried were cargo after all.

He sat now in the taproom of the Drowned Gull, a tavern so far down the coast that even the gulls seemed embarrassed by it, turning the ledger over in his hands without opening it. Across the table, a woman in a grey traveling cloak was waiting for him to say yes.

"You're not listening," she said.

"I'm listening fine," Brannic said. "I'm just not agreeing yet. There's a difference. Most folk don't know it."

"Seven passengers. Salt Caves road, then up through the Thornfell passes."

"That's not a road, that's a rumor with rocks in it."

"You've run it."

"I've run it *once*," Brannic said, "and I was younger, drunker, and being chased, which improves anyone's judgment." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit his wife had hated and his daughter had found hilarious. "You want me to move seven people who can't be moved, along roads that don't exist, past hunters who are paid to exist very loudly. And you're paying me in what, exactly? Gratitude?"

"In trust," the woman said.

Brannic laughed, short and ugly. "Trust doesn't feed mules."

"No," she agreed. "But it keeps you from becoming the kind of man who counts only coin."

She rose, left a silver mark under her cup — too much, deliberately, he was sure — and paused at the door.

"The boy travels first, if you take them at all. The mute one. Whatever you hear about him, whatever you see on his arm — remember that he is seven years old before he is anything else."

Then she was gone into the rain, and Brannic Ferro sat alone with his ledger and the terrible, familiar feeling of a debt coming due.

* * *

Three days later, sober and still furious about it, he opened the ledger to the third column.

He didn't go there often. A sensible man avoided the margins of his own book.

*Owed to no one.*

The entry was twenty-two years old and written in a younger man's hand, steadier than the one that held the pen now.

*Bell. Age nine. Taken at the Ashford registration. Released to the Crown school at Vaelmoor. Visited twice. Third visit refused. Fourth visit —*

He'd never finished the fourth line. Some entries you just couldn't close out.

Bell had been his daughter. Not by blood — by choice, which he'd always maintained was the stronger kind. She'd come up the coast with a tinker caravan the summer Brannic lost his wife, a skinny girl of six with a stammer and a habit of naming every mule after saints, including the ones that didn't deserve it. She'd stayed. That was the whole story, really. She came, and she stayed, and for three years the ledger's second column had been nearly empty because Brannic Ferro, who owed everyone, felt owed by nothing for the first time in his life.

Then came the first hunt. The registrations. The polite men with their polite lists, asking which children showed "sensitivities."

Bell could make flowers open out of season. That was all. Flowers. Out of season. A crocus in November, once, held in her cupped hands like something stolen from a richer world.

They took her for a crocus.

Brannic had spent two years and most of his savings trying to reach her at Vaelmoor. Bribery, petitions, a forged letter of kinship that fooled nobody. On the fourth attempt a clerk had looked at him with genuine pity and said, "Master Ferro, the gifted don't write home. It's kinder for everyone."

Kinder. Twenty-two years, and the word still tasted like copper.

He'd gotten one letter. One. Bell's hand, shakier than he remembered, describing the school gardens and a friend named Liss and how the bread was better than expected. No flowers mentioned. No crocus. As if they'd wrung it out of her, or she'd learned to hide it even on paper, even from him.

The letter stopped. The school said she'd been "transferred." Transferred where, to whom, for what — the clerk had spread his hands, helpless, useless, paid either way.

So Brannic Ferro did what hard men do when grief has nowhere to go. He put it in the third column and got back to work. He ran cargo. He asked no questions about passengers, because passengers had faces and cargo had weight, and weight he could carry. For twenty years he'd been the best smuggler on the coast precisely because he'd trained himself not to look at what he smuggled.

And then a woman in a grey cloak had walked into the Drowned Gull and said *the boy travels first*, and Brannic had heard his own heart do something it hadn't done in two decades: it had knocked.

* * *

The Hollow of Thirteenth Moon. That was the name she'd finally given him, when he'd cornered her on the second visit and demanded a destination worth dying for.

"You won't find it on any map," she'd said.

"I don't use maps. Maps are for people with something to lose."

"And you don't?"

"I've got mules," Brannic said. "Mules are replaceable. It's one of their finest qualities."

But she'd seen through him — grey-cloaked women always could, in his experience; it was why he avoided churches, funerals, and mothers — and she'd told him the truth instead. Not the location. The debt.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "a smuggler named Ferro ran a load of contraband through the Salt Caves road in a storm. Crown riders caught him at the mouth of the pass. They would have hanged him, except someone reached the riders first — someone who convinced them the cargo belonged to a border lord with more soldiers than patience. The riders withdrew. The smuggler lived."

Brannic stared at her. "That wasn't me. I was a boy twenty-two years ago."

"Yes," she said. "You were. And the man who saved you was my teacher, and the Hollow remembers its debts the way you remember yours — in writing, in blood, and forever. He asked nothing then. He asks this now. Seven passengers. The boy first."

It was obscene, really, how fast a life reorganized itself around a single sentence. Brannic had walked back to the Drowned Gull in the rain, sat down, opened the ledger, and added a new line to column two:

*One life, returned. Interest: twenty-two years, compounding.*

He'd have taken the job anyway. That was the humiliating part. Grey-cloak hadn't needed the debt; she'd needed him to stop pretending the debt was the reason. Because the real reason was sitting in the third column, age nine, unfinished, and Brannic Ferro had spent twenty-two years learning exactly what the crown did with children who could make flowers open out of season.

This time, he thought, staring at the ceiling of his bunk while the Drowned Gull creaked around him, this time somebody would be standing between the child and the list. Somebody ugly, stubborn, and heavily armed with paperwork.

Him.

God help them all.

* * *

He met the boy on a Tuesday, because of course it was a Tuesday; the crown's proclamations always seemed to land on Tuesdays, like weather.

The rendezvous was a sheep barn outside Millbrook, and the fugitives arrived looking exactly like what they were: seven people who had been running long enough to forget what doors were for. A healer with ink-stained fingers. An old soldier moving like his joints were negotiating separately with each step. Twin girls who finished each other's silences. A young astronomer clutching a brass instrument like a teddy bear. Two others Brannic clocked and catalogued and didn't ask about, because asking was a luxury.

And the boy.

Tam. Seven years old, small for it, with the particular stillness of children who have learned that being noticed is expensive. He stood at the edge of the group with his sleeves pulled down over his hands, and when Brannic approached — slowly, the way you approach a spooked horse, or a widow — the boy looked up at him with eyes far too old for the face they'd been issued.

Brannic crouched. Knees crackling like green firewood.

"Well then," he said. "You'll be wanting to know about the mules."

The boy blinked.

"Fourteen of them," Brannic went on. "The big grey one's called Saint Ambrose. Don't tell him he's not actually a saint, he's very sensitive. The brown one's Saint Cuthbert, and he bites, but only people who deserve it, so if he bites you we'll both need to have a conversation." He paused. "There's also a donkey. We don't name the donkey. Naming the donkey implies a level of commitment the donkey has not earned."

Something happened at the corner of the boy's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile would live, if a boy had somewhere safe to keep one.

Brannic knew that look. He'd worn it himself for twenty-two years.

"Right," he said, standing up, knees filing another complaint. "Rules of my train. One: nobody rides Saint Ambrose, he's retired and knows it. Two: we move at the speed of the slowest person, and the slowest person is always me, because I'm old and I say so. Three—" and here his voice did something he hadn't planned, dropped into something quieter, rougher, "—nobody on this train gets registered, listed, transferred, or otherwise made into an entry in anybody's book but mine. And mine only counts what you're owed."

The healer — Wren, the grey-cloak had called her — was watching him with an expression he distrusted immediately, because it looked like gratitude, and gratitude made people expect things.

"He means it," she said softly, to the others. "He's gruff about it, but he means it."

"I'm not gruff," Brannic said. "I'm efficient. There's a difference. Most folk don't know it."

The twins giggled. The old soldier snorted. And Tam — Tam stepped forward, just one step, out of the edge and toward the middle, and pulled back his sleeve.

Brannic had been warned. Grey-cloak had told him plainly: *whatever you see on his arm*. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals, and the thing on the boy's forearm was not a mark, not a scar, not a brand. It moved. Slowly, like ink thinking. Black lines coiling and uncoiling across skin, and beneath them — Brannic felt it in his teeth, in the fillings of his back molars, in the old break in his left ankle — a hum. Low. Patient. Like a bell that had been ringing somewhere far away for a thousand years and had just now noticed him listening.

Every instinct he owned said *run*. Every one of them. The same instincts that had kept him alive through twenty years of smuggling and forty years of being Brannic Ferro.

Instead he did the hardest thing he'd ever done, harder than the Salt Caves road, harder than hanging, probably: he reached out and took the boy's hand.

The sigil hummed against his palm. Warm. Alive. Frightened, maybe, or maybe that was just the boy.

"Aye," Brannic said, gruff as gravel, because his voice wouldn't do anything else. "Well. You'll ride beside Saint Ambrose. He likes quiet folk. Thinks they're fellow saints."

Tam's hand tightened on his. Once. Small. Enough.

* * *

They left before dawn, fourteen mules, one unnamed donkey, seven passengers, and a ledger in a breast pocket over a heart that hadn't beaten this hard since Bell.

Brannic rode point and didn't look back at the barn, because looking back was for men with something to lose, and he had already lost the thing, and everything since was interest.

But at the top of the first rise he did permit himself one glance down the line — the healer laughing quietly at something the soldier said, the twins sharing a heel of bread, the astronomer navigating by stars that weren't even up yet, and the boy, riding swaying and half-asleep against Saint Ambrose's grey neck, the sleeve pulled down over the arm that the whole crown apparently wanted.

The crown could want things. Brannic Ferro had a ledger.

That night, camped in the heather with the moon doing its thin, watchful work overhead, he took out the book by lantern light and turned to the third column. He read the unfinished line — *Fourth visit —* — and beneath it, for the first time in twenty-two years, he wrote something new.

*Owed to no one, which is to say, owed to the living: one boy, age seven. Passage guaranteed. Delivery unconditional. Terms: all of them.*

He blotted it, closed the book, and pressed it flat against his chest, where it sat over his heart like a second, better heartbeat.

Two days later, on the moor below Thornfell, the heather moved wrong.

Brannic saw it before anyone — a stillness in the pattern, the particular silence of ground that has men lying in it — and his hand found the boy's shoulder and shoved, hard, shouting the word he'd sworn on the whole ride never to need:

*"Down!"*
