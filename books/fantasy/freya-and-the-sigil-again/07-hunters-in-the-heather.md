## 7. Hunters in the Heather

The moor began where the trees gave up.

Oscar felt it happen under her boots—the soft loam of the high forest thinning to a spongy mat of heather and old stone, the sky opening overhead like a hand unclenching. For three days they had traveled in the green dark of Thornfell's southern skirts, and she had almost convinced herself that the canopy was a kindness. Now there was nothing between them and the horizon but four miles of open ground and the color of rusted iron.

"Bad country," Brannic said beside her. He said it the way other men commented on weather.

"It's the only way across," Oscar said. "We can't go back to the pass, and the river road's watched."

"Everything's watched."

"That's why we're not taking the river road."

Brannic grunted, which from him was agreement. He walked with his ledger strapped under his coat like a second heart, and his eyes did what they always did—counted. Seven fugitives, one mule-train of six animals, forty yards of visibility in every direction, and nowhere to hide. She could see him doing sums he didn't like.

Behind them, the caravan strung out along the old drover's path. Wren the healer had Doss's arm over her shoulder—the old soldier's knee had been complaining since the salt caves, and he complained about her complaining, which was its own kind of affection. The twins, Sef and Ilka, walked together as always, their steps so matched it was like watching one person folded in half. And at the rear, on the lead mule because Tam had refused to ride and then refused to be left behind, came the boy himself, hood up, right arm bound in linen against the cold.

The sigil hummed. Oscar couldn't hear it—only Tam could, and sometimes the mules—but she'd learned to read it in him anyway. He sat straighter when it was quiet. When it woke, he went still in a particular way, like a deer deciding whether the wind carried danger or just weather.

He was sitting very straight now.

Oscar stopped walking.

"What?" Brannic said.

She didn't answer. She turned slowly, scanning the heather, the grey sky, the long shallow folds of land that looked like nothing and were exactly the kind of nothing an experienced soldier would use. Her hands knew before her head did; Maren had trained them that way, years ago, back when Oscar had thought the lessons were stories. *Feel the ground listen, little bird. If the birdsong stops all at once, it isn't the weather.*

The birdsong had stopped all at once.

"Off the path," she said quietly. "Now. Everyone into the fold of the hill, left side, low."

Brannic didn't waste breath asking how she knew. That was one thing she liked about him. He whistled twice—the caravan signal—and the ragged line bent toward the hollow like water finding a channel. Doss moved faster than his knee had any right to allow, putting himself between the twins and the open moor without appearing to decide to. Wren gathered the mules. Tam slid off his mount and pressed himself against Oscar's side, and through his coat she felt the sigil humming like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"How many?" Doss asked her. Low. Calm. A man asking about the price of turnips.

"I don't know." She hated saying it. "Twelve? Twenty? They're spread along the ridge line, waiting for us to commit to the crossing."

"And if we turn back?"

"Three days to anywhere safe, and they'll have riders behind us by nightfall. This is the trap. It was always going to be a trap somewhere."

Doss nodded slowly, the way he did when a plan was bad but honest. "Then we don't die in the middle of the field," he said. "We die at the edge of it, moving."

"Cheerful," said Sef.

"Accurate," said Ilka.

They were alike even in that.

* * *

Captain Ilse Varga did not believe in wasting an advantage, so she spent hers immediately.

The first crossbow bolts came out of the heather with no challenge, no parley, no proclamation read aloud—which told Oscar more than any herald would have. Registration proclamations wanted the gifted alive and documented. This was not documentation. This was culling.

A bolt buried itself in the turf where Tam had been standing a heartbeat before. Another took the lead mule through the neck, and the poor animal went down screaming, and Oscar heard Wren cry out—not for herself, for the mule, because that was who Wren was—and something in Oscar's chest went very cold and very quiet.

"Run!" Brannic roared, shoving the twins ahead of him. "Fold of the hill, GO!"

They ran. Of course they ran. Twelve frightened people and five terrified mules, scattering across the heather while the ridge line sprouted hunters like some terrible crop coming in early. Oscar counted as she ran—she couldn't help counting—sixteen, seventeen, moving with the drilled economy of crown regulars, spreading into a crescent that would close ahead of the fleeing group and herd them back down into the open.

Herd them. Not scatter them. Not cut them down where they stood.

*Herding,* whispered a thought she'd been refusing to look at for days. *Why does everything feel like herding?*

No time. A hunter broke cover thirty feet to her left, young, nervous, sword already drawn, and made the mistake of looking at Tam instead of at her. Oscar did the arithmetic of survival in half a heartbeat: he was between them and the fold, he was fast, and Tam was slow.

She didn't think. That was the horror of it, later—that she didn't think at all.

Her hand moved in a pattern she had sworn never to make again. Two fingers, a quarter-turn, a word bitten off short the way Maren had taught her to bite it—"not spoken, *spent*"—and the heat that lived coiled at the base of every hedge-witch's spine came up her arm like a snake waking, eager, familiar, *glad*, and leapt.

The heather in front of the young hunter ignited in a fan of blue-white flame, hot enough to flash-burn green stalks to ash, hot enough to make him scream and drop his sword and fall backward slapping at sparks that weren't touching him. The fire itself touched nothing else. That was the craft of it. That was the obscenity of it. Maren's battle-craft burned precisely, surgically, only ever what it was aimed at—a mercy and a monstrosity in the same gesture, because a fire that obeys is far more terrible than a fire that doesn't.

The hunter scrambled away, alive, blind with terror. Oscar was already running.

*I promised,* she thought, and her feet beat the rhythm of it into the turf. *I promised her I'd never—*

But she had promised that in a warm kitchen, over bread, with three years' distance between herself and whatever Maren had done in the wars she never talked about. Promises made in kitchens don't survive contact with crossbow bolts.

Another knot of hunters ahead, cutting the angle to the fold. Doss saw them the same moment she did, and she saw the old soldier do his own arithmetic, and she saw him arrive at an answer she refused.

"Doss—"

"Somebody has to be loud," he said, cheerful as a man ordering ale, "or everybody dies quiet." He pulled the twins' joined hands together, shoved them both toward Oscar hard enough to stagger her, and turned to face six men with nothing but a shortsword, a bad knee, and sixty years of knowing exactly how soldiers think.

What he did next bought them ninety seconds. It cost him everything, and it cost Sef less than it should have and more than anyone could bear: because Sef turned back. Because Sef had spent nineteen years never letting her sister out of arm's reach, and she wasn't going to start on a moor in the rain. Ilka screamed her name and Sef laughed—actually laughed, wild and bright—and the two of them held the gap together, twin blades moving in patterns they'd learned from Doss in the salt caves, mirror-perfect, until a crossbow found Sef's chest and Ilka's scream became the whole world's sound.

Wren dragged Ilka away by main force. Doss took a wound across the shoulder that would have felled an ox and kept standing, kept being loud, kept being the most important object on the battlefield, until the fold of the hill swallowed the survivors and the heather closed behind them like a scar.

* * *

They ran for an hour. Maybe two. Oscar stopped counting miles and started counting people—Brannic, bleeding from a graze along the scalp; Wren, hauling Ilka, whose eyes had gone somewhere far away and flat; Tam, white-faced, gripping Oscar's sleeve with the sigil-arm, and the marks beneath the linen were *moving*, she could feel them shifting against her wrist like something restless in a sack.

When the terrain finally dropped into a stream-cut gully with walls deep enough to breathe behind, Brannic called the halt, and everyone simply fell down.

For a long time nobody spoke. The stream talked instead, indifferent and cold. Rain arrived, fine and needling, as if the sky had been waiting politely for the screaming to stop.

Ilka sat apart, holding her sister's sword across her knees. Sef's body was back there on the moor, and Doss was too—he'd gone down at the last, buying the final twenty yards, and none of them had been able to go back. Wren knelt near the girl without touching her, the way you'd kneel near a wounded animal, offering presence instead of comfort because comfort wasn't possible yet.

"We bury them," Ilka said finally. Her voice was scraped raw. "Tonight. We go back and we bury them."

"No," Brannic said gently.

"They're *ours.*"

"They're bait now, girl. Varga will leave them where they lie and watch the graves. Anyone who comes to dig comes to die." His voice cracked on the last part, just slightly, and Oscar understood suddenly and completely that this was not the first time Brannic Ferro had argued someone out of a graveyard. "We carry them with us. That's the only burial we get."

Ilka looked at him, and then at the sword on her knees, and then at nothing. "Then I carry her," she said. "However I have to."

Nobody argued. There was nothing to argue with.

Tam had not let go of Oscar's sleeve since the fold. She looked down at him now—at the boy who hadn't spoken a word in all the weeks she'd known him, who communicated in tugs and looks and the occasional furious stamping of a foot—and found him watching her hands.

She followed his gaze. Her fingers were still faintly outlined in ghost-light, the afterimage of the battle-craft fading off them like heat leaving iron. She curled them shut.

"Don't," she said, though he hadn't said anything. He never said anything. "Don't look at me like I'm—like that's—"

He reached out and, very carefully, unfolded one of her fists. He pressed her palm flat between both of his small hands, the way Wren pressed a poultice onto a wound, and looked at her with an expression that was not fear and was not admiration and was not forgiveness, because she hadn't asked for any of those. It was recognition. *You did what had to be done, and it cost you, and I saw the cost.*

Oscar's throat closed.

"She taught me to heal," she managed, when she could speak. "That's what she taught me first. Knots for binding wounds, words for drawing fever out. The fighting-craft she taught me last, and she made me swear—" Her voice wobbled. "She made me swear on her mother's name that I'd only ever use it to protect someone who couldn't run. And it worked, Tam. It came back like I'd used it yesterday. Like my hands had been *waiting*. Do you understand? I've spent three years being someone who doesn't do that, and it took me half a heartbeat to do it anyway, and the worst part—the genuinely worst part—is that it felt *good*. It felt like coming home."

Tam squeezed her hand once. Then he released it, pulled the linen back from his forearm, and showed her the sigil.

It had changed. She was sure of it. Three days ago it had been a tight knot of black lines, a seal, a brand. Now the outermost ring had loosened, unwinding like thread slipping off a spool, and the loose ends trailed up toward his elbow in delicate black tendrils, each one ending in a tiny mark that might have been a letter in no alphabet she knew.

And around those tendrils, faint as breath on glass, the air itself was *warmer*.

"It's waking up," Oscar said quietly. "Whatever's under the mountains—it knows the moon's turning, doesn't it. It's pulling."

Tam met her eyes and nodded once, slow and grave, and then did something he had never done. He pointed—not at the sigil, not at the mountains, but back the way they'd come. Toward the moor. Toward the hunters. Then he laid his marked arm flat against the ground, palm down, and held it there, and his expression asked a question she didn't yet know how to answer:

*Why are they driving us forward instead of cutting us down?*

Oscar stared at him, and the cold thought she'd been refusing since the first bolt flew came back and sat down beside her and refused to leave.

*Because you don't break a seal by killing the seal-keeper,* she thought. *You break it by delivering the seal.*

"Brannic," she said, standing, her voice steadier than she felt. "How far to the Hollow?"

"Six days. Five, if the weather holds and the knee holds and nobody shoots at us."

"The weather won't hold," Oscar said, looking at the iron sky, "and somebody is absolutely going to shoot at us. But we're not stopping. We're not turning back, either." She looked at the faces around the gully—grieving, bleeding, exhausted, alive—and made herself say the rest. "Varga could have taken us today. Sixteen trained hunters against twelve tired people, and she pulled her crescent wide and *herded* us. That's not a witch-hunt. Witch-hunts want corpses and confessions. Whatever this is, it wants us *delivered*—somewhere specific, somewhere ahead of us. And I think I know where."

Rain needled down. Somewhere behind them, on the open moor, two bodies lay in the heather that no one would be allowed to bury.

And somewhere ahead, six days through the wet and the dark, the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon waited with its arms open—too open, Oscar thought, far too open, like a door that swings inward before you knock.
