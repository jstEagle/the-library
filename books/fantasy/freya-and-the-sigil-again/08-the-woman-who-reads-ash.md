## 8. The Woman Who Reads Ash

The moor was still smoking when Captain Ilse Varga knelt in the heather and put her bare hand flat against the scorched earth.

Her hunters watched from a respectful distance. They always did, after a fight. Not out of fear—Varga had never once raised her gift against one of them—but out of the particular unease soldiers feel around a woman who talks to dead ground. Sergeant Kell had once asked her, half-joking, whether the ash ever talked back. She had told him yes, and that it was rude, and that it mostly complained about being interrupted. He had not joked about it since.

"Report," she said, without turning.

Kell came up beside her, boots crunching on char. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a dropped pie and the best tracking nose in three companies. "Four of theirs down, Captain. Two dead outright. The other two are the ones who held the rearguard—burned bad. Our physiker says they won't last till dusk." He paused. "One of ours dead. Aldric. Arrow through the throat before anyone saw the archer."

Varga nodded slowly. "The old soldier."

"Must have been. Nobody else shot like that."

"Nobody else *would*," she said. "Aldric was the only one of us who forgot to be afraid of an old man."

She lifted her hand from the ground and studied her palm as though the earth had left a message written there. In a way, it had.

"Show me where they broke."

* * *

They walked the field the way she'd taught them to walk every field: slowly, in silence, from the outside in. The caravan's escape route was obvious enough—a ragged line through the heather toward the river cut, mule tracks and footprints churned together in panic. But panic told you nothing you didn't already know. Everyone panicked. It was the cheapest material in the world.

What interested Varga was the fire.

There had been no wind to speak of that morning—a dry, listless breeze out of the northeast—and yet the burns ran in clean, deliberate curves, like script. Like someone had taken the little wind there was and *edited* it. The scorch marks spiraled outward from two points near the rearguard position, each spiral breaking off in a hooked tail, and where the flames had licked across wet heather they had burned anyway, which wet heather did not do unless something had wrung the water out of it first, gently, the way you'd wring a cloth.

Kell crouched beside one of the spirals and squinted at it. "Witchfire?"

"No." Varga knelt again, closer this time. "Witchfire is greedy. It eats everything and looks like a pig ate it. This is—" She stopped. Her mouth had gone dry, which annoyed her, because she was fifty-one years old and past the age when her own body got to have opinions without consulting her.

"This is what?" Kell prompted.

"Controlled deflection burn. Triple-laced knot structure, anchored left to right." She heard herself saying the words and hated how easily they came, like a song from childhood you'd sworn you'd forgotten. "It's academy work."

"There's no academy anymore, Captain. Burned before either of us was born."

"Yes," said Varga. "That's rather the point."

She pulled off her glove—the left one, the one she wore even in summer, even at meals, because the scar beneath it made people ask questions—and pressed her bare fingers into the ash. The ground spoke to her the way it always did: not in words, but in weight. Fire remembered what it had touched. This fire remembered hands. Small hands, quick, sure of themselves and furious about it. A woman's hands, or nearly. Young. And underneath the fury, threaded through every curve of the burn like a signature hidden in a signature—

Fear. Old fear. Practiced fear. The kind of fear that has been rehearsed so long it has become a skill.

"She learned this from someone who loved her," Varga said quietly.

"Captain?"

"The technique. It's not in any text. It's passed hand to hand, master to apprentice, because writing it down gets people hanged." She stood, brushing ash from her knee. "Which means our guide didn't just stumble into hedge-craft. Someone taught her the way the old academy taught—properly, secretly, and at terrible cost."

"You think it's the Oyelaran woman? The runaway from Thornfell?"

"I think," said Varga, "that I've been reading her name in burn patterns for six years without knowing it." She looked west, toward the mountains, where the smoke of the ambush was thinning into nothing. "Every smuggler's fire we've found doused too neatly. Every campsite cold in ways campsites shouldn't be cold. I thought we were chasing a careful criminal." She flexed her scarred hand. "We're chasing a student. And students can be taught anything except how to stop being what their teachers made them."

Kell shifted his weight. "Do we report the technique? To the crown?"

And there it was. The question under all the questions, arriving right on schedule, the way it did after every engagement.

Varga looked at him for a long moment. Kell was many things—slow to anger, slower to suspicion, fond of his supper—but he was not stupid, and he had been asking that question more pointedly every month.

"We report what the crown pays us to report," she said.

"And what's that?"

"That we engaged a band of fugitives and were delayed by weather."

Kell's eyebrows climbed. "Weather, Captain."

"Very unusual weather, Sergeant. Extremely flammable weather." She turned back toward the supply wagons. "Come along. We're burning daylight, and unlike some people, my fire doesn't take direction."

* * *

The masked patron arrived at moonrise, as the masked patron always did.

Not in person. Never in person. A courier came instead—young, silent, gray-cloaked, faceless in the way of people trained to be furniture—and delivered a lacquered box to Varga's command tent, and waited outside while she read what was inside, and would wait until dawn if necessary, because the box could not leave her tent and she could not leave the box unattended and everyone involved understood the trap perfectly.

Inside the box: a mirror, dark as pond ice. And beneath the mirror, as always, a single sheet of paper bearing instructions in a hand so neutral it might have been printed.

Varga sat on her campaign chair, turned the mirror toward the lantern, and waited.

The surface clouded. Then the voice came—not from the mirror exactly, but from everywhere in the tent at once, a voice like dry paper turning pages.

"Captain Varga. You engaged the caravan today."

"We engaged it. We did not take it. Two escaped wounded, seven fled intact, including the boy."

"Aldric is dead."

"Aldric is dead," she agreed. "He died doing what you sent him to do, which was stand in front of an old soldier who used to train royal marksmen. If you'd let me bring my full company instead of your carefully rationed detachment, I'd have the boy in irons by now and Aldric would be complaining about his boots."

There was a pause. The Custodian's silences were famous—or would have been, if anyone but Varga ever heard them. She imagined the pause was disapproval. After four years, she had learned to hear whole sermons in those pauses.

"You lost eight hunters last month at the toll-bridge," the voice said. "You lost Aldric today. Your attrition rate exceeds projections."

"My attrition rate reflects the fact that you keep sending me against armed fugitives with a third of the men I request and orders not to kill the one person—the *child*—who is actually worth killing for." Varga leaned forward. "I've followed your instructions for four years. I've registered the gifted, burned the safehouses, herded your runaways east like sheep toward a fold I'm not permitted to see. I have never asked you why. I am asking now, because today I knelt in the ash and recognized a dead academy's signature, and I would very much like to know how a dead academy's student ended up in your ledger."

Another silence. Longer.

"You read the burns," the Custodian said at last. There was something almost like pleasure in it, which unsettled Varga more than anger would have. "Of course you did. You were the best of them. The last cohort. Do you know, Captain, that I considered recruiting you twenty years ago, before the academy fell? You would have understood everything so much faster."

"I don't want to understand faster. I want to understand *at all*."

"Then listen." The mirror rippled, and for the first time in four years, the Custodian showed her something: an image forming in the dark glass like frost blooming backward. Mountains. A vault door older than any kingdom. Chains of black stone, each link carved with a word, and the words moving, swimming, alive. "The sigil the boy carries is not a curse. It is a cage. And the thing inside it built the golden age your grandmother sang about—before jealous kings buried it and wrote histories calling it a war-god. Break the seal properly, with rite and consent, and it rises restored. No more hunts. No more registries. No more burning children in barns because a village priest had a grudge. An age of light, Captain. That is what I am building. You are not my hunter. You are my *mason*."

Varga stared into the glass. The chains turned. Somewhere behind her eyes, a younger version of herself—the one who had watched the academy burn and spent two decades telling herself it was necessary—whispered that this was the answer she'd been starving for.

The older version, the one with the scarred hand, noticed something else.

"You showed me the chains," she said slowly. "But not the lock. Why show me the cage and hide the key?"

"The key is not yet relevant."

"Everything relevant is relevant."

"*Captain.*" The voice cooled by several degrees. "Your task remains: deliver the boy to the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon before the thirteenth turn. Alive. Unspoiled. The woman Oyelaran is to be taken alive if possible; her knowledge of the old roads is valuable. The rest are expendable, though I note your reports consistently overestimate their threat and underestimate your casualties, and I have begun to wonder whether you are protecting them."

The tent felt suddenly smaller. Varga became aware of her heartbeat, of the lantern flame leaning, of the courier waiting outside in the dark who would carry her answer—or her refusal—to wherever the Custodian actually lived.

"I protect my people," she said. "That includes the ones you call expendable. It does not yet include you. Note the distinction."

"Careful, Captain."

"Oh, I'm extremely careful." She reached out and tilted the mirror so it caught the lantern full-on, flooding the glass with light, erasing the image of the chains. "Here is what I know, Custodian, since you value understanding so highly. Today I read a dead craft in a living woman's fire. Tomorrow I'll read yours. Everything leaves a signature—every burn, every order, every masked voice in a box. And when I finally read *yours*, I will come find you, and we will discuss attrition rates in person."

The mirror went dark. The voice, when it came again, was quieter. Almost gentle. Which was worst of all.

"Four years, Captain, and you still believe you have a choice. That is the kindest thing about you. Deliver the boy. Keep the leash I gave you oiled. We will speak again after the turning—if you have earned the right to see my face."

The lacquered box sealed itself with a click like a small bone breaking.

Varga sat alone in the tent for a long time. Outside, her hunters banked their fires and sang the low, tuneless songs soldiers sing when they're pretending not to grieve Aldric. She thought about the burn patterns in the heather—those controlled, triple-laced spirals, fury wrapped around fear wrapped around love—and thought: *someone taught that girl to write her heart in fire because writing it anywhere else would get her killed.*

And then she thought: *and someone taught me to read it. And then handed me a leash and called it a commission.*

She took out her journal—the real one, the one that lived inside her boot and did not exist in any report—and wrote, in the neutral hand she'd learned from the same extinct academy:

*Day 1,412. Engaged the caravan. Read the girl's signature: Maren's line, final generation, self-taught beyond her teacher. She fights like someone apologizing.*

*The Custodian showed me chains tonight and hid the lock. Masons don't get shown cages unless the mason is also meant to be stone.*

*Aldric is dead. The boy hums when we get close—I felt it through my boot soles at forty paces, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.*

*I am going to find out what I'm leashed to. And then I am going to decide, for the first time in four years, whether I stay leashed.*

She closed the journal, blew out the lantern, and lay back on her cot listening to the moor wind. Somewhere far to the west, seven fugitives and a humming child were moving through the dark toward a hollow in the mountains, and a dead woman's map, and a night nineteen days away when the thirteenth moon would finish its turn.

Varga found, to her considerable irritation, that part of her hoped they'd make it.

She decided not to write that part down. Some signatures, after all, were better left unread.
