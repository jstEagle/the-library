## 9. Salt Caves and Small Kindnesses

### Wren

The salt cave smelled of old sea and older stone, which Wren decided was the closest thing to home she'd found in four months of running.

She'd been a healer in Marrowgate for eleven years. She had delivered forty-one babies, set more broken bones than she could count, and buried exactly one patient she'd loved like family. Then the proclamation had gone up on her own door—*gifted persons to be registered*—and the word *gifted* had been written with a careful, bureaucratic cruelty that made it clear what happened to those who didn't come forward.

So now she was here, kneeling on salt-crusted stone in a smuggler's cave two days' hard walk from anywhere, holding the wrist of an old soldier who insisted he wasn't dizzy while being extremely dizzy.

"You're grey," she told Doss.

"I'm weathered."

"You're the color of porridge left too long."

"That's how I've always looked." He tried to sit up. She pressed him back down with two fingers, which was all it took; the man was mostly leather and stubbornness held together by scar tissue.

"Concussion," she said. "From when you caught the twins when the scree gave way. You put your head where a rock wanted to be."

"Rocks and I have an understanding."

"Well, your understanding has cost you your evening. Lie still." She worked as she talked, fingers reading his skull the way a blind woman reads a page. Her gift was small, embarrassingly small by hedge-witch standards—she couldn't mend flesh, only *nudge* it, encourage a body to do slightly faster what it would have done slowly anyway. A fever broke a day early. A wound closed without weeping. The kind of magic that never got anyone burned at a stake until, apparently, this year, when the crown had decided that any gift at all was a debt owed to the throne.

"There," she said, sitting back. "You'll live to complain another day."

"Pity." But he caught her hand before she could withdraw it, and squeezed once, briefly, with a grip that had once held shield-lines together. "Thank you, girl."

"Don't call me girl. I'm thirty-two."

"You're a child to me. Everything under fifty is a child. Everything over sixty is a corpse that hasn't been informed."

Wren laughed despite herself, and felt something in her chest unclench a fraction—the fraction it had been refusing to unclench since Marrowgate. That was the trouble with healing work, she thought, gathering her little pouches of moss and needle and thread. You spent so long tending other people's bodies that you forgot yours had been clenched like a fist for months.

Around the cave, the others were doing the ordinary, miraculous work of making a home out of nowhere. Brannic Ferro had rigged a fire pit from a broken cart wheel and a handful of stones, and was now glaring at it as if daring it to smoke improperly. The twins—Sable and Sorrel, though Wren still mixed them up half the time—were unpacking the food stores with the solemn efficiency of women defusing a trap. And near the entrance, wrapped in a blanket, sat the boy Tam, watching everything with those dark, quiet eyes, his bandaged arm tucked against his chest.

And Oscar Oyelaran stood apart from all of it, checking the mules' hobbles for the third time in an hour.

Wren watched her a moment. The guide moved like someone who had practiced not being looked at: economical, silent, always angled slightly away from the group's center. She'd saved all their lives twice on the moor and hadn't said ten words since.

Somebody should fix that, Wren thought. And then, because fixing things was the only prayer she knew: *I suppose it'll be me.*

### Doss

Doss had eaten field rations in three wars, siege bread in two sieges, and once, during the retreat at Callow Ford, a boot. He considered himself a connoisseur of terrible food, which was why the stew Sorrel set in front of him offended him professionally.

"This is good," he said suspiciously.

"It's salt hare and cave fungus," Sable said.

"It's *good*. What did you do?"

"Told you," Sorrel said, settling beside her sister with the smugness of a twin whose prediction had landed. "Soldiers can't accept kindness without interrogating it."

"I can too." He ate another spoonful. It really was good—the hare browned first, some wild herb he didn't recognize, the bitter fungus coaxed into tasting almost like mushroom. "What did you *do*."

"Wren showed us the herb," Sable said. "It grows right at the cave mouth. We've walked past it for two days."

"We've walked past food for two days," Doss repeated gravely. "In my day we'd have marched a week past food and called it discipline."

"In your day," Sorrel said, "you also slept standing up and thought lice built character."

"They did build character. Mine."

The fire cracked and spat. Brannic Ferro, who had spent the entire journey radiating the warmth of a tax collector, ladled out second helpings without being asked, and if his face stayed grim while he did it, well, Doss had known a hundred men like that—men who did the kind thing with the expression of a man doing the cruel thing, because somewhere along the way they'd decided their face wasn't allowed to agree with their hands.

That was when Sable stood up, brushed off her skirts, and announced, "Right. New rule."

"Rules are Brannic's department," Doss said.

"Brannic's rules are about watches and fires. This is a better rule." She planted her fists on her hips. "Nobody eats at this fire under their road-name anymore. Real names only. Full ones. The ones your mothers shouted across a yard when you were late for supper."

The cave went quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Doss understood immediately why it mattered, and why it terrified. Road-names were armor. He'd been "Doss" since the first hunt—a name worn smooth and anonymous, a pebble among pebbles. His real name belonged to a farm boy in the Aldervale lowlands, a boy with a mother who'd shouted it across a yard, a mother whose grave he hadn't seen in twenty years because soldiers don't get leave for funerals and deserters don't get leave at all.

To say it aloud, here, in a cave full of strangers—

He looked around. Wren had gone still. The twins were watching him, and behind them, at the edge of the firelight, Oscar had stopped fussing with the mule hobbles and turned, just her head, listening.

Tam was looking at him too. The boy couldn't speak—that much everyone knew—and Doss had caught himself wondering, in the ugly honest hours of the night watch, whether a boy who couldn't say his own name counted as having one at all.

Well. If the boy was going to listen, a soldier ought to give him something worth hearing.

"Dorran Ostwyck," Doss said. His voice came out rougher than intended. "Called Doss because my mother said Dorran made me sound like a lord, and our family had never produced anything grander than turnips." He cleared his throat. "Turnips and one very embarrassing ballad about my great-uncle and a miller's wife."

Sorrel laughed first, bright and startled, and the spell broke into warmth instead of silence. Wren said, "Wrenna Thistlewood, of Marrowgate, healer, and yes, Thistlewood is a real surname, no, I will not be taking questions." Sable and Sorrel gave theirs—Sable Merrow and Sorrel Merrow, born eleven minutes apart, "and she's never let me forget the seniority," "because it's *earned*, Sable"—and even Brannic, after a long pause and a glare at the fire, muttered, "Brannic Ferro. You knew that. It's on the ledger."

Then every eye went, gently, to the boy.

Tam looked down at his blanket. His good hand moved—not quite a gesture, more a shape drawn in the air, and Oscar translated quietly from her place at the edge: "He says his name is Tam. It's the only one he remembers."

"He remembers it," Wren said softly. "That's enough. Tam sits at this fire. Rule stands."

Something settled over the cave then, something Doss hadn't felt since a mess tent in the border wars, twenty men and a bad cook and rain hammering the canvas—the feeling of a circle closed against the dark. Not safety. Nowhere was safe. But *belonging*, which was a different animal entirely, and a rarer one.

He ate his excellent, offensive stew and thought: *I'd fight for this. Whatever comes down the mountain, I'd fight for this.*

He was an old man and had learned not to trust that thought. He trusted it anyway.

### Sorrel

Her sister was crying again, which meant it was Sable's turn to pretend she wasn't, which meant it was Sorrel's turn to pretend she didn't notice.

They'd developed the system over six weeks on the road: they never cried at the same time. One of them always had to stay dry-eyed, to steer, to lie to the others convincingly (*we're fine, we're nearly there, of course Mother's letters will find us eventually*). Tonight it was Sorrel's shift. So when Sable slipped away from the fire toward the cave mouth, hugging her elbows, Sorrel counted to a hundred, then followed at a discreet distance, the way you'd follow a deer you didn't want to spook.

Sable stood looking out at the moonlit valley, shoulders shaking silently.

"The stars are out," Sorrel said, arriving beside her and staring firmly at the sky. "Very out. Aggressively out."

"Shut up."

"Look at that one. Practically showing off."

"I'm fine."

"I know. I'm just standing here admiring stars next to you for no reason whatsoever."

A wet laugh escaped her sister, the exact sound of a cork coming out of a bottle, and then Sable leaned her head on Sorrel's shoulder and cried properly for a minute or two, and Sorrel held still and stared at the stars and let her, because that was the job, that had always been the job, ever since they were small and Sable got the fevers and Sorrel got the worrying.

"They want to trade him," Sable whispered finally. "Some of them. I heard them on the last ridge. Trade the boy to buy the rest of us passage."

"I know."

"How can they? He's—" She gestured helplessly back toward the fire, toward the small figure wrapped in wool. "He carried water for me yesterday when my hands were shaking. He doesn't even know why my hands shake. He just *carried it*."

"People are frightened. Frightened people do sums." Sorrel kept her voice level, though the same sum had been running in her own head for days, ugly arithmetic: seven lives against one. "But nobody's done it yet. Nobody's even said it to his face. There's a difference between a thought and a knife."

"Is there?"

"Yes." She said it with more certainty than she felt, because that too was the job. "Thoughts are cheap. Anyone can have a horrible thought. It's what you do with your hands that counts."

Inside, the fire had grown. Someone—Wren, by the look of it—had dragged Oscar's bedroll closer to the circle, not all the way in, but nearer, the way you'd move a chair for a guest who kept insisting they preferred to stand. And Oscar herself had finally sat down, perched on the very edge of the light with her knees drawn up, watching the fire like it was a document she was trying to read.

"She's lonely," Sable murmured, following her sister's gaze.

"She's *practiced*," Sorrel corrected. "There's a difference. Lonely people want company. Practiced people have forgotten they're allowed to."

"Then somebody should remind her."

"Mm." Sorrel smiled slowly. "I have an idea. You're going to hate it."

"I already hate it. Tell me."

### Wren

The idea, it turned out, involved Wren, because the twins' ideas always ended up involving whoever was nearest, and Wren was beginning to suspect she'd been assigned to this caravan specifically to be volunteered for things.

"Name-telling is done," Sable declared, re-entering the cave with suspicious brightness. "New ritual. Everyone has to tell the fire one true thing. Something small. Something nobody here knows."

"That's not a ritual," Brannic said. "That's an ambush."

"All rituals are ambushes. That's what makes them work."

Wren hid a smile. She'd delivered babies for eleven years; she recognized the technique. You didn't ask a laboring woman if she was ready—you simply told her the next thing, and trusted that her body knew the way. The twins were doing that to the whole cave.

Brannic refused first, on principle, then ruined his refusal by telling them about his ledger anyway—"every debt I've ever owed, written down, because debts you write down can't grow teeth in the dark"—which was either nothing or everything, depending on how you looked at it, and Wren suspected it was everything.

Doss told them about the boot. In detail. It was somehow both horrifying and hilarious, and by the end even Brannic's mouth had twitched.

Wren took a breath and offered her own small truth: "I keep a list. Every person I've ever healed. Forty-one babies, hundreds of others. Names and dates, in a little book I sewed into my coat lining." She touched the coat. "If they catch me and burn it, I'll still know it existed. That's my religion, I think. The list."

Silence, the good kind. Then Sable and Sorrel told a truth between them, finishing each other's sentences the way they did: about the night they'd run, how they'd each secretly packed a bag, how they'd found each other in the kitchen at midnight with identical bundles and identical guilty faces, and neither had known the other was planning to flee until that moment.

"We're not brave," Sorrel finished. "We're just synchronized."

And then the fire crackled, and everyone's eyes went, gently, inevitably, to the edge of the light.

Oscar had gone very still.

"You don't have to," Wren said quickly. "Rule's voluntary."

For a long moment Wren thought she wouldn't. The guide's face had closed like a door, and Wren saw her start to rise, start to find some errand—the mules, the watch, anything—

But then Tam shifted. The boy scooted along his blanket, deliberate and slow, until he was sitting at Oscar's side. He didn't touch her. He just sat there, shoulder an inch from hers, and looked at the fire, and waited, the way you'd wait with a shy dog.

Oscar looked down at him. Something in her face wavered.

"My mistress," she said at last, so quietly Wren had to lean in to hear it, "used to make me memorize roads. Draw them in dirt until my fingers bled, then draw them again from memory. I used to think it was cruelty." She turned her hands over, looked at the palms. "Last month I realized she was writing letters to a future she hoped I'd never need. Every road she beat into me was a door out of a burning house." A pause. "I hated her for years. I'd give anything to hate her for five more minutes."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke, which was its own kind of answer.

Then Tam reached over and, with great solemnity, took one of Oscar's hands and turned it palm-up, examining the calluses like a scholar reading scripture, and nodded once, decisively, as though confirming the roads were indeed written there.

Oscar made a sound that was half laugh, half something else, and didn't pull her hand away.

Wren felt her chest do the unclenching thing again, wider this time. Around the fire, the circle had quietly become a circle—no edges, no one standing outside it—and she thought: *this is how it happens. Not with vows. With stew and stupid rules and one true thing.* Found families weren't declared. They were noticed, afterward, the way you notice spring.

She was still warm with it when Doss, on first watch, suddenly straightened by the cave mouth.

"Quiet," he said.

The cave went silent. Wind, and under the wind—faint, rising, wrong—a sound none of them could name yet, except that Tam's head snapped up and his bandaged arm began, through the cloth, to glow faintly at the seams, humming like a struck bell.

Oscar was on her feet before anyone else, knife already in hand, all the softness folded away.

"Smoke," she said, nostrils flaring. "On the north wind. And under it—" her jaw tightened— "hounds. Everyone up. We move in ten minutes."

The fire that had made them a family was drowned with a kick of salt earth, and the darkness rushed in friendly and familiar, and Wren grabbed her coat with the list sewn inside and thought, wildly, absurdly: *not tonight. We just got started.*

Above the valley, thin and cold, the moon was beginning to turn.
