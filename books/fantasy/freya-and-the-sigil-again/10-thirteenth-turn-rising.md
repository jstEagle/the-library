## 10. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The astronomer's name was Pellam Odo, and he had been counting the sky since before anyone thought to ask him to stop.

He was a small man made almost entirely of elbows and enthusiasm, with a beard that looked like it had lost an argument with a thistle bush, and he traveled with the caravan in a cart stacked so high with brass instruments and rolled star-charts that Brannic had threatened twice to charge him freight for the lot. He'd been a court astronomer once, back when courts still wanted their stars read honestly. Then he'd charted something in the heavens that a duke didn't want charted, and now he charted things for free, out of spite and love in roughly equal measure.

Oscar found him on the third night of the high camp, sitting on an overturned grain barrel with his neck craned back at the moon like a man greeting an old friend who owed him money.

"You're up late," she said.

"The moon is up late," Pellam said, not looking down. "That's rather the point. Come here. No—*here*, where I am. Bring your eyes."

Oscar brought her eyes. She also brought a mug of the barley tea Wren brewed, which tasted like warm dirt but kept everyone's coughs quiet, and Pellam accepted it with the reverence of a man receiving a knighthood.

"Look," he said, pointing one knobby finger upward. "Tell me what you see."

"A moon."

"*Wrong.* Look again. Properly. Where does it sit against the ridge?"

Oscar squinted. The moon hung low over the eastern shoulder of Thornfell, fat and pale as a peeled turnip, sitting just above the notch where the pass dipped between two black peaks.

"It's over the saddle of the mountain," she said. "So?"

"So." Pellam turned to her with the expression of a man about to ruin someone's week and enjoying it only slightly. "Do you know how many times a year the full moon rises exactly there? In that notch? In that precise gap between those two peaks?"

"I'm going to guess you're going to tell me."

"Once." He held up a single finger, trembling with the drama of it. "Once a year, on the turning. And do you know what makes this year's turning different from every other turning since your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk?"

"Pellam."

"Thirteen years," he said, "the moon has been climbing toward that notch a little later each season—a hair's width, a whisper, nothing any shepherd would notice. Thirteen turns, thirteen corrections, because the old reckoning gave the year twelve moons and the sky never agreed to it. The thirteenth moon, they called it. The moon that doesn't belong. And this year—" he jabbed his finger at the notch "—this year it arrives *exactly*. Dead center. Nineteen days from tonight. The thirteenth turn complete. A closing of accounts, girl. The sky settling its ledger."

The tea had gone cold in Oscar's hands, though she couldn't have said when.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked, and her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Why does the sky's bookkeeping matter to us?"

Pellam finally looked down at her. His eyes, behind smudged spectacles, were kind and terrible all at once.

"Because three nights ago," he said gently, "your boy's arm lit up like a lamp when the moon cleared the ridge. And because I've spent thirty years reading dead astronomers who wrote about a seal that could only be unbound under a completed thirteenth turn. And because, my dear, I don't believe in coincidence. I believe in arithmetic."

Somewhere across the camp, a horse snorted. Someone laughed at some joke Oscar didn't hear. The moon sat in its notch, patient as a key waiting for a lock.

"Nineteen days," Oscar said.

"Nineteen days," said Pellam, and went back to his stars.

* * *

Tam's arm was worse.

Oscar noticed it first at breakfast, or what passed for breakfast on the run—hard cheese, dried apples, and Wren's dirt-tea—when Tam reached past her for the salt and his sleeve rode up.

Three weeks ago the sigil had lived below his elbow: a coiled black mark no bigger than a spread hand, shifting slowly, like ink dropped in water that never quite finished spreading. Now it climbed past his elbow, branching along the inside of his arm in fine dark threads, the way frost climbs a window. And the threads moved. Even as she watched, one crept a half-inch higher, delicate as a spider testing silk.

"Tamsin." She caught his wrist before she could think better of it. "Let me see."

He let her. That was the thing about Tam—he let people look at him the way other children flinched away. He pushed his sleeve to the shoulder without being asked twice, and there it was: the sigil had crossed his collarbone in the night. At the edge of his jaw, just below his ear, a single dark thread had begun.

"It hurts," Wren said quietly, appearing at Oscar's elbow with her healer's satchel already open. "Don't ask him if it hurts. Ask him how much. He'll lie if you give him room for it."

Oscar looked at Tam. Tam looked back with those steady grey eyes that held more patience than any nine-year-old should be issued.

"How much?" she asked.

He held up his hand, fingers spread, then folded two of them down.

"Three parts of five," Wren translated. "Last week it was two. It climbs with the moon. I've been dosing him with willowbark and poppy both, and it barely touches it. It's not flesh-pain, Oscar. It's—" she hesitated, hating imprecision the way most healers did, "—it's like pain that's *going somewhere*. Like a river backing up behind a dam."

"Then we take the dam down," Doss rumbled from across the fire. The old soldier was mending a strap with his teeth and his knife, an operation that offended every rule of leatherwork ever written. "Whatever's in the boy, we cut it out. I've seen men carry shrapnel twenty years. There's always a knife."

"There is not *always* a knife," Wren snapped. "Not for this. You don't cut a seal, you great ox, you—"

"You don't cut anything," Oscar said, and heard her own voice go strange and hard. Both of them stopped. Tam was watching her, head tilted.

She realized she'd gripped his arm too tight. She loosened her fingers, one by one.

"We're nineteen days from the Hollow," she said instead. "Maren's map leads there. Whatever answers are, they're at the end of that road. We keep moving, we keep him warm, and nobody—" she looked around the fire, at Doss and his knife, at Wren and her satchel, at the twins pretending not to listen while absolutely listening, "—nobody touches that mark. Swear it."

They swore it, more or less gracefully. Tam pulled his sleeve down and touched her hand once, quick, the way you'd touch a hot pan to check it had cooled—and then he signed something, small and fast, in the private handful of gestures he and Oscar had built between them over the miles.

*You're afraid,* he signed.

"Yes," Oscar signed back, because lying to Tam was like lying to a bridge; eventually everything fell into the gorge.

He nodded, unsurprised, and went to help Pellam argue with a tripod.

* * *

She opened Maren's map fragment that night, alone, by the smallest light she could make.

It was a scrap of hide, cracked with age, inked in Maren's cramped left-slanting hand—the same hand that had written Oscar's first lessons, her first recipes, her first apology after the thrashing Oscar got for burning the herb shed. Oscar had carried it pressed flat against her ribs since the burned village, and she had read it perhaps forty times, and every one of those times she had seen what grief wanted her to see: a route. A refuge. *The Hollow of Thirteenth Moon*, named in Maren's careful letters, with a winding line of trail-marks leading to it, and beneath the name, in smaller script, the words Oscar had taken for a blessing:

*Where the moon comes home.*

But grief reads maps the way drunkards read letters—it sees what it needs. Tonight, with Pellam's arithmetic rattling in her skull, Oscar looked at the fragment properly for the first time in weeks, and the blessing rearranged itself into something else entirely.

*Where the moon comes home.*

Not a welcome. An address.

Her hands were steady. That was the horror of it—they were perfectly steady, the way they'd gone steady the day she'd used Maren's battle-craft on the moor, the way her body apparently decided things without consulting her heart. She laid the scrap flat and began, methodically, to read it as Maren had taught her to read everything: backwards, then forwards, then sideways, then as what it pretended to be, then as what it was hiding.

The trail-marks weren't smuggler's knots. She'd known that much even in her grief—smuggler's marks pointed to safe crossings, toll-free ledges, wells that wouldn't kill you. These marks spiraled. They wound inward, tighter and tighter, each one a little closer to the center than the last, and at the center of the spiral, where a sanctuary should have been drawn as an open circle—a door, a hearth, a hollow—there was instead a closed knot. Thirteen loops, bound tight, with a single line running through them like a skewer through a joint of meat.

And the line ran *down*. Into the hide. Into the mountain symbol beneath it.

Oscar sat back on her heels in the cold and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since Pellam's barrel.

A place called the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon. A seal that could only be unbound at the completed thirteenth turn. A god buried beneath the mountains—that was what the sigil sang to Tam, what he'd shown her in his wordless way, chains of oath-stone and something vast sleeping under stone. A spiral of marks drawing everything inward. A closed knot of thirteen loops.

Maren hadn't sent her to a sanctuary.

Maren had sent her—or sent *anyone* she could reach—to the grave. To the burial vault. To the one place in all the Thornfells where the thirteenth moon, coming home at last after thirteen years of climbing, would shine straight down into the dark and find the lock it had been made for.

"Where the moon comes home," Oscar whispered. "Oh, Maren. What did you know? What were you *doing*?"

Because here was the question that scraped: Maren had been dying when she pressed the map into Oscar's hands, hunter-wrought wounds in her side, and she'd had time for exactly one message. She'd chosen this. Out of everything she might have said—where to hide, whom to trust, how to live—she'd spent her last breath pointing her runaway apprentice at the buried god.

Unless it wasn't a last breath. Unless Maren had known, the way she'd always known things, that hunters herd and wounded women lie and maps travel further than their makers intend. Unless the trap had a second door.

Or unless Maren had simply believed, all those years, that the Hollow was safety, and had died passing on a mistake wrapped in love, and somewhere ahead of them that mistake was waiting with its mouth open.

Oscar folded the map along its worn creases and pressed it back against her ribs, where it lay like a coal that wouldn't cool.

Nineteen days. She could turn the caravan aside—there were passes south, meaner ones, roads even she didn't know. She could buy time. Time for Tam's arm, time to find another answer, time to avoid walking seven fugitives and a mute boy directly into whatever waited at the bottom of Maren's spiral.

But Tam's sigil climbed a little further every night, and the moon climbed a little further every night, and the two climbings were the same climbing, and Pellam's arithmetic didn't care which road she took. The thirteenth turn would complete whether she watched it or hid from it. The only choice was whether they met it standing at the lock—or running somewhere else entirely when it opened.

She looked up. Across the camp, Tam sat beside Pellam's tripod, pointing at the sky and making shapes with his hands, and Pellam was nodding along as if the boy were delivering a lecture, and maybe he was. Maybe the boy knew the sky better than any of them. Maybe that was the worst part.

Oscar banked the fire, shouldered her pack, and went to wake Brannic.

They needed to talk about the route south. Because the destination, she understood now, had always been the trap—and she had nineteen days to decide whether to spring it, dodge it, or walk into it with her eyes open and her family beside her.

Behind her, unnoticed, the moon slid free of the notch in the peaks and kept climbing.

Nineteen days, and it would come home.
