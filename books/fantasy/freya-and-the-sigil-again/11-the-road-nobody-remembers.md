## 11. The Road Nobody Remembers

The road nobody remembered began at a dead alder tree with a lightning scar shaped like a fishhook.

Oscar had known that tree since she was nine years old, when Maren had dragged her up the Thornfell flank in the rain and made her stand before it until she could close her eyes and draw its shape in the mud without looking.

"Again," Maren had said, arms crossed, unbothered by the weather because Maren was never bothered by weather. "And again after that. A road you can't carry in your head is a road someone else owns."

Oscar had hated her a little for it, then. She hated her a little for it now, too, standing in the cold drizzle with forty frightened people and eleven mules waiting on her word — but it was the comfortable kind of hate, the kind that fit like an old coat. The other kind, the kind she'd been wearing since the moor, since Wren had sewn shut the wounds of two people who hadn't survived being covered by Oscar's escape — that kind didn't fit anything, and yet she couldn't take it off.

"The tree," she said aloud, mostly to herself.

Brannic Ferro squinted at it from under his oilskin hood. "That's a tree."

"That's a door." She touched the lightning scar. Beneath the bark, faint as breath on glass, her fingertips found the old trail-sigil Maren had knotted into the wood decades ago — a turning-mark, a *not this way, the other way*. "Everyone drink. Fill every skin you have. What comes next, we won't see open sky for two days."

Doss, bringing up the rear with the mules as he always did, looked at the tree, then at the mountainside beyond it, which offered nothing but scree and heather and a great deal of indifferent stone. "There's no road there."

"There's a road," Oscar said. "It just doesn't remember itself anymore. That's rather the point."

* * *

The entrance was a mine adit hidden behind a curtain of ivy so thick it took four of them hauling on ropes to pull it aside, and behind the ivy, a timbered archway black with age, and behind the archway, darkness that smelled of wet iron and old water.

"A drowned mine," Wren said flatly. She stood with her healer's satchel hugged to her chest like a shield, peering into the dark the way one peers down a well. "You're leading us through a drowned mine."

"It's not drowned all the way through."

"Oh, well," said Doss. "That's fine, then."

Oscar almost smiled. Almost. "There are galleries above the waterline. The old smugglers' cut runs dry for most of the route. There are three crossings where we wade, and one stretch—" she hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because hesitation was a smell and frightened people could smell it— "one stretch where the water comes to the chest. It's short. Twenty paces. Maybe thirty."

"And if it's risen since you last walked it?" That was Sefa, one of the twins — the quieter one, the one who'd barely spoken since her sister Lira had been caught whispering with strangers on the moor road. Her voice came out thin. "How long has it been?"

Oscar did the arithmetic honestly, because lying about numbers was the one lie that killed. "Nine years."

Sefa looked at the dark. The dark looked back. Somewhere deep inside the mountain, water dripped with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry.

"We can't go back," Brannic said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd taken the rear-guard position out of habit, and he'd seen what habit showed him: dust on the far ridge, moving wrong for weather. Varga's hunters were sweeping the high passes in a pattern too neat to be chance. Someone was combing the mountains for them, methodical as a louse-hunt. "Back is burned. Forward is wet. I've been on worse roads." He glanced sideways at Oscar. "I've *paid* for worse roads."

Tam stood at the edge of the group, small and still, his hood up against the drizzle. As Oscar turned toward the dark, the boy drifted closer to her — not touching, never touching anyone, but near, the way a candle stands near a window. His sleeve had ridden up. The sigil on his forearm had spread again: three new tendrils of black curling past his elbow now, delicate as frost-fern, and when the wind gusted off the mountain they seemed to lean into it, hungry for cold.

He caught her looking. He didn't hide it. He never hid it from her, and she'd stopped pretending she wasn't looking, and between those two honesties lived whatever it was the two of them had instead of conversation.

*Not the Hollow,* Tam had signed to her last night, hands quick and urgent in the firelight. *Anywhere but.*

*I know,* she'd signed back. And then, because he deserved more than that: *I'm working on it.*

She was working on it right now, in fact. Working on it with a dead alder tree and a flooded mine and nine years of memory that might have rotted along with the timbers.

"Rope line," she said, shaking off the thought. "Hands on shoulders where the floor's uneven. No lanterns past the first bend unless I say — the air needs to stay good. Wren, you're second. Doss, mules in the middle, twins behind them, Brannic rear. If anyone hears running water getting *louder*, you tell me immediately. Not your neighbor. Me."

"Why you?" asked Lira.

"Because I'm the only one who knows which sounds are supposed to be there." She swung her pack tight, took the lead rope, and stepped into the dark. "Welcome to the road nobody remembers. Try to leave it the same way."

* * *

For the first hour, it was almost easy.

The smugglers' cut ran level and dry, floored with gravel that crunched satisfyingly underfoot, and the mountain closed around them like a hand around a coin — dark, but a held kind of dark. They walked in a chain of murmurs and mule-breath, and somewhere in the middle of the line somebody started humming, very softly, an old road song about a peddler and a magistrate's wife, and nobody told them to stop, and the humming traveled down the chain person to person until even Doss was doing it, tunelessly, like a man whittling sound instead of wood.

Oscar walked at the front with one hand trailing the left wall, reading Maren's marks by touch.

Her mistress had signed this tunnel the way other women embroidered linens. Here, a spiral scratched into the stone: *water rises fast here after thaw, do not linger.* There, a double notch: *safe camp, two hearths' worth of dry wood cached behind the fall.* Once, absurdly, a tiny crude drawing of a cat, which meant nothing, which had never meant anything, which Maren had carved purely, Oscar now understood with a lump in her throat, because a nine-year-old walking this tunnel terrified had needed one thing in the whole black world that was silly.

"You knew this place as a child," Wren said, falling into step beside her. It wasn't a question either. Wren's questions rarely were; she just stated things at you until you confirmed them.

"Maren ran goods through here for years. Salt, mostly. Sometimes people." Oscar's fingers found another mark — a chevron, meaning *steep drop, mind the young ones.* Maren had cut it at exactly the height of a child's trailing hand. "She made me memorize the whole network. Every junction, every mark. I used to think it was because she loved me."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was because she loved me *and* she planned for everything, including the versions of the future where loving me wasn't enough to keep me safe." Behind them, a mule sneezed explosively, and the whole line flinched and laughed shakily, and Oscar waited for the laugh to settle before she went on. "She used to say a map is a promise someone makes to a stranger. So she never wrote these down. She put them in me instead. Said paper burns."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "And the Hollow? Is it on this road?"

The question landed precisely where Wren had aimed it, because Wren missed nothing and had spent nineteen days watching Oscar flinch whenever the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon came up.

"No," Oscar said. "This road goes around it. A long way around."

"How long?"

"Long enough."

Wren let that sit between them, patient as a splint. "Oscar. Nineteen days ago an astronomer told us the moon turns thirteenth in nineteen days. We've spent six. The Hollow is where the sigil can be unbound, and we are walking away from it through a hole in a mountain, and every day Tam's marks climb higher, and every night he sleeps sitting up because lying down hurts. So I'll ask it plainly, and you may hate me for asking: how much longer do you mean to buy?"

Oscar kept her eyes on the wall, on the marks, on the little carved cat. "As long as I can."

"Yes," Wren said gently. "That's what I thought."

* * *

They made the first crossing at midday, such as midday was underground — a wide gallery where the smugglers' cut dipped to meet an underground stream, shin-deep and cold enough to make the twins yelp in unison, which was the first time either of them had made the same sound on purpose in days, and Brannic declared it a miracle worth marking.

The second crossing came at dusk, thigh-deep, slower, the rope line doubled. Tam crossed pressed close behind Oscar, and she felt the sigil's hum through the rope — a faint wrongness in her palm, like a struck tuning fork held against bone. When she glanced back, his face in the lantern-glow was gray with strain.

*How bad?* she signed, water to their knees.

He considered lying. She watched him consider it. Then he held up four fingers, then folded one down. Three days, maybe less, before the marks reached his shoulder. After the shoulder, Maren's fragment had implied — though the fragment was half-burned and cruelly ambiguous — things stopped being pain and started being something else entirely.

*Soon,* she signed. *I promise. Soon.*

He nodded, and believed her exactly as much as she deserved, which was some, and not all, and both of them knew it.

* * *

The trouble came in the night, as trouble prefers.

They'd camped in the wide gallery with the cached firewood — Maren's cache, nine years stale but dry, and Oscar fed the little hearth with a strange tenderness, warming forty people with wood her mistress had stacked for fugitives who hadn't existed yet. Supper was barley and salt pork and Wren's merciless nettle tea. The twins fell asleep shoulder to shoulder. Doss took first watch, because Doss always took first watch, arguing that sleep was for people with better dreams.

Oscar woke to the sound.

At first she thought it was the mountain settling. Then she understood: water. Running water, louder than it should be, louder than the stream at the second crossing had any business being.

She was on her feet before her boots were fully on. "Doss."

"Already awake." His voice was very calm, which from Doss meant the opposite. "It started an hour ago. Rising slow. I didn't want to wake them for nothing."

"Third crossing," Oscar breathed. "The thaw melt. It backs up the lower gallery when the snows go — Maren's mark said—" and then she stopped, because she was already running, rope in hand, and the others were waking behind her in a rustle of blankets and fear.

The third crossing was not a crossing anymore.

Where twenty paces of chest-deep wade should have been, the gallery mouth vomited white water — the melt coming down off the high snows had found the mine's old drainage and reversed it, and the passage below was a churning brown throat. The far side, where the cut climbed safe and dry toward the exit, might as well have been another country.

"We go around?" Brannic shouted over the roar.

"There is no around! This *is* the around!" Oscar's mind raced through Maren's marks, the spiral, the warning — *water rises fast after thaw, do not linger* — and found, with a cold drop in her stomach, the mark she'd read that morning and filed away and not acted on. *Do not linger.* They had lingered. She had lingered them.

"The ledge!" Doss pointed. Along the right wall, a narrow shelf of rock ran above the flood — smuggler-cut, maybe eighteen inches wide, slick with spray. It vanished into the roaring dark. "We hug the wall and edge across!"

"With children and mules?" Wren's voice cracked. "On that? In the dark?"

"With a rope line and no better ideas," said Brannic.

They went. Of course they went. That was the terrible, holy thing about the ragged caravan of them: they complained, and then they went.

The ledge work was agony — inches at a time, backs to stone, the flood sucking at their heels like a crowd trying to pull them into a dance. Two mules balked and had to be blindfolded and coaxed. The twins went third and fourth, Lira ahead of Sefa, and Oscar heard herself saying *heel to heel, don't look down, look at my lantern, only my lantern* in a voice she didn't recognize, calm and hard, Maren's voice, worn like a borrowed coat that fit better every year.

They were ten feet from the far shelf when Sefa slipped.

It happened the way disasters happen — slowly, then all at once. Her boot skated on wet rock, she cried out, Lira grabbed her wrist and was pulled off balance herself, and for one horrible hanging moment both twins were on the ledge only by Lira's grip and gravity's indecision.

Doss didn't shout. Doss didn't hesitate. The old soldier simply uncoiled along the ledge like a man twenty years younger, hooked one arm through the rope line, and caught Sefa's collar in his fist as she went — and the weight of two girls and the flood's drag took him to the very lip of the water, his boots skidding, his arm creaking in its socket, his face set in the expression of a man negotiating terms with a river and refusing to accept the river's first offer.

"Hold— the— *line!*" he roared, and the line held, because Brannic had anchored it and Wren had wrapped it twice around a timber and because forty frightened people pulled as one animal, and between them — between the rope and the old man's grip and the screaming collective will of the road nobody remembered — the twins came up out of the water's reach and collapsed onto the ledge, gasping, alive.

Doss hauled himself up last. He lay on his back on eighteen inches of wet stone, chest heaving, and after a moment he began, wheezingly, to laugh.

"You," he said to Sefa, "owe me a drink. You too," to Lira. "Both of you. Good ones. None of Wren's nettle swill."

Lira was crying and laughing at once. Sefa had her forehead pressed to the old soldier's shoulder, saying something over and over that the flood drowned out, but that looked, from the shape of it, like *thank you, thank you, thank you.*

They gained the dry cut within the hour, and made a fire, and nobody slept, and that was fine. Some nights weren't for sleeping.

* * *

Near dawn, Oscar sat apart, at the tunnel's mouth where the first gray hint of morning leaked in, and Tam came and sat beside her the way he did — near, not touching.

She didn't pretend she hadn't been crying. There seemed little point; her face was a public document.

Then she heard footsteps, and it wasn't only Tam. Wren. Brannic. Doss, damp and stiff-jawed. The twins, hovering. Even Lira, who avoided Oscar lately the way one avoids a stove that has burned you once.

Oscar looked at all of them, and found that the speech she'd been rehearsing in her head for nineteen days came out all at once, like a knot finally slipping.

"I chose this road," she said. "Not for safety. I chose it because it goes around the Hollow. I've known since the astronomer spoke — since before, since Maren's map — that the Hollow is where this ends, and I have been buying time ever since. Days. Hours. Whatever I could steal." Her voice shook and she let it. "Because I'm afraid. Not of the hunters. Hunters, I know how to fight, God forgive me. I'm afraid of the Hollow. Of what's buried there. Of what the rite costs. Of choosing — of having to choose between Tam and—" she gestured helplessly at all of it, the fire, the faces, the family she hadn't asked for and could not now imagine losing— "and this. And every mile I led us the long way was a mile I stole from Tam's shoulder, and I told myself it was caution, and it wasn't. It was fear wearing caution's coat. Maren would have seen through it in a heartbeat. You all should have."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere, patient as ever.

Then Doss cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he rasped, "I've followed officers who felt no fear at all. Every one of them got people killed." He worked his sore shoulder. "Fear that confesses itself is just honesty with its boots on. I'll march behind that any day."

"We lost time," Wren said — not accusing, measuring. "But we're not out of it. Fourteen days. The direct road to the Hollow is five."

"Three, if we push," said Brannic. "I know a way through the fells that would make a customs man weep."

Lira stepped forward, chin up, eyes red. "My sister nearly died on your shortcut," she said, and Oscar braced herself — "so the next one, you tell us the truth *before* we walk it. Deal?"

Oscar let out a breath that had been held for nineteen days. "Deal."

Tam tugged her sleeve. When she turned, he raised his hands, and signed, slow and deliberate so the whole circle could follow:

*Hollow. Fast. Together.*

And the sigil on his arm, in the firelight, seemed — for the first time any of them had seen — to flicker, like a lamp steadying.

Behind them, far down the flooded gallery, the water went suddenly, strangely quiet.

Every head turned. Oscar felt the hair rise on her arms, and beside her Tam's breath caught — and the sigil began, softly, unmistakably, to sing.
