## 12. Varga's Offer

Ilse Varga had read a thousand men in her years wearing the crown's grey, and she had learned early that most of them were simple creatures: they wanted coin, or safety, or to be admired by someone they shouldn't admire. Oscar Oyelaran was not simple. That was precisely what made her worth the walk through freezing heather in boots that had never once been dry since the moor.

Varga stood at the edge of the caravan's camp as dusk came down like a lid, watching the fugitives move around their fire. The old soldier was sharpening a knife with the unhurried rhythm of a man who'd done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. The healer was wrapping someone's blistered feet while scolding them gently about lacing their boots too tight. The twins were arguing over whose turn it was to carry the water skins, an argument that had clearly been running for years and showed no sign of resolution.

And at the center of it all, Oyelaran herself, crouched by the fire, turning something small and dark in her hands — the map fragment, if Varga's readers were right, and her readers were always right.

*You could take them tonight,* said the tactical voice in her head, the one she trusted least because it was usually correct. *Fourteen against nine. You have crossbows.*

She could. She'd lose three or four hunters doing it, and she'd lose Oyelaran, who would burn half the moor before she let them touch the boy, and Varga needed Oyelaran alive and willing. Willing was the whole trick of it. The Custodian wanted the boy delivered, not dragged — there were reasons, reasons wrapped inside reasons, the way the Custodian wrapped everything — but Varga had her own reason, simpler and heavier: seven amnesty papers sat folded against her heart, and she meant to use every one of them.

She raised the parley flag — a strip of white linen on a broken spear shaft — and walked into the firelight alone.

* * *

The camp went quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Doss was on his feet first, knife low, and Wren pulled the twins behind her, and Brannic Ferro rose from his bedroll with the slow menace of a man who had buried one daughter and did not intend to practice.

"Parley," Varga said, and planted the spear shaft in the frozen ground where everyone could see it. "One rider, no steel drawn, no craft worked. Your law or mine, it means the same."

"And why would we honor your law?" Ferro asked. "You've burned two of ours already."

The words landed where she'd let them land. She didn't flinch from them; flinching was for people who hadn't ordered the volley. "Because I'm offering you a trade you'll want to hear, and dead men make poor merchants. Send me Oyelaran. Alone. Ten minutes. If she comes back harmed, hang me from your wagon."

"She'd come back harmed by nobody," Doss muttered. "Girl'd talk your ears off."

Oscar stood. Up close, in the firelight, she looked younger than her reputation and older than her years, which Varga supposed was what happened when you spent three years pretending to be nobody. There was knotwork braided into the girl's collar — trail-marks, wardings, little magics hidden in plain thread — and Varga noted each one without letting her eyes linger. A good reader never let the subject see the reading.

"Ten minutes," Oscar said. "Away from the fire. And if I shout, my soldier friend throws this knife, and he does not miss."

"He doesn't," Varga agreed. "I've watched him throw. It's the only thing about your camp that genuinely frightens me."

That earned her the ghost of a smile, gone before it finished arriving. They walked out past the picket line, into the blue dark between the fire and the hills, far enough that voices wouldn't carry. Varga stopped where the ground rose slightly, so that the campfire silhouetted them both — a courtesy, showing Oyelaran she carried no hidden blades at her sides. Old habits of honesty. They were the only habits she had left.

"You read ash," Oscar said. No greeting. Good. Greetings wasted time and time was the one currency neither of them had.

"I read everything." Varga clasped her hands behind her back, the parade rest, though there was no parade within four hundred miles. "Your burn patterns at the ambush site. The spiral-and-break. That's academy work. Third Cohort style, the battle-craft they stopped teaching thirty years ago when the last woman who mastered it died in a cellar in Vail with the king's men around her."

"Maren Oyelaran died in a kitchen," Oscar said quietly. "Last spring. You made sure of that."

"I didn't order it." And that was true, and true things were the sharpest tools she owned. "My patron did. I've regretted it more than any order I've ever followed, including ones that ended worse. Maren taught me to read ash, girl. Before you were born. I was her student for two winters, and then I chose the crown over the craft, and I have been paying for that choice ever since, in installments."

Oscar had gone very still. Stillness, in Varga's experience, was either fear or calculation, and this girl did not do fear where other people could see it. "So this is a recruitment pitch. From my dead mistress's other student."

"This is arithmetic." Varga turned to face her fully. "Listen to me, because I will say this once and then I will let you decide, and either way I ride back down the mountain tomorrow and tell my patron whatever serves us both. Nine people in that camp. Seven of them gifted, registered or registerable under the new proclamation. You know what registration means now — it stopped meaning paperwork in the autumn. It means the pits at Corvane. It means collars."

"I know what it means."

"Then here is what I hold." She drew the papers out — slowly, deliberately, letting the firelight catch the wax seals. "Seven amnesties. Full and unconditional. Names struck from every list in every archive. Your healer walks into any city in the kingdom and buys bread without looking over her shoulder. Your twins sleep in real beds. Ferro's ledger burns clean. The old man gets his pension, even, if he wants it — the crown owes him that much for Harrow Ford, whatever else it's done."

Doss's name on the papers had been a calculated kindness. Everything was.

"And the price," Oscar said.

"The boy."

The word hung between them. Somewhere behind them the fire cracked, and one of the twins laughed at something — a bright, ordinary sound, the sound of people who did not yet know they were being priced.

"Not killed," Varga said. "Delivered. Alive, unharmed, to my patron. That's the whole of it. You hand him over at the next crossroads, and I give you these papers, and every soul in that camp goes free except one who was never free to begin with. He's been hunted since the day he was marked. You cannot unmark him. Nobody can. All you can choose is whether he runs until the whole kingdom's knives find him, or whether his capture buys seven lives' worth of freedom."

"You're lying about the amnesty."

"I am not." Varga met her eyes and let her see the truth there, plain as a signature. "I have wanted those papers signed since before you were born, Oyelaran. I have watched gifted children go into the pits and come out as lists of numbers. This is the first time in twenty years anyone has offered me a way to pull seven of them back out. Do you think I'd risk it on a lie?"

"Then you're lying about what happens to Tam after delivery."

There it was. The girl's voice didn't rise; it dropped, went flat and cold as the stone under their feet, and Varga felt the temperature of the conversation change the way you feel weather change. Academy-trained. Maren's work all over her, in the stillness and the reading and the way her hands had drifted, casually, toward the knots at her collar.

"No," Varga said. "I'm not lying about that either. Because I don't know. And I want you to understand that I know that I don't know, because I think you're the only person on this mountain who'll respect the difference."

* * *

They stood in silence for a moment. Above them the sky was doing its winter performance, stars sharp enough to cut, and somewhere up in that black the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its turn, invisible among its sisters, patient as everything else in this cursed business.

"Tell me about the Custodian," Oscar said at last.

And here was the strange part — the part Varga had rehearsed on the ride north and then thrown away, because rehearsal was for liars and she was done lying to this particular girl. She told the truth instead.

"I've served the crown for twenty-two years and the Custodian for six, and I have never seen their face. Orders come sealed, in a hand that never varies, with gold that never runs short. At first I assumed ambition — some minister building a private instrument. Then I assumed madness. But I've read the Custodian's letters, girl. Hundreds of them. Madness doesn't footnote itself. Whatever else my patron is, they believe — truly, completely, with the whole weight of a lifetime — that what they're doing is salvation."

"Salvation for whom?"

"For everyone." Varga laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and bitter in the cold air. "That's the trap of it. That's why I follow, maybe. Listen. The Custodian believes the sigil is a cage, and that the god inside it — chained under these mountains since the war — has been poisoning everything near it for three hundred years. Every hunt, every pit, every burning. Not politics, not greed. Leaking. The god's fear leaking up through the stone into the hearts of kings, until the whole world above it grew crueler year by year. Break the seal, end the leak, and—" she spread her hands— "a golden age. The Custodian believes it the way farmers believe in rain. And I have stood in rooms full of reasonable people and heard myself explain it, and watched reasonable people nod."

"You believe it too." Not a question. The girl read people the way Varga read ash, and it was unsettling to be on the receiving end.

"I believe the world has been getting crueler," Varga said carefully. "I have the ledgers to prove it. Burnings per year, registrations per year, pits opened per year. Twenty-two years of numbers, and the curve only bends one way. Something is making us worse, Oyelaran. I don't know if it's a buried god or just human nature left unsupervised. But the Custodian's answer and my answer happen to be the same road for now, and I am not a woman who can afford to inspect her convictions too closely. None of us are. That's the honest truth of it, and I'll thank you not to quote it back to me in front of my hunters."

For a long moment Oscar said nothing. When she spoke, her voice had changed — softer, almost puzzled.

"You really think handing over a child could buy a golden age."

"I think a child who has never spoken a word in his life is carrying a war-god's prison on his arm, and that prison is spreading, and when it reaches his heart he dies in agony, and the god wakes anyway, and everything the Custodian fears comes true regardless. I've seen the marks climb, girl. Have you?" She saw the answer land. "So weigh it honestly. Not the pretty version where you protect him forever. The real version. Every hunter in the north is coming for that boy. I'm the gentlest thing chasing him, and I promise you I am not gentle. The Custodian's other instruments are already on the mountain. Things I don't command and don't fully understand. If I don't deliver him, they will, and they won't bother with parley flags."

"And if you do deliver him?"

"Then seven people walk free, and I keep the only promise I've ever cared about keeping." Varga held out the papers. "Take them. Read the seals. You'll find they're genuine."

Oscar looked at the papers and did not take them. Her hands stayed at her sides, and Varga realized, with the peculiar clarity that sometimes arrived at the worst moments, that the girl wasn't deciding. She had decided already, probably before Varga finished speaking, possibly before Varga arrived. She'd listened to the whole offer the way you listen to a storm you've already boarded your windows against.

"You love them," Oscar said. "The people in that camp. You've been watching them all evening. The healer scolding. The twins fighting over water. You looked at them like—" she paused, searching— "like someone reading a letter from home."

Varga said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be a wound.

"My mistress used to say that the most dangerous argument isn't the wrong one," Oscar went on. "It's the one that's almost right. Because almost-right arguments get inside you and set up housekeeping." She stepped closer, close enough that Varga could see the firelight reflected twice in her eyes. "Here's my almost-right back at you, Captain. You're right that the marks are spreading. You're right that hunters are coming who are worse than you. You might even be right about the leak — gods know this world is sick enough for it. But you're wrong about the cure, and deep down, in the place where you keep your twenty-two years of ledgers, you know it. Nobody builds a golden age on a child's back. Not a real one. Whatever the Custodian breaks that seal to build, it won't be rain for farmers. It'll be a weapon with a golden handle. And when you finally see it — and you will, because you read everything — I want you to remember that a runaway mule-guide told you first."

The wind moved through the heather. Down at the fire, someone had started a song, low and wandering, the twins' voices twining together the way twin voices do.

"Ten minutes," Varga said finally. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "It's been twelve. Your soldier is reaching for his knife."

"Let him reach. He likes the exercise." Oscar backed away a step, then another, and stopped. "Keep your papers, Captain. But keep them close. I have a feeling you're going to need them sooner than you think — and not for the seven names written on them."

"What is that supposed to mean?"

"It means," Oscar said, turning back toward the fire, "that you should ask yourself why the Custodian needs *me*. You could take the boy any night with crossbows. You said it yourself — you're the gentlest thing on this mountain. So why the parley flag? Why the offer? Why does your patron, who footnotes everything, need Oscar Oyelaran willing?"

She walked back toward the light, and Varga stood alone in the dark with the question, turning it over like a stone found in a field, feeling the shape of it, feeling how heavy it was, feeling — with the cold certainty of a woman who had read ash for twenty years and never once liked what the ash told her — that the girl had just handed her the truest thing anyone had said on this mountain all winter.

Behind her, unseen in the heather, a hunter who was not hers shifted his weight, and began, silently, to withdraw toward the pass — to report, in a hand that never varied, exactly what the captain had offered and exactly what the guide had refused.

* * *

Varga walked back into the firelight to collect her flag, because leaving a parley flag planted in an enemy's camp was bad manners and she had very few manners left to spare. The song had stopped. Nine faces watched her, and not one of them was afraid, and that frightened her more than anything else had all day.

Oscar met her at the picket line. "Captain. One thing, free of charge."

"Those are always the expensive kind."

"When you write your report tonight — and you will write it tonight — leave something out. Something small. A detail. Anything." The girl's eyes were steady and terribly kind. "Because the day will come when the Custodian asks you for something you can't give, and you'll want a small disobedience already practiced. Trust me. The first one is the hardest."

Varga took her flag and walked away into the dark, and it was not until she was half a mile down the pass, alone with the stars and the cold, that she admitted to herself which detail she had already chosen to omit.

She also admitted, somewhere below thought, that she had not yet decided whether she'd send it after all.
