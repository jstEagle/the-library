## 13. What Loyalty Weighs

The fire was small and the circle around it was smaller, huddled into the lee of a granite shelf where the wind couldn't strip the heat away. Oscar stood at the edge of it with her hands in her pockets and her heart somewhere around her boots, and she made herself say the words she'd been chewing on for three days.

"I have to tell you all something," she said. "Several somethings, actually, and none of them are good."

Doss looked up from sharpening his knife. "That's usually how speeches start."

"Brannic's not back yet," Wren said quietly. She'd been watching the dark like a hawk watches a field, waiting for the big man's silhouette. He'd gone scouting the pass with two of the twins' mules and hadn't returned by moonrise.

"He'll be back," Oscar said, and hoped it was true. "And when he is, I want this already said. I'm tired of deciding things for you in my own head and calling it protection."

She told them about the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon — that it wasn't a sanctuary at all, or wasn't only one. That beneath its lantern-lit caverns lay the burial vault of a god, chained under oath-stone since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother had learned to write. That Maren's map fragment didn't lead to safety. It led to the thing itself.

She told them about the thirteenth turn — how the astronomer, old Pellam with his ink-stained fingers and his star charts rolled up like contraband, had confirmed what the sigil already seemed to know: in nineteen days, the moon would complete a turning that came once in an age, and on that night the seal could be unbound.

"And here's the part I've been choking on," Oscar said. "Varga found me alone under a parley flag. She offered amnesty for every soul in this caravan — papers, pardons, freedom to go home — if I deliver Tam to someone called the Custodian."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale.

"That's it?" said Ferro's cousin Sela, who had joined them after the ambush with a broken arm and a talent for finding fault. "That's the whole offer? Papers for everyone?"

"For everyone," Oscar said. "Except Tam."

Sela turned to look at the boy, who sat at the circle's edge with his hood up, his marked arm wrapped in linen against the cold. The sigil had spread past his elbow now, black lines crawling like frost across a window. He'd stopped sleeping well. He'd stopped eating much. He watched the fire the way drowning men watch shore.

"He's dying anyway," Sela said. Not cruelly — that was the worst of it. She said it the way you'd read a ledger. "Look at him. Whatever's in his arm is eating him alive. We hand him over, we all walk free, and he gets... what did she promise? A golden age? Maybe the Custodian can even help him."

"You don't know that," Wren said.

"Nobody knows anything," Sela shot back. "That's my whole point. We're guessing. But I know what registration means. My husband took it. They marched him north in a column and I never saw him again. So when someone offers me papers, I listen."

"The offer's a lie," Doss said flatly. He hadn't stopped sharpening the knife. "Thirty years a soldier. You don't give amnesty to seven fugitives and let them walk. You take the boy, you take the guide who knows your roads, and then you decide the other five are witnesses. Witnesses get graves."

"Then why is Varga offering at all?" Sela demanded. "If they just wanted to kill us, they had chances. Two of ours are dead already because of this road. Because of *him*." She caught herself, but too late, and everyone heard it anyway.

Tam flinched. Small, quick, like a dog that knows the kick is coming even when it isn't aimed at him.

Wren rose. She was a small woman, a healer, with grey in her braid and hands that smelled forever of yarrow, and she crossed the circle and planted herself between Sela and the boy like a fence post driven into rock.

"You'll not say that again," she said. "Not where he can hear it. Not where any of us can hear it."

"He can't hear anything," Sela said. "He's mute."

"He hears everything." Wren's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You think silence is deafness? That boy has heard every argument, every whisper, every time someone thought they were being kind by talking over his head instead of to him. He heard you just now. And I will tell you what I know, since we're trading truths tonight: I've held his arm while the marks burned. I've felt what moves under them. It isn't eating him. It's *straining*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a straining thing can be held, and he holds it. Every day. Every hour. Has anyone else in this circle carried anything half so heavy?"

Nobody answered. The fire cracked. Sela sat down slowly, arms folded, unconvinced but outflanked.

Oscar knelt by Tam. "I'm sorry," she said. "For all of it. For discussing you like freight."

He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious, and he pulled his sleeve down hard over the sigil as though he could hide it from a room that had already seen everything.

"We should vote," said Pellam from behind his charts. "It's only fair. Majority decides."

"Vote on a person," Wren said. "Lovely. Next we'll weigh him and divide by coin."

"A vote at least counts everyone," Pellam said. "A road doesn't. A road just takes whoever's still standing when it ends."

Oscar stood up. Her legs felt like borrowed things.

"No votes," she said. "I've been making choices for this caravan since the pass, and half of them were me running from my own fear dressed up as strategy. So here's the truth underneath all the other truths: I chose the drowned-mine road to avoid the Hollow. I bought us days we didn't need, because I was afraid of what Maren's map really meant. That's mine to carry. But I won't carry a decision about Tam. Nobody carries that alone, and nobody makes it *for* him."

She turned to the boy. "Do you understand me? Nobody decides for you. Not me, not them, not the Custodian, not the god in your arm. When we reach whatever we reach, it'll be because you chose the road. Do you hear?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded once, slow, and something in Oscar's chest loosened and broke at the same time.

It was Wren who noticed first. "Where's Sela gone?"

The circle turned. Sela's bedroll was empty. So were the twins', gone to check the mule-lines, that was normal — but Sela had taken her coat, and her pack, and the night was black as a closed eye.

"She wouldn't," Doss said. "Not to them. Not after her husband—"

"People do strange arithmetic in the dark," Pellam murmured. "Grief plus fear equals anything."

They found her twenty minutes later, or rather they found where she'd been, because the snow told the story before anyone could: her tracks leading south toward the pass road, and then crossing them, and then a confusion of prints, and then nothing at all, because the wind had come up off the ridge and begun erasing the world.

But there was another set of tracks. Smaller ones. Bare feet, half-frozen, leading the opposite way — up, toward the shrine ruin on the shoulder of the mountain, the one Oscar had marked on their second day and avoided ever since.

Tam's tracks.

"He heard all of it," Wren whispered. "Every word. And he walked out into the snow rather than sit there and be counted."

Oscar was moving before she'd decided to, grabbing her coat, her staff, the little lantern she kept for emergencies and hated using because light made you a target. Behind her she heard Doss swearing, Wren calling for the boy, the camp dissolving into noise — and above all of it, faint and rising, the sound she'd learned to dread more than hoofbeats.

The sigil's hum. Carried on the wind. Louder than she'd ever heard it.

Louder than it had ever needed to be — unless it wanted to be found.

* * *

The shrine ruin crouched on the mountainside like an old animal that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway. Broken pillars. A roof open to the stars. Snow drifted across the floor in pale dunes, and in the middle of it, small and still as a carved figure, sat Tam.

His bare feet were blue. His marked arm was bare to the shoulder, the sigil uncovered, and it was *moving* — the black lines shifting, crawling, brightening at their edges like coals breathing. The hum filled the ruin, filled Oscar's teeth, filled the space behind her eyes.

"Told you," she said, forcing her voice gentle as she crunched through the drifts toward him. "Told you nobody decides for you. And the first thing you do is decide to freeze to death on a mountain. Very stubborn. I respect it and I'm furious about it."

He didn't turn. Snow gathered on his shoulders. On his lashes. His lips were moving — shaping words with no voice, over and over, and Oscar got close enough to see what he was looking at.

The frost on the flagstones in front of him was thick, smooth, untouched by wind — and written across it, in a fine careful hand, were words. Scratched by a fingertip, over and over, deep enough to catch the lantern light:

**NOT CARGO**

"Oh, lad," Oscar breathed. She sank down beside him, heedless of the cold soaking through her knees, and pulled off her own coat and wrapped it around his shoulders. He was shivering so hard the motion barely registered. "No. No, you're not. You never were. I should have said it sooner and louder and to their faces, and I'll say it every day from now until you're sick of hearing it, which, fair warning, will take years, because I plan to be extremely annoying about it."

His hand came up — the marked one — and touched the frost again. Slowly, deliberately, letter by letter, new words forming under his finger as the old ones melted and ran:

**THEY ASK WHAT I WANT**

"Then tell us," Oscar said. "Any way you can. Frost, mud, signs, screaming — I don't care. Tell us."

His finger hesitated. The sigil pulsed, and the hum climbed, and for one terrible moment Oscar thought the thing in his arm was going to answer for him.

Then Tam bent his head, and wrote:

**NOT TO BE SAVED**

Oscar stared at it. The wind moaned through the broken pillars. Somewhere below, torchlight was bobbing along the pass road — too many torches, moving too neatly, the way soldiers move.

"To be what, then?" she asked softly.

Tam looked up at her. And in the frost, with a hand that shook from cold and something far older than cold, he began to write the answer.
