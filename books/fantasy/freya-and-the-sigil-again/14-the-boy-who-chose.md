## 14. The Boy Who Chose

The snow did not feel like anything.

That was the first thing Tam noticed, and the only thing he trusted about it. Cold was supposed to bite. This cold just sat on his shoulders like a heavy coat someone else had chosen for him, and he walked through it because walking was easier than standing still, and standing still was easier than going back, and going back was impossible, so: walking.

Behind him the caravan's fire had shrunk to an orange coin in the dark. He could still hear them, if he listened — Wren's voice rising and falling like a bird arguing with the wind, Doss's low rumble answering it. They were talking about him. He had heard enough before he left. *The boy is a burden. The boy is a target. The boy is cargo with a heartbeat.* Nobody had said *the boy is Tam*, because nobody knew there was a Tam inside the boy. Even Oscar only half knew, and she was the one who didn't flinch, and that was its own kind of ache, because not flinching meant she looked at him properly, and being seen properly was the most dangerous thing that had ever happened to him.

His arm burned under his sleeve. The sigil had been spreading all week — black lines crawling past his elbow now, fine as spider silk, and where they went, they sang. Not words. Images. It had been images since the night of the toll-bridge, when the stones under the caravan had shivered and the sigil had hummed through his bones and held the bridge together like a hand pressed flat against a drumhead.

*Come down,* the images said tonight. *Come down and see.*

He didn't want to see. But the snow was deep and the dark was deeper, and the sigil was warm, and warmth wins arguments with tired boys more often than anyone likes to admit.

There was a ruin ahead. He felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the ground, a hollowness, like a tooth pulled from a jaw. Then the trees opened and there it stood: a shrine, old beyond old, its roof fallen in, its walls furred with frost. A stone doorway gaped onto darkness. Above it, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, some patient carver had left a shape that might have been a moon, or might have been an eye, or might have been both.

Tam went in, because the sigil wanted him to, and because it was out of the wind, and because a boy can only refuse so many things in one night.

Inside, the frost had grown strange. It climbed the walls in feathers and ferns, silver-white, catching what little light the clouds let through. In the center of the floor, beneath the broken roof, a circle of open sky showed stars, and the frost around that circle glittered like a spilled jewelry box. Someone had loved this place once. Someone had swept this floor and lit these lamps and knelt here, and now the snow came in through the roof and kept the candles' memory company.

Tam sat down against the wall, because his legs were done voting, and pulled his sleeve back.

The sigil moved.

It always moved a little — that was its horror, that it was alive, that it shifted like ink dropped in water — but tonight it moved with purpose. The lines gathered themselves, tightened, arranged. And the images came, and this time they were not gentle.

He saw the mountains from underneath. That was how the god saw everything — from underneath, from behind chains of oath-stone older than any kingdom, older than any oath anyone living had sworn. The chains were not metal. They were promises, made solid, made heavy, laid over something vast the way you'd lay timbers over a cellar. And the something vast was not sleeping, exactly. Sleeping things dream. This was more like waiting. Like a held breath that had been held so long the holder had forgotten there was ever any other way to breathe.

And threaded through the waiting, thin as wire, came other images. Not the god's. These were recent. These had edges.

A vault of black stone. Men in crown colors kneeling to a figure in a mask. A forge built where no forge should be — built *on top of* the chains, its heat sinking down through the oath-stone like a brand sinking into hide. And hands. So many hands, reaching for the sigil's pattern, copying it, enlarging it, hammering it into a shape it was never meant to hold. The sigil was a lock. But every lock, turned the other way, is a key. And the masked figure knew it, and the masked figure was not trying to free the god at all.

The masked figure was trying to *wear* him.

Tam gasped — a small, stupid sound, swallowed by the ruin — and the vision broke apart into sparks, and he was sitting in the frost with his heart slamming and his arm glowing faintly, faintly blue-black, like a banked coal.

That was when he heard footsteps in the snow outside.

He knew them. Of course he knew them. Three years of mule-trains had taught Oscar Oyelaran a walk like everyone else's walk, except faster and angrier, as if she were perpetually late for an appointment with someone who deserved it.

She came through the doorway with a lantern held high and her breath steaming and her face doing several things at once — relief first, he saw it land before she could kill it, then fear, then the particular fury of a woman who has spent an hour imagining a frozen child and found the child merely sitting in a ruin looking back at her.

"You," she said, "are in *so much trouble*."

Tam blinked at her.

"Don't give me that look. You walked out of camp in the dark, in the snow, with hunters on the moor and your arm doing—" she gestured at the glow, "—*that*. Do you know what I thought? I thought Varga had you. I thought I'd find—" She stopped. She crouched down to his level, which she always did, which was one of the thousand small reasons he had decided, without ever saying so, that he would follow her off the edge of the world if it came to that. "I'm not angry," she said, in the voice people use when they are extremely angry. "I'm glad you're alive. Both things. It's complicated."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the sigil pulsed, and the vision flickered behind his eyes again — the vault, the forge, the mask — and whatever his face did next made Oscar go very still.

"What is it?" she asked. "What did it show you?"

He shook his head. And hated it. Hated the shaking head, hated the silence, hated the whole dumb architecture of a boy who had things to say and no road for them to travel down. His voice hadn't worked in two years — not since the soldiers came to his village, not since the night he learned that speaking could get people killed, that his mother's last word to him had been *don't*, and he had obeyed her so thoroughly that the obedience had eaten the words themselves. Some silences are wounds. His had become a wall, and walls do not come down just because you suddenly need a door.

Oscar waited. She was good at waiting. She settled beside him against the wall, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and set the lantern between them, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of speech.

The frost glittered. The stars turned in their little circle of sky.

Then Tam had an idea.

It arrived the way his ideas always arrived — sideways, uninvited, smelling faintly of the sigil. He looked at the frost-feathers on the wall beside him. At the perfect, glassy stillness of them. At the way the cold had written its ferns and fronds across every surface like a page waiting for a pen.

He put out his finger and wrote.

Not letters. He couldn't do letters — his letters had gone the way of his voice, rusted shut. But the sigil knew shapes. The sigil had shown him the vault, the forge, the mask, the chains. And the frost, obedient to the humming in his blood, followed his finger like a dog follows its master's hand.

Black lines bloomed in the white. Not frost-dark — sigil-dark, drawn up through his fingertip and laid into the ice crystal by crystal, precise as engraving. The vault. The kneeling men. The masked figure above them, tall, holding something that could only be a hammer.

Oscar's breath stopped.

"That's—" Her finger hovered over the image without touching it, the way you hover near a sleeping animal. "That's the Custodian. That's what Varga serves. How do you know this? Tam. *How do you know this?*"

He wrote on. The forge next, squatting atop the chains, its heat rendered in jagged strokes. Then the chains themselves, and then — this was the hard part, this took everything he had — the sigil itself, lifted out of a boy's arm, stretched wide, nailed into a frame of oath-stone, made vast. Made into a door. With the god behind it, not freed, not sleeping. *Harnessed.* The door opening not outward into the world but inward into a weapon, and the weapon shaped like a man, or like what a man becomes when something enormous pours itself into him and calls the pouring mercy.

Oscar read it the way she read trails — fast, hungry, missing nothing. He watched her arrive at the end of it. Watched her understand.

"They don't want to break the seal," she whispered. "Varga's amnesty, the Custodian's golden age — it's all — they want to *reforge* him. They want a vessel. They want someone wearing the god like armor." She laughed, one short, awful note. "And the only vessel in the world that fits is sitting in a ruin eating frost."

Tam looked at her. Then deliberately, slowly, he pointed at himself, and shook his head.

"No?"

He pointed at himself again. Then at the image of the masked figure. Then he drew, in quick sure strokes, a line from the figure to the hammer, and from the hammer to the forged sigil, and then he crossed his arms over his chest — *no* — and pointed at the whole arrangement.

"Not him. Them." Oscar exhaled. "They mean to take it themselves. Or make one of their own." She stared at the frost a long moment. "Then why hunt you? Why herd every gifted soul in Thornfell toward the Hollow like sheep toward a — "

She stopped. He watched her get there. Watched her face do the thing faces do when a map turns out to be upside down.

"Because a broken seal needs mending," she said quietly. "Because the original vessel is dying, and the pattern has to be renewed, and the Custodian needs a bearer who can carry the sigil to the forge intact. They're not hunting witches. They're hunting a *keyhole*." Her jaw tightened. "They've been driving us toward the Hollow the whole time. Every registration notice, every burned village, every pass closed — it's a funnel. We're not running from the hunt, Tam. We're running *inside* it."

The sigil hummed agreement against his skin, and for once he didn't hate it. For once it was telling the truth he already knew.

Oscar put her head back against the wall and looked up through the broken roof at the turning stars. "Maren's map. The Hollow. Nineteen days until the thirteenth turn." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I was going to take us around it. Lose time. Keep you away from that place until I figured out something clever. And the whole time the clever thing was a lie I told myself, because I'm scared, and scared people call their fear strategy."

Tam reached over and tapped the frost image of the vault. Then he drew a new line — from the vault, downward, to a shape he added carefully: thirteen small circles, clustered like a handful of moons.

Oscar frowned at it. "Thirteen. The thirteenth turn. What about it?"

He didn't know. That was the honest truth of it — the sigil showed him the number over and over, thirteen moons, thirteen points of light ringing the buried dark, and it felt less like a warning than like a *door left open somewhere*, an answer waiting for its question. He shrugged, and hoped the shrug said *I don't know yet, but it matters*, and judging by the way Oscar went quiet, it did.

"All right," she said finally. She stood, brushed the snow from her knees, and looked down at him with an expression he'd seen her wear at toll-bridges and river crossings — the expression of a woman measuring a load and deciding it would hold. "Here's what happens now. You're coming back to camp. Wren has been sharpening her needles for whoever let you wander off, and I'd rather it be me. And tomorrow — " she hesitated, and he watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the easy one, "— tomorrow I tell them everything. The Hollow. The trap. All of it. They deserve the truth even if the truth breaks us apart."

Tam stood too. His legs had stiffened and the cold had finally found its teeth, and none of it mattered, because a decision had landed in him while he wasn't looking, the way decisions do when they've been circling for days. He caught Oscar's sleeve. She turned.

He crouched, and wrote one last thing in the frost. Not an image this time. He made himself do letters — clumsy, crooked, a child's letters, dragged up from two years down in the dark where his mother's *don't* had buried them. It took a long time. Oscar didn't hurry him. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far off, a wolf said one sentence to the moon and got no reply.

When he finished, the frost read:

**I CHOOSE THE HOLLOW**

Oscar read it twice. "Tam," she said gently, crouching again, "you don't have to. Whatever it's showing you, whatever it thinks you owe it — you're a boy, not a sacrifice. Nobody's putting you on that altar."

He shook his head. And wrote again, slower, pressing harder, the sigil-light bleeding up through his fingernails:

**NOT TO BE SAVED.**

**THEY THINK I AM THE KEY.**

**SO I GO WHERE THE LOCK IS.**

**AND I CHOOSE WHAT IT OPENS.**

Oscar stared at the frost for a long, long moment. When she looked up at him, her eyes were wet, and she was smiling the fierce, cracked smile of someone watching a small thing turn out to be enormous.

"Well," she managed. "Listen to you."

He grinned — actually grinned, the muscles surprised to remember how — and wiped the frost clean with his sleeve, all of it, every image, keeping the secret a little longer between the two of them. Because the caravan would need to hear it from Oscar, in daylight, with fires and food and Wren's needles sheathed. But also — and this was the new thing, the thing that warmed him better than the sigil ever had — because for the first time in two years, Tam had something worth keeping.

A plan. A choice. A voice, even if it lived in frost and borrowed light.

He took Oscar's lantern without asking, the way family does, and they walked back toward the orange coin of the campfire together, two sets of footprints stitching across the snow, and behind them in the ruined shrine the frost was already growing back over the place where a boy had spoken — softening, filling in, forgetting — though the stars through the broken roof kept their circle, thirteen bright points wheeling slow above the dark, counting down.
