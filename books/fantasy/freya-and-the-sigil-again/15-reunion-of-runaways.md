## 15. Reunion of Runaways

The Hollow announced itself the way a good hiding place should: by pretending to be nothing at all.

Oscar had been watching for it since dawn—a fold in the mountainside, a stream that vanished too neatly into stone—but what she saw first was a goat. A single brown goat, tethered to a scrub pine, chewing with the profound indifference of an animal that had never once in its life been hunted.

"Nobody ties a goat out where hunters can see it," she said, reining up. "Unless there's nothing left to hide from."

Brannic grunted beside her. After the gate, after the lanterns and the knives and the long cold weeks of it, he moved like a man carrying his own weight plus several others'. He still did. But he was here, which was more than Oscar had dared expect, and when he looked at the goat he smiled with half his mouth.

"Or," he said, "somebody's gotten comfortable."

The trail bent around a shoulder of granite, crossed the stream on stepping stones worn smooth by generations of careful feet, and then the mountain simply opened. Not a cave—Oscar had expected a cave, a hole, something they'd have to squeeze through with their hearts in their teeth—but a valley folded inside the mountain like a letter inside an envelope. Terraced gardens climbed the walls. Smoke rose from chimneys built into the rock itself, so thin and well-drafted it barely showed. Water fell somewhere out of sight, a sound like slow applause.

And people. Hundreds of them.

They came out of doorways and garden rows and a smithy whose hammer went suddenly quiet. Old men with hedge-craft in their knuckles. Women whose shadows moved a half-second behind them. Children—gods, children—chasing each other between bean rows, one small girl levitating a turnip purely out of spite while her brother shrieked with delight.

Wren made a sound Oscar had never heard from her before, somewhere between a laugh and a sob. Doss took off his hat, which for Doss was roughly equivalent to weeping. The twins gripped each other's hands.

Tam slid down off the mule and stood very still, his head tipped, listening to whatever the sigil sang him. Then he looked up at Oscar and tugged her sleeve twice—their old signal, *safe*, learned on the road—and once more, softer. *Home?*

"Don't get attached," she said, because her voice was doing something embarrassing. "We haven't even met the landlord."

* * *

The landlord met them halfway across the valley floor.

She was small and silver-haired and walked with a stick she clearly used as a scepter rather than a support, and the crowd parted around her the way water parts around a keel. She stopped six feet away and looked them over—one by one, unhurried, the way you'd count coins you intended to spend wisely.

"Thirteen days early," she said. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "The road usually thins people out more than this."

"The road tried," Brannic said.

Her eyes found Tam immediately. Of course they did. Everyone's eyes found Tam eventually; the sigil saw to it. But she looked at him the way you look at a locked door you already hold the key to, and something in Oscar's gut went cold and stayed cold.

"I am Sef," the woman said. "I keep the Hollow. You'll be fed, warmed, and asked nothing tonight. Tomorrow there will be questions." She turned, then paused. "You'll want to wash. And—" her gaze flicked to Oscar, and for the first time something almost gentle moved through it, "—there's someone asking after you. Has been since autumn. By the infirmary, third terrace."

Oscar's heart performed a maneuver she hadn't authorized.

"Maren," she said. Not a question. A name falling out of her mouth like a dropped tool.

Sef's face gave away nothing. "Third terrace," she repeated, and walked on, and the crowd closed behind her like water over a stone.

* * *

Oscar took the terraces two at a time and hated herself for it, because a sensible woman would have walked, would have prepared, would have spent those ninety steps rehearsing every furious and relieved thing she'd carried for three years and three thousand miles. Instead she arrived at the infirmary doorway breathless, dirty, and completely unarmored, and Maren was sitting in the sun shelling peas into a bowl like the world had never once caught fire.

She was alive. That was the first fact, and it broke over Oscar so hard she forgot all ninety steps' worth of fury. Maren was *alive*—thin, gray at the temples now where the gray used to be only at the edges, but upright, whole, her hands steady on the pea pods. The hunter-wrought wound that Oscar had watched bleed her dry on the smoke-black floor of their old cottage was nowhere on her. Not even a scar.

"You're dead," Oscar said. It came out wrong. Everything was coming out wrong. "I buried you. I mean—I didn't bury you, I *ran*, I—Maren, I held your hand and you were dying, I felt you dying—"

"You felt me dying," Maren agreed. She set down the bowl. "Come sit down, child, before you fall down."

Oscar didn't sit. Sitting required accepting that any of this was real, and she needed another moment of standing in the doorway being certain of exactly one thing, which was that her mistress was breathing.

Then she crossed the space and hugged her, hard enough that the peas scattered, and Maren smelled the same—woodsmoke and comfrey and something faintly of hot iron—and Oscar cried like a girl of fifteen instead of a woman who'd led eleven fugitives through a flooded mine three weeks ago.

"There now," Maren said into her hair, patting her back in the old rhythm. "There now. Look at you. You've gone and become somebody."

"Somebody terrible."

"Somebody *useful*. There's a difference, and the difference is mostly guilt, and you can put the guilt down now. It doesn't fit you. Never did."

Oscar pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve, laughing wetly, and it was perhaps four full seconds before the questions reassembled themselves and marched back in.

"How. The wound was hunter-wrought. I've seen that craft kill goats, Maren. I've seen it kill—"

"And so you have." Maren picked the bowl back up, resumed shelling, and Oscar recognized the gesture for what it was: a woman buying herself a moment. Maren had done it her whole life. Shelling peas while the cottage burned around them, once, literally. "The Hollow healed me. There's magic pooled in this rock older than the crown, older than the war, maybe. It doesn't like wounds that carry intent. It argued with them until they lost."

"It *argued*?"

"Mm. Everything here argues. You'll see." A pause. Peas ticking into the bowl. "There's something else you should know before you hear it sideways, because you always hear things sideways first and build a fortress out of the worst version."

Oscar's stomach dropped. Three years of apprenticeship had taught her the exact pitch of Maren's voice that preceded bad news dressed as practicality.

"The proclamation," Maren said. "The one nailed to every tavern door from Thornfell to the coast. Gifted persons to be registered. Hunters in the passes."

"What about it?"

"I wrote it."

The peas kept ticking. Somewhere below, a child laughed. The waterfall applauded its slow, endless applause, and Oscar stood very still in the warm sunlight and felt the ground open under her with no sound at all.

"You—"

"Not the words. The words were the crown's, gods help us, and ugly enough without my help. But the rumor of it—the whisper that ran ahead of the hunters, the one that emptied the hedgerows and the herb-lofts and the cunning-folk's back rooms—I seeded that myself. Autumn, last year. I sent word down every smuggler's road I know, and I know most of them, and I made sure it reached every gifted household within a month's walk of the passes." Maren set the bowl down again, finally, and looked up, and her eyes were the same eyes that had taught Oscar knotwork and star-reading and how to lie to a tax collector with a straight face. "Panic moves people, Oscar. Safety never has. I could have written letters saying 'come to the Hollow, you'll be welcome,' and half of them would have suspected a trap and the other half would have dithered till the hunters came. But tell them they're about to be hunted, and they run. And running folk run toward shelter whether they meant to or not."

"You flushed them." Oscar's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Like rabbits. You burned the bracken so they'd bolt toward your nets."

"I saved them."

"Two of ours died on the moor." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and she didn't try to take them back. "Hunters caught our scent because the roads were thick with fleeing gifted, because *you* filled the roads. Varga's riders. Two people, Maren. One of them was nineteen."

Maren flinched. Actually flinched, the pea bowl rattling against her knee, and some vicious part of Oscar noted it and another part hated the noting.

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "I know. Sef keeps the tally. Every soul the flush cost on the way here, she writes it in a ledger, and I read it every night, and I will read it every night for the rest of my life. Nineteen names as of yesterday." She looked up. "Do you think I don't know what I did? Do you think I lit that fire from a warm room? I did it from a sickbed, child, half-dead myself, because the alternative was watching them all be registered one by one—registered, and then *used*, though I didn't know for what yet, and I know now, and it's worse than I thought. Panic was the only shepherd fast enough. So yes. I burned the bracken. And yes, it killed people. And if you can show me the clean way to have done it, I'll kneel to you right here among the peas."

Oscar couldn't. That was the worst of it. She stood there turning over every alternative—letters, warnings, riding out herself—and every one of them ended in the same ledger with different handwriting, and she hated Maren for being right and hated herself for understanding, and underneath both hates sat the oldest one, small and stubborn as a burr: *you didn't tell me. You let me grieve you. You let me run three years thinking I'd abandoned you to die.*

"You let me believe you were dead," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she was furious about that too.

"I was dying. The Hollow's shepherds found me on the pass road and carried me in. By the time I could walk, the flush was moving, and a dead woman's apprentice travels lighter than a living woman's—no hunter thinks to look for a ghost's kin. It was tactics." Maren's voice gentled. "It was also cowardice, a little. I'd sent you away three years before that, remember. Pushed you out the door with a map fragment and a hard face because keeping you close was going to get you killed. Letting you go on grieving me seemed—tidy. Safe. For you." She shook her head. "Tidy is just cruelty wearing an apron. I know that. I'm sorry, Oscar. Not for the fire. For the silence."

Oscar breathed. Once. Twice. The waterfall applauded. Down the slope, Tam sat on a garden wall showing a fascinated child how to fold a grass whistle, and the sigil on his arm caught the light and threw it back black.

"Maren," she said slowly. "The map fragment. The Hollow. You sent me here too, didn't you. Not to sanctuary."

Maren held her gaze, and didn't lie—that was the one thing Maren had never done to her, even when lying would have been kinder, and Oscar clung to that like a handhold on scree.

"I sent you to the truth," Maren said. "The Hollow isn't a sanctuary, child. It's a keeping-place. And in nineteen nights, on the thirteenth turn, everything this valley is, everything I am, everything that boy carries—it all comes due at once. I couldn't write that in a letter. Some truths you have to walk into the middle of." She picked up the pea bowl a final time and pressed it into Oscar's hands, warm from the sun, absurdly domestic. "Now. You're filthy, you're grieving, and you're hungry—you get that look when you're hungry, you've had it since you were twelve. Go eat. Tonight Sef will call a gathering, and you'll want your strength, because she's going to tell you what the founder told me, and it's going to make my little fire in the bracken look like a candle."

"Maren. What comes due?"

But Maren only smiled, tired and loving and utterly immovable, the exact smile that had sent a fifteen-year-old Oscar out a door with a map fragment and no explanations.

"Eat first," she said. "Some doors you knock on with a full stomach."

* * *

The gathering was held in a cavern that might once have been a temple and was now mostly a dining hall, which struck Oscar as the healthiest transformation any religion had ever undergone. Long tables. Lantern light. Several hundred people passing bread and stew and arguing cheerfully about whether the bean terraces on the south wall got enough sun, which was the kind of argument Oscar could have listened to forever, having spent three years in places where arguments were held in whispers if at all.

Sef stood at the head table and rapped her stick once, and the hall went quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"You came by fire," she said, without preamble. "Every one of you came by fire—the fire of fear, the fire of the hunt, the fire of loss. Good. Fire is honest. Now I'll be honest back." She looked down the tables, and her gaze snagged briefly on Tam, seated between Wren and Doss, and lingered. "This valley is not a refuge. It never was. It is a lock, and you have all been brought here as parts of the mechanism. In nineteen nights the moon completes its thirteenth turn, and on that night the seal beneath this mountain can be unbound—or renewed—or broken entire. What it cannot be is ignored. The boy at the third table carries the key on his skin. The crown wants him. Something older than the crown wants him more. And I—" her jaw tightened, and for the first time the dry seed-husk voice roughened, "—am bound to tell you all of it, because the one who built this place commands it, and I have carried that command longer than most of you have been alive."

A murmur ran the length of the hall. Brannic's hand had gone to the table edge. Wren's arm had come around Tam's shoulders, casual and absolute, a wall made of a hug.

"Who built it?" Oscar called out, before she could stop herself. "Who commands you?"

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Around the hall, hundreds of faces turned—faces Oscar had eaten bread with twenty minutes ago, faces that had sung to the twins and mended Doss's coat and shown the children how to raise turnips.

"She does," Sef said. "The founder. She sleeps beneath the vault, and wakes for no one. But on the thirteenth turn, she wakes for the seal." The stick rapped the stone floor, once, like a period at the end of a sentence. "And she has left instructions. For the boy. For the seal. And—" the pause stretched, deliberate, merciless, "—for the woman who guided him here. Oscar Oyelaran. Your mistress wrote the fire that drove you. The founder wrote the hand that lit it. Nothing in this valley has happened by accident, and nothing in it is finished. Rest tonight. Tomorrow, the vault opens."

The lanterns guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

At the third table, Tam touched Oscar's wrist. Twice. *Safe.* Then, slowly, deliberately, a third time—a signal they'd never made before, one he invented right there, pressing her finger to the black mark on his forearm and then pointing down, through the floor, through the terraces, through the mountain's whole patient weight.

*It knows we're here.*

Oscar looked at the pea bowl still cradled in her arms, warm as a living thing, and understood that she had come home to a house that had been expecting her all along—and that the door had just begun to open.
