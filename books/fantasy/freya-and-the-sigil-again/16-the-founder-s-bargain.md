## 16. The Founder's Bargain

Maren had spent seventy years learning to read people the way other folk read weather, and the thing she could not stop reading, in the long gallery beneath the Hollow, was her own apprentice's face.

Oscar stood three paces away with her arms crossed and her jaw set in that particular angle Maren had first seen on a girl of fourteen caught feeding the neighbor's goat a stolen turnip. Stubbornness, then. Stubbornness laid down like foundation stone.

Good. She'd need it.

The founder — Ysolde, though she had worn eleven names before that one, and would wear more after — moved through the gallery with the unhurried gait of someone who had outlived every deadline ever set against her. She was small. That was the first surprise, always. Folk expected the keeper of a buried god to be tall as a doorframe, voice like thunder down a well. Instead Ysolde came up to Maren's shoulder, white-haired, brown-faced, with laugh lines deep enough to lose coins in, and hands that were never still: braiding grass stems, straightening a lantern, smoothing the hem of a passing stranger's cloak as though the whole world were a child who'd been playing too rough.

"Sit," Ysolde said now, gesturing at the long table hewn from living rock. "All of you. What I have to say is best heard sitting down, because some of you will want to fall over."

Around the table they gathered, and Maren counted them the way she counted everything these days, with an ache underneath: Oscar, arms still crossed. Tam, pressed close to Oscar's side, his marked arm wrapped in clean linen Wren had changed twice since their arrival. Brannic Ferro, who had looked at Maren across the Hollow's threshold with an expression she was still deciding whether it was gratitude or grievance. Wren the healer, Doss the old soldier, the twins Nettie and Sorrel sitting shoulder to shoulder the way twins do when something has passed between them that hasn't yet been said aloud. And at the far end, silent and watchful, Captain Ilse Varga, whose presence at this table should have been impossible and who sat anyway, hands flat on the stone, like a woman waiting for a verdict she already knew.

The gallery itself was beautiful, Maren had to grant it that. The Hollow's founders had carved no pillars; instead the cavern's natural columns of flowstone rose pale and ribbed as candlewax, glowing faintly where veins of moon-silver ran through them. Lanterns hung on iron hooks older than any kingdom above. Somewhere below — far below, past doors Maren had not been permitted through — the mountain held its prisoner, and the whole Hollow hummed around that fact like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

"You've all felt it," Ysolde began, settling into her chair. "The hum. Good. Then you know I won't insult you by pretending there's nothing under this floor but roots and old bones." She folded her small hands. "Nineteen days ago you were told the thirteenth turning approaches. Tonight I'll tell you what it means, and what it costs, and I'll tell it plain, because I've watched clever people dance around plain things for nine hundred years and it has never once helped."

"Nine hundred," Doss muttered. "I've served under lieutenants younger than your lies."

"Almost certainly," Ysolde agreed pleasantly, and Doss blinked, wrong-footed, and Maren felt the first flicker of warmth in her chest since the pass. *Oh, she's good.*

"The sigil on that boy's arm is not a brand and not a curse," Ysolde said. "It is a lock. The oldest lock ever made, and the only one of its kind. When the war ended — the real war, the one your histories call a border squabble — thirteen oath-stone chains were sunk into the earth to hold the god beneath this mountain. Twelve of those chains are anchored in the land itself. Rivers. Ridges. The roots of the fells. They cannot be moved because they are not objects. They are promises the ground made."

"And the thirteenth?" Wren asked softly.

"The thirteenth chain needed a promise the ground couldn't make. It needed a will. A soul willing to be the last link." Ysolde's eyes went to Tam, gentle and unflinching. "So they made one. Or rather, they found a family who agreed, and the sigil was set into the bloodline, mother to daughter, father to son, for two thousand years. Each bearer carried one link of the god's chain inside their own life. As long as the bearer lived free and unbroken, the chain held."

Tam's hand crept to his linen-wrapped arm. He didn't pull away from the look. Maren noted that, too. *Brave past his size.*

"But chains rust," Ysolde continued. "Promises fray. Two thousand years of bearers, each one hunted or hidden or married off to scatter the bloodline thin, until the line narrowed to one child who could not speak — because, we believe, the sigil took his words as its anchor. His voice became part of the lock." Her gaze softened. "And then the Custodian found him."

"The Custodian wants to break it," Oscar said. Flat. Certain. "Varga told me—"

"Varga told you what she believes, which is what the Custodian let her believe." Ysilde raised a finger. "Here is the truth, and it has two edges, so hold it carefully. On the night of the thirteenth turning, when all twelve earthly chains stand aligned with the moon, the sigil can be unbound. There are exactly two ways to unbind it, and no third way, and I have spent nine centuries looking for a third way, so do not waste our remaining days suggesting one."

She held up one finger.

"The first: destroy the sigil. Burn it out of the bearer entirely. The chain-link dies with it — and so does the bearer. Not quickly. Not painlessly. The god's chain doesn't snap; it unravels, and it unravels through the person holding it. Tam would have perhaps a day. A day of agony, and then nothing, and the god would sleep on, chained by twelve links instead of thirteen — weaker, angrier, and awake enough to seep. Dreams. Whispers. Slow rot in everything rooted near this mountain. For a century, perhaps two. Then the twelve fail too, because they were sized for thirteen."

Silence. Tam looked down at his arm. Oscar's knuckles had gone white where her fingers gripped her own sleeves.

Ysolde raised the second finger.

"The second: transfer the sigil whole. On the turning night, a volunteer may take the entire chain-link into themselves — all of it, the full weight the boy has carried in fragments. The sigil passes complete. The boy goes free. The volunteer becomes the sole bearer, the thirteenth chain, the prison entire — for as long as they live. Which, bound to a god's anchor, is a very long time indeed. You would not age as others age. You would not die as others die. You would feel the god's dreams every night and hold the door shut against them every morning, alone, forever, in whatever place you chose to sit and be a wall."

*Alone,* Maren thought. *Forever.* The word went through her like a needle finding an old scar, and she realized, with a strange floating calm, that she had already decided. Perhaps she had decided years ago, back when she'd taken in a frightened girl with burn-scarred hands and taught her knots and stars and how to be brave quietly. Perhaps love had been making this decision the whole time, patiently, the way water makes a canyon.

"I'll do it," Maren said.

It came out simply. That surprised her. She had rehearsed harder versions of this speech on the walk down — dramatic ones, tearful ones, ones with rhetorical flourishes. What emerged instead was just a woman's voice in a lantern-lit room, steady as a hearth.

Every head turned. Oscar turned slowest.

"No," Oscar said.

"Maren—" Wren started.

"You don't get to decide that yet," Oscar said, louder, and now she wasn't looking at Ysolde at all, she was looking at Maren, and her eyes were bright and furious and terrified, and Maren thought: *there she is. My girl. My stubborn, brilliant girl.* "You've been back among us for two days. You nearly died on the pass. You're still gray around the mouth, I can see it, everyone can see it—"

"I'm eighty-one years old, Oscar Oyelaran," Maren said. "Gray around the mouth comes with the territory."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It is *not*—" Oscar's voice cracked, and she stopped, and dragged a breath in through her nose the way Maren had taught her, once, in a kitchen that no longer existed, over a pot of tea that had gone cold because neither of them wanted the lesson to end. "You don't get to volunteer. Volunteers choose. You're choosing *because* you think nobody else will, and that's not choosing, that's — that's throwing yourself on the fire because you've decided you're the most expendable thing in the room."

"And what," Maren asked gently, "would you call what you were planning? Because I've known you three years, girl, and I watched you take Varga's offer apart in your head the moment she made it. Don't think I didn't see. You were going to trade yourself for the caravan. Some bargain where you carry the boy's burden and everyone else walks free. Am I close?"

Oscar said nothing, which was answer enough.

"There it is," Maren said. "We are a pair of fools, you and I. Cut from the same stubborn cloth. So let us be fools together for a moment and reason it through, since reasoning is supposed to be my trade." She ticked points off on her fingers, hedge-witch style, the way she'd once tallied herb measures. "One: the vessel must be willing, truly willing, and must have lived long enough to know what they're refusing. Tam is a child. No. Two: the vessel must be strong enough in the gift to hold the anchor without cracking. Most here are kind, and kindness isn't the same as strength of craft. Three: the vessel must be someone the god cannot tempt. Ysolde will confirm this, I expect. The god offers you your deepest want. It needs a soul whose deepest want is small enough to refuse."

"And yours is?" Ysolde asked, watching her with those ancient, amused eyes.

"A cottage," Maren said. "A garden. My girl safe, and tea that stays hot. The god can't build me a cottage. It can't make Oscar forgive herself for running, which is the want under all her wants. It offers power, gold, resurrection, glory — and I'd wave it off like a wasp at a picnic." She smiled. "I am, frankly, ideal."

"You're *old*," Oscar said.

"I'm *experienced*."

"You'd be alone. Forever. Under a mountain. While I—" Oscar's voice failed again. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You sent me away to keep me safe. You burned your village's name off every map. You hid for three years rather than risk being seen, and now you want to sit in the dark for eternity and call that a bargain?"

"Yes," Maren said. "Because it's not the dark I mind, love. It's the losing." She reached across the table, slowly, giving Oscar every chance to pull away — she didn't — and covered her girl's scarred knuckles with her own. "Listen to me. I buried a husband. I buried a daughter. I have buried friends until grief got so familiar it started doing chores around the house. I know exactly what forever-alone costs, because I've paid its little cousin again and again. And I also know this: if I let that boy die, or let anyone else chain themselves in his place, I will spend whatever years remain to me knowing I had the strength to stand between and stepped aside. That is a loneliness worse than the mountain's. That one follows you into sunlight."

The gallery hummed. Somewhere below, the god turned over in its sleep, and the lantern flames all leaned the same direction, like grass in wind.

"Counterpoint," said Brannic Ferro.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't spoken since the threshold. Now he leaned forward, big hands flat on the stone, and Maren saw the ledger-keeper in him come to the surface — the man who weighed everything, because weighing was how he survived losing.

"Life-debt," he said. "Your founder knows about mine. I owe the Hollow. I owed it before I ever took the job moving the boy." His jaw worked. "I lost a daughter to the first hunt. Fifteen years old. They burned her registration papers in front of her and then they burned her. I have been paying debts ever since because it's easier than thinking about her." He looked at Maren, and his eyes were wet and hard at once, which Maren privately considered the most honest expression a man could wear. "I'm fifty-three. I'm strong. I'm mean enough to hold a door shut against a god, ask anyone I've ever haggled with. And my deepest want is a thing no god can give, because she's ash. So by your own arithmetic, witch, I'm ideal-er."

"That's not a word," said Doss.

"It is in my ledger."

"Nobody is volunteering anything tonight," Oscar said, standing, palms on the table, and for a moment Maren saw her not as the runaway mule-guide but as the woman she was becoming — the one who'd led seven fugitives through a flooded mine and faced down a witch-hunter captain under a parley flag. "Nobody. We have nineteen days. We just learned the rules *tonight*. Ysolde — you said you've had nine hundred years to look for a third way. Look again. Look with all of us. Thirteen moons, thirteen hands — that was *my* idea, and everyone told me it was impossible, and it wasn't. Maybe splitting the sigil isn't the only impossible thing that isn't."

Ysolde was quiet a long moment. The lantern light made her laugh-lines into silver seams.

"When I was young," she said at last, "I believed every problem had a key, and the wise merely knew where the locks were kept. Nine hundred years has taught me otherwise. But—" and here she raised one small hand before Oscar could speak, "—nine hundred years has also taught me that the moment I stop looking is the moment I deserve to stop existing. So. Yes. We look. All of us. Nineteen days, and every eye, and every craft, and every mad idea welcome." Her gaze slid to Maren, and gentled. "But hear me, hedge-witch. If the nineteenth day arrives and there is no third way, the choice stands as I gave it. Destroyed or transferred. And I will not permit a child to burn nor a stranger to be conscripted. Whatever happens between now and then—" her eyes flicked, briefly, meaningfully, toward the far end of the table, where Captain Varga sat very still, "—the choice will be made by the willing."

Maren squeezed Oscar's hand once and let go.

Later — much later, when the others had drifted off to cots and salt-cave fires and the gallery held only the two of them and the humming dark — Oscar spoke without looking up from the knot she was worrying in a length of trail-cord. Maren knew that knot. It was the one Oscar used for fear.

"If it comes to it," Oscar said, "and there's no third way. Promise me you'll wait. One day. Just one day after the turning. Let me look for one more day."

Maren considered lying. She was, after all, very good at it, and had practiced on this exact girl for years with mixed results.

"One day," she said instead. "But only if you promise me something back."

"Anything."

"When this is over — whichever way it goes — you stop hiding. Knots in fences and marks on stones and a name whispered like it's contraband. You walk in daylight and you sign your work." Maren smiled, tired and fierce. "I didn't teach you magic so you could spend it being invisible."

Oscar's knot stilled. Above them, faint through miles of stone, the moon was rising toward its thirteenth turn, and far below, the god dreamed, and dreamed, and waited to be offered something worth wanting.

Neither of them heard the soft footsteps retreating up the gallery stairs. Neither of them saw Nettie pause in the shadows, hand pressed to her mouth, or the way her eyes went — guiltily, helplessly — toward the eastern passage, where a hunter's campfire glowed beyond the mountain's skin.

Nineteen days, Ysolde had said.

They would not get nineteen.
