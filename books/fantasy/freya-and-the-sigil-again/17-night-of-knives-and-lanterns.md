## 17. Night of Knives and Lanterns

* * *

Sera had counted the lanterns three times before she admitted what she was doing.

Forty-one lanterns hung along the Hollow's upper galleries, each one a small warm heart of tallow and glass, and every one of them was a thing her sister would never see again if the papers didn't come. That was the arithmetic she'd done in the dark of the salt caves, over and over, turning it like a stone in her pocket: one sister against four hundred strangers. One sister against a war.

It had seemed so clean, that night. Ilse Varga had sat across from her at the edge of the moor with snow in her red hair and no judgment on her face at all — just patience, the terrible patience of someone who had already won.

"Registration papers," Varga had said. "Signed. Sealed. Your sister walks into any town in the crown's reach and buys bread like a person. You know what she is, Sera. You've watched her hide it since you were children. She deserves to stop hiding."

And Sera had said yes, because Lark did deserve to stop hiding, because Lark had spent eleven years swallowing her gift down until it made her sick, because love was supposed to be worth something more than sentiment.

She had not let herself think about the word for what she'd done. Not once, not all the way back through the passes, not while Lark braided her hair by the fire and told her about the boy with the sigil, about Wren's ridiculous soup, about how maybe — maybe — this place could be home.

Now she stood at the mouth of the third gallery with a hunter's signal-lamp cupped in her shaking hands, and she thought about it.

The lamp was cold. Unlit. All she had to do was strike the flint, hold it high for ten breaths, and set it on the ledge where the men below could see it. Ten breaths. Then she could slip away to the lower tunnels, find Lark, put the sealed papers in her sister's hands, and never explain anything ever again.

Her thumb found the flint.

"Sera?"

Lark's voice, from behind her. Bright, breathless, carrying the particular excitement of a twin who has found something.

"You have to see this," Lark said, hurrying up the gallery path, lantern-light catching her grin. "The astronomer — Pell? He's got the old star-charts out, and he says the thirteenth turn isn't just a night, it's a whole hour, an entire hour when the moon stands still, and he wants everyone to watch it together, even the children, even Doss, who pretended to be asleep but I saw his eye open—"

"Lark."

"—and Wren's making honey-bread, actual honey, Brannic had some hidden in the mule-packs this whole time, the great sentimental fraud—"

"Lark." Sera's voice cracked on the second try. "I have to tell you something."

Below them, far below, in the dark throat of the mountain, something went *click*. The sound of a flint striking steel.

Sera looked down at her own hands. The signal-lamp was still there. Still unlit. Her thumb had stopped moving without her permission.

But somewhere else in the Hollow — someone else had lit theirs.

* * *

Oscar heard the first scream come up through the floor of the world.

She'd been in the founder's archive with Maren, arguing — they'd been arguing for two days now, gently, stubbornly, the way family argues — about who would carry the sigil on the thirteenth turn. Maren kept saying *me* in the calm voice she used for weather. Oscar kept saying *no* in the voice she'd learned from smugglers.

Then the scream, thin and distant, and both of them went still as deer.

"The gate," Maren said.

"The gate," Oscar agreed, and they ran.

The Hollow's entrance was a wound in the mountainside disguised as a rockfall, and Brannic Ferro had spent three days pretending it wasn't his problem. "I move cargo," he'd grumbled. "I don't defend architecture." But tonight he stood in the gap with a smuggler's boarding-axe in one hand and a lantern hooked to his belt, and around him the runaways had formed themselves into something that almost resembled a line — Doss with his old soldier's spear, the twins side by side, Wren with bandages already looped over her shoulder like a scarf.

They came through the rockfall in a flood of torchlight. Crown livery, black and gold, thirty of them at least, boots finding purchase on the scree with the horrible competence of trained killers. And ahead of them, walking unhurried, her sword still sheathed, came Captain Ilse Varga.

"Hold the line," Doss said quietly. "Hold the line, hold your nerve, and aim for the knees. Knees don't grow back."

"They do," said Wren. "Slowly."

"Wren, my dove, you are not helping."

Oscar arrived at a dead sprint and skidded to a halt beside Brannic, chest heaving. "How many?"

"More than us," Brannic said. "Fewer than I'd like for them." He rolled his shoulders. His face in the lantern-light was grey, and older than it had been that morning, and absolutely steady. "Get them back. The galleries. This gap narrows twenty paces in — I can hold it narrow."

"Brannic—"

"I moved cargo my whole life, girl." He didn't look at her. He was watching the hunters come, counting them the way he counted coin. "You think I don't know what I'm for?" A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle: "Take care of the boy. Take care of all of them. That's the ledger settled."

"Your ledger will never be settled," Oscar said. "You'll die owing somebody."

"Aye." The ghost of a smile. "Wouldn't feel right otherwise."

Then the hunters hit, and there was no more time for talk.

* * *

Tam felt the battle before he heard it.

The sigil woke on his arm like a second heartbeat — hot, urgent, singing. It sang in images, as it always did: iron and fire, chains being tested, the buried god stirring in its sleep of oath-stone like a man rolling over in bed. But threaded through the god-song was something new, something human: fear. Hundreds of hearts beating fast in the dark, and the sigil heard them all.

He pressed his palm flat against the cavern wall and listened with his skin.

*Left,* the stone told him. *They're coming left, through the flooded gallery, six of them, quiet ones.*

He couldn't say it. He hadn't spoken in three years, and the words lived in him like birds in a closed room, beating against the glass. So he did the next best thing. He grabbed the nearest person — Pell the astronomer, who squeaked — dragged him to the wall, and pressed Tam's own finger to the stone, then pointed left, then held up six fingers.

Pell stared. "Six... coming from the left gallery. Through the water. Oh. *Oh.*" He turned and bellowed in a voice no one would have guessed lived inside such a skinny man: "LEFT GALLERY! SIX IN THE WATER!"

Doss peeled off with four volunteers, and Tam ran after them, because the sigil was pulling him like a fish on a line, because the god under the mountain wanted to watch, and because — though he'd have died before admitting it — Doss had started teaching him spear-drills three mornings ago, slow and patient, never once making him feel foolish, and Tam wanted very badly to be useful to someone who was kind.

The flooded gallery was waist-deep and black. Torches bobbed in it, six little burning eyes. Doss waded in first like a man going out to check on his garden.

"Well," the old soldier said pleasantly. "Evening, lads."

What followed was short, ugly, and nothing like the drills. Tam learned that real fighting smells of wet wool and blood, that spears are louder than you'd think, that Doss grunts when he kills, a small apologetic sound, like a man closing a door carefully. Two hunters went down. Three fled back the way they'd come. The last one lunged for Tam — for *him*, specifically, eyes fixed on his sleeve where the sigil showed black between the wet cloth — and Tam did the only thing his body knew how to do.

He went silent and still, and the sigil answered.

Black light poured off his forearm like ink in water. The hunter froze mid-strike, staring at the shifting mark, and whatever he saw in it — the god, the dark, the thirteen moons wheeling — made him drop his sword and run, screaming about chains, about a voice, about *the mountain is looking at me.*

When it was over, Doss put a heavy hand on Tam's shoulder and squeezed once. No words needed. Tam squeezed back.

Somewhere above, deeper in the fight, someone screamed a name. Sera's name. Lark's voice, cracked wide open.

* * *

Lark found her sister standing over a dead hunter with a signal-lamp in one hand and a stolen knife in the other, and neither of them knew, yet, which hand had done what.

There had been a moment — Lark would replay it for years — when Sera had stepped out of the shadows of the third gallery directly into the path of two hunters advancing on Wren from behind. There had been a moment when Sera could have simply stepped aside. Hunters killing witches was, after all, the arrangement. Instead Sera had driven a knife into the first man's thigh and shouted a warning, and the second hunter had turned, and struck, and now there was blood on the stones and Sera was looking at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

"Sera." Lark caught her before she fell. "Sera, look at me. Look at *me.*"

"He was going to kill Wren," Sera whispered. "He was going to kill Wren and I — I brought them here, Lark, I lit the lamp, I *lit the lamp*, they're only here because—"

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I've known since the toll bridge," Lark said, holding her sister's face in both hands, forcing those wild eyes to focus. "You sleep-talk. You've slept-talked since we were six. You said 'the papers' forty times between the bridge and the salt caves, and I lay awake every night deciding what to do about it, and do you know what I decided?" Her voice broke and she pushed through it. "I decided that if you'd traded our souls for mine, then the least I could do was stay beside you while you figured out how to give them back. And look at you. Look at your hands. You gave them back *tonight*, you stupid, brave, awful woman."

Sera made a sound that was half sob, half laugh. "I'm going to hang."

"We're all going to hang," Lark said. "Wren says the trick is to be hanged among friends." She wiped her sister's cheek with a rough thumb, leaving a smear of soot. "Come on. Wren needs binding up, and you're going to help, and afterwards you're going to tell Captain Varga exactly where she can put her registration papers. I want to watch your face when you do it."

* * *

At the gate, Brannic Ferro was losing.

Not the fight — the fight was holding, barely, the narrow throat of the passage choked with hunters who couldn't come at him more than two at a time, and Brannic had been fighting in narrow places since before their captain was born. But his left arm had gone numb where a blade had opened it shoulder to elbow, and his breathing had developed a wet rattle he recognized from other men's deaths, and the axe was getting heavier with every swing, and still they came.

Varga herself stepped forward at last, drawing her sword, and the hunters fell back to give her room.

"Stand down, smuggler," she said. "You've bought them time enough to die with dignity. Let me pass."

"Time," Brannic wheezed. "That's the trade, isn't it. Time for coin. Coin for blood." He spat pink. "I knew a girl once. My girl. First hunt, fifteen years back. They burned her name off the parish rolls like she'd never been." He lifted the axe again. "Everything since has just been me, walking toward this door."

"That's not a reason to die here."

"No," Brannic agreed. "It's a reason it doesn't matter much where."

Varga's blade flashed. Brannic caught it on the axe-haft — caught it, turned it, drove her back a step, two steps, three, roaring like the mule-train man he was, buying another ten seconds, another five — and then the second hunter, the one nobody had counted, the quiet one from the flooded gallery who'd lost his sword to a mute boy's black light and taken up a crossbow instead, put a bolt through the gap under Brannic's arm.

The big man looked down at it with mild surprise, the way he might regard an error in a ledger.

"Ah," he said. "There's my balance."

He went down slow, the way a felled tree goes down, and even falling he swept the axe in one last arc that took the crossbowman's legs out and dropped the passage's loose scree in an avalanche across the throat of the gate, sealing it — half-sealing it — buying minutes, precious minutes, with the last strength in his enormous stubborn body.

Oscar reached him through the dust.

His eyes found hers. Still steady. Still counting something.

"The ledger," he rasped. "Tell them. Tell them all of it. Every debt. Don't let them—" a cough, wet and long, "—don't let them grieve clean. Grief should be messy. Messy means it was real."

"Brannic—"

"And the honey-bread," he said. "In the third mule-pack. Wasn't hidden. Was saving it. For after." His hand twitched, searching, and Wren was suddenly there, catching it, pressing it between both of hers. "For after," he said again, softer. "Always believed in after. Foolish old..."

The sentence never ended. Some sentences aren't meant to.

Wren bowed her head over his hand and did not move for a long time, and the battle roared on around her like weather, and she let it.

* * *

Later — it might have been an hour, it might have been a year — the hunters withdrew.

They withdrew because the vaults of the Hollow fought back, because the flooded galleries swallowed patrols, because a hundred frightened people discovered that fear shared is courage multiplied. But most of all they withdrew because Captain Ilse Varga stood in the smoke of the broken gate, looked at the scree-choked passage, at the dead smuggler beneath it, at the lanterns guttering in the upper galleries where hundreds of faces watched her with hatred and grief and no fear at all anymore — and something in her calculation quietly failed.

"Fall back to the moraine," she ordered. "Regroup at dawn."

Her lieutenant hesitated. "Captain, the Custodian's orders—"

"The Custodian," Varga said, in a voice that carried, "is not here." She looked at the fallen gate a moment longer. At the axe lying in the rubble like a fallen standard. Something flickered across her face — doubt, or its first cousin — and then she turned and walked into the dark, and her hunters followed her out of the mountain like a tide going out.

In the upper galleries, the survivors came together the way survivors do: slowly, touching each other as if checking that they were real. Lark held Sera up. Sera held Lark. Doss sat on the ground with Tam beside him and taught him, silently, with two fingers against the boy's palm, the soldier's sign for *well fought* — and then, after a moment, the sign for *well done*, which is different, and means more.

Oscar stood over Brannic where Wren had laid him out with dignity, his axe across his chest, his face finally, genuinely at rest.

"Messy," she said aloud, to him, to the mountain. "We'll grieve messy. I promise."

And then, from deep below — from the burial vault, from under everything — the sigil-song rose through the stone floors, through boot-soles and bones, and every gifted soul in the Hollow felt it at once:

The god beneath the snow had felt the blood spilled above it.

And it was waking up anyway.
