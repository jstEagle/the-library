## 18. The Custodian Unmasked

The vault door stood open.

Oscar had climbed the last stretch of the Hollow's stair with her heart in her throat and a lantern in one hand and Maren's old knife in the other, and none of it—not the knife, not the lantern, not three years of running—had prepared her for that. The great seal-door of oath-stone, the one Tam's sigil had been cut from, the one older than any kingdom that had ever taxed a road or burned a village: standing open like a barn door left careless on a windy night.

Beyond it, the burial vault glowed with a light that had no source she could name. Not firelight. Not moonlight. Something patient and pale, like the memory of light kept in a jar.

And atop the vault itself, silhouetted against that glow, stood a figure in a mask of white porcelain, smooth as an egg, featureless as the moon.

"You're late," said the Custodian. The voice was dry and old and, absurdly, a little amused. "Though I suppose everyone is late, from where I stand. I've been waiting rather a long time."

Oscar stopped at the top of the stair. Behind her, faintly, came the sounds of the battle still rolling through the lower caverns—shouts, the ring of steel, Wren's voice raised in a healer's command. Somewhere down there Brannic was holding a gate with his body and his stubbornness and whatever else he had left. She should be down there. Every part of her screamed to be down there.

But Tam was up here. Or he had been. She'd followed the warmth of the sigil through the warrens of the Hollow, following a feeling in her own chest she couldn't explain—thirteen fragments now, thirteen hands, and hers one of them—and the feeling led here, to this door, to this figure.

"Where is the boy?" she said.

"Safe. Asleep, actually. The rite takes it out of them." The masked head tilted. "You did something remarkable tonight, Oscar Oyelaran. Thirteen bearers. Do you know how many archivists worked on the original seal? Forty-one. The finest minds of an age, and they could only manage one bearer and a lock. You managed thirteen and no lock at all." A pause. "I confess I'm trying to decide whether to admire you or undo you."

"That's usually my line," Oscar said, and started walking along the vault's rim, keeping her feet on the stone, keeping the lantern high. "People say it to me about once a week lately."

She was babbling. She knew she was babbling. It was what she did when terror wore a polite face. She babbled at toll-keepers and witch-hunters and now, apparently, at whatever this was.

"The hunters," she said instead, steadying herself. "Varga's hunters. They stopped fighting. Halfway through the assault they just—stopped. Lowered their weapons. Why?"

"Because I told them the truth," the Custodian said. "Or most of it. Truth is such a useful tool when you ration it properly." They gestured with one long, gray-gloved hand toward the open vault. "Come and see. You've earned seeing, if anyone has. And bring your lantern. It won't help, but it will make you feel better, and feeling better is half of courage."

Oscar did not come and see. She stayed exactly where she was, because Maren had taught her one lesson above all others—*never walk toward the thing that wants you closer*—and because her legs had quietly informed her they were on strike.

"No," she said. "You come down here. Or you talk from up there. But I've spent three years learning which invitations to refuse, and yours has too many teeth in it."

A silence. Then the Custodian laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startled out of them, the laugh of someone who hadn't been surprised in decades and found they'd missed it.

"Maren taught you that," they said. "I can hear her in it. *Never walk toward the thing that wants you closer.* She used to say it with her hands on her hips, scolding apprentices who wandered too near the hedge-gates." The mask turned, considering. "How is Maren?"

"Healed," Oscar said carefully. "Bound. Angry about both."

"Yes, that sounds like her." The Custodian sat down on the edge of the vault, letting their legs dangle over the drop like a child on a dock, which was somehow more unsettling than everything that had come before. "I sent her here, you know. Thirty years ago. Before your time, before the first hunt even. I sent her to keep watch on the door."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "You're lying."

"I am the last surviving archivist of the War Beneath the Mountains," the Custodian said simply. "There were forty-one of us. We built the seal. We bound the god. And then we discovered the problem that no one warns you about, when you cage a god." The gloved hand flexed, slow. "Cages leak. And everything that lives near a leaking god begins to want things. Terrible, specific things. The town of Vaelmoor grew rich and cruel in a single generation. The order that guarded the seal began hoarding knowledge, then hoarding power, then hoarding each other. My colleagues—one by one—they all became its instruments, and called it devotion. I broke the seal deliberately, thirty years ago, to let the corruption escape into something smaller. One boy. One sigil. One bearer who could be moved, hidden, kept away from power."

"You put it in a *child*."

"I put it in the only vessel that could hold it without wanting anything," the Custodian said, and there was something raw under the dryness now. "A child who cannot speak cannot bargain. Cannot covet. The god offers gifts, girl—it offered me immortality and I took it, and look at me, look what I am, a porcelain egg sitting on a grave for thirty years. Tam was the first pure vessel we ever found. The sigil chose him before he could choose anything, which I have told myself every night since is mercy and every morning since is a lie."

The lantern shook in Oscar's hand. She made it stop shaking. That was a skill too, one of the ones nobody thanked you for.

"So the hunt," she said. "Varga. The registrations. The burning passes. All of it—that was you."

"I needed the gifted gathered," the Custodian said. "Not harmed—I gave orders against harm, though orders travel badly through frightened men—but gathered. Because on the thirteenth turn, the seal can be remade. Properly. Stronger than the original. And I needed—" the mask dipped, almost sheepish, "—materials."

"Materials," Oscar repeated.

"The sigil itself. The boy. Broken open at the turning, the god's essence drawn out and recast into a new seal of my design. One that doesn't leak. One that doesn't corrupt. One that holds forever." The Custodian spread their arms, and the pale light behind them flared, and for just a moment Oscar saw what they wanted her to see: a vision, golden and clean, mountains free of the buried weight, kingdoms unpoisoned, an age without gods rotting underneath them. "A golden age, Oscar. I have worked thirty years for it. Is that so monstrous?"

And the horrible thing—the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that would keep her up nights—was that it wasn't. Not on its face. It was the same argument Varga had made under the parley flag, the same argument Maren had made volunteering for the vault, the same argument Oscar herself had nearly made on the moor when she'd thought about destroying the sigil and herself with it. Everyone in this whole wretched story kept offering themselves up as the price of everyone else's safety, and calling it wisdom.

"It's monstrous," Oscar said, "because you didn't ask him."

The mask went very still.

"He's a person," she said, and her voice cracked and she let it crack, because some things shouldn't be said smoothly. "He's nine years old and he likes salt bread and he's afraid of deep water and last week he learned to write his own name in frost and he practiced it until dawn because he was so proud he couldn't sleep. He's not materials. He's not a vessel. He's *Tam*. And every single person down in those caverns fighting your hunters right now decided—he decided—to split that sigil thirteen ways so that no one would ever again have to be what you made him. So no one would ever be hunted for carrying it. So no one would ever be *used* for carrying it. And you want to know the difference between us?" She pointed the knife at the vault, at the mask, at thirty years of careful terrible planning. "We asked. All thirteen of us asked each other. That's the whole difference. That's everything."

For a long moment there was only the sound of the distant battle, and the pale light breathing.

Then boots on the stair behind her—many boots, moving fast but not charging—and Oscar spun, knife up, and there was Captain Ilse Varga, ash-reader, hunter, the woman who had cornered her under a parley flag and made the enemy's argument better than anyone. Behind her a dozen hunters, weapons sheathed, faces grim and uncertain as men who have just watched the floor move.

"Captain," the Custodian said pleasantly. "Good. I need the vault secured and the boy brought to—"

"No," Varga said.

The word landed in the vault-light like a dropped coin.

"I read ash for eleven years," Varga said, climbing onto the vault's rim beside Oscar, close enough that Oscar could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the burn-scars on her knuckles. "I read the ambush site on the moor. I read the burn patterns at Thornfell. I read the ash of every village your 'gatherings' got out of hand in. And tonight, while your hunters fought, I read the vault door." She held up her hand, and in the palm lay a curl of blackened parchment, and Oscar recognized the script on it—archivist script, the same flowing hand that covered Maren's map fragment. "Your orders. Your contingency orders, left with my patron's seal, in case the rite failed. In case the thirteen-bearer plan succeeded." Her voice went flat and cold as a winter river. "*If the sigil cannot be recovered whole, the bearers are expendable. Recast requires essence; essence may be drawn from bearers post-mortem.*"

"Weighing them," Oscar whispered. "You were going to weigh them. Like cargo."

"The golden age has costs," the Custodian said.

"Everything has costs," Varga snapped, and for the first time Oscar heard something other than iron in the captain's voice—heard the grief under it, the leash, the woman who had been handed a beautiful argument and a bag of gold and told it was righteousness. "I lost soldiers to your hunt. I burned my own honor to fund it. And you were going to render *children*." She turned to her hunters. "Captain's orders. The Custodian is relieved of command. Take the mask."

They moved. The Custodian rose—fast, impossibly fast for something thirty years old—and the pale light flared, and Oscar felt the fragment of sigil in her chest go hot, thirteen small fires answering one another across the cavern—

And then the light guttered. Because Tam was there.

He must have woken and felt it too, the way all thirteen of them felt each other now, a compass of belonging pulling through stone. He came up the stair with frost still melting off his sleeves, hair sleep-wild, and planted himself between the Custodian and the hunters, and looked up at the porcelain mask with the flat, unimpressed stare of a small boy regarding a large beetle.

The Custodian froze.

"You," they said softly. "You shouldn't be able to— the god sleeps in you, it can't—"

Tam reached up and, with the unhurried confidence of someone who had spent his whole life being handled by people who thought they knew best, pushed the mask.

It came away easily. Of course it did. Masks always do, Oscar thought, when the person wearing them has forgotten they're wearing one.

Beneath it was an old woman's face. Ordinary. Tired. Human—appallingly human, cheeks soft with age, eyes wet and wide, a face that might have belonged to anybody's grandmother, anybody's neighbor, anybody's friend. The most dangerous face in the world: a face that looked like it might offer you soup.

"Archivist Sefla Hune," Varga said quietly, reading the face the way she read ash. "The records list you dead. Killed at the fall of the archive."

"The records were kind," Sefla Hune said. Her voice without the mask was smaller. Older. "Dead women don't have to watch what their work becomes." She looked at Tam, and her composure finally, fully cracked. "I'm sorry," she said, and it sounded true, and it sounded insufficient, and it was both. "I truly believed—"

"I know," Oscar said. "That's the worst part. Everybody in this story believes something."

Below them, the battle-sounds were dying—not ending, but changing, the ring of steel giving way to voices, arguments, the sound of enemies discovering they'd been aimed at each other by a grandmother in a mask. Somewhere down there, Wren was probably already triaging both sides. Somewhere down there, Doss was probably complaining about it, which meant things were returning to normal.

Sefla Hune sat back down on the vault's edge, suddenly looking every year of her long unnatural life, and the hunters closed around her gently, the way you handle something that might break—or explode—and Oscar let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the proclamation went up on every tavern door in Thornfell.

"Well," she said. "That's that, then."

"No," said Tam.

Everyone turned. He was crouched at the lip of the open vault door, frost blooming under his fingertips, words forming in the ice in his careful, proud, newly-learned letters:

**THE GOD IS AWAKE.**

And beneath the vault, beneath the mountain, beneath thirteen small warm fragments scattered through thirteen chests, something vast turned over in its sleep—and opened one eye.

* * *

The light changed first. The pale sourceless glow sank from gold to red, like sunset arriving indoors, and every fragment of the sigil sang at once—a high thin note Oscar felt in her teeth, in the scar-tissue of her palms, in the place behind her ribs where Maren's map fragment used to sit.

Thirteen voices cried out across the Hollow, all at once, all saying the same thing in thirteen different mouths: *it's looking at us.*

"Oh," said Sefla Hune, in a tiny voice, staring down into the red-lit dark of the vault. "Oh, no. The rite. Splitting it—it didn't weaken the chains. It woke the dreamer. I never accounted for—"

"You never account for anything," Oscar said, grabbing Tam's shoulder, pulling him back from the door. "That's been the whole problem."

Above the mountains, through a hundred feet of stone, something exhaled.

The thirteenth turn was four hours away. And the god beneath the snow was awake to see it.
