## 19. Thirteen Moons, Thirteen Hands

The Hollow had never been quiet, not really. Even in its deepest hours there was the drip of water through living stone, the murmur of the hot springs, the breathing of three hundred hidden people stacked into caverns like cups in a peddler's pack. But tonight the silence was different. Tonight the whole mountain seemed to be holding its breath along with them.

Oscar stood at the mouth of the burial vault and watched the thirteenth moon rise through the great crack in the cavern roof.

It was a sliver of a thing, thin as a paring knife, and it hung directly over the vault's black doorway like a coin someone had balanced on the world's edge. Nineteen days of preparation, argument, terror, and one collapsed smuggler's road behind her. Below her feet, beneath forty feet of oath-stone, something old turned in its sleep and dreamed of chains.

"Oscar." Maren's voice, from behind. Her mistress moved slowly these days—the Hollow's magic had healed her body but left her walking like a woman wading upstream. "They're waiting."

"I know."

"You've been staring at that moon for an hour."

"I'm trying to decide if it looks more like a knife or a coin," Oscar said. "I can't tell if that's ominous or lucky."

Maren came to stand beside her, following her gaze. "Your grandmother used to say a thin moon was a door left ajar."

"Was my grandmother right about anything?"

"She was right about you." Maren's hand found hers, rough and warm. "Come on, love. Whatever you're going to say, say it before you lose your nerve. I've known you since you were seven. You lose your nerve exactly once per idea, and then it never comes back."

* * *

The great cavern held everyone. Three hundred fugitives, packed onto the stone tiers, lanterns strung on wires overhead so the whole space glowed amber and gold, like the inside of some enormous honest heart. Wren sat near the front with Doss, his arm still bandaged from the drowned mine. The twins sat apart from each other—Sefa at the edge with her face turned away, Lira beside her with a chair-length gap between them that might as well have been a canyon. Brannic's ledger lay closed on the seat where he should have been. Varga stood at the back with her hunters, out of uniform, looking like wolves who'd been invited to a wedding and weren't sure whether they were guests or dinner.

And Tam.

Tam sat front and center, because Tam always did now, and his arm—his poor arm, marked from wrist to shoulder in shifting black script—lay bare in the lantern light. The sigil moved tonight. It crawled and rearranged itself like ants disturbed in their nest, and every few minutes a low hum rolled off it and made the nearest lanterns flicker.

Oscar stepped forward onto the flat stone that served the Hollow for a speaking place, and three hundred conversations died at once.

"I'm going to tell you what I know," she said. "All of it, even the parts where I look like a fool. And then I'm going to ask you for something impossible, and you can all tell me no, and we'll figure out something else, or we won't, but nobody gets lied to tonight. Not anymore."

She told them. About the vault below, and the god chained under oath-stone. About the Custodian, the archivist who had broken the original seal believing a caged god poisoned everything near it—and who had planned, all along, to reforge the sigil into a weapon once the god was loose enough to catch. About Varga's hunters standing down, and the crown's hunt collapsing without the Custodian's gold, and how none of that mattered if the moon finished its turn and the seal failed on its own.

"And here's the problem," Oscar said. "The founder explained it to me this morning, and I've been chewing on it since. On the thirteenth turn, the sigil has to go somewhere. It can't just be destroyed—that kills whoever bears it. Tam would die. Or it transfers whole, into a volunteer, who becomes its prison forever. One person. Alone. For the rest of their life, hunted by anyone who ever learns what they carry."

Her eyes found Maren. Maren looked back at her steadily, and did not flinch, because Maren had volunteered that morning and hadn't told her until after, the way Maren did everything—as though love were a thing you did quietly and mentioned later, if at all.

"So those are the choices we've been given," Oscar said. "One dead boy, or one imprisoned woman. A sacrifice or a prison. And I've been thinking—" her voice cracked, and she stopped, and started again, "—I've been thinking that every terrible thing that's happened to us happened because somebody decided one person could carry something alone. The sigil. The secret. The guilt. The hunt. Every witch burned in Thornfell was burned alone. Every runaway on this mountain ran alone. That's the whole trick of it. That's how they beat us. Not with fire. With *alone*."

She let that sit. Somewhere in the tiers, a baby fussed and was hushed.

"So here's my impossible thing." She turned to Tam. "May I?"

Tam nodded. He'd been practicing words again—Wren worked with him every evening—but tonight he kept his silence, and pointed instead at his own chest, and then spread both hands wide toward the whole cavern. *All of us.* He'd understood before she'd said a word. He always did.

"The sigil is a chain," Oscar said. "But chains are made of links. Thirteen moons. Thirteen turns. What if it didn't have to be one link? What if we broke it into thirteen pieces—thirteen fragments, small enough to live inside a person without devouring them—and thirteen of us carried them? Not a prisoner. Not a sacrifice. Bearers. Together. No single soul hunted, because no single soul holds it. No hand able to wield the god alone, because no hand *has* it alone. You'd feel each other. Always. Like—" she groped for the word, and found it laughing at her, "—like a compass that points at your family."

Silence. Then Doss, the old soldier, cleared his throat.

"Thirteen volunteers," he said. "Out of three hundred. That's a long queue, girl. Who picks?"

"Anyone who wants it," Oscar said. "That's the whole point. Nobody's chosen. Nobody's *sent*. You walk up there because you choose to, or you don't, and either way you eat breakfast tomorrow with the rest of us."

"And if it doesn't work?" That was Sefa, from the edge, her voice raw. "If splitting it kills all thirteen? Or worse—if it wakes him?"

"Then we'll know very quickly," said the founder, from the shadows by the vault door.

Everyone turned. The founder of the Hollow came forward into the lantern light—a small, ancient person whose age refused to settle on any particular century, wrapped in robes that might have been gray or might have been the memory of gray. Their eyes were the color of deep water.

"The rite will show its teeth early," the founder said. "If the fragments cannot hold, they will pull back together rather than destroy themselves. Chains are conservative things. If it fails, we will know within minutes, and we will be left with the original two choices and considerably less time." They smiled, and it was not entirely comforting. "So. Volunteers. Thirteen hands. Who among you is fool enough?"

Oscar raised her own hand first. "Me."

Maren, second. "Me, and don't argue, Oscar Oyelaran, I've been dying to get back at you for three years of worry."

Wren stood. Doss grumbled something about being too old for this and stood anyway. Lira rose without looking at her sister. Varga came forward from the back with four of her hunters, which drew a ripple of unease through the crowd until Varga said, flatly, "I helped build this hunt. I'll help bury it," and the ripple settled. An astronomer-fugitive named Pell who'd calculated the turn. A salt-cave cook called Hesta who said, "Someone ought to feed it properly if we're keeping it." A quiet ferryman named Aldous. And last, a girl of perhaps fifteen, Juno, one of the newest runaways, who walked up the tiers with her chin set and said only, "Nobody gets left alone. Not on my mountain."

Thirteen. Tam counted them twice on his fingers, then looked at Oscar and nodded once, solemn as a judge.

"All right," Oscar said, and discovered her knees had opinions about the whole enterprise. "Let's go wake a god."

* * *

The vault was older than language, or close enough. Oath-stone walls carved with the same thirteen-moon pattern that crawled on Tam's arm, worn smooth by centuries of nothing. In the center, a circular floor of interlocking stones, each etched with a single link of a broken chain.

The founder directed them to stand in a ring, twelve around the circle, and Tam at the center, because the sigil lived in him and had to be persuaded to leave gently.

"It will not want to split," the founder warned. "A chain prefers to hang together or hang someone. You must each offer it a reason. Not a bargain—it will try to make bargains, and you must refuse them all—but a reason. Why you'll carry it. Hold that reason in your mind like a lit lamp, and when the fragment comes to you, show it the flame."

"What reason works?" asked Hesta the cook, practical to the last.

"Honest ones," said the founder. "Lies burn out fast in that company."

Above them, through the crack in the vault ceiling, the thirteenth moon slid into position over the oculus, and light fell—thin, silver, precise as a surgeon's line—onto the circle of stones.

The stones began to sing.

Not loudly. A hum first, felt in the teeth, then in the ribs, then in the oldest part of the mind that remembered being prey. The sigil on Tam's arm flared black-white-black, and Tam cried out—the first sound Oscar had ever heard him make that wasn't frost-scratched—and dropped to one knee.

"Tam—" Oscar started forward.

He held up a hand. *Wait.* His other arm blazed, the script lifting off his skin like smoke given letters, coiling upward into the moonlight, gathering, gathering—

And the god spoke.

It did not use words, exactly. It used wants. Oscar felt it move through the ring like wind through wheat, touching each of them, offering. She saw Wren sway, saw her jaw tighten against whatever had been promised—healing, probably; Wren had lost patients she still mourned. She saw Doss straighten like a man called to a battle thirty years cold. She saw Varga's fists clench, and the captain shake her head once, hard, at something only she could hear.

Then it reached Oscar, and it showed her Maren's cottage, whole and unburned, and herself at seventeen kneading bread while Maren scolded her for salting the charm-jars wrong, and the proclamation never nailed to any door, and the road home always open, always safe, always *hers*—

"No," Oscar said aloud, and meant it down to her boots. "I want that. But not from you. I'll build it myself, badly, with these people. That's the whole point of me."

The presence withdrew from her—not angry, she thought. Almost approving. A chain testing its links.

Tam stood at the center with his arms raised and the sigil swirling above him like a storm learning to write, and Oscar knew—Irrevocably, the way you know a bridge will hold—that he was refusing too, refusing everything, and that his refusal was the hinge on which the whole night swung. His lips moved. No sound came. But the storm above him shuddered, and bent, and began, link by link, to come apart.

"Now!" the founder cried. "Bearers—your reasons! Show it why!"

And thirteen voices spoke at once, not in unison but in tangle, overlapping, stumbling, human:

"—because my daughter deserved a world that didn't burn her—" (Brannic's ledger, open at last in Doss's hands.)

"—because I fed half this mountain and I'm not stopping now—"

"—because I read the stars and they say *together*, they've always said together—"

"—because I built the hunt and I'll carry its leash myself—"

"—because my sister is still in the room, even if she won't look at me—"

"—because nobody gets left alone—"

"—because I love her, and love isn't a prison, whatever he thinks—"

Oscar spoke last and loudest, both hands raised: "Because survival shared is survival! Because alone was always the lie! Take all of us or take none!"

The sigil *broke.*

Not with a crash—with a sound like a bell struck underwater, like thirteen doors opening in one house. The storm divided, whirled, and flew—one thread of black fire to each bearer, striking each of them over the heart, sinking in with a warmth that was almost painful in its gentleness, like a coal passed hand to hand by people who trusted each other completely.

Tam collapsed. Oscar caught him before he hit the stone.

For one heartbeat, two, ten—nothing. The vault silent. The moonlight steady. The god beneath them utterly, perfectly still.

Then Tam opened his eyes, looked up at Oscar, and said, in a voice rusty as an unused gate but unmistakably, gloriously his own:

"That tickled."

Oscar laughed, and it came out as a sob, and she didn't care who saw.

* * *

They were still on the vault floor—thirteen bearers sitting in a ring like children after a bonfire, comparing the strange new warmth behind their breastbones—when the first crack ran through the ceiling.

Small. A trickle of dust. Pell the astronomer looked up and went pale. "The rite unbound the oath-stone's binding function," he said. "The vault was sealed *by* the sigil. We've taken the keystone out of the arch."

The second crack was not small.

"Everyone out," Oscar shouted, and the vault became motion—bearers hauling each other up, the founder moving with surprising speed, Tam gripping Oscar's hand as dust rained silver through the moonlight and the ancient stones groaned like a ship in weather. Behind them, as they fled up the passage, the burial vault folded inward on itself with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and the last thing Oscar saw, turning in the falling dark, was the thirteenth moon through the collapsing oculus—

—and something beneath the rubble, vast and patient, settling deeper into its sleep.

Unchained. Uninvited. And no longer anyone's alone.

Tam squeezed her hand. Above them, muffled by stone, faint but unmistakable, twelve other hearts beat in time with hers—and one of them, she realized with a jolt, was beating wrong. Too fast. Too cold.

Varga's voice echoed down the passage, sharp with alarm:

"Something's wrong with the fragments. Everyone stop—*stop!*—and look at your hands."
