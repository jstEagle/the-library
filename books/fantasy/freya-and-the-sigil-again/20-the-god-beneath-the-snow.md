## 20. The God Beneath the Snow

The sigil did not want to be split.

Tam felt it the moment the rite began—thirteen hands laid in a ring around the burial vault's broken seal, thirteen voices murmuring words that Wren had stitched together from three dead languages and one living argument. The black mark on his forearm pulled against the others like a child refusing to let go of a sleeve. It stretched. It thinned. It sang.

And beneath the singing, something old opened one eye.

*Oh,* thought Tam, who had lived with this voice for as long as he could remember and had never once answered it aloud. *There you are.*

He had expected terror. He had expected the god to roar, to flood him the way it flooded his dreams—images of mountains walking, of chains of oath-stone snapping link by link, of a sky the color of an open wound. Instead the voice that moved through the splitting sigil was almost gentle. Almost patient. The voice of something that had been buried so long it had learned manners.

*Little bearer,* it said, and the words arrived not as sound but as warmth, spreading up his arm like sunlight through a window. *You are giving me away. Do you know what you are giving away?*

Tam stood in the ring between Oscar and Maren, his bare palm pressed flat against the vault stone, and he did not answer. He never answered. That was the whole architecture of him: a boy built around a silence, the way a pearl is built around a grain of sand.

But tonight the silence was cracking, because the sigil was cracking, and everything inside both of them was spilling out into the lantern light.

Around the ring, the thirteen stood shoulder to shoulder in the collapsing dark. Dust sifted from the vault ceiling in pale ribbons. Somewhere above them, the mountain groaned—the Custodian's machines still tearing at the rock even now that Varga's hunters had turned their blades aside. Brannic was dead at the gate. There would be time for grieving later, Oscar had said, her voice steady in the way that meant it was costing her everything. Right now there was only this: thirteen people, one god, and a choice being made in real time by everyone willing to hold a piece of it.

Wren began the transfer-words again. The sigil shivered.

And the god began to bargain.

* * *

It went for Doss first.

Tam felt it happen the way you feel weather change—a pressure shift, a hush. The old soldier's jaw tightened. His hand, scarred and square, pressed harder against the stone.

Later, Doss would describe it in four words, because Doss described everything in four words or fewer. But Tam felt all of it: the god sliding into the old man's memory like a hand into a glove, finding the shape of what he wanted most, and offering it up whole.

A morning. A specific morning, twenty-nine years gone. His daughter—his first daughter, the one before the hunt took the second one—standing in a kitchen doorway with flour on her nose, laughing at something he'd said, alive, alive, alive. Come back, the god whispered into his bones. Hold this piece of me and I will give you back every morning I burned out of the world. I keep them all. Nothing dies where I am. I am very good at keeping.

Doss's eyes were wet. Tam saw it from two feet away, saw the tears standing in the old soldier's lashes, saw his throat work.

"No," Doss said. It came out rough. He cleared his throat and said it again, louder, the way he'd once barked orders across a battlefield. "No. She wouldn't want to come back as a bribe." His voice cracked down the middle. "And I don't get mornings back. I get mornings forward. That's the deal. That's always been the deal."

He pressed his palm flatter to the stone, and a thread of the sigil's black light crawled off Tam's arm, across the gap, and settled into the lines of Doss's knuckles like ink soaking into wood.

One. Tam counted it the way he counted everything, silently, carefully. One refusal.

The god turned its attention to the twins.

* * *

Sefa went next. Tam knew her best of the two—she was the one who'd traded secrets for her sister's papers, the one whose guilt had a taste he could almost sense through the sigil, bitter as frostbite. The god found her want fast and easy, the way water finds a crack.

Her sister. Whole and unbetrayed. A version of last winter where Sefa had never walked into the hunter camp, never spoken a name, never sold a gate. Take me and take the shame too, the god offered, silky and kind. I will unmake your worst night. I will make you innocent again.

Sefa made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"You can't," she said, and her voice shook but held. "That's the whole point of a worst night. You carry it. You—" Her breath hitched. "My sister forgave me while we were fighting for our lives in a burning tunnel. If I trade that away, I trade the forgiveness too. No. Keep your clean slate. Mine's earned."

Two.

Then Liss, the other twin, gripping her sister's hand hard enough to whiten both their knuckles. The god offered her safety, Tam sensed—a life where no one she loved could ever be hunted again, walls high and thick and permanent. A cage with her whole family inside it, safe forever.

"We tried that already," Liss said quietly. "The Hollow is a cage with good lighting. I'm done with cages." Three.

Wren refused a garden—the healer's paradise, every sickness curable, every wound reversible—and said, "Things end. That's why they matter. Next offer, please, I'm busy." Four. Old Yeva of the salt caves, who had joined the circle at the last moment with her arthritic hands and her no-nonsense squint, refused her dead husband and told the god, "He snored. You're romanticizing." Five. Six was the astronomer-fugitive Pell, who was offered the sky itself—all of it, every star, the whole turning map of heaven—and said, "I've spent my life learning the sky doesn't need me to own it to be beautiful. Pass." Seven and eight were two Hollow folk Tam barely knew, a stonemason and a midwife, who refused a rebuilt city and an endless line of safe births respectively, each with the same stubborn finality: *that work isn't mine alone to have; it's mine to share.*

Nine was Varga.

Tam had been afraid of this one. He had watched the captain fight her way to their side in the tunnels, watched her turn her hunters with three sentences and a drawn blade, and he had not trusted it—not fully, not yet. Trust, for Tam, was a bridge built plank by plank, and Varga had contributed perhaps four planks.

The god offered her the academy. The real one, the extinct one, the one whose battle-craft Oscar carried like a scar. Restored. Reborn. With Varga as its first true captain in a hundred years, hunting nothing, guarding everything, finally the thing she had trained her whole life to be.

Varga stood in the ring in her scorched half-cloak, blood on her cheek that might have been her own, and was silent for a long time.

"I wanted that," she admitted, to the god, to the circle, maybe to herself. "I want it still." Her jaw set. "But I've seen what happens to institutions that promise safety. They start collecting people to protect them from." She exhaled slowly. "Build it yourself, Captain. That's the job. Refused."

Nine refusals, and the sigil on Tam's arm was nearly transparent now, its black threads scattered across nine pairs of hands, and the god's voice was getting quieter and stranger, and Tam understood—with the particular clarity of someone who has spent his whole life listening—that the god was running out of arguments.

It had one left. It saved it for Oscar.

* * *

Tam felt it reach for her and braced himself, because Oscar's want was the biggest one in the circle. He'd known it since the toll bridge, since the moor, since the drowned mine where she'd confessed her fear to people she'd known for weeks. Oscar Oyelaran wanted, more than anything, to stop being alone.

*Maren,* the god offered. Not the bound Maren of the Hollow, not the mother-shaped ghost who had sent the proclamation rumor and called it rescue. The real one. The one from before. Whole, free, hers, forever. And a road with no endings on it. And a family that never fractures. Take my largest piece, little guide, and I will build you a home that cannot break.

The cavern groaned. Dust fell. Oscar's hand trembled against the stone—Tam could see it trembling, could see her looking at Maren across the ring, at the woman who had lied to protect her and loved her anyway and was standing here now, old and healed and bound and *hers*, offering herself as the god's eternal prison just hours ago.

"Here's the thing about a home that can't break," Oscar said, and her voice wobbled and steadied, wobbled and steadied, like a candle flame deciding to live. "If it can't break, it can't grow either. Everything I love has cracked somewhere along the way, and every single crack is where somebody else got in." She laughed, wet and sudden. "You're offering me a finished house. I don't want a finished house. I want the one we're building. It leaks."

Ten.

Maren refused next—refused freedom, which was her deepest want, freedom from the founder's will, freedom from the Hollow, freedom to be Oscar's mother without ancient bargains sitting between them like a third chair at the table. "Love that costs nothing teaches nothing," she said simply. "I'll earn my way out. Refused." Eleven.

Twelve was a boy named Fen from the Hollow, fourteen years old, who had volunteered for the circle because someone had to and he was tired of waiting to be chosen. The god offered him significance. To matter, permanently, to be the boy who held the god, remembered forever. "I'd rather be forgotten and kind," Fen said, "than remembered and owned." Twelve.

And then there was one.

* * *

The last thread of the sigil hung on Tam's arm like a cobweb of night, thin and bright and humming. Thirteen pieces, twelve given away. The god gathered whatever remained of its voice and turned to him, and the whole cavern seemed to lean in.

*Little bearer,* it said, and now the gentleness was gone, and underneath it Tam could hear the truth of it at last: vast, and tired, and terribly lonely, and proud, and frightened. Frightened. A god, frightened. *They have all refused me. But you—you have never spoken. You have kept me your whole life. You know me better than any of them. Give me your silence and I will fill it. I will give you a voice like a landslide. I will give you your name—*

And there it was. The knife under the kindness.

Because the god knew. Of course it knew. It had lived under his skin since before he could walk. It knew that Tam's deepest want was not freedom, not safety, not family, though he wanted all of those with a hunger that scared him. It was smaller than that and infinitely larger.

He wanted to say his own name.

He had been mute since the fever took him at three years old—mute since before anyone had asked him who he was and waited for an answer. Everyone called him Tam. The smugglers called him cargo. The hunters called it. Even the caravan, gentle as they'd become, even Oscar, who never flinched from him—even they had named him out loud in rooms he sat in, kindly, carefully, *the boy, the boy with the sigil, poor Tam.* And somewhere inside him lived the word his mother had given him before the fever, the true word, the one nobody had ever heard him say, because he had never said anything, because his mouth was a locked door and the word was behind it gathering dust.

*I will speak it for you,* the god offered. *I will say your name into the world. You need never struggle again. Every word you've ever wanted, poured through me. Isn't that what you want? To be heard?*

Tam looked down at his arm.

And he thought about the twelve refusals he had just witnessed. Doss crying and refusing. Sefa choosing her shame. Varga choosing the long hard way. Oscar choosing the leaking house.

Every one of them had been offered the shortcut and taken the road instead.

His mother hadn't named him so a god could say it. She'd named him so *he* could.

Tam lifted his head. He looked at Oscar, and Oscar was already looking at him, and her eyes were wide and wet and absolutely certain, and she nodded once, small, like she was handing him something across a great distance.

The god waited. The mountain groaned. The lanterns guttered.

Tam opened his mouth.

Nothing came out. Of course nothing came out; doors locked for fifteen years don't swing open on the first push. Panic rose in him, hot and familiar, the old panic of the locked room—

And then he stopped pushing and simply *walked through*, the way you step off a ledge into water, and the word came up from wherever words go when they wait, up through his chest, past the place where the fever had lived, past the place where the god had lived, out into the cold air of the burial vault:

"Tamsin."

His voice came out rusty and cracked and far too loud and completely, perfectly ordinary. A boy's voice. Just a boy's voice.

"My name," he said, and his voice broke and mended and broke again, "is Tamsin Oyelaran-Vey. My mother gave it to me. Not you."

He pressed his palm flat against the stone, and the last thread of the sigil rose off his arm like smoke off a snuffed candle, and he gave it away freely, thirteenth of thirteen, and it settled into the stone between his fingers and went dark.

The god did not rage. Tam had braced for rage. But what he felt instead, in the last instant before the seal closed, was something that undid him worse than any roar could have: relief. Enormous, ancient, exhausted relief—the relief of a thing that had been holding its breath for a thousand years finally being allowed to put down its arms.

*Well answered,* the god said, very faintly, from very far below. *All of you. Well answered.*

And then it slept. Not chained—there was nothing left to chain it with, thirteen fragments sleeping warm in thirteen willing bodies, scattered like embers no wind could gather. Unchained, and uninvited, and—for the first time in the whole long history of its burial—unwanted, in the way that a guest is unwanted when the host has politely, firmly, thirteen times over, shown it the door.

The vault shuddered. The rite was done. The mountain was coming down around them whether they liked it or not.

"Tamsin," Oscar said.

He turned. She was crying and laughing at once, dust in her hair, her hand outstretched toward him as the first crack split the floor between the ring of bearers.

"It's very nice to meet you," she said. "Now *run*."

* * *

They ran.

Thirteen bearers and forty-some fugitives and one traitor twin carrying her sister on her back, up the vault stairs as the burial mountain folded itself closed behind them like a hand making a fist, and Tam ran in the middle of the pack with snow falling through the shattered roof and his own name ringing in his ears like a bell that had finally, after fifteen years, been allowed to strike.

Behind them, deep beneath the snow, something enormous sighed and turned over and dreamed of nothing at all.

Ahead of them, dawn was coming up gray and gold over the Thornfell passes, and Oscar was shouting orders, and Wren was counting heads, and somewhere in the crowd a boy's new voice—cracked, rusty, unstoppable—was asking, over and over, delighted, breathless:

"What happens now? What happens *now*?"
