## 21. After the Turning

The snow on the Hollow's terraces had stopped falling sometime in the night, as if the sky itself had decided to hold its breath and watch what the living would do next.

Oscar stood at the edge of the burial ground with a shovel that was too big for her hands and exactly right for the work. Below her, the valley opened in white terraces where the hidden gifted had kept their gardens for two hundred years—terraces now half-trampled by hunters' boots, half-burned by lantern-fire, and yet, absurdly, still holding. A row of winter cabbages poked through the snow near the eastern wall like stubborn little green fists.

"You're staring at the cabbages again," Wren said, coming up beside her with two mugs of something hot and smelling of pine.

"I'm admiring them," Oscar said. "Anything that survives Varga's cavalry deserves admiration."

"Varga's cavalry is currently eating our soup."

"That too."

They drank. The mug warmed Oscar's palms all the way to the wrist, and beneath the skin of her left forearm—under the bandage, under the new scar—the sigil-fragment sat quiet as a banked coal. Not dead. Sleeping. She could feel it the way you feel a splinter you've decided to live with: present, patient, hers.

Thirteen fragments. Thirteen bearers. Somewhere down in the caverns, Tam was teaching Doss to play knucklebones and cheating at it with the serene confidence of a boy who had spent years unable to speak and intended to make up for lost time. Somewhere on the south wall, Maren stood watch out of pure habit, wrapped in three blankets and refusing all offers of a fourth. And somewhere in the guest quarters that had become a cell, one of the twins sat alone with a door she had locked from the inside.

But first: the graves.

* * *

They buried Brannic Ferro at dawn, because he had always been an early man and would have complained about sleeping in.

The ground was hard as iron, so they built the cairn instead—river stones hauled up from the frozen shallows, each bearer of the thirteen laying one, then everyone else who wished to. It took most of the morning. By the end the cairn stood taller than Doss, who had insisted on placing the final stone himself and then had to be helped down from the pile, grumbling, by both twins before remembering there was only one twin left to help him.

Oscar laid her stone last. She'd found it down by the water: flat, grey, ordinary, except that when she turned it over there was a knotwork pattern in the lichen, natural as anything, that looked almost exactly like the trail-mark she used to scratch into fence posts meaning *safe road ahead.*

"He'd have hated this," she said quietly. "All the ceremony."

"He'd have hated being dead more," said the surviving twin—Sera, Oscar reminded herself; Sera was the one who stayed—and the laugh that went around the circle was wet and grateful and exactly what Brannic would have wanted, which was people arguing about him.

Maren spoke the old words, the hedge-witch words, the ones Oscar had heard over chickens and birthing goats and never once over a smuggler with a ledger full of life-debts. *Earth to earth, road to road.* When she finished, she pressed her palm flat against the cairn and left it there a long moment, and Oscar saw her lips move around something private.

Later, Oscar asked her what she'd said.

"Told him his debt was paid," Maren said. "He argued. Even now, I expect, he's arguing."

* * *

The second grave took longer, because it wasn't a grave at first. It was an argument.

The two fugitives who had died on the moor covering the escape—Corrin the tinker and little Ashka who could sing birds out of trees—they had no bodies to bury. Varga's riders had taken the field, and though the captain herself had turned her hunters aside at the last, the moor was three days back along a road none of them meant to travel again in winter.

So the Hollow did what the Hollow had always done for its unburied dead: they planted a tree.

Wren chose a rowan, "because Corrin whittled rowan spoons and complained about it constantly." Sera brought a ribbon from her sister's hair—her own hair now, since Ilsa had cut hers off in the night, but that came later. Doss drove the stake. Tam, solemn as a magistrate, scratched into the frozen earth with a stick the only word he'd ever written freely: *HERE.*

And Oscar, who had spent three years hiding every gift she had, stood before a tree that would grow tall enough to see from the passes and let her magic rise through her hands like breath after a long dive—warming the roots, waking the wood, coaxing the rowan toward spring a season early. Green showed at the tips of the bare branches while the snow still lay around it. Nobody flinched. Somebody applauded. An old woman Oscar had never met shouted, "Do the other side!"

She laughed until she cried, and then she cried a while longer, and Wren stood next to her the whole time not saying anything, which was its own kind of skill.

* * *

The reckonings happened in the founder's hall, which was less a hall than a cavern with pretensions, lit by lanterns and hung with two centuries of carved names.

Varga came first. She arrived without her sword—not surrendered, just left behind, which somehow meant more—and stood before the assembled survivors of the Hollow with the posture of a woman reporting a defeat she intended to learn from.

"The hunt is over," she said. "Not because I say so. Because the gold has stopped." She held up a purse, heavy, and dropped it on the floor where it landed with the dull clink of crown coin. "The Custodian paid us in minted gold from the old war treasuries. With the Custodian gone, the treasury seals are worthless—forgery, once anyone looks closely. My hunters haven't been paid in six days. Half have already gone home. The rest will follow." Her jaw worked. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking to be useful. I know every safe house, every register, every name the crown collected in ten years of hunting. I can unmake what I helped build."

Silence. Then Doss, arms crossed, said, "Can she cook?"

"Doss," Wren hissed.

"It's a fair question. We're feeding two hundred people and half our cooks ran off with the cavalry."

Varga blinked. "...I make a passable stew."

"Passable stew," Doss declared to the room, "is citizenship anywhere I've ever served." And that, more or less, was how Captain Ilse Varga became the Hollow's quartermaster, an outcome that would have gotten Doss executed in any army he'd ever served in, and which he therefore considered the finest joke of his long career.

* * *

Ilsa's reckoning was harder, and Oscar didn't stay for the middle of it.

She saw the beginning: Ilsa standing before the assembly, hands bare, telling them everything—the masked rider on the moor, the messages passed, the registration papers promised for her sister's safety, the gate-code given on the third night of the assault. She told it plainly, without excuse, and when she finished she said only, "Brannic died holding the gate I opened. Ask me anything, or ask me nothing. Both are fair."

What Oscar heard afterward, secondhand, from Sera, was this:

Sera had walked across the hall in front of everyone and taken her sister's face in both hands and said, "You did it to save me. I forgive you. That part's done, and no vote changes it."

And then Ilsa had said, "That's worse. Forgiveness means I don't get to punish myself into a hole and call it penance. Forgiveness means I have to actually *live* somewhere knowing what I did."

"Yes," Sera said. "Welcome to it."

But Ilsa couldn't. Not there. Not among the lanterns she'd sold the map of. So she chose exile—not banishment; the Hollow offered her a home, and refused, heartbreakingly, to make it a punishment—and asked instead for the one task nobody else wanted: walking the passes alone in early spring, carrying word of the new proclamation to every village, every toll-house, every tavern door where the old ones were still nailed up. Taking down the huntsman's notices with her own hands, one nail at a time.

"She'll be back," Sera said, when she told Oscar, standing at the gate as Ilsa's small figure disappeared up the white switchbacks. "By summer. She'll come back because she'll run out of tavern doors, and there's nowhere else in the world with decent stew."

Oscar squeezed her hand. "Then we'd better keep the stew warm."

* * *

The sigil-fragments made themselves known slowly, the way spring does.

The first time, Oscar thought it was the fire. She was sitting with Tam over supper when warmth bloomed under her bandage, faint and specific, like a hand resting briefly on the inside of her wrist. She looked up. Tam was looking at her too, rubbing his own arm, and his face split into a grin.

"Warm," he said. He had maybe forty words now, deployed with sniper precision. "Warm when close."

"Close," Oscar agreed, and felt the warmth answer, and understood.

By week's end they'd mapped it: thirteen points of heat scattered through the valley, each one aware of the others. Near Wren, a soft pulse like a heartbeat. Near Maren, steady as a hearthstone. Near Varga—this surprised everyone—a low, even glow, as if the fragment had decided she was trustworthy before the rest of them had finished voting. When any two bearers came within twenty paces of each other, the marks warmed. When all thirteen gathered, as they did on the first clear night, standing in a ring in the snow, the warmth joined up into something that felt less like magic and more like being *known.*

"A compass," Doss said, watching the frost steam off thirteen forearms. "Pointing at each other."

"Not north," Tam said. "*Home.*"

Nobody corrected him. There was nothing to correct.

Oscar caught herself testing it in small ways after that—walking past the kitchens just to feel the pulse quicken as she neared Wren, taking the long way to the wall so her path crossed Maren's. It was, she admitted to herself, a bit like scratching a trail-mark and then walking back to check someone had seen it. Proof of presence. Proof of *together.* Three years of invisibility, undone by a coal of sleeping god under her skin that refused, bless it, to let her be alone.

* * *

On the last evening of winter, Oscar climbed to the terrace above the burial cairns with two mugs again, because some rituals deserved repeating.

Maren was already there, of course, watching the passes.

"They say the roads will open in a month," Oscar said. "Snowmelt's early. The rowan's budding already—it's showing off."

"Mm." Maren sipped. "And what will you do when they open?"

"Guide." Oscar said it simply, and was startled how easy it was. "Openly. No more knots and shadows. There are hundreds more out there—runaways, registered folk, families. Someone has to walk them in."

"Someone does." Maren looked at her sidelong, that old hedge-witch look that had made Oscar feel transparent at fifteen and seen at twenty-three. "You know I lied to you. About the proclamation rumor. About flushing the runaways here."

"I know."

"And you're not angry?"

"Oh, I'm furious," Oscar said comfortably. "I've scheduled it for spring. I find grief and gratitude don't share a grave well if you dig the hole angry."

Maren laughed—a real one, rusty, startling a couple of ravens off the wall—and then went quiet. "Your mistress hid things. Your smuggler died for strangers. Your hunter stews for witches now." She shook her head. "Strange family."

"The best kind," Oscar said. "The kind you choose twice."

Below them, lamplight was kindling window by window through the Hollow—two hundred small fires against the dark, and among them, if she reached inward, thirteen warm points arranged like a constellation someone had drawn just for her. Tam's voice drifted up from the caverns, mid-story, loud and unstoppable, Doss's laughter rolling under it like a river.

"One more thing," Maren said. "A rider came up the pass this afternoon. Wouldn't give a name. Left this." She held out a scrap of paper, snow-damp, travel-worn.

Oscar read it by lantern-light. Her breath went out slow.

It was a proclamation—but not the crown's. The seal was unfamiliar: a thirteenth moon, waxing. And beneath it, in careful print:

*To the gifted of Thornfell and beyond: You are sought. Not for the register. For the road. Guides are gathering. Come to the lighted windows.*

"They're fast," Oscar murmured.

"Someone is," Maren said. "And they knew the Hollow's location—which, until a month ago, four living people did."

Oscar folded the paper slowly, feeling the sigil-fragment stir against her wrist—not warning, exactly. Not warmth either. Something between. A question.

"Well," she said, tucking it into her coat. "Looks like spring's bringing company."
