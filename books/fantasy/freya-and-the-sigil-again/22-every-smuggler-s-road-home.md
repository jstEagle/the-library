## 22. Every Smuggler's Road Home

The thaw came late to Thornfell that year, and when it came, it came all at once—like a held breath finally let go.

Oscar stood at the mouth of the Kettle Pass with her boots in six inches of meltwater and her face turned up to a sky so blue it looked freshly painted. Snowmelt roared down the mountainsides in a hundred silver threads. Somewhere below, a lark was making an absolute fool of itself with joy. The world smelled of wet stone and pine sap and the particular green sweetness of things deciding, all at the same moment, to grow.

"Your left boot's leaking," Maren said beside her.

"I know."

"You've known since the ford."

"I know that too."

Maren snorted and kept walking. Three years of hiding had taught Oscar to read every shadow; one winter at the Hollow had taught Maren to stop pretending she didn't notice everything. It was, Oscar reflected, a fair trade. Her old mistress moved slower now—the Hollow's magic had mended what the hunters' blades broke, but it had done so honestly, without pretending the mending hadn't happened—and she walked with a carved staff she claimed was for the ice but which everyone knew was for leaning on when she wanted to make a point about patience.

They crested the shoulder of the pass together, and there it was: the road.

Not the smugglers' road—not the knife-edge trail that threaded through the drowned mine and the salt caves and the shrine ruin, the road that existed only in memory and in the knotwork patterns Oscar had spent three years tying into fence posts and bridge rails. This was the old high road itself, the king's road, wide enough for two carts, running down through the pass like a river finding its old bed after a long diversion.

And on it, people.

A family with a handcart, the father carrying a sleeping child on his shoulders. Two women leading shaggy ponies loaded with everything they owned. An old man walking alone with his coat over his arm like a man who had decided the weather no longer got a vote. They were coming *up* the pass, toward the Hollow, toward safety—and they were doing it in daylight. In the open. Without looking over their shoulders every fourth step.

Oscar watched them until her throat hurt.

"Well," Maren said. "You did say you'd reopen them."

"I said I'd think about reopening them."

"You said, and I quote, 'I'm going to walk every smuggler's road from here to the sea and nail a sign to every door between.' You were very drunk on Wren's elderflower wine at the time."

"That wine should be classified as a weapon."

"It *was* a weapon. It got Doss to dance." Maren leaned on her staff and looked out over the valley. "The roads are open, girl. All that's left is to tell people."

* * *

Telling people turned out to be Tam's favorite part.

He'd found his voice in the snow outside the shrine ruin—one word scratched in frost, then another, then a flood—and he had apparently decided that after years of silence, no sentence worth saying deserved to be said quietly. He talked the way spring water ran: constantly, joyfully, and over everything.

"—and Brannic says the toll-keeper at Gorse Bridge still asks for truths instead of coin, except now he mostly gets *lies*, because everyone tells him the same lie, which is 'I have nothing interesting about me,' and he HATES it—" Tam scrambled up onto the cart bench beside Oscar, all elbows and enthusiasm, his sleeve slipping down to show the faint warm shimmer of sigil-fragment on his forearm. Thirteen fragments now, scattered across thirteen arms across the Hollow and beyond, each one no bigger than a thumbnail, each one humming softly only when another bearer was near. "—and Wren says if I ask one more question during rounds she's going to sew my mouth shut with catgut, but she was smiling when she said it, I checked—"

"Breathe," Oscar said.

"I am breathing. Breathing is how the words get out." He settled, though, and for a moment just watched the road unroll ahead of them: the high road, the honest road, the one she'd sworn never to use again and now used every day. "Do you think the proclamation will work? The new one?"

Oscar thought about the paper rolled in the oilcloth at her feet. She'd written it herself, four drafts, and burned three of them in the stove because they sounded either too much like begging or too much like a trap. The fourth draft was short. Short was harder.

"There's only one way to find out," she said.

The first tavern stood where the high road met the valley floor, its sign—a fat brass goose—creaking in the wind. The kind of place Oscar had avoided for three years. The kind of place where proclamations got nailed to doors and men read them aloud in voices like knives.

She tied the mule to the rail, took the roll of paper, a hammer, and a handful of nails, and stood in front of the door for longer than she meant to.

Tam, mercifully, said nothing. He just stood beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched her arm, and waited.

Over the old notice. Over the crown's seal and the word REGISTER and the list of punishments that had sent her running into the dark at nineteen years old, someone had already nailed a plank of pale new wood, sanded smooth. On it, in the crown's own hand—Varga had told her, later, that the order had come down from the palace within a month of the Custodian's fall, as if the ink had been waiting for permission—were the words:

*Let it be known throughout Thornfell and the high passes: the register is closed. The hunts are ended. No person shall be marked, taxed, or pursued for the nature of their gift. The gifted are called to service, not suspicion.*

It wasn't charity. Varga had been clear about that, in her flat captain's way. The crown needed weather-readers and wound-menders and stone-listeners, and it had finally done the arithmetic that fear had always made impossible. But arithmetic was how the world actually changed, most of the time. Oscar could live with that.

Below the royal proclamation, someone had left space. A strip of blank wood, waist-high, at exactly the height of a hand holding a nail.

Oscar unrolled her own paper and read it once more before pinning it up.

*To any soul on the roads who does not know the way:* it began. *There are guides again. We know the passes, the fords, the tolls, and the doors that open. We were runaways ourselves. Come to the Hollow of Thirteenth Moon, or flag down any cart with a green knot tied to its rail, and we will take you where you're going. No fee for the frightened. No questions for the quiet. — O. Oyelaran, guide, late of nowhere in particular.*

She hammered it up beneath the crown's words, four nails, square and true. Then, because the blank strip was there and her hands were shaking slightly and she'd promised herself she would:

She signed her name. Full name. *Oscar Oyelaran.* In ink, in public, on a tavern door, where anyone could read it.

It cost her nothing but courage, and courage, it turned out, was renewable.

Tam was grinning at her. "You wrote 'late of nowhere in particular.'"

"It's accurate."

"It's funny."

"It can be both." She stepped back and looked at the door: the old hunt above, the new invitation below, her own name bridging them. Somewhere inside the tavern, she could hear a kettle and low voices and, unmistakably, someone practicing something small and bright—a charm, maybe, or a song, the kind of sound that used to be hidden under floorboards and was now just... happening. Out loud. Before noon.

"Come on," she said. "We've got eleven more doors between here and the sea, and I made a promise while drunk."

* * *

They made a ritual of it, over the weeks. Tam would pick the tavern—he had opinions about signs, and distrusted anything with a weasel on it—and Oscar would nail up the notice, and Maren would stand in the doorway drinking whatever the house poured and telling anyone who'd listen that her apprentice was famous, which was a lie, and that she'd taught her everything she knew, which was also a lie, and that the stew was better at the Hollow, which was true and did wonders for the kitchen's pride.

Word traveled faster than carts. By mid-spring, people were waiting for them.

At the ferry crossing, a hedge-witch named Pell—nervous, freckled, gifted with making bread rise impossibly even—asked Oscar to check her knotwork, because she'd been hiding her gift in her loaves for twenty years and didn't know how to stop. Oscar sat with her on the dock for an hour and showed her how to let the magic sit on top of the work instead of underneath it. "Like salt," she said. "You don't hide the salt in the bread. You just put it in, and everyone tastes it, and nobody panics."

At the Gorse Bridge, the half-mad toll-keeper demanded a truth. Oscar gave him one: "I was afraid of this bridge for three years." He considered this, nodded gravely, and waved the whole caravan across without another word, which Tam swore was the most anyone had ever gotten out of him.

And at a crossroads inn with a weasel on the sign—Tam sulked magnificently—they found a girl of perhaps sixteen waiting on the step with a packed bag and a look Oscar recognized from mirrors, from three years ago, from every dark road she'd ever walked. The girl's gift flickered around her like heat-shimmer; she couldn't hold it in, and she flinched every time it showed.

"You the guide?" the girl asked. Her chin was up. Her hands were shaking.

"I'm the guide."

"They say you killed a witch-hunter captain."

"I didn't kill anybody. She's currently arguing with our cook about barley rations." Oscar swung down from the cart and offered her hand. "What's your name?"

The girl hesitated. Oscar waited. She knew how long this part could take. She knew the exact weight of a name you'd been hiding, and the strange lightness when you finally set it down in front of a stranger and it didn't break.

"Sable," the girl said. "My name's Sable."

"Hello, Sable. Climb up. Mind the boy—he talks."

"I do not talk," Tam said indignantly, from the back of the cart. "I *communicate*. There's a difference. Ask Wren. WREN! Tell her there's a difference!"

Sable laughed—startled out of her, the way laughter comes back to people, like birdsong after a hard frost—and climbed up.

* * *

Summer found them on the last leg of the tour, the coast road, salt wind and gull noise, and one evening in a cliff-top inn with a whale on the sign (Tam approved), Maren set down her cup and said, "You never asked me."

Oscar looked up from the map she was marking—green knots drawn at every safe crossing, a whole country slowly stitched into welcome. "Asked you what?"

"Why I sent the rumor. About the proclamation. Why I flushed every runaway in the north out of their holes and pointed them at the Hollow, knowing some wouldn't make it." Maren's face was calm, but her thumb moved along the rim of her cup, around and around. "You had the right to ask. You never did."

Oscar put down the charcoal. Outside, Tam was teaching Sable to skip stones off the twilight sea, badly, both of them laughing.

"For a long time I was too angry," Oscar said. "Then I was too busy. Then..." She turned her cup, thinking. "Then I started guiding people, and I understood. Fear keeps people alive, but it doesn't keep them *living*, and you can't talk somebody out of a hole they've convinced themselves is safety. Sometimes you have to burn the village down behind them." She met her mistress's eyes. "You lied to us to save us. I'm allowed to hate it. I'm not allowed to pretend I wouldn't have done the same."

Maren's breath went out of her, slow, like a knot finally untied. She reached across the table and gripped Oscar's wrist—hedge-witch fashion, pulse to pulse, the old greeting between women who worked with living things.

"You turned out," she said, voice rough, "better than anything I dared teach you."

"Don't get sentimental. You'll ruin your reputation."

"What reputation?"

"*Exactly.*"

They were still laughing when the door banged open and Tam burst in, soaked to the knees, triumphant, holding up a stone. "SEVEN skips! Sable only got five! Also there's a man downstairs asking for the guide, and he's wearing a crown badge, but Wren already checked him with the truth-charm and he's clean, and he says—" Tam paused dramatically, which he'd learned from Brannic's ghost-of-a-memory, invoked at every campfire, "—he says the queen wants to meet the woman who signed the tavern doors."

Oscar's laugh died in her throat, not with dread, for the first time in her life, but with something that took her a moment to name.

It was curiosity.

She looked at Maren. Maren raised her cup. She looked at the map on the table—all those green knots, all those open roads, her own handwriting covering the country like a blessing. She looked at Tam, bouncing on his heels, and past him through the window at Sable skipping stones in the last light, and further, at the darkening sea and the road home winding north through the passes, every mile of it known to her now, every mile of it hers.

Three years ago, a proclamation on a tavern door had made her run.

Tonight, one had made her *wanted*—in every sense—and she found she could bear it. More than bear it.

"Tell him," Oscar said, standing, signing her name to the day the way she'd learned to, openly, gladly, "I'll be right down."

And she was.
