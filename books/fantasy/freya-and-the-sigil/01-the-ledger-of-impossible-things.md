## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The lamp above Oscar Hartwell's desk burned with a steady, honest flame — no enchantment in it, no whisper of the uncanny. He had bought the oil himself from a chandler on Tallow Row who had never once asked his name, and he counted this among the small mercies of his life: that everything within arm's reach could be explained.

He dipped his pen, blotted it against the rim of the well, and wrote, in a hand so even it might have been set by a printer:

*Item 447. One reliquary finger-bone, purportedly Saint Ameline's. Provenance: unverifiable. Appraised value: 12 crowns. Note: bone is carp. Recommend reclassification as "devotional curiosity."*

He underlined *carp* twice and allowed himself the smallest satisfaction. Somewhere in the world there were men who fought duels over insults and women who wept at operas, and somewhere else there were hedge-witches muttering over cauldrons and kings marching their armies across other kings' fields. And here was Oscar Hartwell, twenty-six years old, senior keeper of accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, correcting a smuggler's fraud with two strokes of a pen. It was not glory. It was better than glory. It was *order*.

The counting room occupied the third floor of the guildhouse on Candlemaker's Stair, a narrow stone building that leaned companionably against its neighbor like a drunk against a friend. From its single window Oscar could see the rooftops of Corvane descending in terraces toward the harbor, tile after tile after tile, red-gold in the late afternoon light, gulls wheeling above the fish market with their small greedy cries. Nine years he had lived in this city, and he knew it the way a blind man knows a room — by touch, by sound, by the map he kept folded inside himself rather than any he'd ever drawn.

That was the point. He drew no maps. He left no maps.

Below him, the guild went about its evening business. The Guild of Impossible Things did not, despite its name, deal chiefly in impossibilities. Most of what crossed its ledgers was merely illegal: untaxed brandy, forged relics, love philtres cut with nothing worse than beetroot juice and confidence. The name had been Serel's idea, or Serel's predecessor's — a joke that had calcified into a brand. *Impossible things* sounded romantic. It let wealthy clients pretend they were buying wonder rather than contraband, and it let the city watchmen pretend they hadn't noticed the crates moving through the night market, because everyone in Corvane understood that a guild with a charming name was a guild worth tolerating.

But some fraction — perhaps one crate in fifty — was genuinely impossible. And those were Oscar's to catalogue.

*Item 448. One glass eye which weeps salt water of unknown composition. Owner reports the tears taste of the sea and that dreams of drowning have ceased since acquisition. Value: negotiable. Storage: dry goods, far wall.*

*Item 449. One key which opens no lock but has opened, per seller, three marriages and one monastery door. Value: 40 crowns. Note: do not test.*

He had written *do not test* on forty-one entries now. It was the closest thing the ledger had to a sense of humor, and he guarded it jealously.

Oscar had built this life the way a wren builds a nest — one stolen twig at a time, woven tight, hidden deep. He had arrived in Corvane at seventeen with a burn-scarred left hand, eleven crowns, and a story about being orphaned by the fever-boats out of Marsh Hollow. The story was true in every particular except the fever-boats. He had apprenticed himself, without papers, to a bookkeeper named Doss who drank, and when Doss drank himself into the harbor one winter night, Oscar had quietly finished Doss's clients' books so well that three of them offered him permanent work. He chose the guild because the guild paid best and asked least. Serel had looked at him across the interview table — Serel, then newly master, all sharp cheekbones and sharper smiles — and said, "You're a careful one, aren't you," and Oscar had said, "I'm nobody," and Serel had laughed and hired him on the spot.

*I'm nobody.* It was the truest thing he'd ever told anyone in Corvane, and the whole architecture of his life rested on it. No family name used twice in the same year. No debts. No lovers who knew where he slept. He paid his rent in coin, never credit; he drank at two taverns, never three; he walked home by six different routes according to a rotation only he understood. The other clerks called him Hartwell and assumed Hartwell was his name because people always assume the name you give them is your name. It was, in its way, the finest working he had ever done — not a spell at all, just nine years of small consistent lies laid down like courses of brick, until the lie bore weight.

And if sometimes, late at night, the orderliness of it all felt less like peace than like a held breath — if sometimes he woke with his scarred hand throbbing and a word half-remembered dying on his tongue, a word like *Freya*, though he never let himself finish thinking it — well. Everyone paid something for safety. He had simply read the price carefully before signing, which was more than most men ever managed.

The bell below rang the hour of Vespers, and with it came the knock.

Three raps, spaced wrong — too long between the second and third. That was Pell. Everything about Pell was slightly wrong-spaced, a big slow man with a carter's shoulders and a clerk's conscience, who had worked the receiving dock for six years and had never once lied to Oscar, which in the Guild of Impossible Things made him practically a holy relic himself.

"Enter."

Pell filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. His face, usually an open page, was doing something complicated.

"Evening, Master Hartwell. There's a crate come in off the *Merrow's Tithe*. Harbor run, out of Sallowmere." He hesitated. "Serel wants it catalogued tonight. Priority entry."

"Priority entries get catalogued tomorrow like everyone else," Oscar said mildly, capping his ink. "Unless it's perishable."

Pell's throat moved. "It's warm," he said. "The crate. It's warm, Master Hartwell. And it's — " He stopped, started again, and Oscar felt the first cold thread go through him, because Pell did not stop and start. Pell said things once, plainly, like a man laying bricks. "It's making a noise. Down in the hold. Like someone crying, but — wetter. I've been on the dock six years. I've heard cursed things cry. This isn't that."

Oscar sat very still for a moment. Then he took up the ledger, tucked it under his arm, and lit a second lantern.

"Well," he said. "Let's go meet it."

* * *

The receiving hold smelled of tar, brine, and the particular sourness of money changing hands in the dark. Crates stood in ranks down either side, stenciled with the false shipping marks the guild used — *CORVANE SOAP WORKS*, *M. & DAUGHTERS, FINE WOOLS* — each lie concealing its little truth. Lamplight swung on hooks overhead. Half a dozen porters lingered at the edges, pretending to work, drawn by the same unease that had brought Pell up the stairs.

The crate sat alone in the middle of the floor, apart from the others, as if even the dockhands' hands had known to keep their distance.

It was a standard sea-chest, oak-bound, unremarkable — except that frost should have covered it, coming off a northern run, and instead the wood glistened. Not with condensation. When Oscar knelt beside it and touched the planking, the warmth that met his fingers was unmistakably the warmth of a living body. Ninety-eight degrees, or near enough. Blood-warmth.

And the sound. Up close, it resolved into something worse than crying. It was a murmur — many voices, layered, overlapping, the way a congregation murmurs through a prayer they know by heart. Words surfaced and sank. He caught *please*, and *let her live*, and *I didn't mean it*, and a child's voice saying *thank you thank you thank you* over and over with a joy that curdled the air around it.

"Manifest," Oscar said, and his voice came out steadier than his hands felt.

Pell handed over the shipping paper. Oscar read it twice.

*One (1) crate, sealed, contents: ANSWERED PRAYERS, assorted. Shipper: R. Coll, Sallowmere. Condition on loading: inert.*

Answered prayers. The phrase had begun appearing in guild correspondence four months ago — first as rumor, then as rumor with prices attached. Prayers to the old gods, the ones who had gone silent a century past, the ones every educated person knew were dead or sleeping or had never existed at all. And yet lately, in scattered corners of the free cities, prayers were being *answered*. A widow's candle relighting itself. A lame man's crutch blooming. Small things, mostly. Impossible things. The guild's brokers had moved fast, as brokers do, and now the impossible was arriving by the crate-load, packed in straw like crockery.

Oscar had catalogued eleven such items already. A knot of rope that would not fray while its holder spoke a true regret. A coin that always landed mercy-side up. Each one had been strange. Each one had been *dry* — inert, finished, a prayer already answered and cooled into an object.

This crate was still answering.

"Pry bar," he said.

"Master Hartwell — "

"A priority entry can't be appraised sealed, Pell. And if whatever's inside is alive, it's cargo of a kind this guild has rules about. Open it."

Pell got the bar under the lid. The nails came out with a sound like small screams — or perhaps that was only the murmuring rising, gathering itself, the way a crowd hushes when the doors open. Warm air breathed out of the gap, smelling of beeswax and rain and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church that had been closed a hundred years.

The lid came off.

Straw, packed deep. And lying in the straw, dozens of them, hundreds — slips of paper. Prayer-slips, the kind the old faithful used to tie to temple trees or burn in offering-bowls, back when the temples still stood. They were written on in every hand: charcoal, pencil, a child's crayon, ink gone brown with age. Some were fresh. Some were yellowed decades ago, creased and soft as cloth, carried in a pocket through years of unanswered asking before someone finally told the asker, *try again, they're listening now.*

Every slip was glowing faintly. Every slip was damp — beaded with moisture, as though the paper itself were sweating, or weeping. And as the lamplight touched them, the murmuring swelled into a chorus, hundreds of voices speaking at once, and Oscar understood with a lurch that he wasn't hearing an echo. He was hearing the prayers *happening*. Still happening. Answered and answering, caught mid-miracle, stacked in straw and shipped freight-class to Corvane.

"Saints preserve us," whispered one of the porters, and spat, old habit against bad luck.

"Nobody touches anything," Oscar said. He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the bookkeeper's voice, the voice he retreated into when the world stopped adding up. "Pell, seal the hold. Nobody in or out but me and you. If Serel asks, tell him I'm doing a full inventory before appraisal."

He lifted a single slip from the straw with two fingers, the way he'd handle anything fragile and possibly armed.

The paper was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything about this crate was warm, blood-warm, alive. The prayer written on it was short, in a laborer's blunt hand: *For my daughter. Fevered nine days. Let her live. Please. I will give anything.*

Beneath it, in different ink — newer, darker, in a hand that made the bottom drop out of Oscar's stomach — someone had written the answer: *She lives. Paid: one memory, freely given. Which memory is the god's choice. — V.*

*V.*

Not a signature. An initial. But Oscar knew the hand. You do not spend three years of your childhood watching someone write — grimoires, receipts, letters, the looping marginal curses she muttered at her own mistakes — without carrying that hand with you forever. He had spent nine years not thinking about that hand. Now it lay in his palm, warm and damp, answering a stranger's prayer in ink that was still drying.

*Freya.*

The name rose in him like floodwater and he slammed the gates down on it, the way he had practiced, the way he had gotten frighteningly good at. But his scarred hand — the sigil branded into it at fourteen, the mark of his apprenticeship, faded nearly to nothing these last nine years — his hand *burned*. Once. Sharp and brief, like a coal touched and withdrawn.

"Master Hartwell?" Pell's voice, far away. "You've gone white as sailcloth."

"Fine," Oscar said. "It's fine." He set the slip down with exaggerated care, as though slowness could undo what knowing had done. Around him the murmuring went on — *please, please, thank you, let her live* — hundreds of voices, and somewhere among them, he suddenly could not help wondering, was there one addressed to him? Was there a slip in this straw with his name on it, answered in that same dark ink?

No. That was fear talking. Freya Vane did not know he existed anymore. That had been the entire point. He had walked out of her hedge with his hand branded and his heart shut and he had never once looked back, and she owed him nothing, least of all —

Least of all what? He didn't let the thought finish.

"Catalogue it," he told Pell, and was already climbing the stairs two at a time, ledger clutched to his chest like a shield. "Entry 450. Contents: answered prayers, active. Status: pending. I'll write the full entry upstairs."

Upstairs, in the lamplight, with the door bolted and the window shut against the gulls, Oscar sat before the ledger and found his pen hovering and shaking above the line.

*Item 450,* he wrote at last. *One crate, contents as manifested. Answered prayers, assorted, ACTIVE. Provenance: Sallowmere. Answering authority: unknown.*

His pen stopped. The honest entry — the complete entry — required more. It required the initial. It required the hand he recognized. It required him to write, in his own meticulous script, the first sentence of the end of his hidden life.

Oscar Hartwell had built himself out of small consistent lies, and he knew exactly how much weight they bore, and he knew — with the dread of a man watching the first hairline crack cross a foundation — that the gods had come back to a world that had buried them, and they had come back *answering*, and one of their answers was signed with the letter V.

He wrote: *Note: do not test.*

Then, beneath it, because the ledger deserved the truth even if he couldn't give it yet:

*Note: the answers are in a hand I know.*

Outside, the gulls cried over Corvane, and the crate below murmured its hundred warm voices in the dark, and somewhere north, in a hedge at the end of a road, a woman he had spent nine years forgetting set down her pen — though he could not know it — and looked, for no reason she could name, toward the south.
