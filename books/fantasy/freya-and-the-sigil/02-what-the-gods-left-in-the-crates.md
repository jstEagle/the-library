## 2. What the Gods Left in the Crates

Oscar did not sleep that night.

He told himself it was the ledger — quarter-end was coming, and Serel's books were a nest of deliberate tangles that only he could unpick — but the lie wore thin before midnight. The truth sat in the counting room below his narrow bed above the guildhouse stables: a crate of impossible things, still warm, still weeping, and a stain on his left hand that would not stop itching beneath its glove.

He had done what a careful man does with an anomaly. He had documented it.

The entry stood in his private ledger now, in his smallest, most defensible hand:

*Item 447. One crate, oak and iron, manifest origin "the Salt Road." Contents: eleven objects of unknown provenance, hereinafter "answered prayers." Condition on arrival: warm to the touch. Exuding fluid (clear, saline, non-corrosive). All eleven objects emit low sound when handled — see appended notes. Disposition: pending guildmaster's instruction.*

*Appended notes:* He had spent two hours with the crate after the others went home, because someone had to, and because the alternative was admitting how badly his hands shook. Eleven objects. A child's wooden bird that flapped its wings when you said *please*. A knot of grey yarn that untangled itself into a shroud-sized length whenever anyone wept nearby. A tin whistle that played a lullaby no one in Corvane could have known — except that Oscar knew it, knew it down in his marrow, knew it from a kitchen in a hedge-witch's house ten years gone, and had dropped the whistle as if it were a snake.

The fluid, he had decided, was seawater. Or tears. There was no honest way to distinguish them, and honesty, in the Guild of Impossible Things, was a matter of bookkeeping rather than confession.

Now, past midnight, he gave up on sleep, dressed, took the lamp, and went back down.

* * *

The counting room smelled of ink, tallow, and — faintly, wrongly — salt. The crate sat where he'd left it, centered on the long table under a canvas sheet, like a patient awaiting surgery. Around it, the guild's ordinary inventory kept its orderly silence: a jar of bottled luck from the Amber Coast; three vials of borrowed courage; a mirror that showed you your mother's face whether or not she lived. Ordinary things. Things with prices. Things that obeyed the old law of the trade, which was that everything impossible could be made possible for someone willing to pay, and everything paid for could be entered in a column.

The answered prayers fit no column. That was what had cracked something loose in him all day, he realized — not the warmth, not the weeping, but the arithmetic. Eleven items, and not one of them had a seller. No provenance, no chain of custody, no margin. They had simply *arrived*, the way rain arrives, the way grief arrives. And Oscar Hartwell had built his entire life on the principle that nothing arrived unbidden if you kept your ledgers clean.

He lifted the canvas.

The crate breathed out its salt-and-tallow smell, and beneath it something older — woodsmoke, dried herbs, the particular bitterness of yarrow steeped too long. His stomach turned over. He knew that smell the way you know a voice in a dream: you couldn't place it until it was already inside you.

*Careful,* he told himself. *You are a man who counts things.*

He counted. Eleven objects, confirmed. He lifted each one with tongs, catalogued it, set it aside. The wooden bird. The grey yarn. The whistle — he did not touch the whistle again, merely noted its position. A river stone split cleanly in half, both halves warm. A lock of hair braided around an iron nail. A scrap of sailcloth embroidered with a name worn illegible. Six more, each stranger, each humming that low note he'd recorded as *sound when handled*, though handled was generous; they hummed when *looked at*, when *thought about*, and once, when he had paused too long over the inventory list, they had all hummed together, briefly, like a congregation finding its pitch.

At the bottom of the crate, wedged against the iron strapping where a careless loader might have missed it, lay a folded slip of paper.

Not paper. Prayer-slip. He knew the format instantly — every hedge-witch in the free cities used them, cheap rag-paper stamped with a sigil-wheel, sold a penny apiece to people who wanted their wishes taken seriously by somebody. You wrote your need on the lines, burned the slip or buried it or drowned it according to taste, and mostly nothing happened, which was the entire theological basis of the old gods: they heard, and they declined, and you learned to live small.

This one hadn't been burned. It hadn't even been properly filled in. The printed lines waited empty, but across the top, in a hand that slanted hard to the right and crossed its letters like fences, someone had written a single word.

*Oscar.*

His own name. Not Hartwell, not *the apprentice*, not any of the careful aliases he'd maintained through six years of guild work — just *Oscar*, the name only three living people had ever had reason to use, one of whom was dead, one of whom was himself, and one of whom —

He did not let the thought finish. He picked up the slip anyway. Compulsion has a texture all its own, colder than fear and steadier; it is the feeling of a door opening in a wall you had bricked over, and finding yourself already walking through it.

Below the name, in the same slanting, fence-crossing hand, ran a single line:

*You asked me once why the gods went quiet. I lied to you. Forgive me. Come and ask again. — F.*

The lamp flame leaned sideways though there was no draft. Somewhere in the walls of the guildhouse, water moved that had no business moving — the weeping of the crate, he told himself, seeping upward through the timber. The eleven objects on the table began, very softly, to hum.

Freya's hand. He would have known it blind, the way you know a footstep on a stair. Ten years since he'd fled her hedge with a brand on his palm and a lie in his mouth, ten years of making himself into a man with no history, and all it took was four words in her handwriting to put him back in that kitchen, thirteen years old, watching her write prayer-slips by lamplight while the storm argued with the chimney.

*Come and ask again.*

He read it eleven times. On the eleventh reading, he noticed what he should have noticed first: the ink was fresh. Not days old. Hours. Whatever road this slip had traveled, it had been written recently — written *now*, in a world where Freya Vane had been a rumor and a regret for a decade, in a world where prayers were supposed to go unanswered.

The gods were answering again. Serel had said it that morning with the flat disgust of a merchant reading bad news, and the whole guild had felt the floor tilt beneath the words. And the first answered prayer in this crate — the only one addressed to anyone by name — was addressed to him.

The lamp guttered. The humming stopped all at once, the way birdsong stops, and in the silence Oscar became aware of a second silence behind it: someone standing in the doorway of the counting room.

* * *

Serel Voss-adjacent — no. Serel was Serel, guildmaster of the Corvane chapter, a broad soft-spoken man who wore his authority the way other men wore cardigans, comfortably and without thinking. He stood now in his nightshirt with a candle in one fist, and his face, in the candlelight, was doing something Oscar had never seen it do.

It was afraid.

"You're awake," Serel said. Unnecessary. His eyes went past Oscar to the table, to the slip in Oscar's hand, and stayed there a moment too long.

"Couldn't sleep," Oscar said. "I came down to finish the disposition notes. Item 447 needs —"

"Burn it."

Oscar blinked. "The notes?"

"The crate." Serel came forward and set his candle on the table with a hand that was almost steady. Almost. "All of it. Slip, objects, box, everything. Tonight. Before the chapter steward sees the manifest."

"Serel, these are eleven unique artifacts of —"

"I know what they are." The softness in his voice didn't crack so much as reveal itself as something else entirely — a membrane stretched over pressure. "That's rather the point, Oscar. Do you imagine the Salt Road consignments come with *no provenance* by accident? Someone wants these things untraceable. Someone very high up. Our business is selling impossibilities, not *asking questions of them*." He looked at the prayer-slip again, and this time he reached out and took it from Oscar's fingers — gently, the way you take a blade from a child.

Oscar let him. There was no version of resistance that didn't end with questions he couldn't answer, chief among them *why does a prayer-slip have my name on it*.

"It'll be ash within the hour," Serel said. "And Oscar —" He paused at the door, candlelight carving the soft planes of his face into something older. "The men will be watching you for a few days. Standard practice after an anomalous consignment. Don't take it personally."

"Of course," said Oscar, who took everything personally, silently, and filed it.

He climbed back to his bed above the stables and lay in the dark listening to the guildhouse breathe. Through the floorboards, faintly, he heard the counting-room stove door open and shut. He heard Serel feed the fire. He did not hear the crate burn — no crackle of resin, no pop of iron expanding — but then, a crate full of miracles might well burn quietly, out of courtesy.

What he heard instead, sometime in the deepest hour of the night, was whistling. Tin whistle, playing a lullaby no one alive should have known, drifting up through the floorboards from a stove that was supposedly consuming it.

He got up. He packed a bag without letting himself think about packing a bag — spare shirt, ink, the private ledger, a knife, the glove for his left hand. Then he sat on the edge of the bed and held his branded palm in the moonlight and waited, because some part of him, the part that had been a witch's apprentice before it was ever a bookkeeper, understood that the night was not finished with him.

The sigil woke at the third bell.

* * *

It began as heat — the old ache he'd carried for ten years, the scar-tissue throb that came with weather and worry. Then the heat found edges. Then the edges found shape. It was as if someone had taken the brand on his hand and begun, slowly, to redraw it, line by burning line, and the lines were not the sigil he'd memorized in the mirror all those years ago. They were new. They were *writing*.

He watched his own palm the way you watch a scrying bowl. The skin glowed a dull ember-orange, and across it moved shapes that resolved, reluctantly, into sense: not letters of any alphabet he knew, but pictures — no. A picture. One picture, drawn and redrawn, insistently, the way a dream repeats itself.

A bridge.

It arched across his palm from the heel of his thumb to the base of his fingers, a span of pale stone hung between two darknesses, and it was burning. Not consumed — burning the way a wick burns, steadily, from both ends toward the middle, and along its length walked figures, tiny as grains of rice, some fleeing one dark, some fleeing the other, meeting in the middle with nowhere left to go. Below the bridge, in the space between realms — he knew it was *between* realms the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without evidence — the water was black and full of hands.

The pain crested. He bit the glove he'd been holding and made no sound, because the stable boys slept twelve feet away and a man who screamed in the night stopped being invisible, and invisibility was the only property he owned outright.

In the vision, the bridge burned lower. The figures stopped walking. And at the near end of the span, where the stone met the darkness he somehow understood to be *his* side, a figure stood apart from the rest, tall and still, wearing a shape he refused to recognize until recognition arrived anyway, inevitable as the tide: a woman with her sleeves pinned back, arms crossed, watching the bridge burn with her head tilted like a woman listening to a kettle.

Freya. Standing at the broken border of the world, waiting.

The sigil cooled. The vision drained out of his palm like light out of a closing door, and Oscar sat in the moonlit loft with his heart slamming and his hand throbbing and the taste of salt in his mouth — seawater or tears, there was no honest way to distinguish them.

He thought about staying. That was the terrible thing; he actually weighed it, sitting there with his bag packed, weighing the counting room against the burning bridge. Stay, and tomorrow he would report to Serel, be watched by Serel's men, be quiet and meticulous and safe for another year, maybe five, maybe the rest of a life measured out in balanced columns. Stay, and the slip with his name on it would be ash, and the lullaby would be ash, and whatever was waking in the world would wake without him.

But the slip had said *come and ask again*, and the sigil had shown him a woman waiting at the end of a burning bridge, and underneath both, older than either, lay the question he had swallowed ten years ago on the road away from her hedge: *why did the gods go quiet, and what did you do?*

He had asked it once, at thirteen, and she had lied to him, and he had run rather than live with the lie. He was twenty-three now and tired of running and about to do it anyway, because the past had stopped being content to stay in its grave and had begun, instead, to send him mail.

Oscar Hartwell — no. Not tonight. Tonight, climbing down the stable ladder with his bag on his shoulder and his branded hand wrapped in its glove, he was nobody's ledger-keeper. Tonight he was a runaway again, and the shame of it sat in his chest beside something that felt disgracefully like relief.

He went out through the kitchen, past the sleeping guildhouse, past the counting room where the stove had long gone cold and the smell of ash hung oddly sweet, like burnt sugar. At the yard gate he stopped once and looked back at the dark windows of the building that had been his life.

In the window of the counting room, faint and brief, something glowed ember-orange — the color of a brand, the color of a burning bridge — and was gone.

Serel had said the crate would be ash within the hour. It was now past the second hour, and the fire had never crackled, and the whistle had played, and Oscar understood with the cold clarity of a man reading a final column of figures that Serel had not destroyed item 447 at all.

Serel had *kept* it. Which meant Serel knew what it was worth. Which meant Serel knew — or suspected — whose name was written on the slip hidden somewhere in that crate besides his own.

Oscar pulled the gate shut behind him and walked, unhurried, a bookkeeper going about early business, toward the south gate and the salt road and the hedge at the end of it. Behind him the city slept. Ahead of him, somewhere past the marshes, a woman who had lied to him was waiting at the edge of a burning world.

He did not look back again. But all the way down the dark street, his left hand burned like a lantern he couldn't put out, spelling its message over and over to anyone who knew how to read it:

*Come. Come. Come.*
