## 3. The Usurper's Proclamation

The bells of the Citadel began at dawn, and Maren Voss counted them the way she counted everything: precisely, without flinching.

Nine strokes. One for each year since Aldous took the throne. The old king had died in his sleep, they said, though Voss had read the physician's sealed report and knew better — the man had drowned on dry land, lungs full of river water that no river had touched. That was the first sign, though no one had called it one then. The first answered prayer. Someone, somewhere, had wanted the old king dead badly enough, and purely enough, and the world had simply *obliged*.

She stood at the window of her quarters in the Inquisitors' Wing and watched the square below fill with people summoned by those nine bells. Frost silvered the rooftops of the capital. Her breath fogged the glass, and she wiped it away with a cloth she kept for exactly this purpose, because a blurred window was a small disorder, and small disorders were how large ones began.

Her uniform hung on its stand by the door: grey wool, high collar, the silver censor's chain across the chest. She had polished the links herself last night, link by link, an hour of work that most inquisitors delegated to their servants. Voss did not delegate. Delegation was where corruption entered, seeping through the gaps between what you ordered and what was done.

Below, the crowd had gone quiet in the way crowds did when power approached — not silent but *hushed*, a thousand breaths held at once. Then the trumpets, and the great doors of the Basilica of Records opened, and King Aldous came out onto the balcony with the sun behind him so that he seemed lit from within, which Voss suspected was not entirely an accident of architecture.

She did not go down to hear the proclamation from the square. Inquisitors heard proclamations from the gallery above, where they could watch the crowd instead of the king. A censor's work was reading faces. Kings made speeches; censors made notes.

* * *

In the gallery, forty feet above the hushed square, Voss stood among her peers and listened to Aldous give the speech he had been building for three months, ever since the first verified miracle in the fishing town of Saltmere.

"A plague," the king said, and his voice carried without strain; the acoustics of the Basilica were themselves a kind of answered prayer, raised to some forgotten god of oratory two centuries ago. "A plague of the impossible. I know you have heard the stories. Bread multiplying in ovens. The lame walking. The dead — " here he paused, letting the word fall into the silence like a stone into a well — "the dead sitting up in their shrouds and asking after their families."

A ripple through the crowd. Hope, mostly. Hope was the most dangerous substance in the world, more volatile than any alchemist's fire, and it was Voss's profession to contain it.

"I do not doubt these wonders," Aldous continued. "I have seen them myself. And because I have seen them, I know what follows them." He let his gaze travel across the square, slow as a lighthouse beam. "I grew up in a village that worshipped the old gods. I saw what their 'mercy' cost. A child given to the river every seventh spring. A harvest bought with blood on the threshing floor. When the gods answer prayers, they are not gifts, my friends. They are *invoices*. And the price is always paid by those who can least afford it."

Voss wrote nothing. She already had this part memorized; she had helped draft it. But she watched the faces below, and she marked the ones that hardened at the mention of the old sacrifices — the farmers who remembered, or whose grandparents remembered, the true accounting of divine favor. There were more such faces than there had been a month ago. Fear was spreading faster than hope now, and fear was easier to organize.

"Therefore," the king said, "by right of crown and covenant — " and here Voss felt, rather than heard, a faint wrongness in the hall, a harmonic out of place, as if the building itself disapproved of the phrase — "I declare the returning gods a plague upon the realms. And I appoint myself their censor. No prayer shall be answered in my lands unlicensed. No miracle shall pass unrecorded. No relic, no charm, no whispered bargain with the uncanny shall escape the Office of Inquiry. What the gods have loosed, we will leash."

The crowd cheered. It was a good cheer, disciplined, released at the right moment like water through a sluice gate. Voss noted the pockets that did not cheer — near the fish market, near the temple quarter, a cluster of grey-cloaked figures by the fountain who stood with their heads bowed as if in prayer.

She marked them. By evening they would be interviewed. By tomorrow, if they prayed again in public, they would be detained. Not punished — detained. There was a difference, and the difference was her life's work.

* * *

The summons came at midday, before she had finished her account of the morning's non-cheering clusters.

The Office of Inquiry occupied the lower floors of the Censor's Tower, and the higher your clearance, the deeper you went. Voss had been four floors down for two years now, which put her among the youngest ever to reach that depth. Today the stairwell took her further — five, six, seven — down past doors she had names for but no knowledge of, until she reached a room with no number at all, only a sigil burned into the oak: a circle crossed by seven lines.

She paused before it. Something about the sigil snagged at her attention the way a loose thread snags at a fingernail. Seven lines. She found herself counting them twice, and could not say why.

Inside, Lord Inquisitor Havelock sat behind a desk bare except for a single object under a glass dome: a strip of paper, yellowed, covered in cramped handwriting. A prayer-slip. Voss had seen dozens in the past three months, seized from shrines and smugglers and desperate private altars. This one looked older than the others.

"Sit, Voss."

She sat. Havelock was sixty, grey as ash, with the patient eyes of a man who had spent thirty years listening to lies and had long ago stopped being surprised by any of them. He was, she thought, the only person in the Office she genuinely respected, and she respected him the way one respects a well-built bridge: for what he bore, not what he was.

"Do you know what this is?" He tapped the dome.

"A prayer-slip. Pre-Cessation, by the paper stock. Sealed wax is missing."

"It is a prayer-slip that was *answered* last week." He lifted the dome. "Read it."

She read. The handwriting was a child's, laborious, the letters leaning like fence posts in wind. *Please let my brother come home from the war.* Below it, in a different hand entirely — a hand that moved like water finding its level, effortless and strange — someone had written: *He is coming. Wait by the eastern road on the third day.*

"The soldier came home," Havelock said. "Forty-one years after he was reported dead at the Battle of the Fords. He walked out of the hills outside Saltmere, not aged a day, carrying his own death-notice folded in his pocket." He watched her. "His sister is seventy-three years old, Voss. She waited by the eastern road on the third day. She is waiting there still. She will not leave it. She has not eaten in six days. She says if she stops waiting, he will stop being real."

Voss set the slip down carefully, aligning its edge with the desk's edge. "And the brother?"

"Wanders. Doesn't speak. Weeps when anyone says his own name." Havelock leaned back. "That is what an answered prayer looks like, Voss. That is why the king calls it a plague. Not because the gods are cruel — though they may be — but because mortal hearts cannot hold what the gods give. Every miracle is a wound wearing a gift's clothing."

"You believe that," Voss said. It was not quite a question.

"I have spent three months believing it very hard," Havelock said, "because the alternative is worse." He drew a folder from the drawer and slid it across. "Corvane."

She opened it. Reports, maps, intercepted correspondence. The free cities had always been a thorn — ungoverned, guild-ridden, proud of their lawlessness — but the material inside this folder was different in kind. Lists of relics moving through smugglers' channels. Prices for miracles. And at the center, flagged in red:

*A source within the Guild of Impossible Things reports the guild has taken delivery of goods described as "answered prayers, live." A second source confirms the guild possesses, or recently possessed, an individual bearing a sigil of pre-Cessation make. The sigil is believed to be functional.*

"Functional," Voss said.

"The sigils the old orders used weren't decoration, Voss. They were instruments. Keys. The Covenant of the Seven — you'll find the fragments in the restricted archive, what's left of it — was held together by oath-bearers, and the oath-bearers were marked. If the covenant broke, and the gods are coming back through the break, then whoever holds a working key stands at the door." He folded his hands. "The king wants the sigil-bearer found. Not harmed. Found, and brought to the capital, alive and willing."

"And if unwilling?"

Havelock's face did not change. "Then we must become very persuasive. But Voss—" and here something shifted in his voice, a hairline crack of something that might have been weariness or might have been warning, "—remember what we are. We are not the sword. We are the hand that keeps the sword from falling on the innocent. The moment we forget that, we are only another plague with better paperwork."

"I remember," Voss said.

"I know you do. That is why it is you." He pushed the folder fully into her keeping. "You leave for Corvane tonight. You'll travel as a records auditor — the free cities respect nothing else. Find the sigil-bearer. Learn what the guild knows about the covenant. And Voss — the source who sent that report. Treat everything they say as bait until proven otherwise. Guildmasters sell their own mothers. Selling a stranger is a pleasure to them."

* * *

She packed that evening, and packing was a rite she performed the same way every time, in the same order, because ritual was order made portable. Grey traveling clothes. The censor's chain wrapped in oilcloth. Her kit of instruments: not thumbscrews — the Office had retired those a generation ago, and Voss agreed with the retirement, cruelty being the lazy interrogator's tool — but inks, papers, a lens for examining seals, and a small case of drugs that loosened tongues without damaging minds. Mercy, properly tooled.

Her assistant, a nervous young man named Pell, came to carry her trunk down and hovered at the door while she worked.

"They're saying the whole city will be praying by winter," he said. "That you can't stop it. That the gods are like — like weather."

"Weather can be forecast," Voss said. "Weather can be endured. Weather can be built against." She closed the trunk. "Pell, do you know why I joined the Office?"

He blinked. "To serve the crown?"

"To serve the *people*." She said it without heat, as plain fact. "When I was nine, a hedge-witch in our village sold charms against fever. My mother bought one. It didn't work. My mother died anyway, but she died poorer, and she died believing she'd been protected, and the witch sold eleven more charms that week to eleven more frightened families." Voss buckled the trunk's straps. "Charm-sellers prey on hope because hope is unregulated. The king understands this. The gods understand it better — they invented it. Someone has to stand between the desperate and the things that feed on them. That is all an inquisitor is, Pell. A wall between the flock and the wolves. Even when the wolves wear crowns of light."

"Yes, my lady," Pell said, and she saw that he believed her, and she was glad, because it was true, and truth deserved believers.

What she did not tell him — what she told no one, what she barely admitted in the privacy of her own disciplined mind — was the dream. It had come three times now, always the same. A bridge burning between two dark shores, and on the bridge a figure with a branded hand, and the brand shining like a doorway opening, and behind the doorway something vast turning toward her with an attention like falling. She woke each time with her own hand aching, the right one, across the back, where no mark had ever been.

Superstition, she told herself. Contagion of the age. Dreams were the mind's ledgers, and sometimes the entries arrived before the transactions that caused them. She would note it in her report as an observed phenomenon. Nothing more.

* * *

The road to Corvane ran north along the river, and Voss traveled fast, with two troopers in plain clothes and a wagon of audit supplies. On the third day they passed through a village where the shrine at the crossroads — a thing of mossy stones older than any kingdom — had been freshly swept, and laid with bread, and crowded with small offerings: a child's shoe, a lock of hair, a wooden bird.

Her troopers wanted to burn it. Standard practice, since the proclamation.

"No," Voss said.

They looked at her.

"Burn the offerings and you teach them that the crown desecrates. Leave them, and watch who comes. We are not here to wage war on grandmothers." She dismounted and studied the shrine without touching it. The bread was fresh. The wooden bird was carved with real skill, lovingly, the way things were carved for graves. "We are here to find one person. Everything else is weather."

But that night, at the inn, she overheard the innkeeper's wife telling a table of travelers about her sister in the next valley, whose well water had turned sweet after twenty years of bitterness, and how the whole family knelt at the well each morning now, and how the crops — the woman lowered her voice, half-fearful, half-triumphant — the crops were *coming in wrong*, growing overnight, heavy with grain no one had planted.

Voss sat in the corner with her audit ledger open and wrote nothing for a long time.

Coming in wrong. Growing without planting. She thought of the soldier walking out of the hills with his death-notice in his pocket, and the sister waiting on the eastern road, starving into sainthood beside a road that would never give him up again. Gifts that wounded. Miracles as invoices.

And yet.

And yet the innkeeper's wife had not looked wounded. She had looked like a woman standing in sunlight.

Voss closed the ledger and put the thought away where she kept all inconvenient thoughts, in the deep drawer behind discipline, and slept soundly, and dreamed of the burning bridge, and woke with her right hand aching.

* * *

Corvane announced itself by smell before sight: tar and brine and spice, the perfume of a city that traded in everything the kingdoms forbade. From the ridge road Voss looked down at its crooked spires and its harbor crowded with ships flying no flag at all, and she felt the familiar tightening in her chest that she had learned, over years, to interpret correctly. It was not fear. It was readiness.

Somewhere down there was a sigil-bearer. Somewhere down there was a guildmaster selling information about him. Somewhere down there, prayers were being crated and priced like pepper.

She opened her folder one last time and read the red-flagged line aloud, quietly, committing it to memory the way she committed everything: *an individual bearing a sigil of pre-Cessation make.*

Seven lines in a circle. She thought of the sigil on Havelock's door, and the ache in her hand, and dismissed both.

"Come along," she said to her troopers, and rode down into the free city, carrying order in a trunk and mercy in a case of vials, certain — with the terrible, gentle certainty that would follow her all the way to the end of this road — that she was the kindest thing Corvane had ever been sent.
