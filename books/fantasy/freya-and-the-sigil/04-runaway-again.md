## 4. Runaway, Again

The trouble with building a life out of ledgers, Oscar thought, was that a ledger was a thing that could be read.

He had learned this too late — or perhaps he had known it all along and simply refused to do the arithmetic. For six years he had written himself into the Guild of Impossible Things in the smallest hand he could manage: *Oscar Hartwell, junior accountant, third floor, room nine, no family, no debts, no history worth the ink.* He had made himself a marginal note in the guild's great book, and marginal notes were easy to overlook. That had been the whole design. In Corvane, where a man's name was his credit and his credit was his life, anonymity was a kind of wealth. He had been proud of it. He had been proud the way a mouse is proud of its hole.

Now, on the fourth night after the weeping crate, someone was reading him.

* * *

It began with small things, the way fires begin with smoke.

Marta in the counting room stopped meeting his eye. The porter who brought up his supper left it outside the door instead of handing it to him, and when Oscar thanked him through the wood there was no answer at all. On the stairs he passed two of Serel's enforcers — Brannic and the one everyone called Weevil — and they did not move aside for him as they always had, with the bored courtesy men show a clerk who balances their wages. They stood shoulder to shoulder across the width of the stair and let him squeeze past, and Weevil smiled at him with his whole face empty.

Oscar climbed the rest of the way with his heart going like a dropped coin.

In his room he sat down at his desk and forced himself to be methodical, because methodical was what he had instead of brave. He took out a fresh sheet and drew three columns, as he did for every problem: *What I know. What I suspect. What I can do.*

What he knew: the crate had come from the Saltmarsh road, unmanifested, paid for by an account Serel kept off the books. Inside it had been forty-one prayer-slips, each one answered, each one still warm, and one of them addressed to *him* — to Oscar Hartwell, runaway apprentice of Freya Vane — in a hand he would have known blindfolded, drunk, dead. The sigil on his right hand had burned for three nights running, and last night it had shown him a bridge burning between two shores that were not any shores that existed.

What he suspected: that Serel knew what the prayer-slip said. That Serel had not ordered the crate destroyed because it frightened him, but because it told him something worth selling. And that the woman who wanted a sigil-bearer — Inquisitor Maren Voss, whose proclamation was nailed to every post in Corvane, *by order of King Aldous, appointed censor of the returning gods* — paid very well for things that were worth knowing.

What he could do: run.

His pen hovered over the third column for a long time. Then he wrote, in his smallest hand: *Pack light. Take nothing that names you.* And beneath it, after a moment, because honesty was a habit even now: *You have done this before. You are, God help you, good at it.*

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat by the window with the ledger open on his knees — the master ledger, the true one, the one Serel didn't know existed, in which Oscar had recorded every impossible thing the guild had ever moved, because some part of him had always understood that a record was a weapon and a refuge both — and he watched the street below the guildhouse windows.

At midnight, a covered wagon rolled into Lantern Row and stopped where it should not stop. At half past, a man in the grey livery of the crown's postal service — no letters moved at midnight — spoke briefly to the guild's gate-warden and pressed something into his hand. At one o'clock, the lamp in Serel's study went out, which meant Serel had gone to bed, which meant Serel had already done whatever he meant to do, because Serel never slept before a transaction closed.

Oscar packed. It took four minutes. Six years of careful life reduced to what could be carried at a dead run: the ledger in its oilcloth; the prayer-slip folded against his chest, inside his shirt, where the warmth of it seemed still to linger; a knife; flint; three days of bread and cheese wrapped in a scarf; and the clothes he stood in, dark ones, unremarkable, chosen years ago for exactly this purpose and then resented every day since, because keeping them had felt like a betrayal of the life he'd built.

The sigil on his hand had gone quiet. That frightened him more than the burning had. A brand that stops hurting, Freya had once told him, is a brand that's finished doing whatever it started.

He was at the door when he heard the first shout.

* * *

Fire, in a city built mostly of stone, starts in the places where stone fails — the timber roofs, the tarred seams, the warehouses full of things that burn. The Guild of Impossible Things was full of such things. Oscar stood in the corridor outside his room and watched the smoke begin to crawl along the ceiling from the direction of the manifest hall, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading a column of figures that finally sums, that the fire was not an accident and not an attack. It was a receipt. Serel had sold him, and the fire was how you erased the sale — no guildhouse, no witnesses, no ledger but the one in the clerk's own hands, which the buyer's agents would take from the ashes or from his corpse.

Down the stairwell came screaming, and the smell of burning myrrh, and under it something else — a smell like rain on hot iron, like the air before lightning, the smell of the crate. The fire was finding the relics. The fire was finding the prayers.

Oscar went down instead of up, because up was the roof and the roof was death, and because the ground floor held the old delivery tunnel, bricked over officially since before his time but not, he happened to know, bricked over actually — he had found it in his first year while reconciling the inventory of the cellar, a discrepancy of eleven feet between the outer wall and the inner measurements, and he had never reported it. Eleven feet of unrecorded space. His whole life, he realized, prying loose the first brick with the knife, had been spent finding the places where the numbers didn't add up, and hiding in them.

Behind him, the manifest hall collapsed inward with a sound like a giant closing a book.

Smoke followed him into the tunnel, thin and greedy, feeling along the ceiling. He crawled with the ledger strapped to his back and the prayer-slip clutched in his teeth, and the bricks scraped his shoulders, and somewhere above him the guildhouse — his guildhouse, six years of quiet rooms and honest columns and supper at the same hour every night — roared and settled and died. He did not look back. Looking back was for people who had something to return to.

He came out through a coal merchant's yard three streets east, filthy, coughing, alive, and lay in the black shadow between coal heaps and watched the sky over Lantern Row turn the color of an open wound.

That was when the sigil woke again.

Not burning this time. *Pulling.* A slow, patient tug at the bones of his right hand, as if something on the far side of the world had hooked a line through the mark and begun, gently, to reel. He turned his palm up. In the coal-dark the sigil glowed faintly, a lattice of fine lines like frost on glass, and the lines were moving — rearranging themselves, he realized with a lurch, spelling and respelling, trying out shapes. For one horrible instant they formed a word he almost recognized, a name with too many syllables, and then they broke apart and became, unmistakably, an arrow. A direction. North-northwest, toward the river gates, away from the fire, away from Corvane's walls.

Toward the marshes. Toward the hedges. Toward her.

"No," Oscar said aloud, to his own hand, and the hand ignored him.

He understood then — fully, finally, in the way one understands a debt that has come due — what the prayer-slip had been telling him all along. The gods were answering prayers again. The covenant Freya had helped break was failing, its borders bleeding, and everything that poured through was looking for the same thing: a key. A sigil-bearer. Him. He was not being hunted by accident. He was being hunted *accurately*. The mark on his hand was a beacon lit from both sides — the usurper's inquisitors following the smoke, the gods themselves pulling on the line — and every mile he ran would be a mile someone else had charted.

And yet.

He lay between the coal heaps and laughed, quietly, horribly, until the laugh turned into coughing and the coughing into something that was not quite weeping, because underneath the fear, underneath the grief for the burning building and the buried life, there was this: his legs already knew what to do. His mind was already moving — river gates, not the west gate, the west gate would be watched; a barge, not a horse, horses were remembered and barges were not; the ferryman Tammas Oduya, who owed the guild nothing and hated Serel and worked nights. Six years of stillness, and the old machinery turned over on the first crank. Running was the one skill he had never let rust. Freya had seen to that, all those years ago, drilling him through the hedge-country until he could cross a county leaving no more trace than weather.

She had trained him to run from her. Now the sigil was dragging him back. There was a symmetry there that would have amused him if he'd had the leisure.

* * *

The river gates at dawn were chaos, which was mercy. Half of Corvane had come to watch the guildhouse burn, and the other half had come to loot the shops whose owners were watching the guildhouse burn, and the city watch was stretched between them like a thread about to snap. Nobody looked twice at a soot-grey young man in a clerk's coat who joined the line of early barges waiting for the tide.

Tammas Oduya was mending a net on the deck of his flat-bottomed ferry, and when he saw Oscar his broad dark face did something complicated — surprise, then pity, then a weary kind of decision, as though he had been expecting this passenger for years and had only just gotten the boat ready.

"Heard it burning from the water," Tammas said. "Ugly fire. Green at the edges." He spat over the side. "Green fire means god-stuff burning. You want to tell me why god-stuff was in your counting house?"

"I want to ask you for a crossing," Oscar said. "South channel, past the customs moorings, no questions logged."

"Everything's questions." Tammas coiled the net with hands like knotted rope. "Question is who's asking, and what they'll pay me for answers. Inquisitor's agents were at the customs house yesterday, boy. Paying gold for word of a man with a marked hand. Gold enough that half the waterfront would sell its mother." He looked up, and his eyes were old and kind and absolutely without illusion. "I'm not half the waterfront. But I'm also not cheap."

Oscar counted out what he had — most of six years' savings, sewn into his coat lining against exactly this morning — and Tammas weighed the purse in his palm, nodded once, and cast off.

They slid south with the ebb, past the customs moorings where a grey-livered agent stood scanning the lines of barges with a slip of paper in his fist, past the drowned quarter where the old streets sank into the river and only rooftops showed at low tide like the spines of sleeping animals. The sigil pulled steadily north-northwest, patient as tide. Oscar sat in the stern with the ledger on his knees and did not open it, because opening it would mean admitting that the life inside it was over, and he was not yet ready to balance that particular account.

"Where am I taking you?" Tammas asked, after the city had shrunk to a smudge.

Oscar looked at his glowing hand. He thought of the prayer-slip against his chest, warm as a living thing. He thought of a hedge at the end of a road, and a woman who had not aged, and a bridge burning between realms, and a king who believed the gods were a plague to be censored, and a guildmaster who had sold him for less than the price of the fire.

"The Saltmarsh road," he said. "As far as the water goes."

Tammas grunted. "Saltmarsh," he said, "is where people go to disappear."

"I know," Oscar said. "I've done it before."

The ferryman looked at him for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, laughed — a low, rolling sound, like the river itself chuckling over something it had seen many times.

"Runaway, eh?" he said, and put his shoulder to the sweep. "Then hold tight, boy. Rivers always run home."

Ahead, where the water thinned into marsh and the marsh thickened into mist, the sigil on Oscar's hand burned steady and bright as a compass needle — pointing, with the patience of the gods and the certainty of a debt long deferred, straight at the hedge at the end of the road.
