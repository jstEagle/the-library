## 6. A History Written in Oaths

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Freya finished the third pot of tea and admitted to herself that she could not put this off any longer.

Oscar sat across from her with his hands wrapped around a chipped clay mug he hadn't drunk from. He had not touched it in an hour. He had not moved much at all, except when the sigil on his hand flared — and it flared often now, little pulses of heat that made his fingers twitch like a sleeper dreaming of falling. She watched him press his thumb against the brand the way a man presses a bruise to learn how deep it goes. He had been doing it since they'd arrived. Since before they'd arrived, if she was honest, since the moment she'd opened her door and seen him standing in her hedge-shadow with forty miles of road dust on him and her own handwriting burning a hole through his glove.

She had known, looking at him, that the reckoning would come tonight. There was no version of this where she got to keep her silence and keep him too.

"Ask me," she said.

"I've been asking you for two days."

"You've been asking me questions." She set down her cup. "There's a difference. A question is a door you knock on. What you need is the whole house." She looked into the fire, because looking at him while she said what came next would have been more than either of them could carry. "So I'll open every room myself. And then you can decide whether you want to live in it."

Outside, the wind moved through the hedge — her hedge, grown wild and deliberate over decades, thorned and tangled and warded so thoroughly that even the crows had forgotten there was a garden behind it. The cottage smelled of dried yarrow and lamp oil and the particular dust of a life kept deliberately small. She had built this smallness the way other people built fortresses. It had never once been mistaken for one.

"The covenant," Oscar said quietly. "Start there."

* * *

"There were seven," Freya said. "Seven oath-bearers, sworn at the anchor-stone beneath the drowned chapel, in the days when the chapel still had a roof and the river still respected its banks. Four mortal. Three divine. That ratio mattered — I'll come back to why, but hold it for now. Four hands and three gods, bound together in a single knot, each strand holding the others true."

She rose, because sitting still had become impossible, and went to the shelf above the hearth where she kept the things she could not throw away and could not look at. A river stone worn smooth as an egg. A bird's skull no bigger than her thumbnail. A strip of bark from a tree that had died a century ago and still, faintly, smelled of green.

"In those days the border between the realms was not a wall," she said, her back to him. "It was a membrane. Skin. The divine world breathed through into ours and we breathed back, and everything passed between them the way water passes between roots and leaves. Prayers went up. Answers came down. Miracles were not *events*, Oscar — they were weather. Ordinary. Expected. A farmer prayed over his seed and the seed heard him. A mother prayed over a fevered child and the fever negotiated. Not always won. But negotiated. The gods were present the way rivers are present. You didn't worship a river by building it a golden temple. You worshipped it by keeping its banks clean and teaching your children to swim."

"And the oath-bearers kept that?" His voice was careful. He was already writing the ledger in his head; she could hear it, the neat columns, the balanced sums. He had been hers once. Some things survived erasure.

"They *were* it. That's what people forget, or never knew. The border wasn't held by magic. Magic is just the language. It was held by *agreement*. Seven beings who met at the anchor-stone and renewed the terms, season by season, year by year, generation upon generation. When one died — the mortals did die, that was part of the design — a successor took up their token and their seat. The tokens weren't trinkets. They were the physical memory of the oath. Touch a token to the stone and the covenant remembered you. Lose the token, and the covenant began to forget you existed as a bearer at all." Her mouth twisted. "You see now why Serel's warehouses full of stolen relics made my blood run cold. Half of what that guild has been trafficking for years — sold, bartered, locked in crates marked *miscellaneous* — are the severed memories of godhood."

She heard him set the untouched tea down. "Who were they? The seven."

Freya turned around. Better to do this facing him. Better to let him see her face while she buried them both.

"Tammas Oduya you know — or will. In those days he was a young ferryman on the Corvane delta, and the river itself sat beside him at the stone: Oshunmere, the god of waters, old beyond counting, gentle as silt. They were paired — mortal and divine, keeper and kept. Each of the four mortal bearers had a divine counterpart, and the seventh—" she paused, and the pause cost her something visible, something he would remember— "the seventh stood alone. Veyra. The god of thresholds. Of doors and crossings and the space between one thing and the next. She needed no counterpart because she *was* the counterpart to everyone. Every oath passed through her. Every promise crossed her threshold on its way to being kept."

"The burning bridge," Oscar said. "The one the sigil showed me."

"Yes." The word came out flat as a coin laid on a dead man's eyes. "That's her. Or it was. We'll get there."

She came back to her chair but did not sit. She stood with her hands folded, the way she had once stood before her own teachers, and hated the posture and needed it anyway.

"The second pair was Maren Hale — a name you'll be hearing again soon enough, though perhaps not the way you'd hope — and her counterpart, the god of oaths themselves, whose true name is not pronounceable in any throat that owns only one heartbeat. Your inquisitor descends from her. The blood remembers, even when the family doesn't. The third pair was Sefa Ilori, a midwife from the salt marshes, and the god of small mercies — fevers broken, rains come late-but-in-time, the mercy of a quick death when death was owed. And the fourth mortal seat was held by a woman named Brigid Vane."

Oscar's breath caught. She heard it clearly in the quiet room.

"My grandmother," Freya said. "And after her, my mother. And after my mother—" she lifted her right hand, palm outward, and for a moment let the old scar show through the glamour she wore over it like a glove: a thin white line crossing her lifeline, the mark of a token long surrendered, "—me. The seat of the hedge-witch. Keeper of the boundary between cultivated ground and wild. My counterpart was the god of growing things, of root and bloom and rot that feeds the seed. Ashka. She was—" and here, for the first time, her voice failed her, just slightly, just enough that she had to stop and start again. "She was the best of us. That's not sentiment. That's bookkeeping. She gave the most and asked the least, and when the breaking came, she fought it hardest, and the cage cost her the most, and I have spent ninety years trying to earn the right to say her name without flinching."

"Ninety years," Oscar repeated softly. He was looking at her face, at the face he had known as a boy, and she saw him doing the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the hedge opened. The unaging. The wrongness of her. "Freya. How old are you?"

"Older than the question deserves." She sat down at last, heavily, like a woman setting down a load she'd carried so long her spine had grown around it. "But younger than I should be, and that is not a gift. Hold that too. It matters later."

* * *

"For three hundred years the covenant held," she said, when she had gathered herself. "Three hundred years of permeable borders and negotiated miracles and seven seats filled at the anchor-stone every turning of the year. And then the prayers changed."

"Changed how?"

"Grew heavier." She stared into the fire. "You have to understand what prayer was, in those days. Not begging. *Traffic*. Two-way. You sent up your need and something came back down, and both directions had weight, and the covenant's whole purpose was to keep that traffic honest. To make sure no god took more than was offered and no mortal demanded more than the world could pay. Veyra governed the exchange rate, if you like. The toll-keeper of the threshold."

"But something started taking more."

"A war," Freya said. "A long one, far to the east, beyond the free cities, in the lands that don't appear on guild maps because the guild can't smuggle anything out of a graveyard. Plague behind it, famine behind that, and behind *that* — desperation. Millions of prayers, all at once, all screaming the same word: *save us*. And the gods heard. Of course they heard. They are made of hearing. Ashka poured herself into the blighted fields until there was almost nothing left of her. The god of small mercies worked herself threadbare. Oshunmere flooded a battlefield to stop a slaughter and drowned eleven villages doing it, and wept for a decade, and the weeping changed the weather along every coast in the world."

"And Veyra?"

"Veyra said *no*." The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and the silence after it went down a long way. "For the first time in three hundred years, the toll-keeper closed the gate. She weighed the prayers coming up against the power going down and found the exchange ruinous, and she refused to honor the drafts. *You are asking the realms to bankrupt themselves*, she told us at the stone. *Compassion without limit is not compassion. It is consumption wearing mercy's face.* And she was right, Oscar. That's the poison of it. She was right about the arithmetic. Gods who give everything become nothing, and a realm that spends its divinity to stop one war has none left for the ten wars after. She was right."

"But?"

Freya laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "But she was right the way a tax collector is right. Correct to the copper, and hated for it. The mortals turned on her first. Then the diminished gods turned, because her refusal shamed them — every miracle they managed despite the rationing was an accusation aimed at her gate. And we—" she spread her hands, the hedge-witch's hands, dirt under the nails and old scars across the knuckles, "—we were tired. That's the truth I have carried longest and cleanest: we were *tired*. Three hundred years of stewardship and then a century of triage, and here was Veyra telling us the suffering was structural and would not be fixed by spending ourselves into the ground. And a faction formed. My grandmother's faction, at first. Then mine. Four of the seven — two mortals, two gods, Ashka among them, though she came late and weeping and left early and never spoke of it again. We decided the covenant was failing. We decided it needed to be *rebuilt*. On firmer terms. With clearer limits. With a border that was a wall instead of a membrane, so that the traffic could be controlled, rationed, made *just* — by our definition of just."

"You broke it," Oscar said. Not a question. His voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when he was balancing a column that refused to sum.

"We broke it," Freya agreed. "At the anchor-stone, at the winter turning, ninety-one years ago. We brought chains forged from the covenant's own language — oaths twisted inside-out, bindings written in the grammar of the original vow. You cannot cage a god with iron. You cage her with her own promises. And Veyra stood at the center of the stone, and she watched us come, and she did not fight."

"She didn't?"

"She *argued*. Right up until the last moment, she argued — terms, conditions, amendments, a hundred compromises she offered like a merchant watching a mob decide to burn her shop. And when we refused them all, she stopped. She looked at each of us in turn. All seven of us, or six of us and the empty place where the seventh should have been standing, which was wherever she was. And she said—" Freya closed her eyes. Ninety-one years, and she could still hear it perfectly, in a voice like a door closing in an empty house. "*When the wall you build fails — and walls fail, children, walls always fail — everything I have swallowed will come back up. And I will not be gentle, because you will have taught me, by then, what your gentleness is worth.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the hedge rustled, and Freya thought — not for the first time — that Ashka might still be listening through it, or what remained of her, the way a felled tree keeps listening through its roots.

"We sealed her beneath the anchor-stone," she said. "We sealed the border with her. The wall and the prisoner became the same structure — her body the mortar, her consent the lock, her silence the seal. And the prayers stopped being answered. Not gradually. *Immediately.* One winter turning, the realms were skin; the next, they were glass. The gods withdrew or dwindled or went silent behind the wall they were built into. Ashka faded fastest — she had given the most during the wars, and the sealing took what was left. The last time I felt her, she was small enough to live in a single hedgerow, and then she was small enough to live in a single thornbush, and then—" Freya's hand closed slowly around nothing. "Then the hedge was just a hedge. And I stayed. Because someone had to sit by the cage. Because someone had to watch the wall for cracks."

"And now the prayers are answered again," Oscar said.

"Now the prayers are answered again," she echoed. "Because the wall is failing. Because cages fail — she told us they would, and she was right about that too, she was right about everything except the part where she trusted us to be better than our fear. Every answered prayer that comes through is a crack. Every crate of warm, weeping miracles in Serel's warehouse is mortar crumbling. The gods aren't returning, Oscar. They're *leaking*. And Veyra is waking up underneath all of it, and she has had ninety-one years to curdle, and she made us a promise about what she would be when she got out."

* * *

He was silent for a long time. The fire settled. Outside, the first grey suggestion of dawn was feeling its way through the hedge, and Freya realized with dull surprise that they had been at this all night — that confession, done properly, takes exactly as long as the sin took to commit.

"Why tell me the faction was four?" he asked finally. "You said two mortals, two gods. You named Ashka. Who else?"

"Maren Hale," Freya said. "Your inquisitor's ancestor. And the god of oaths himself — the one whose name can't be spoken. Which means the family that helped build the cage has spent nine generations serving the king who wants to steal what's inside it. Aldous doesn't know what he's digging for. But his blood does. Blood always does."

"And the fifth and sixth? You said four of the seven turned. Two mortals, two gods. Tammas wasn't one of them. Sefa wasn't. So the fourth was—"

"Brigid Vane." Freya met his eyes and did not look away, because looking away would have been the last cowardice available to her and she had used up all the others. "My grandmother led it. I inherited the guilt the way you inherit land — sight unseen, boundaries already surveyed, the deed signed before you were born. By the time I understood what had been done, the only thing left to inherit was the watching. So I watched. For ninety years. And I taught apprentices, because the seat required a keeper and the hedge required hands, and I swore to myself I would never bind another soul to any of it."

His hand was on the table. The sigil showed dark through the burnt-open glove, and it pulsed as she looked at it — slow, patient, like something breathing in its sleep.

"Except you did," Oscar said. "Didn't you. That night my parents died. Whatever you wrote on my hand — it wasn't an apprentice's mark."

Freya looked at the sigil, and the sigil seemed, in the dying firelight, to look back.

"No," she said. "It wasn't. And before I tell you what it was, there's something you need to understand about the cage, and about why it's failing *now*, ninety years after it was built, and why it chose this year, and this city, and—" her voice caught, steadied, "—and you. The seal was made from seven oaths. Six were broken willingly. But the seventh — the binding at the very heart of it, the keystone — required something we didn't have that night. Something Veyra demanded as the price of holding still while we chained her. A vessel. A living heir, unbaptized to any god, born outside every covenant, who would carry the keystone-oath in their own blood until the day the cage either held forever or broke for good. We told her no. We told her we would find another way. And she smiled — I will hear that smile until I die — and she said, *then the cage will fail, and it will fail through the child you refuse to give me.*"

Oscar had gone very pale. Very still. The stillness of a man reading a ledger line he has just realized is written in his own name.

"Freya," he said. "What did you write on my hand?"

She reached across the table and took his burned hand in both of hers, gently, the way she had once held it when it was small enough to disappear inside her grip, and the sigil woke beneath their joined skin like an ember remembering air.

"I wrote the keystone," she said. "I wrote the seventh oath. The night your parents died saving mine, I had a dying child in my arms and a cage failing overhead and one chance to choose which failure I could live with — and I chose you. God forgive me, Oscar. I made you the lock."
