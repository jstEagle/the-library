## 7. The First Oath-Bearer

The drowned quarter of Corvane had been sinking for two hundred years, and the city had long since stopped pretending it would save it. The streets below the Weirline were canals now; the grand houses of the old merchant families stood to their second stories in green water, their windows dark or boarded or bricked over entirely, their rooftops colonized by gulls and the stubborn white flowers that grew from every crack in every chimney. The people who remained lived on the upper floors and traveled by boat, and they had developed the particular pride of those who refuse to be pitied: they called it the Low City, and they called everyone else, with a small curl of contempt, the Dry.

Oscar had never been down here as a guild man. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers above the waterline, in rooms that stayed dry enough for ink. But Freya Vane walked the flooded streets like a woman returning to a house she'd once owned, her staff tapping the planks of the ferry-landing, her eyes moving over the water with an expression he couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition.

"He's here," she said. "He's always been here. That's the whole point of Tammas Oduya."

"Who is he? Exactly?"

Freya looked at him sidelong. In the flat grey light off the water, the lines around her eyes seemed deeper than they had a week ago, and Oscar tried not to think about what that meant, what each working was costing her, what she was quietly spending out of herself to keep them both alive and unremarked between Corvane's checkpoints. "He was a man," she said. "First. That matters more than anything else I could tell you. He was a man who made a promise, and then he kept it for forty years past the point where keeping it made any sense at all."

She stepped onto the ferry-landing. A boat was already coming toward them through the mist.

* * *

It came without being called. That was the first impossible thing, though Oscar supposed by now he should have stopped counting. The Low City's ferries were licensed, painted, numbered; this one was none of those things. It was a long, low skiff of blackened wood, its hull scarred and patched and patched again, and the man at the oars was old in the way that stone is old — not frail, but worn smooth, as if everything soft about him had been rubbed away by decades of water. He was broad through the shoulders still. His hands on the oar-handles were enormous, knotted like roots. He wore oilskins gone grey with age, and a hat whose brim dripped a slow, steady line of water onto the deck, though the morning fog had not yet thickened enough to rain.

He brought the skiff alongside the landing without a word and held it steady against the current, one hand on the piling, waiting.

"Tammas," Freya said.

The ferryman looked up. His face did what faces do when they meet someone they buried a long time ago — a flicker, a settling, something rearranged behind the eyes. "Vane." His voice was low and rough, like water over gravel. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone that. It saves trouble." She stepped down into the skiff; it barely rocked. Oscar followed less gracefully, and the old man's gaze moved to him, and stayed there, and Oscar felt the look like a hand pressed flat against his chest — not hostile, exactly. Reading him. Reading the sigil beneath his glove.

"You've brought me a marked one," Tammas said.

"I've brought you the last key there is. May we ride?"

Tammas considered. Somewhere across the water, a bell rang in one of the half-drowned chapels, three strokes, muffled and strange, as if the sound itself had to swim. Then the old man pushed off from the piling and took up his oars, and the skiff slid out into the green silence of the drowned streets.

* * *

He rowed without seeming to row. That was the second thing Oscar noticed, once his heart stopped hammering from the first. The oars dipped and drew, dipped and drew, but the motion was so even, so unhurried, that it seemed less like effort than like ritual — the way a monk turns a prayer wheel, not to make anything happen but because the turning is the prayer. And the boat went where Tammas willed it, threading the sunken lanes, passing under bridges whose arches had become the mouths of caves, past the upper windows of houses where laundry flapped and children watched them pass with the incurious stares of those raised on the water.

"Where are we going?" Oscar asked.

"Nowhere," Tammas said. "Everywhere. I'm showing you my route." And then, after a silence long enough that Oscar had almost given up on more words: "Forty years, I've rowed this route. Same streets. Same stops. You want to know why I'm the first oath-bearer? Not because I was strongest, or bravest, or holiest. Because I never stopped."

"The covenant," Freya began.

"I know what broke it." The oars dipped. Drew. "I felt it go, girl. Forty years back, on a night just like this one. Felt it like a bone snapping somewhere behind the world." He glanced at her, and there was no anger in it, which somehow was worse. "I felt it, and I knew whose hands had done the breaking, because I'd helped tie the knots myself. And I got in my boat the next morning, and I kept rowing."

"Why?" Oscar asked. It came out before he could soften it. "If the covenant was broken, if your god was—" He didn't know the word. Diminished. Caged. Starved.

"Because a promise doesn't stop being a promise when the thing it promised goes quiet." Tammas's voice didn't rise. It lowered, if anything, until it was nearly part of the sound of the water. "You're a ledger man. I can see it on you, the way you sit, the way you count the bridge-stones as we pass. So understand me in your own language. A debt entered in the book doesn't vanish because the creditor dies. It waits. Somebody has to keep the page clean. Somebody has to be there when the account comes due." The oars drew. "I'm the page, boy. Forty years, I'm the page."

Ahead of them, the lane opened into what had once been a plaza — a wide pool now, ringed by the shoulders of drowned buildings, and in its center the top of a fountain rising from the water like a drowning woman's reaching hand. Tammas shipped his oars and let the skiff drift.

And Oscar saw the passenger.

There was someone sitting in the bow. He hadn't noticed — that was the third impossible thing, and the worst, because Oscar prided himself on noticing, on cataloguing, on missing nothing. But there was a figure seated at the front of the boat, slight and still, wrapped in a traveling cloak that might have been fine once and was now only wet. No face turned toward them. No movement at all. Just presence, the way a cold room has presence.

"That's—" Oscar started.

"My fare," Tammas said. "Been my fare forty years. Every morning I collect her at the fountain — or where the fountain used to have a mouth, before the water took it. Every evening I set her down. In between, we ride." He said it simply, the way another man might say *I take my wife to market on Thursdays*. "Her name was Ysolt, when names mattered. She was the river. All of it — the Corvane from the fells to the sea, every tributary, every well that fed the city, every tear anyone cried into it. She signed the covenant seventh-from-the-last and swore the borders open, and for six hundred years she kept her side, and the city kept hers, and the water ran both ways."

"And then the covenant broke," Oscar said quietly.

"And then the covenant broke." Tammas finally turned fully around, and in the grey light Oscar saw his eyes clearly for the first time — pale, river-colored, patient as sediment. "A god needs worship the way a fire needs air, boy. Not because gods are vain. Because worship is the shape belief gives to power, and power without shape scatters. When the prayers stopped — when your Freya's faction walled the realms shut and the old roads closed — the great ones starved first. Ysolt lasted longer than most. Rivers are stubborn. People need water whether they pray or not; some part of them keeps honoring you just by drinking." He looked at the still figure in the bow. "But a whisper is all that's left of her now. Some mornings I'm not sure she hears me. Some mornings I'm not sure I hear her. But I collect her at the fountain, and I set her down at dusk, and I do it again the next day. Because that's the vow. Not the big words they had us swear at the forging. This. The small one underneath. *You will not be carried nowhere by no one.*"

The phrase landed strangely in Oscar's chest. It sounded like nonsense — until it didn't. He thought of the prayer-slip addressed to him in Freya's hand. He thought of the sigil burning on his palm at night, showing him a burning bridge between realms. *You will not be carried nowhere by no one.* A promise that nothing — no soul, no god, no prayer — would fall through the cracks of the world with no one to bear it across.

"That's what the covenant was," he said slowly. "Not walls. A ferry service."

Freya made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could hurt. "He sees it faster than I did," she said. "Took me twenty years to understand that's all we'd broken. Not a treaty. A crossing."

* * *

They moored at the foot of a stair that climbed out of the water into a narrow street of boat-builders' sheds and net-menders' lofts, and there, over tea brewed on a brazier that smelled of tar and salt, Tammas heard them out. Freya told him about the answered prayers, the crates, the sigil, Aldous and his inquisitors and the harvest of prayers at swordpoint. She told him about the anchor-stone. She told him, in a voice that stayed very level, that reforging the covenant required all seven oath-bearers, living or consenting dead, and that his token — the token of the first bearer, the oldest binding in the whole design — was the keystone without which the rest were only pretty metal.

Tammas listened the way he rowed: evenly, without hurry, giving nothing away.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long time. The brazier ticked. Somewhere below them, under the floorboards, under the water, the drowned city breathed.

"No," he said.

"Tamm—"

"No." He held up one huge hand. "You don't get to come down here after forty years, Vane. You don't get to break the world and then come ask the man who kept sweeping the floor to help you rebuild it. I know why you did it. I've had forty years to know why you did it, and I'll grant you this much: Veyra demanded too much. What she asked of the small folk — the tithe of years, the tithe of children's names — no covenant should have carried it. You were right to refuse her. You were even right to cage her, may the deep forgive us both for saying so." His jaw worked. "But you didn't just cage her. You broke the road under everybody's feet. Do you know how many died in the years after? How many prayers went up into a sealed sky and came back down unanswered, and how many people decided, one by one, that they'd been fools ever to believe? You didn't save the world from Veyra. You taught it to stop asking."

"We know," Freya said. "Believe me. We know what we did."

"Knowing's cheap." But something in him had shifted — Oscar saw it, the way you see a current change under still water. The old man looked at Oscar instead, at last, directly. "And you. Marked boy. Ledger man. Why are you here? She broke it. Her debt. Why does your hand burn?"

Oscar hesitated. He was getting better at lying — the guild had taught him that much — but under Tammas's river-pale eyes, lying felt like trying to float a stone. "Because I ran," he said. "From her. From the apprenticeship. I built a life where nobody knew my name and nothing was asked of me, and I liked it, and I was good at it. And then the gods started answering again, and everything I hid inside came looking for me." He flexed his gloved hand. "I don't want to be the key. I want my ledgers back. But the inquisitors burned my life down, and Freya says the cage is failing, and if somebody doesn't reforge the covenant, Aldous is going to put the gods in chains and milk them for miracles until the world runs dry. So I'm here. Not because I'm brave. Because running forward is still running."

For the first time, something moved across Tammas Oduya's worn-smooth face that might, in a younger man, have been a smile.

"Honest," he rumbled. "Well. There's a novelty." He rose, joints cracking like mooring lines in a storm, and went to a chest in the corner of the shed. When he turned back, he was holding something wrapped in oilcloth, and he laid it on the table between them and unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon.

Inside lay a coin. Or what had been a coin once — a disc of river-gold the size of a palm, worn nearly featureless, its face rubbed to a soft gleam by four decades of handling. Oscar could feel it from across the table, a faint pressure against his sigil, like a voice heard through a wall.

"My token," Tammas said. "Ysolt gave it to me at the forging, six hundred years back, when I was a fisherman's son with strong arms and no sense. It's the proof of the vow. Without it, the covenant doesn't know me. Doesn't know any of us. It's the signature on the page." He covered it again, slowly, and Oscar's stomach dropped before the old man even spoke. "And it's not here."

"What?"

"It's in a warehouse, boy. Guild warehouse — *your* guild, unless I miss my guess, the one on Anchor Lane with the black doors. Seized eight days ago when the king's men took the quarter's stores. I'd have fetched it myself, but—" he spread his enormous hands, "—a man who's spent forty years being invisible doesn't walk into an imperial depot full of inquisitors. They'd see me. First time in forty years, they'd see me." He sat back down, and the weariness in him was suddenly vast, older than the boat, older than the vow. "Bring me my token, and I'll stand with you at the anchor-stone. Bring me my token, and Ysolt gets a voice again — a real one, not this whisper — and believe me, you'll want a river at your back before this is done. But I don't move without it. A promise isn't kept in pieces."

Freya and Oscar exchanged a look. Oscar knew the warehouse on Anchor Lane. He had inventoried it himself, twice a year, in his careful anonymous hand. He knew the manifest, the locks, the watch rotations — and he knew, with a cold settling in his gut, that anything seized by the crown in the last week would not be sitting in a manifest anymore. It would be in the inquisitors' special holdings. Under seal. Under Maren Voss's seal, if the reports he'd stolen were true, and they were.

"There's more," Tammas said, as they rose to leave. He looked out at the water, at the still figure waiting in his boat, patient as rain. "The token will call to your mark, boy — gold knows gold. But listen to me, and listen close, because I've had forty years to learn this and you've had a month. When you hold it, you'll feel what I feel. Every day of it. The weight of the vow. Most folk can't carry that weight for an hour." His pale eyes found Oscar's. "We'll see what you're made of."

They went down to the boat. Tammas rowed them back through the drowned streets in silence, past the fountain, past the listening windows, and as the landing came in sight, the still figure in the bow moved — just slightly, just once — and a voice reached Oscar that was not a voice at all, but a feeling, like cold water closing over his ears:

*The ledger-man. Tell the ledger-man: they are counting my waters in the warehouses too. Hurry.*

That night, from a rooftop overlooking Anchor Lane, Oscar counted the guards at the black doors of the guild's seized warehouse, and found there were far too many — and that among the inquisitors' grey cloaks, holding a lantern high over the unloading crates, stood a face he knew from ten years of double-entry columns and quiet nods across the counting-room floor.

Serel was helping them search.
