## 8. Inquisitor's Mercy

The cells beneath the Corvane garrison had been granaries once, back when the free cities still trusted their own walls. Maren Voss walked the row of them with her boots striking the old stone in an even rhythm, and behind her, at a respectful distance, came Brother Anselm with his ink and his ledger of confessions, and two soldiers of the Ninth with pikes they did not yet need.

She had been in Corvane four days. In that time she had learned the shape of the city the way she learned every city: by its fear. Fear had a geography. It pooled in the harbor district where the god-fever burned hottest, where chalk marks of petition covered doorposts like lichen and old women sold bottled blessings out of market stalls. It ran thin and sour through the merchant quarters, where men counted their coin and wondered if coin would mean anything in a year. And here, in the garrison, it sat very quiet and very deep, in the cells, in the men waiting inside them.

"Names," she said to Anselm, without turning. "Again."

Anselm licked his thumb and turned a page. "Doss Merrow, smuggler, taken at the Saltgate warehouse. Pell Ashby, smuggler, same. Joreth Kine, wheelwright by trade, caught driving a cart of relics toward the river gate. And—" a pause, the sound of paper, "—one more brought in this morning from the guildhouse fire. Unlisted. He gave his name as Serel Vane."

"Not Vane." Voss stopped walking. The torchlight moved on the wet stone. "Serel is the guildmaster's name. I have his description from three informants and half a burned portrait. If he claims kinship with Freya Vane, he is lying about something, and men lie about kinship when they want mercy."

"He wants to speak with you specifically, Inquisitor. He said—" Anselm consulted his notes with the scrupulousness of a man who believed everything written was sacred, "—he said you were the only honest person the king employs."

Voss almost smiled. She did not, because smiling in front of soldiers taught soldiers things, but she felt the small warm pull of it and set it aside like a coin she could not spend. *The only honest person the king employs.* Either flattery or a trap, and after eleven years in the Censorate she had learned that the two were often the same object viewed from different angles.

"Show me the smugglers first," she said. "Then him."

* * *

Doss Merrow wept before she asked him anything, which saved time. He was perhaps twenty-five, with rope-burned hands and a sailor's sunburn peeling off his nose, and he knelt on the cell floor as though kneeling were a language he'd been fluent in since childhood.

"You're going to burn us," he said. "That's what they say you do. You burn the sigil-thieves and the relic-mongers and anyone who touches the answered prayers."

"The king has decreed that traffic in divine matter is a plague," Voss said. She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice she used for frightened people because frightened people heard tone before they heard words. "Plagues are treated. Not everyone who falls ill dies of it."

Merrow looked up at her with red-rimmed eyes, and what she saw there was not defiance. It was worse than defiance. It was *belief* — the raw, unarmored belief of a man who had touched something holy and been told by everyone in power that he had touched filth.

"I carried one," he blurted. "In the crate. From the Saltgate run. They told us not to open it, but the crate was warm, Inquisitor, warm like a body, and there was a sound in it, like — like someone praying right next to your ear, and I put my hand flat on the wood and I swear on my mother's grave I heard my own name in it. My name. Nobody's said my name like that since she died."

He was shaking now. One of the soldiers shifted his pike, and Voss lifted two fingers — *wait* — without looking away from the man.

"What did you hear," she said quietly, "when you heard your name?"

"That I was — that somebody knew. That somebody had been listening. All those years I prayed into the dark and nothing ever came back, and then—" His voice cracked. "It came back. It *knew* me."

Voss stood in the silence of the cell for a long moment. Somewhere above them, water dripped through old masonry, counting seconds no one had asked it to count.

"Brother Anselm," she said finally, "record: Doss Merrow, carrier, acted in ignorance of cargo. Recommend labor service, not the pyre." She turned for the door.

"Inquisitor." Merrow's voice followed her, small and desperate. "Was it wrong? What I heard? Was it a sin?"

She paused with her hand on the iron. Eleven years of doctrine sat on her tongue, ready-shaped: *the gods are a fever, the gods are a flood, the king builds the levee.* But what came out, before she could stop it, was neither yes nor no.

"It was true," she said. "That's all I know. It was true."

She left him weeping again, but differently.

* * *

Pell Ashby lied to her for an hour, which she let him do, because liars showed her the shape of what they were protecting better than honest men ever did. Joreth Kine, the wheelwright, told her about the black market on Tallow Street where miracles sold for houses — a blessing against fever for forty crowns, a whispered word from a river god for the price of a fishing boat — and how the prices had doubled every week since the first crates came in warm. She recorded it all. She filed none of it in her mind under *heresy*. She found, to her private discomfort, that she was filing it under *grief*.

By the time they brought her to the last cell, the torches had burned down twice and Anselm's ink pot needed refilling, and Voss had begun to feel the particular tiredness that came not from the body but from the slow accumulation of other people's truths.

Serel Vane — Serel nothing; the surname was theater — sat on the cell bench like a man receiving visitors in his own home. He was broad and soft at once, with the hands of someone who had signed documents for thirty years rather than carrying anything heavier than a pen, and rings on six fingers that he had somehow persuaded his captors not to take. His eyes were quick and gray and entirely without shame.

"Inquisitor Voss," he said, rising with a small bow. "I confess I hoped for you. The others they sent — a boy with a list of questions written by someone else. You read things yourself, I'm told. Before you decide."

"I read everything myself," Voss said. "Sit down."

He sat. He folded his hands. He looked, she thought, like a merchant at a fair who already knew which of his goods the customer wanted and was simply enjoying the ritual of haggling.

"I'll be direct," Serel said. "You want the sigil-bearer. Oscar Hartwell. Former apprentice to Freya Vane. Clerk, bookkeeper, the invisible man of my organization — until the prayers started coming back and he became the most important piece on the board. I can tell you where he was, where he went, who harbors him. I can give you the Guild of Impossible Things entire: its routes, its contacts in five cities, its warehouses, its buyers. Everything your king needs to cut the head off the smuggling trade in one stroke."

"And in exchange?"

"My life. A ship, if you're feeling generous. A man of my talents should not end on a pyre; he should end in bed, rich, and late."

"You've offered this to my subordinates already," Voss said. "They wrote it down. I've read it."

"Then you know I keep good records." Serel smiled. "But here is what I did not offer them, because boys with lists don't understand value. I have something better than testimony. I have the ledger itself. Oscar Hartwell's master ledger — every impossible thing the guild moved in nine years, catalogued in his own neat hand. Provenance, buyer, price, origin. Your Censorate has been seizing crates and burning them blind. That ledger tells you what you've actually been burning."

Voss kept her face still. Inside, something leaned forward.

"Why would you give it to me? It's worth more than your life on any market in the free cities."

"Because markets are closing, Inquisitor." For the first time, something moved behind the gray eyes — not fear exactly, but its accountant cousin, calculation wearing fear's clothes. "Because the free cities are going to burn whether I profit or not, and because I have watched your king's men work these past weeks and I have concluded that Aldous does not want what I thought he wanted. A man in my position survives by reading his customers correctly. I misread him. I would like to correct the error while correction is still possible." He spread his soft hands. "And because you, Inquisitor, are the only person in this whole rotten apparatus who might actually read the thing properly. The boy with the list would have used it for kindling."

She should have refused. Some part of her, the part drilled by eleven years of Censorate discipline, noted that accepting contraband from a confessed trafficker outside proper chain of custody was itself a crime against the order she served. She noted it, and set it beside the memory of Doss Merrow's ruined face asking *was it a sin*, and found that the two weights did not balance anymore.

"Bring it," she said.

* * *

They brought it to her that night in the quarters the garrison had given her: a plain room, a plain desk, one lamp. The ledger came wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a paving stone, and when she unwrapped it she understood immediately why Serel had guarded it like a relic. It was beautiful. Whatever else Oscar Hartwell was, he had been a craftsman of the ordinary — columns ruled true to the width of a hair, entries dated and cross-referenced, marginalia in a small precise hand that flagged discrepancies and resolved them. Reading it was like watching a mind walk a tightrope for nine years without once looking down.

She began at the beginning, because beginnings were where the truth of any enterprise lived.

*Entry 1. Relic: pilgrim's medal, allegedly weeping. Origin: fraud (vendor-confessed). Disposition: returned to vendor, deposit forfeited.*

*Entry 4. Relic: love-charm, claimed efficacy 60%. Origin: hedge-craft, mundane. Note: efficacy unverifiable; priced accordingly.*

Page after page. A saint's fingerbone that was a pig's tooth. A curse-scroll that was gibberish in bad Old Corvanic. The Guild of Impossible Things, it emerged, had spent most of its existence doing precisely what its name implied: selling impossibilities to people who wanted so badly to believe that they never checked. Serel's empire was built mostly on hope's gullibility, catalogued without cruelty and without reverence by a clerk who clearly checked everything twice.

Voss read on, and the lamp burned lower, and somewhere past midnight the ledger changed.

*Entry 214. Item: prayer-slip, answered. Origin unknown. Contents: petition for healing of a child's lung-rot, signed Mara Elloway, fishwife. Status: FULFILLED. Buyer: grandmother of the child. Price: everything she had.*

He had written *FULFILLED* the way he wrote everything, evenly, without flourish. But below it, in the margin, smaller:

*Note: verified. The child lives. I checked twice. I don't know what to do with this.*

Voss sat back. The lamp hissed. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were not quite steady, and she looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger — to some younger woman who had once believed things cleanly, before the Censorate had taught her that certainty was a tool and tools must be kept sharp and never questioned.

She turned pages faster now. Answered prayer after answered prayer. A widow's petition for rain on her dead son's salt-killed fields — fulfilled, verified, fields green by autumn. A drowning man's bargain made mid-flood with whatever listened — fulfilled; the man lived and would not speak of it. Dozens. Hundreds. The guild had barely had to smuggle divinity at all; mostly it had smuggled *receipts*. Proof that the old gods, silent for a century, had suddenly begun saying *yes*.

And the buyers. She made herself look at the buyers' column, and there it was in Hartwell's dispassionate hand: not sorcerers, not cultists. Fishwives. Grandmothers. Sailors' wives. A bricklayer who paid for a prayer that his brother's broken back might mend. People who had prayed into the dark for years and, when the dark finally answered, had been forced to buy their own miracles back through a smugglers' guild because the lawful world offered them nothing but a pyre.

*This is the plague,* Voss thought, and the thought had edges now, and the edges cut. *This is what I crossed half the country to stamp out. A grandmother buying back her grandson's breath.*

She should have felt doubt. What she felt instead was colder and more precise: the sensation of a structure shifting underfoot, a hairline crack running through load-bearing stone. She had built her life on a simple architecture: the returning gods were a flood, floods killed the innocent, King Aldous had built the levee, therefore Aldous was mercy. Every interrogation, every pyre, every hard dawn had rested on that architecture. And now a clerk's neat columns were showing her that the flood waters, examined closely, were mostly tears — and that the levee had gates in it she had never been shown.

Because she had read the seized correspondence too. She knew what the requisitions from the capital asked for. Not intercepted prayers for study. Not confiscated relics for destruction. *Live cargo. Warm crates. Unopened.* Whole consignments of answered petitions routed not to the Censorate's bonfires but to the royal artificers at Vhal Mora, where the king's engineers had lately begun building something they called, in their careful memos, *the Engine of Ordered Devotion*.

Ordered devotion. She had read those words a hundred times and heard only bureaucracy. Now, sitting in a lamplit room over a smuggler's ledger full of grandmothers' grief, she heard what the words actually meant. You did not censor a god. You could not. A censored god still answered whom it pleased. But a god bound — fed on harvested prayers, channeled through engines, its yeses and noes dispensed by royal warrant at royal prices — a bound god was not a plague to be treated. It was a well to be owned.

Aldous was not building a levee. He was building a tap.

Voss closed the ledger. Her reflection looked back at her from the dark window, a woman in an inquisitor's coat with a smuggler's book under her hands, and for the first time in eleven years she did not recognize the architecture of her own certainty — could see, with terrible clarity, the hairline crack running through it, and could not yet tell whether the whole wall would fall or hold.

One thing she could tell. The sigil-bearer whose trail she had been given — the runaway clerk, the invisible man — had catalogued all of this, entry by entry, and had written in the margin of a miracle, *I don't know what to do with this.* Which meant that somewhere ahead of her was a man who asked the same question she was only now learning to ask.

She opened the ledger again, to Entry 214, and read the fishwife's name aloud to the empty room, because it seemed suddenly important that someone in authority say these names as names and not as inventory.

"Mara Elloway," she said. "Whose child lives."

Outside, the garrison bells rang the third hour, and far to the south, where the royal road left the free cities for the heartlands, the vanguard of the Ninth Army was making camp — and in its baggage train, under seal and guard, rode the first components of the Engine of Ordered Devotion, bound for a chapel by the water that Voss did not yet know existed, carrying orders she had not yet read, signed by a king she had served for eleven years and was beginning, entry by entry, line by line, to understand she had never met at all.
