## 9. The Market of Answered Prayers

The road into Corvane had changed in the three weeks they'd been gone, and not in any way a map would show.

Oscar felt it before he saw it — a hum under his boots, faint as a struck tuning fork that wouldn't quite die. Freya felt it too. He could tell by the way she walked, her chin lifted slightly, like a woman tasting smoke on air no one else had noticed yet.

"The city's *praying*," she said quietly. "All of it. At once."

They came over the last rise at dusk, and Corvane lay below them wearing a face he didn't recognize. He had spent six years learning that city the way an accountant learns a ledger — every street a column, every district a sum that always balanced. Now the sums were wrong. Lanterns burned in windows where honest folk went to bed early. Bells rang from towers that had stood silent since the old gods withdrew, and the ringing wasn't ordered by any bell-master Oscar knew. It was ragged, overlapping, ecstatic — a hundred hands hauling on a hundred ropes because they could not help themselves.

And above it all, rising off the rooftops like heat-shimmer, was the sound. Thousands of voices, murmured and shouted and sung, all asking for something.

"It's louder than the harbor," Oscar said. "Louder than the fish-market on a feast day." He swallowed. "Freya, what are they *asking*?"

"Everything." Her voice was flat, which he'd learned meant frightened. "Cures. Coin. Dead mothers back. Rain for crops, sun for weddings, vengeance for insults. Everything anyone ever wanted and never got." She pulled her hood lower. "When prayer stopped being answered, people learned to want quietly. Now the wanting is answered, and they've forgotten how to be quiet."

They entered by the Salt Gate, where Oscar expected questions and found none — the gate-guards weren't checking papers. They were holding out their hands, palms up, eyes closed, lips moving. One of them had a rosary of knotted twine wrapped around his fingers so tightly the tips had gone purple. Beyond them, the streets were rivers of people moving in both directions with the unhurried urgency of pilgrims, and the smell was incense and tallow and something sweeter underneath, something that made the back of Oscar's teeth ache.

He knew that sweetness. He'd smelled it leaking from a crate in the guildhouse cellar, the night everything ended.

*Answered prayers,* he thought, and his hand — the sigil hand — curled shut inside his glove.

* * *

The market hadn't existed a month ago. That much he was sure of, because it filled the Grand Assay Square, and six years of balancing the Guild of Impossible Things' books had taught him every legitimate use of that square: grain inspections, the spring wool fair, once — memorably — a menagerie whose fire-breathing wyrm had gotten loose and eaten two tents. There had never been anything like this.

Stalls packed the square shoulder to shoulder, lit by lanterns of colored glass, and every stall sold miracles.

Not relics — Oscar knew relics, had catalogued four hundred of them, and most were bone and brass and confident forgery. These were *live*. A woman in a merchant's silks sold bottled voices: uncork one, and your dead father's laugh filled the room for exactly eleven seconds. A boy no older than Oscar had been when he ran away offered luck by the pinch, scooped from a sack that glowed faintly gold and screamed very softly if you held it too long. In the square's center, on a scaffold hung with ribbons, a priest of no order Oscar recognized — his vestments were cut from sailcloth and stitched with prayer-slips — auctioned healings to the highest bidder while a line of the sick wound around the fountain, some of them kneeling, some of them weeping, one small girl simply standing and staring up at the priest with the terrible patience of children who have learned what hope costs.

Prices were chalked on slate boards everywhere, and the numbers made Oscar's professional soul flinch. A healing: forty crowns, or "equivalent in devotion." A bottled voice: sixty. Luck: ten a pinch, twenty if you needed it *desperately*, and there was a clerk beside the sack with a little brass instrument that measured desperation somehow, because the price changed between customers.

"They're selling it," Oscar said. His voice came out strange. "The answers. People are *selling* them."

"What did you think a smugglers' guild would do with a miracle?" Freya didn't look at him. She was watching the crowd with the expression of someone counting exits. "Gods come back after a century of silence, and the first thing free folk build is a till. It's not corruption, Oscar. It's just people. Power runs downhill into ordinary hands same as water, and ordinary hands squeeze."

"You don't have to sound like you approve."

"I don't." Something moved behind her eyes, quick and dark, there and gone. "I've seen what happens next. I helped make it happen once, remember." She touched his elbow, steering him left, away from the healing-scaffold. "We're here for Tammas Oduya's warehouse records. Serel's seized stock will be listed in the harbormaster's confiscation rolls, and the harbormaster owes me a debt older than you are. We get in, we get the list, we get out before—"

She stopped.

Oscar followed her gaze and understood why.

Across the square, past the luck-sack and the bottled voices, past a stall where a man sold *forgiveness* in wax-sealed jars (the queue for that one was longest), stood a notice-board. And nailed to the notice-board, in rows of thirty, were broadsheets. Oscar couldn't read them at this distance, but he didn't need to. He knew the woodcut at the top of each one, because he'd seen it burned into a doorpost in a village they'd passed through two days ago:

A hand, palm open, marked with a sigil like a key crossed with a wound.

Below it, in letters sized to be read across a room: **THE SIGIL-THIEF WALKS AMONG YOU. THE CROWN PAYS FOR HIS HAND.**

"He got ahead of us," Oscar said. His mouth had gone dry. "Voss. Or Aldous. Someone printed these in the capital and shipped them downriver faster than we walked."

"Usurpers are good at printing," Freya said. "It's half of what usurping is." She turned him by the shoulder, gently, away from the board. "Don't look at it again. Don't look at your own hand. Walk."

But of course everyone else was looking. The broadsheets were being torn down and passed around, read aloud by those who could read to those who couldn't, and Oscar heard the words chasing him through the crowd in fragments — *sigil-thief*, *steals the gods' gifts*, *that's why prayers go unanswered for some*, *the Crown says one man is eating everyone's miracles* —

"That's not even what it is," he whispered. "That's not — it's a covenant, it's a *key*, it's not—"

"It doesn't matter what it is." Freya's grip on his arm tightened. "It matters what it's *for*. Fear needs a shape. They've given fear your shape. Now walk faster."

* * *

They almost made the harbormaster's office.

Later — much later, in the years when he was no one and remembered everything — Oscar would take the memory apart a dozen times, looking for the moment it went wrong, and never find a single point of failure. It was, in the end, nothing but arithmetic. Too many people, too many broadsheets, too much fear and hunger in one square, and one hooded man walking too quickly past the healing-scaffold at exactly the wrong hour.

It was the little girl who started it. The one who'd been staring at the priest with patient eyes. As Oscar passed, she turned — following his movement the way flowers follow light — and pointed.

"Mama," she said, clear as a bell over the crowd. "His hand."

Her mother looked. So did the priest. So did forty people in a widening ring, and Oscar understood, with the cold clarity of a ledger balancing, that his glove had slipped. Half an inch of wrist showing. Half an inch was enough. The sigil caught the lantern-light, and the crowd made a sound he had never heard a crowd make before — not a roar, not a scream, but a single indrawn breath, thousands of lungs at once, like the sea pulling back before it breaks.

"*Sigil-thief*," someone said.

"No," Oscar said, hands up, backing. "No, listen — it's not what the broadsheets say, I'm not stealing anything, the sigil is a *key*, it belongs to a covenant, if you'd just let me explain—"

"Explain!" A big man in a butcher's apron stepped into the ring, and he was laughing, and the laugh was worse than shouting. "The thief wants to *explain*! My daughter's been deaf since she was four, friend. Forty crowns for a healing. Forty! You know what a butcher makes? Explain to me why the gods' own gifts cost more than my girl's ears!"

"We don't set the prices—" Oscar began, and knew instantly it was the worst thing he could have said, because it was true, and truth was fuel now.

The butcher hit him.

It wasn't a fighting blow; it was the blow of a man swatting something verminous, and it took Oscar off his feet and into the luck-stall, which collapsed in a shriek of gold light and screaming canvas, and then the ring closed. Boots. Fists. A knee. Someone had a boat-hook — he'd catalogued boat-hooks once, in a manifest, *twelve units, harbor district* — and its hook caught his collar and dragged him upright so the crowd could see his face, see the sigil, and the faces around him were the worst part. Not cruel faces. *Hungry* ones. People who had queued all day for miracles priced out of reach, looking at the one man alive who carried the gods' doorkey in his skin, and wanting it the way starving things want.

"Cut it off," the butcher said. "Cut the hand off and the prayers stop being stolen. Cut it off and maybe my girl hears the bells."

There was a knife. Of course there was a knife. Oscar felt the flat of it against his wrist, cold through the glove, and heard himself saying the most accountant's prayer ever prayed — *there has to be a discrepancy, there's always a discrepancy, find the error in the column* — and finding none, because sometimes the books are simply right, and the sum owed is the sum owed—

Then the world went white.

Not bright — *white*, total, a page torn out of existence. The boat-hook's drag vanished. The knife vanished. The roaring crowd vanished, and for one endless instant Oscar hung in a silence so complete he could hear his own heart stammering, and beneath the silence, faintly, a sound like a great many voices receding — not angry anymore, only confused, murmuring, *where did the square go, where did the evening go* —

And beneath even that, close against his ear, a voice he knew, saying words in a language that predated every ledger in every city, and the voice was *straining*, the way a rope strains, the way a bridge strains.

The world came back.

They were standing in a drainage culvert under the Salt Gate road, forty yards from the square, in the dark, in the smell of wet stone and old tide. Oscar was on his knees. He didn't remember getting there. Across from him, Freya stood with one hand braced against the culvert wall, and in the gloom he could see that her hand was shaking, and that it wasn't the only thing.

Because Freya Vane was *older*.

Not aged — nothing so gentle as aging. It was as though someone had reached into the painting of her and thinned the paint. The lines around her eyes had deepened from etchings to crevices. Her hair, which had been iron-grey with black threads running through it, was now grey through and through, and thinner, and two of the black threads seemed to have simply *left*. She was leaning on the wall the way old women lean, and when she lifted her head and saw him staring, she smiled with cracked lips and said, in a voice sanded rougher than it had been an hour ago:

"Don't look at me like that. It's rude to audit a lady."

"Freya." He crawled toward her. "Freya, what did you do? What did it *cost*?"

"A working that size, unanchored, no circle, no preparation?" She waved a hand, dismissive, and the hand trembled like a leaf. "I paid in the only coin I had left. Years, boy. Call it five. Maybe seven. The gods keep sloppy books about that sort of thing — fitting, really, given whose apprentice you are." She laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on too long.

Oscar took her arm and eased her down onto the culvert's dry ledge, and she let him, which frightened him more than the cough. Freya Vane did not let people help her. He crouched in front of her and took her thin hand in both of his, and the sigil under his glove pulsed once, warm, as if it recognized the debt now written against her.

"Seven years," he said. "You spent seven years of your life so a mob wouldn't cut off my hand."

"So a mob wouldn't cut off *the key*," she corrected. But her eyes, sunk deeper now in their sockets, held his, and neither of them believed the correction. "Besides. I'm old, Oscar. I've been old longer than you've been alive — there are reasons for that, and one day, if we live, I'll tell you all of them, and you'll wish I hadn't. Seven years off the back end of a hoard like mine is a rounding error." She squeezed his fingers with surprising strength. "Whereas you are the only oath-vessel left in the world, and you are *not allowed to die in a fish market*. Do you understand me? Whatever else happens — whatever you learn about me, whatever you learn about yourself, however angry you get — you carry that sigil to the anchor-stone. That is the whole of your job now. Everything else is commentary."

Oscar looked down at their joined hands. Hers, spotted and veined. His, still trembling from the knife's cold kiss.

"When it caught my wrist," he said quietly, "they weren't angry. That's what I can't — everyone talks about mobs like they're monsters, but they weren't. They were *hungry*. That butcher's daughter is deaf, and a healing costs forty crowns, and somewhere above us a priest is auctioning miracles off a scaffold like prize pigs. The gods came back and the first thing we built was a *tollbooth*." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Six years I kept books for smugglers and told myself the numbers were clean because I kept them honestly. But I never once asked what the columns were *of*."

"No," Freya said. "You didn't. That's not a sin, boy. It's a luxury. And luxuries are the first thing power takes." She pushed herself upright, slowly, using his shoulder, and stood swaying in the culvert dark like a tree that had lost too many rings. "Now. The harbormaster's rolls are still waiting, and we have perhaps an hour before the square realizes its thief vanished mid-miracle and starts dragging the drains. Can you walk?"

"Yes." He rose. Then, because the ledger in his head refused to stay shut: "Freya. The years you spent. Will you spend more? Before this is done?"

She was quiet for a moment. Above them, muffled by stone and earth, the bells of Corvane were still ringing, and the murmur of ten thousand prayers sifted down through the cracks like fine ash.

"Oh, child," she said at last, and started walking. "I intend to spend *all* of them. The only question worth asking is whether I'll have bought anything worth the price. Come along. We have a ferryman to find, and I suspect we're about to learn what Serel's been doing with confiscated stock — because if the harbormaster's rolls say what I think they'll say, then our dear guildmaster hasn't merely been selling tokens to the Crown."

She glanced back at him, and in the dark her deepened eyes caught the distant lantern-light from the grate above, and held it, burning.

"He's been selling *pieces of the covenant itself*. And there's a caravan leaving Corvane tomorrow at dawn that I would very much like to rob."
