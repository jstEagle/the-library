## 10. What Serel Knew

The road out of Corvane ran through the salt marshes like a scar through old skin, and for three days Freya had been teaching Oscar how to lie in wait.

He had thought he knew how to hide. Ten years of keeping ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things had made him a master of small concealments — the unremarkable face, the ink-stained hands that drew no second glance, the careful habit of never being the most interesting thing in any room. But hiding was not what Freya taught. What she taught was stillness: how to become a feature of the landscape rather than an absence in it. How to breathe shallow and slow behind a wall of gorse until your heartbeat matched the wind's. How to let a patrol pass so close you could smell the tallow on their boots and feel no fear at all, because fear was a scent too, and dogs could find it.

"You run beautifully," she'd said the first night, watching him come down off the dike with his heart hammering. "You always did. But running is what they expect from you now. We're going to teach them to look for a runner while something else is happening."

So they waited, and the something else happened without them.

Tammas Oduya brought word of the caravan by way of his river, which apparently spoke to him in the manner that other men's neighbors spoke to them — gossip carried downstream, rumor pooled in eddies. Six wagons under guild colors, heavy-laden, escorted by twelve hired blades and one man in a fine coat who rode with his hood up against the drizzle as though rain were beneath his dignity. They would take the marsh road north, because the king's soldiers held the causeway, and because Serel Doss had always believed that discomfort was a tax other people paid.

"He's going to Aldous," Tammas said. He sat in the bottom of his ferry-boat with his oars across his knees, looking older than the forty years he'd given the river, or perhaps exactly as old as those years had made him. "Not to sell you out again. That market's closed — Voss has what she wants from him, or thinks she does. No. He's going to deliver."

"Deliver what?" Oscar asked, though some cold part of him already knew.

"Tokens." Tammas turned his hand over, and there in his palm lay a disc of greenish bronze, crusted with river-silt, worn smooth along one edge as if it had been rubbed by a thumb for decades. "Oath-tokens. There were seven once. I've kept mine where no thief would think to dig. But the others—" His jaw worked. "The others were left lying about when we scattered, after. And Serel has spent ten years picking them up like a magpie picking up buttons."

Freya took the token from Tammas's hand and held it up to the grey light. Something moved inside it — not light, exactly; more like the memory of light, a slow turning gleam, the way water remembers the moon after the moon has gone. "This is Oduya work," she said quietly. "River-forged. It shouldn't have left the water. It's dying out here." She gave it back with a gentleness Oscar had rarely seen her spend on anything, and Tammas closed his fingers around it like a man closing his hand around a wound.

"That's why the whispers got quieter," the ferryman said. "Every token they take, the god it belonged to gets a little deafer. A little smaller. Mine's been whispering forty years. If he sells this one too—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Oscar had heard enough whispers lately, in the burning of his own hand, to know what silence would mean.

* * *

They chose their ground the way Freya chose everything: precisely, and with an economy that looked like carelessness until it was far too late to argue with.

The marsh road crossed a drainage cut on a stone bridge no wider than two wagons abreast, its parapets furred with moss, its arch half-silted so that the black water backed up behind it into a slow lagoon fringed with reeds. On the near side stood a ruined tollhouse, roofless, its walls good to shoulder height. It was the only cover for a mile in any direction, which meant it was the first place any competent escort would look — and so, of course, it was empty. Freya wanted them to look there. She wanted them to look there and find nothing and relax, and meanwhile the real trap would be the road itself, the reeds, the water, the ordinary world turned traitor.

She worked all night by feel, her hands moving over mud and reed-stem, murmuring words that Oscar's sigil translated into pressure rather than sound — he felt each phrase land somewhere behind his sternum, a soft repeated knocking, as though something on the other side of a door were checking to see if he was home. By dawn the lagoon was a lie. The reeds stood where reeds should stand, but the water beneath them had been taught to hold a man the way a hand holds a moth: gently, and without any intention of letting go. The mud had been taught to remember being a mouth. Even the drizzle had been bent a degree off true, so that it fell thickest exactly where the escort's lanterns would be, and nowhere else.

"You could have done all this yourself, ten years ago," Oscar said. "You wouldn't have needed me at all."

"I didn't do it alone even then," Freya said. She was crouched at the lip of the cut, washing her hands in the black water, and she did not look at him. "And what I did need, I took. That's rather the point of everything that's gone wrong since." She stood, wiped her hands on her coat, and finally met his eyes. "Get down in the reeds. When they come, you don't move until I move. Not when you're afraid. Not when you're angry. *When I move.*"

"And if you don't move?"

"Then stay in the reeds and let the marsh do its work, and hate me afterward." She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the hedge-witch's kitchen, sharp as a paring knife. "But I'll move, Oscar. I've spent ten years not moving. I'm rather looking forward to it."

* * *

The caravan came at mid-morning, under a sky the color of dishwater, and it came wrong.

That was Oscar's first thought, crouched in the reeds with cold water seeping through his knees: *this is wrong.* Six wagons, yes. Twelve blades, yes. But the wagons rode too high on their axles for goods that heavy, and the guards rode too loose in their saddles, scanning the hedgerows instead of the road ahead, and Serel —

Serel was not in the column at all.

For one long, sickening moment Oscar thought the whole thing was a decoy, that Serel had split his force and was already miles away on the causeway road, laughing at them from under his fine hood. Then the last wagon crested the bridge, and Oscar saw the cage built onto its bed — iron-barred, curtained, luxurious as a bishop's palanquin — and understood. Serel hadn't sent himself ahead. He had simply promoted himself out of the weather and into the furniture. Of course. Ten years of selling other people's holiness by the ounce, and the man had learned to travel like the merchandise.

The lead wagon's wheels touched the bridge. The second followed. The escort bunched, as escorts do, into the narrow throat of the crossing, and Freya moved.

There was no signal Oscar could have named afterward. One moment the marsh was a marsh; the next it was a war. The lagoon exhaled. Reeds that had stood all morning in perfect innocence leaned inward like a crowd pressing toward a spectacle, and the water beneath the bridge's shadow rose — not in a wave, nothing so honest as a wave — but upward, silently, a swelling black lens that lifted the third wagon's rear wheels clear of the stones before the driver had finished his curse. Horses screamed. Men shouted orders at each other across the noise, and the drizzle thickened obediently around every lantern until the whole bridge was a blind white room.

Oscar moved when Freya moved, and Freya moved like something the marsh had dreamed.

He lost sight of her almost immediately — that was the design of it — and found his own work waiting where she'd said it would be: the rear guard, three men who hung back from the panic with the wary competence of professionals, already turning toward the reeds with their crossbows level. He had planned, in the nights of waiting, to be afraid. Instead he found a strange calm, almost ledger-like in its clarity. Three men. Crossbows, wet strings, poor footing. Distances. Angles. It was, he realized with something like horror, exactly like balancing a column of figures: you simply could not afford sentiment about what the numbers added up to.

The marsh handled two of them. The mud remembered being a mouth, and the men learned it personally, going down without even time to scream properly, the black water folding over them with a courtesy that was worse than violence. The third fired — the bolt went through the reeds a hand's width from Oscar's cheek, close enough that he felt the wind of it — and then Freya was simply *there*, out of the white rain, and whatever she said to him was quiet and short and the man folded up like a letter and did not get up.

"The wagon," she said. "The cage. Go."

* * *

Serel Doss had locked himself into his own luxury, and it saved his life and damned his dignity in equal measure.

Oscar reached the caged wagon through chaos — horses plunging free of their traces, guards floundering in water that gripped their boots, somewhere ahead the deep groan of the bridge's arch giving up a little more of itself — and put his knife through the curtain to find the guildmaster pressed back against the far bars with both hands raised, his fine coat soaked, his hood fallen, his face the color of tallow.

"Hartwell." Serel's voice cracked on the name. "Oscar. Now, let's be reasonable—"

"You sold me." Oscar hauled the curtain wider. Inside the cage, among velvet cushions and a traveling writing desk, sat an iron strongbox the size of a coffin-foot, and beside it a leather case he knew better than his own heartbeat — corner-scuffed, strap-replaced twice, stamped with the guild's impossible sigil. His ledger. His ten years. "I balanced your books for a decade, Serel. I made your smuggling legible. I made your sins *tidy*. And you sold my name to the first inquisitor who knocked."

"I sold your *location*," Serel said, with the pedantic precision that had always made Oscar want to strike him, "which is a different line item entirely, and if you'll open that strongbox you'll understand why I couldn't afford to be sentimental about—"

Freya arrived at the wagon's tail and put her hand flat against the iron bar. The bar glowed dull red. Serel snatched his fingers back with a yelp.

"Open it," Freya said, "or I will open it, and you won't enjoy the difference."

What was in the strongbox was worse than gold, though there was gold in it, packed around the edges like packing straw. In the center, nested in cloth, lay six objects. Oscar knew them at once for what they were, because he had seen the seventh that morning in Tammas's palm: discs of bronze and bone and one of dark glass, each crusted with the dirt of wherever it had lain forgotten, each carrying that same slow internal gleam, the memory-of-light. Two of them were dim. Not dulled — *dimmed*, the way a lamp is dimmed, deliberately, from outside.

"They were going to waste," Serel said. He had recovered his voice, and worse, his rhythm; he talked the way he negotiated, filling silence with certainty. "Scattered. Rotting in attics and graves and riverbeds while the gods they anchored faded to folklore. I consolidated. That's all the guild ever was, Hartwell — consolidation. And King Aldous pays magnificently for consolidation. Do you know what he's building? Do you know what a decade of my commissions has bought him?" He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, wet and genuine. "You think he wants to censor the gods. Everyone thinks that. The proclamations are very good. But a censor needs nothing but paper. What Aldous is buying with my tokens is a *harness*."

"Ten years," Oscar said. The numbers assembled themselves in his head with terrible ease, the way they always did. "You've been selling to him for ten years. Before the prayers started coming back. Before anyone knew the covenant was failing. You were feeding him pieces of it a decade ago."

"I was feeding him pieces of it when it wasn't *failing*," Serel snapped. "Do you imagine I broke it? Do you imagine I *could*? I found a crack and I widened it, the way every guildmaster before me did, because that is the trade, boy. The Guild of Impossible Things was founded on that crack. Ask your mistress. Ask her who held the hammer." He jerked his chin at Freya, and his smile was pure venom. "Ask her whose oath-token is the one I never managed to buy."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere behind them, the bridge made another low grinding sound, patient as tide.

"The guild's founder," Oscar said slowly. He had read the founding entries a hundred times, in his first year, hungry for the history of the institution that had taken him in. *Established by a benefactor who wished to remain unnamed.* Every record of the guild's origin stopped at that same polite wall. "The benefactor. The unnamed one."

"A former oath-bearer," Freya said. Her voice was flat, and very tired, and it was the tiredest Oscar had ever heard her. "One of the seven. After the breaking, she couldn't bear to keep her token and couldn't bear to destroy it, so she built an institution around hiding things like it. She thought she was making a sanctuary for the dangerous leftovers of the old world." She looked at the strongbox, at the six dimming tokens nested in their gold. "She made a shop."

"And her token," Oscar said, "is the seventh. The one Serel never bought."

"It's yours," Freya said. "It has been yours since the night you were branded. Which is why I am going to take these six out of this box, and you are going to stand there and let me, and then we are going to burn this wagon to the waterline, and Serel—" she turned, and the rain seemed to bend away from her face, leaving it dry, leaving it terrible, "—Serel is going to tell us where Aldous is taking them."

"Am I," said Serel.

"You've been delivering to the same place for ten years," Freya said. "Your own ledgers will say so. And your ledgers are in that case, and the case is in my apprentice's hands, and unlike you, Serel, my apprentice never once fudged a figure."

* * *

In the end, Serel told them — not because of the fire, though Freya lit a small, persuasive flame on the wagon's iron rail and let it crawl; and not because of the marsh, though the water had begun lapping over the bridge stones around the trapped wheels with unmistakable appetite. He told them because he was, underneath everything, a merchant, and Freya offered him the only currency he had ever truly valued.

"Walk away," she said. "That's the price. Tell us where, and walk away, and live. Keep your mouth shut about tonight. Because I promise you, Serel — and I say this with love, such as it is — if you speak of us to Aldous or to Voss or to any living soul, I will know, and what I send for you the second time will not be negotiable."

"You're bluffing," Serel said. "You're *weak*. You've been spending yourself for weeks — I can see it, woman, you're translucent—"

"I'm expensive," Freya agreed. "But I have one purchase left in me, and I have decided what it's for. Choose."

He chose. Of course he chose. He told them in the clipped cadence of a man dictating a delivery schedule, because that was what it was — ten years of deliveries, same destination, same arrangement, the tokens handed over to a king's factor at a site chosen long before Aldous wore the crown. And when he finished, Freya stepped back from the wagon, and the marsh opened a lane of dry road through the floodwater, all the way to the causeway, as clean and courteous as a shopkeeper sweeping a doorstep.

Serel gathered his coat around him and climbed down. At the edge of the lane he paused, and looked back at Oscar with an expression that was almost, horribly, wistful.

"You were the best clerk I ever had," he said. "Truly. You should know the last thing I sold Aldous wasn't a token. It was information. About the anchor-stone. Where it sleeps, and what it sleeps under, and how much prayer it takes to wake it the wrong way." He smiled. "He doesn't need to reforge the covenant, Hartwell. He means to forge a new one. With himself at the center. Check the ledger — entry nine hundred and eleven. I always did keep immaculate books."

Then he walked away down the lane of dry road, and the marsh closed behind him like water over a stone, and Oscar did not watch him go, because he was already opening the ledger.

* * *

They read it in the ruins of the tollhouse, with the burned wagon smoking behind them and Tammas standing guard on the dike, his recovered token clenched in his fist like a talisman, which, Oscar supposed, was exactly what it was.

Entry nine hundred and eleven was dated four months ago, and it was written in Serel's smallest, most confidential hand — the hand he used for bribes. Oscar read it aloud, because Freya asked him to, and because reading it aloud made it real in a way that silent comprehension refused to be.

*"Sold to the crown, final consignment: one (1) item, provenance covenant-era, described by seller as 'the keystone.' Location per seller's family records: the chapel of Saint Verene, drowned parish, below the Old Reservoir, Corvane lower city. Seller notes: the keystone is the covenant's anchor-stone, the point at which the original seven oaths were bound into the world. Seller notes further: His Majesty's artificers believe the stone may be induced to accept a substitute covenant, provided sufficient harvested prayer is applied and a vessel of suitable blood is provided to serve as the binding-point. Seller has identified a candidate vessel. Seller awaits instructions."*

Oscar stopped reading. The rain ticked against the broken walls. Somewhere out on the water, a bird cried once and was silent.

"A vessel of suitable blood," he said. His voice sounded far away to him, as though someone else were using it. "He means me. He's known about me since before Voss ever came to Corvane. Serel didn't sell my location to the inquisitors because I became dangerous. He sold it because I was always the *merchandise*."

"Yes," Freya said.

"And the anchor-stone is under Corvane. Under the reservoir. Under the drowned quarter." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "I used to take my lunch on the reservoir wall. Ten years. I ate my bread above it every single day."

"Stones don't mind being slept on," Freya said. She was looking at the ledger, at the entry, and her face had gone very still — the stillness of deep water, of a thing holding itself down. "Oscar. Give me the book."

He handed it over, and watched her turn the pages backward, faster and faster, ten years of his meticulous columns blurring past — and then slower, and slower, until she stopped at pages he had never written, in a hand he had never seen, pressed into the ledger's first leaves so faintly that only the raking light of her cupped flame revealed them. The founder's hand. The benefactor's. The former oath-bearer's.

"What is it?" Tammas asked. "What does it say?"

Freya read for a long moment. When she looked up, the flame in her palm had gone out, and her eyes in the grey light were the color of the reservoir on a windless day.

"It says the stone can't be moved," she said, "and it can't be destroyed, and it can't be woken the wrong way — *unless the waking is done by consent.*" She closed the ledger and held it out to Oscar, handle first, like a key. "Aldous has the stone's address and a mountain of stolen prayer. What he doesn't have is consent — and he's about to discover that the only blood that can give it is currently holding this book." Her mouth curved, but there was no comfort in it. "Congratulations, apprentice. Your ledger just stopped being a refuge. It's a map now, and a weapon, and a confession. And we have maybe six days before the king's artificers reach the drowned chapel — so I suggest we read every word of it on the way."

Oscar took the ledger back. It weighed what it had always weighed. It weighed everything.

Behind them, the marsh settled back into being merely a marsh, and the road north lay open, and somewhere beneath the black water of home, an old stone waited to hear whose name would be spoken over it.
