## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been drowning for two hundred years, and it had done it beautifully.

Freya stood at the water's edge where the road simply gave up and slid beneath the surface — a road she remembered walking dry-shod, once, with six others whose names she had not spoken aloud in a century. The flood had come slowly, kindly even, the way grief comes: an inch at a time until the bell tower was only a broken tooth above the waterline and the god of the valley had moved his congregation to higher ground or into the ground itself.

Now the water was black and still as poured ink, and beneath it, forty feet down, the anchor-stone waited in the crypt where they had set it, singing to itself in a frequency that made her back teeth ache.

"You're doing the thing," Oscar said behind her. "Where you look at something like you're deciding whether to bury it or marry it."

"I'm deciding whether to drown us both," Freya said. "It's a shorter list than usual."

He came up beside her at the bank, and she watched him take in the drowned chapel the way he took in everything now — with the ledger-keeper's eye, cataloguing, measuring, converting dread into columns of manageable fact. He'd changed since Corvane. Or rather, he'd stopped changing; the boy who had run from her hedge twenty years ago had built himself into a careful, closed system, and the last weeks had been prying him open joint by joint. His left hand was wrapped in linen against the cold, but the sigil burned through the cloth. She could see its light between the weave, faint as a coal under ash. It had been burning since the anchor-stone's song had found them on the road, three days ago, and called them here like a hook through the meat of the world.

"Tammas says the water doesn't want us dead," Oscar said. "He asked his passenger."

"And what did the whisper say?"

"That it remembers this place." Oscar paused. "It said it used to sing here. Before."

Before. The word sat between them like a stone neither wanted to pick up. *Before we broke it,* Freya thought. *Before seven people who loved the world too much decided they knew better than the world did.*

She waded in before she could talk herself out of it.

* * *

The descent was worse than she remembered, because last time there had been no remembering to do.

The water was cold in the old way — not river-cold or winter-cold but covenant-cold, the chill of a border crossed without permission. It pressed on her ears, on her chest, on the thin membranes of her intention. Light came down in shafts from the collapsed roof, green-gold and trembling, and fish scattered from her like startled thoughts. Below her the chapel nave spread out in silhouette: pews furred with silt, an altar draped in weed like a mourner's veil, and beyond the altar, down steps that had once led to a crypt and now led to a throat, the anchor-stone.

It was smaller than memory insisted. That was the first lie the deep told her, and she knew it for a lie because she had helped carve it. A block of grey granite no bigger than a millstone, set into the crypt floor, its face covered in the seven-fold sigil — and every line of it cracked.

Not chipped. Not eroded. *Cracked*, the way ice cracks on a pond in spring, in long radiating fractures that wept a light the color of an old bruise. Through each crack, faintly, she could feel pressure — the pressure of prayers pushing through from the other side, thousands upon thousands of them, the accumulated weight of two centuries of human longing finally finding hairline gaps in the wall she'd built. Each prayer that slipped through became a miracle somewhere in the free cities. A fever broken. A coin found. A dead child breathing. And each one widened the crack by a hair's width.

The cage was failing, and it was failing *because people still hoped*, which was either the most beautiful thing she had ever learned or the most damning.

Oscar descended beside her, awkward in borrowed oilskins, his wrapped hand held out before him like a lantern. The sigil flared as he neared the stone, and by its light she saw his face underwater — wide-eyed, mouth slightly open, bubbles escaping in silver strings he didn't seem to notice. He was looking at the cracks. Counting them, probably. She almost laughed, which would have killed them both.

She touched the stone.

* * *

The reforging was supposed to be partial. That was the whole design of the attempt: not to mend the covenant entire — that required all seven oath-bearers, and she had perhaps four, if Tammas's river god counted and if the two tokens they'd traced to the eastern cities proved real — but to brace it. To pack the cracks with her own power the way a mason packs lead into failing mortar. Buy years. Buy time enough to gather the rest.

She knelt on the crypt floor, water churning around her knees from the force of the stone's song, and laid both palms flat against the granite.

"Whatever happens," she had told Oscar on the bank, "if I stop moving, you pull me up. Don't think. Don't be clever. Pull."

He had nodded with the terrible earnestness that had always been his undoing, and she had thought, not for the first time: *I am about to do something unforgivable to this boy again.*

She opened herself.

Power is not what hedge-witch stories say it is. It is not fire, not lightning, not any of the pretty metaphors. It is *attention* — the willingness to hold the whole shape of a thing in your mind without flinching, and to insist, with everything you are, that the shape can change. Freya had spent twenty years practicing not-attention. Living small. Letting the world go unheld. Now she gathered all of it back — every hoarded year, every unspent working, the great cold reservoir of herself — and poured it into the cracks.

For a moment, it worked.

She felt the covenant respond the way an old dog responds to its name: slow, stiff, astonished to be called. The seven-fold sigil brightened under her palms. In her mind's eye she saw the wall between realms as it truly was — vast beyond vastness, shot through with fissures — and she saw her own power flow into those fissures like wax into seal-mold, hardening, holding—

And then the wall pushed back.

She should have expected it. She *had* expected it, in the part of herself that had voted for the breaking, all those years ago in this same crypt, while Veyra screamed behind the stone. A cage does not want bracing. A cage wants either freedom or collapse, and the thing caged inside the covenant had spent a hundred years learning the architecture of its prison. As Freya's power entered the cracks, it met something already there — patient, coiled, intelligent — and that something *took hold of her*.

Not her body. Her body stayed kneeling, safe as a glove. It took the rest. It reached up through her open self and began to pull her *through* — through the attention she had offered, along the thread of her own insistence, unwinding her the way a child unwinds a ball of string, and Freya understood with perfect clarity that this was how the god fed now, this was how it had survived a century in the dark: anything that touched the cracks from outside could be drawn in, unraveled, worn.

She felt her edges go. Her name went first — she felt it loosen like a knot, felt the word *Freya* become negotiable, then optional, then a rumor. Then her memories began to slide, not fading but *relocating*, pouring out of her into the dark behind the wall: the hedge, the apprentices, the smell of bread, the face of— 

The face of the boy on the bank.

A hand closed over hers.

Warm. Impossibly warm, in water that could freeze marrow. Small for a grown man's hand, scarred across the knuckles where a ledger clasp had bitten him a thousand times, and branded across the palm with a sigil that now blazed like a sunrise seen through cloth.

Oscar had come down after her. Of course he had. She had told him to pull her up and instead he had dived, because obedience had never once taken root in him, and he pressed his marked hand flat against hers against the stone, and the covenant *recognized him*.

The pull reversed.

She felt herself snap back into her own shape like a sail catching wind — name, memories, edges, all of it slamming home so hard her teeth clicked and her nose filled with blood that bloomed pink in the water. The god behind the wall recoiled from the sigil with something she could only describe as recognition and horror in equal measure, a vast attention flinching away from a mark it knew, a mark it had *agreed to*, a hundred years ago, in another life.

Then the vision came, and it did not ask permission.

* * *

Seven figures stood in a circle on dry flagstones, in this crypt, when it was a crypt and not a throat. She knew their faces because they were hers to know: Tammas Oduya young and laughing, a ferry-token bright on his chest. A woman in armor whose descendant wore an inquisitor's coat now. An old man with a farmer's hands. Twins who shared one token between them. Herself, younger, certain, righteous in the way that only precedes catastrophe.

And in the center of the circle, the seventh presence — not standing, not shaped, but *poured*, a fullness of light and voice that the circle faced with love and fear in equal measure.

Veyra. Before the cage. Before the curdling. The god of thresholds and passages, the opener of ways, who had kept the borders between realms permeable and just — who had begun, gently, reasonably, with the best arguments in the world, to demand more. A tithe of years. A tithe of names. A tithe of children, given willingly, *surely willing counts for everything*. And the seven had looked at each other across the circle and agreed, silently, in the way of people who have run out of courage, that the answer was not to refuse her but to *shut her out*.

The vision showed her the moment of the breaking: six hands and hers pressing the anchor-stone into place while Veyra's voice went from argument to pleading to a sound Freya had spent a century not dreaming about. And the vision showed her the terms, written into the stone in the covenant's own blood-language, terms she had helped draft and had somehow never understood:

*Seven seals the circle. Six makes a wall. The seventh must consent, or nothing holds, and nothing opens.*

Six of them had sealed the stone. The seventh seat in the covenant had never been emptied. It had been *occupied* — by the prisoner. The covenant required Veyra's consent to stand complete, and Veyra would never consent, and so the covenant had stood incomplete for a hundred years, a wall instead of a circle, straining, cracking, failing — and every answered prayer leaking through the cracks was Veyra's breath against the glass.

The vision released her.

* * *

They lay on the bank in the grey afternoon light, both of them streaming, both of them coughing, her nose still bleeding freely onto the grass. Oscar had dragged her the last ten feet; she had no memory of it. Above them the broken bell tower stood against a sky going the color of pewter, and somewhere far off, thunder that might have been artillery.

"The seventh bearer," Oscar rasped, when he could speak. He was staring at his hand. The linen had burned away entirely; the sigil stood bare on his palm, raised and glistening, and it had *changed* — the seven-fold pattern now closed, complete, a circle where before there had been an open arc. "It's Veyra. The seventh is the god herself. The covenant can't be reforged without her consent."

"Yes."

"And she'll never consent. You heard what she—" He stopped. He had not heard what she screamed; he had only seen Freya's face afterward, all those years ago, and drawn his own conclusions. "You put her in there. All of you. And now you need her to agree to lock herself in forever."

"Not forever." Freya sat up, wrung the river from her hair, and found her hands were shaking. "That's the cruelty of it. The original terms were a circle — permeable, just, *consensual*. She passed between realms freely because we all consented to let her. What we built instead was a wall, and walls don't need consent, which is why cowards build them. But the covenant as written — the true covenant — needs all seven. Including her. Willingly."

"So we have to ask the god we betrayed for forgiveness."

"We have to ask the god we betrayed to *trust* us," Freya corrected quietly. "Forgiveness would be easier. Forgiveness is cheap. Trust has to be paid for, and I've seen the price list."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped off his chin. Down in the flooded nave, the anchor-stone's song had changed key — lower, slower, almost thoughtful, the way a wolf goes quiet when it catches a scent it hasn't smelled in a hundred years.

"It recognized me," he said at last. "Down there. When your hand was in the crack. It pulled at you and I put my hand on yours and it — Freya, it *flinched*. Like it knew the sigil." He turned his palm toward her, and the completed circle caught the dying light. "Why does a caged god know my hand?"

Because I wrote it, she thought. Because the night your parents died keeping mine, I did the only thing I could think of to keep you safe, and it was the worst and best thing I have ever done, and I have lied to you about it every day since.

What she said was: "We need Tammas. And the tokens. And we need to move tonight, because that song has changed key, and I've heard that key before." She got her feet under her, joints protesting, century-old joints. "It's the sound Veyra makes when she's decided something."

Oscar looked at the water, and the water looked back, black and patient, and far below it the stone sang its new note into the bones of the world.

"Decided what?" he asked.

Freya pulled him upright by the wrist — the marked one — and did not let go.

"That she's done waiting," she said.
