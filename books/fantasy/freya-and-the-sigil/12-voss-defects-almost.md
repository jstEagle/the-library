## 12. Voss Defects — Almost

The throne room of Aldous had once been a chapel.

Maren Voss understood the symbolism the way she understood most things: completely, and too late to matter. The vaulted ceiling still bore its faded frescoes — seven figures hand in hand around a circle of light, their faces worn to smudges by centuries of candle smoke. The king's masons had plastered over three of them and left the rest, as if a partial erasure were somehow more honest than a whole one. Now the light that fell through the eastern windows came stained glass-red, pooling on the flagstones like something spilled, and at the far end of the room, where an altar had stood, sat a machine.

She had been summoned at dawn. She had ridden through the night from Corvane with Serel's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart, and she had told herself, mile after mile, that she was bringing it to the king because that was what an inquisitor did. Evidence went up the chain. Judgment came down. Order was mercy; her order's motto, stitched into the collar of every censor's coat since before her grandmother's grandmother. She had believed it the way one believes in gravity — not as a creed but as a fact of the world, the thing that kept everything from drifting apart.

Now she stood forty feet from the throne-that-was-an-altar and watched King Aldous feed a prayer into his machine.

It was a small prayer. That was what made it obscene. A child's slip of vellum, the kind sold in temple markets for a copper, covered in a laborious childish hand: *please let my mother stop coughing.* Aldous held it between two fingers the way a physician holds a tongue depressor, and when he fed it into the brass throat of the engine, the machine drank it with a sound Voss felt in her teeth — a wet, satisfied sound, like a swallow after a long thirst. Somewhere in the engine's coiled interior, something glowed briefly gold. Somewhere in the world, presumably, a mother stopped coughing.

Or didn't. There was no way to know. The ledger said otherwise.

*Harvest yield per thousand slips: variable. Binding efficiency: 11 percent. Wastage acceptable.*

Wastage. Eleven prayers out of every hundred did nothing but die in the machinery, and the other eighty-nine were not answered so much as *spent* — torn open for the divinity coiled inside them, rendered down like tallow, poured into the engine that would let Aldous bind the gods themselves. Serel had known. Ten years of selling oath-tokens north, and the guildmaster had known exactly what he was feeding.

"Voss." Aldous did not turn around. He was a spare man, the usurper, narrow as a blade kept long in its sheath, and his voice had the particular softness of someone who has never needed to raise it because everyone else lowers theirs first. "You rode hard. I could see your dust from the tower."

"Your Majesty." She crossed the floor, boots ringing on stone that had heard ten generations of hymns, and stopped at the respectful distance. Protocol said kneel. Her knees said nothing at all. "I've brought you the guildmaster's ledger. Complete records of the Impossible Things trade going back eleven years. It names every smuggler, every relic, every—" she made herself say it evenly, "—every oath-token he sold you."

"I know what it contains." Still he did not turn. The engine hummed. Up close she could see what it was built around: a core of pale stone, veined with gold, that pulsed with a slow rhythm entirely unlike a heartbeat. Slower than a heartbeat. Patient as geology. An anchor-stone fragment — she recognized it now from the ledger's descriptions, and the recognition settled in her stomach like a swallowed stone of its own. "I asked for you, Voss. Not for the book. The book was always coming. You were the question."

"Your Majesty?"

"Now you may kneel," Aldous said mildly, "or stand. I find I don't care which. What I want is your answer." He turned then, and his eyes were the color of the light through the red glass, and Voss realized with a lurch that this man had rehearsed this conversation. Had wanted it. Had perhaps wanted *her*, in the cold administrative way kings want instruments. "Tell me what you learned in Corvane. Tell me what you believe. And be careful, Inquisitor, because I will know if you lie, and I have grown so very tired of being lied to by people who think they are protecting me."

* * *

She should have lied. Later, on the road, hunted, bleeding through her sleeve where a crossbow bolt had opened her shoulder, she would turn the moment over and over like a coin worn smooth, looking for the face on it where she'd gone wrong. If she had knelt and said *the gods are a plague, Your Majesty, burn them all*, she would still be wearing the white-and-grey of the Censorate instead of a dead smuggler's cloak. If she had said nothing at all — but no. Silence had never been available to her. It was the first thing they burned out of you in the inquisitors' academy, along with doubt. *Order is mercy,* they had chanted, eight years old in grey novices' coats, and Maren Voss had chanted loudest because her mother had died in the god-fever riots of her home city and order was the only thing that had ever come for her, afterward, in the form of soldiers who put out the fires and hanged the worst of the mob and gave the orphans bread.

They had given her bread and a doctrine and a purpose, and she had repaid them with thirty years of perfect service, and now she stood in a desecrated chapel and told the truth, because he had asked, and because some stubborn clerk-deep part of her still believed evidence belonged to whoever had the right to hear it.

"The ledger shows the prayers aren't being censored," she said. "They're being consumed. Your engine doesn't suppress the returning gods, sire. It eats their substance. And the oath-tokens Serel sold you—" she unslung the oilcloth bundle, unwrapped it, held out the ledger with both hands, the way one presents a sword hilt-first, "—are the keys to the old covenant. Seven bearers. Seven tokens. With all seven, whatever you're building stops being an engine and becomes a cage. Or a leash. I don't know which word frightens me more."

Aldous took the ledger. For a moment he only looked at it, thumb moving across the cover's stamped sigil — the Guild of Impossible Things, a closed eye above an open hand — and something moved behind his face that Voss, in thirty years of reading faces for a living, could not name. Not anger. Something almost like grief.

"You read it," he said. "All of it."

"All of it."

"And?" He looked up. "What did you feel, Maren? Truly. Not the doctrine. The feeling."

The use of her given name should have warned her. It did not, quite, because she was busy with the feeling itself, which rose in her now like floodwater finding every crack in a wall: the memory of page after page of entries that were not relics at all. *One answered prayer, minor: rain for a dying orchard. One answered prayer, major: a drowned boy returned breathing, condition unknown. One curse, self-inflicted, purchased by a woman who wished to forget her son's face.* The impossible things the guild trafficked had turned out, page after page, column after column, to be people. Desperate, ordinary, praying people, buying miracles at ruinous prices because the gods had started answering again and hope, it turned out, was the most addictive commodity in the free cities.

"I felt," Voss said slowly, "that we were told the gods were a plague. And a plague is something you survive. But this—" she nodded at the ledger, at the engine, at the pulsing stone in its brass cage, "—this isn't medicine, sire. This is farming. We're not curing the fever. We're milking it."

For a long moment the only sound was the engine's patient hum and, beneath it, faintly, from somewhere deep in the palace, the sound of the city waking — carts, bells, a hundred thousand small human noises that Aldous intended, she understood suddenly and completely, to keep making forever, in cages, on leashes, harvested.

Then the king smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen in thirty years of seeing terrible things, because it was warm.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly that. And now, Maren, the part where I offer you everything."

* * *

He led her past the engine, past the throne, to a side chamber that had been a vestry in the chapel's former life. Robe-hooks still lined one wall, empty. On a table in the center, on a cloth of white silk, lay six objects.

Tokens. She knew them instantly from the ledger's careful descriptions, and knowing them, she knew what she was looking at, and her mouth went dry. A river-smoothed stone pierced with an iron ring. A wooden comb with seven teeth, six of them broken. A bird skull no bigger than a thumbnail, silvered with age. A scrap of burial shroud embroidered with a name worn illegible. A key of green bronze, corroded as if fished from the sea. And a small clay lamp, unlit, its spout blackened by a century of old flame.

Six. The covenant had seven bearers.

"This one," Aldous said, and lifted the sixth token — and Voss's whole body went still, the trained stillness of a woman who has spent her life ensuring her body never tells anyone anything, because the sixth token was a plain iron signet ring, worn thin, bearing the stamp of a scales balanced over a sword.

Her family's mark. Her ancestor's mark. Justice Voss, the Justiciar of the old covenant, whose portrait hung in her mother's house and whose name Maren carried like a debt. The oath-bearer who had stood, according to every history the Censorate taught, on the *right* side — the side of law, of judgment, of the mortal half of the compact between realms.

"It was stolen," Aldous said, watching her. "Three generations ago, from your family's keeping, by agents of the old guilds. I recovered it. At considerable cost." He set it back on the silk with a gentleness that curdled her. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Inquisitor? Your blood signed the covenant. Your blood has a *seat* at it. When I reforge the compact — and I will reforge it, with or without the seventh token, though it will be cleaner with — there is a place in the new order for the house of Voss. Not a place below. A place beside."

He stepped closer. Red light from the chapel windows followed them both like a witness.

"You believe in order," he said softly. "I have read your reports for nine years. Every one of them precise, every one of them honest, every one of them written by a woman who genuinely loves the law. Do you know how rare that is? How precious? Everyone else I must manage, bribe, terrify. You I need only *show*. So look." He gestured, and beyond the doorway the engine hummed, and the anchor-stone pulsed, and somewhere in the free cities a child's prayer was being poured out like wine. "The old covenant failed, Maren. It failed because it depended on consent — on gods who could withdraw, on mortals who could defect, on oaths that required tending like gardens. Consent is chaos wearing a kind face. I am offering the world something better. A covenant of *compulsion*. Gods bound to answer. Prayers guaranteed. No more droughts that prayer cannot fix, no more plagues, no more mothers coughing while heaven looks away. Every suffering soul answered, forever, whether the gods like it or not." His eyes were bright. He meant every word. That was the horror of him; he meant every shining word. "You call it a cage. I call it the end of abandonment. Which of us, truly, is the merciful one?"

And there it was — the trap, sprung so gently she hadn't felt the bar close. Because the terrible thing, the thing she would carry the rest of her life, was that for one full breath, standing in the red light with her ancestor's ring gleaming on its silk, Maren Voss *wanted* it. Wanted the world he described. Wanted a heaven that could not look away. Wanted, God forgive her, the coughing mothers answered, all of them, always, by force if force was what it took.

One breath. Then she thought of the ledger's margins, where Serel had noted wastage percentages in a tidy accountant's hand, and she thought: *a covenant of compulsion does not ask the sufferers either.* Eighty-nine souls served, eleven souls spent, and no one consulted — not the gods, not the prayed-for, not the children whose slips of vellum went into the brass throat. Compulsion, offered as mercy, was just harvest with better lighting.

"No," Voss said.

Aldous tilted his head. "No to the place beside me, or no to the plan?"

"No to both." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Your Majesty — sire — I am going to say this once, as the last honest officer you have, and then I am going to run, and you are going to hunt me, and we both know how this ends eventually. So hear it. A covenant without consent isn't a covenant. It's a theft with a liturgy. And the seventh bearer — the one you're missing — will never consent to you. Whatever else happens, whatever it costs, that token will never be yours."

Something flickered across the king's face — not rage; calculation, cold and fast as an abacus — and Voss understood, in the same instant, two things. First, that he had expected refusal; had perhaps even engineered the whole audience to measure her, the way one measures a blade before deciding whether to use it or melt it down. Second, that the guards were not outside the door. They were inside it. They had been inside it since she entered, two of them, in the shadow of the vestry arch, and the door had closed behind her with the softness of a purse string drawing shut.

"A pity," Aldous said, and sounded, horribly, like he meant it. "Take her token-hand first. Gently. She'll want to write her confession."

* * *

Later — much later, miles downstream, hiding in the reeds off a barge channel with her shoulder soaked red and her ancestor's ring clenched in her fist — Voss would reconstruct the escape the way she reconstructed any crime scene: piece by piece, cause by cause, refusing the comfort of calling it miracle.

The truth was simpler and more damning. She escaped because she had trained the men sent to catch her. The younger guard, Pell, hesitated a half-second when she shouted his name and the academy command for *hold* — a half-second she spent driving her elbow into the older guard's throat and putting herself between the king and the table. She escaped because Aldous, for all his brilliance, still thought of inquisitors as instruments, and instruments do not grab burning lamps off vestry tables and hurl them into silk. She escaped because the fire bought her four seconds, and four seconds was enough to reach the window, and the window was enough because the vestry, thank every god living and caged, had been built for men who needed to flee angry congregations.

She dropped twelve feet into the palace herb garden, rolled, ran. The alarm bell began tolling before she cleared the wall. By the time she reached the river quarter, the white-and-grey coat was in a drainage ditch and she was wearing a dead smuggler's cloak bought from a fence who hadn't asked questions because she'd paid in inquisitor's scrip, which he would spend at his peril, and they had both known it, and there was a strange fellowship in that.

They hunted her properly. Of course they did. She had trained the hunters too. By dusk, checkpoints sealed the bridges; by midnight, her description was in every garrison house in the capital — *grey eyes, cropped hair, burn scar left forearm, consider armed* — and Voss crouched in the reeds with an arrow-graze weeping down her arm and understood that she had finally, at forty-six years of age, become exactly what she had spent her life catching.

It should have broken her. She examined herself for the breaking and found, instead, something that frightened her more: relief. Thirty years of carrying the doctrine like a stone in the chest, and the moment she set it down, she could breathe. *Justice outside the law,* her ancestor's ring seemed to say against her palm, warm as if the iron remembered being worn. *It's still justice. It has to be. Someone has to carry it when the law goes rotten.*

One problem remained, and it was the size of the world. Six tokens sat on silk in a king's vestry. She had the seventh — her own blood's token, pried from under the burning table with a fire-eaten sleeve, worth more now than the crown it had fled. But the covenant needed bearers, not baubles. The ledger had named names, and one name had surfaced again and again in Serel's columns, annotated in the guildmaster's tidy hand with a single recurring note:

*Sigil-bearer. Location unknown. Priority.*

Oscar Hartwell. Runaway apprentice. The man the gods could find.

And beneath that entry, in fresher ink, a line that had chilled her even in Corvane and chilled her worse now, alone in the dark with the search-lanterns sweeping the far bank:

*Sold to the Censorate. Delivery arranged. Payment pending.*

Serel had sold him already. Which meant the sigil-bearer was running blind, with no idea that the price on his head had been set months ago — and the only person alive who knew both where the hunt would strike next and which side she had chosen was floating in the reeds, bleeding, holding a dead woman's ring and a stolen ledger's worth of truth.

Voss bound her shoulder with strips of smuggler's cloak, found the current, and began to swim south, toward Corvane, toward the drowned quarter, toward the company of people she had spent her life hunting — carrying, like a thief in reverse, the one thing the king could not afford to lose.

Behind her, the search-lanterns multiplied along the banks like a constellation falling to earth.

Ahead, somewhere in the dark, the free cities waited to burn.
