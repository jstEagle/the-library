## 13. Three Tokens and a Funeral

The road to the Saltmarshes smelled of brine and old rain, and Oscar spent most of it pretending he wasn't afraid.

He had gotten good at that, over the years. Fear was a thing you could file, if you were disciplined about it — a column of figures in a ledger you never opened. He had kept his fear of Freya filed away for eleven years. His fear of Voss's inquisitors, who were even now combing the free cities for a sigil-bearer, he kept in a separate volume entirely. And his newest fear — the one that had arrived with Tammas Oduya's river god whispering through the drowned quarter of Corvane, *the seventh token sleeps with the weaver, go north, go north* — he was trying very hard to pretend was not there at all.

It wasn't working.

They traveled as pilgrims, which was the safest lie left in the world. Since Aldous's proclamation, the roads between the free cities ran thick with the devout: processions in gray wool, relic-carts under guard, whole villages walking barefoot toward whatever shrine had last produced a miracle. Nobody looked twice at four travelers heading north with their hoods up — a hedge-witch, a ferryman, an ex-inquisitor, and a bookkeeper.

Oscar still didn't understand how any of them had become a *we*.

Voss rode at the rear, her former comrades' colors stripped from her cloak, her hand never far from the stolen oath-token she carried wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. She had not yet earned trust — Tammas's river god had vouched for her, but gods vouched strangely, and everyone knew it — so she rode alone, and watched, and said little. Tammas walked beside the cart, humming to himself, an old man with rope-scarred hands and the patient look of someone who had spent forty years listening to a passenger no one else could see. And Freya drove the cart.

She sat at the front with the reins loose in her fingers and her face turned into the wind, and every mile north she seemed both more present and less real to him. Eleven years, and she hadn't aged the way people aged. There were new lines around her eyes — the Market of Answered Prayers had put some of them there, when she'd burned years of herself to pull him out of a mob — but underneath them she was still the woman from the hedge at the end of the road, and he still did not know what to do with that.

*You could have told me,* he wanted to say. *About the sigil. About what I am. You let me believe I was running away from an apprenticeship when I was carrying a covenant in my skin.*

But they had only just stopped shouting about it, three nights ago, somewhere south of the marsh causeway. There would be time. There was always time, until there wasn't.

"Saltmarsh ahead," Tammas called back, and his voice had that doubled quality it got sometimes, his own voice and something older underneath it, like two bells struck together. "The weaver's house is on the stilts past the tide-line. She'll know we're coming. She's known for forty years."

"How can she have known for forty years?" Voss asked, from behind. It was the first thing she'd said since dawn.

"Because," Tammas said, "she's the one who wove the cage."

---

Maren Oduya — no relation to Tammas, though the coincidence made the old ferryman laugh until he had to sit down — lived in a house on stilts above a tidal flat that changed its mind twice a day. She was ninety-one years old by her own account, though her hands, when she took them from her weaving, were the smooth brown hands of a woman of thirty. The rest of her had been allowed to grow old. Only her hands stayed young, because her hands were what remained of the oath.

She received them in a room that smelled of dye and lamp-oil, walls hung with half-finished tapestries: bridges, mostly. Bridges burning, bridges mending, bridges made of light strung between two shores that did not touch. Oscar recognized the burning one immediately. It was the bridge from his sigil-dreams, rendered in thread with a precision that made his stomach turn.

"You're the sigil-bearer," she said, before anyone spoke. Her eyes found him across the room and held him like a knot holds. "I dreamed your hand thirty years ago. I've been waiting for it to walk through my door ever since." She turned to Freya. "You waited too long, Vane."

"I know," Freya said.

"Weaver," Tammas began gently, "we've come to ask—"

"I know what you've come to ask." She rose, stiffly, an old woman's rise, and went to a loom in the corner where a single small object hung among the warp threads like a bead caught in a spider's web. A ring of woven silver, no bigger than a bracelet, dense with knots too fine to count. "The third token. Take it and go. I've done my part. I want to finish this last piece before—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "Before the tide comes in."

Oscar reached for the token, and Maren Oduya caught his wrist.

Her grip was astonishing. Old shoulders, young hands, and strength in them like mooring-rope. She turned his palm up and looked at the sigil branded there — the mark he had carried since he was nine years old and believed, until weeks ago, was nothing but ink and scar tissue from his apprenticeship.

"Yes," she murmured. "Yes, that's the binding-knot. That's my work, or my pattern's work." Her thumb traced the lines, and where it touched, the sigil warmed. "Do you know what you're carrying, boy?"

"A covenant oath," Oscar said. "Written in my blood. I know."

"Do you know what an oath-bearer *is*?" Her eyes came up to his. They were clouded with age, everything about her was clouded with age except those impossible hands, and yet they saw him more clearly than anyone had in eleven years. "It isn't a title. It isn't a job. It's a shape your life gets bent into whether you consented or not. I was a girl making nets on this flat when the seven came to me and asked me to hold a border. I said yes because saying yes to gods seemed safer than saying no. Forty years later I'm still holding it, and my hands won't age, and every tapestry I weave shows me the same burning bridge." She released his wrist. "So take the token. But understand what you're taking."

"I'm not taking anything," Oscar heard himself say. "I'm not an oath-bearer. I'm a bookkeeper. I balance ledgers. I—"

"You're the vessel of the seventh," Maren said, simply, terribly. "There is no version of the coming months in which you are a bookkeeper."

The room went quiet. Outside, the tide was turning; he could hear it, a long low exhale across the flats.

Freya moved as if to speak, and Maren raised one young hand, and Freya — Freya Vane, who had argued with kings' shadows and unmade curses and burned years of her own life without blinking — closed her mouth.

"The fourth bearer," Maren continued, addressing all of them now, "was a woman named Sefa Ilves, a glassblower out of the eastern cities. She died eleven years ago. Her token passed to her daughter, and her daughter to hers, and none of them knew what they carried — which was the point, once. Which was the *mercy*, once." Something moved across her face, regret or anger, hard to tell. "The granddaughter lives in the fen-town of Wrenlow. She sells charms and cheap glass beads to pilgrims. Go to her. Tell her the weaver sent you. Tell her—" and here the old woman's voice caught, frayed like a worn thread, "tell her I finished the piece. Tell her it's done."

"What piece?" Tammas asked softly.

Maren Oduya smiled, and it was the saddest smile Oscar had ever seen. "The shroud," she said. "Every weaver of the covenant weaves her own. It's how we go. Now get out of my house. The tide's coming in, and I don't weave for company."

---

Wrenlow was three days north, a fen-town built on pilings and peat, where the pilgrim traffic thinned and the air itself seemed to apologize for existing. They found the granddaughter's stall on the second morning: a crooked table of glass beads and wire charms, presided over by a woman perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, sun-brown, with her grandmother's hands.

Sefa Ilves's granddaughter was named Dagny, and she did not believe them.

"Grandmother Sefa's been dead since before I was born," she said, sorting beads with sharp, dismissive clicks. "And I don't know any weaver, and I don't sell to liars. Move along."

"She means Maren Oduya," Freya said. "Of the saltmarsh stilts. She says the piece is finished."

The clicks stopped.

Dagny looked up slowly. Whatever she saw in their faces — and Oscar supposed they looked like what they were: exhausted, hunted, desperate — made her set down the beads. "Come round the back," she said. "Not here. Half this town would sell you to the inquisitors for a blessed nail."

Behind the stall, in a hut that smelled of hot glass and peat smoke, Dagny took down a box from the rafters. Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a sphere of blown glass the size of a plum, and inside the glass — suspended, unaging, catching the lamplight like a captive dawn — swirled a curl of something that was not quite fire and not quite light.

"She left this to my mother with one instruction," Dagny said. "*Keep it whole, and keep it hidden, and when someone comes asking about oaths, give it freely and be glad.* My mother thought it was madness. I thought it was a family story." She looked up at Freya, then at Oscar, and her gaze snagged on his hand — on the sigil, which had begun, in the last day, to itch. "But you. You I've seen before."

"No," Oscar said. "I've never been to Wrenlow."

"Not in the flesh." Dagny's voice had gone strange and quiet. "In the glass. When I blow a piece, sometimes I see things in the gather. I've seen your hand since I was a girl. I've seen a bridge burning. I've seen—" she hesitated, and her eyes flicked to Freya, and something passed over her face that Oscar couldn't read, "—I've seen a lot of things. Take it. Grandmother Sefa said gladly, and I find I am. Glad, I mean. Like putting down something heavy I didn't know I was carrying."

Oscar took the glass sphere, and the moment his fingers closed around it, the sigil on his hand flared — not pain, exactly, but recognition, the way a lock recognizes a key — and inside the glass the swirl of not-fire flared in answer.

And then the sphere grew warm, and then hot, and then Dagny said, "Oh," in a small voice, and the glass began to sing.

It was not a sound so much as a memory of sound, a resonance that lived in the teeth and the breastbone. Through it, thin as thread, came a voice. An old voice, dry and warm, with the cadence of someone speaking from very far away and very long ago.

"*Is it time?*" the voice asked. "*Is the vessel come?*"

Everyone stared at Oscar.

"I don't—" he started.

"*Ah,*" said the voice in the glass, softening, settling, the way an old woman settles into a chair. "*There you are. I blew this the year the covenant broke, did you know that? Caught a breath of the border itself in the gather. It's been waiting for you a long while, child. We all have.*"

"Sefa," Freya breathed. "Sefa Ilves. You're alive? In there?"

"*A sliver,*" the voice agreed. "*The oath keeps what it loves. Listen now, there's little time and less breath. The tokens must be joined at the anchor-stone, and the stone will wake when five are gathered — five, do you hear me, not six, not seven. Five opens the door; the sixth turns the key; and the seventh—*" The voice frayed. The light in the glass guttered. "*The seventh must consent. And child — vessel — listen. When they offer you the oath, and they will, and soon — you may refuse. Do you understand me? Whatever they tell you, whatever the covenant claims, refusal is permitted. I refused nothing. I gave everything. Look what it bought: forty years in a bauble, watching my grandchildren's grandchildren sell beads.*"

"I don't understand," Oscar said. "Refuse what? What's being offered?"

But the voice was fading, stretching thin as blown sugar. "*Ask the weaver's shroud,*" it whispered. "*Ask what Maren wove. And when the choice comes — and it will come sooner than any of you wish — remember that a runaway is a man who has practiced leaving. Practice staying, instead. Stay, and—*"

The glass went dark. Not broken — whole, cool, ordinary, a plum-sized sphere of good clear glass with nothing in it at all. But the warmth of it sank into Oscar's palm, into the sigil, and spread up his arm like dawn coming into a cold room.

Dagny sat down hard on her stool. "Forty years," she whispered. "She's been in there forty years. My mother kept her grandmother on a shelf."

Tammas took off his hat. Voss, standing by the door with her arms crossed, uncrossed them slowly, and for the first time since Oscar had known her, the ex-inquisitor looked genuinely shaken.

Freya crossed the hut and put her hand over Oscar's closed fist, over the sphere, over the sigil. Her fingers were trembling.

"Five tokens," she said quietly. "We have yours, Tammas's, Voss's, Maren's, and now Sefa's. Five. The stone will wake."

"And the sixth?" Oscar asked. His voice sounded far away to himself.

Freya met his eyes, and he saw her decide — saw the calculation and the guilt and the love all war behind her face before she answered.

"The sixth token is mine," she said. "I've carried it since the night the covenant broke. Since the night your parents died."

* * *

They buried Maren Oduya the next evening, though there was nothing to bury.

Word had come by pilgrim-post, a folded scrap passed hand to hand up the road from the saltmarshes, written in a shaking hand by a neighbor: *the weaver died with the morning tide, sitting at her loom, the shroud finished on the frame and her hands folded in her lap, and when we lifted her the shroud was gone, and so were her hands' marks on the cloth, and the loom was empty as if no one had ever sat there.*

"She wove herself out," Tammas said. He stood at the edge of the fen with his hat in his hands, and beside him the air shimmered faintly, the way it did when his passenger pressed close. "That's the old way. The weavers don't leave bodies. They leave the work." He bowed his head. "My passenger says the border is one thread stronger tonight, for her going. Says she held her post to the last inch of the tide."

They had no body, so they buried the words. Freya spoke them — a hedge-witch's burial, salt and ash and a spoken name — and Voss stood at rigid attention as though the funeral were a military ceremony, which, Oscar reflected, in a way it was. Dagny came, closing her stall early, and stood with her big hands hanging at her sides, weeping for a woman she'd met once.

Oscar stood apart, as he had stood apart his whole life, and tried to feel what he was supposed to feel, and found instead that he was furious.

It crept up on him during Freya's words, rose through him during the salt and ash, and by the time the company turned back toward the fire and the cold supper waiting, it had him fully. He stood at the fen's edge after the others had gone, staring at the small mound of peat with its marker of twisted reeds, and the fury finally found its voice.

"Refusal is permitted," he said aloud, to no one. To the grave. To the glass sphere in his pack, which had grown warm again, as if listening. "That's what she said. Refusal is permitted. Then why does nobody tell us these things *until we're already carrying them?*"

"He's talking to a grave," Voss observed from behind him. She had come back without him noticing — she moved quietly for a big woman in soldier's boots. "In my experience that's either grief or madness, and you don't strike me as mad."

"Don't you have prayers to censor?"

"I resigned." She came to stand beside him, arms crossed, watching the dark water. For a while neither of them spoke. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of judgment, she said: "Inquisitors are taught that the worst heresy is the honest question asked in good faith. Because it can't be answered with doctrine. It just sits there, being true." She glanced at him. "Yours is an honest question. Why does no one tell us until we're already carrying it?"

"Because," Oscar said bitterly, "if they told us first, we might refuse. And they need us to carry it. That's the whole rotten architecture of it, isn't it? Freya wrote an oath in my blood when I was nine years old. Maren got conscripted as a girl making nets. Sefa's been trapped in a paperweight for forty years. Every one of them *chosen* without consent, every one of them told afterward, every one of them expected to smile and call it duty." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I ran away from an apprenticeship. One apprenticeship. And the whole time I was already enrolled in something I never signed up for, and the tuition is—"

He stopped. Because the sigil had begun to burn.

Not the warm ache of recognition, not the flare of a token touching it. This was the old burn, the dream-burn, the burn that meant the sigil was trying to show him something. He cried out and dropped to one knee at the fen's edge, clutching his hand, and the others came running, and the brand on his palm opened like a window.

He saw: the flooded chapel, black water rising up its nave. He saw the anchor-stone on its altar, and carved into the stone, in letters of slow-moving light, a word writing itself — letter by letter, deepening as it wrote, the way a brand deepens in hide.

*A-L-D-O-U-S.*

He saw, last of all, a figure standing waist-deep in the chapel water, robed in gray, head bowed — and the figure raised its head, and it had Serel's face, and Serel smiled at him across the distance of the vision with the serene expression of a man who has finally found the ledger entry he's been looking for.

Then the vision snapped shut, and Oscar was on his knees in the peat, gasping, and Freya was holding his hand, reading the afterimage of the letters still fading on his skin.

"Aldous knows where the stone is," she said. Her voice was terrible and calm, the voice she used for working. "Serel's shown him. Or Serel's shown the stone. Either way—" She looked up at the others, at the firelight, at the dark road north. "We're out of time. The stone wakes at five tokens. We have five. If we reach it before Aldous does, we choose how this ends. If he reaches it first—"

"He begins a false reforging," Voss said. "With harvested prayers. I've seen the engine plans. I defected over the engine plans."

"Then we march tonight," Tammas said simply, putting his hat back on. "The river's been telling me for days. *Go north, go north.*"

They broke camp in the dark, and Oscar stood a moment longer at the weaver's grave, flexing his burned hand, the glass sphere warm against his heart through his coat.

*Refusal is permitted,* Sefa had said. *When the choice comes, practice staying.*

"But staying to *what*?" he asked the grave, softly. "Nobody will even tell me that."

The peat gave no answer. But far to the north, past the fen and the black water and the burning cities, something ancient felt five tokens moving toward it — and, deep beneath the flooded chapel, began, with the slow patience of the caged, to wake.
