## 14. The God Who Demanded Too Much

Freya Vane had spent a hundred years learning how to be small, and she had gotten very good at it. She could make herself a smudge in a doorway, a rumor of a woman at a market stall, a hedge-witch whose name was spoken only in the past tense. Smallness was a craft, like any other, and she had practiced it the way other women practiced embroidery — daily, patiently, until the stitches disappeared into the cloth.

But there was no being small tonight.

She stood at the mouth of the descent with a lantern in one hand and nothing else, because Veyra's cage admitted no iron, no charm, no borrowed strength. Only what a person carried in their own body. Only what they were willing to spend.

The entrance was an old barrow-mouth behind the drowned chapel, half-swallowed by ivy and the roots of a beech that had been young when the covenant was whole. The stone around it was scarred with oath-script so old that the letters had begun to forget themselves, wearing down into mere suggestion, like names on gravestones left too long in the rain. Freya put her palm against it anyway. The stone was warm. That was the worst part. Stone should not be warm.

*You came back,* said the dark below her, in no voice at all — or rather in every voice she had ever refused to answer: her mother calling her in from the fields, Tammas Oduya laughing across the water, a young man named Oscar Hartwell saying *I'm not going with you, I have a life now.* The cage spoke in the voices you loved. It had learned, over a century, that this was how you made a person climb down.

"I came back," Freya said aloud, because silence was a courtesy she did not owe it.

*You always said you would.*

"I always say many things." She lifted the lantern and stepped through the barrow-mouth onto stairs worn hollow by feet that had stopped coming a hundred years ago. "Most of them lies."

*Yes,* the dark agreed, almost fondly. *That was always your gift.*

The stairs went down farther than the hill above them should have allowed. This was the first mercy of the cage's architecture — or its first cruelty; after a century Freya had stopped distinguishing — that the way down was long enough for a person to change their mind, and long enough to know they wouldn't. She counted the steps out of habit. Two hundred and eleven. When she had helped seal this place, she had counted four hundred and six. The stair was shortening. Things in cages grew toward their doors the way roots grow toward water.

At the bottom, the lantern found the chamber, and the chamber found her back.

It was not a dungeon. That had been the second argument, a hundred years ago, among the seven who stood in this room with their oaths still wet on their tongues. A god could not be kept in a dungeon; a dungeon implied crime, and crime implied judgment, and none of them had been willing to say aloud that what they intended was a judgment. So they had built a chapel instead. A beautiful one. Vaulted ceilings gone soft with centuries of condensation, pillars carved with vines and larks and the faces of the harvest, a floor of pale stone that still, impossibly, held the ghost of mosaic light. They had built a chapel and put a god in it, and told themselves the beauty was kindness.

Veyra hung above the altar dais where the mosaic sun used to be.

There was no other word for it. She did not sit; she did not stand. She was suspended in a slow turning knot of her own divinity, which had once been silver-gold and was now the color of a bruise healing wrong — deep violet shading into a green like pond-water, shot through with threads of black that moved when you looked away from them. Her form was still vaguely a woman's, if a woman were stretched on a wheel of her own light. Her face was the hardest thing to look at, because it was still beautiful, and because it was still, in some ruined way, familiar. Freya had broken bread with that face. Had sworn beside it. Had loved it, in the broad and terrible way one loves a thing that is larger than oneself.

"Hello, Veyra," Freya said.

The god's eyes opened. They had been closed. Freya would have sworn they had been closed.

*Little oath-breaker,* Veyra said, and now the voice was only her own — vast, layered, each word arriving slightly before or after the lips moved, as though sound itself had grown uncertain which of them it belonged to. *A hundred years and two months. You kept better count than I expected.*

"Fourteen days off," Freya said. "I lost track during the plague winter."

*During the plague winter,* Veyra repeated, tasting it. *You lost track. During the winter my faithful burned their prayer-slates to stay warm, because the prayers went nowhere. Because you had seen to it.* Something in the knot of light tightened, and the black threads writhed. *Tell me, Freya. Did you lose track of anything else? Your apprentice, perhaps. The boy with the sigil. Did you keep count of him?*

"You know about Oscar."

*I know everything that touches the covenant. He is touching it rather a lot lately.* The god laughed, and the laugh moved through the chapel like weather, rattling condensation from the vaults in a fine cold rain. *He balances ledgers now. For smugglers. Oh, that's lovely. The seventh bearer's vessel, keeping books for men who sell miracles by the ounce. You must be so proud.*

Freya set the lantern down on the lowest step, deliberately, so that her hands would have something to do besides shake. "You've been watching him."

*I've been watching the door of my cage. He is written all over it. His blood is the lock, little witch. You saw to that too.* The bruise-colored light dimmed, gathered, and when it brightened again Veyra's face had drifted closer, though her body had not moved at all. *So. You've come to negotiate. I can smell it on you. Guilt smells like wet wool. You've worn it for a century; I'd know it anywhere.*

"I've come to ask for your consent."

The chapel went very quiet. Even the dripping stopped, as if the water itself were listening.

*My consent,* Veyra said softly.

"The covenant broke because we took something from you without asking. It can only be reforged if you give it back freely. All seven bearers. All seven consents. Yours included." Freya made herself hold those ruined, beautiful eyes. "I am asking. A hundred years late, but asking."

For a long moment the god said nothing, and Freya understood that this was the most dangerous moment yet — more dangerous than the laughter, more dangerous than the watching. Because Veyra had been alone in the beautiful chapel for a hundred years and two months, and a caged thing that stops speaking has either died inside or grown teeth.

*Ask me again,* Veyra said at last. *Properly. Kneel.*

"No."

*Kneel, and ask, and I will consider it the beginning of an apology rather than the insult it currently resembles.*

"I didn't come here to grovel. I came here to make things right, and making things right doesn't require me to be smaller than I am. That was the whole mistake, Veyra. Ours and yours both. Someone demanded someone else be smaller." Freya's voice cracked on the last of it, and she hated that, and let it crack anyway. "I won't kneel. But I will listen. To anything you actually want."

The god was silent again. Then, slowly, the knot of light began to unwind — not releasing her, merely rearranging, until Veyra's feet nearly touched the dais and her face hung level with Freya's own, close enough that Freya could see that the black threads ran under the god's skin like veins, like ink spilled through water.

*What I want,* Veyra said, *is to walk out of here.*

"That isn't mine to give. Not alone. The reforging—"

*Is a story you tell yourselves so you don't have to hear the simpler request.* The god's voice dropped, and it was suddenly, horribly intimate — the voice of a friend in a dark kitchen, a hundred years ago, passing the bread. *Freya. I asked you once for a tenth of the harvest. One field in ten, burned for me, and I would have kept the borders kind and the winters short and the children fed. You called that demanding too much. And then you and yours took a hundred percent of me, and sealed it in a pretty room, and called it mercy.*

"We were afraid."

*Of course you were. Fear is honest. I liked you better when you admitted it.* A thread of black crept along Veyra's jaw like a tear that had learned new roads. *Here is what I want, then. Since listening is on offer. I will consent to the reforging. I will stand at the anchor-stone with the rest of your precious seven, and I will let the borders be drawn kindly, and I will even forgive you — not forget, forgive — and I will do all of it gladly.*

"And in exchange?"

*Your remaining years. All of them. Every hour the hedge-work and the hiding have left you — I felt them just now, standing this close, a thin gray ribbon of them, maybe thirty winters' worth, and you'd have spent them anyway on that working in the market, saving the boy from the mob. You spend yourself on him constantly. It's almost admirable.* The god smiled, and the smile was the old smile, the one from the covenant-night, and that was the cruelest thing yet. *Give me the ribbon. And give me the sigil.*

The word landed in Freya's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"No," she said.

*You haven't heard the terms.*

"I heard the terms. My years I might have considered — they're mine, and I've been spending them in installments for decades, and what's left of a life matters less than what it buys." Freya picked the lantern back up. Her hand was steady now, which surprised her; terror often arrived late with her, like a guest who missed the fight. "But the sigil isn't mine to sell. It's Oscar's. It's his blood, his name, his inheritance — the one thing his parents bought him with their lives, and the one thing I never asked his leave to write. I have taken enough from people without asking. I won't add him to the list."

*He'd give it,* Veyra said, silk-soft. *You know he would. He's been carrying it like a debt since he was nine years old. Ask him. Watch his face. He'd hand it over to end this, and thank you for the opportunity, and go back to his ledgers with an empty hand and call it peace.*

"That's exactly why I won't ask him. A person who's been taught he's a debt will pay any price to stop owing." Freya backed up one step, then another, keeping her eyes on the god. "You've been in here a hundred years learning how to argue, Veyra. You argue beautifully. But I notice you're not denying what you are anymore."

*And what am I?*

"A god who demanded a tenth," Freya said quietly, "and got a hundred, and became the thing you swore you'd never be. We caged you, and it was monstrous, and I will carry it to my grave. But somewhere in the last century, the cage stopped being the worst thing in this room."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water.

Then Veyra screamed.

Not with sound — with light. The bruise-violet blaze went white, then black, then a color Freya had no name for, and the chapel's beautiful vaults split along seams no eye could find, raining stone dust and a century of condensation. The mosaic sun beneath the dais flared as though remembering itself. The stair behind Freya lengthened — she felt it, felt the geometry turn against her, two hundred and eleven steps becoming four hundred and six becoming a number with no end, the cage deciding that its door was not done with her.

*You will bring me the sigil,* the god said, in every voice at once now, in Tammas's voice, in Oscar's, in her own. *Or I will tear the covenant open myself, and I will not draw the borders kindly, and when the free cities burn — and they will burn, little witch, they are already burning — every soul that dies screaming will die knowing your name. You had a chance to buy mercy. Mercy must be paid for. That was always the lesson. That was always the lesson you refused to learn.*

Freya ran.

She was ninety years old in a body that lied about it, and she ran up a stair that was unmaking itself beneath her, and the lantern died in her hand, and she ran in the dark by the light of her own spent years, the gray ribbon of them streaming off her like smoke as the cage pulled at everything she had. At step three hundred she stumbled and skinned her palms on the oath-script and felt the letters bite back, hungry, recognizing her. At step three hundred and fifty she heard, below and behind, the sound of the chapel's beauty finally giving out — the vaults coming down, the pillars folding, the god rearranging her prison into something with more room to grow.

She came out of the barrow-mouth into night air and lay on the ivy gasping like a landed fish, and above her the beech tree's leaves shivered though there was no wind, and beneath her, far beneath, she felt the cage flex — not breaking, not yet, but no longer holding the shape they had given it. Holding the shape Veyra preferred.

Oscar was waiting at the treeline. Of course he was; she had told him to wait, and he had waited, and he was holding the ledger against his chest like a shield, and his face in the moonlight was the face of a man about to ask a question he already dreaded.

"Freya." He crossed the grass quickly, catching her elbow as she rose. "You're bleeding. What happened down there? What did she say?"

Freya looked at him — at the sigil-scarred hand gripping her arm, at the ledger, at the whole improbable stubborn fact of him — and she thought: *he'd give it to her. He'd hand it over and thank her.* And she thought: *I have perhaps thirty winters left, and I just showed a god exactly where I keep them.*

"She said yes," Freya lied, straightening, wiping her palms on her cloak. "She'll consent to the reforging. We need the tokens, all of them, and we need to move faster than we planned. Aldous's army is ten days out, and Serel knows where the anchor-stone is."

Oscar frowned. "Then why are you shaking?"

Because the cage had learned her voice, she did not say. Because it had offered to buy mercy with his blood and hers, and when she refused, it had shown her the price of refusal, and the price was a city on fire with her name in its mouth. Because a god who demanded a tenth had become a god who demanded everything, and Freya had looked into the heart of power's corruption and recognized, with sickening clarity, the same hunger she had once carried herself, the night she and six others had decided they knew best.

"Old stairs," she said instead. "Come on. We need Tammas, and we need the ferry running before dawn."

She turned toward the road, and behind them the beech tree dropped a single branch across the barrow-mouth — not blocking it, she noticed. Sealing it shut from the outside. As if the hill itself had decided which of them belonged in the cage.

And far below, patient as rot, the god began to practice saying a new name.

*Oscar. Oscar. Oscar.*
