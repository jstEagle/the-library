## 15. The Free Cities Burn

Maren Voss had burned her first heretic at nineteen and had not slept properly since.

She told herself it was the road. Fifteen days of hard riding from the capital, through country that had stopped pretending to be loyal — hedgerows stripped for barricades, shrines to the old gods springing up in ditches like mushrooms after rain, little clay figures with no faces that the peasants pressed their thumbs into when they thought no one was watching. She told herself the sleeplessness was the saddle, the cold, the rations. It was easier than telling herself the truth.

The truth was that she had read the ledger.

Three nights ago, by candlelight, in a tent that smelled of tallow and wet wool, she had turned the pages of the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts and found that the impossible things were mostly grief. A widow's payment of two coppers for one hour of her dead husband's voice. A farmer's entire harvest-price for rain in the right month. A child's shoe, offered as pledge, for a mother's fever to break. Entry after entry in a small, precise hand — Oscar Hartwell's hand, though she had not known the name then — cataloguing desperation the way other men catalogued grain.

And beneath the accounts, in Serel's blunter script, the correspondence. The shipments south. The tokens. The prices Aldous paid for things that could not be priced, and what he intended to do once he had enough of them.

*Not censor,* the ledger had said, in the language of ledgers, which is the language of truth whether its keepers like it or not. *Bind.*

She had sat with that word for a long time. Around her the camp slept, four hundred soldiers of the Censor's Third Column, men who believed — because she had told them so, because she had believed it herself — that they rode to quarantine a plague of gods. And Voss had held the ledger over the candle flame, and had held it there, and had held it there, until her hand shook, and then she had folded it closed and put it inside her coat instead.

That was the night Maren Voss stopped being the King's inquisitor. She simply had not yet told anyone, least of all herself.

Now the outer free cities were burning on the horizon ahead of her, and the smoke told her she was too late to stop the first of it, and the column behind her rode at her pace without knowing they followed a traitor.

* * *

Vessik burned first. Then Marrow's Ford. Then the river-town of Callowmere, which had surrendered, which had opened its gates and laid its petition-scrolls on the ground before the army like flowers before a bull, and burned anyway.

Voss watched it from the ridge above the ford, and what she watched was not a sack. Sacks were chaos; she had seen sacks. This was procedure.

Aldous's engineers had built the harvesting columns in the capital's forges — tall iron frames on wagon-beds, ringed with hollow sigil-plates, each plate etched with a prayer-catchment lattice that Voss recognized from her training as a censor's tool scaled up a hundredfold. They rolled the columns into the surrendered squares of Callowmere while the town elders stood waiting with their scrolls, and the priests of the returning gods stood with them, arms raised, calling down blessings on the King's mercy.

Then the soldiers began to cut them.

Not to kill — that came later, and quickly, almost as an afterthought. First they cut: shallow, precise wounds, forearms and palms, the old covenant places, and caught the blood in the catchment-lattices while the harvested screamed prayers that were half agony and half appeal to gods who, Voss understood now, could actually hear them. The plates drank it in. She saw one glow amber, then white, and an engineer call out a number to a tally-master, and the tally-master write it down.

They were farming prayers at swordpoint. They were making the faithful bleed devotion into iron, and the iron was going south, wagon after wagon after wagon, toward whatever engine Aldous was building in the deep vaults under his usurped throne.

An officer near her — young, earnest, a boy named Pell who still wrote letters home — said, "Inquisitor, the elders surrendered. Is this— is the harvest sanctioned? I understood we were to suppress unauthorized worship."

"It's sanctioned," Voss said. Her voice did not shake. She had trained it never to shake. "From the Crown itself."

Pell looked relieved. That was the worst part. He looked relieved, and grateful, and he went back to his men, and Voss stood on the ridge with the ledger against her ribs like a second heart and understood exactly how a kingdom convinces itself to do anything: someone kind stands at the front and says *this is order*, and everyone behind breathes easier.

Order is mercy. She had believed it since she was nine years old, since the feuds and the god-tithes and the burning seasons of her childhood, since the day an inquisitor had pulled her family's cottage out of a mob's path with nothing but a warrant and a calm voice. Order was mercy. She had built her whole self on that stone.

And now the stone said *bind*, and the smoke of Callowmere rose straight and black into a sky where, if you looked long enough, you could almost see the old constellations shifting, restless, like birds off water.

There was one more thing in the ledger. A name, circled in Serel's hand, with a price beside it that made the widow's coppers look like dust: *the sigil-bearer.* And beneath it, in Aldous's own seal-red ink, a directive she had copied out and burned the paper of and still knew by heart:

*Bring me the vessel intact. The seventh token lives in his blood. Without it the engine turns empty.*

Voss turned her horse south, away from the smoke, away from the column, and did not look back at Pell or any of them. Somewhere ahead, hunted by the same machinery she had served, was a runaway clerk and a hedge-witch and whatever remained of the covenant that had kept the world permeable and just.

She had stolen one oath-token from Aldous's own vault — her ancestor's, the Voss token, the reason her family had produced inquisitors for six generations without ever being told why. It weighed less than a coin. It weighed more than the ridge, more than the burning town, more than every warrant she had ever signed.

She rode all night.

* * *

She found them by accident, or by the river's arrangement, which amounted to the same thing.

The drowned quarter of Corvane lay a day's ride east, streets knee-deep in green water where the river had been allowed — or had chosen — to reclaim them. Voss came in on foot, leading her horse, because horses died in the drowned quarter; everyone knew that. The water was warm and smelled of reeds and old stone, and lanterns hung from drowned doorways lit with a light that did not flicker, and she passed a woman washing clothes in water that washed back, gently, folding the fabric clean without hands.

They were at a chapel — of course they were; everything in this affair bent toward chapels — a roofless thing rising out of the flood, its bell-tower leaning like a drunkard. Four figures on the chapel steps. An old woman with grey-streaked hair and eyes like struck flint, whom Voss knew from thirty wanted-sketches: Freya Vane, the hedge-witch, the covenant-breaker, the beginning of everything. A lean man beside her with a brand on his bare left hand that even across the flooded square made Voss's stolen token grow cold against her skin — the sigil-bearer himself, Oscar Hartwell, looking less like a fugitive than like a clerk interrupted at his sums. A broad-shouldered ferryman with a pole across his knees. And a fourth figure, hooded, whose outline refused to settle, as though her eye kept sliding off it the way water slides off oiled cloth.

"Stop there," the witch said, when Voss was forty paces out. The water around Voss's boots went still. All of it, every puddle and channel in sight, went still at once, like a held breath. "You're the one who burned Marrow's Ford."

"I was there," Voss said. "I didn't burn it. I couldn't have stopped it either. Both facts are true."

"The last inquisitor who told me both-facts-true hanged eleven hedge-folk in Corvane." Freya Vane had not raised her voice, and yet it carried across the water the way a blade carries. "Why are you here?"

"Because I read your guild's ledger." Voss opened her coat slowly — she knew how movements read to people expecting violence — and drew out the ledger and the token together, holding them up. "Because your clerk's accounting taught me what my king is building. Because I stole this from his vault, and I'd rather it be in the hands of people trying to hold the world together than the man welding it shut."

The sigil-bearer stepped forward, and Voss watched him look at the ledger the way a man looks at his own grave. "That's mine," he said. "Serel sold it?"

"Serel sold you, the ledger, and the location of every oath-token the guild ever fenced. The King has had most of them for a decade." She let that land. It landed; she saw it in the way the clerk's jaw set, in the witch's flint eyes going narrow. "I'm giving you the one he doesn't have. The Voss token. My family kept it six generations without knowing what it was. Apparently we were collateral."

"And why," the ferryman said, speaking for the first time, "should we believe a word of it?" His voice was river-deep and river-calm, but underneath it Voss heard something older, something that made the still water shiver. "Inquisitors don't defect. They're sent. This is how they hunt — they make themselves the wounded bird."

"I've been sent to take him alive," Voss said, nodding at Hartwell. "That part's true too. I'm declining the assignment."

"Declining," the witch repeated softly. "How tidy."

"You want proof." Voss knelt, deliberately, in the flooded street, and set the ledger and the token on the water's surface. Neither sank. She hadn't expected them to; she had begun, in the last weeks, to understand that the world was full of surfaces that decided things. "I can't give you proof. I can only give you what I have and stand here while you decide. But ask your passenger. Ask the one who's ridden your ferry for forty years. Rivers remember everyone who's ever crossed them, and I crossed yours when I was twelve, going to my mother's funeral, and I prayed to a god everyone said was dead, and something answered. Ask it if I'm lying now."

Silence. The hooded figure turned its head — or the suggestion of a head — toward the ferryman.

Tammas Oduya looked at Voss for a long moment. Then he closed his eyes, and laid two fingers on the surface of the flood, and spoke quietly, in the tone of a man resuming a conversation decades long: *"Old one. The woman in the grey coat. You know her?"*

The answer did not come as sound. It came as the water. Every flooded street in the drowned quarter moved at once — a slow, vast exhalation, current running where no wind blew, and the lantern-light rippled gold, and Voss felt the river pass over her like a hand reading braille, patient and total, taking her measure from the soles of her boots to the top of her skull. It found the ledger's truth in her. It found the token. It found, she suspected, everything else — the burned heretic at nineteen, the sleepless nights, the boy Pell writing letters home, the stone called order cracking down its middle.

When it withdrew, she was shaking. She did not try to hide it.

"She crossed my water as a child," Tammas said, translating a whisper only he could hear, and his voice had gone gentle, "and it heard her, and it has waited a long time to be able to say so. It says: *she is cracked, not broken. Cracked things let the light through. Take what she carries.*"

Freya Vane studied Voss a moment longer — one professional appraising another, one keeper of secrets weighing a bag of them — and then, abruptly, the hostility went out of her posture like water out of a sluice, leaving something tireder behind.

"Well," the witch said. "Come up out of the water, Inquisitor, before the river decides it likes you better than we do. And start talking. From the beginning. Leave nothing out — I'll know."

Voss waded forward, retrieved her offerings from the unbroken surface of the flood, and climbed the chapel steps into the lamplight of the enemy she had spent her life hunting.

* * *

They talked until the false lanterns guttered toward dawn.

She gave them everything: the harvesting columns and their numbers, the tally-masters' counts, the route of the prayer-wagons south. She gave them Aldous's dispositions — the Third Column swinging west to close the mountain passes, the vanguard under General Ives already three days from the last free city, the inquisitor-companies ordered to take the sigil-bearer *intact*. She gave them the vault inventory, and watched Hartwell go grey as she listed the oath-tokens in the King's possession: five. Five of seven, and the sixth standing on the chapel steps in her own coat pocket, which meant—

"Which means the only token Aldous lacks," Freya said slowly, "is the one that was never a token at all." Her eyes went to Hartwell's branded hand. The clerk looked down at it as if seeing it for the first time.

"And the anchor-stone," Voss continued. "Serel sold its location weeks ago. The flooded chapel outside Corvane — the true one, not this quarter. Aldous means to begin the binding there, at the covenant's own root, using the harvested prayers as mortar. Ives reaches it within the week."

"Then we have less than a week," Hartwell said, "to find a way to reach a stone guarded by an army, reforge a covenant missing five tokens, and persuade a god who hates us all to consent to its own cage being rebuilt differently." He laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "I used to worry about quarterly audits."

Voss should have felt triumph, or relief, or at least rest. Instead she felt the particular vertigo of a woman who has jumped and not yet landed. These people owed her nothing. The witch had lost friends to warrants with Voss's own seal-shape on them. Every hour she sat among them was an hour the Third Column rode unopposed, following orders she had abandoned — and if they turned her away, she had nowhere in the world to go.

It was Tammas Oduya who settled it, or rather the whispering passenger settled it through him. The ferryman listened a long moment to something below hearing, then nodded, and looked around the circle of faces.

"The river says she rides with us," he said. "It says: *the censor who unmakes the censoring is worth ten of the faithful.*" A pause, and the ghost of a smile. "It also says she snores. Forty years, and it has never once lied to me about anything."

Freya Vane snorted. Even Hartwell's mouth twitched. And just like that — not friendship, nothing so warm, but a place in the boat — Maren Voss was no longer alone.

She was reaching for the token to hand it over formally when the water spoke again.

All of it. Not the gentle reading-touch of before but a single vast convulsion, every flooded street surging at once, lanterns swinging on their drowned hooks, and Tammas staggered upright with both hands pressed flat to the flood and his face gone the color of ash.

"What is it?" Freya was on her feet. "What's wrong?"

"It's the wagons," Tammas whispered. "The prayer-wagons. Three hundred of them, crossing the fords tonight, all bleeding at once into the iron." His voice cracked. "The river can feel them, Vane. Every prayer they took at swordpoint is passing over its waters right now, screaming. And it says—" he swallowed— "it says the iron isn't going to the vaults anymore. It's turning. All of it. Toward the anchor-stone. Aldous isn't waiting for the week."

The witch and the clerk exchanged a look that carried whole chapters between them.

"How long?" Freya asked.

Tammas listened. The flood shuddered around his knees like a living thing in pain.

"Two days," he said. "We have two days."
