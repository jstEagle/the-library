## 16. The Sigil's Name

The camp was a hollow in the hills above the drowned quarter, where the pines grew thick enough to swallow the light of Corvane's burning markets. Oscar had claimed the smallest fire, the one that threw its glow no farther than his own knees, and he sat hunched over the guild ledger with Tammas's river god whispering somewhere behind him — a sound like water over stones, like someone trying to remember a song.

He had not slept in two days. Sleep had become a country he could not afford to visit.

The ledger lay open across his thighs, and by firelight he read it again — not as an accountant this time, not as the man who had spent six years balancing impossible columns for Serel's smuggled miracles, but as something else. Something he did not yet have a name for.

Because there were names in it now. Names that had not been there before.

* * *

It had started three nights ago, in the aftermath of the funeral, when the dead oath-bearer's token still hung warm against his chest. He had opened the ledger to record their losses — a habit so deep it was practically a heartbeat — and found that the columns would not stay still. Figures crawled. Entries rearranged themselves. And in the margin of page one, in ink that had certainly not been there when Serel's men sold him out, a single line had appeared:

*What is bound in blood is written in blood.*

He had thought it a trick of exhaustion. Then the second line came. Then the third. The ledger, which had catalogued every relic and curse and small miracle the Guild of Impossible Things ever moved, was writing itself a new book inside the old one — and the new book was about him.

Tonight, finally, he had stopped being afraid of it.

He turned to the earliest pages, the ones Serel's predecessor had written in a cramped, careful hand, and held them close to the fire. And there, bleeding up through the accounting like water through plaster, the hidden text emerged.

*Seven bearers. Seven tokens. One covenant.*

*The hedge-witch Freya Vane, who broke what she helped make.*

*The ferryman Tammas Oduya, who carries what cannot be carried.*

*And the seventh, whose token is not a thing but a person —*

Oscar's breath stopped.

*— whose token is a sigil, and whose sigil is a name.*

His hand was shaking when he raised it into the light. The brand on his palm — the mark he had carried since he was nine years old, the mark he had believed was nothing more than an apprenticeship seal, a witch's signature on a contract of servitude — caught the firelight and *answered* it. The lines of the sigil moved. Not flickering. Moving. Tracing themselves deeper, the way a river cuts a canyon, the way a truth cuts a life in half: *before* and *after*.

"No," he said aloud. The word came out cracked. "No, that's not — I was an apprentice. That's all I was. She took me in, she taught me —"

But even as he said it, memory was already betraying him, already rearranging itself around the new truth the way furniture rearranges around a hole in the floor. He remembered the night of the branding differently now. He had always remembered it as a formality — Freya pressing a heated iron sigil-stamp to his palm, saying the words of indenture, the whole thing over in a minute, the smell of his own scorching skin, her face carefully blank.

But that wasn't the whole memory. There had been blood first. His blood — he'd cut his palm on broken glass that same night, running through the hedgerow, running from the soldiers' torches, from the screaming. He remembered her catching his wrist, looking at the cut, and going very, very still. Remembered her saying, softly, in a voice he had never heard from her before or since:

*"So it's you."*

He had thought she meant: *so you're the orphan from the burning farm.* 

She had meant: *so you're the seventh.*

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to confront her. He simply found himself on his feet, ledger under his arm, stalking through the dark camp toward the far fire where Freya sat awake — she was always awake; he suspected now that sleep had been one more thing she'd traded away, year by year, working by working.

She looked up at his approach and knew. He saw it land in her face before he said a word — saw her recognize the ledger under his arm, the look on his own, and go pale in the way of someone who has been waiting decades for a blow.

"You've read it," she said.

"Read *what?*" His voice came out louder than he intended. Somewhere in the dark, Tammas stirred but did not intervene. "Read what, Freya? Because from where I'm sitting, I've just learned that the only thing I've been sure of my entire adult life — that I was a runaway apprentice, that the mark on my hand was the price of my keep — is a lie. So yes. I've read it. Now you're going to explain it."

Freya set down the stick she had been feeding the fire. For a long moment she only looked at him, and he realized with a fresh surge of fury that she was deciding how much of the truth to give him — still weighing, still rationing, after everything.

"Don't," he warned. "Don't you dare decide for me what I can carry. You've done that my whole life."

That landed. He watched it land. Her shoulders dropped half an inch, and when she spoke, her voice had none of its usual dry armor.

"Sit down, Oscar."

"I'll stand."

"All right." She folded her hands. "Then stand. Your parents weren't strangers who died in a border raid. They were oath-bearers' kin — your mother was sworn to the covenant's outer circle, a keeper of records, like you. Like you," she repeated, and something almost broke in her voice. "You balance ledgers because she balanced ledgers. It's in the blood, and I mean that literally."

The fire popped. He made himself breathe.

"The night they died, the cage was failing for the first time. Veyra's agents — her faithful, what was left of them — came for me. I was the one who'd driven the final nail, and they wanted the nail back. Your parents stood between them and me. They bought me the hour I needed to seal the failing seam." Her eyes went to his hand. "But a sealed seam needs an anchor. A living anchor. Blood of the blood of a keeper, freely given — or given without knowing, which is the only mercy I could manage. I wrote the covenant's seventh oath into your palm with your own blood while you slept off the fever of your burns. The iron was theater. The blood was the oath."

"You *branded* a child."

"I *saved* a child," she shot back, and for a moment the old Freya blazed through — the woman who had argued down gods. "The alternative was that Veyra's faithful finished the job and took you too, because you'd seen my face, because your parents' blood was on your hands, because you were *there*. The sigil hid you. It made you unreadable — to them, to inquisitors, to every power that hunts by name and lineage. It kept you invisible for twenty years. Don't tell me you didn't love your invisibility. I built it for you."

"And never told me. Never once, in all those years —"

"What was I supposed to tell you?" She was on her feet now, and the years fell away from her; he saw the woman who had caged a god. "That the boy I'd orphaned was also the key to the prison I built? That every prayer answered wrong, every miracle gone lethal, might be traced back to a mark I put on his hand? That if Aldous ever learned what he had, I'd have delivered the world into a tyrant's fist by way of my own guilt?" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I told myself I was protecting you. That's what we all told ourselves, Oscar. That's how the covenant broke in the first place. Good people, protecting everyone but the truth."

The rage in him crested — and then, horribly, began to recede, because he could see it now from the other side. He could see the nine-year-old asleep by the fire, and the exhausted witch with a hot iron in one hand and a dying covenant in the other, choosing the only door left open. He could see twenty years of silence as a debt she'd paid in a currency he hadn't known existed.

He sat down. Not because she'd asked. Because his legs gave out.

"Why me?" he asked finally. "There must have been other keepers' children. Why me?"

Freya lowered herself slowly onto her log. When she answered, her voice was very quiet.

"Because your mother wrote the original covenant's ledger. Every oath, every token, every bearer — recorded in her hand. When she died saving mine, the covenant's memory passed to her blood. The seventh bearer isn't the strongest, or the bravest. The seventh bearer is the one who *remembers*. Who keeps the accounts." She met his eyes. "You were always going to be a ledger-keeper, Oscar. The only question was whose."

Behind them, faint as breath, the river god whispered — and Oscar, holding his branded hand open to the fire, understood at last why the gods had always been able to find him. Not despite the sigil.

Through it.

Far to the south, beyond the hills, the sky over the last free city glowed a dull, harvest-orange — Aldous's armies, gathering prayers at swordpoint. And on his palm, unbidden, the sigil began to burn, tracing letters of heat he could not stop reading:

*The seventh vessel comes. Bring the ledger. Bring the name.*

Veyra knew he was coming. And she was glad.
