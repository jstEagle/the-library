## 17. March of the Censor

The vanguard came down from the hills at dusk, and the last free city of Vellamare saw it the way a drowning man sees the surface of the water — close, and bright, and not for him.

From the walls, the army looked almost gentle. Ten thousand torches strung along the ridge road like festival lanterns, banners of the crowned quill snapping in the sea wind, the great god-binding engine hauled on forty wagons behind the ranks, its brass throat gleaming. King Aldous rode at the head of his Censors in white and grey, and he did not look up at the walls. He looked at the city the way a clerk looks at a page that has been written on by someone else — with the patient displeasure of a man preparing to correct it.

In the high tower of the Citadel of Bells, Aldous stood at the window while his officers argued below him about siege lines and grain stores, and he listened to none of it. He was listening to the city itself.

Vellamare was praying. It had been praying for months now, ever since the first answered prayer had come out of Corvane in a crate still warm, and the fervor had spread like spilled wine through cloth. Every rooftop held someone on their knees. Every shrine — and there were hundreds, old ones dug out of cellars, new ones chalked on doorposts — smoked with incense and desperation. The prayers rose off the city like heat off a summer road, and Aldous could *taste* them. That was the engine's gift, or the stolen token's gift; he no longer troubled himself to distinguish. A thousand voices saying *please*, and every one of them a drop of power waiting to be harvested.

"Your Majesty." Inquisitor-Captain Dain, at the door, helm under his arm. "The outer quarters are evacuating toward the harbor. Shall we—"

"Let them run to the water," Aldous said mildly. "Prayers travel over water beautifully. Seal the land gates at midnight. I want the harvest complete before dawn."

Dain hesitated. "And the defenders, sire? The guild remnant, the oath-folk—"

"The oath-folk," Aldous said, and smiled without warmth, "will be elsewhere tonight. My other guest is seeing to that."

He turned back to the window. Below, in the gathering dark, ten thousand men began to sing the Censor's hymn, low and disciplined, and the sound rolled against the walls like a tide against a sandcastle.

*One night,* Aldous thought. *One night, and the gods will have a master.*

---

Serel had not slept in four days, and did not need to.

That was the first thing the corruption had given him — sleeplessness without cost, hunger without appetite, a body running clean and cold as oiled machinery. The second thing it had given him was certainty, which was far more valuable. For thirty years Serel had trafficked in impossible things while privately believing in none of them, a merchant of miracles who kept his faith in ledgers because ledgers never lied. Now he believed in something larger than ledgers, and it had set him free.

It had also, though he did not put it this way even to himself, hollowed him out like a gourd. Something moved behind his eyes some nights, when the candle guttered. Something patient. He had learned not to look in mirrors after dark.

He rode ahead of the army with six Censors and a stolen map, down the drowned road toward the flooded chapel where the covenant's anchor-stone lay beneath forty feet of black water. The false reforging required three things: the harvested prayers, the stolen tokens, and the stone itself. Aldous had the first two. Serel had promised him the third, and Serel had never yet broken a contract.

"You understand," said the Censor captain beside him, a woman named Ilse with frost-grey eyes, "that if this is a trap, His Majesty's mercy does not extend to guides."

"I understand arithmetic," Serel said. "You get your god-engine fed, I get my life and my percentage, and the world gets order. Everyone's column balances."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. "You were a smuggler."

"I was an *accountant*," Serel said, "which is worse, and better at this."

They came down through the salt marshes as full dark fell, and Serel felt the anchor-stone before he saw the chapel roof breaking the water — a pull behind his sternum, faint and wrong, like a hook set in him long ago and only now reeled taut. The thing inside him stirred and leaned toward it, and Serel let it lean. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement.

At the water's edge he dismounted and stood looking out at the drowned chapel, its bell tower tilted like a drunkard, its windows glowing faintly from beneath — not with light, the Censors would realize too late, but with memory.

"It's guarded," Ilse said. She had a spyglass up. "There are boats. Four, five — people moving on the water."

"Yes," Serel said pleasantly. "I'd be disappointed otherwise. They'll have split their strength — most of them will have gone back to defend the city, they're sentimental like that. What's left here will be the hedge-witch, the ferryman, maybe the boy." He smiled, and the smile did not fit his face properly anymore. "The boy especially. He can't stay away from the ledger of himself. None of us ever can."

He waded into the shallows, and the water around his legs went still and glassy, and things below it turned to look.

---

Oscar heard the city's bells change their pattern at midnight and knew, with the sick clarity that had followed him since Corvane, that the land gates were sealed.

He stood on the seawall above the harbor with the wind pulling at his coat, and below him Vellamare burned in slow motion — not all at once, but quarter by quarter, the Censors moving through the streets with methodical fire, harvesting as they came. He could see the light of the pyres reflected in the water. He could hear, threaded under the screaming and the bells, the sound he had learned to dread more than any other: the engine, feeding. A vast rhythmic swallowing, like the tide being drunk.

"They're taking the prayers off people," Tammas Oduya said beside him. The old ferryman's voice was flat, which was how Oscar knew he was frightened; Tammas only went flat when the fear was too large for inflection. "Off them while they pray. You can see it — look at the shrine district. People kneeling and then just... stopping. Like lamps going out."

Oscar looked. He wished he hadn't. The kneeling figures weren't dead — they were *emptied*, swaying, mouths still moving around prayers that had been taken out of them mid-word. Somewhere in the crowd a child was crying in a voice that sounded already thinned, already harvested.

"We should be down there," Oscar said.

"No," said Freya, behind them both.

She came up the seawall steps slowly, leaning on her staff, and in the pyre-light Oscar could see what the last weeks had done to her — the working in the market had cost her years she could not spare, and she spent more every time she raised her hands now, a woman burning her own candle at both ends and in the middle. But her eyes were the same eyes that had branded the sigil into his hand, and they brooked no argument.

"We should be *here*," she said. "All of us. Because the moment we scatter to save one street, Serel walks into the chapel unopposed, and every street burns for nothing. The city can survive a night of this. The covenant cannot survive another dawn broken."

"And the people?" Oscar's voice cracked on it. "Freya, they're *drinking* them—"

"I know what they're doing." For a moment her composure slipped and he saw the girl she had been, the one who had helped shatter a covenant because a god demanded too much, and who had spent sixty years paying for it. "I have known what it is to feed people to power, Oscar. I wrote that book. Which is why I am telling you: tonight we do not fight the army. Tonight we close the wound. Everything else is triage."

Tammas put a hand on Oscar's shoulder. His grip was a boatman's grip, hard and sure. "She's right, lad. And besides—" the ghost of his old dry humor surfaced, "my passenger says the river's on our side tonight. All the waters are. Whatever happens at that stone, the water will remember whose side it chose."

Below them, the third bell tolled the hour, and somewhere across the dark harbor, small on the black water, the boats were already being loaded for the chapel.

---

Voss rode through the burning city with her Censor's white cloak turned inside out, grey side outward, and hated herself with a cleanliness that felt almost like relief.

She had told herself, riding south from Aldous's camp with the stolen tokens sewn into her coat lining, that she was still an inquisitor — that she was simply correcting an error in the record, the way any honest officer must when the law and the truth diverge. She had rehearsed the argument all day. Law serves justice or it is only violence with letterhead. An institution that harvests prayers out of the mouths of children has forfeited its claim to the word *mercy*. She had believed every word when she composed it.

Then she had ridden past the shrine district, and seen the emptied people kneeling in the ash, and all her careful arguments had gone quiet, because there was nothing in her training for this. There had never been anything in her training for this. Order, she understood now, had been the shape her mercy took because she had never been asked to look directly at what was being ordered away.

A patrol hailed her at the corner of the Bellfounders' Row. She showed them papers — real ones, hers, still valid, still bearing the crowned quill — and the young Censor saluted her and asked if she needed escort, and she said no, thank you, I'm on His Majesty's business, and watched him go with the terrible knowledge that everything she had ever been still worked perfectly. That was the worst of it. Betrayal, done properly, used all the same tools as service.

She found the company's boats at the old fish-stairs, as the message had said they would be, and stood in the shadow of the customs house with her hand on the tokens in her coat, and for a long moment she simply breathed.

*You could still turn back,* said the voice that had kept her alive through twenty years of inquisition. *Ride north, report the tokens lost, keep your rank, keep your certainty. It isn't too late to be the person you were yesterday.*

"No," Voss said aloud, to no one. "Yesterday is the problem."

She walked down the fish-stairs to the water, and the boats took her out into the dark.

---

The flooded chapel rose out of the sea like a half-remembered promise, and Freya stood in the bow of the lead boat and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she had done here a hundred years ago.

Seven of them had stood on that tilting altar. Seven hands on the anchor-stone. She could still name them all — Tammas and his river-god, silent beside her now in the second boat; old Marta of the kilns; the twins Sef and Illen; Brother Callow, who had wept while he broke his oath and been the first to die for it; and Veyra, radiant Veyra, who had demanded one sacrifice too many, one child too many, until Freya had stood in this very water and said *no further*. They had called it mercy, what they did next. They had caged a god to save a village, and the cage had become a prison, and the prison had become this — a century of unanswered prayers, and now a century of answered ones curdling into war.

Every thread of it ran through the stone below her feet. And every thread ran, God help her, through the boy in the third boat, whose hand bore her handwriting in blood and who did not yet know the whole of what she had written there.

"Freya." Tammas, quietly. "We're close enough. Orders?"

She looked back. Three boats: Tammas and two of his river-cousins, human-shaped and not; herself; Oscar, pale and steady at the tiller of the third, with Voss sitting grimly among the oath-tokens like a wolf invited into a sheepfold and choosing, so far, to behave. Behind them, Vellamare burned against the sky. Ahead, the chapel windows glowed under the water, and around the drowned nave she could see movement — shapes in the shallows, white cloaks, the brass glint of the engine's outriders. Serel had not waited for the main force. Serel was already beginning.

"He's early," Oscar said. "Freya — he's starting *now*, without the full harvest, the false reforging won't hold, it'll tear the—"

"I know what it'll do." Her knuckles whitened on the staff. "If the stone is reforged falsely, the covenant doesn't mend. It shatters. Both realms, all borders, everything loose at once — and Veyra out of her cage with a century of interest owed." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "So we do not let him finish. Tammas — the water. Can you hold the ferry-gate?"

The old man smiled, and it was the saddest and bravest thing Oscar had ever seen on a living face. "Forty years I've carried one passenger," Tammas said. "Tonight I carry an army. Aye, witch. The gate holds as long as I do."

"Voss." Freya turned to the ex-inquisitor. "You know their formations, their signals, their officers. You're our eyes in the water. And Oscar—"

He was already looking at her. In the glow from below, the sigil on his hand had begun to burn, and the burn was spelling letters up his wrist, and Freya read them and felt the bottom drop out of the night.

*What is it?* his face asked. *What does it say?*

What it said was a single word, repeating, in a hand she recognized because she had invented it: the caged god's name, rising through the boy's skin like a drowning woman rising through water.

*VEYRA. VEYRA. VEYRA.*

"She knows we're coming," Freya said. "Good. Let her know. Let her watch." She raised her staff, and the sea around the boats went silver and still, and every window of the drowned chapel lit at once. "Because tonight either the covenant dies forever —"

"— or we finish what we started," Tammas finished, and pushed off from the gunwale, and the boats slid forward together into the glowing water, toward the stone, toward the bells tolling under the sea, toward the single night on which everything — the realms, the gods, the burned city at their backs, and the name of a runaway apprentice — would finally be decided.
