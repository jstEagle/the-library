## 18. The Battle of the Anchor-Stone

The chapel had been drowning for a hundred years, and it drowned beautifully.

Oscar saw it first from the ridge above the flooded valley — a stone spire rising out of black water like the last finger of a buried hand, its bell tower tilted, its windows dark. Mist lay on the flood in long gray ribbons. Beneath that water, he knew, was a nave, an altar, and the anchor-stone: the keel-weight of the old covenant, sunk by Freya's own faction when they broke it, so that no one could ever find it again.

Someone had found it.

Lanterns moved on the water below — dozens of them, strung in a line from the shore to the spire, floating on barges and punts, and around each lantern clustered soldiers in the king's gray. Aldous's vanguard had arrived two days ahead of them, and they had not come to destroy the anchor-stone.

They had come to use it.

"They're running cables down," Voss said beside him. She had her spyglass up, her face lit green-gray in the lens. "Chain, actually. Look at the barge at the center — that's not siege equipment. That's a winch. They're lowering something into the nave."

"Harvested prayers," Tammas said quietly.

Oscar looked at him. The ferryman stood with his pole planted in the mud of the ridge like a man standing his boat in shallow water, and his eyes were fixed on the flood below with an expression Oscar had learned to read over the past weeks: the look of a man watching an old friend be insulted.

"The crates we used to move," Tammas went on. "You catalogued them, boy. You know what's in them better than anyone. Prayer-slip after prayer-slip, answered and bottled and sold. The king has been collecting them for years. A prayer is a thread between a mortal heart and a god. Cut enough threads and wind them together, and you have a rope. And a rope—" his jaw tightened, "—can pull things through doors that should stay shut."

Below them, on the central barge, men were feeding a great chain into the water. It went down and down without end, and where it entered the flood the water glowed — faintly, sickly, a gold-white shimmer like light through fevered skin.

"He's going to do a reforging," Freya said.

She had not spoken since the ridge. She sat slightly apart from them on a fallen trunk, wrapped in a cloak too thin for the season, and she looked older tonight than Oscar had ever seen her — not unaged anymore, not the woman who hadn't changed in thirty years, but spent, drawn down to the wire of herself. The working in the market had cost her. The descent toward the chapel had cost her more. Every mile closer to the anchor-stone seemed to take another year out of her, as if the covenant itself were taxing her passage.

"A false one," she said. "He doesn't have the tokens. He doesn't have the bearers. He has rope instead of oaths, and chain instead of consent. He means to force the door open and bind whatever comes through." Her hands, folded in her lap, were trembling. Oscar pretended not to see. "And if he succeeds, there will never be a true covenant again. Only a leash."

"Then we go down there and stop him," Voss said. It was said flatly, without drama, the way she said everything — but Oscar had watched her these last days, watched the hairline fracture in her certainty widen into something she could no longer ignore, and he knew what it cost her to say *we*. Three weeks ago she had been hunting them. Now she carried a stolen oath-token against her breastbone and the battle plans of the army she'd once commanded, and her former soldiers would hang her from the nearest tree if they caught her.

"There are four hundred of them," Oscar said.

"Three hundred and eighty," Voss corrected. "I counted."

"And six of us."

"Five," Freya said softly, "and a god."

They all looked at Tammas.

The ferryman smiled. It was a small smile, tired and warm, and it made Oscar's chest ache, because he had seen that same smile on the drowned quarter's docks a hundred times, on men who had given their whole lives to a river and been paid in nothing but the river's company.

"The Lady is weak," Tammas said. "A whisper in a reed bed. But she remembers the chapel. She was prayed to here, before it drowned. And water, children, always finds its way home." He lifted his pole from the mud. "There is a ferry-gate beneath that chapel. An old crossing, one of the first, back when the borders between realms were permeable and just. If the king forces the gate open with his rope of prayers, someone must hold it open *properly* — hold it steady, hold it fair, keep it from tearing — until the true tokens can reach the stone. That is ferry-work. That has always been ferry-work."

"How long can you hold it?" Freya asked.

Tammas considered the question the way he considered everything: patiently, as though the answer had been waiting for him all along and deserved a proper greeting.

"As long as it takes," he said.

---

They went down through the reed beds in the last hour before dawn, when the mist was thickest and the lantern-line below burned like a string of orange beads dropped on black silk. Tammas poled them across the flood on a flat punt he'd kept hidden in the reeds for forty years — *for exactly this*, he'd said, and refused to explain further — and the water made no sound around them, which Oscar understood, dimly, was the Lady's doing. A whisper of a god, spending her last coin to buy them silence.

The chapel's submerged porch became an air pocket beneath the tower — a vault of trapped breath, dripping, cold, lit by the gold-white glow seeping up through the flooded nave below. Through a broken grate in the floor, Oscar could see down into the nave itself.

Serel was there.

The guildmaster stood on the altar steps in a coat that had once been fine and now hung off him like a shed skin, and he was shouting orders in a voice cracked raw, and around him the king's engineers worked the winches, and the chain sang taut, and the water of the nave churned with light. On the altar itself lay the anchor-stone: a block of black granite no larger than a coffin lid, carved all over with seven interlocking sigils, six of them worn nearly smooth and the seventh blazing like a brand.

Oscar's hand began to burn.

He gasped and clutched it against his chest. The sigil — his sigil, the mark he'd borne since he was nine years old and had believed, all this time, was only an apprenticeship brand — flared white-hot, and through the pain he felt it *reaching*, straining toward the stone below like a dog toward a whistle only it could hear.

"It knows," Freya whispered. She took his burning hand in both of hers, and her touch cooled it a little, the way shade cools a burn. "It knows it's home. Oscar. Listen to me. When we go down there, the stone will call to you, and Serel will call to you, and the god behind the gate will call to you, and every voice will sound like truth. The false reforging will feel like the answer to everything you've ever wanted. Do you understand me? Whatever you hear down there — you come back to my voice. Only mine."

"Why yours?"

She held his gaze, and for a moment — one unguarded moment — thirty years of guilt and silence and unfinished feeling passed between them like a current under ice.

"Because I wrote your name," she said. "No one else did. No one else can."

* * *

They came down into the nave through the drowned crypt, wading waist-deep through glowing water, and for ninety seconds it almost worked.

Voss went first, in the gray uniform she'd stolen back from her own past, and the sentries at the crypt stair saluted her before their brains caught up with their eyes. By then Tammas's pole had already taken two of them into the water, gently, the way you'd guide a drunk friend home, and Freya's hands were moving through shapes that made the lantern-light bend and curdle, and Oscar was running for the altar with the ledger under his arm and his sigil screaming —

— and then a bell rang.

Not the chapel's drowned bell. A small, sharp alarm-bell from the barges above, and the nave erupted. Torches. Shouting. Crossbows cranking. Serel turned on the altar steps, and even across the length of the nave Oscar saw his face change — saw the fear in it curdle instantly, characteristically, into greed.

"*There!* There he is! The sigil-bearer — take his hand, take the hand, the king wants the hand —"

"Scatter!" Voss roared, in the parade-ground voice of a woman who had commanded three hundred men, and to Oscar's eternal astonishment, some of the king's soldiers *obeyed her* before they remembered they shouldn't.

Then the nave was chaos.

Oscar ran. He didn't remember deciding to run; his legs simply understood, before the rest of him did, that the ledger and the prayer-slip and his own branded hand were the only things in the world that mattered, and everything else — the bolts hissing past his ears, the torchlight strobing on black water, Freya's voice cutting through the din calling his name, *his name, come back, come back* — was weather.

He reached the altar.

Up close, the anchor-stone hummed. Not a sound — a pressure, a vibration in the teeth and the breastbone, and the seventh sigil carved into it matched the sigil on his hand exactly, mark for mark, line for line, and Oscar understood with a lurching, vertiginous horror that the stone had been waiting for him the way a lock waits for its key.

Behind him, Serel screamed: "*Winch! Pull! PULL!*"

The chain hauled taut. The water of the nave began to spin — slowly at first, then faster, a whirlpool forming around the altar, and in its center the air split.

It split like a wound.

Oscar had seen the gate once before, in the sigil's burning visions: a bridge between realms, burning. This was worse. This was a door being *forced*, hinges shrieking, and through the widening gap poured light that was not light — a gold-white torrent, the harvested prayers of ten thousand desperate hearts, ripped from their givers and spent like coin — and behind the light, pressing against the gap, something vast turned over in the dark beyond the world and began, with the slow inevitability of a glacier, to come through.

"Freya!" Oscar screamed.

"I see it!"

She was fighting her way toward him through the whirlpool, hair loose and streaming, and she was *aging* as she came — he could see it happening, year by year, each step costing her, her working tearing power out of her body to slow the gate's forced opening, buying seconds at the price of her life. Behind her, Voss held the crypt stair with a captured sword, holding off a dozen of her former comrades with a skill that looked less like fighting than like argument — precise, economical, *reluctant*. And at the edge of the whirlpool stood Tammas Oduya, planting his ferry-pole in the spinning water.

"Hold the gate open!" Freya shouted to him. "Steady and fair, Tammas! Steady and fair!"

"Aye," said Tammas, "as ever," and drove the pole down into the heart of the gate.

What happened next, Oscar would spend the rest of his existence trying to describe, and never quite manage.

The whirlpool stopped.

Not slowed — *stopped*, all at once, the way a riot stops when a child walks into it. The churning water went still and smooth as glass, and the torn edges of the gate — the shrieking, splintering wound in the air — steadied, widened *gently*, became not a wound but a doorway: tall, arched, dignified, the shape of every ferry-arch in every drowned quarter of every free city. And Tammas stood in it, hip-deep in still water, pole planted, and he was *everywhere* in it at once — his figure repeated in the arch's frame like a man reflected between facing mirrors, holding the crossing open from every side at once.

Through the doorway, the vast thing in the dark stopped pushing.

It waited. It regarded the ferryman. And somewhere beyond the realms, Oscar felt — everyone felt, soldier and smuggler and oath-bearer alike, every soul in the nave felt it pass through them like a cold tide — a god's attention settle on Tammas Oduya, and recognize him.

Forty years, the attention seemed to say. Forty years, one passenger, never once late, never once asked for fare.

"*Steady and fair,*" Tammas said aloud, to the god, and grinned his small tired grin. "That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

"Tammas, get *out* of there!" Oscar screamed. "We have the tokens, we can close it —"

"You can't close it, boy." Tammas didn't turn his head. His voice was calm as deep water. "Forced gates don't close. They tear. Someone holds the crossing while the true work is done, or everyone in this valley drowns in what comes through. That's ferry-work." He rolled his shoulders, settling, a man preparing to stand a long watch. "Go on, then. Do the true work. I'll mind the door."

"Tammas —"

"*Go.*"

Oscar went.

He went because Freya seized his sleeve and hauled him bodily onto the altar steps, because the anchor-stone's call had become unbearable, because somewhere underneath the terror a voice that sounded horribly like his own meticulous ledger-keeping soul observed that the arithmetic admitted no other entry. He knelt at the stone. He pressed his branded hand against the seventh sigil.

The world went white.

* * *

He was in the covenant.

Not near it — *in* it. He stood in a space that was not a place, among seven pillars of light arranged in a ring, six of them guttering like dying candles and one of them — his — blazing. And he understood, in the wordless way of visions, what the false reforging lacked and what the true one required: not rope but oath, not chain but consent, seven bearers or none, and the seventh bearer's vessel kneeling at the stone with his hand upon it and his name in his mouth.

*Say it,* the stone said, in a voice like his own. *Give me the name.*

And Oscar opened his mouth —

— and heard, beneath the roaring light, a small tired voice saying: *steady and fair.*

He looked back through the vision. The gate still stood. Tammas still stood in it. But the reflections were fewer now — five figures where there had been mirrors without number — and the vast thing beyond the door had begun, very gently, very patiently, to help him hold it. The god and the ferryman, bearing the weight together, one crossing, one watch.

"Boy," Tammas said, and now he did turn his head, and his eyes found Oscar's across the impossible distance between altar and arch, and they were not afraid. "Tell the Lady — tell her the fare was always paid. Forty years, and she never once asked me for fare. Tell her I'm squaring the books."

"Tammas, *no* —"

"Steady and fair," said Tammas Oduya, and let go of everything except the door.

The gate blazed. The reflections collapsed inward, one by one, like candles going out, and each one that went out, Tammas paid for — Oscar could see it, the toll being taken, years and strength and finally the man himself, spent coin by coin to keep the crossing dignified while the true work was done. The last reflection stood alone in the arch, an old ferryman grown suddenly ancient, smiling his small tired smile.

"Mind the books," Tammas said.

And the door closed — gently, the way a good ferryman ties off a rope — and the still water of the nave shivered, and where the ferryman had stood there was only a wooden pole, drifting.

* * *

Afterward, Oscar could never remember the order of things clearly. The vision released him. The chain snapped, its harvested prayers unravelling into the water like spilled thread. Serel fled screaming up the crypt stair, and Voss let him go, because some animals aren't worth the arrow. The soldiers broke and ran, hundreds of them, fleeing a chapel that had shown them a god's courtesy. And Freya knelt in the shallows at the center of the nave, holding a wooden ferry-pole, weeping without any sound at all.

Oscar waded to her. He knelt. He put his arms around her, and his branded hand — cool now, quiet, the seventh sigil settled into his skin like a key returned to its lock — rested against her shoulder.

"He held it," Freya said at last. Her voice was wrecked. "All the way to the end. Steady and fair."

"Yes."

"The Lady will carry him now. Across. Properly. With fare paid and honors given." She looked up, and her eyes were old and wet and fierce. "Do you understand what we bought tonight, Oscar? What it cost? We know the rite now. We know what the stone demands. Seven bearers, or none — and the seventh must consent, and the seventh's vessel must give his name."

Oscar looked down at the anchor-stone, humming quietly in the ruined nave, its six worn sigils waiting, its seventh satisfied.

He thought of a ledger, and a hidden life, and a name he had guarded like treasure for fifteen years.

"Yes," he said. "I understand."

Above them, through the broken roof, dawn was coming up gray over the flooded valley — and far away, on the road from the east, dust rose in a column miles high.

Aldous was coming, and he was bringing his engine.
