## 22. The Last Entry

The river had no name, and neither did he.

He knew this the way one knows the weight of an oar in the hand — not as a thought but as a fact of the body. The water ran silver-dark beneath a sky that was not quite sky, and the banks slid past without ever coming closer, and the boat went where it was needed, which was always forward, always across.

He had been poling this stretch for a long time. He did not know how long. Time between the realms moved like honey poured from a great height — slow, unbroken, sweet in a way that made counting feel obscene. There were passengers to carry, and that was enough. There was always someone at the landing who needed taking over: the newly dead with their hands full of unfinished sentences, pilgrims walking out of dreams, occasionally something older and stranger that paid its fare in memories it no longer wanted. He took them all. He asked nothing of them except that they sit still and let him work.

He was good at his work. That much had survived whatever had been taken from him. His hands remembered things his mind could not: how to read a current by the shiver of reeds, how to balance a load so the gunwales kissed the water evenly, how to wait — how to be patient past all reason, because patience was the whole craft of ferrymen, and he had been one, he understood dimly, for longer than most men lived.

He did not have a name. This did not trouble him, exactly. It was more like a room in his house whose door stood open onto darkness — you didn't go in, but you noticed it every time you passed. Sometimes a passenger would say *thank you* and look at him expectantly afterward, waiting for a name to attach their gratitude to, and he would say nothing, and they would nod slowly, understanding in the way the dead understand things, and look away. Once, very early — or what felt like early; the river kept no calendar — a child had asked him outright. *What's your name?* And he had opened his mouth and found nothing there, no word, no shape, only a smooth blank place where something should have been, and the child had frowned and said, *That's sad,* and he had agreed, though he couldn't have said why it sat in his chest like a stone dropped through ice.

He carried people. He crossed them over. The river took what it took.

He told himself that was enough.

* * *

The passenger came at dusk, if the space between realms can be said to have a dusk — a softening of the light, a hush falling over the reeds, the water going still as held breath. She was standing at the landing when he brought the boat in, and even before she stepped aboard he knew her.

That was the strange part. He knew no one. Names slid off him like rain off oilcloth; faces faded within a day, sometimes within an hour, the way frost fades off a window in a warm room. But this woman — small, sharp-eyed, grey-streaked hair pinned back with what looked like a blackberry bramble that was, impossibly, still flowering — this woman he knew. Not her name. Not her history. Just a bone-deep recognition, the way you know the smell of bread baking, the way your body knows a road it walked in childhood.

She looked at him for a long moment before stepping down into the boat, and her eyes were wet, and she smiled like someone trying not to break.

"Well," she said. Her voice caught on the word and steadied itself. "You're a long way from your hedge."

"I don't—" He heard his own voice as if from a distance. "I don't keep a hedge."

"No," she said softly, settling herself amidships, arranging her skirts with the fussiness of someone buying time. "I don't suppose you do. Not anymore."

He pushed off. The current took them, gentle as a hand under the shoulders. For a while neither spoke. The banks drifted by — meadowland here, though he had never seen meadows on this stretch, all smoke-grey sedge and drowned stones — and the woman watched the country change around her with an expression he couldn't name, wonder and grief braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which strand was which.

"You'll want my fare," she said at last.

"The river takes what it takes." The words came out of him smooth and worn, a coin rubbed featureless by handling. "I don't set the price."

"Mm. I remember that about you." She reached into her coat — a good coat, travel-worn, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and ink — and drew out a book. A ledger, he thought at first, and something in him flinched toward it with a hunger that frightened him, an ache so sudden and specific it was like pressing on a bruise he hadn't known he had. But it wasn't a ledger. It was smaller, softer, bound in green leather gone dark at the corners, swollen with use, a ribbon marker hanging out of it like a tongue.

"What's that?" he asked.

"A record," she said. "Of a man who deserved better than the one he got."

The current carried them on. Somewhere behind them, faint as a bell rung underwater, something vast shifted in the deep places of the river, and the woman glanced over the side without fear, the way you glance at an old dog stirring in its sleep.

"Where are we going?" he asked. It was not a question ferrymen asked. Ferrymen knew the crossings; that was the whole of their competence. But this crossing felt different. The banks were wrong. The light was wrong. The river was running somewhere it had never run in all his long, blank tenure, and he found he did not mind, and that frightened him worse than anything.

"Home," the woman said. "If you'll take me."

"It isn't my—"

"Say it," she interrupted, gently, terribly gently, the way you speak to a horse you're asking to walk into water. "Say the thing you've been circling since I stepped aboard. You looked at me like a man looking at his own front door. Say it."

His hands were steady on the pole. They were always steady; that was the one mercy of what he was. But inside, something was moving, some door swinging open onto that dark room, and from the darkness a voice — his own voice, younger, angrier, alive — was saying words he did not remember learning.

"I know you," he said. "I don't know how. I don't know your name, or mine, or whether I ever had one. But I know you. When you laughed just now — you didn't laugh, but you almost did, and I know what that almost sounds like. I've spent—" He stopped. Started again. "There are years missing out of me. Whole years, like pages torn out of a book, and every one of them has you somewhere in the margin. Who am I?"

The woman closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were fierce and bright and full of a grief she had clearly carried so long it had become a kind of furniture.

"You're going to hate me," she said. "For a little while. And then you're going to understand, and then — well. We'll see what comes after understanding. I'm too old to promise more than that."

She opened the book.

* * *

"This is what happened," Freya Vane said, and began to read.

She read to him as the river bore them through the changing country — through meadows now, real ones, gold-headed grass bending in a wind that smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke, a wind from somewhere warm and particular and named. She read of a boy whose parents died keeping a witch's door against the king's men, and of the witch who took the boy in because the debt was written into her bones. She read of an apprenticeship: herbs and ledgers, sigils and sums, a hedge-house at the end of a road that ended in nothing but hedges and sky. She read of a quarrel — *you'll forgive me for skipping the worst of it, you were insufferable and so was I* — and of a boy who ran away to the free cities and built himself a life of neat columns and careful anonymity, keeping books for smugglers because numbers never lied and never left.

And then she read of the crates. The answered prayers, warm and weeping. The prayer-slip addressed by name. The burning bridge between realms that showed itself in the sigil on a sleeping hand. She read of the guildhouse fire, and the flight, and the reunion thick with old resentment, and the covenant broken a century before by seven oath-bearers who had thought they were saving the world and had only caged a piece of it. She read of Tammas Oduya and his forty-year passenger. Of Maren Voss, who believed order was mercy until she learned what her king meant to buy with it. Of tokens gathered and oaths inherited, of a funeral in the drowned quarter, of a god curdling in her cage, of a battle at a flooded chapel where a ferryman held a gate open with his dying hands.

She read of the reforging.

Here her voice changed. It went quieter, and slower, and he heard in it the sound of someone reading words they had written with their own blood on the page.

"The covenant required a vessel," she read. "A living bearer of the original oath, willing, who would offer up the sigil and everything bound to it. The sigil was written in his blood when he was nine years old, the night his parents saved my life. He was always the seventh bearer. He was always the key. And the price of turning a key is that the key stops being yours — the covenant took his name out of every record, out of every memory, out of every mouth. Out of mine, too. Or it tried."

She looked up at him. The meadows had given way to orchards, and the orchards to hedgerows, and beyond the hedgerows he could see — impossibly, unmistakably — a road. A narrow dirt road running along the top of the far bank, heading nowhere, ending in nothing but hedges and sky.

"The covenant cannot touch what is truly written," Freya said. "It erased him from every ledger, every register, every memory in every realm. But I had already begun writing a book. And I wrote his name in it. Oscar Hartwell. In my own hand, in ink I made myself, with intent — and intent, in the old craft, is the strongest binding there is. What is truly written cannot be unwritten. So the world forgot him. And I sat down at my table every morning and wrote him back."

He could not speak. The pole was in his hands and his hands were steady, but everything else in him was shaking, was flooding, was a lock-gate bursting after a hundred dry years. Pages torn out of a book — he could feel them now, not returning, nothing so simple as that, but *pressing*, the way a scar presses when weather changes. A woman's voice teaching him the difference between foxglove and comfrey. The scratch of his own pen balancing a column to the copper. Firelight. An argument. A bridge burning between realms. A chapel filling with water. Hands — his hands — holding a gate open while everyone he loved passed through.

"Oscar," the woman said. "Your name is Oscar Hartwell. You were my apprentice. You were my — " and here, for the first time, her voice broke clean through, no craft holding it, " — you were the best thing I ever did, and the worst thing I ever did was let the covenant take you rather than tell you the truth while you still had time to choose differently. I have regretted it every day of the years since. I expect to regret it every day of the years to come. That's my fare. That's what I'm paying the river: the truth, out loud, to the one man owed it. Now you know what you gave. Now you know what it cost. And now—"

"Now what?" His voice came out rough, a stranger's voice, his own voice. "Now I remember being a name? I can't — Freya—" The name came out of him whole, easy, like a key turning. "I can't go back. Whatever I was, the world's been rearranged around the hole where I stood. Everyone I—"

"Not everyone," she said.

* * *

The boat touched the bank.

He hadn't poled it there. Or he had — his hands had moved without his deciding, drawn by the same pull that had bent the river from its course, the same pull that had carried a green leather book across the boundary between realms, because the covenant was new and young and still learning its own rules, and it had never reckoned with a stubborn old woman who wrote with intent.

The road ran away ahead of them, narrow and rutted, hemmed in by hedges heavy with late blackberries. The air smelled of woodsmoke and turned earth and, underneath it, faintly, of ink. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and fell silent, satisfied.

Freya climbed out and stood on the bank, and did not offer him her hand, because she was wise enough to know that some steps must be taken alone.

He looked down at his hands. Ferryman's hands, scarred and strong, rope-callused. And on the right one, faint as a watermark, pale as a scar long healed — the sigil. Not burned away. Transformed. An oath fulfilled leaves a mark, the way a ring leaves a mark on a finger worn for fifty years. He was still marked. He was still, in whatever way mattered to the old powers, *written*.

"If I step off this boat," he said slowly, working it through the way he'd once worked through a ledger, line by line, trusting the arithmetic, "I'm not the ferryman anymore."

"You were never only the ferryman," Freya said. "You were a boy who liked tidy columns and hated injustice, who ran away once and came home once, and who gave up everything he had — including having a self — so that strangers he'd never meet could pray to something kinder than a cage. The river was a job, Oscar. It was never a name."

"And if the river needs me?"

"Then the river knows where to find you. You of all people should know the way works both directions." She almost smiled. "You carried the dead across for — however long it was. Don't you think the dead talked? Don't you think a certain ferryman of the drowned quarter, wherever he's moored now, put in a good word?"

Something loosened in his chest. Tammas. He could almost — not see him, not yet, the pages were still pressing, not returned — but almost hear him, that weary chuckle, *took you long enough, lad.*

He set the pole down. It seemed suddenly a strange thing to be holding, a tool for a trade, and trades were for men with names.

He stepped off the boat.

The ground held him. Grass, real grass, bent under his boots, and the smell of it rose up green and overwhelming, and he stood there swaying like a man come ashore after a long voyage, and Freya Vane put her hand in the crook of his arm as if he were an old man and she were escorting him home, and perhaps they both were, and perhaps that was fine.

They walked up the road together. Behind them, the nameless river ran on, carrying whoever needed carrying, and the boat waited at the landing, empty and patient, for the next ferryman — because there was always a next ferryman; that was the nature of crossings — and if the boat looked, somehow, content to wait, well. Rivers keep their own counsel.

The hedge at the end of the road came into view as the light went gold: a cottage with a crooked chimney, smoke rising, a garden gone gloriously to seed, a second chair on the doorstep that had not been there that morning and had been there, Freya would insist, for years.

At the door, Oscar stopped.

"There's a book," he said. "Yours. The one with my name in it. Is there room for another entry?"

Freya looked up at him — at the scarred hands, the steady eyes, the man the world had tried to erase and a green leather book had refused to surrender — and this time she did laugh, wet and bright and entirely unashamed.

"Oscar Hartwell," she said, "the last entry hasn't been written yet. That's rather the point of coming home."

She pushed open the door. Inside, a fire was already lit, and two cups already poured, and on the table by the window lay the ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things, its columns waiting, its last page blank — and above the mantel, hung where a hunting trophy might hang in a prouder house, a single framed page of old paper, browned at the edges, bearing one line in a child's careful hand:

*Prayer answered. — F.V.*

Oscar Hartwell stepped over the threshold, and the door closed behind him, and the hedge at the end of the road kept what it kept.
