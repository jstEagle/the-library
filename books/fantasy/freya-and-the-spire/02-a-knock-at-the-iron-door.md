## 2. A Knock at the Iron Door

The rain that night was the color of ink left too long in a pot — not falling so much as settling over the city like a decision already made. Theo Whitlock noticed it because it was the only thing he'd noticed all evening that wasn't an entry in the Ledger of Small Debts.

*Item: one memory of a first kiss, traded by J. Ambrose, lamplighter, against arrears. Condition: slightly wept upon.*

He dipped his pen. The guild's ink was famous for never fading, which Theo had always thought less a virtue than a threat.

*Item: three summers of childhood, sold by M. Corvane, cooper's daughter, to cover her father's standing debt. Note: summers selected by seller. Note appended: she chose the ones with the river in them.*

There was a particular sound a quill made when copying someone's life at four lines per life, and Theo knew it intimately. It sounded like nothing. That was the trick of the Guild of Impossible Things — everything impossible passed through its doors eventually, and by the time it reached a desk like his, it had been made small enough to file.

He blew on the page and reached for the next folio.

That was when the knock came.

Three knocks, uneven, low on the door — the height, he would think later, of someone who had to reach up. The iron door of the guild did not get knocked on. It got *petitioned*, through the brass slot, with proper forms and proper fees and the proper expression of despair. Nobody knocked. Knocking implied you expected the door to be answered by a person.

Scribe Pellory looked up from his ledgers across the long room, candlelight catching the lenses of his spectacles so that his eyes vanished entirely. "Did you hear—"

"I heard."

"Nobody knocks," said Pellory, with the satisfaction of a man confirming the laws of nature still held.

The knock came again. Three more, more urgent now, and beneath them — Theo tilted his head — a sound like breathing, quick and small.

The porter, old Brannick, was meant to handle the door. But Brannick had gone down with a fever two days ago, and the provost had declared a replacement an unnecessary expense until the Centennial Audit was concluded, which meant that for two days the iron door of the Guild of Impossible Things had been answerable only by whoever drew the short straw.

Theo looked at the straws in the cup by the hearth. They had been drawn at sundown. He had drawn the short one. He had known this would happen; he always drew the short straw, because the straw-drawing was itself recorded in a ledger, and the ledger was kept by the man who owned his debt, and the man who owned his debt found it economical for Theo to be cold and wet and awake at inconvenient hours. It kept him pliable. Theo had learned to recognize the shape of these arrangements the way sailors learn weather: not by studying them, but by being rained on.

"I'll go," he said, before Pellory could suggest it.

"Good lad." Pellory returned to his entries without another glance. In the guild, this was courtesy.

Theo took the lamp from his desk and went down the stairs, past the seals.

Every guild had its decorations. The Guild of Impossible Things had its seals: great waxen discs the size of wagon wheels pressed into the stone walls of the stairwell, each one stamped with the sigil of some founding master dead five centuries, each one layered under so many renewals of office that they'd become geological. Theo passed them every day and saw them never. They were as much a part of the building as the damp.

But as he descended past the third landing, lamp raised, he noticed something he had never noticed in nine years of night duty.

The oldest seal — the cracked one, the one nobody could read anymore, the one the junior scribes called the Burnt Biscuit — was glowing.

Faintly. Faintly the way a banked coal glows, or the way a word glows in your mind half a second after you've forgotten you were trying to remember it. A dull amber pulse, slow, rhythmic, patient.

Theo stopped on the stairs and stared at it.

Then, sensibly, he decided he was tired, and kept walking.

* * *

The iron door had no window. You opened it a hand's width on its chain and asked intentions, and if the intentions were improper you closed it again and let the rain do the rest. This was guild policy, and guild policy had been refined over centuries into something very close to cruelty, which everyone agreed was efficient.

Theo opened it a hand's width on its chain.

Standing in the black rain, plastered head to foot in mud, was a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, perhaps ten — it was hard to tell, because she was caked in river clay to the eyebrows, and her hair was a matted rope of it, and she was shivering so hard her whole outline seemed uncertain, as though the rain were still deciding whether to keep her. She wore no coat. She carried nothing except, clutched against her chest with both arms, a flat piece of slate no bigger than a book, wrapped in what might once have been a scarf.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the most extraordinary thing about her, which was saying something, given the mud. They were pale grey, almost silver, and they were utterly calm. Everyone who came to the iron door came desperate. This child looked at Theo the way he looked at ledgers: reading him.

"The guild is closed," Theo said. "Petitions are heard at dawn, through the slot, with the proper forms. If you've come about a debt—"

She said something.

It was not a language. That was Theo's first thought, and his second, and for a while his third. It wasn't that he failed to recognize the words — he spoke passable Low Vellish and could order dinner in three other tongues — it was that the sounds didn't behave like words at all. They were round where words were sharp. They moved in a way that made him think of water finding its level, of smoke deciding which way to rise. And underneath the strangeness there was a rhythm, a cadence, that his ear insisted was *speech*, actual speech, meaning-shaped, and the insistence of it made the hair on his arms stand up.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I don't—"

She tried again, slower, and pointed past him, up, up — and here was the thing Theo would turn over in his mind for many nights afterward — she pointed not at the guild but *through* it, upward, toward the Spire whose root the guild was built into, the great pale tower that stood over the city like a finger held to lips.

Then she shivered once, enormously, and swayed, and Theo — who had been raised better than his circumstances, and who had a younger sister once, before, before that entry in a ledger he did not let himself think about — unhooked the chain.

Later, filling out the incident form, he would write: *Door opened in accordance with humanitarian discretion.* There was no such clause in guild policy. He wrote it anyway. Forms, he had learned, believed whatever you wrote on them.

* * *

Getting her into the scriptorium antechamber without waking the whole floor was a matter of some care. The guild slept lightly in audit season. Theo sat her on the bench by the cold stove, fetched the blanket from Brannick's fever-nest — Brannick owed him for covering two shifts, so technically this was an exchange of value, and therefore permitted — and knelt to work the worst of the mud off her with a rag.

She let him. She watched him do it with that same ledger-reading calm, and when he paused, she touched her own chest and said a single word.

"Freya," Theo guessed, because it sounded like a name, or near enough.

Her face changed. Not a smile exactly — more the way ice changes when the sun first finds it. She nodded, and said the word again, and then said a great deal more, rapidly, hopefully, gesturing at herself and the room and the ceiling and finally at him, and none of it meant anything he could catch, and yet —

And yet the seals were glowing.

All of them now. Down the length of the antechamber wall, the ancient waxen discs had woken, amber light pulsing in time — Theo felt his stomach drop as he understood it — *in time with her voice*. Each syllable she spoke, the seals breathed. When she paused for breath, they dimmed. When she ran on eagerly, they brightened until their cracks threw little veins of light across the stone.

"Stop," Theo whispered. "Please — stop, you have to stop—"

She stopped. The seals guttered back to dormancy, sullen embers. In the sudden dark Theo became aware of how loudly his own heart was going.

She was looking at the seals too. And her expression, in the last of the light, was not wonder.

It was recognition. The look of someone seeing, after a very long journey, a landmark she'd been told about.

* * *

Provost Hale was woken at half past two, which Theo knew because Hale made sure everyone knew, arriving in the antechamber in a dressing gown of severe grey with his silver hair combed flat and his face arranged into the expression of a man who has been personally inconvenienced by the universe and intends to file a complaint.

Hale was not a large man, but rooms got smaller when he entered them. He had been provost for eleven years, and in eleven years Theo had seen him raise his voice exactly twice, both times to a hush. His anger worked like the guild's ink: it never faded, and it got into everything.

"Well?" said Hale.

"A child, Provost. Found at the door. Alone. Speaking—" Theo hesitated. How did one file a language? "Speaking something unidentified. She's maybe eight. Ten. Half-frozen. I judged turning her out would reflect poorly on the guild, in audit season especially."

Hale's eyes narrowed a fraction. Theo had learned to read fractions of Hale the way other men read whole sentences.

"You opened the door," Hale said, "on your own judgment."

"On humanitarian discretion, Provost."

"There is no such discretion."

"No, Provost."

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but the brief arithmetic of a man deciding whether an error is worth the cost of correcting. Audit season. Witnesses. A blanket already dispensed. Hale let it go the way a rich man lets a copper fall.

He crossed to the bench. Freya watched him come with her silver eyes, the slate hugged to her chest, and when Hale stopped in front of her she looked *up* at him, unhurried, and said something in the round, flowing tongue.

The nearest seal — the Burnt Biscuit, right above the stove — flared like a struck match.

Hale went very still.

Theo watched the provost's face do something he had never seen it do. It *flickered*. Underneath the official surface, something moved — something old and frightened — and then Hale smoothed it away so quickly that Theo doubted anyone else in the world would have caught it. But Theo had spent nine years learning to read the fine print of powerful men.

"Where did you find her," Hale said quietly. He hadn't turned around.

"At the door, Provost. As I said."

"In the rain. Alone." Hale's voice was perfectly level. "No escort. No note. No claimant."

"No, Provost."

"And she speaks... this."

"Yes, Provost."

Hale was silent for a long moment. The seal above the stove dimmed slowly back to nothing, like an eye closing. Rain ticked against the high windows. Somewhere below, the pipes of the old building groaned and settled, the sound the Spire's root made when the weather leaned on it.

"Take her to the holding room," Hale said at last. "Dry clothes. Food. Nothing with a edge sharper than a spoon. And Whitlock—" he turned, and the official face was back, seamless, "—you will draft the intake record tonight. Full particulars. Everything she says, everything she carries, everything you observed. I want it on my desk by morning."

"For processing," Theo said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Everything that comes through that door gets processed, Whitlock." Hale adjusted the collar of his dressing gown with two precise fingers. "That is what the guild is *for*."

He paused at the doorway, and — Theo would remember this too, later, when remembering was all he had — he glanced back at the child on the bench, and for one unguarded instant his face did the flickering thing again, and Theo realized with a cold slide in his gut that Provost Hale was afraid.

Not of the girl.

Of what she was *for*.

"By morning, Whitlock," Hale said, and was gone up the stairs, his candle bobbing away between the sleeping seals.

* * *

The holding room was a converted vault, warm enough, dry enough, equipped with a cot, a bucket, and a view of nothing. Theo brought her bread and broth and a wool shirt that had belonged to a debtor who'd traded his wardrobe against arrears and then, presumably, walked home in the rain. The guild was efficient like that.

Freya ate the way she did everything — watchfully, completely. Then she unwrapped the slate from its sodden scarf, and Theo saw that the scarf was embroidered, in thread gone grey with age, with letters. Round letters. Flowing letters. The same shapes, he realized, that were carved — worn nearly to invisibility — into the base of the oldest seal downstairs.

She turned the slate over. On its face, drawn in something white and waxy, was a picture: a tall tower, and beneath it, deep beneath it, a small figure with its arms spread wide. Around the figure, circling it like a fence, were rows and rows of those same round letters.

She tapped the tower. Then she pointed up — through the ceiling, through the floors, up and up — and said one word, gentle as a lullaby, and every seal in the holding room glowed soft as candlelight, and somewhere far overhead the pipes of the Spire's root sang one low, resonant note, like a bell struck under a blanket.

Then she looked at Theo, waiting, as if he must surely understand. As if anyone would understand. As if the whole world were written in this language and only the guild had lost the habit of reading.

"It's late," Theo said uselessly, in Vellish, and tucked the blanket around her, and took the slate, and did not give it back.

He told himself he was confiscating it for the intake record. Which was true. Everything that came through the door got processed. That was what the guild was for.

He climbed back to the scriptorium with the slate inside his coat, against his chest, where its edges pressed coldly through his shirt like a small, patient accusation. At his desk he laid out a fresh intake folio and wrote the header in proper guild hand: *Item: one child, unnamed, recovered [date], condition: muddy, articulate in unknown tongue.*

His pen hovered.

Below it, faintly, through the floorboards, he could still feel it — that low sung note, fading by degrees, the way a struck bell fades. The Spire's root, humming to itself. Or answering.

Theo looked at the blank space under the header, where the truth was supposed to go, and thought about the word *processing*, and about what, exactly, happened to things after the guild finished processing them. He had copied the results for nine years. Four lines per life.

He wrote: *Child gives name as FREYA.*

And then, in smaller script, in the margin, where margins were where Theo kept everything that mattered:

*Note appended: the seals know her.*

Outside, the black rain went on settling over the city like a decision already made, and upstairs, behind a door marked with a brass plate reading PROVOST, a candle burned past three o'clock, and past four, and did not go out.
