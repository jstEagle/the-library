## 4. What the Provost Keeps

Provost Hale had a ritual for mornings when the world required him to be terrifying before breakfast. He rose at the fourth bell, dressed in the grey-and-silver of his office, and stood before the tall mirror in his chambers practicing the expression he called *the Audit Face* — a look of mild, patient disappointment that made grown scribes confess to crimes they hadn't committed just to make it stop.

The mirror was cracked down one side. He had never repaired it. There was something useful about a face with a flaw running through it.

"Good," he told his reflection, and the reflection agreed with the weary air of a man who had heard this speech many times.

Then he went down to check on the ledger.

The intake ledger for the night of the black rain sat on his desk where he'd left it, chained to its stand by a lock older than three provosts. Hale unlocked it with the small brass key he kept on a cord around his neck — not because anyone would steal it, but because the guild respected nothing it wasn't forced to respect — and turned to the page he had written himself.

*One (1) child, female, approx. eight years. Found at iron door, 11th bell. Speaking unregistered language. Seals responsive. Disposition: processing, dawn.*

It was a tidy entry. Hale prided himself on tidy entries. A tidy entry was a thing that could be found again, or — more often, in his line of work — *not* found again.

He turned the page.

And stopped.

There was a wrongness to the paper that took him a moment to name, the way you notice a room is quiet before you realize the clock has stopped. The next leaf of the ledger was thinner than it should have been. He held it up to the lamp, tilting it so the light came through, and there it was: a ghost-line of sizing along the spine, the faint scar where a page had been cut away with a razor and replaced with a sheet of matching stock, stitched back in with thread a half-shade too dark.

Someone had removed a page from his ledger and put a forgery in its place.

Hale set the book down very carefully, as though it might be listening.

Now. Let us be clear about what Provost Ambrose Hale felt in that moment, because history — the honest kind, which is to say the kind no one wrote down — would want it recorded properly. He did not feel outrage. Outrage was for men who believed their institutions were sound. He did not even feel surprise, not exactly. What he felt was the particular cold dread of a man who has spent thirty years learning every trick of erasure the guild possesses, and who has just recognized one of them being used against him — and worse, recognized it *well*. It was like hearing your own voice call your name from an empty street.

Whoever had done this knew the craft. Knew the sizing, the thread, the way old ledgers drank light. That narrowed the field considerably. There were perhaps nine people in the Guild of Impossible Things capable of a forgery this clean, and Hale could account for seven of them: two were dead, one was blind, and four were himself on various bad nights.

Which left the night scribes. And of the night scribes, only one had been alone with the intake ledger between the eleventh bell and dawn.

"Theo Whitlock," Hale said aloud, tasting the name.

He said it without anger. Anger would come later, if it was earned. What he felt now was closer to grief, though he would have denied it under torture, and had, once, over a different matter entirely.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he always did when the ground shifted beneath him: he made lists.

This was the secret of Hale's authority, the thing the junior scribes mistook for genius. Anyone could be clever. Cleverness was cheap in a building full of people who traded in bottled storms and secondhand years. But almost no one was willing to sit still for six hours and write down everything they knew in order of how much it frightened them. Hale had built a career on that unwillingness in others.

His list, in order:

1. One page removed from intake ledger, replaced with forgery. Content unknown.
2. The child. Currently in Holding Room Three, fed, unharmed, singing to herself in a language that made the third-floor seals hum like struck glass.
3. The masters arrive tomorrow for the Centennial Audit. All masks, all weights, all accounts open.
4. If a page is missing from my ledger when they arrive, it will be *my* neck in the balance, not Whitlock's. Provosts are accountable for their ledgers the way captains are accountable for their ships. The sea does not care which sailor dropped the cargo.

He looked at item four for a long time.

Then he added:

5. I know what was on the missing page. Or near enough. I wrote the disposition myself. Whatever Whitlock saved from the blade, it can only be the record of the child's arrival — her words, the seals' response, the things she drew on the holding-room wall in mud before anyone thought to stop her.

He remembered those drawings. He had stood in the doorway of Holding Room Three the first morning and watched the child work, mud to the elbows, utterly absorbed, drawing spirals and branching lines that hurt to look at directly — not because they were ugly but because they were *orderly*, orderly in a way that predated order, like the skeleton of a rule the world had obeyed before anyone wrote rules down. The seals on the wall behind her had glowed faintly, patiently, the way coals breathe.

Hale had ordered the drawings washed off within the hour. He had also, privately, memorized two of them. He could not have said why. He had not tried to say why. Some questions a man keeps in a drawer and does not open.

* * *

At midday he went down to the scriptorium on the pretext of an inspection, which fooled no one, least of all himself.

Theo Whitlock was at his desk in the corner, copying debt entries in his small, careful hand. He was twenty-four, maybe twenty-five, thin in the way of men whose meals were deducted from their wages, with ink permanently shadowed into the creases of his fingers. His father had been a guild man too. His grandfather before him. The Whitlocks were guild property the way the building was guild property — load-bearing, unremarkable, assumed.

"Scribe Whitlock."

Whitlock rose so fast his stool skidded. "Provost."

"You copied the intake entries last night."

"Yes, Provost." Not a flicker. Good posture, lowered eyes, the whole liturgy of servility performed without a single wasted motion. Hale had watched men lie for thirty years and Whitlock was either innocent or the finest liar in the building, and both possibilities annoyed him equally.

"The audit begins tomorrow," Hale said. "Every desk will be examined. Every file weighed. You understand what that means."

"Perfect records, Provost."

"*Perfect* records." Hale let the word hang. "Do you know what the masters weigh, Whitlock? Not paper. Paper is nothing. They weigh *gaps*. A ledger with no gaps tells them everything is in order. A ledger with gaps tells them someone is hiding something worth more than the gap. Do you follow me?"

"I— believe so, Provost."

"There are no gaps in my intake ledger." Hale held his eyes a moment longer than kindness required, then turned and walked the rows of desks, trailing one finger along their edges, collecting dust he didn't look at. Behind him he heard Whitlock sit back down, slowly, the way a man sits when his knees have opinions.

On his way out, Hale paused at the false floor panel in the far corner of the scriptorium — a relic of some earlier provost's paranoia, a hollow space under the tiles big enough for a body, or a box of papers, or a child. He had sealed it himself, years ago, with wax stamped with his own signet.

The wax was intact.

He noted this. He added it to no list. But he noted it.

* * *

That evening, unable to sleep, Hale did the thing he did perhaps twice a year, and hated himself for it each time, and would do it again.

He opened the drawer.

Not a real drawer. A real drawer could be searched. Hale kept his private archive in the one place in the guild no auditor would ever look: inside another ledger, bound and shelved among ten thousand others in the deep stacks, catalogued under a title so boring that curiosity died on contact. *Consolidated Tariff Adjustments, Third Register, Years 41–58.* No one had opened it in forty years. Hale had checked.

Inside, sewn into the binding between pages of tariff nonsense, were eleven documents. Eleven names.

He untied the cord and unfolded the oldest one, soft as cloth now from handling.

*Edmund Hale,* it read, in his own younger handwriting, dated nineteen years ago. *Age six. Cause of record: fever, winter quarter. Disposition: name withdrawn from all registers per paternal request. Fee waived.*

Fee waived. As though mercy were a discount.

Edmund had been his son. Edmund had died in the fever winter like forty other children in the lower city, and Hale — then a rising scribe with a future and a wife who had already gone into the ground the year before — had done the only thing the guild had ever taught him to do with unbearable facts. He had filed them. He had gone to the Registrar of Names and paid to have his own boy erased from every ledger in the city, so that no clerk, no creditor, no well-meaning acquaintance would ever again hand him a condolence note addressed to *Provost Hale and son*, and gut him in a corridor between meetings.

It had worked. That was the horror of it. It had worked perfectly. Within a month, Edmund Hale had never existed, and Hale had discovered that a man can survive anything except being efficient about his own grief.

He had kept the withdrawal slip. Of course he had. It was the only proof left in the world that his son had drawn breath, and he had hidden it inside a book about tariffs, because the guild had taught him that too: everything true must be buried somewhere no one would ever think to dig.

Eleven documents. Eleven names he had personally erased over thirty years — some at masters' orders, some for fees, one, early on, out of simple cruelty he could no longer reconstruct the reasons for. Each one a page cut from someone's life and replaced with smooth blank stock, stitched in with thread a half-shade too dark.

He had recognized the forgery in his intake ledger instantly because he had *made* forgeries like it. That was the joke, if you wanted to call it that. The guild had trained him so thoroughly in disappearance that he could spot his own handiwork in another man's hands.

Hale sat in the stacks a long while, the slip soft in his fingers, listening to the building settle around him.

Here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing he was telling no one now, here, alone, in the dark between shelves: he did not regret the erasure. That was the part that shamed him most. Grief had demanded it, and he had obeyed, and it had *worked*, and some traitorous practical corner of his soul whispered, on nights like this, that the guild's whole terrible machinery ran on exactly that transaction — pain on one side of the counter, silence on the other, everyone walking away lighter and poorer and calling it relief.

But lately — and he could date it precisely, to the morning he had stood in the doorway of Holding Room Three and watched a mud-covered child draw the skeleton of the world on a wall — lately the whisper had changed key. Lately it sounded less like justification and more like a question:

*If a name can be sold and bought and buried, whose name is actually safe?*

*Yours?*

*Edmund's?*

He refolded the slip along its worn creases, tied the cord, shelved the tariff register, and pressed it back flush with its neighbors until no seam showed. Then he stood in the dark of the stacks with his hand flat on the spines, an official face in an unofficial place.

Tomorrow the masters would come down from the Spire in their grey masks, and they would weigh the guild's accounts, and they would find — if he did nothing — a gap in his ledger, and they would ask him about it in that mild patient voice, and he would give them a name, because that was what the machine required: a name given up, a page restored, order maintained. And the easiest name to give them, the cheapest, the one already loaded like a bolt in a crossbow, was *Whitlock*.

Hale turned that over. He was honest enough with himself to admit he was looking for a reason *not* to do it, and honest enough to be suspicious of his own honesty. Sentiment at audit time was how provosts became examples.

But underneath the calculation, something else moved — something with mud on its hands and a dead boy's name on a slip of paper nineteen years old.

*Every century,* the old archivists muttered, when they thought no provost was listening, *the masters go up the Spire and renew the terms, and every century we forget what the terms cost.* Hale had filed those mutterings under superstition for thirty years.

He was beginning, coldly, quietly, to wonder what else had been filed under superstition. And who did the filing. And whether his own signature was anywhere in the margins.

He climbed back up to his office, lit the lamp, and pulled the intake ledger toward him. The forged page lay smooth and blameless under his thumb. He could restore it tonight — cut it out, burn it, write the entry fresh in his own hand, close the gap before the masters ever saw it. Clean. Safe. And Whitlock would never know how close the blade had come, and the child would go up the Spire at dawn after the ceremony, and the terms would renew, and in a hundred years no one would remember any of it, including him.

His pen hovered.

Below, faint through the floor, he could hear it again — that humming from Holding Room Three, rising through stone and timber like water through roots. The seals along his office wall glowed their patient coal-glow. The child was awake. The child was *singing*.

And the song, Hale realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the season, was not random. It repeated. It had structure. It was climbing, note by note, through something — and the seals were answering, and the answers were getting louder, and somewhere above his head, up through nine floors and the archives and the vaults, the Spire itself had begun, very softly, to hum along.

Hale put down the pen.

He reached instead for a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top of it, in his tidy, terrible, official hand, he wrote a single line:

*Inventory of gaps, personal, unsanctioned —*

and then, after a long moment, added a second line beneath it:

*Item one: a page I may not want returned.*

Outside, the black rain began again, and the Spire hummed, and tomorrow the masters were coming.
