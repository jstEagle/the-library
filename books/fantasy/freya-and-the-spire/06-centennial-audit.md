## 6. Centennial Audit

The masters arrived on a Tuesday, which Theo found characteristically cruel of them. Tuesdays were ledger days, the one morning each week when the scriptorium's work was meant to be finished before the noon bell, and now every scribe in the Guild of Impossible Things was instead polishing brass fittings, beating dust out of curtains that had not been dusted since the last audit a hundred years ago—give or take the guild's flexible relationship with arithmetic—and pretending, with the desperate energy of the condemned, that nothing in this building had ever gone wrong.

"They're coming down the Spire road," announced Pem, the youngest copyist, who had appointed himself lookout by virtue of having the window desk. "I can see the carriages. There are seven of them."

"Seven," repeated Old Cassia, who had survived four audits and spoke of them the way sailors speak of storms they'd been dismasted in. "Last time it was five. The century must have been profitable."

"It's always profitable," said Pem.

"That," said Cassia, "is what worries me about you, boy."

Theo said nothing. Theo was very good at saying nothing; it was practically his trade. He stood at his desk with a rag in one hand and a bottle of guild-standard ink in the other, buffing the wood to a shine that would have made his mother proud, if he could still remember her face well enough to know what pride looked like on it. He had sold that particular memory three weeks ago, and some mornings he caught himself reaching for it the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth.

Three years of his life, traded for closing an inquiry. The transaction had been logged with what he could only describe as cheerfulness. *Asset acquired: Whitlock, T., three (3) years, condition: gently used.* Somewhere upstairs, in a vault he would never see, those years sat in a drawer like coins.

He put the thought away. He was getting better at putting thoughts away. It was, he reflected, the single most marketable skill the guild had ever taught him.

* * *

Freya had been in the false floor since dawn.

They had refined the arrangement over the past month, the way one refines any dangerous habit. The compartment beneath the scriptorium's eastern row of desks had been built—according to Cassia, who remembered everything except why she remembered it—for storing records too valuable to destroy and too embarrassing to keep. It was shallow, cold, and smelled of old glue, but it held a girl of nine years comfortably enough if she curled up, and it held her drawings, her scraps of translated alphabet, and the true intake page Theo had hidden there a lifetime ago, all of it tucked beneath a floorboard that sat flush and innocent under a desk belonging to a scribe named Dorrit who had died owing the guild eleven years and whose file nobody opened anymore.

Before she went down each morning, Freya performed a ritual that Theo had come to dread and rely upon in equal measure. She would stand very straight, look him in the eye, and say, in her own language first and then in the Common Tongue he had taught her word by word:

"I will be quiet as a closed book."

And he would answer, because the ritual required it:

"And I will be brave as an open one."

She had invented the second half herself. He suspected she had invented it specifically to make him live up to it.

This morning, as the carriage wheels began to grind audibly up the Spire road, she had paused at the edge of the compartment, mud-brown eyes enormous in her thin face, and asked the question she asked every morning, though today it landed differently.

"If they find me, what happens?"

"The same thing that happens if they don't," Theo said. "Eventually. But later, if they don't. Later is the whole art of being alive in this building."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she lay down among the dead debtor's papers, pulled the board over herself, and said, muffled, "Closed book."

"Open one," Theo whispered, and pressed the floorboard home.

* * *

The masters came into the scriptorium at mid-morning, and the room changed temperature.

There were seven of them, robed in grey so deep it seemed to drink the lamplight, their faces covered by masks of white porcelain worked to resemble blank pages—smooth, featureless, with only the faintest suggestion of lines where a face might have been written. They moved without hurry. They did not need to hurry. Hurry, Theo understood, was for people who could be refused.

Provost Hale walked beside them, and here was the thing that unsettled Theo most: Hale, who ruled the lower floors with a voice like a filing cabinet slamming, had gone quiet. Not calm—quiet. There was a difference. Calm is a choice. Quiet is what happens to a man walking beside something that could erase him with a signature.

"Scribes," Hale announced, "you will continue your work. You will not speak unless addressed. You will not look directly at a master's mask. You will render whatever is asked of you, completely and accurately." A pause, thin as paper. "Accuracy is the whole of your value."

*And the whole of my danger*, Theo did not think, because thinking it felt like writing it down somewhere a master could read.

The audit itself was simple in mechanism and monstrous in implication. Each master took a section of the ledgers and began reading—not skimming, *reading*, running one gloved finger down column after column of entries while a junior clerk trembled beside them with the matching files. And as they read, they weighed. That was the only word for it. When a master paused on an entry—a bottled storm from the harvest of some debt-ridden farm, twenty years of a cooper's life, a widow's memory of her wedding day—the master would lift the corresponding asset from its case, hold it a moment, and murmur a figure to the clerk. The clerk wrote the figure down. The figure was never disputed. Nothing in the room suggested that the figures were anything but holy.

Theo bent over his desk and copied. That was his assigned task during audits: to produce, in fair hand, a fresh summary of the quarter's acquisitions, so that the masters could compare the new record against the old and confirm that no entry had been altered, added, or—guild forbid—removed. His pen scratched. His hand was steady. His hand was always steady; he had trained it to be steadier than his heart, and on days like this the training held.

Mostly.

Because underneath the copying, underneath the servility worn smooth as river stone, a different exercise was running, one he had practiced in the dark hours: *notice everything, write nothing.* The third master from the left favored his right side—he was carrying something heavy in a sleeve pocket, which meant he expected to collect assets personally today. The fifth master had lingered over the letter M in the index longer than any letter deserved. And the lead master, the tall one whose mask bore no lines at all, not even the suggestion, had spent four full minutes on a page near the front of the intake ledger.

Near the place where a certain page used to be.

Theo copied the next line. His handwriting did not waver. Inside, behind the stillness, something screamed in a very small, controlled voice, like a mouse in a jar.

* * *

It happened at the noon bell, or rather it failed to happen until the noon bell, and Theo would spend the rest of his life grateful for the accident of timing.

The tall master finished with the intake ledger. He set it down with a softness worse than any slam. And then, instead of moving on, he began to walk the rows of desks.

This was not standard. Cassia's four audits had featured no such procession, and Theo saw her knuckles whiten around her pen, saw Pem's eyes go wide and fixed on his blotter with the discipline of a boy trying to become furniture. The master drifted along the eastern row—Dorrit's desk, where the floorboard lay, where a girl was lying beneath forty years of a dead man's unopened shame—and paused there just long enough to make Theo's blood forget its job.

Then he moved on. Past Dorrit. Past Cassia. To Theo.

The master stopped.

Up close, the blankness of the mask was worse than any expression could have been. There was nothing to read, so Theo's mind supplied readings the way a frightened man supplies footsteps in an empty house. The mask tilted slightly, considering him, the way one considers a page that might contain an error.

"You are Whitlock," the master said. The voice was neither male nor female, young nor old; it sounded like words read aloud by someone who had long ago stopped needing to mean them.

"Yes, Master."

"Your family name appears in our records with unusual frequency."

"We have been," Theo said, "an indebted family for some generations, Master."

"Mm." The mask turned toward the desk. Toward the inkwell. And then the master did the thing that stopped Theo's heart mid-beat: he leaned down, close to the inkwell, and breathed in. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a hound breathes over a trail.

"This is not guild-standard ink," the master said.

The scriptorium went silent around them. Not quiet—silent. Every pen in the room had stopped. Theo could hear the noon bell finishing its last stroke somewhere above, could hear the dust settling, could hear his own pulse tapping at the inside of his skull like a creditor at a door.

"There has been," Theo heard himself say, "a supply problem, Master. The standard stock ran short in the autumn. I supplemented from private purchase rather than let the summary fall behind schedule."

A lie. A clean, complete, catastrophic lie, delivered in the flat voice of perfect servility, and the worst part—the part that would keep him awake for years—was how *good* he had gotten at it. The guild had taught him this too. Everything the guild touched, it taught.

"Private purchase." The master straightened. The mask regarded him. Somewhere under the porcelain, a mind was doing arithmetic, and Theo was a variable in it. "Show me the bottle."

Theo's hand moved before his fear could veto it. He opened the desk drawer, took out the small brown bottle he kept there for exactly this contingency—he had prepared the lie's props a week ago, rehearsing deception the way other men rehearse prayers—and set it on the desk.

The master picked it up. Turned it. Held it to the light.

And Theo understood, with the strange floating clarity of a man watching his own execution from the gallery, that the bottle contained exactly what it claimed: ordinary purchased ink, the cheap iron-gall sort scribes bought in the lower city. Because the *real* ink—the ink the master had smelled, or thought he had smelled—was not in the bottle at all. It was downstairs, in a dead man's file, on a hidden page written in Freya's borrowed alphabet, in marks that predated every kingdom and glowed faintly when the oldest seals woke to hear them.

The master set the bottle down.

"In future," the master said, "use the guild's ink. Your summaries will be compared against the originals line by line this afternoon. Discrepancies in hand, color, or composition will be noted." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Noted *thoroughly*."

"Yes, Master."

The mask held on him one moment longer. Then the tall master moved on down the row, robes whispering, and took up the next ledger as though nothing had occurred, and the scriptorium exhaled all at once, fifty pens resuming their scratching like rain starting again after a held breath.

Cassia did not look at him. Pem did not look at him. Nobody looked at anybody, which in the grammar of the Guild of Impossible Things was the loudest possible statement of *we saw nothing, we are nothing, please let us remain nothing.*

Theo copied the next line. His hand was steady.

Underneath the desk, beneath the floorboard, in the dark that smelled of glue and old paper, he knew—did not believe, *knew*—that Freya was lying perfectly still, breathing through her mouth the way he had taught her, quiet as a closed book. And he sat above her in the open light, performing bravery, and waited for the afternoon.

* * *

The line-by-line comparison began at two o'clock and was, by some mercy Theo did not trust, uneventful. His summaries matched. His forged provenance for the ink bottle went unexamined. By four, the masters had concluded their weighing, murmured their figures, and ascended back up toward the Spire in their seven carriages, taking with them three crates of collected assets—including, Theo noticed with a lurch, the entire memory-inventory of a debtor named Sorrel, whose file he knew, whose face he had seen in the corridors, a woman who had sold memories piecemeal for years and was now, presumably, about to become a great deal less herself.

Hale dismissed the scriptorium at dusk with a speech about diligence. His eyes found Theo's for half a second across the room—half a second, no more—and in them Theo read something he had never seen there before. Not suspicion. Suspicion he could have handled; suspicion was weather.

It was calculation.

That night, after the lamps were trimmed and the wardens had made their rounds, Theo knelt on the cold boards and lifted the floorboard, and Freya blinked up at him, stiff and bleary, and whispered, "Did I do it right? Was I a closed book?"

"You were perfect," Theo said, and meant it, and hated how easily the word came.

But as he helped her out, stiff-limbed, into the lamplight, she caught his hands and stilled, the way she did when the old language stirred in her, and said quietly:

"One of them stopped. Above me. I heard the breathing. Like someone reading a page very slowly." Her fingers tightened. "Theo—it wasn't reading me. It was listening. The way you listen to a door to hear if anyone's behind it."

Theo looked at the floorboard. At the honest, innocent, flush-seated wood.

And in the morning, when he reported to his desk, there was a slip of guild paper waiting on his blotter, written in a hand he recognized as Hale's, containing a single line:

*Whitlock. My office. Bring nothing. Speak of nothing. — H.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in ink that was definitely not guild-standard:

*I know what sleeps under Dorrit's desk.*
