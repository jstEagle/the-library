## 7. The Price of a Name

The summons came at the worst possible hour, which is to say any hour at all.

"Theo Whitlock," said the page-boy, a nervous child with ink on his chin and a slip of vellum in his fist. "The masters wish to see you. Chamber Nine. Now."

"Now," Theo repeated. It was not a question so much as a prayer that the boy would take it back.

"Now," the boy confirmed, and fled, clearly grateful to be delivering bad news to someone else for once.

Theo sat very still at his desk in the scriptorium, quill hovering over an entry he was copying — *one summer afternoon, lightly used, belonging to a cooper's widow, transferred to the estate of Master Ellery-Vane* — and listened to his own heart doing something undignified against his ribs.

Below him, in the false floor beneath the third archive shelf, Freya was asleep. He had tucked her in an hour ago with a blanket stolen from the linen closet and a promise that he'd bring supper. She had drawn him a picture before drifting off: two stick figures under a sun drawn as a spiral, with letters around the border in her old, impossible alphabet. He had been teaching himself to read them by lamplight, letter by patient letter, like a man learning to swim in the dark. The word along the top, he was fairly sure now, meant *stay*.

He could not stay. He was wanted in Chamber Nine.

* * *

Chamber Nine was where the guild conducted its gentler business. Not the confiscations, not the erasures — those happened below, behind doors you could feel humming if you pressed your palm against them, like a hive of very organized bees. Chamber Nine was for *transactions*. Voluntary ones, technically. The guild was very proud of the word *voluntary*, the way a cat is proud of the word *gift* when it leaves a dead bird on your pillow.

The master waiting there wore the silver half-mask of the Second Circle, its surface polished to a mirror that showed Theo a small, bent version of himself, holding his cap in both hands like a man at a funeral.

"Scribe Whitlock." The master's voice was warm. That was the worst part. Everything about the guild's higher ranks was warm — the lamplight, the upholstered chairs, the decanter of wine on the side table. Warmth was what they did instead of kindness. "Sit. Please."

Theo sat. The chair was the most comfortable thing he had touched in eleven years, and he hated it instantly, because he understood it was bait.

"You've been with us since you were fourteen," the master said, consulting a folder that Theo knew contained his entire existence, itemized. "Apprenticed at nine. Your father's debt, assumed at his death, plus accrued interest, plus the cost of your keep, your schooling, your ink, your boots — ah, yes, the boots, we do log the boots — comes to a sum that, frankly, neither of us expects you to work off in a natural lifetime."

"No, Master."

"And yet." The master folded his hands. "And yet here is an interesting thing. During yesterday's audit inquiry, a discrepancy was noted. A missing page. An intake record, unaccounted for. And the inquiry has developed a habit of circling, like a moth around a lamp, around one particular desk in the scriptorium. Yours."

Theo kept his face still. Eleven years of practice. His face could have taught a course.

"I wouldn't know anything about a missing page, Master."

"Of course not." The master smiled behind the mask; you could hear it in the voice, a soft folding of sound. "Of course not. But inquiries are ugly things. They pull files. They interview colleagues. They measure ink. And they are scheduled to continue tomorrow morning, first bell, unless something happens to close them."

The decanter caught the light. Theo looked at it rather than at the mask, because looking at the mask meant looking at himself, and he couldn't afford that right now.

"What would close them?" he asked, and heard in his own voice exactly how much he already knew the answer.

"A settlement." The master slid a single sheet across the desk. It was written in the guild's transactional hand, all flourishes and small print, and Theo read it the way you read a diagnosis. *The undersigned offers three (3) years of recorded life, drawn from the earliest entries, in exchange for immediate closure of Audit Inquiry 44-C and all associated records.* At the bottom, a line waited for his signature, and above the line, in red, the words he'd copied ten thousand times for other people: *voluntary*.

Three years. He tried to think about what three years even was. Three years was — what? He was twenty-five. Three years back put him at twenty-two, and twenty-two was —

Twenty-two was his mother teaching him to make her honey-bread in the little kitchen above the chandler's shop, flour on her nose, laughing because he'd salted it instead of sugar. Twenty-two was —

No. Wait. That wasn't twenty-two. That was younger. That was fifteen, sixteen. When had she —

He reached for it the way you reach for a name on the tip of your tongue, and found the shape of her — small, quick, an apron, a laugh like — like —

Like nothing. Like a door painted on a wall.

His hands had gone cold. The master watched him with the patience of a fisherman watching a float.

"The earliest entries," the master said gently, "are always the dearest. Childhood, family, first loves. The guild finds them very valuable. Sentiment appreciates, you see. But I want to be honest with you, Scribe Whitlock, because honesty is a policy of this house: three years from the earliest entries will not leave you gutted. You will lose some color. A face, perhaps. A smell. A Sunday. The mind is generous; it papers over the gaps. Most sellers report only a vague sense of having been somewhere once."

*Most sellers.* Theo thought of the cooper's widow whose summer afternoon he'd copied that morning. He thought of the dead debtor whose file now held Freya's true intake page, a man named Oskar Vell who had sold so much of himself that at the end he'd reportedly asked strangers to remind him of his own middle name.

"And if I don't sign," Theo said, "the inquiry continues."

"The inquiry continues," the master agreed pleasantly. "Inquiries are thorough. They find pages. They find girls, sometimes, when they look hard enough." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Though of course there is no girl. There is no missing page. There is nothing at all, if the ledger says there isn't. That is the whole genius of a ledger, Scribe Whitlock. It doesn't describe the world. It *is* the world."

Theo looked down at the contract. Somewhere beneath his feet, three floors down, a child was asleep in a false floor, trusting him. Trusting him with the specific, total trust of someone who has nowhere else, which is either the purest trust in the world or the cruelest kind to be given.

He thought: *I am already owned. Every hour of my day belongs to them. What exactly am I protecting?*

He thought: *Her name is Freya. If I forget everything else, I will make sure I don't forget that.*

He picked up the pen.

"One condition," he said.

The master tilted his head, amused. "Buyers don't usually negotiate."

"I want the closure in writing. Inquiry 44-C, closed, sealed, all associated records destroyed — not archived, *destroyed*. Before I sign. Before the ink dries on my signature, I want to watch you burn the inquiry file."

Something flickered behind the mask — surprise, maybe, or the particular irritation of a craftsman whose mark has been second-guessed. But the guild honored its contracts; that was the load-bearing wall of their whole cathedral. A guild that welched on a deal was just thieves with stationery, and thieves could be hanged.

"Agreed," the master said.

They burned the file together. It took less time than Theo expected — one thin folder, a handful of pages, a clerk's summary in a neat round hand. The fire in Chamber Nine's grate ate it in under a minute. Theo stood and watched every page curl, and felt nothing like victory. It felt like paying a ransom. You got the hostage back, and you also learned exactly what your child was worth to the men holding her, and both truths lived in you forever after.

Then he signed.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about selling years of your life, or perhaps what they tell you and you don't believe until it happens:

It doesn't hurt.

That's the horror of it. A clerk — a pleasant, apologetic woman with ink-stained fingers, brought in from the Reconciliation Office — pressed a brass instrument to his temple that hummed like a struck tuning fork, and somewhere behind his eyes a drawer opened, and something was lifted out with the care of a librarian handling a rare edition, and it did not hurt at all. It felt like the moment when you walk into another room and forget why you came. It felt like that, stretched over three years.

"There," the clerk said softly. "All done. You may feel slightly — untethered. It passes."

She made a note. The guild logged gleefully; that was the phrase Theo had always privately used for it, though never aloud. The transaction went into the great ledgers with a flourish and a date and, he noticed, a small notation of *quality: excellent, early-period material* — as if his mother's kitchen were a vintage of wine.

He walked out of Chamber Nine lighter. That was not a metaphor. He checked. His boots hit the marble with less weight than they'd carried on the way in, as if the years had been ballast sewn into him and someone had unpicked the seams. He felt the absence of them the way you feel a missing tooth: not pain, exactly, but a wrongness whenever your tongue — your attention — went looking.

In the corridor outside, he stopped and did inventory, the way the clerk had suggested. He knew his own name. Good. He knew the guild, his debt, his desk, his duties. He knew the rain last night, black and loud, and a knock at the iron door, and —

Freya. Her name arrived intact, whole, defended, as if some stubborn garrison in his mind had refused to surrender it. *Freya, age approximately eight, mud to the knees, speaks the old tongue, draws spirals, calls the Spire the root of the tower.* All of it there. He nearly laughed with relief in the empty corridor, and then the relief curdled, because he tested further:

Mother. Apron. Kitchen. Laugh.

Nothing. Not a blank, precisely — blanks would have been honest. Worse: a smooth, plausible surface where the memory should be, like ice over water. He knew he had a mother. Everyone had a mother. He knew she had died — the debt file said so, the debt file was thorough — but the file was all he had. He knew her the way he knew the kings of a country he'd never visited: as an entry. *Widow Maren-adjacent — no. No, that was someone else. Widow —*

Her name was gone too. He hadn't even thought to check for her name until it wasn't there.

Theo stood in the corridor of the Guild of Impossible Things, in the best-lit hallway in the building, and cried the way scribes cry: silently, efficiently, without smudging anything important. Then he wiped his face on his sleeve, straightened his collar, and went down to the linen closet to steal a second blanket, because the nights were getting colder and the false floor was drafty, and grief is no excuse for a child catching chill.

* * *

Supper that night was bread, hard cheese, and two apples of dubious provenance, eaten cross-legged on the floor of the scriptorium by lamplight while the rest of the guild slept.

"You look strange," Freya said. She said it with the devastating directness of children and cats. "Lighter."

Theo froze mid-chew. "What do you mean, lighter?"

She squinted at him, head tipped, the way she did when reading her drawings. In the old tongue, she murmured something — two syllables, soft — and the lamplight leaned toward him, just slightly, like a plant toward a window. Then she frowned harder.

"There's a hole in you," she announced. "A you-shaped hole. Near the beginning." She patted her own chest, indicating roughly where childhoods were kept. "Everyone has a beginning. Yours has a piece torn out. Like a page."

*Like a page.* Of course she'd say it that way. Of course the child who was a living sentence would diagnose him in bookkeeping terms.

"I sold it," Theo said. He hadn't planned to say it. It simply came out, the way confessions do at lamplit hours. "Three years. To close an inquiry. To buy time. To keep you safe."

Freya considered this with enormous seriousness, chewing her cheese. Then she set down the apple she'd been hoarding — he saw her do it, deliberately, the way you'd set down a coin — and crawled across the floor and hugged him. It was an inexpert hug, all elbows, delivered with the force of someone mooring a boat.

"In my language," she said into his shoulder, "there isn't a word for selling years. Because nobody would ever." She pulled back, frowning at the gap in his grammar as if it offended her personally. "But there IS a word for giving them. It's the same word as *planting*. You give a thing to the ground and it isn't gone. It comes up later as something else."

"That's lovely," Theo said, "and also I'm fairly sure what I did was the opposite of planting."

"You don't know," Freya said, with the serene authority of a prophet or an eight-year-old, which are often the same thing. "You haven't seen what grows."

She retrieved her apple, settled back against the archive shelf, and was asleep within minutes, mid-sentence, the way children are — one moment arguing theology, the next gone, breathing slow, one hand curled around a stub of charcoal.

Theo sat a while longer. Below the floorboards, the city slept. Above the city, the Spire stood against the stars, vast and silent, wearing its century of secrets. Somewhere in his chest, where three years used to live, the ice-sheet lay smooth and plausible, and he found that if he didn't press on it, he could almost pretend nothing was missing.

Almost.

He took out the drawing she'd made him — the two stick figures under the spiral sun — and turned it toward the lamp, and worked at the border letters again, patient as tide. *Stay,* the top line said. He was learning the alphabet one stolen hour at a time, and tonight the letters seemed to come easier, as if the language itself had decided to meet him halfway, as if it approved of what he'd paid.

Near dawn, copying a routine entry to keep his hands busy — *one memory of a wedding, secondhand, slightly faded, seller reports no longer recognizing the bride* — Theo's quill stopped.

Because the audit inquiry was closed. Sealed. Burned. He had watched the ashes.

So why, in the margin of the morning's assignment sheet, in the provost Hale's narrow, precise hand, was there a single instruction, dated after the burning?

*Whitlock — prepare the Vell file for transfer to the Spire archive. All of it. By week's end.*

The dead debtor's file. The file with a certain hidden page sewn into its spine.

Theo read it three times. Outside, the black rain began again, tapping at the glass like a knock.
