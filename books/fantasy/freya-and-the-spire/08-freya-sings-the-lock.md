## 8. Freya Sings the Lock

The scriptorium at night smelled of ink and tallow and the particular loneliness of a room full of other people's words.

Freya had learned the smell of it over eleven nights now. She knew which floorboards creaked (the third one from the hearth, always; she had named it Old Creak in her head, because everything deserved a name), which quills were good and which had been chewed by some scribe long dead, and which of the sleeping mats behind the false floor still held the shape of Theo Whitlock's shoulders when he lay down beside her to whisper translations by candlelight.

*House*, he'd say, tapping her drawing of a crooked little building. *That means house.*

*Haus*, she'd answer, and then say it properly, in the old tongue, and watch him shiver.

He shivered every time. Freya had decided this was not fear exactly. It was more like what happened to a person who had spent his whole life drinking rainwater and was suddenly handed honey. He didn't know what to do with his mouth.

Tonight Theo was gone — summoned upstairs by a provost with a voice like a filing drawer, which was how Freya thought of Provost Hale, all sharp edges and no warmth, a man made entirely of ledgers. The masters had come for their Centennial Audit, whatever that meant, and the whole guild had gone stiff as a dried eel. Theo had pressed one finger to his lips before he left, then pointed at the false floor, then at her, then at his own eyes: *stay hidden, stay quiet, I'll come back.*

She intended to do exactly that. Truly.

But the scriptorium was so quiet, and the quiet had a hole in it.

Freya had noticed the hole three nights ago. There was a place in the room where sound went wrong — near the back wall, past the shelf of ledgers nobody ever opened, where the air felt thick the way water feels just before it freezes. When she walked past it, her own footsteps arrived at her ears a half-beat late, like they were being checked at a door before being allowed through.

A sealed thing. She knew seals. She had been raised on them, or on the memory of them, in the tower that wasn't there anymore. Seals sang, if you knew how to listen. Every lock in the world was a song holding its breath.

She hadn't meant to open anything. That part was true, and later, when everything was terrible, she would hold onto it like a rope: *I didn't mean to.* She had only meant to listen.

She knelt by the back wall, past the shelf of sleeping ledgers, and put her palm flat against the stone. Cold. But underneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat under a blanket, something hummed.

It hummed in her language.

Not the whole of it — a fragment, a scrap, the way you might find one bead from a necklace you lost years ago and know instantly it belonged to your mother. The seal down there was singing an old word, a binding-word, one of the first ones children learned in the tower before the tower fell out of the sky and into history:

*Shut. Shut. Stay shut.*

Freya listened to it the way you'd listen to someone snoring. And then, without deciding to, without any plan at all, she answered it.

She hummed.

Just the opening phrase — the greeting-phrase, the one you used to wake a door politely instead of kicking it. *Open-if-openable, friend-if-friendable.* Her grandmother had sung it to the pantry. Her teacher had sung it to the archive doors of the glass tower, doors tall as ten men, and the doors would sigh and swing and smell of starlight and dust.

The wall under her hand went warm.

Then it went *loose*, in a way walls are not supposed to go loose, and a seam appeared in the stone — thin as a hair, then wide as a finger, then wide enough that Freya scrambled backward on her knees with her heart banging like a dropped pot.

The seam became a door. The door swung inward without a sound, which somehow frightened her more than if it had groaned, and cold clean air breathed out of the dark beyond it, smelling of nothing at all. Not dust. Not age. Just *nothing*, the way the space between stars smells, if stars have spaces between them, which Freya suspected they did.

"Oh," she said aloud, in guild-speech, because it seemed like the appropriate language for trouble. "Oh, no."

The door waited. Doors, in her experience, were very patient, which was why people trusted them with secrets.

She should close it. She should sing it shut again and never tell anyone, especially not Theo, especially not the provost with the filing-drawer voice, and spend the rest of her life being a very small, very forgettable girl in a very large guild.

But.

But she had spent her whole life — both of them, the remembered one and this one — being told what things meant by people who kept the meanings locked up. And here was a door that had opened *for her*, unasked, in a building full of people who owned locks the way spiders owned webs.

"Friend-if-friendable," Freya whispered, and crawled inside.

* * *

There were stairs. Of course there were stairs. There were always stairs; stairs were the universe's way of making sure you really wanted something.

They went down farther than the guild had floors, which Freya found rude. She counted forty-one steps and then stopped counting because counting felt too much like promising. The walls were smooth stone without a single torch bracket, and yet she could see — dimly, gently, the way you can see your own hands underwater — because the old tongue lived in the stone here, soaked into it, glowing the faintest grey-green like moss that had learned patience.

At the bottom: a room.

Not a vault, quite. A library that had given up pretending. Shelves cut directly into the rock, and on them — Freya's breath caught — *books*. Real ones, bound in leather the color of dried plums, spines stamped with characters she knew the way you know your own name in a crowd.

Her language. Whole shelves of it. Rows and rows and rows.

The guild could not read these. She understood that instantly, the way you understand a joke is a joke even when it isn't funny. These books had been sitting down here in the dark beneath the scriptorium — beneath the scribes, beneath the provosts, beneath the whole Guild of Impossible Things with its bottled storms and traded memories — and not one soul above her had ever read a single word.

They had been keeping a library they couldn't open. Guarding a door they couldn't unlock. For a hundred years, or two hundred, or however long it took for a guild to grow fat and forget why its cellar hummed.

"They've been sitting on us," Freya said to the empty room, and was surprised at how angry her voice came out. "Like a hen. A great smug hen sitting on eggs it can't even—"

She stopped, because one spine had caught her eye.

Most of the books were plain. This one was not. Its cover bore a stamp pressed deep into the leather — a sigil, a maker's mark, the kind of thing a family puts on its work so the work can be blamed on them properly.

Two keys crossed over a closed eye.

And beneath the sigil, in careful, confident, old-tongue lettering, a name.

Freya knew names. Names were her whole trade, lately; the guild collected them like a miser collected coins. She sounded this one out slowly, translating in her head into the guild-speech Theo had taught her, syllable by syllable, expecting nothing, feeling only the pleasant scholar's thrill of reading something forbidden—

*Whitlock.*

The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

No. It was an old book. Old books had old names in them; names got reused; Theo had said so himself once, complaining about a debtor whose grandfather had also been a debtor, "debt runs in families here like brown eyes." Plenty of people were called Whitlock. It probably wasn't even a real name, just a—

But the sigil. Two keys crossed over a closed eye. And below the name, smaller, in the cramped notation seal-makers used for their credentials, a line that translated itself in her head whether she wanted it to or not:

*Forgers of the Binding. Keepers of the Terms. Paid in perpetuity.*

Paid in perpetuity.

Freya stood very still in the humming dark, and the library hummed back, and somewhere far above her Theo Whitlock was walking home through the black rain with candle-smoke in his clothes, tired, kind, shivering at her language like a man tasting honey — and his family name was stamped on the cover of a book about locking things away.

She opened the cover with fingers that did not feel like hers.

* * *

The handwriting inside was beautiful. That was the worst part. Whoever had written it had loved the writing — loved the curves of the letters, the discipline of the lines — the same way Theo loved his copying, the same way he smoothed each page flat with his palm before setting a single word down, the same way he'd said *careful, careful* the first time he let her touch a quill, gentle as a nurse with a bird.

The first pages were records. Boring ones. Measurements of a thing — the entries never said *what*, only *it*, *the subject*, *the sealed* — its depth, its temperature, the pitch of its humming, taken season after season, year after year, in a patient looping hand.

*The subject sings less this winter,* one entry noted. *Terms hold.*

*The subject has begun repeating a phrase. We do not translate it. Translation invites conversation.*

Freya turned pages faster.

The entries changed hands over the decades — different loops, different slants, generations of the same family trained into the same beautiful script — but the substance never changed. Feed the terms. Check the seal. Do not speak to it. Do not translate it. And every hundred years, in red ink, a single line:

*Renewal due. Speaker required.*

Speaker required.

Freya's mouth went dry. She looked at the phrase for a long time. Around it, the neat looping hand grew slightly less neat, as though the writer's own fingers had flinched.

*Renewal due. Speaker required. The child arrived Tuesday. She has her mother's ear for it. This is the part of the work we do not write poems about.*

*This is the part of the work we are paid for.*

*Paid in perpetuity.*

Freya closed the book.

She sat down on the cold stone floor of a library no one had entered in a century, hugging the plum-colored ledger to her chest the way you'd hug the only warm thing in a freezing house, and she did not cry, because crying was loud and loudness was dangerous, and also because the tears wouldn't come. What came instead was worse: a memory.

Theo, three nights ago, cross-legged on the false floor, tracing the shape of her drawings with one careful finger. *You're safe here,* he'd said. *I know it doesn't look like much. But nobody finds anybody in the scriptorium. It's the one place in this whole guild where things get forgotten on purpose.*

Safe. In a guild whose cellar held a book with his name on it, a book that said, in her own language, in letters lovelier than any she'd seen outside the tower: *we take a child, every hundred years, and we are paid forever.*

Maybe it wasn't him. Maybe the name was a coincidence, a borrowed name, a cousin-line gone cold. Maybe the Whitlocks who forged the seal had died out centuries ago and the name had drifted like a leaf on water until it landed, meaningless, on a soft-eyed scribe who chewed his quills and forgot to eat and shivered at the sound of the old tongue like it was honey.

Maybe.

But Freya had grown up in a tower of glass where every word had weight, where you learned early that names were not decorations — they were *handles*, and whoever held your handle could lift you, turn you, set you down wherever they pleased. And the book in her arms said the Whitlock handle had been gripping the lock on a prison for longer than the guild had existed.

The seal-hum in the walls shifted. Very slightly. Almost like breathing.

Almost like something down here, deeper than the library, deeper than the stairs, turning over in a sleep that had lasted a hundred years and noticing — noticing *her* — the way a sleeper notices a candle lit in the next room.

Freya went very still.

*Don't talk to it,* the ledger had warned, in Theo's ancestors' lovely hand. *Translation invites conversation.*

But the humming was saying something. Faintly, brokenly, the way a bird sings after years alone in a cage — half the notes missing, the phrases worn down to nubs. It took her a long moment to catch the shape of it, and when she did, she wished she hadn't, because it wasn't a threat. It wasn't a curse. It wasn't any of the things the guild's locked door had promised.

It was a question. The same question, repeated, worn smooth as a pebble from a hundred years of asking:

*Is someone there? Is someone finally there?*

Above her, faint through forty-one steps and a hairline door and a shelf of sleeping ledgers, Freya heard the scriptorium's third floorboard creak.

Old Creak.

Theo was home.

She looked down at the ledger in her arms — at the crossed keys, the closed eye, the beautiful handwriting of his blood — and for the first time since the black-rain night at the iron door, Freya was afraid of the person coming down the stairs to find her.

She did not hide the book. Some part of her, a stubborn tower-part, refused. If he was a jailer's son, he would be a jailer's son standing in the open, with the evidence in the light.

She heard his footsteps pause at the top of the hidden stair. Heard his breath change — that sharp little inhale he did whenever the world surprised him, which lately was often.

"Freya?" His voice echoed down, warm and worried and utterly ordinary. "Are you down there? The door was open, I—"

He saw the light of the old tongue glowing up the stairwell. He saw her face, whatever it was doing. He stopped.

And Freya, keeper of a language older than kingdoms, holder of questions worn smooth as pebbles, asked him the only thing she could ask:

"Theo," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate, "what did your family *do*?"
