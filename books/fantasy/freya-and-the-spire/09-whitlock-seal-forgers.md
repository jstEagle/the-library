## 9. Whitlock, Seal-Forgers

I had always assumed that if the guild ever came for me, it would come with wardens and wax seals and a great deal of paperwork. That was how the guild did everything—violence first, then the forms to make it legal.

It had not occurred to me that it might come as a child standing in the doorway of my cell of a room at the top of the scriptorium stairs, holding a ledger older than the guild itself, her eyes wet with something far worse than tears.

"Freya," I said carefully, setting down my pen. Ink, I have learned, is a dangerous thing to spill in moments like these. "You're supposed to be behind the false floor."

"I was." She stepped inside and closed the door behind her, which she had never once done in all our weeks together. Freya treated doors as suggestions. "But I found something, Theo. And I couldn't stay down there with it."

"Found what? Did someone hear you? Are they searching already?"

She didn't answer any of it. She just stood there in the lamplight, mud still faintly crusted at her hem from some journey she'd never explained, holding the book against her chest the way you hold something that has bitten you.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The ledger was not guild-standard. Anyone could see that at a glance. Guild bindings were grey calfskin, stamped with the seal of the scales; this was bound in leather gone dark as old blood, stitched with thread that caught the light wrong, the way certain things from before the Spire's shadow always seemed to. The pages were not paper. They were something thinner and tougher, like skin that had decided to become vellum out of professional ambition.

And it was written in her language.

I had spent weeks learning that language from Freya's drawings—the looping characters that looked like rivers deciding to be letters, the grammar that ran sometimes left to right and sometimes, maddeningly, inward, toward the heart of the word. I could read perhaps one sentence in ten without asking her. This ledger, I could read almost none of.

But I knew my own name when I saw it.

There it was, near the beginning, in a hand so confident it looked less written than carved: *Whitlock.* And beneath it, more Whitlocks. A column of them. Generations of them, each entry longer than the last, each signed with the same mark—a small spiral, closed at its center, like a snail shell or a fist.

"What does it say?" I asked. My voice came out very flat, the way voices do when they are bracing themselves.

Freya looked up at me. She was nine years old, or thereabouts, though she had told me once that where she came from nobody counted years the way we did—they counted sentences, and she was only partway through hers. Right then she looked about four hundred.

"It says," she said slowly, choosing each translated word the way you choose stepping stones across a flooded street, "*the House of Whitlock, keepers of the binding, servants of the Spire, paid in perpetuity.*"

The lamp guttered. Or maybe I did.

"That's—" I laughed, because laughing is what cowards do when the alternative is screaming. "That's a mistake. That's some other Whitlock. There were Whitlocks everywhere once. Bakers. Boatmen. One of them allegedly wrestled a bear."

"Theo." She said my name the way the provost said *debtor*. Not cruelly. Just finally. "Read the next line."

I read the next line.

I will not pretend I understood all of it. But the shape of it went through me like cold water through cloth. It spoke of a voice. A child's voice, taken down beneath the earth, set into the root of the tower, wound into the words that held the sealed thing still. It spoke of the price agreed upon for that voice: protection, position, precedence. It spoke of renewal every hundred years, and of the family whose duty—and privilege—it was to arrange the renewal.

The first entry was dated, by the old reckoning, one hundred and ninety-nine years ago.

"Freya," I said. "Who was the child?"

She didn't answer. She turned the page instead, and there, in the margin beside the oldest entry, was a drawing—not in the formal hand of the ledger but quick, childish, done by someone who had been given a stylus to occupy themselves while the adults talked. A little figure with wild hair, standing in a circle of letters.

Beneath it, a name, in the old tongue.

"You know this name," Freya said quietly. "You've been saying it for weeks without knowing. You say it every time you call me."

My mouth was dry. "That's not possible."

"They named her after her," Freya said. "Or she was named after them. I don't know which way it goes, in your language. In mine, names don't work that way. In mine, a name is a door. You don't give a child a name that's already open unless you mean for something to walk through it."

I sat down on my stool rather heavily. The stool, loyal friend, bore it without complaint, which was more than I could say for myself.

* * *

Let me tell you what I had believed, all my life, about my family.

I had believed we were minor people. That was the story my mother told, when she told anything at all, which grew rarer as the debt ate deeper into her memory and then, eventually, into her entirely. We were minor people, she said. Scribes and clerks and one disappointing uncle who'd tried his hand at alchemy and produced only a smell. We worked for the guild because the guild employed half the city, and we owed the guild because everyone owed the guild, and that was simply the weather of being alive in this city under the Spire.

I had believed my own debt was an accident of birth. Bad luck. Weather.

Now I sat in my narrow room with two centuries of my family's handwriting in my lap, and I understood that the debt was not weather. It was architecture. My ancestors had built it, stone by stone, entry by entry, and every generation since had lived comfortably inside it, thanking their forefathers for the walls.

Because here was the thing the ledger made brutally, beautifully clear: the Whitlocks had not been paid in coin. Coin runs out. Coin gets stolen, taxed, gambled away by disappointing uncles. The Whitlocks had been paid in *standing*. In exemption. In the quiet, invisible privilege of being the one family in the city whose debts were always, mysteriously, negotiable. Whose sons got scriptorium positions instead of tannery vats. Whose daughters married well. Who were never audited, never erased, never processed.

Until the privileges thinned. Until the payments became advances. Until somewhere in the last century, the arrangement curdled into what I had grown up inside: not reward but leash. The guild no longer served the Whitlocks. The Whitlocks *owed* the guild—for services rendered two hundred years ago, for a crime committed before anyone alive was born, compounding forever, principal and interest, world without end.

"We didn't sell a child," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, distant, like someone reading aloud in another room. "We sold children. Every hundred years. And then we sold the right to sell them, and then we borrowed against the right to sell them, and now I—" I gestured at myself, at the whole shabby apparatus of me, the ink-stained fingers, the ledger of my own debts chained to my wrist like jewelry. "I'm the interest, Freya. I'm two hundred years of interest payments walking around in a coat."

She climbed onto the bench beside me, which was her way of announcing that a conversation was now going to happen at her level whether I liked it or not.

"The other ones," she said. "The first me. The ones before."

"What about them?"

"They're still down there." Her voice went careful, the way it did when she was translating something she wished she didn't understand. "The ledger says the binding needs a speaker. It doesn't say the speakers stop being speakers when you put them underground. Theo—I've been listening to the Spire since I came here. At night. Through the floors. There's more than one voice under there. There's a whole choir, and most of them have forgotten the words, but they're still humming."

I thought of the vault she'd opened. I thought of the ledgers predating the guild, the ones written in her language, including the one that had started all of this. I thought of the sound I'd heard once, working late, that I had told myself was the building settling.

A building. Settling. Two hundred years of children, settling.

"Why didn't you tell me sooner?" I asked, and hated myself even as I said it, because I knew the answer.

"Because I watched your face just now," Freya said, "when you found out what your family did. And I didn't want to be the reason you made that face." She picked at a loose thread on the ancient binding. "Also," she added, with the merciless honesty of the young, "because you're a coward, Theo. A nice coward. But a coward. I wasn't sure which way you'd go."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen which way you went with *me*. You hid my page. You taught yourself my letters off my drawings, badly." She glanced at my attempt, months back, at rendering the word for *home*, which I had rendered as something closer to *boiling soup*. "Badly," she repeated, with feeling. "So I thought maybe the coward has a floor. Maybe underneath all the flinching there's a person who, when it matters, writes the true page instead of the false one."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, the night shift of scribes scratched away at their copies, thousands of pens whispering the guild's version of the world into existence, one obedient line at a time.

"Here's what I can't get past," I said. "It wasn't me. I didn't do it. I was three hundred years too late to do it or undo it. And yet I've spent my whole life paying for it, and worse—" I stopped. Started again. "Worse, I've spent my whole life *benefiting* from it. Every advantage I ever had, every kindness the guild showed my family, every time a door opened for a Whitlock that stayed shut for everyone else—that was the child's voice, Freya. That was her voice, converted into currency, and I spent it without ever knowing where it came from."

"Yes," Freya said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What do you want me to say? That it isn't fair?" She shrugged, a small sharp motion. "Nothing down here is fair. I fell out of the sky, Theo. Somebody put me in the sky on purpose. Do you think I sit around cataloguing unfairness?" She tapped the ledger. "This book doesn't care about fair. It cares about *true*. So. Now you know the true thing. What are you going to do with it?"

And there it was. The question, sitting in the lamplight between us, patient as a debt.

I thought about running. Of course I did. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of everything that follows. I thought about the escape routes Maren had described, the ash-lane exits, the boats. I thought about taking Freya's hand and walking out of the city and finding some village where nobody kept ledgers, and living small and scared and free until the guild found us, because the guild always finds you, and they would find us faster now, because I was not merely a debtor anymore. I was a debtor who knew.

I thought about obedience, too. About carrying the ledger to Provost Hale and laying it on his desk and saying *this is what was missing from the intake file, sir, I found it in the girl's possession,* and watching him weigh whether my treachery was worth more than my silence, and knowing it wasn't, knowing I'd be erased by morning, my whole life burned down to smoke and a receipt.

Both roads ended with me destroyed. The only difference was what I'd be destroyed *for*.

And underneath both thoughts, quieter than either, was my mother's face—which I could no longer fully picture, thanks to a transaction I'd made in a moment of fear, three years of myself traded away to close an inquiry. I couldn't remember her face. But I could remember her hands, turning pages. Reading to me. And I could remember what she'd said, once, the only political statement of her entire careful life, made over a supper of barley and onions:

"*Everything the guild knows, it knows because somebody wrote it down. Which means everything it knows can be un-known the same way.*"

I had thought she meant forgery. I had spent years assuming my mother was teaching me forgery.

She hadn't been. She'd been teaching me the opposite.

"Freya," I said. "How many entries are in this ledger?"

She frowned. "Hundreds. Why?"

"How many renewals? How many children?"

Her frown deepened. She began counting under her breath in the old tongue, lips moving, finger traveling down the columns. I watched her reach the bottom of the second page and start a third, and I watched her expression change as the arithmetic assembled itself behind her eyes.

"One every hundred years," she said slowly. "For two hundred years, that's... but wait. Some entries mention *two*. Twins, once. And here—" her finger stopped. "Here's one that says the usual speaker was unavailable, and they used..." She looked up at me. "Theo, this line says they used a *scribe*. Someone who'd learned the language instead of being born to it. He wasn't as strong. The binding weakened early. That's why the last renewal came early, why the intervals aren't even—"

"A scribe," I repeated.

"A scribe who learned it," Freya said, "the way you're learning it. Badly. From drawings." She stared at me, and I stared back, and between us, unspoken, hung the thought neither of us wanted to say aloud: *it doesn't only take born speakers. It takes anyone who reads.*

Which meant the guild could never afford to let the language spread. Which meant every grammar burned, every foreign text confiscated, every scholar disappeared—it wasn't superstition or greed alone. It was maintenance. The guild wasn't guarding a secret. It was guarding a *shortage*.

And shortages, I knew—no one better—can be broken by supply.

"Freya," I said, and my voice didn't shake this time, which surprised us both. "Teach me faster."

She blinked. "Faster?"

"Faster. Properly. Not drawings on scraps—grammar, structure, all of it. If a badly-taught scribe could wind himself into that binding two hundred years ago, then a properly-taught one—" I stood up, and the room felt suddenly too small for what was happening inside it, which was, I suppose, the point of rooms. "They think the language is a treasure they can lock in a vault. They think it's a shortage they can maintain. But it's not. It's a *literacy*, and literacies spread, and you cannot collect a word once it's been read."

Freya was grinning now. It transformed her whole face, that grin—all the weight of choirs and centuries lifting off her at once.

"You're going to get us both erased," she said happily.

"Almost certainly."

"Good." She hopped off the bench and dragged my spare quill and the back of a ruined draft toward her, already sketching the first characters, large and clear, a teacher's letters. "Lesson one. You've been pronouncing this wrong for weeks and it's been driving me mad."

I sat down beside her. On the desk between us lay the ledger of my family's shame, and I did the only decent thing I could think of to do with it.

I turned it face-down.

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low and the bells rang the hour that belongs to no honest person. Freya drilled me through the vowels that exist nowhere in our alphabet, sounds like water finding its level, and corrected my pronunciation with a patience she reserves exclusively for people she has decided not to give up on. Twice, footsteps passed in the corridor, and twice we went silent as stones until they faded.

On the third pass, the footsteps didn't fade.

They stopped outside my door. And then—worse than knocking, worse than shouting—came the soft, deliberate scrape of wax being pressed to wood. The guild's summons-seal. The kind they use when they want you to know they know exactly where you are, and want you to come to them on your own feet, so the paperwork stays simple.

Freya looked at me across the ruined draft, her lesson abandoned mid-vowel.

"That's for you," she whispered.

"I know," I said.

And beneath the fear—genuine, stomach-deep, the old familiar fear of a lifetime's obedient man—something else was burning now, steady and low, a fire lit from the worst fuel there is: shame, refined into resolve.

I tucked the ancient ledger into the space behind the false floor where Freya had hidden for weeks, kissed the top of her head—she tolerated this with the grim dignity of a cat being bathed—and went to answer the guild's summons, wondering, with a calm that frightened me a little, which of us they had decided to collect first.

When I opened the door, the corridor was empty except for the wax seal on the wood, still warm, and a single line of fresh ink on my doorframe, in a hand I recognized as Hale's:

*"The masters wish to discuss the renewal. Bring nothing. Tell no one. — H."*

*Renewal.*

Two hundred years of children, and now they wanted mine.

I touched the ink to be sure it was real. It was. Everything true, in this city, was written down somewhere.

The question—the only question that had ever mattered—was who would get to write the next line.
