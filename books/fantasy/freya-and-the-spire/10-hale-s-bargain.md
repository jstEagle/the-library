## 10. Hale's Bargain

Provost Hale had a theory about bad news: it always arrived in good handwriting.

The summons from the masters came on vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, the seal pressed in wax the color of dried blood, and the hand itself—loopy, confident, faintly smug—belonged to Master Corvane's personal secretary. Hale read it twice at his desk, then a third time standing at the window, because some orders required you to look at something other than the page while they settled into your chest like swallowed stones.

*Deliver the child to the Spire's heart. Renewal of terms. Centennial observance. All protocols of silence apply.*

All protocols of silence applied. Which meant no one would write down what happened next, which meant that whatever was done to the girl would happen outside the ledger entirely, and Provost Hale had spent forty years learning that the things outside the ledger were the only things that mattered.

He set the letter down and looked at his hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the trouble with him. Even the day he'd signed the erasure order for his own son's name—even then, his hand hadn't shaken until afterward, alone, when he'd held the pen so hard the nib had snapped and inked a small black star onto his thumb. He still sometimes caught himself looking for it, as if grief might leave a mark he could point to.

"Provost?" His assistant, Pell, hovered at the door with the particular expression of a man carrying news he hoped would be someone else's problem. "The intake clerk from the night of the storm is here again. The one who filed the child's record."

"Theo Whitlock?"

"He's asked to see the original entry. Says there may be an error in the transcription." Pell hesitated. "He said it very politely, sir. He's the polite one."

"They're all the polite ones," Hale said. "That's how we know they're frightened." He straightened his collar in the window's reflection—a small vanity, but a man had to own *something*—and found, to his mild surprise, that he already knew what he was going to do. The knowledge had been sitting in him since the letter arrived, fully formed, like a coin at the bottom of a pocket you'd forgotten you owned.

"Send him up," he said. "And Pell—clear my afternoon. If anyone asks, I'm auditing the western vaults."

"You hate the western vaults, sir."

"Yes," Hale agreed. "I do."

* * *

Theo Whitlock looked worse than the last time Hale had seen him, which was saying something, because scribes generally looked like paper left out in the rain. There was a new hollowness under his eyes, the specific hollowness of a man who has recently paid a bill he didn't know he'd been running up. Hale had read the audit logs. Three years, traded to Master Ives to close an inquiry. A stupid trade. A brave trade. The kind of trade that got men erased.

"You requested access to the original intake record," Hale said, not looking up from a blank sheet of paper he was pretending to study. "There is no error in the transcription. I checked myself."

Whitlock went very still. It was, Hale thought, almost impressive how still. Most people fidgeted when they lied; this boy simply stopped, like a clock someone had put a hand over.

"I don't know what you mean, Provost."

"No," Hale said. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of you." He finally looked up and let himself study the young man properly—the ink-stained cuffs, the careful posture, the way his gaze kept flicking to the door as though calculating escape routes he'd never take. "Sit down, Whitlock. You're swaying."

"I'm standing quite normally, sir."

"You're swaying like a candle in a draft. Sit."

Whitlock sat. Hale rose, crossed to the cabinet behind his desk, and poured two measures of plum brandy—the good bottle, the one he kept for occasions he dreaded—and set one in front of the young man without ceremony.

"I don't drink on duty, Provost."

"On duty," Hale repeated. He sat back down and drank his own measure in one swallow, which was unlike him, and felt it land somewhere behind his sternum like a small warm fist. "Do you know what 'on duty' means in this building, Whitlock? It means your whole life is a shift that never ends. It means every hour you've ever lived is written down somewhere and owned by someone else. Drink the brandy."

After a moment, Whitlock did. He coughed. Hale pretended not to notice, because kindness that noticed itself wasn't kindness, it was bookkeeping.

"Now," Hale said. "I'm going to tell you what happens next, and you're going to listen without interrupting, because interrupting will waste both our time and yours is measurably more expensive than mine. The masters have ordered the child delivered to the Spire's heart. Tonight, after the second bell. For what they call a renewal of terms."

Whitlock's face did something complicated and terrible. "Renewal of—what terms? Terms of what?"

"If I knew, I'd be a master instead of a provost." Hale turned his glass slowly on the desk. "What I know is this: once a century, the guild brings a speaker of the old tongue into the Spire, and once a century, the guild emerges wealthier, safer, and more forgetful than before. I have served three such renewals. I signed the intake records for two of them. Both speakers were children. Neither was ever entered into any ledger afterward, because—" he tapped the desk, "—protocols of silence. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That she'll be erased."

"Worse. Erasure leaves a gap. Gaps can be found, measured, mourned. What happens in the Spire doesn't even leave a gap. It leaves nothing at all—not in the ledgers, not in the city, not in anyone's memory. In a month, you won't remember her name. In a year, you'll wonder why the scriptorium smells faintly of rain." Hale heard his own voice go flat and official, the voice he used for reading sentences aloud, and hated it, and kept using it, because the alternative was the other voice, the one that had once read bedtime stories to a boy whose name no longer existed anywhere in the world.

Whitlock stood up. He didn't seem to have decided to; his body simply made the decision for him, the way bodies do when they're younger and stupider and better than their owners' heads.

"Then I'll get her out tonight. Before the second bell. I'll need—"

"Sit down."

"—the eastern stair codes, and access to the paper-vaults, and—"

"*Sit down*, Whitlock." Hale let a little iron into his voice, enough to make the young man stop mid-sentence, trembling, half-risen from the chair. "Because if you burst out of my office shouting escape plans, you'll be in a cell by supper and she'll be in the Spire by dark anyway, and I'll have wasted the last favor I intend to spend in this life."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the great counting-room murmured its eternal murmur—quills scratching, coins being weighed, lives being divided into columns.

"What favor," Whitlock said carefully.

Hale took a folded paper from his drawer and laid it on the desk between them. He did not unfold it. He had unfolded it eleven times in the past day and knew every line by heart.

"There is a door," he said. "In the sub-cellar beneath the ash-chute, old, pre-guild, sealed with a lock nobody living knows how to open—which, I grant you, sounds like a fatal flaw in the plan, except that your child opened the Meridian Vault last week by humming at it, so I'm told, and I'm choosing to believe the reports because the alternative is despair. Beyond that door are the old service ways, the ones the Spire's builders used. They come up in the tannery district, past all the wardens' posts, past everything. I can give you the route. I can give you the hour the patrols change. I can lose your names from the duty rosters for exactly one night—one night, Whitlock, not two, because on the second morning the masters count their assets, and missing assets get hunted."

"Why?" It came out of Whitlock almost angrily, which Hale respected. Suspicion was healthier than gratitude. Gratitude made debtors. "Why would you help us? You signed the order. You're delivering her yourself."

"I am," Hale agreed. "That's the arrangement. Here is mine." He pushed the folded paper an inch closer. "You bring the girl to me at the ash-chute door before the second bell. Calm, willing, believing whatever story you've told her. I walk her the rest of the way. And in exchange, you walk out through the tanneries and keep walking, and live, and never come back, and never write anything down about any of it, ever, for the rest of your natural life."

"And she goes into the Spire."

"She goes into the Spire."

"To be erased. To be *unmade*."

"To be renewed," Hale said, and the word tasted like the ash it was named after. "Yes."

Whitlock stared at him. And here was the thing Hale had expected and steeled himself against: not rage, not tears, but the slow dawning horror of a decent person realizing that the offer on the table was real, was generous, was *kind*—and was monstrous. He watched the young man try to find the shape of it, the trap in it, the trick. There wasn't one. That was the worst part. Hale had made sure there wasn't one. A corrupt bargain with a knife hidden in it would have been easier to refuse.

"You're buying her with my life," Whitlock said quietly.

"I'm buying your life with hers. Let's be precise; precision is the guild's one honest virtue." Hale refilled his own glass, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "One of you walks away. I've arranged it so it's you. You may spend the rest of your days deciding whether that was mercy or bribery. I recommend not deciding. Deciding is what ruined me."

"What ruined you?" And there it was—the question asked too fast, too honestly, the brandy doing its work. Whitlock seemed to hear himself and flinch. "Forgive me, Provost. That's not—"

"It's the first honest question anyone's asked me in twenty years. I'll answer it, and then we'll never speak of it again." Hale looked at the window, at the Spire rising above the rooftops, pale as a bone in the afternoon light. "I had a son. He asked questions like yours. Loud ones, in public, at the wrong times. When he was fourteen, the masters gave me a choice: his name in a debtor's ledger for the rest of his life, or his name nowhere at all. I chose nowhere. I told myself it was protection. I signed the erasure with a steady hand, because my hands are always steady, and I have spent thirty years being promoted for it." He turned the glass a quarter-turn. "They gave me your child's file to process the morning after. A coincidence of scheduling. I have often wondered whether it was a test, and whether I passed, and what passing meant."

Whitlock said nothing. Good. The boy was learning.

"So," Hale said. "Here is where we are. At the second bell, I collect the girl. If you've run, I collect her anyway—it will simply be louder, and involve wardens, and end with you burned out of every ledger in the building while she goes into the Spire regardless. If you've brought her, you go free and she goes up. Those are the terms. I'm told the guild invented the phrase *renewal of terms*." He smiled, and was aware it was a poor thing. "We're very proud of it."

"You could refuse," Whitlock said. "You could just—refuse. Walk us both out the ash-chute yourself. You know the way. You clearly know everything."

For a moment—just a moment—Hale let himself consider it. Himself, the girl, the boy, the dark stair, the tannery smell, the road east. He was sixty-one years old. What did he owe the guild that the guild hadn't already taken?

Then he thought of Corvane's secretary's loopy handwriting, and the twelve masked masters, and the fact that the Spire did not miss its appointments, and that a provost fleeing with two fugitives would be caught by dawn and would buy nothing except three erased names instead of one saved one.

"I could," Hale said. "And we'd be caught by the third bell, and it would accomplish nothing, and I'd die having failed at the only two things I've ever actually cared about. No, Whitlock. This is what refusal looks like, from inside a machine like this. It looks like a bargain. It looks like paperwork. It looks like a provost losing one name from his roster and telling himself it's the best arithmetic available." He stood, signaling the audience was over, and held out his hand for the brandy glass. "The second bell, Whitlock. Ash-chute door. Bring her calm."

Whitlock rose too. He stood there a moment, gripping the back of the chair, and Hale watched him wrestle with the last question, the obvious one, the one any decent person would ask.

"Why should I believe you'll treat her gently? Up there. In the Spire."

And Hale, who had prepared answers for everything—who had rehearsed this conversation in the bath, at breakfast, in the grey hour before dawn—found he had nothing prepared for that. So he told the truth, which was the one currency the guild had never taught him to counterfeit.

"You shouldn't," he said. "Believe me, I mean. You should watch me every step of the way with those clever frightened eyes, and you should hate me thoroughly and correctly, and you should spend your whole free life telling people what kind of man delivers a child to a tower. That's the payment I'm actually asking for, Whitlock. Not your silence." He looked at the folded paper on the desk, at the name written inside it that no longer existed anywhere else in the world. "Your memory. Someone has to remember what we did. The guild can't erase what it doesn't know you're carrying."

Whitlock picked up the paper. His hands shook now—finally, belatedly, humanly. He tucked it inside his coat, next to his heart, which was either sentiment or tactics, and Hale genuinely could not have said which.

"At the second bell," Whitlock said.

"At the second bell."

The door closed behind him. Hale stood alone in his office with the empty glasses and the long afternoon light, and listened to the counting-room murmur below, and thought: *well. That's done. Whatever I am now, that's done.*

Then he sat down, pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and began drafting the delivery order in his best official hand—because the machine required paperwork even for its cruelties, especially for its cruelties—and it was only halfway down the page, writing the words *one (1) speaker, juvenile, for renewal*, that his hand finally, after thirty years, began to shake.

He watched it shake. He did not stop it. Some debts, he was learning far too late, you were supposed to feel.

Outside, the light was going gold, then grey. Two bells remained. And somewhere in the scriptorium below, a girl who spoke the language of before was drawing pictures on scrap paper, laughing at something, not knowing yet what the evening held—or perhaps, Hale thought with a chill he couldn't explain, knowing perfectly well, and drawing anyway.
