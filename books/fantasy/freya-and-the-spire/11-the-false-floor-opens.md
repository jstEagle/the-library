## 11. The False Floor Opens

The knock came at the wrong hour.

There was a right hour for knocks at the Guild of Impossible Things — the gray hour before dawn, when wardens collected debtors who had defaulted on their memories, and the whole building pretended not to hear. But this knock arrived in full daylight, three sharp raps and then the flat voice of Warden Osk calling my name through the scriptorium door, and I understood with the particular cold clarity of a man who has spent his life reading other people's ledgers that the audit inquiry had not been closed after all.

"Whitlock." Another rap. "Provost's orders. Full inventory of the scriptorium. Open the door."

Freya looked up from her drawings. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor beneath the tall window, sketching in charcoal on the backs of spoiled intake pages — she drew towers, mostly, and rivers of light, and once a cat with far too many legs that she insisted was accurate. She had learned to read eleven words of common script by then. *Door* was one of them. She looked at the door now, then at me, and her eyes asked the question her mouth no longer bothered with: *is it bad?*

"It's fine," I said, which was the first lie I ever told her out loud, and we both knew it.

I had perhaps forty seconds. Maybe less. Long enough to sweep the charcoal into the drawer, long enough to slide the true intake page back under its dead debtor's camouflage — but not long enough for what I actually needed to do, which was the thing I had been circling for six days like a moth around a lamp: the false floor.

Maren had told me about it years ago, back when she still worked the archives and still spoke to me in more than whispers. *Every old guild room has one*, she'd said. *A compartment under the boards where the founders hid the things they didn't want even their own masters counting.* The scriptorium's was beneath the third row of shelving, the row where we kept the ledgers of traded storms — fitting, I'd always thought, that the weather we'd bottled should sit above the weather we'd buried. The catch was a knot in the wood shaped like a closed eye. Press it, and the floor opened like a mouth.

I had never pressed it. For six days I had hidden Freya there every time footsteps passed, and every time I had thought, *not yet, not until there's no other way,* because a hiding place you've never used is still a hope, and a hope you've used is just a plan, and plans can fail.

Osk knocked again. "Whitlock. I will count to ten."

I pressed the knot.

The boards parted with a sound like a held breath released — soft, wooden, final. Freya didn't need telling. That was the thing about her that undid me, then and always: she moved like someone who had done all this before, in some other life, in some taller place. She slid into the dark below the floor with her charcoal stick still in her fist and pulled the boards down over herself, and I dragged the storm-ledger cart back over the seam just as the lock turned and Osk walked in with four men and a clerk carrying an empty ledger.

An empty ledger. That was the detail that made my stomach drop through the floorboards — through the real ones, the honest ones, the ones with a child underneath them. You bring an empty ledger when you intend to fill it. You bring an empty ledger when the inventory is not inventory at all but confiscation.

"Scribe Whitlock," said the clerk, a narrow man named Pell whose ink-stained cuffs I had envied once, years ago, before I understood what the stains cost. He set the ledger on my desk with terrible gentleness. "By order of the masters, seated during Centennial Audit: all documents in this room are to be catalogued, sealed, and removed."

"All documents," I repeated.

"All documents." Pell smiled the small apologetic smile of a man who has delivered worse news than this and will deliver worse again. "Including working papers. Including drafts. Including—" he consulted his list, and I watched him reach the line and hesitate, just slightly, the way a man hesitates at a stair edge in the dark— "including any non-standard materials."

Non-standard. Meaning her language. Meaning her drawings. Meaning, if they were thorough, meaning if they lifted the right board—

They were thorough. Of course they were thorough. They were the Guild of Impossible Things; thoroughness was our one unimpeachable virtue. Two clerks began at the window wall, lifting each ledger, weighing it in a brass scale, entering its weight beside its title as though a book were only its weight. One warden knelt to check beneath the desks. And the fourth — young, earnest, with the unfortunate habit of tapping things — began along the third row of shelving, tapping each shelf upright with his knuckle, listening.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

I stood at my desk and copied nothing, because my hands would have betrayed me, and instead I did the only useful thing left: I talked.

"You'll want the storm ledgers weighed twice," I said. "Bottled weather settles. A storm from the spring weighing can be half again heavier by autumn."

Pell glanced up. "Is that so?"

"Guild standard since the Second Charter," I lied smoothly, and something in me sang and something in me flinched, because lying had never come easily to me and now it was coming easily and I wasn't sure which feeling was worse. "Also the third shelf sits on a damp course. The boards creak. If your man hears movement down there, it's the damp. It's always the damp. We've petitioned for repairs four times."

Tap. Tap. The young warden had reached the third row.

Under the floor, in the dark, Freya was silent. I knew she was silent because I could feel it, the way you can feel a held note in music. And then — I swear this happened, though no one else in the room seemed to notice — the faintest hum came up through the boards. Not loud. Not even audible, really, except in the teeth. A single low tone, the tone she used when she was frightened and wanted to be brave, the tone that had opened the vault door last week as if locks were only doors that hadn't been asked politely.

The young warden tapped the third shelf and frowned. Tapped again. Knelt down and put his ear to the boards.

And the boards, I am quite certain, hummed back at him — a warm, woody, entirely innocent hum, the hum of old timber settling on a damp course, exactly as advertised. The warden sat back, looking vaguely soothed, the way a man looks after hearing a sound he cannot name and does not distrust. He stood, marked something on his list, and moved on to the fourth row.

It had answered him. The floor had *answered* him. In her voice.

I kept my face arranged into the expression of a scribe mildly inconvenienced by an inventory, which is to say I kept my face arranged into nothing at all, and inside I was two men: one screaming, one laughing, both of them terrified, both of them — and this was new, this was the new thing — *proud.*

* * *

They took everything. Three hours of it. Every ledger, every draft, every blotting sheet with a shadow of ink upon it, stacked into crates and sealed with wax stamped by the master's signet. Pell worked down his list with the patience of a man defrosting a cellar, and near the end he paused over my desk, over the drawer where the charcoal lay, and held up a stub of black stick between two fingers.

"Drawing?" he said.

"Blotting corrections," I said. "My hand shakes. It runs in my family."

He looked at me a moment too long. Then he dropped the charcoal in a crate and sealed it, and gave the nod, and the wardens shouldered the crates, and the door closed behind them, and the scriptorium was empty and stripped and mine again in the way that a gutted house belongs to no one.

I waited until the footsteps faded. I waited past that, until the tower bell rang the hour, because paranoia is simply diligence applied to fear. Then I knelt at the third shelf, pressed the knot, and lifted the boards.

Freya blinked up at me from the dark, charcoal-smudged, furious, magnificent.

"They took my drawings," she said, in her careful, accented common tongue, and it was somehow the angriest sentence I had ever heard anyone say, because she said it quietly.

"I know. I'm sorry. I'll get them back."

"How?"

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "But I know how we get *you* out. Because that inventory wasn't an inventory, Freya. They're going to search this room properly next. With lamps and levers and men who tap floors for a living. Tonight, maybe tomorrow. When they find the compartment — and they'll find it, the damp-course story buys us days, not weeks — they'll find the scratch marks on the underside of the boards, and the charcoal dust, and they'll know exactly what was hiding here. And then they won't stop at searching."

She climbed out of the dark and stood, small and straight, in the emptied room. "Then we go now."

"Now," I agreed. My heart was doing something unprofessional. "Now. Which means you need to know what I'm about to do, because it's theft, and treason, and probably several other things ending in -eason, and I want you to have the chance to object."

She tilted her head. "Object to what?"

I led her to the far wall, to the cabinet where the guild stored its most liquid asset: vials of traded memory. Every debtor who sold the guild a year, a face, a first love, left behind a little glass record of it — pale blue for ordinary days, deep green for childhoods, one furious amber vial that everyone avoided because it contained somebody's entire wedding. The guild kept them the way a dragon keeps gold: not to use, but to own.

"We're going to pour them out," I said. "Behind us. As we run."

Freya frowned. "Pouring memories makes people forget?"

"Poured on stone, spilled, broken open — the memory escapes and goes looking for whoever it belongs to. Or whoever's nearest. Maren calls it ghosting. A corridor full of spilled memory is a corridor full of confusion — wardens stumbling around suddenly convinced they're at their own weddings, or back in their mothers' kitchens, or nine years old and losing a tooth. You can't chase a fugitive while you're reliving your confirmation ceremony." I picked up a crate of vials and found my hands were steady, which surprised me. "It wears off in minutes. No one's hurt. But it's guild property, and it's theft, and those are the terms."

Freya considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, then took the second crate.

"Their memories are stolen already," she said. "We are only stealing them back. It is not the same shape of theft."

"That's either very wise or very convenient."

"Both," she said, and handed me the amber vial. "Start with the wedding. Weddings are strongest."

* * *

The paper-vaults ran beneath the scriptorium like the roots of some terrible tree — corridors of shelving sunk into the Spire's foundations, holding every record the guild had ever deemed too dangerous or too valuable to destroy, which is to say too profitable. The guild built upward, but it lived downward; everyone knew the Spire was tall, and nobody asked what it stood in.

We went down the maintenance stair with two crates of memories and no plan beyond *down, then away,* which is the plan of every fugitive in history and works roughly never. At the first junction, where the corridor split toward the debtor cells and the valuation halls, I heard boots — a patrol, coming up from the cells, four men at least, torchlight swinging along the wall ahead of us.

No time to hide. So I did the thing. The first open rebellion of my life took the form of hurling a vial of a stranger's wedding down a corridor.

It shattered against the flagstones and the memory came out like weather. I felt it even standing at the edge of it — a warmth, a smell of bread and lilies, a swell of joy so specific and so borrowed that my eyes stung. The torchlight stopped. The boots stopped. From around the corner came a sound I will remember all my life: a grown warden, armed and armored, saying, wetly, "Oh — oh, there she is, there's my girl, look at her, look at her *go*—" and then laughter, helpless and delighted, the laughter of a man watching a bride come down an aisle twenty years gone.

Freya gripped my sleeve. Her eyes were enormous. "He is happy," she whispered.

"For a few minutes," I whispered back. "Someone sold those minutes. Someone needed bread more than they needed their wedding day. That's the guild, Freya. That's what we're running from. Don't forget it while it's helping us."

She nodded slowly, and I saw her tuck the lesson away somewhere permanent, and I felt the guilt arrive on schedule — exhilaration's twin, riding pillion, whispering that I had just weaponized a stranger's happiest hour, that the man around the corner was poor and grieving and being used, that I had spent my whole life hating the ledger and here I was spending from it all the same. I let the guilt sit where it landed. Maren says guilt is the tax honesty pays on survival. I paid it and kept moving.

We ghosted three more corridors. A green vial of somebody's childhood flooded the archive stair with the smell of cut grass and the sound of a dog barking, and two clerks sat down on the steps and argued happily about nothing for four minutes while we slipped past behind them. An amber-and-blue mixture — someone's entire first love, courtship and heartbreak together, sold as a lot — turned a checkpoint into a wake; the guards there wept openly and waved us through without seeing us, because you cannot see a fugitive when you are attending your own funeral-of-the-heart.

Freya carried her crate like it was made of eggs and sin. She poured carefully, precisely, wasting nothing, and after each vial she murmured something in the old tongue — short, soft, apologetic. I didn't ask what it meant. Later I would learn it was a word for *borrowed*, and that she was telling each escaped memory, in the language it was born in, that it was allowed to go home.

At the fourth junction we hit trouble: a warden alone, young, alert, not yet ghosted, stepping into the corridor ahead of us with his hand already going to his whistle. Freya moved before I did. She pulled the stopper from a deep-green vial and blew across the top of it — actually *blew*, as if the memory were a dandelion — and the childhood rolled out over the flagstones, and the warden's whistle died unblown, and he sat down right there in the middle of his duty and began, very quietly, to remember learning to swim.

"He will be all right?" she asked me.

"He'll be better than all right for five minutes. Then he'll be confused, and then he'll report a gap in his evening, and then we'd better be miles away."

"Then we should be miles away," she agreed, and took my hand.

That was new. She had never taken my hand before. Her fingers were charcoal-gray and iron-cold, and I held on like a man holding a rope over a drop, and we ran.

* * *

We might have made the ash-gate clean, if the Spire hadn't woken.

I felt it first as a change in the air pressure, the way you feel a storm before you see it — a leaning, a listening. Then every seal on every wall of the paper-vaults began to glow, the old guild sigils lighting up amber one after another down the corridor behind us, like lamps being lit by a hand moving fast. An alarm, but older than alarms. The building itself, noticing.

And beneath the glow, beneath the stone, I heard — or didn't hear, exactly, but *understood*, the way you understand a word in a dream — a voice. Vast and slow and frayed at the edges, like a rope worn halfway through. It said one thing, in the old tongue, and Freya stopped dead and translated it for me in a whisper, because translating was becoming her way of holding my hand back:

"It says: *the sentence moves.*"

"What sentence?"

"I don't know yet," she said. "But it knows my name."

Behind us, far down the glowing corridor, boots — many boots, and the flat official voice of Warden Osk, and the crackle of a spilled memory failing, wearing off, the wedding dissolving back into duty. Ahead of us, the ash-gate, rusted shut since before my grandfather's debts. Between us and it, thirty feet of open corridor and no vials left worth pouring.

Freya looked at the gate. Then at me. Then she stepped forward, filled her lungs, and spoke to the lock in the language older than any kingdom — and the gate, the seals, the whole listening Spire, went quiet to hear her.
