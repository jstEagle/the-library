## 12. Streets That Forget

The lower city smelled of fish and tallow and rain that had given up trying to be clean. Freya had decided, in her third day of running, that she did not like it here — not the way the buildings leaned on each other like tired old people sharing a secret, not the way the gutters ran brown even when it hadn't rained, and especially not the way everyone looked at her.

Not *at* her, exactly. That was the problem. They looked *through* her, the way you look through a window at weather you've already decided to ignore. In the Spire quarter, people had stared because she was strange. Down here, strangeness was so common it had stopped being interesting. A mud-caked child with no shoes and an accent from nowhere ranked somewhere below the one-legged fiddler and well below the woman who sold pies out of a wheelbarrow and swore each pie had been blessed by a saint who did not exist.

"Keep your hood up," Theo whispered, for perhaps the fortieth time that morning. He walked beside her with his shoulders hunched and his scribe's hands shoved deep in his coat pockets, looking like a man trying to fold himself small enough to post through a letter slot. "And don't talk."

"You said that."

"I'll keep saying it until it works."

"It has worked. I have said nothing since the bridge."

"You hummed."

"Humming is not talking."

"Humming is how you opened a vault door that the Guild of Impossible Things has been failing to open for two hundred years," Theo said, with the particular tightness he got when he was frightened and trying to be funny about it, which was most of the time. "So forgive me if I've developed strong feelings about the sounds that come out of you."

Freya considered pointing out that the vault door had been very useful, actually, seeing as it had contained the ledger that proved his whole family were seal-forgers and liars, but she had learned in three days of companionship that this was not a subject Theo could discuss while walking. It required him to stop, sit down, and look at his own hands for a while. So she said nothing, which was its own kind of talking, and they went on down the street together like two conspirators disguised as nobody in particular.

They were hunting her. She understood that now, though the word *hunting* still felt too grand for what actually happened, which was that men in grey coats appeared at the ends of streets and asked questions in flat voices, and shopkeepers shook their heads, and then the men moved on, patient as water finding cracks. The guild did not run. The guild did not shout. The guild simply arrived, the way damp arrives in a wall.

What Freya did not yet understand — what she would understand within the hour, and wish she didn't — was why they kept losing her.

* * *

It happened at the tannery market, which was the worst-smelling place in a city that seemed to be competing for the title.

They had come down from the rookery roofs at dawn, Theo leading, because Theo knew the city the way a rat knows a house: every gap, every forgotten stair, every door that hadn't locked since before he was born. He'd found them a room above a chandler's shop with a bed that sagged like a hammock full of regret, and for one day and one night they had almost rested. Then, in the grey of the second morning, there had been a knock at the chandler's door — three soft knocks, polite as a tax collector — and Theo had gone white and said, "Back stairs, now," and that was that.

Now they were in the tannery market, pressed between a stall of hides and a wall, watching four grey coats move through the crowd in that unhurried way of theirs.

"They're not searching," Freya breathed. "Look. They're just standing."

She was right, and it made her skin prickle. The wardens weren't turning over stalls or shoving through crowds. Three of them stood at the corners of the square like fence posts, and the fourth — a big man with a face like a closed ledger — stood in the center and simply watched people go by. Watching their faces. Not hers.

"He's looking for something else," she whispered. "Not me."

"Theo?" said a voice behind them.

Freya's heart performed a maneuver she had previously only read about.

A woman stood at the mouth of the alley beside them, holding a basket of dyed wool, squinting at Theo with the dawning delight of someone recognizing an old friend in an unexpected place. She was broad and cheerful and had wool fluff stuck to her chin.

"Tamsin," Theo said, in a voice like a man stepping carefully onto ice. "Hello. Good — good wool."

"It's madder red, do you like it? What are you doing down here? You never come down here, you said the smell offended your delicate scribal constitution—" Her eyes slid sideways and landed on Freya, and her smile widened. "And who's this? Your daughter? She has your exact scowl, I hope you know that—"

"Tamsin." Theo's voice had gone very quiet, which Freya had learned meant the opposite of calm. "You haven't seen me."

"Well, obviously I'm seeing you right now, you great lump, are you unwell? You look terrible, is that ink or bruising—"

Behind her, in the center of the square, the warden with the closed-ledger face turned his head. Slowly. Like a weathervane finding the wind.

Freya didn't think. Thinking was for people with time.

"*Don't see us*," she said.

She said it in the old tongue, because the old tongue was where her words lived when she wasn't guarding them — three syllables that meant, more or less, *pass by, be smooth water, nothing here to catch your eye.* She had said such words before, back home, back up, back whenever-and-wherever home had been; they were small words, courtesy words, the kind you used to slip past a sleeping dog.

But she had never said them *here*. And here, the air took them differently.

The change rippled outward from her like a ring in still water. Tamsin blinked. The delight drained out of her face not into sadness or anger but into a mild, pleasant blankness, the expression of someone who had been about to say something and had generously decided it wasn't worth the effort. She looked directly at Theo — looked *into* him, almost — and then her gaze slid off him the way rain slides off oiled cloth.

"Funny weather," Tamsin remarked, to no one, and turned, and walked away through the crowd with her basket of madder red, humming.

In the center of the square, the warden frowned. He looked left. He looked right. He had the expression of a man who had come into a room to fetch something and could no longer remember what room it was. He conferred briefly with his fellows at the corners, and Freya saw the same fog pass over each of their faces in turn, and then all four grey coats turned and drifted out of the market like smoke deciding it had better places to be.

The square went on being a square. Fishwives argued. A dog stole a sausage. Nobody screamed, because nobody knew anything had happened.

Nobody except Freya, who was shaking.

* * *

Theo took her up three alleys and down two, bought meat pies from a man who didn't seem entirely sure he'd been paid, and finally holed up under the arches of an abandoned aqueduct where pigeons had built a civilization older than some nations. He sat her down on a dry stone and crouched in front of her.

"What happened back there," he said. "Exactly. Word for word."

"I told her not to see us."

"In *their* language. The old one."

"Yes."

"And she forgot you. Both of us." He rubbed his face. His hands smelled of ink even now, even after everything; it was, Freya had decided, simply what Theo was made of, the way some people were made of salt. "Freya. When you spoke at the guild door, the first night — the seals on the walls glowed. Hale saw it. Everyone saw it. We assumed the language *did* things to objects. To locks. To stone."

"It does."

"But it also does things to *people*." He said it slowly, tasting the shape of the idea and clearly disliking the flavor. "That's what the wardens couldn't do. That's why they kept circling. They weren't incompetent, they were — they were walking past you. For days. Every time you got close enough to speak, you — "

"Don't say it."

"— erased them."

"I said don't say it." Freya pulled her knees up. Under the arch, the pigeons muttered among themselves about bread. "I didn't erase anyone. I told a woman not to see us. That's different."

"How?"

"She can still see! She sees everything, she just doesn't remember *this*, she doesn't remember *us*—" Freya heard her own voice climbing and hated it. She made herself stop. Breathe. Count the arches. Eleven arches, one pigeon with a feather out of place, Theo's worried face going soft the way it did when he thought she couldn't tell he was worried. "It's a small thing," she said, quieter. "It's a courtesy. Where I come from — where I *came* from — you say those words to pass a sleeping dog, or to walk through a market without every vendor shouting at you. It's politeness. It costs nothing."

"Does it?"

"Of course it costs nothing."

"Then why did Tamsin forget my name?"

The question sat between them like a stone neither wanted to pick up.

Freya looked at her hands. They were filthy. Everything about her was filthy; three days of running will do that. But under the grime her hands were just hands, small ones, seven years old or whatever age she was this week — ages worked strangely when you came from a tower of glass — and they had done a small, easy, courteous thing, and a cheerful woman with wool on her chin had lost a piece of her morning. Maybe a small piece. Maybe the memory of a friend she'd been pleased to see. Maybe, Freya thought with a cold sliding feeling, more than that. Maybe the shape of knowing Theo at all. Maybe years of it, gone as smoothly as water off cloth, and Tamsin would never know anything was missing, because that was the cruelty of it — the theft came pre-forgiven. The victim signed the receipt without ever seeing the pen.

"It's the same magic," she said at last. "Isn't it. The one that built the seal."

Theo didn't answer, which was an answer.

"A generation's names," Freya said. "Maren's ledgers said it. The sealing cost a generation's names — everyone forgot the people who were taken, forgot the price, forgot the war. And I always thought — " she groped for it — "I thought that must have been some *big* magic. Some enormous machine of a spell. Towers and engines and a hundred speakers chanting. But it isn't, is it. It's this. It's just this, said ten thousand times, by ten thousand mouths, one small courtesy at a time. Until a whole city forgets a whole war." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "It's not a weapon. It's *manners*. Horrible manners."

"Hey." Theo sat down beside her on the stone, which was his way of making a speech shorter. "You told a woman not to see two fugitives so she wouldn't be dragged into a guild hunt. That's not forging a seal. That's not feeding a century to a Spire."

"Isn't it the same verb, though?"

"...What?"

"The same *word*." Freya hugged her knees tighter. "In the old tongue, forgetting and binding are nearly the same word. Just different endings. *To make not-seen.* My teachers — " she stopped. Her teachers were a memory of light and voices, a tower that might never have existed except inside her. "Whoever taught me would say the ending matters. The grammar matters. You can say *make not-seen* to protect, or you can say it to possess. Same root. Different soul." She looked at him. "But Theo — the person who is not-seen doesn't get to choose which soul used the word. Tamsin didn't choose. I chose *for* her. That's what the guild does. That's what the seal did. Choose for everyone, kindly, permanently, and call it protection."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. A pigeon landed on his head. He let it, which told Freya how far his thoughts had traveled.

"When I was a boy," he said eventually, "my mother used to send me to settle accounts with the pie man on Ash Lane. And I'd get there and realize I'd spent the coin on sweets for myself, so I'd tell him mother had already paid. A little lie. A courtesy lie. It cost nothing." He flicked the pigeon gently; it relocated with dignity. "Except it didn't, because the pie man trusted her, and every little lie I spent was spending *her* trust, not mine. I was spending money that belonged to someone else and calling it nothing." He glanced at her sidelong. "I'm not saying you're a nine-year-old embezzler of memories."

"That is exactly what you're saying."

"I'm saying I recognize the feeling. Of using someone else's account." He shrugged. "For what it's worth, the guild spends other people's accounts too. Constantly. Enthusiastically. With ledgers. The difference is they *like* it."

"I don't like it."

"No," Theo said. "I know. That's rather the point of you."

Freya snorted despite herself, which she suspected was the intended effect. Theo was annoyingly good at that — at putting a joke in exactly the place where the grief would otherwise pool. She was beginning to suspect it was how he survived.

"So I just don't speak," she said. "Around people. Ever. I hold the words behind my teeth and I let the wardens catch up and I hope they only erase my *life* instead of my—"

"Or," Theo said, "we find someone who knows what your words actually do. All the endings. All the souls. Someone who's spent thirty years collecting the things the guild threw away." He was already pulling a scrap of paper from his coat, unfolding it, smoothing it against his knee. On it, in his cramped scribal hand, was a map of the lower city with one street marked in ink: a narrow crooked line labeled, in letters that had been written and rewritten until the paper wore thin, ASH LANE.

"There's an archivist," he said. "Disgraced. Deals in forbidden histories. If anyone alive knows the grammar of that language — the difference between a lock and a theft — it's her."

"Will she help us?"

"She'll complain about helping us at length, and then help us. It's an archivist thing." Theo stood, brushed off his coat, and offered her his hand — the ink-stained one, the one that had copied debts for years and had recently started copying truths instead. "Also, fair warning: she will feed us until we beg for mercy. Apparently I look underfed."

"You look like a startled broom."

"See, this is the gratitude I get for a lifetime of crime."

Freya took his hand and let him pull her up. But as they stepped out from under the aqueduct, into the noise and stink and stubborn life of the lower city, she made herself a promise, quiet and hard, the kind of promise that gets carved somewhere internal where erasure can't reach it:

*I will learn every ending of every word,* she promised. *And I will never again spend what isn't mine.*

Behind them, unnoticed, a grey coat detached itself from a doorway and began, patiently, to follow — for the warden had not forgotten after all. He had simply been waiting, at a distance, beyond the reach of her voice, and now he knew exactly where the girl with the dangerous mouth was going.

Ash Lane was a long street. And it had only one way out.
