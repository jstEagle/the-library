## 15. Recaptured

The trap was set with bread.

That was the humiliating part, Theo thought later—not the iron grip of the wardens, not the cold efficiency of the guild's machinery closing around them like a ledger snapping shut. It was the bread. Warm, crusty, smelling of rosemary, sitting on the windowsill of a debtor's tenement in Cooper's Row as if the world were still a place where people gave things away for free.

"Is it a gift?" Freya had asked, tugging his sleeve. In her own tongue the word for *gift* was a small bright syllable that made the lampposts flicker, so she'd learned to say it in Common, carefully, the way you carry water across ice.

"It might be," Theo had said, and his stomach—three days on stolen memories and one very regrettable turnip—had overruled every instinct he possessed. "It might be exactly what it looks like."

It was not what it looked like. It was a signal. The moment Theo's fingers touched the crust, the shutters along the row banged open like a row of black teeth, and six wardens in grey oilcloth dropped into the street with the practiced grace of men who had done this to better people than him.

He should have known. He should have remembered that the debtor who lived there—a wiry cooper named Ansel Petch, whose debt Theo had once processed with the cheerful numbness of a man stamping someone else's coffin—had reason to hate them both. Three weeks ago, fleeing through the lower city, Freya had spoken a word to make Ansel forget he'd ever seen them. The word had worked. The word had also, apparently, worn off around the edges, the way all her mercies did, leaving behind a man who couldn't remember what he'd forgotten but knew, with the animal certainty of the wronged, that something had been taken.

"He looked at me," Freya whispered now, pressed against Theo's side as the wardens closed in. "In the window. Before they came. He looked at me like I was weather. Like something that happens *to* you."

"I know," Theo said.

"Theo. I don't want to be weather."

The first warden's hand closed on his collar before he could answer, and then the night became a series of indignities: wrists bound with debt-cord—the guild's signature restraint, inked thread that recorded your pulse as it tightened, so that even your fear became evidence—hoods pulled down, and the long jolting ride in a cart that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Somewhere in the dark of the hood, Freya found his hand. Her fingers were cold and small and steady, which was more than could be said for his.

"They'll put us in cells," she said quietly, under the rattle of the wheels. "Cells have walls. Walls are just sentences that haven't been finished yet."

"That is either very comforting or deeply alarming," Theo said, "and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Neither can I." A pause. Then, with the small fierce humor that had gotten them both through Ash Lane and Maren's crowded back room and the whole long winter of running: "Mostly alarming."

* * *

They were processed separately, because the guild understood that separation was half the punishment and charged accordingly. Theo heard it in the intake clerk's voice through the wall—a bored recitation of charges, each one priced. *Harboring an unregistered speaker. Theft of guild property, being one (1) child, condition used. Sedition by reading.* That last one made the clerk pause, almost respectfully. Reading aloud in public. There hadn't been a charge like that since the Founding.

Then the hood came off, and Theo was standing in a cell he knew intimately, because he had catalogued its contents himself two years ago during an inventory audit. Narrow cot. One bucket. One candle stub, rated at four hours. And the wall—blessed, ordinary, lime-washed stone—with a hairline crack running from floor to ceiling like a sentence waiting for its verb.

He pressed his ear to it.

Nothing. Then, faintly, a hummed note. Two notes. The opening fragment of the song Freya sang when she was frightened, the one about the tower of glass, the one that made his teeth ache pleasantly and the candle flame stand up straighter.

He knocked. Once, twice, three times—the old scribe's rhythm, *copy, check, copy*.

A pause. Then three knocks back, slightly off-beat, followed by a fourth that was unmistakably deliberate and unmistakably wrong, and Theo laughed out loud in his cell, alone, hooded hours ago, sentenced to nothing yet but already certain it would be terrible—laughed until his ribs hurt, because Freya had never learned rhythm. Not in any language. Music, apparently, was the one grammar she couldn't steal.

*She's alive,* he told the wall. *She's scared and she's alive and she knocks like a drunk woodpecker.* It was, at the bottom of the fall, an astonishing amount to have.

* * *

The trial—if the word could stretch that far—took place the next morning in a chamber Theo recognized as the Disbursement Room, where debts were normally settled rather than lives. Provost Hale presided from behind a desk that had been cleared of everything except a single ledger and a single bell, and Hale looked, Theo thought, like a man wearing his own face slightly askew. There were grey patches under his eyes. His hands, folded on the desk, were perfectly still in the way of hands that had been ordered to be still.

The masters did not attend in person. They attended as a mirror—a tall oval glass propped against the far wall, in which shapes moved that were almost figures, robed and faceless, their attention a physical weight, like standing inside a held breath. Theo had seen the mirror once before, at his induction, and had spent years telling himself it was a trick of polish. He was done telling himself things.

"The prisoner Whitlock," Hale read, and his voice caught on the name in a way that only Theo seemed to notice. "Charges accepted. Sentence as follows."

Here Hale stopped. Here Hale looked down at the ledger for longer than reading required. When he spoke again, his voice had gone flat and official, the voice of a man reading someone else's handwriting.

"For the crime of sedition by reading, the prisoner is sentenced to full erasure. Every entry. Every record. Every transaction, indenture, and debt pertaining to the individual designated Whitlock, Theo, scribe, third class, shall be burned from the master rolls. Upon completion, the individual will remain legally alive—" the mirror shimmered faintly, amused— "but unrecorded. No debt may be collected from him. No wage paid to him. No door of the guild opened to him. No person bound by guild law may speak his name, as no record of it will exist to be spoken."

Theo's mouth was dry. "And if someone remembers me anyway?"

"Memory without record decays," said the mirror, in a voice like pages turning. "Within a year, you will be a rumor. Within five, a mistake. Within ten, nothing at all." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. "You will find, scribe, that being uncollectable is its own kind of freedom. We are, in our way, generous."

*Generous.* Theo almost laughed again. Almost. Because the horror wasn't the dying—it was the specific shape of it. He thought of his mother's face, already half-gone, sold three years ago to buy time during the audit. Erasure wouldn't take her from him. She was already taken. Erasure would take the *wanting* of her, the ache where her face should be, the last proof that he had ever been anyone's son.

"And the girl?" he asked.

Hale's stillness deepened. "The speaker is not subject to erasure. The speaker is an asset. The speaker will be conveyed to the Spire for renewal of terms, per the Centennial mandate." He rang the bell once. It was a small, cheap sound, absurdly domestic, and it ended the proceedings the way a lid ends a pot.

As the wardens took his arms, Theo looked back at Hale—one heartbeat, two—and saw the provost looking not at him but at the mirror, with an expression Theo had never seen on an official face before. It took him a moment to name it, because he'd only ever seen it in mirrors of the ordinary kind, on his own face, in the years before Freya.

It was the look of a man watching himself do something unforgivable and checking, hopefully, whether anyone had noticed that he hated it.

* * *

The days in the cell had no numbers, so Theo made some. He scratched them into the wall beside the crack with the edge of his belt buckle—*one, two, three*—and invented small ceremonies to keep time honest. On day four, he taught himself to whistle the first phrase of Freya's tower-song, badly, and pressed his ear to the wall until her answering hum came through, off-key and perfect.

On day five, they took his belt.

On day six, a warden brought his meal and lingered at the bars, a young man with the unhappy eyes of someone new to the profession, and said, too quietly for the corridor: "They're moving her tomorrow. Dawn. There's a carriage with no windows." Then he left before Theo could thank him, which Theo understood; thanks was a record, and records were dangerous now.

That night, Theo sat with his back against the shared wall and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding. Tomorrow, Freya went into the Spire, where a century-old speaker waited in the dark, begging to be finished. Tomorrow, Theo's name began burning page by page, and within a year the city would forget there had ever been a coward named Whitlock who finally, too late, found his voice. He had read the true ledgers aloud in the square—he had *done* the great thing, the thing songs would have been written about if songs were allowed—and it had bought the city a season of doubt and bought them both a cell.

Despair, he discovered, was not loud. It was not weeping or screaming or shaking fists at heaven. It was this: sitting calmly on a cot, doing sums, arriving at zero, and finding that zero had excellent posture and intended to stay.

He put his forehead against the stone. "Freya," he whispered, knowing she couldn't hear words through a foot of lime-washed rock, speaking anyway because a scribe's habit was to address the record even when no record remained. "If they finish it—if the Spire takes you—I want you to know the accounting. You came to my door in the rain and you couldn't say a single word of Common, and I was supposed to erase you, and instead you erased everything I was afraid of. Whatever they write in whatever ledger they keep—" his voice cracked, and he let it, because who was left to see— "the balance was in your favor. It was always in your favor."

Silence. Then, through the wall, faint and sleep-thick and hopelessly off-beat, she began to hum.

Not the tower-song. Something new. Something slow and rising, note by note, like dawn deciding whether to bother—and as she hummed, the hairline crack in the wall began, very softly, to glow.

Theo sat up. The light ran along the crack like ink along a nib, tracing the fault line from floor to ceiling, and beneath the hum he felt—not heard, *felt*—a word assembling itself in the stone, patient as water, older than the guild, older than the forgetting:

*a sentence that hasn't been finished yet.*

Her voice, through the wall, saying it without saying it. Or perhaps the wall saying it. Or perhaps, Theo thought, heart hammering against the bottom of the fall, the building itself beginning, at last, to choose a side.

Dawn was coming. The windowless carriage was coming. But so, apparently, was something else—and it was knocking.
