## 17. Renewal of Terms

They came for her at the hour when the candles burned lowest, and Freya was almost grateful.

Almost.

She had spent three days in a cell with one wall and no window, listening to the building breathe. That was the thing about the Guild of Impossible Things that nobody warned you about: the building breathed. She had felt it from her first night, that slow swell and settle in the stones, like a sleeper turning over. Theo would have called it the Spire's weight pressing down. Freya knew better. Buildings didn't breathe. *People* breathed. Whatever was under this one had been breathing a very long time.

"Up," said the warden, a broad woman with kind eyes and a cruel job, which Freya was learning was the guild's favorite combination. "The masters want you presentable."

"I'm always presentable," Freya said. "It's a gift."

The warden's mouth twitched. She smothered it professionally, but Freya saw it, and she tucked it away the way she tucked away every small human thing in this place—Theo's terrible handwriting, the cook who had slipped her extra bread, the warden's almost-smile. If she collected enough of them, maybe they would weigh something. Maybe they would matter.

They washed her. They dressed her in white, which she hated on principle, because white was what you put on things you intended to use up. They combed her hair with a silver comb and did not ask permission, and Freya let them, because she had learned in three days of cell-sitting that the old tongue was a door with no lock on her side of it. She could speak and people would forget. She could not speak and be free.

Then they walked her up.

She had expected down. Everything in her bones said the breathing thing lived *below*, in the dark, in the roots. But the guild's wardens took her to a stair she had never seen, a spiral of black stone that went up and up through the Spire's throat, past floors where scribes slept at their desks, past a library where the books were chained like criminals, past a landing where a single window showed her the whole grey city spread out below like a spilled purse.

And then the stairs stopped, and there was a door.

It was old. That was the first thing. Everything else in the Spire was old the way a grandfather is old—wrinkled, but still recognizably a person. This door was old the way a river is old. It had no handle. It had no hinges she could find. Across its face ran a line of script in the language before language, and Freya read it without trying, the way you read your own name.

*WE PAID. WE ARE PAID FOR. DO NOT OPEN UNTIL THE SENTENCE IS FINISHED.*

"You're not going to open it," she said. "You can't. That's why you need me."

The warden looked at her feet. The other warden looked at the ceiling. Nobody answered, which Freya had learned was the guild's word for *yes*.

They brought out an instrument. It was small and silver and looked like a tuning fork that had been to a very expensive school, and when they pressed it to the door, the script began to glow, and the door—slowly, with a sound like a held breath finally let go—opened.

The heat came out first. Not fire-heat. Body-heat. The warmth of a living thing that has been lying in one place for a hundred years.

"Wait here," the warden said, which was the first sensible order anyone had given Freya all week, because she had no intention of waiting anywhere.

She stepped through.

* * *

The heart of the Spire was not a heart at all. It was a room, and the room was a throat, and the throat was full of light.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale gold glow with no source, and it moved. That was the worst part. It moved the way light moves through water, slow and rolling, and as Freya's eyes adjusted she saw that the light had *texture*, that it was made of—she reached for the word in the old tongue and found it immediately, the way you find your own hand in the dark—

*Words.* The light was made of words. Thousands of them, millions, drifting and settling and drifting again, a snowfall of language that had been falling in this room for a hundred years without ever once touching the ground.

And in the middle of it, something sat.

She thought at first it was a statue. Then she thought it was a tree. Then, as she came closer, she understood that it had been a person once, or something like a person, and that the guild had done to it what the guild did to everything: they had filed it. They had organized it. They had taken a living speaker of the first language and bound it into the shape of a *record*—limbs become columns, face become a page, breath become the slow turning of some enormous ledger only it could read. It was rooted in a dais of black stone, and its eyes were open, and its eyes were the color of the light.

Freya had come prepared to hate it. She had practiced her hatred for three days in the cell, polishing it like a stone. Here was the engine. Here was the thing that ate speakers of the old tongue and ground them into forgetting, the thing her first mother had died sealing, the thing the Whitlocks had built their cage around. She had a speech prepared. It had been a very good speech.

She got as far as the dais, and the thing looked at her, and its mouth moved, and it said, in the language before language, in a voice like pages turning:

"Are you the century?"

Freya's speech died in her throat.

"I'm Freya," she said.

"Freya." The thing tried the name. Its face, what was left of one, moved with difficulty, like a door in a house nobody lived in. "They gave me a name too, once. I have lost it. I lost it around the fourth decade. Or the fifth. One loses track." A pause. The light-words swirled around it, and she saw now that they weren't random—they were *its* words, its thoughts, shed and drifting, and the guild's machines at the Spire's roots were drinking them like sap. "Are you the century?"

"I don't—what does that mean?"

"The sentence," it said patiently, the way you speak to a child, and then it seemed to remember something and corrected itself, the way you speak to a person. "Forgive me. I forget that the young are not told things. It is a guild habit. They taught it to me." The light dimmed, just slightly, the way a face dims when it smiles without meaning to. "There is a sentence. The oldest sentence. It was being spoken when the war came, and the sealing stopped it in the middle. A sentence stopped in the middle does not end. It *waits*. And everything it was holding up—" it gestured, or tried to; its arm had become architecture somewhere over the last century, and the gesture became more of a settling, like a house shifting on its foundations. "Everything it holds up waits with it. I wait with it. I am the part of the sentence they could not erase, so they made me into a comma."

Freya sat down. She hadn't decided to; her legs simply reported that the conversation was going to be a long one and took matters into their own hands.

"You're the one from before," she said. "The one in the ledgers. The one they feed."

"Feed is a generous word. They *top me up*. Every hundred years they bring a speaker and read the renewal terms into me, and the terms are always the same—*continue, continue, continue*—and the speaker's voice keeps the sentence suspended and the city standing and the guild's ledgers balanced, and then the speaker is used up, and they write a very tidy entry about it, and everyone goes home." The thing's eyes found hers. They were terribly, unbearably tired. "You are very small to be a century. They are getting cheaper."

"I'm not a century," Freya said. "I'm the key. Theo says—" She stopped. Saying Theo's name in this room felt like bringing a candle into a cathedral. "Theo says I can finish it. The sentence. That's what I was sent down to do. *Finish the sentence.*"

The thing went very still. The light-words stopped falling. For one long moment the whole heart of the Spire held its breath, and Freya felt the building above her stop breathing too, all those floors of scribes and ledgers and chained books suddenly silent, as if the entire guild were leaning in to listen.

"Finish it," it repeated. "You can finish it."

"That's the idea."

"Child." It leaned toward her, and the movement cost it something; she heard stone grind somewhere in its middle. "Child, listen to me, because I have had one hundred years to compose this request and I would like to deliver it properly." The light steadied. "They will tell you that finishing the sentence will free me. They will tell you it will destroy me. They will tell you it will bring the city down on all our heads. I have heard their versions. I have had a century to compare them against my own memory, and my own memory says this: I no longer care which it is."

Freya's hands had gone cold in her lap.

"I was a speaker," it said. "Like you. I stood where you are standing, or someone like you did, and I was afraid, and I let them persuade me that a suspended sentence was safer than a finished one. That waiting was a kind of living. It is not. Waiting is a kind of *filing*. I am a record now, Freya-who-was-sent-down. I am the guild's oldest asset. And I am asking you—" and here its voice did something she had not thought a voice made of stone and light could do; it *broke*, gently, the way an old book's spine breaks, "—I am asking you not to free me. Free is just another word for *continue, but outside*. I am asking you to finish me. End it or open it, dawn or dark, I will take either. I will take *anything*. But do not read the renewal terms. Do not give them another century of me. I have given them a century of me, and I have nothing left over, and I am so tired of being useful."

The light-words had begun to fall again, slowly, and Freya watched one drift down past the thing's face, and she saw that this one was not a word at all. It was a name. A small, ordinary name, in the old script, and it fell and fell and never landed.

"Was that yours?" she asked. "Your name?"

"It was someone's," it said. "They all are, eventually. The room fills up with what they take. I am the only part of me they couldn't carry out."

Freya looked at it for a long time. The monster. The engine. The sealed presence beneath the Spire that the whole city's fear was built on top of, the thing Theo's family had chained, the thing the guild had milked, and it sat in its own shed names like an old man in his own shed tools, and all it wanted—the whole vast terrible engine of it, the thing entire armies had been spent against—was to be *done*.

"I'm not going to read their terms," she said.

"Thank you," it said, with the enormous dignity of the very tired.

"But I'm not going to decide anything until I've read the whole sentence. All of it. Beginning to end. If I'm going to finish something, I want to know how it ends first. That's not cowardice, that's *reading properly*." She stood up, and her knees cracked, and she was suddenly, fiercely aware that she was nine years old and the fate of a city was sitting on her like a coat that didn't fit yet. "And I want to bring someone. He's a scribe. He's got terrible handwriting and he sold three years of his life to protect me, and he reads everything, even the things he's not supposed to. He'll want to see this."

The thing regarded her. Something moved in the light around it—she thought, later, that it might have been the first laugh it had laughed in a hundred years, though it came out as a settling of stones.

"A scribe," it said. "They do breed them, don't they. Very well. Bring your scribe. But come soon, child." The light guttered, and for a moment Freya saw through it—saw the thing's outline thin as paper, a page with one line left on it. "The masters are coming for the renewal. And I do not think I can refuse them a second time."

* * *

They walked her back down the black stairs, and the wardens did not speak, and Freya did not speak, and the city below went on not knowing what it stood on.

But in the cell, alone, with the candle burning low and the wall between her and Theo's breathing, Freya pressed her palm flat against the stone and began, very quietly, to hum.

It was the old trick—the one that had opened the vault, the one that made people forget. But she wasn't using it to take anything this time. She shaped the hum into the oldest pattern she knew, the one that meant *message*, the one that meant *listen*, and she sent it through the wall like a knock.

*Theo. Wake up. I've met the monster, and it's not a monster, and we haven't got much time.*

Somewhere on the other side of the wall, she heard him sit up.

And somewhere above them both, faint as a held breath, the masters began to descend for the ceremony.
