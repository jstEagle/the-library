## 18. Reading Aloud

They dressed me in guild grey for the ceremony. Of course they did. Even a man about to be erased deserves to look tidy while it happens.

The robe was too long in the sleeves—I have always had short arms, a fact my mother used to find charming and the Guild of Impossible Things found merely inefficient—and the collar chafed at the scar where my debt-brand sat beneath the skin like a second pulse. Two wardens walked me through the paper-vaults toward the Spire square, and I noticed, in the way a drowning man notices the color of the water, that the shelves we passed were half-empty. The masters had been withdrawing records all week. Preparing the ground. A century ago they would have burned the evidence; now they simply moved it somewhere no one could read it, which is the same thing as burning, only slower and better organized.

"You'll stand at the east rail," said the taller warden, whose name was Brannic and who had once, years ago, sold the memory of his own wedding to cover a gambling debt and now could not remember why he wore a ring. He was not unkind. He was worse than unkind: he was procedural. "You'll keep your eyes down. You'll make no sound until you're called."

"And then?" I asked.

"Then nothing," he said. "There's no 'then' for you. That's rather the point of erasure."

I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the whole point of what follows, so I will admit: my legs were shaking. Not trembling—shaking, the full-body tremor of a man who has spent thirty-one years being small on purpose and is about to stop, and whose body has just realized what stopping costs. I thought about Maren's kitchen on Ash Lane, the smell of burnt coffee and older paper. I thought about Hale's face when I told him what I intended, how he had looked at me for a long moment and then said, quietly, "Then you'd best speak loudly," which from Hale was practically a blessing.

Mostly I thought about Freya.

She would already be in the Spire's heart by now. They had taken her up before dawn, dressed in white this time, because the masters liked their sacrifices to match their linens. Somewhere above me, behind a hundred feet of stone that had been glass once, she was standing in front of the thing her language had sealed, preparing to do what every Freya before her had done: finish a sentence she never started, pay a debt she never owed.

I had promised her, in the last hour we shared through the wall between our cells, that I would come. She had laughed at me—a real laugh, the kind that made the warden outside shift uncomfortably—and said, "Theo, you're the most frightened person I've ever met. You flinch at loud doors."

"I do," I'd agreed. "But I'm coming anyway."

"Good," she'd said. "Frightened people check twice. You'll need that."

* * *

The Spire square held perhaps ten thousand people.

I know because I counted them, or tried to, the way I count everything when terror arrives—it's an old scribe habit, turning the world into entries. Ten thousand citizens of the lower city, packed shoulder to shoulder under a sky the color of unwashed wool, all of them facing the great western dais where the masked masters stood in a row like expensive furniture. The Centennial came but once in a hundred years, and even the poorest districts closed their shops for it. There were sausage vendors. Someone was selling little tin Spire charms on strings. A child near the rail was eating a candied apple with the single-minded devotion of someone who has correctly judged that ceremonies are boring.

That child, more than anything else, steadied me. Because that was the thing, wasn't it? Ten thousand people had come out of habit and festival hunger, and none of them—not one—knew what the ceremony actually was. They thought it was a blessing. They thought the masters renewed the city's fortunes the way a farmer renews a field. They did not know that the renewal had a price, and that the price had a name, and that the name this century was eight years old and drew pictures of towers in the margins of stolen ledgers.

The herald's voice rolled across the square, amplified by some guild engine I didn't recognize: *"Citizens! On this hundredth year, the Guild of Impossible Things renews its sacred covenant with the city it serves!"*

*Serves,* I thought. There was a word that deserved its own audit.

They brought Freya out onto the dais at mid-morning.

Even from the east rail, even at this distance, I knew her instantly—the small stubborn set of her shoulders, the way she looked at ten thousand strangers the way she'd once looked at me through an iron door: cataloguing, unimpressed, waiting for the interesting part. Her white robes swallowed her. Her hands were bound with silk cord, because the masters had learned over centuries that ordinary rope offended the old tongue and iron made it sing, and silk, apparently, was the compromise. The crowd murmured appreciatively. Somewhere a mother lifted her own child to see.

The First Master stepped forward. His mask was gold, featureless except for a single vertical seam down the center, and when he spoke his voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, an effect I had always assumed was majesty and now understood to be machinery.

*"For one hundred years,"* the doubled voice proclaimed, *"the covenant has held. The city prospers. Memory is kept clean. Today, as in every century, the terms are renewed."*

He raised his hand. Far below the dais, deep in the Spire's root, something answered—a low vibration I felt in my teeth, in the ledger pressed against my ribs beneath the grey robe, in the brand on my neck. The crowd gasped pleasantly, the way crowds gasp at fireworks.

And Freya turned her head, deliberately, and looked directly at the east rail where I stood.

She didn't smile. She didn't nod. She just looked at me with those gray eyes that had read my handwriting before I ever taught her mine, and she raised her bound hands maybe two inches—an amount small enough that the masters wouldn't notice, large enough that I would—and she mouthed one word in the old tongue.

I don't know how to spell it. We argued about the spelling constantly; she claimed my vowels were cowardly. But I knew what it meant. It meant *now.*

My hands stopped shaking.

It is a strange thing, the moment fear leaves. It doesn't drain away gradually, like water. It simply goes, all at once, replaced by something quieter and much more durable—the same feeling, I imagine, that a locked door feels when it finally admits it was wood all along. I reached inside my robe and touched the ledger there. Maren's copies. Three months of work, every page verified twice, because frightened people check twice.

Brannic leaned toward me. "Eyes down, scribe."

"Brannic," I said, "do you remember your wedding?"

He went very still. "What?"

"You wear a ring. Do you know whose wedding it was?"

His jaw worked. Whatever lived behind his eyes reached for the answer and found a smooth blank wall where a summer day should have been. "That's—" he started. "That's not—"

"The guild took it," I said. "They took it and they logged it and they sold it, and in about four minutes everyone in this square is going to know exactly how many things like that they've taken. Including yours." I pulled the ledger free. "I'm sorry. Truly. But you deserve to be angry, and anger needs facts."

The herald was announcing the Renewal. The masters were descending the dais steps toward the Spire's root-door, robes whispering, masks gleaming, the whole apparatus of a century of theft moving with the smooth confidence of people who have never once been interrupted.

I stepped over the rail.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it looks ridiculous from the outside.

A man in an ill-fitting grey robe climbing down from a ceremonial railing, clutching a book to his chest like a life-vest, stumbling on the second step and nearly going face-first into a sausage vendor's cart—that is not the stuff of ballads. The crowd around me recoiled. Wardens shouted. Somebody's tin Spire charm went skittering across the cobbles. And I stood there in the middle of the square, heart slamming, voice nowhere to be found, and for one horrible moment I thought thirty-one years of obedience had won after all, that my throat had simply forgotten how to do anything but say *yes, provost* and *of course, master.*

Then I saw the child with the candied apple, staring up at me, juice running down her wrist.

"Sorry about this," I told her. "It's going to get loud."

And I opened the ledger, and I read.

"*Entry four thousand one hundred twelve,*" my voice cracked out across the square, thin at first, then gathering, the way a fire gathers. "*One summer, age six, collected from Odessa Vail, cooper's daughter, in payment of her father's debt of nine crowns. The summer contained: a river, a dog named Button, and the learning of swimming. Sold onward to Master Ellery Voss. Current holder unknown.*"

The square did not go silent all at once. Silence spreads outward from a stone dropped in water, and so did this. Nearest me first—the vendor, the child, the wardens who had frozen mid-stride—then ring by ring, ten thousand faces turning, ten thousand conversations dying, until the only voice in the Spire square was mine.

"*Entry five thousand nine hundred three. The name Merrow, first given, collected entire from a debtor's infant son, per contract. The child was re-registered as 'the boy.' He answers to it still.*"

"*Entry eleven thousand forty. Forty years, collected in installments from Tomas Bright, lamplighter, who believed he was paying a fine. He died at fifty-one believing he was sixty-one. The discrepancy was noted by no one, because noting things is our business, and we do not note ourselves.*"

The masters had stopped on the dais steps. The First Master's golden mask turned toward me, and the doubled voice rang out—*"Seize that man!"*—but here is the beautiful thing, the thing I will treasure on whatever shelf they shelve me in: words, once public, cannot be re-collected. Every syllable I spoke passed into ten thousand ears and became theirs. The guild could burn my ledger. They could burn me. They could not reach into ten thousand heads and take the reading back. For the first time in a hundred years, the Guild of Impossible Things was holding an asset it could not collect.

So I kept reading.

I read the names of the traded memories. I read the bottled storms and the sold years and the borrowed faces. I read the entry where a widow's grief was purchased at market rate and resold at profit to a playwright who needed authentic sorrow for Act Three. I read the names of debtors born owing, entered into the ledgers before they could walk, because their grandparents had signed something in a famine winter and the interest had been compounding ever since. My voice grew hoarse. Someone in the crowd—Maren, I think, though I couldn't see her—began passing hand-copied pages forward, and other voices took up entries I hadn't reached yet, so that the readings overlapped and braided, a dozen voices, then dozens, the whole square becoming a scriptorium with the roof torn off.

And through it all, the masters stood frozen on their steps, because here was the flaw in their machine, the flaw they had built into it themselves: their power was bookkeeping, and bookkeeping requires that the books be believed. A ledger contradicted aloud, publicly, in front of the entire city, isn't just exposed. It's voided. Every mask in that square was suddenly just a mask.

The First Master descended the last steps and came toward me himself, and the crowd parted before him the way water parts, and I confess my knees remembered how to shake. Up close, the golden mask was scratched and old, and through its single seam I could hear breathing—just breathing, human and rapid. Underneath all that machinery, a man was suffocating in there.

"Scribe," the doubled voice said, and then, quieter, in a single unamplified thread, almost pleading: "Do you know what you've done?"

"Yes," I said. "Finally, yes."

"You'll destroy the covenant. You'll destroy the *city*—"

"No," I said. "I'm going to destroy the *bill*. The city's been paying it for a hundred years without seeing the itemized charges." I lifted the ledger so he could see the page. "And in a few minutes, the last line gets read. By the only person alive who speaks it properly."

Above us, the Spire began to hum.

It was not the pleasant ceremonial hum the crowd had gasped at earlier. This was deeper, older, rising through the stones and the soles of my feet and the fillings of my teeth, and along the Spire's black flank, seams that had been invisible for a century began to glow a faint, patient silver—the same light I had seen on the guild's oldest seals the night a mud-covered child knocked at our door. The masters' masks turned upward in unison, ten identical gestures of sudden, undignified alarm.

"She's speaking," the First Master whispered. "She's at the seal and she's *speaking*, the binding will fail, everything we—"

"Everything you built on top of a prison," I said, "is going to find out what happens when the prisoner is finally asked what she wants. Which, I notice, is a question none of your ledgers ever thought to ask anyone."

The silk cords binding Freya's hands, I learned later, had come apart like morning frost the moment she opened her mouth. The masters' wardens on the dais had found themselves unable to move—not held, exactly, but *unpersuaded*, every argument for obeying quietly dissolved by a voice speaking the grammar the world was drafted in. And the Spire, that great black finger pointing at heaven, had begun—not to fall, not yet, not the way anyone feared—but to *open*, light seeping through its seams like dawn through shutters.

In the square, the reading went on. My voice was nearly gone. It didn't matter. There were hundreds of voices now, shopkeepers and lamplighters and children, reading entries they'd only just been handed, learning the true accounting of their own lives in real time. The child with the candied apple was holding a page upside down and reciting it anyway, with tremendous conviction.

I looked up at the glowing Spire, at the place where a girl in white robes stood at the root of everything, deciding.

Whatever she chose next, the guild's claim on memory had already shattered here in the square, irrecoverably, one read-aloud entry at a time. But the seal beneath the Spire was another matter entirely—a war frozen mid-sentence, a speaker begging to be finished, and one eight-year-old girl with the oldest language in the world finally, freely, hers to use.

Freya drew a breath that the whole city felt.

And she began to speak.
