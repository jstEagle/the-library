## 21. A New Grammar

The school had eleven chairs, and Freya had counted them so many times she could have named each one's splinters.

They stood in a room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, where the wax smell seeped up through the floorboards and made everything feel faintly sweet, even in winter. The walls were Theo's doing—whitewashed, then covered, at her insistence, with words. Not guild-standard script. Not the cramped ledger-hand that had been drilled into every scribe for a hundred years. Her words. The old tongue, written large and looping and unapologetic across the plaster, the way it was meant to be written: *for reading aloud*, as she kept telling him, *not for hiding*.

"You've spelled *morning* wrong again," Theo said from his desk by the window. He said it the way he said most things now—dry as old paper, with the smile hidden underneath where only she could find it.

"I have spelled it *right*," Freya said. "You spell it like a guild clerk who learned it from a dead man's notes."

"I learned it from *you*."

"Then you weren't listening."

He set down his pen and gave her the look—the one that meant he was about to be pedantic and knew it and was going to do it anyway, because arguing about spelling was, they had both quietly agreed without ever saying so, the finest entertainment either of them had ever been given.

"The vowel cluster," he said, tapping the page. "In your rendering, the second syllable opens downward. In mine, it opens upward. Mine matches the inscription on the third vault door."

"Which I opened," Freya said, "by singing it correctly."

"Which you opened," Theo conceded, "by humming at it until it gave up out of embarrassment."

She laughed. She was still not entirely used to laughing—still caught herself sometimes in the middle of it, surprised, the way you might be surprised by sunlight in a room you'd known only dark. In the Spire's heart there had been no laughter. There had barely been sound at all, except the long slow breathing of the sealed one, and the scratch of the masters' pens recording that breathing as an asset.

That was before. This was after. After smelled like candle wax and chalk dust and, this morning, the bread Maren had sent up from Ash Lane, still warm, wrapped in cloth that had once been a forbidden archive's dust cover. Maren had declared it a fitting end for the cloth. "Everything forbidden should end up holding bread," she'd said. "It's the only honest retirement."

Freya crossed to the wall and looked at the word she'd written—*morning*, in her hand, in the old tongue—and touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped would stay asleep.

The word did nothing. That was the whole point.

When she had first come down from—wherever she had come from, the tower of glass that lived now only in her remembering, in the shape of homesickness with no address—her words had *done things*. They had opened locks. They had made listeners forget. They had hummed against seals older than kingdoms and made the oldest thing under the city stir in its long half-sleep. Every word she spoke had weight, and every weight landed on someone else.

Now the words were spread thin. Deliberately, patiently, over two years, spread thin as butter over too much bread—which was how Theo described it, and which was exactly right. Eleven chairs. Sometimes fourteen students, when the weather was bad and the dockworkers' children came in out of the rain. A fishwife who came on her one free afternoon a week. An old warden, formerly of the guild, who sat in the back and wept quietly the first time he successfully pronounced the word for *forgive*, and who now never missed a lesson.

Every person who learned a word carried a piece of its power away with them. No single voice could gather it all again. The language could not be owned—not by a guild, not by a girl, not by anyone. It could only be shared, and sharing, Freya had learned, was the strongest seal there was. The good kind. The kind that held doors open instead of shut.

"You're brooding," Theo said. "I can tell because you're touching the wall like it owes you money."

"The wall owes me nothing." She turned around. "I'm counting chairs."

"Eleven?"

"Eleven."

"And how many do we need?"

"More than eleven." She said it simply, because it was simply true. Word had traveled, the way word does when a thing is wanted badly enough. Children came whose parents had lost names to the guild's ledgers and wanted those names back, or at least wanted to know how to write new ones properly, in a script that belonged to no institution. Adults came who had spent their whole lives being told what their memories were worth and had decided, finally, to keep their own accounts. One woman came who had sold six years to pay her husband's debts and wanted, more than anything, to learn the word for *enough*.

Freya taught her that word first. It took three lessons. On the fourth, the woman wrote it on her own front door, and the neighbors began to come.

"We need more chairs," Freya said now, "and a bigger room, and Theo—we need to talk about the alphabet."

Theo groaned with genuine feeling. "No."

"Yes."

"There is nothing wrong with the alphabet."

"There is everything wrong with the alphabet. You have forty-one letters arranged in an order that makes no sense, three of which are identical except for a dot, and one—" she pointed accusingly at the chart on the far wall, which he had copied out in beautiful, meticulous, completely wrong-handed script, "—one which is *upside down*. You have hung it upside down since the beginning and refused to hear otherwise."

"It is not upside down. The descender goes below the line. That's structural."

"In the tower of glass," Freya said, with the patience of someone deploying a weapon she knew was unfair but loved anyway, "children learn that letter before they learn to walk. Its tail goes *up*. It is shaped like a bird taking off. Yours looks like a bird that has been dropped."

"A bird that has been *rooted*—"

"Birds don't root, Theo."

"This one does. It's in the vault inscriptions."

"The vault inscriptions were carved by frightened people copying frightened copies," Freya said. "Half your sacred alphabet is fossilized mistakes. Which is why—" and here she softened, because underneath the argument there was the real thing, the thing they were actually building, "—which is why we're going to fix it together. Slowly. With everyone. Not me decreeing and not you decreeing. The students vote. Maren arbitrates when we deadlock, and she enjoys it far too much, and we let her, because a school where nobody argues is a school where somebody is afraid."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Then he picked up his pen again, and she saw him write something small in the margin of his page, and she didn't ask what it was, because some things a teacher lets a student keep.

He had been her student first, though neither of them framed it that way anymore. Back when she was mud-caked and nameless at the iron door, back when he was supposed to erase her and instead hid her true page inside a dead debtor's file—that had been the first lesson either of them ever taught. He had taught her that a rule could be broken gently. She had taught him that a word could be kept instead of collected.

The bell over the chandler's door rang downstairs, and footsteps came up, and the morning arrived the way mornings did now: not processed, not audited, just arriving.

* * *

The first through the door was always Tam, the chandler's daughter, eight years old and built entirely of questions, who took the chair nearest the wall-words and immediately pointed at the upside-down-or-not letter.

"That one's wrong," Tam announced.

"See?" Freya said to Theo.

"She's new, she doesn't know the tradition—"

"Traditions are just mistakes that got comfortable," Tam said, which she had absolutely learned from Maren, and Freya laughed and Theo put his face in his hands, and the lesson began.

They started, as always, with names. This was Freya's own ritual, and she guarded it fiercely. Before anything else, each student wrote their own name in the old tongue, said it aloud, and heard the room say it back. Not because the name gained power—though it did, a little, the harmless kind, the kind that settled into a person like warmth into stone—but because the guild had spent a century teaching this city that names were entries, assets, lines in other people's books. The first act of the school was to take the name back.

Today there was a new student. A young man, maybe nineteen, who hovered in the doorway with the particular posture of someone who expected to be thrown out, and who introduced himself in a voice that shook only slightly:

"Pell. My father was a debtor. He's dead now. I found—I found one of the old ledgers, after, with his name in it. Under 'assets.' I want to know if there's a word that can..." He stopped. Started again. "I want to know if names can be taken *out* of books."

The room went quiet. Freya felt Theo go still beside her, the old stillness, the audit-day stillness, and she reached over without looking and rested her hand on his arm until it eased.

"Yes," she said. "There is such a word. But it isn't one you say alone, and it isn't one I give you. It's one we'll build together, all of us, and when it's finished, we'll read your father's ledger entry aloud in this room, and then we'll cross it out, and then—" she smiled, and it was the smile that had once unmade a seal, repurposed now for gentler demolition, "—then we'll burn the page to light the stove. It's cold in here on Thursdays."

Pell sat down in the twelfth chair—the one they kept for exactly this, the chair with no assigned place—and Tam leaned over and whispered to him, loudly enough for everyone to hear, that the bird letter was upside down, and the lesson rolled forward like a cart finding its ruts.

Afterward, when the students had gone and the chalk dust had settled into the wax-smell and the light through the window had gone gold and low, Freya stood at the wall adding the day's new words while Theo banked the stove fire.

"Do you ever miss it?" he asked, from the floor, not looking up. "The power. Having a word that could crack a vault."

Freya considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because she asked herself the same thing on quiet evenings.

"When I speak *morning* now," she said, "and forty-one letters' worth of students say it back to me, wrong, most of them, beautifully wrong, each in their own accent—" she tapped the wall, the looping bright word, "—it opens something bigger than any vault. It just doesn't open it *for* me. It opens it for everyone. That's better. That's the sentence I was sent down to finish, and it turns out the last word was *ours*."

Theo stood, brushed off his knees, and came to stand beside her. For a while they just looked at the wall together, teacher and teacher, student and student, the two roles worn smooth from trading.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we're voting on the bird letter."

"We are voting on the bird letter, and you're going to lose."

"I am going to lose with dignity, and then I'm going to spend ten years insisting the old form was structurally superior, and our students will roll their eyes, and it will be wonderful."

It would be, she thought. It already was.

She was reaching for the chalk to add one final word to the day's wall—a small word, a private one, the old-tongue word for *home*, which she had never yet taught because she was saving it, saving it for the day the school felt permanent instead of borrowed—when the knock came at the door.

Not the students' knock, which was a cheerful racket. Not Maren's knock, which was three sharp raps meaning *I've found something and you won't like it*.

This was a single knock. Soft. Almost apologetic.

And when Freya opened the door, the hallway beyond was empty—except that on the threshold, laid neatly on the boards where no one stood, was a single sheet of paper, written in the old tongue, in a hand she did not know.

She read it twice. Then she read it a third time, and her breath went strange in her chest, because the handwriting was impossible, and the message was simple, and it said:

*There is another speaker. She is coming down. And she is asking for the one who finished the sentence.*
