## 1. Salt and Small Wonders

The tide came in grey that morning, and Xanthe Fairweather counted it a good omen.

Grey weather meant the fishermen would be nervous. Nervous fishermen bought charms. She had learned the arithmetic of fear long ago—learned it in her first winter in Greyharbor, when she'd stood in the market square with nothing but cold hands and a talent she could not name aloud, watching an old woman sell knotted cord to men who kissed the knots before walking down to their boats. The old woman had charged two coppers apiece. The men had paid without haggling. Fear, Xanthe had discovered, was the one coin that never lost its value.

So she rose before dawn in her rented room above the chandler's shop, and she worked.

Her stall occupied the third arch of the Fishmongers' Colonnade, between a woman who sold eels still writhing in their barrels and a man whose entire trade was dried cod arranged in shapes suggestive of other things. It was not a dignified position, but it was a defensible one: close enough to the harbor mouth that the salt wind reached her, far enough from the customs house that the customs officers rarely bothered. She hung her wares from iron hooks driven into the stone—luck-knots braided from tarred twine, storm-glasses in blown glass the color of bottle-green, little woven bracelets for wives who wanted their husbands home before dark. And at the back of the stall, half-hidden behind a bolt of oilcloth, the expensive things: sea-charms stitched with a single thread of silver, guaranteed—or so the card said, in her careful merchant's hand—to keep a hull upright in any weather short of wrath.

She never guaranteed anything about wrath. Wrath was her own country. She did not sell what she could not part with.

"Fairweather!" The voice came down the colonnade ahead of its owner, as Tam Breech's voice always did, arriving like weather. He was a trawlerman built like his own mast, wide through the shoulders and splintered at the crown, and he arrived at her stall breathing fish and urgency in equal measure. "Tell me true, now. Tell me honest. What's the water say?"

"The water says," Xanthe said, "that you'll want to take the eastern channel and be back inside the point by midafternoon."

Tam squinted at the sky, which offered him nothing but a low, unbroken lid of cloud. "You can't know that."

"No," Xanthe agreed pleasantly. "I can't." She plucked a luck-knot from its hook and turned it over in her fingers, letting him watch her consider it, letting him watch her *not* sell it to him yet. This was the dance, and they both knew the steps. "But I've been reading this sky since before you had all your teeth, Tam, and the sky agrees with me. Eastern channel. Home by midafternoon. There's a squall coming up from the southwest after that, and it will have teeth."

"You're certain?"

"I'm never certain. Certainty is for priests and liars." She held out the knot. "But I'll stake my reputation on it, such as it is. Two coppers."

He paid four, because he always did, and pressed the extra two into her palm with the gravity of a man making an offering at a shrine. "For the little saint of the colonnade," he said, grinning through his beard. His crew called her that behind her back; she pretended not to know. "If we come back rich, I'm buying your whole stock."

"If you come back rich," Xanthe said, "buy the storm-glass instead. It'll last longer than your luck."

He laughed and went, and she watched him go, and only when his back was safely turned did she let her fingers curl closed around the warmth of the morning—the faint pressure of the air, the taste of the wind off the water, the deep slow pulse of the tide that she felt in her sternum the way other people felt their own heartbeat. The squall was real. She had not read it in the clouds. She had felt it being born three hundred miles southwest, out past the Serrated Isles, a child of heat and open water, and she knew it would arrive hungry.

She could have unmade it. That was the joke she kept locked in her chest where no one could hear it laughing. Three hundred miles away, a storm was gathering itself out of the warm sea, and if Xanthe Fairweather had wanted—if she had stood on the breakwater at dawn and opened that door inside herself that she kept barred and padlocked and painted over—she could have reached out along the wind and put her hand on its shoulder and told it *no*. Told it *not today*. Told it *turn aside, go starve somewhere else*.

Instead she sold knots for two coppers and let the fishermen trust the knot.

It was better this way. She had twenty years of evidence that it was better this way, and most of that evidence was buried.

* * *

Midmorning brought the market to its full roar, and with it the small commerce of small wonders that made up her days.

A girl of perhaps ten came with her mother to buy a charm against night terrors, and Xanthe talked them down from the silver-threaded one to a simple blue worsted braid, because night terrors in children were almost never what the parents feared and almost always what the child ate before bed. A young couple argued cheerfully over whether a fertility charm should hang above the bed or below it, and Xanthe, who had opinions but no standing, suggested the windowsill as a compromise and made two sales instead of one. An old crab-woman named Doss, who had been selling off the same pier since before Xanthe was born, bought nothing but stayed an hour, because Doss considered conversation a form of rent and paid it to everyone.

"You've got a look today," Doss said, settling onto the upturned crate she had claimed as hers sometime during the previous century. "Tight around the eyes."

"It's the light."

"It's never the light in Greyharbor. Light here is a rumor." Doss cracked a boiled crab claw between her teeth with the sound of a door slamming. "Tight around the eyes means trouble coming, or trouble remembered. Which is it?"

"Neither." Xanthe straightened a row of storm-glasses that did not need straightening. "I slept badly."

"You slept badly because you were listening."

Xanthe's hands went still. Only for a moment—a breath, no more—and then they were moving again, sorting, arranging, the merchant's smile sliding back into place like a mask she'd worn so long it had grown roots. "Listening to what?"

"To whatever it is you listen to." Doss gestured with the remains of the claw, encompassing the harbor, the sky, the world. "Twenty years I've watched you stand in this colonnade, girl, and I'll tell you what I see. Everyone else looks at the weather. You *greet* it. Like it owes you money." She chewed. "I don't ask questions. Questions are bad for business, and my business is crabs. But I'm old, and old women get to say one true thing a day, and here's mine: whatever you're hiding under that smile, it's getting heavier. Heavy things sink. Best learn to float before the water finds you."

Xanthe laughed—light, easy, a laugh she kept oiled for exactly these occasions. "Doss, if I didn't know better, I'd say you were trying to charm *me*."

"Everything in this city is charming something," the old woman said, and got up, and left her crate, and shuffled away toward the crab-boats, and Xanthe stood in the noise of the market feeling as though someone had drawn a knife along a seam she'd thought was invisible.

Because it was true. That was the unbearable thing about Doss. It was true. Twenty years of smiling, twenty years of two-copper magic and carefully mundane forecasts, twenty years of feeling the storms come up the coast like letters addressed to her that she refused to open—and every year the door inside her got heavier, the bar across it thicker, the lock rustier and more desperate. Some mornings she woke with her hands aching from clenching in her sleep, and the pillow smelling of ozone, and she would lie there in the grey light and think: *one day the bar breaks. One day it breaks and everything I am comes out sideways and I cannot choose where.*

She had chosen, once. Long ago. She had chosen wrong, or someone had chosen for her, and the shape of that memory was a locked room even in her own head—she knew the room existed, knew it sat at the center of her like a stone in a fruit, and whenever she reached for it her mind slid away, smooth as wet glass. Twenty years ago. Her sister's face. A burning room. A signature on paper. Fragments, no more, like shards of a mirror too dangerous to reassemble.

Isolde. Even the name hurt now, the way a healed bone hurts before rain.

"Charm-seller." A new voice, young, breathless. Xanthe looked up to find Wren at her stall—the girl from Tanner's Lane, sharp-elbowed and quick-eyed, who bought a luck-knot every week with coins earned running messages and spent them like a duchess. "Have you got anything for—" Wren stopped, glanced over her shoulder, lowered her voice. "For keeping secrets? Something a person could wear, so nobody could... read them?"

"A secret-keeper." Xanthe reached beneath the counter, genuinely amused. "That's a new request. Most people want the opposite—they want to be *known*, loudly, by everyone." She rummaged past the storm-glasses and found what she was looking for: a plain grey knot, unremarkable, tied in a pattern she had learned from no book and no teacher. "This won't stop anyone determined. Nothing does. But it'll make you easy to look past. Unmemorable. Two coppers."

Wren paid, and then, instead of leaving, lingered, turning the knot over in her fingers with an odd, frowning attention. "Where did you learn to tie like this?" she asked suddenly. "The loops. My mum's charm has loops like that, the crossing-over kind, three and three. She got it from a peddler years ago, a dying woman passing through Hollowmere, upcountry. Mum says it was the finest work she ever saw."

The market noise did not stop. The gulls went on screaming. The eel-woman went on shouting about the freshness of her eels. None of it reached Xanthe, because in the space of one sentence the ground had quietly opened beneath her stall and swallowed her whole.

Three-and-three, crossing over. The Fairweather knot. Their grandmother's pattern, taught to two girls on a hillside in another life, in another country, before fire and paper and a signature Xanthe could not remember. Isolde had tied it left-handed. Xanthe tied it right. Every charm Xanthe had ever sold carried that signature, and she had told herself for twenty years that it meant nothing, that patterns traveled, that ten thousand hands in ten thousand towns might stumble on the same crossings—

Hollowmere. A dying woman. Upcountry. Inland, toward the mountains, toward the court beneath the mountain that existed mostly in smugglers' stories and mothers' warnings.

"Charm-seller?" Wren was looking at her strangely. "You've gone white as cod."

"Fine work," Xanthe heard herself say. Her voice came from very far away, from the bottom of the opened ground. "This peddler. Did your mother catch her name?"

"No. Dying women don't leave names." Wren tucked the secret-keeper into her collar, and her quick eyes narrowed, and for a moment she looked much older than her years. "Why? Do you know her?"

*No,* Xanthe wanted to say. *Yes. She is dead, or worse than dead, and I have spent twenty years not thinking about it, and you have just made me think about it in the middle of a fish market on a Tuesday.*

What she said was: "I knew someone who tied like that. Once."

And then the bells began.

* * *

They came up from the customs house side, and everyone in the colonnade heard them before anyone understood them: the raid-bells, the double-stroke, the rhythm that meant *close your stalls, clear the square, the crown is among us.* The market changed in a heartbeat—not scattering, Greyharbor was too old and too cynical to scatter, but *hardening*, the way a shellfish hardens, everyone drawing inward, voices dropping, hands moving to hide the small illegal margins that every stall kept. The eel-woman kicked a barrel into shadow. The cod-man flipped his cloth. Somewhere a mother pulled a child indoors by the wrist without breaking stride.

Xanthe's hands were already moving on their own, twenty years of practice outrunning thought. Luck-knots were legal. Storm-glasses were legal. Charms were legal, so long as they were *only* charms—so long as they were rope and glass and thread and nothing else, so long as no one could prove that the woman who sold them was anything more than a clever merchant with a feel for weather. She swept the silver-threaded pieces into their box, slid the box beneath the oilcloth, and stood behind her stall with her heart going like a drum and her face arranged into pleasant, harmless blankness.

The hunters came down the colonnade in a wedge of six, in the grey-and-white livery of the Crown Registry, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a keel. They were not soldiers, exactly. Soldiers had a job and went home. These walked like men who had found a job that fed something in them. At their center moved a clerk with a ledger chained to his belt, and a reader—a thin, grey-lipped official wearing the brass collar of a Truthfinder, whose gift was small and state-approved and whose gaze moved across the crowd like a lantern across a field, hunting.

Xanthe knew how this worked. Registration rolls. Names taken, marks noted, promises extracted. And then, weeks later, in the dark, the seizures—the quiet doors, the quiet wagons, the neighbors who saw nothing and said less. It had started in the capital. It had crept down the coast all spring. Everyone had said it wouldn't come to Greyharbor. Everyone always said that.

The reader stopped three stalls away, at the fortune-teller's corner, where old Sefa read palms with cards and greasepaint and no gift at all. The reader's lantern-gaze passed over Sefa, found nothing, moved on—

—and swung toward Xanthe.

There was no reason. There was nothing to find; her stall was clean, her papers were clean, her face was clean. But the reader's grey eyes fixed on her anyway, and took a step, and Xanthe felt it happen—the thing she had spent two decades preventing, the door inside her shuddering on its hinges, because fear called to the storm in her blood like blood calls to blood. Pressure gathered behind her breastbone. The air above the colonnade tightened. Out past the harbor mouth, where an hour ago there had been nothing but flat grey water, the sea darkened, and a wind came up the channel that had no business existing yet, that she had *felt* being born three hundred miles away and known was not due until afternoon—

*No.* She slammed the bar down. She did it the way she had done it ten thousand times, the ugly internal violence of it, taking all that rising power—all that bright, furious, magnificent *self*—and crushing it small, folding it down, drowning it in the cellar of her body until it screamed and went quiet. The wind died mid-gust. The dark patch on the water smoothed over. To anyone watching, nothing had happened at all except weather behaving oddly, and weather in Greyharbor behaved oddly by trade.

But the reader had stopped walking.

The grey eyes narrowed. The clerk's pen paused above the ledger. For three heartbeats—four—the whole machinery of the crown's attention hung suspended on the thread of a stranger's suspicion, and Xanthe stood behind her stall with her hands folded and her smile fixed and her magic bleeding quietly in the cellar of her, and understood with perfect clarity that she had maybe ten seconds to decide whether this careful life of hers had just ended.

Then a fish barrel overturned.

It was nothing—an eel-woman's boy, clumsy, mortified, eels everywhere, the whole colonnade descending into chaos and cursing—and by the time the eels were recaptured the reader had moved on, drawn away by some other flicker, some other suspicion, and the wedge of grey-and-white passed down the colonnade and out the far end, and the bells stopped, and the market exhaled.

Xanthe did not exhale. She stood very still, counting her own heartbeat, waiting for her hands to stop trembling. They did not stop. They were still trembling when Doss appeared at her elbow, silent as a crab, and pressed something into her palm: a single boiled claw, wrapped in cloth.

"For the shaking," the old woman said. "Eat it. And then, girl—I'd think hard about floating."

Across the square, Wren was watching her too, hand at her collar, fingers curled around a grey knot—and behind her, tacked to the customs house door, flapping in the recovered wind, a fresh proclamation in red wax and black ink that the criers would read aloud within the hour.

Xanthe couldn't read it from here. She didn't need to. She already knew, in the marrow of her, that whatever it said, it was addressed to her.
