## 2. The Thirteenth Moon Proclamation

The bells woke her before the criers did.

Xanthe Fairweather had lived in Greyharbor long enough to know its every sound—the groan of the tide-gates at dawn, the gull-cries over the fish stalls, the particular clatter of the rope-walk when the net-menders started early. She knew the bells too: the harbor bell that rang in fog, the chapel bell that rang for weddings and drownings alike, the small cracked bell above the chandlery that the owner's boy struck whenever a ship came in heavy-laden.

But this was none of those. This was the great bell in the customs tower, the one that had not sounded since the old king died, and it did not ring so much as toll—slow, deliberate strokes that rolled down through the terraced streets like stones dropped into deep water.

She lay in her narrow bed above the charm-shop and listened, and the storm she kept always coiled behind her breastbone stirred in its sleep.

*No*, she told it, the way she told it every morning. *Not today.*

By the time she dressed and shuttered her lamp, the criers were already in the streets below. She heard them before she saw them—a drumbeat, then a voice pitched to carry across rooftops, flat and official as a writ read aloud.

"Hear ye! By decree of the Crown Regent, in the season of the Thirteenth Moon: all persons gifted with unlicensed craft shall present themselves for registration within one turning of the moon. Registration is protection. Registration is provision. Those who conceal their gift after the appointed day shall be seized as enemies of the realm's peace."

Xanthe stood at her window with her hands on the sill and watched the first crier come down Salt Lane. He was young, a city guard's tabard over his shoulders, and he walked with the stiff pride of a man who believed what he was reading. Behind him two soldiers carried a board painted with the crown's seal—and beneath the seal, inked fresh and black, a single image: a thorned branch, curled like a sleeping serpent.

Her mouth went dry.

She had seen that sigil before. Not on any proclamation, not on any soldier's shield. On charms. On old charms, decades old, worked into knot-craft by hands long dead—the kind of sigil that appeared in pattern-books from the mountain towns, the kind her teachers had called *old road-marking* and never explained. A thorned branch. A root.

And now it hung beneath the crown's own seal, proclaimed from every corner in Greyharbor, and no one in the crowd below seemed to find it strange.

They should have. That was the thing about port cities—she had learned it in twenty years of selling luck-knots to fishermen. Port folk were superstitious but not stupid; they could smell a lie the way sharks smelled blood. And yet they stood in the grey morning light with their baskets and their children and listened to *registration is protection* as though it were weather news, as though it were the price of herring.

An old woman near the fountain crossed herself with salt. A fishwife spat. But most of them only nodded, murmuring, and moved along, and Xanthe understood with cold clarity that the hunt had been prepared for longer than the proclamation had existed. The country had been taught to accept it before anyone announced it.

The storm behind her ribs rolled over in its sleep, restless.

"Down," she whispered to it. "Down. We've been quiet twenty years. We can be quiet a little longer."

---

The shop opened late that day, because her hands would not stop shaking and she refused to let customers see it.

Fairweather Charms occupied the ground floor of a narrow house on Tidewalk Row, wedged between a sailmaker and a woman who sold pickled eels out of barrels. It was, by design, an unremarkable shop. Luck-knots in red and grey cord. Weather-charms braided into whalebone rings. Small woven stars for children's cradles, sea-glass beads for safe passage, the little knotted hands fishermen nailed above their doors against drowning. Nothing that smelled of true craft. Nothing that would draw the eye of a hunter or the envy of a rival charm-wife. Xanthe had spent two decades making herself small, and the shop was the shape of that smallness: tidy, dim, forgettable.

She lit the lamp, set out her wares, and tried to work.

Her fingers knew the patterns without her mind's help—over, under, draw tight, bless and release—and so her mind was free to run in circles around the proclamation, around the thorned branch beneath the crown's seal, around the word *seized* delivered in a young man's clear proud voice.

Registration is protection.

She had heard that promise before. In another town, in another life, when she was a girl of nine with lightning under her skin and a sister who held her hand through everything. *Register the girl*, the village elders had said then, kindly, reasonably. *It's for her own safety.* And Isolde, sixteen and fierce, had stood in front of her and said, *Over my dead body,* and meant it, and the registering had never happened—but something else had, something worse, something Xanthe could not remember no matter how she turned the missing years over in her mind like a stone with a hole where its heart should be.

Twenty years ago. Twenty years since she'd woken on a cart heading south with no memory of the fire, no memory of the choice, no memory of anything except that Isolde was gone and everyone looked at her with pity or fear and no one would say why.

Now the crown wanted names. Now the crown wanted gifts laid out on tables like goods at market, weighed and listed and owned.

She thought of the mountain towns. Hollowmere, the caravanners called it, up past the passes where the fog came down like wool. She hadn't thought of it in years—had trained herself not to—but the name rose now with the taste of woodsmoke and pine resin, and underneath it, faint as a scent through a closed door, the memory of a voice saying *we'll be safe there, little storm, I promise you.*

Isolde's voice.

Xanthe pressed her palms flat against the counter until the grain bit into her skin.

*One moon*, said the proclamation. One turning of the moon to register, and after that, seizure. She had watched seizures happen in other ports, other years—quiet ones, a family gone overnight, shutters closed, boat sold cheap. The gifted who registered were never seen trading again. The gifted who fled were hunted. There was no third door. There never had been.

She would have to run. The knowledge settled over her like a wet cloak, heavy and familiar. She'd known this day would come eventually; she'd simply hoped to die first, quietly, an old charm-wife with knots in her fingers and nothing stranger than a talent for weather-sense. Twenty years of hiding deserved a better ending than a hunters' wagon.

But there were preparations. Coin to gather, hidden under the floorboard where she kept her escape purse. Stock to sell off or abandon. A story to leave behind—she was good at stories; she'd been telling Greyharbor that she was merely a widowed charm-wife with arthritic hands and no gift at all for longer than some of her customers had been alive.

And one more thing. The thought arrived quietly, the way important thoughts do, dressed in ordinary clothes.

*Hollowmere.*

If the court was hunting the gifted—if the crown's seal now bore a thorned root that belonged to old mountain craft—then somewhere in the archives of whatever power ran this hunt, there might be records. Records of registrations. Records of sales. Records of a girl named Fairweather who vanished south twenty years ago, and a sister named Fairweather who—

Who what? Who stayed? Who was taken? Who sold herself?

The question had teeth. Xanthe had spent two decades refusing to let it bite, because every time she reached for the memory of that last night in the mountain town, she found smoke and a locked door and nothing else. But now the crown itself was pulling threads, and if the crown was pulling threads toward Hollowmere, then perhaps the answers were being dragged into the light whether she wanted them or not.

She could run south, as she'd always planned. Or she could run *up*—into the mountains, into the fog, into the town whose name tasted like woodsmoke and grief.

The storm behind her ribs turned over again, and this time it felt less like restlessness and more like attention.

*Later*, she told it. *First we survive the week.*

---

* * *

The last customer came an hour before dusk, when the light through the shop window had gone the color of weak tea.

Xanthe was packing. She hadn't meant to start packing in plain sight, but her hands had decided the matter for her somewhere around midday, and now the shelves stood half-empty, the luck-knots bundled in oilcloth, the whalebone rings in a canvas sack. Any customer walking in would see a shop closing down, and that was fine—that was exactly the story she wanted Greyharbor to tell after she was gone. *Old Fairweather, her joints got bad, went south to her sister's people.* Let them say it. Sisters made excellent fiction.

The bell above the door jangled.

"I'm closed," Xanthe said, not looking up. "Come back—"

"Please." A girl's voice, breathless, young. "Please, they said you were the best. They said if anyone could mend it, you could."

Xanthe looked up.

The girl in her doorway couldn't have been more than fourteen, maybe fifteen, thin as a gull's keel, with wind-chapped cheeks and hair the color of wet kelp escaping from under a knit cap. Her eyes were red-rimmed, and she clutched something in both fists against her chest the way children clutch things they've been told are precious and are afraid will be taken.

Xanthe knew that clutch. She'd worn it herself once, a lifetime ago, standing in front of a village elder while her sister's hand gripped her shoulder.

"What is it?" Xanthe asked, and heard her own voice go gentle despite everything.

The girl crossed the shop in three quick steps and opened her hands on the counter.

A charm lay in her palms. A knot-work charm, sea-grey cord gone soft with age and handling, worked in a pattern Xanthe knew the way she knew her own heartbeat—because she had learned that pattern at six years old, sitting cross-legged on a mountain floor, watching her sister's fingers fly.

*Over, under, draw tight. No—like this, little storm. You're rushing. The knot has to hold the wish the way a hand holds a bird: firm enough it stays, gentle enough it lives.*

The room tilted.

Xanthe put her hand on the counter to steady herself and stared at the charm, and the shop, the dusk, the girl—all of it receded as though she were looking up from underwater. Because this was not a pattern from any pattern-book. This was not common craft. This was *Isolde's* pattern, the one her sister had invented herself, the one she'd called the *holding-knot*, the one she used for charms meant to keep something precious from slipping away—and no one else in the world worked it, because no one else had ever been taught it, because Xanthe was the only person Isolde had ever—

"Where," Xanthe said, and her voice came out wrong, scraped thin, "did you get this?"

The girl flinched. "I—I'm sorry, is it—is it stolen? I didn't steal it, I swear, a lady gave it to me—"

"A lady." Xanthe's heart was slamming against the storm behind her ribs, and for once she didn't tell the storm to lie down. "What lady? Where?"

"In Hollowmere." The words tumbled out of the girl like water from a tipped bucket. "My gran's from there, we went last summer to visit her sister, and there was a lady in the lane by the chapel, and she was ill, you could see it, all grey-faced and holding her chest like something inside her was *leaking*—and she saw me staring at her charm and she gave it to me. Just gave it to me. Said—" The girl's brow furrowed, reaching for exact words the way you reach for something floating just out of grasp. "Said, *keep it close, it knows the way home*. And then she coughed, awful, and my gran pulled me away, and by the time we came back down the lane she was gone. Gran said she was probably dead by winter. Gran said the whole town's half-empty now, everyone gifted's leaving or being took."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, a gull cried once and fell silent, as though it too were listening.

*Xanthe. Little storm. Keep it close—it knows the way home.*

Twenty years dead—or sold, or taken, or lost—and her sister's voice still found its way through the world's noise to land precisely where it would hurt most.

"What was her name?" Xanthe asked. "The lady. Did she give a name?"

The girl shook her head. "No. But—" she hesitated, chewing her lip. "Gran said afterward, when she asked around, someone told her the lady used to be called something like... Sol. Or Solde. Everyone called her Sol."

*Sol.* Short for Solde. Short for Isolde. The nickname Xanthe herself had coined at seven years old, because *Isolde* was too many syllables for a child in a hurry, and her sister had laughed and said *fine, then Sol I am, and Sol I'll stay.*

Nobody else had ever called her that. Nobody else *could* have.

The storm behind Xanthe's ribs rose at last—not in rage, not in the wild surging panic she'd spent twenty years smothering, but in something quieter and far more dangerous. It rose the way dawn rises: inevitable, illuminating, changing the shape of everything it touched.

Dread had brought her to the edge of flight. But this—this was not dread anymore. This was purpose, arriving with the sudden weight of a keel settling into water.

Because the charm in the girl's hands was not merely a keepsake. It was a message. *It knows the way home.* Isolde had given away her own holding-knot to a passing child, knowing—somehow knowing—that someday it would travel, that someday it would arrive, that someday Xanthe would stand in a shop in Greyharbor and look at her sister's handiwork and understand: *come find me. Come find what they did.*

Or perhaps: *come find what I did.*

Either way, the path pointed one direction. Up. Into the mountains. To Hollowmere.

"How much do I owe you?" the girl asked, misreading her silence entirely. "For the mending? I've got four coppers and a button."

Xanthe looked at her—at the chapped cheeks, the hopeful frightened eyes, the whole skinny brave frame of her—and something in her chest ached with a tenderness that surprised her. This child had no idea what she was carrying. No idea that she'd just handed a fugitive the map to her own buried life.

"Nothing," Xanthe said. "Keep your coppers." She reached out and touched the charm—one finger, feather-light, feeling the old cord hum faintly against her skin like a plucked string still ringing. "But tell me your name. If I'm to mend a charm, I like to know who it belongs to."

"Wren," the girl said. "Wren Marsh."

"Wren Marsh." Xanthe nodded slowly, committing it to memory the way she committed all important things. "Well, Wren Marsh. This charm doesn't need mending. It needs—" She stopped, hearing her own words arrive from somewhere deeper than caution. "It needs following."

Wren blinked. "Following?"

"The lady who gave it to you." Xanthe met the girl's eyes, and let her see—not everything, never everything, but enough. Enough to make the child understand this mattered beyond all ordinary measure. "I knew her. A long time ago. And I think she left that charm where you'd find it because she wanted me to know she's still—"

*Still what? Alive? Dead? Sold? Waiting?*

"—still owed something," Xanthe finished. "By me. And I pay my debts."

Outside, the customs bell tolled again—three slow strokes, evening count, the crown counting its city like coins in a purse. Somewhere down the street, a second crier had begun the proclamation again, the words drifting through the dusk: *registration is protection, registration is provision, those who conceal their gift—*

Xanthe tuned it out. She had twenty years of practice tuning out crowns.

"Come back tomorrow," she said to Wren. "Early, before market. I'll have questions, and you'll have answers, and maybe—" she allowed herself a smile, small and real, the first in days, "—maybe I'll teach you a knot or two. A holding-knot. For keeping precious things safe."

The girl's face lit like a lamp. "Truly?"

"Truly. Now go home before the watch starts asking strays for names."

Wren tucked the charm back against her chest—that same fierce protective clutch—and bolted for the door, bell jangling, kelp-colored hair flying. At the threshold she paused, glanced back, and said, "Thank you, mistress. Gran says you're the only honest charm-wife on the Row."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading down Tidewalk Row into the gathering dark.

Xanthe stood alone in her half-packed shop with the lamp burning low and the storm rising in her chest like a tide that had finally found its moon.

*It knows the way home.*

She looked down at her hands—her mother's hands, her sister's hands, hands that had tied ten thousand harmless knots to bury one terrible gift—and slowly, deliberately, she began to untie the oilcloth bundles. Not to pack them for flight.

To unpack them. To choose what a fugitive carries.

South was safety. South had always been the plan: run, hide, grow old, die small. But Isolde had spent her whole life standing between Xanthe and the world's cruelty, and if her sister's soul—her sister's *self*—was tangled in whatever bargain the crown had struck beneath the mountains, then no distance in the world was far enough, and no hiding place deep enough, and twenty years of careful smallness had been nothing but a long delay.

The proclamation wanted the gifted registered by the turn of the moon.

The thirteenth moon.

Xanthe lifted the grey holding-knot charm from where Wren had left it on the counter and hung it on the hook by her door, where the lamplight caught it, where she would see it first thing in the morning and last thing at night.

"Wait for me, Sol," she said quietly, to a woman twenty years gone, in a town a hundred leagues away. "I'm coming."

Outside, the criers' drums faded toward the harbor, and the thirteenth moon rose thin and sharp over Greyharbor's rooftops—a sliver of bone-white light, beginning its final count.
