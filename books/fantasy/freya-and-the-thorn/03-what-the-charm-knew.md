## 3. What the Charm Knew

The girl came back at dusk, when the market stalls were folding themselves up like tired birds and the lamplighters were making their rounds with long poles and shorter tempers.

Xanthe was counting her day's earnings into a tin box—eleven coppers, two silvers, and a fishhook someone had paid her with in lieu of coin, which she supposed was its own kind of compliment—when a shadow fell across her table. Small. Thin at the edges. The kind of shadow that belonged to someone who had learned to make herself take up less room than she actually did.

"You're still here," the girl said.

Xanthe looked up. It was the same child from that morning—the one who had stood at the edge of her stall while the hunters' drums rolled through the square, clutching a charm against her chest like it was the last warm thing in the world. She couldn't have been more than twelve, maybe thirteen, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with hair the color of wet sand and eyes too old for the face they'd been set into. Greyharbor did that to children. The sea took the parents and the city took the rest.

"I'm always here until the lamps are lit," Xanthe said, keeping her voice easy, merchant-smooth. "Luck doesn't keep market hours."

The girl didn't smile. She set something on the table instead, carefully, the way one sets down an egg that might already be cracked.

It was a charm. A knot-charm, braided cord and river-stone, the kind sold by the hundred in every market from here to the capital—and yet not like any of them. Xanthe's hands went still over her tin box before she could tell them to.

* * *

She had seen ten thousand knots in her life. Her mother had taught her the first ones on a kitchen table in a house that smelled of bread and rain, her mother's fingers moving through the cords with the patience of someone who believed that a knot tied well was a small prayer answered. Every charm-seller had a hand. Every hand left fingerprints in the work—the tension of the braid, the way the ends were tucked or burned, the little signatures of habit that no one noticed unless they knew what to look for.

Xanthe knew what to look for. She had spent twenty years looking away from it.

This charm was braided in the old Fairweather pattern: three strands crossed twice before the first binding, then a hitch that most weavers called wasteful because it used extra cord and added nothing visible. Useless, unless you knew that the hitch was where you hid the working. Unless you knew that the woman who invented it had been left-handed and impatient and had never once in her life done anything the simple way when a complicated way would do.

Isolde's way.

"Where," Xanthe said, and her voice came out wrong, scraped thin, "did you get this?"

The girl's chin lifted a fraction. Wary. She'd been warned about strangers asking questions, clearly. "Bought it fair."

"From whom?"

"A lady." The girl's eyes flicked toward the darkening street, toward the corner where the proclamation boards had gone up that morning, white paper and black wax. "A dying lady. In Hollowmere."

Hollowmere. The name landed somewhere under Xanthe's ribs and sat there, heavy as a swallowed stone. A mountain town, three weeks inland if the roads were kind, and the roads past the border villages hadn't been kind in years. A town she had thought of exactly never, on purpose, the way you don't press a bruise.

"Hollowmere's a long way from Greyharbor," Xanthe said. "How does a girl your age come to be carrying a dead woman's charm across half a country?"

"She's not dead." The girl said it quickly, fiercely, as if correcting the record mattered more than anything else. Then her shoulders dropped. "Or she wasn't. When I left. My gran sent me to her, see? Gran said there was a woman in Hollowmere who could tie a charm that would keep me safe on the road, real safe, not market-safe. Said she'd known her when they were girls." The girl's throat worked. "When I got there she was already sick. Dying, I think, though she wouldn't say so. But she sat up and she made this anyway. Took her near an hour. Kept stopping to breathe."

Xanthe turned the charm over in her palm. The stone at its heart was river-grey, worn smooth, and threaded through the braid beside it was a single strand of red cord—a thread of warning, of watching, woven so fine you'd miss it unless your fingers knew to search for it. Isolde had always added the red thread. *So it can see trouble coming,* she used to say, *even when you won't.*

"What was her name?" Xanthe asked. She wasn't sure she wanted the answer. She wasn't sure she'd survive not having it.

The girl hesitated. Outside, a lamplighter's pole tapped against glass, once, twice, and the street beyond the stall went gold at the edges.

"She didn't give one," the girl said at last. "But—" and now she leaned in, dropping her voice, and there was something in her face that made Xanthe's skin prickle, some inheritance of fear passed hand to hand down a long chain of frightened people—"she talked. While she worked. She talked the whole time, like she was afraid of running out of words before she ran out of breath. And some of what she said was nonsense. But some of it wasn't."

"Tell me the parts that weren't."

The girl glanced over her shoulder again. The market was nearly empty now; a fishwife was scraping down her boards two stalls down, and a dog was worrying at something in the gutter, but no one close enough to hear. Still, the girl lowered her voice until Xanthe had to lean across the table to catch it.

"She said the court beneath the mountain buys souls." The words came out flat and quick, like stones skipped fast across water. "That's what she said. *Buys.* Not takes. Buys. People go to them willing, or their families do, and they sign papers, and the court gives gold or grain or a pardon or whatever's needed, and the person just—" the girl made a gesture, vague and terrible, opening her empty hand— "*stops being there.* Their body keeps walking around but they're not in it anymore. She said she'd seen them. In Hollowmere. Husbands who didn't know their wives' names. Mothers who looked at their children like strangers at a market stall."

The tin box was cold under Xanthe's fingers. She realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"And she said—" the girl swallowed— "she said the souls don't get destroyed. That's the worst part, she said. They get *kept*. Fed to something. She called it the Thorn. She said it sleeps under the mountain, under the court, and it eats what the court feeds it, and what it eats makes the throne strong. Weather. Harvests. Memory. She said the crown's new magic hunt isn't a hunt at all—it's a harvest. They're not seizing the gifted to punish them. They're seizing them to *sell*."

Somewhere behind Xanthe's sternum, the storm stirred. Twenty years of practice clamped down on it—she pictured the iron door in her mind, the one she had built bar by bar since she was nine years old, and she threw the bolt the way she had thrown it ten thousand times before. But her hands were shaking, and the lamp above her stall flickered though there was no wind.

*Isolde.* The name rose in her like floodwater. *Isolde, who stayed behind. Isolde, who wrote one letter in twenty years, unsigned, untraceable, four lines long: I am well. Do not come back. Burn this. Isolde, whose knot-work is sitting in my hand right now, tied by a dying woman in a town I swore never to think about.*

Because there was only one Fairweather who tied knots like that. Only one left. Xanthe had left the craft behind along with everything else—had rebuilt her hands around merchant knots, luck-knots, pretty useless things—and even if she hadn't, even if some part of her had kept the old patterns alive in secret, there was no one else. There had never been anyone else. Two sisters, one craft, and one of them exiled before her seventeenth birthday.

Which meant the dying woman in Hollowmere was—

"That's mad," Xanthe heard herself say. "The court beneath the mountain. Souls as debts. It's a story grandmothers tell."

"My gran believed it." The girl's jaw set. "And the lady believed it. She made me memorize what she said. Made me say it back three times before she'd finish the charm. She said—" the girl closed her eyes, reciting, and her voice took on a strange cadence, the cadence of borrowed words carried a long way and repeated often enough to wear grooves— "she said: *Tell the charm-sellers. Tell every charm-seller on the coast. When the thirteenth moon comes full, the Thorn wakes, and what it has eaten, it keeps. Tell them to count the moons. Thirteen turns, and the last one is coming.*"

The lamp flickered again. This time the fishwife looked up too, frowning at the flame, and Xanthe forced her heartbeat down, down, down into the deep water where she kept everything that might spark.

"The thirteenth moon," Xanthe said carefully. "What happens at the thirteenth moon?"

"I don't know. She died before she'd say. Or stopped talking, anyway." The girl opened her eyes, and they were wet and furious and very young. "I stayed three days. On the fourth day a neighbor came and said the lady's house was empty and there were men asking after visitors, men with pale eyes, and Gran's letter said if that ever happened I should run. So I ran. I've been running for two months. And everywhere I stop, there's more white paper on the walls, and more people gone quiet, and I thought—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, Xanthe could see her hating it— "I thought maybe a charm-seller would know what to do. Gran said charm-sellers know things. Gran said—"

"Gran says a lot," Xanthe said gently.

The girl wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, furious at her own young face for betraying her. "Are you going to buy it off me? Some of the sellers offered. For the charm. They said it was fine work."

Something twisted in Xanthe's chest, sharp and cold. Other charm-sellers had seen this. Had held Isolde's work in their hands and offered coppers for it, the way you'd offer coppers for any pretty trinket, and none of them had known, none of them could have known, and still it felt like desecration.

"No," Xanthe said. "I'm not going to buy it."

The girl's face fell.

"I'm going to give you a better one," Xanthe said, "and you're going to keep this one hidden, and you're going to tell me everything again, slowly, from the beginning. What the lady looked like. What her house was like. What she said, word for word, even the nonsense. Especially the nonsense." She was already reaching under her table for her travel satchel, already pulling out cord, her fingers remembering movements they hadn't made in twenty years. "Can you do that?"

The girl stared at her. "Why?"

Because the knot-work is my sister's. Because my sister is dying in a mountain town full of pale-eyed men. Because my sister sold something once—I've always known she sold something, I've felt the shape of that absence for twenty years without letting myself look at it directly—and now I know what the court buys, and I know what feeds the throne, and I know that my sister's four-line letter said *do not come back* in a hand that shook.

"Because," Xanthe said, "I knew her. The lady in Hollowmere. A long time ago."

The girl went very still. "You did?"

"We grew up together." Xanthe's voice held steady, mostly. Under the table, out of sight, her hands had begun to braid—three strands crossed twice, the old pattern, the hitch that wasted cord and hid the working. Her fingers remembered. Of course they remembered. You could exile a woman from her country, her name, her family, her own magic, but the hands kept their own counsel. The hands had never agreed to forget. "And I think she may have been trying to send me a message. Through you. Through this."

She held up the charm between them, and in the lamplight the red thread caught the flame and held it, a single bright line through the grey, like a vein, like a lifeline, like a word written in a language only two people in the world had ever spoken—one of them dying in a house in Hollowmere, and one of them sitting in a shuttered market stall in Greyharbor with a storm locked behind her teeth and a decision forming in her chest like weather.

The girl looked at the charm, and then at Xanthe, and something shifted in her young face—the wariness giving way, not to trust, not yet, but to the particular hunger of someone who has been alone a long time and has just heard the first syllable of a reason.

"What was her name?" the girl asked softly. "The lady. Your friend."

Xanthe closed her hand around the charm. The river-stone pressed into her palm, cool and smooth, and beneath it she could almost feel another hand—smaller than hers, quicker, left-handed and impatient—guiding the cord the way it had guided it a thousand mornings at a kitchen table that no longer existed.

"Isolde," she said. "Her name was Isolde."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the first stars were coming out over Greyharbor, and above them, low and thin on the horizon, the moon was rising—waxing, climbing, one turn closer to its thirteenth fullness. Xanthe looked at it for a long moment.

Then she reached for her ledger, tore out a blank page, and said, "Start at the beginning. Everything she said. And then you're going to help me find a smuggler, because I need to get to Hollowmere before the moon does."
