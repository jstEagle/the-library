## 4. The Saltwife's Offer

The tide was going out when Xanthe went looking for the Saltwife, and it took the harbor's smell with it—leaving behind the honest stink of mud and rot and fish heads, the smell of a city with its skirts lifted. She walked down through the Warrens with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, past shuttered charm-stalls and a crier's abandoned stand, its bell still hanging crooked where someone had knocked it in their hurry to be elsewhere.

Three days since the proclamation. Three days of watching neighbors look at one another sideways, weighing what a name might fetch from the crown's new registry. Old Tam the netmender had already gone—taken in the night, or fled before dawn; his door wore the same painted thorn-sigil she'd seen on Wren's charm-box, white as bone against the black wood. *Registered.* Or something worse.

She found the Saltwife's door where the map in her memory said it would be: at the end of Saltpan Row, where the houses leaned together like conspirators, a low stone building with no sign above it except a dried starfish nailed to the lintel. The door was salt-bleached oak, iron-banded, and utterly unremarkable, which was itself remarkable. Everything about it said *nothing to see here* so loudly that only someone looking for nothing would ever find it.

Xanthe knocked the way you knock on a door that might not want to open: twice, softly, then once more after a count of ten.

The slot slid back. One eye, brown and rheumy and sharp as a gutting knife, regarded her.

"Buying or selling?"

"Neither," Xanthe said. "I'm looking for passage."

"Passage's for ships."

"To Hollowmere."

The eye did not blink. The slot did not close. Somewhere inside, a kettle whistled and was silenced by an impatient hand.

"Hollowmere's inland," the voice said. "No ships go there."

"I'm told there are roads under the mountain," Xanthe said, "and people who know them. I'm told the woman who knows them best used to move the gifted across the border for the price of a good story and half her fee up front. I'm told they call her the Saltwife because she preserved everything she touched." She paused. "Including secrets."

A long silence. Then, from behind the door, a dry laugh like paper tearing.

"Who told you all that?"

"A dead woman's knot-work," Xanthe said. "And a girl named Wren."

The slot snapped shut. Bolts turned—one, two, three—and the door swung inward on a dim hallway smelling of tallow smoke, dried kelp, and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a deep cave.

"Well," said Maren the Saltwife, when Xanthe stepped into the light of her kitchen. "You'd better sit down, storm-child. This is going to take longer than the tide."

* * *

Maren was smaller than her reputation. That was the first thing. Xanthe had imagined a woman built like a breakwater, all shoulders and scarred knuckles, but the person pouring tea at the chipped hearth was a sparrow of a creature, sixty if she was a day, with white hair cropped short as a sailor's and hands so knotted with age and work that they looked like driftwood roots. Her eyes were the only young thing about her—brown, quick, and lit from within by a kind of patient amusement, the way a lamp is lit inside a lighthouse: not for warmth, but to show you exactly where the rocks were.

The kitchen was a museum of a life lived sideways. Dried herbs hung from beams in braided bundles. Charts covered one wall, some of them sea charts, some of them—Xanthe noted with a small cold thrill—mountain charts, inked in a careful clerk's hand, with tunnels marked in red. A shelf above the fire held jars of salt, each labeled in faded script. Xanthe caught one label as she sat: *Tears, widow of Brancaster, 3 drams.*

"You keep them," Xanthe said quietly. "Even now."

"I keep everything," Maren said. "That's the whole trick of me." She set down two cups of tea, dark and bitter-smelling, and lowered herself into the chair opposite with the care of someone whose joints had opinions. "Now. You said a girl named Wren brought you my name. And before that?"

Before that, Xanthe told her. Not all of it—she kept the knot-work folded in her pocket, kept Isolde's name behind her teeth—but most of it. The charm stall. The raid. The proclamation. Wren's charm, bought from a dying woman in Hollowmere, tied in a pattern Xanthe had known since childhood: the weather-knot, the Fairweather knot, the one her sister's fingers could tie blind and drunk and grieving, the one no other hand in the world made quite the same way.

Maren listened without interrupting, which Xanthe came to understand was her principal art. When Xanthe finished, the old woman drank her tea, looked into the cup as though reading leaves, and said:

"You're the sister."

It was not a question. Xanthe's spine went cold. "How—"

"The knot told you who tied it. Who else would come asking after Hollowmere three days after the proclamation, with that look on your face?" Maren set the cup down. "You've got the face of a woman carrying a debt twenty years old. I've seen that face before. Usually in mirrors."

"My sister's name," Xanthe said carefully, "was Isolde."

"*Was*," Maren repeated. "Careful with your tenses, girl. They'll betray you faster than any hunter." She leaned forward. "Tell me what you think happened to her."

"I don't know what happened to her. She left when I was fourteen. There was a fire. After the fire, she was gone, and I was—" Xanthe stopped. The sentence had a shape she had never been able to finish. *And I was alone, and I didn't remember why, and every time I tried to remember why, my mind slid off it like rain off oiled canvas.* "After the fire, she was gone."

"And the court beneath the mountain buys souls," Maren said. "Willingly sold. That's what the dying woman told your little Wren, yes?" She nodded at Xanthe's expression. "It's true. I moved cargo for that court for six years, before I understood what the cargo was. Souls in sealed jars, mostly. Sometimes in people—poor devils walking north with someone else's eyes. The court pays well and asks no questions about where things come from, which is how the worst markets always work." She tapped the table with one root-knuckled finger. "But here's the part your dying woman likely didn't know, because nobody outside the mountain knows it. The court doesn't buy souls to *keep* them. It buys them to *feed* them. To something old under the throne. Something the mountain grew around like a pearl around grit."

"The Thorn," Xanthe said.

Something flickered across Maren's face—the first genuine surprise Xanthe had seen there, quickly mastered. "Where did you hear that word?"

"In a dream," Xanthe said, which was almost true. She had heard it in the space between sleeping and waking, in the three nights since Wren left her stall, a whisper like wind through a keyhole: *thirteenth turn, thirteenth turn.* "Does it matter?"

"It matters because words have weight, and that word has been buried a long time." Maren studied her for a long moment, and Xanthe felt herself being weighed in turn—not her story, but something else. Her steadiness. Her seams. Whether she would tear.

Then the old woman laughed again, short and dry, and seemed to arrive at a decision.

"All right. Here's what I can offer, and here's what it costs, and you'll listen to both before you say yes, because I've buried enough fools to fill a churchyard."

* * *

The offer was this: Maren still ran a crew, smaller now, quieter—a barque called the *Drowned Lantern*, seven souls aboard, working the grey trade along the coast. In nine days she sailed north to Sennet Fjord, where an old miners' road went up into the mountains toward Hollowmere and, beyond it, the deep ways under the mountain itself. She could put Xanthe ashore with a guide. She could even, if Xanthe's business proved what Maren suspected it might prove, take her crew all the way in.

"And the cost?" Xanthe asked.

"Half up front, as the story goes. But I don't deal in coin for this kind of passage. Coin's traceable." Maren's eyes glinted. "I deal in truth. You'll tell me your whole name, your whole business, and your whole gift—and you'll swear on salt that none of it is a lie. My crew will do the same. We'll know each other completely, or not at all. That's the price of trust, storm-child. It's paid in exposure."

Xanthe looked at her tea. The truth was a locked room she had kept locked so long she sometimes forgot the door existed. Her name—her real name, not the softened thing she'd worn for fifteen years in Greyharbor. Her gift, which she had smothered in public for so long that using it felt like undressing in a market square. And her business, which was a sister, a fire, a hole in her memory shaped exactly like a night she could not recall.

"Why?" she asked. "Why help me? Why risk your crew on a dead woman's knot-work and a stranger's grief?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. She rose, joints cracking like rigging in a blow, and took down one of the salt jars from the shelf. She held it to the firelight. Inside, the salt was ordinary salt—but around it, faint as breath on glass, something shimmered. A residue. An echo.

"Because thirty years ago, a woman came to this door with her son," Maren said. "He was gifted—weather, like you, if the stories are right. The hunters were coming. I moved him over the border, and I charged her everything she had, and I told myself that was just the trade." She set the jar down. "Two years later the court's men came anyway. Different border. Different smuggler. Cheaper loyalty." Her voice stayed level, but her hand, Xanthe noticed, did not. "I've spent thirty years learning exactly how much the court pays for a name, and it is always less than you'd hope and more than most folk can refuse. So now I test everyone who walks through that door—including myself. And when someone comes asking after the court's deepest cupboard, carrying grief in one pocket and a storm in the other, I pay attention. Because either you're the bait they've sent to hook me at last—" her eyes glittered, entirely without fear— "or you're the storm they should have hooked twenty years ago. Either way, I want to know which. Before the moon turns."

"The moon," Xanthe said slowly. "What about the moon?"

Maren looked at her for a long time.

"Thirteen turns," she said finally. "There's a clock in that mountain, girl, and it runs on moonlight, and the thirteenth turn of this season's moon falls four weeks from tomorrow. I don't know what happens when it completes. But I know that the court has been buying souls at a pace I haven't seen in thirty years. I know the roads inland are watched by pale-eyed men who don't eat, don't sleep, and don't blink. And I know that whatever the Thorn is waking up for—it intends to wake *full*." She sat back down. "So. Your name, your gift, your business. On salt. Now, while you're still deciding whether to trust me—because that's the only moment trust is ever truly tested."

* * *

Xanthe stood at the kitchen window while she decided, looking out at the grey water of the harbor, where the fishing boats were coming home ahead of a sky that promised weather. Her chest ached with the old familiar pressure—the storm that lived under her sternum like a second heart, the one she had spent fifteen years teaching to sleep. *Smother it. Smile. Sell luck to people whose luck you could actually make, if you dared.*

She thought of Wren's face in the market, holding out a charm tied in Isolde's knots. She thought of the empty doorway with the white sigil. She thought of a burning room she could not remember, and a signature on paper she could not see, and a hole in her life shaped like a sister.

Trust, Maren had said, is paid in exposure.

"Xanthe Fairweather," she said, turning from the window. "Storm-caller. Born of the high moors, third daughter of Aldous and Merrin Fairweather, both dead twenty years in a fire I cannot remember. My gift is weather—all of it, wind and rain and lightning, called or calmed. And my business is this: I believe my sister Isolde sold her soul to the court beneath the mountain, and I believe the price of that sale was my life, and I am going to Hollowmere to find out if it's true, and if it is, I am going under the mountain to take her back."

The words hung in the tallow-light. Saying them aloud changed them somehow—they became heavier, realer, a cargo loaded and manifest-signed. Xanthe felt lighter and more frightened simultaneously, the way she always felt after telling the truth.

Maren took a jar of coarse grey salt from the table, poured a small circle on the boards between them, and nodded for Xanthe to speak the last of it.

"On salt," the old woman said, "that all you've said is true."

"On salt," Xanthe said, "it's true."

Maren swept the circle up with the side of her hand, and just like that, it was done. A bargain. Xanthe had sold charms long enough to know the feeling of a knot pulled tight.

"Nine days," Maren said briskly, all business now, the confessional closed. "Pier eleven, the night tide, ask for the *Drowned Lantern*. Bring nothing you can't run with and no one you can't account for." She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and her voice dropped into something gentler and graver. "One more warning, free of charge, because you paid me honestly. The mountain road is watched, and the court buys loyalty cheap—cheaper than you believe, cheaper than *anyone* believes until the night they sell. I've lost crew to gold, to fear, and once, worst of all, to love. Whatever happens up there, remember: the person who betrays you will not look like a villain. They'll look like someone doing the arithmetic of their own desperation. Watch for the ones with something the court can hold over them. Those are the ones it holds."

She opened the door. Cold harbour air came in, smelling of mud and ebb-tide.

"And Maren," Xanthe said, at the threshold. "The crew. Will they swear too? On salt?"

"They already have," Maren said. "Every soul aboard the *Drowned Lantern* has something the court wants. That's why they're aboard." Her eyes crinkled, not quite a smile. "It makes for loyal sailing, mostly. Mostly."

* * *

Xanthe walked back through the Warrens as the lamps were being lit, and the city felt different around her—lighter, as if she had set down a bag she'd forgotten she carried, and heavier, as if she'd picked up a new one. Nine days. Nine days to close her stall, settle her accounts, say the careful goodbyes that wouldn't sound like goodbyes. Nine days to decide what to bring of her old life and what to burn.

She was halfway up Tanner's Stair when she saw the notice, fresh-pasted on the customs house wall, the paste still wet and gleaming in the lamplight. A proclamation, printed in the crown's new crisp type, beside the older one about registration. This one was shorter. It read:

*By order of the Court: a reward of five hundred crowns for information leading to the seizure of any unregistered gifted person. Five hundred crowns. No questions asked.*

Five hundred crowns. Enough to live on for years. Enough to buy a fisherman's freedom from debt, a widow's winter, a drunkard's forgiveness. The court's prices, written plainly on a wall for anyone desperate enough to read them.

Xanthe stood in the lamplight reading it, and around her the evening crowd flowed past—netmenders and fishwives and dockhands, ordinary tired people, and she caught herself doing it, catching herself doing it: studying their faces, one by one, wondering *which of you is counting five hundred crowns against my name.*

That was the price of trust, she thought. Not paid once, at a saltwife's table. Paid daily, in glances, in doors left unlocked or barred, in the arithmetic of everyone you passed.

She pulled her hood lower and climbed the stair toward her rooms above the chandler's, and behind her, at the corner of Tanner's Stair, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the customs house and followed—soft-footed, unhurried, keeping just far enough back to be deniable, just close enough to be certain.

Xanthe did not see it. But the storm under her sternum stirred in its sleep, the way animals stir before earthquakes, and she paused on the landing with her hand pressed flat against her breastbone, listening to a warning she couldn't yet read.

Nine days, she thought. She only hoped the road to the pier would let her keep them.
