## 5. Crew of the Drowned Lantern

The barque was called the *Drowned Lantern*, and she looked it—salt-scoured hull, patched sails dyed the grey of old storm-clouds, a figurehead of a woman holding a lantern that had long ago lost its light to the sea. She sat low in Greyharbor's outer anchorage like something that had learned to be unremarkable, and Xanthe understood the strategy at once. It was her own.

Maren met her at the water-stairs before dawn, a lantern of her own swinging from one knuckled fist, its flame burning a strange, steady green.

"Salt-fire," the old smuggler said, catching Xanthe's glance. "Burns underwater, burns in rain, burns when a hunter's charm tries to smother it. Only thing on this boat I trust completely." Her eyes, pale as fish-belly, traveled over Xanthe's bundle, her plain merchant's coat, the luck-knots braided into her sleeve where any charm-seller might wear them. "You'll want to remember three names. Corvin has the wheel and no conversation. Pell has the charts and too much of everything else. And Ash—" Maren paused, and the green light made her face look carved from driftwood. "Ash is my first mate, and he smiles too easily for a man who works this coast. Mind him."

"Should I mind him kindly or carefully?"

"Yes," said Maren, and led her up the boarding-ladder.

* * *

The deck smelled of tar and herring brine and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a cave mouth. Xanthe felt it against her skin before she could name it—the faint static pressure of other people's gifts, banked and hidden the way coals are banked under ash. So Maren hadn't lied about that part either. There were gifted aboard.

Corvin stood at the helm even in harbor, hands resting on the spokes as though the ship might bolt without him. He was a broad, weathered man gone grey at the temples, with a beard cropped close and eyes the color of deep water under cloud. When Maren introduced them he gave Xanthe a single nod, the kind of nod given to cargo, and turned back to the horizon.

"He doesn't talk," Maren said.

"I noticed."

"He talked once. Don't ask him about it." She moved off toward the sterncastle, and Xanthe was left standing on the deck with her bundle and the strange banked-fire feeling pressing gently against her gift like a hand laid flat over a struck bell.

Pell found her within the hour, because Pell seemed to find everything within the hour. She was a small, quick woman with ink-stained fingers and hair bound back so tightly it pulled her eyebrows into an expression of permanent skepticism, and she circled Xanthe twice before speaking, like a gull deciding whether a dropped thing was food or threat.

"You're the passenger. The one paying in charm-work instead of coin." She held out her hand, palm up, imperious. "Give us your knot, then. The one you wear."

Xanthe hesitated only a moment before untying the small braided loop from her sleeve and setting it in Pell's palm. She watched the navigator turn it over, thumbs reading the pattern the way a blind woman reads a page.

"Hm. Double-wound at the third crossing. That's not market-work." Pell's eyes came up, sharp as a gutting knife. "That's storm-knotting. That's the kind of braid you learn when you're holding down weather inside your own ribs." She handed it back slowly. "I was a clerk, before. Court registry, customs, tax rolls. I know what registration papers look like, and I know what a person looks like when they're deciding how much of themselves to admit to a stranger." A thin smile. "You're deciding right now. Take your time. We've got six days to Hollowmere if the wind behaves, and the wind never behaves."

She spun away toward the bow, calling something to Corvin that was half navigation and half insult, and Xanthe stood holding her own knot and feeling, uncomfortably, *read*.

And then there was Ash.

He appeared at her elbow as the anchor came up—as though he'd been waiting for exactly the moment she had nowhere to retreat to—and he was, she saw immediately, precisely the sort of trouble Maren had warned her about. Younger than Corvin by fifteen years, maybe thirty all told, with dark hair going loose from its tie and a face built for smiling, dimples already loaded. He wore his coat open despite the cold and moved across the pitching deck like the ship had been designed around him.

"Xanthe Fairweather," he said. Not a question. "Charm-seller. Luck-knots, weather-wards, fair-wind tokens. Best prices on the Salt Row, if you believe the fishermen, and fishermen believe everything."

"You've been asking about me."

"I ask about everyone." His smile widened, easy and warm, and did not reach his eyes, which were busy—cataloguing her hands, her sleeves, the way she kept her weight centered as the deck rolled. "It's a habit of the curious. And the careful. They look the same from outside." He offered his hand. "Ash. First mate, second opinion, and the only one aboard who'll tell you the truth about dinner."

She took his hand. His grip was warm and calloused, and beneath the salt-and-tar smell of him she caught again that faint mineral coldness, stronger here than anywhere else on the ship, strong enough that her gift stirred against it like a cat recognizing another cat through a wall.

"What are you?" she asked, before she could decide to be more careful.

Something flickered behind the smile—a door closing, quickly, politely. "Crew," Ash said. "Same as everyone. Welcome aboard the *Drowned Lantern*, Xanthe Fairweather. Try not to drown; we've got enough lanterns."

* * *

Three days out from Greyharbor the wind died entirely, and the sea went flat and oiled-looking, and every soul aboard grew quiet in a way that told Xanthe they knew what stillness meant on this coast.

"It's a sucking calm," Pell explained, spread-eagled over her charts by lamplight in the narrow cabin. "Pressure falling somewhere west of us, fast. Something big is building out past the Verren Shoals and it's going to come looking for us." She tapped the chart without looking up. "We can't outrun it. We can't go around it. Corvin says we ride it, which means Corvin wants to die in an interesting way."

"Can we beat it to the shoals and shelter?" Xanthe asked.

"With this wind? We'd arrive just in time to be scenery." Pell finally looked up, and there was something under her sharpness that Xanthe recognized: fear, worn smooth by handling. "No. We ride it. We shorten sail, we lash everything, and we pray to whatever gods don't answer sailors."

Xanthe went up on deck. The sky to the west had begun to bruise, a green-black wall building along the horizon with a patience that felt personal. She had spent twenty years reading weather the way other people read letters from home—with hunger, and dread, and the ache of things unsaid. This storm was enormous. This storm was beautiful. And it was coming straight down the throat of the shipping lane with the *Drowned Lantern* in its teeth.

Her chest tightened. Under her breastbone, the storm she kept there—the one she had been smothering since Greyharbor, since Hollowmere, since always—turned over in its sleep and answered the distant thunder like a child answering its mother's call.

*Not now,* she told it. *Not here.*

But storms do not listen, and neither, it turned out, did this one.

* * *

It hit them two hours after midnight.

Xanthe woke to the world tilting and the sound of the sea changing its mind. She came up the companionway into chaos—rain moving sideways, the green salt-lanterns guttering but alive, men hauling canvas while the wind tore at it like a living thing, and above it all Corvin at the helm, both hands white on the spokes, wrestling the ship through waves tall as chapels.

"We can't hold her!" someone screamed—it might have been Pell, it might have been anyone; the wind ate voices whole. "She's broaching—we have to run before it—"

"If we run before it we run onto the shoals!" Ash's voice, raw, from the mast base. "Hold! HOLD!"

A wave took the deck amidships, green water waist-deep, and swept a sailor off his feet and toward the rail, and Xanthe lunged and caught his wrist and held him against the pull of the sea with strength she didn't know she had until the moment she needed it. The man's eyes were white-rimmed with terror. Beyond him the lightning opened the sky like a wound, and in that white instant she saw the whole storm at once—its shape, its spin, its center churning ten miles off the beam and dragging them around like a toy boat circling a drain.

And her gift rose.

Twenty years of practice said: *smother it, swallow it, smile.* Twenty years of practice said: *you are a merchant, you sell charms, your hands are empty.*

But the ship was dying, and the sea was eating it, and the storm in her chest pressed up against the backs of her teeth like words she had refused to say for two decades.

*Xanthe,* said the storm, in a voice like her mother's, like her sister's, like her own at nine years old standing on a jetty with rain in her hair. *Let me.*

She let go.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing, how quiet it was. She simply stopped holding, the way you stop holding a breath, and the storm came up through her—out through her palms, her shoulders, the soles of her feet against the pitching deck—and into the air around the ship.

She didn't stop the gale. You cannot stop a gale; that's like arguing with a mountain. But she *spoke* to it, in the old grammar, the grammar of pressure and charge and the space between raindrops, and she bent it. Just slightly. Just enough. She took the spinning drag of the storm's heart and eased it a degree off the beam. She flattened the wave-train astern of them into something a helmsman could fight rather than something that would kill him. She walked the lightning away from the mast, drew each strike off to port, into the empty sea, a patient shepherd moving wolves aside from a flock one animal at a time.

To the crew it must have looked like fortune. The ship steadied. The waves, while still monstrous, began arriving in a rhythm instead of a riot. Corvin's hands eased a fraction on the wheel, and the *Drowned Lantern* rode up one black hill of water after another, climbing toward dawn.

Xanthe stood amidships with her arms out and the rain running off her chin, and for the first time in twenty years she did not feel like a woman hiding. She felt like a lighthouse. She felt like a door finally opened in a house that had been shut for winter.

It lasted until the sky went grey, and the storm's eye passed, and she felt the vast pressure begin to tire—and she closed herself again, gently, gathering her gift back in like a net full of fish, and the exhaustion hit her so hard she went to one knee on the flooded deck.

When she looked up, the crew was staring at her.

Not all of them. Some were still fighting canvas, still bailing, still doing the work of staying alive. But Corvin was looking. Pell was looking, from the shelter of the companionway, her expression stripped of all its sharpness and left with only wonder. And Ash—

Ash was looking at her the way a man looks at a lock he has spent years trying to pick, hearing the tumblers fall.

* * *

They put into a cove south of the Verren Shoals to repair what the storm had torn, and that evening the crew gathered in the sterncastle around the green salt-lantern, and Maren sat down across from Xanthe with the finality of a judge taking a bench.

"You bent it," Corvin said.

Everyone turned. It was the first time Xanthe had heard his voice at more than a word's length, and it was rough, disused, like a hinge in a forgotten door. He wasn't accusing. He was stating, the way he might state the wind's direction.

"A degree," Xanthe said. "Maybe two. Nothing more."

"A degree off the beam was the difference between my ship and the bottom of the sea." He looked at her for a long moment, deep-water eyes unreadable. Then he nodded—that same cargo-nod, but different now, weighted—and left the sterncastle without another word.

"Well," said Pell, into the silence. "That was practically a sonnet. For Corvin." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Storm-caller. Full gift, not hedge-craft. I clerked the registries for eleven years, Fairweather, and I know what the crown pays for information about your kind now. Enough to buy this ship twice over. Enough to buy a life free and clear." She let that sit. "So the question everyone's thinking and nobody's saying is: what's to stop one of us getting rich?"

"The same thing that stops me," Xanthe said quietly. "I'm going to Hollowmere to steal my sister's soul back from the court beneath the mountain. Anyone who sells me will have to explain to the Inquisitors why they were sailing there at all." She looked around the lamplit circle, at the faces gone still. "I'm not asking you to hide me. I'm telling you what I am, so you can choose with your eyes open. That's more than the court would give you."

Silence. The salt-flame burned its steady green.

Then Ash laughed—not his easy deck-laugh, but something lower, realer, startled out of him—and stood, and pulled his shirt off one shoulder.

On his collarbone, old scar tissue spelled a crude brand: a circle with a line struck through it. The mark they burned into the registered gifted, twenty years ago, before the hunts went quiet and polite.

"Registry, 990," he said. "My father sold me out for a pardon that lasted four months." He let the shirt fall and sat back down, and the charm of him was gone, and what was underneath was younger and wearier and far more dangerous to her peace. "Half this crew is marked or hiding, Fairweather. Maren doesn't carry passengers. She carries us. So no—" he met her eyes, and his voice gentled into something that frightened her more than Serel's hunters ever could, "—nobody's selling you. But you should know that you weren't the only one choosing with your eyes open tonight. We chose you the minute you stepped aboard. The storm just made it official."

Maren snorted. "Sentimental fool," she said, and poured spirits all around, and the circle broke into the rough warmth of survivors.

But later, when the others had gone and Xanthe stood alone at the stern rail watching the moon rise—fat, and bright, and counting down its turns toward the thirteenth—she heard footsteps behind her and knew without turning who they belonged to.

"You watched me the whole voyage," she said. "Before the storm. Why?"

Ash came to stand beside her, forearms on the rail, and for once he didn't smile.

"Because three weeks ago," he said, "I saw a court ledger. And there was a name in it I've been trying to find for six years." He turned to look at her, and the moonlight made his face very grave. "Your sister's name was next to mine, Xanthe. Isolde Fairweather and my daughter, entered the same night, bought by the same buyer." His voice didn't shake, but it cost him visibly. "I didn't take this job to ferry you to Hollowmere. I took it because I'd follow that ledger anywhere it leads. Even here."

Far out over the dark water, the thirteenth moon climbed a finger's width higher, and Xanthe felt the whole voyage tilt beneath her, like a deck in a rising sea.
