## 6. Storm Oath

Ash had watched a great many people lie in his life, and he liked to think he'd gotten good at telling the difference between the ones who lied for profit and the ones who lied to survive. The storm-caller in the hold of the *Drowned Lantern* was the second kind. She lied the way drowning men breathed—instinctively, desperately, and without any real hope of fooling anyone.

She was lying right now, in fact, sitting on an overturned crate with her hands folded in her lap like a merchant's widow at a funeral, saying, "I don't know what you think you saw," while her hair still crackled faintly with static and the smell of ozone hung about her like a second cloak.

"You stopped a squall," Ash said. "A black-line squall, out of the northeast, the kind that takes ships apart. You put your hands on the deck and told it *no*, and it listened." He leaned against the mast, arms crossed, letting his voice stay easy. Easy was important. Fear made people run, and running people did stupid things, and Ash had buried enough stupid things off the coast of Greyharbor to last two lifetimes. "I'm not asking you to admit anything. I watched it happen. I just want to know why you're still pretending."

Xanthe Fairweather looked up at him, and for a moment he saw past the merchant's smile she wore like armor—the smile she'd been wearing since Pell fished her out of the surf alongside the wreckage of her cover story—and saw what was underneath. Exhaustion. The particular gray exhaustion of someone who had been holding a door shut against a gale for years and had just felt the hinges give.

"I'm pretending," she said slowly, "because every person who has ever learned what I am has either tried to sell me or bury me. Forgive me if I need a moment to work out which you are."

"Fair." He crouched down so they were eye to eye, which was a trick he'd learned from his daughter, of all people. *Get low, Papa. People trust you when you get low.* "Let me save you the working-out. Corvin saw too. So did Pell. And Tam, and the twins, and old Sefa in the galley. That's seven people who know what you are, and not one of them has said a word, because we've been sailing under this same sky together for three days now and nobody's turned you in."

Her eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"That," Ash said, standing, "is a longer conversation, and it deserves better than a hold that smells of bilgewater. Come above. And bring your hands."

"My hands?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The deck of the *Drowned Lantern* at dusk was a study in controlled chaos. Maren stood at the rail arguing with the wind itself—not magically, just the way old captains do, as though the weather were a business partner who'd reneged on terms. The twins were re-rigging the jib with the wordless efficiency of people who'd shared a skull in some previous life. And near the stern, half-hidden in the shadow of the cargo netting, Corvin sat with his massive hands wrapped around a mug of tea he never drank, watching Xanthe come up the ladder with the expression of a man watching a lit fuse walk toward powder.

Ash raised his voice just enough to carry. "Crew! Gather round. We're having the talk."

The effect was immediate and strange. Not surprise—recognition. The twins exchanged a glance and came down from the rigging without complaint. Pell abandoned her charts mid-calculation. Even Sefa emerged from the galley wiping her hands, and Tam, the youngest, practically ran. They arranged themselves in a loose circle around the mainmast with the ease of long practice, and Ash realized, watching them settle, that they had done this before. Perhaps many times. A ritual worn smooth by repetition.

"What talk?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"The one I give everyone," Ash said. "Maren calls it the Storm Oath. I call it the truth, because we don't have much else worth keeping." He took a breath. This part never got easier. Every time, there was a small voice in him that said *you could stop, you could keep the family small, small is safe*—and every time, he ignored it, because small and safe was how the hunters found people. Isolated. Alone. Afraid to reach sideways for help.

"Sefa," he said. "You want to start?"

The old cook shrugged. Her gray braid swung. "I boil water," she said, "and it boils faster when I'm angry. Been doing it sixty years. My mother boiled water the same way, and her mother before her, back to somewhere in the Ashfall Isles where they used to call us hearth-speakers and leave us offerings." She smiled, gap-toothed and unbothered. "Now they call us unregistered gifted and leave us warrants."

"Pell."

The navigator didn't look up from her own hands. "I can find true north blindfolded, underground, in a storm. Always could. Thought everyone could, till I was nine." A pause. "I worked for the court for eleven years before I understood what my little talent meant. What they'd have done with it. What they'd have done to me." She finally lifted her chin. "That's all you get for free."

"The twins can calm animals," Ash said, when neither of them spoke. "Any animal. Horses that would bite their own harnesses eat out of their palms. We smuggled a whole herd of stolen horses through a checkpoint once with those two humming at them like lullabies. Tam—" he nodded at the boy, who flushed with pride, "—can hold his breath for a quarter hour and doesn't feel cold. Ever. Corvin."

The helmsman's silence stretched so long that Xanthe shifted uncomfortably. When Corvin finally spoke, his voice was gravel dragged over stone. "Ships talk to me. Timber and rope and sail. I know where they hurt." His eyes went to Xanthe, then away. "My son had it worse. He could do what you do. Smaller. Rain, mostly. He thought rain was friendly."

Xanthe went very still. "Had?"

"Hunters took him in the spring," Corvin said, flat as a ledger line. "Eight months ago. Not weeks. Months. Whatever the criers are telling you about the magic hunt being new—it isn't. The proclamations are just the court admitting out loud what it's been doing quietly since winter. They've been taking people since the first snows. Quietly. In the night. Villages lose someone and call it fever, or flight, or debt-running. Nobody wants to say the word, because saying the word means admitting the crown eats its own children."

The deck was silent except for the creak of timber and the slap of wave against hull. Ash watched Xanthe absorb it—watched her merchant's mask crack and fall away entirely, leaving only the woman beneath, pale and fierce and suddenly, terribly awake.

"Months," she repeated. "Then Hollowmere—"

"Is probably already empty," Pell said, not unkindly. "If that's where you're bound. I'm sorry. I decoded enough court correspondence to know how they work. They take the gifted first, then anyone who sheltered them, then anyone who might have. By the time the criers arrive, there's no one left to cry to."

* * *

They gave her space after that—or tried to. She walked to the bow and stood gripping the rail, staring north toward the darkening line of the coast, and Ash let her have ten full minutes of it before he joined her. Some things needed company sooner rather than later; grief left alone too long calcified into something you couldn't share anymore. He'd learned that the hard way, in a house that had gone very quiet eight months ago, in a room where a boy's boots still stood by the door because no one could bear to move them.

"They'll want your oath," he said, coming to stand beside her. "The crew. It's not a formality. Maren will explain the words, but the shape of it is simple: no one gives anyone up. Not under torture, not under offer, not to save their own skin. If the hunters board us, we're all just smugglers moving untaxed silk, and you're a passenger paying your way in charm-work. Everyone forgets everything else. Forever."

"And if someone breaks the oath?"

"Then the crew handles it." He kept his voice level. "It hasn't happened yet."

"And your oath? You're not gifted. Why do you carry it?"

Ash looked out at the water. The thirteenth moon was rising behind the clouds, a day closer to full than yesterday, its light silvering the swells. Somewhere under that light, in a vault he'd only heard whispers of, a small soul-light burned with his daughter's name on it. Or no name at all. Names were the first thing the court took.

"Because I know what it costs," he said, "to love someone the court decided to take. And because a ship is just a promise people make to each other—that we'll get where we're going or drown trying, but nobody gets thrown overboard to lighten the load. I've captained crews that broke that promise. I won't again."

"You're not the captain."

"Maren owns the ship. I keep the crew alive. Ask anyone aboard which job matters more." He smiled, and was surprised to find it wasn't entirely a performance. There was something about this woman—the way she carried her fear like ballast instead of running from it—that made honesty come easier than it should. Dangerous. He knew better than to be honest with strangers. But the sea had been empty for hours, and the moon was climbing, and his daughter's face had been in his dreams every night this week, sharper as the turn approached.

"Your sister," he said quietly. "Pell says you didn't sign on for smuggling. You signed on for a name in a ledger."

Xanthe's knuckles whitened on the rail. "Isolde," she said, and the name came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh—relief and pain in the same motion. "Twenty years gone. Sold, maybe. To the court beneath the mountain. I don't even know if it's true. I have a charm with her knot-work on it and a dying woman's secondhand story, and I'm staking all of your lives on a maybe."

"People stake lives on less," Ash said. "Corvin staked his on a rumor of where the hunters' wagons were headed. It was wrong. He's still here. Still rowing." He turned to face her fully. "Here's what I actually wanted to say, storm-caller, and then I'll leave you to your moon. What you did in that squall—you didn't fight the storm. You *listened* to it. I've seen gifted go up against weather like that and it's a war, always a war, and wars wreck ships. You just... talked it down. Like you were apologizing to it."

"I was," she said, surprising him. "For twenty years I've been apologizing to storms. It's a long story."

"We've got three weeks of sailing and a road under watch ahead of us." He stepped back toward the circle of lantern-light where the crew waited, patient as stones. "Long stories are the only currency we take."

* * *

The oath was given at moonrise, because Maren insisted oaths taken in daylight could be read by anyone, but oaths taken under a turning moon belonged only to the taker and the sky.

They stood in a ring, all nine of them, and Maren spoke the old words—salt on the tongue, a knot tied in a length of tarred cord, each person naming themselves not by blood but by ship: *I am of the Drowned Lantern, and what the Lantern holds, I hold.* When the cord came to Xanthe, she hesitated, and Ash watched her look at each face in turn—Sefa's gap-toothed grin, Tam's open eagerness, Corvin's ruined tenderness, Pell's careful neutrality hiding eleven years of court-shaped scars—and whatever she was looking for, she found it, because she tied her knot tight and said the words clear.

When the ring broke, Tam appeared at Xanthe's elbow holding a chipped mug of tea, thrusting it at her with the ferocious shyness of a fifteen-year-old boy offering tribute. "Sefa makes it wrong on purpose," he confided. "So nobody asks for seconds. But it's warm."

Xanthe took the mug. Something in her face loosened—a door unbarred, a window opened onto a room that hadn't seen light in twenty years. "Thank you," she said, and then, to the whole ring of them, awkward and formal and utterly sincere: "I haven't been part of anything in a long time. I'd forgotten what it weighs. It weighs less than I remembered."

"It's not weight," Sefa said, gathering mugs. "It's rope. Feels heavy till you're falling."

Later, when the others had dispersed to their watches, Ash found himself alone at the rail beside Corvin, both of them pretending to check rigging that didn't need checking.

"She'll do," Corvin rumbled.

"She'll do more than do." Ash kept his voice low. "Did you feel it, when she called the squall down? The whole ship felt steadier. The timbers sang. Your ships talk to you—what did they say?"

Corvin was quiet a long moment. "They said," he said at last, "that they remember her. From before. From somewhere deep." His jaw worked. "And they said the mountain remembers her too. Ash—when we get under that stone, the rock's going to know what she is. I felt it in the hull tonight, just sailing north. The old tunnels hum when she sleeps."

Ash looked up at the thirteenth moon, one night fuller than the last, and felt the familiar cold settle into his chest—the cold that had lived there since spring, since a wagon he hadn't been fast enough to stop.

"Then we'd better make sure," he said, "that what the mountain remembers is a friend."

Behind them, unnoticed by either man, Xanthe stood at the bow with her tea going cold in her hands, and in the depths of the ship's hold, in a crate of luck-knots she had not yet unpacked, one charm made in another woman's hand had begun, very softly, to glow.
