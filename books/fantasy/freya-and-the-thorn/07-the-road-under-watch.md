## 7. The Road Under Watch

The Drowned Lantern put them ashore at a fishing village called Brackwater, three days north of Greyharbor, and from there the sea let them go.

Xanthe felt the parting like a wound. For eleven days aboard the barque she had lived inside the salt-smell and the creak of timber, inside the strange fragile family Ash had sworn together in the belly of a storm, and stepping onto solid ground felt like stepping out of a warm room into weather. The land did not know her. The land was watching.

They went inland in ones and twos, as Maren had ordered—Corvin first with the mule cart, then Pell and Wren disguised as a clerk and his daughter, then Xanthe and Ash a half-mile behind, dressed in the drab wool of wool-merchants traveling to market. Maren herself had stayed with the ship; someone had to keep the Lantern afloat and ready, and the old smuggler had gripped Xanthe's wrist hard enough to bruise before they parted.

"The mountain road is watched," she'd said. "Not by soldiers. Soldiers you can bribe. Watch for doors."

Xanthe hadn't understood until the second day.

Brackwater's inland road climbed through country that should have been green with early spring—the kind of rolling farmland where barley came up silver and sheep dotted the hills like spilled chalk. Instead the fields lay fallow in patches, whole acres gone to thistle, and the farmhouses they passed had a quality Xanthe couldn't name at first, until she did: no smoke. Chimneys standing clean and cold against the sky, one after another after another, like rows of unlit candles at a funeral.

"They've moved people out," Ash said quietly, riding beside her on a borrowed pony. He had been subdued since the storm, since his oath, watchful in a way that wasn't suspicion anymore but something more painful—guilt, maybe, or grief wearing guilt's clothes. "Or moved them *in*. There are holding farms east of here. The crown calls them resettlement villages."

"And what does the crown call it when a whole parish goes dark?"

Ash didn't answer. He didn't have to. The road answered for him.

* * *

The first sigil appeared at a crossroads shrine outside a village called Marrow's End—or what had been Marrow's End.

The shrine itself was old, older than the crown, a squat stone pillar worn smooth by centuries of travelers' touching hands. Someone had painted it. A single black mark, curling and branching like a root seen through skin, like a vein of ink spreading under paper. It was still wet enough to gleam.

"Thorn-sigil," Pell said, coming up the road behind them with Wren's small hand tucked in hers. The navigator had shed most of her sharpness on the road, or rather had redirected it, aiming it at every farmhouse and hedgerow like a woman taking inventory of a crime. "I saw them in the capital archives. Old records. They date back two hundred years—every time there was a bad harvest or a plague winter, somebody painted the Thorn on their door and left an offering."

"An offering to what?"

"To whatever would take the blame." Pell's mouth twisted. "That's the genius of it. You paint the Thorn, you give it your fear, and the court gives you absolution. It's not your fault your child was born gifted. It's not your fault your neighbor vanished. The *Thorn* did it. The Thorn is hungry. All you have to do is feed it someone else."

Wren pulled closer to Pell's side. The girl had barely spoken since Hollowmere became a word they traveled toward, but her eyes missed nothing, and Xanthe kept catching her looking at the empty houses the way children look at wells—fascinated and afraid of what might look back.

Marrow's End had perhaps forty homes. Every single threshold bore the mark.

Some were fresh, painted in the last week by the look of them. Some had faded to gray ghosts, years old. And some had been painted over other marks, layers upon layers, so that the doorstones looked bruised, blackened, like the houses themselves had been bleeding slowly for generations and no one had ever thought to stop it.

At one doorway, someone had left an offering: a child's shoe, filled with dried flowers gone to dust.

Xanthe made herself look away. "People did this to their own neighbors' doors."

"People did this to their own doors," Pell corrected. "That's worse. That's the whole trick of it. You don't need an army to make a country complicit. You just need to teach it which mark keeps the hunger off *your* house."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a stand of beeches, no fire, cold rations, Corvin taking first watch with a crossbow across his knees. Xanthe lay awake listening to the silence of the countryside—a wrongness she couldn't shake. Spring nights in farm country should have been loud. Dogs, owls, nightbirds, the low complaint of cattle. Instead there was only wind moving through empty fields, and once, very far off, a sound she chose to believe was a fox.

Ash sat up beside her, knees drawn, turning something in his fingers. In the dark she couldn't see what it was, only hear the small click of it against his rings.

"You're not sleeping either," he said.

"I'm counting chimneys."

"How many?"

"Too many without smoke."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "When I was a boy, my mother painted a sigil on our door. Before I even knew what it meant. She'd heard the hunters were coming through the valley, and she'd heard—" His voice caught, smoothed itself flat. "She'd heard they took a charm-singer's whole family in the next town. So she painted the Thorn, and she put a sprig of rowan above the lintel, and she told me never, ever to hum where anyone could hear. And I grew up thinking that was just what home smelled like. Paint and rowan and fear."

"What happened to her?"

"She died safe," Ash said, and the words had a terrible weight to them, the weight of a man who has done arithmetic he hates. "Safe and complicit. She pointed the hunters at a tinker family two lanes over when they asked if she'd seen anything odd. I watched her do it. She cried while she did it. Both things were true at once." He turned the object in his fingers again—click, click—and Xanthe realized it was a small carved thorn, a talisman, the kind pilgrims bought at shrines. "That's what I keep thinking about, on this road. Not the hunters. The mothers."

Xanthe stared up through the bare branches at the moon, three nights past full now, waning toward its thirteenth turn. Somewhere ahead of them, under a mountain, her sister hung in the dark like a lantern.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said, "before we become someone's reason to paint a door."

* * *

The fourth day brought them into hill country, and the first sign of the patrol.

Pell saw it from the ridge—an hour before it mattered, because Pell always saw things an hour before they mattered. She came sliding down the slope on her heels, breathless, and dropped to a crouch beside Corvin at the cart.

"Riders. Six, maybe seven, coming up the river valley. Blue-and-silver livery. And there's a wagon behind them, covered, drawn by four horses." Her jaw worked. "And a man walking in front who doesn't ride."

"Inquisitor?" Corvin asked. He said the word the way sailors say *reef*.

"Pale eyes. They say Serel's eyes are pale as frost. Can't tell at this distance, but nobody else walks in front of a hunter column."

Corvin looked at the map spread on the cart's tailboard, then at the road, then at the sky, doing the silent arithmetic Xanthe was learning to read on him. "There's a drover's track through the gill," he said finally. "It'll cost us half a day and the mule will hate it. But it puts a hill between us and the river road."

"Then we hate it too," Xanthe said.

They cut north through a steep wooded ravine where the track was barely a scar on the hillside, all loose stone and exposed root, and the mule expressed its opinion at length and eloquently. Twice they stopped and killed all movement, because Pell had heard something on the wind—hooves, voices, the particular jingle of harness that trained ears can pick out of birdsong. Once, through a gap in the trees, Xanthe saw them: the hunter column moving along the river road below, blue cloaks bright as kingfishers against the brown water, and the covered wagon swaying behind.

And walking before it, a figure in gray.

Even at distance, even across half a mile of valley air, the figure was wrong. Not large. Not armed, so far as Xanthe could see. Just *still*, in a way that living people aren't—each step placed like a chess move, the head never turning, never checking the road or the sky, as though the world had already agreed to hold nothing dangerous for him. The riders gave him a wide berth. Even the horses seemed to know.

"That's him," Wren whispered. She had crept up beside Xanthe without a sound, and her small hand found Xanthe's sleeve and gripped it. "That's the one who came to our house. He talked to Mama in a soft voice, like he was sorry about everything. And then they took Papa, and he *thanked* her. He thanked her for her cooperation." The girl's voice didn't break. That was somehow the worst part—it stayed level and thin as wire. "Is he going to thank my mama again someday?"

Xanthe knelt and took the girl's shoulders. Down in the valley, the gray figure walked on, patient as erosion.

"No," she said, and heard her own voice come out with a low roll buried in it, distant thunder looking for permission. "No, Wren. Because we're going to take away the thing he thanks people *for*."

She didn't know if it was true. She said it anyway, and felt the storm in her chest settle slightly, the way a dog settles when given a job.

* * *

They came down out of the gills at dusk into a wider valley, and the villages here changed character, and the change was worse than emptiness.

These villages were inhabited. Smoke rose from chimneys. Goats bleated behind fences, a smithy clanged somewhere, children's voices carried across a stubble field. Ordinary life, going on. But every door, every gatepost, every milestone and boundary stone bore the Thorn-sigil—fresh, black, meticulous—and along the main street of the first village they passed through, called Hallowford, someone had strung a line of white ribbons between the eaves of the houses, dozens of them, fluttering in the evening wind.

"For the taken," Ash murmured. "It's an old custom. You hang a ribbon for each person the court takes, so they can find their way home."

"How many ribbons, would you say?"

Ash counted in silence for a long moment. "Forty-one," he said finally. "In a village of maybe sixty households."

An old woman was drawing water at the well in the square. Corvin, playing the role of a carter better than any of them could have, drew rein and asked her—in the flat, bored tone of a man asking directions—if the road to Hollowmere was clear ahead.

The woman looked up at him with eyes that had run out of expressions to try. "Clear," she said. "Nothing on the road but the honest and the taken." Then, lower, without being asked, without looking at any of them: "You'll want to be through the pass before the new moon. They do their collecting on the dark nights. Easier, they say." She lifted her yoke and walked away, water slopping gently, a woman performing the last chore of a life that had stopped believing in chores.

On the well's rim, someone had scratched words into the stone, old and weathered but deep enough to survive: **WE PAINTED THE DOOR. THEY CAME ANYWAY.**

Xanthe read it twice and committed it to memory, the way she'd once memorized knot-patterns—as something that might save a life later, though whose, she couldn't yet say.

* * *

They made Hollowmere's outskirts on the sixth night, and the mountains rose up before them like a wall built by something with more ambition than mercy, black against the stars, snow on the high shoulders catching the last of the moonlight. The thirteenth turn was coming. Xanthe could feel it in her teeth, in the pressure behind her sternum, in the way her magic stirred restlessly against its chains whenever she faced that massif—as though the storm in her recognized something in the rock, kin calling to kin, or prisoner to jailer.

They sheltered in a ruined drover's barn a mile short of the town, and it was there, as Pell checked the map by shuttered lantern-light, that Corvin came in from the perimeter walk and said, in his voice like gravel settling:

"Fire on the mountain road. Below the pass. One fire, ringed."

They went to the rise above the barn and looked, and there it was: a single point of orange light in the black throat of the pass, and around it, faint in the dying wind, shapes. Riders dismounting. A wagon. And figures made to kneel.

"A collection," Pell breathed. "Tonight. On the dark-of-moon schedule. They're collecting *tonight*."

"Those could be travelers," Ash said, but his voice said he didn't believe it, and none of them believed it, and everyone knew the count that mattered: six hunters visible, one Inquisitor presumably among them, five of them, plus a girl, plus a mule, plus whatever waited in the dark between here and there.

Xanthe stood on the rise with the cold coming down off the mountain and felt the oldest feeling of her life rise to meet the newest: the storm, pressing at the inside of her ribs like a hand from within, asking—not begging anymore, not since the sea, not since the oath—*asking to be used.*

Below, in the pass, the firelight flickered. Somewhere in that ring of light, someone was being thanked for their cooperation.

"Hollowmere's just beyond the pass," Pell whispered. "If we go around, we lose the night and maybe the town. If we go through—"

"We go through," Xanthe said.

She was already untying her sleeves.
