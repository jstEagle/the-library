## 8. Hollowmere's Empty Cradles

The road into Hollowmere smelled of cold ash and unharvested apples.

Xanthe felt it before she saw it—the wrongness of the place, a hush that sat on the skin like the pressure before lightning. Her magic had been restless since they left the tree line, prickling along her arms like static caught in wool, and now she understood why. Hollowmere was not empty the way abandoned places were empty. It was empty the way a house is empty after a funeral: swept, tidied, and still full of everything that had been taken out of it.

They came down from the ridge in the grey of late afternoon, six travelers leading two mules, dressed as wool-buyers because Maren insisted wool-buyers drew less attention than pilgrims or peddlers. The town spread below them in a shallow bowl between the mountain's lower folds—slate roofs, crooked chimneys, a single street that followed a stream gone thin and quiet as a held breath.

No smoke rose from any chimney.

"No dogs," Pell said quietly. She stood at Xanthe's shoulder with her ledger-satchel clutched to her chest like a shield. "A town this size should have dogs. Chickens, at least. Something."

"Something ate them," Corvin said, and said nothing more.

Maren raised one gnarled hand and brought the column to a halt beneath a rowan tree whose berries hung heavy and untouched, scarlet against the grey sky. "We go in slow," she said. "Ash, you're my nephew today. Xanthe, his wife. Pell walks behind with the mules and keeps her mouth shut for once in her life." Pell opened her mouth; Maren's look closed it again. "Corvin, you circle wide through the back lanes. If there's anyone left, they'll be hiding where the walls are thickest. Whistle like a thrush if hunters come."

"And if I find someone?" Corvin asked.

"Then ask them what happened here," Maren said, "and pray they can answer."

* * *

They walked into Hollowmere the way you walk into water you know will be cold.

The first houses were shuttered but not broken. Doors closed, windows latched, garden gates hooked—that was the detail that made Xanthe's stomach turn over. People who fled in panic did not latch their gates. People who fled in panic left doors hanging open, pots on stoves, lives spilled out behind them. These doors had been closed by hands that expected to come back, or by other hands that wanted the street to look respectable.

On the third door down, someone had painted a sigil in white lime: a circle crossed by a thorned line, the barbs turned inward.

"The mark," Ash murmured beside her. He had been quiet since the ridge, wearing his charm-smile like a mask he'd forgotten how to take off. "I've seen it on three villages since we landed. Always on the gifted households."

"How do you know which those are?"

"You don't," Ash said. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Paint it on enough doors and everyone starts wondering if their neighbor was taken—or wondering if their neighbor reported them." His voice stayed light. His eyes didn't. "Fear does the crown's work cheaper than soldiers do."

Further up the street the signs grew worse. A workshop with its sign torn down—a comb-maker, by the shavings still scattered across the threshold, shavings no one had swept. A well with its bucket rope cut. And on the chapel steps at the street's end, a child's shoe, small and brown and placed neatly side by side with its pair, as though its owner had stepped out of them and simply risen into the air.

Xanthe stopped walking.

She hadn't meant to stop. But her body had decided something her mind refused to name, some animal recognition of the place itself—not the town, she'd never seen the town—but the *kind* of it. Mountain town. Slate and stream and terraced gardens going to seed. Isolde had lived in a town like this. Isolde had died in a town like this, according to a girl named Wren and a knot-worked charm that Xanthe had held in her own two hands and known the way you know your own heartbeat.

"Hollowmere," she said aloud, just to hear it. The word fell into the empty street and lay there.

"Easy," Ash said softly. "Whatever happened here, it's already happened. We're not too late to save anyone—we're only in time to learn."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," Ash said. "It's supposed to keep you walking."

* * *

The chapel door was locked, but the lock was old and Corvin's knife was patient, and inside they found dust, dust, and then—faintly, impossibly—the smell of stew.

Maren lifted her hand again, and everyone went still.

From behind the altar, from the shadowed hollow where a vestry door stood ajar, came a sound like a spoon scraping iron. Then a voice, cracked and thin and furious: "I've nothing left worth taking, so you may as well burn me with the rest."

"We're not the crown," Maren called back, in the tone she used on customs men—bored, unhurried, faintly insulted. "We're wool-buyers, and terrible ones, and we'd like to sit down somewhere warm. You have a fire back there. We can smell it."

Silence. Long enough that Xanthe counted her own heartbeats. Then, grudgingly: "Wool-buyers don't pick locks."

"Wool-buyers with honest locks don't get picked," Maren said. "May we?"

Another silence, and then the scrape of a bench being dragged, and Maren waved them forward.

The old woman behind the vestry door was small and fierce as a wren herself—white hair cropped short, hands spotted and steady, wrapped in three shawls against a chill no fire seemed able to touch. She had set a stool before the little stove and clearly intended to defend it. Her eyes went across each of them in turn, quick as an abacus, and lingered longest on Xanthe.

"You're not wool-folk," she said. "You walk like soldiers' nightmares. Sit anyway. Stew's turnip and river-trout, and there's bread hard enough to build with."

They sat. There were seven of them and one stool, so most crouched or leaned, and the vestry filled with steam and the smell of turnip, and for a few minutes nobody spoke because eating was holy work and the old woman watched them eat with open satisfaction, as though feeding people were a rebellion she could manage three times a day.

"Maren Saltwife," Maren said at last, when the bowl was scraped. "And these are mine. Who are you, mother?"

"Sister Aldith, when there was a sisterhood, which there isn't anymore." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "Now I'm the woman who didn't run fast enough and got lucky twice. What do you want?"

"The truth about this town," Xanthe said.

The old woman looked at her again, longer this time, and something moved behind her eyes—recognition, or its shadow. "You've got a voice like someone I knew," she said slowly. "Or half a voice. Like hearing a song through a wall." She shook her head. "Ask your questions. Truth's cheap now. Nobody's buying."

* * *

Hollowmere had held perhaps four hundred souls when the year began.

By midsummer, the crown's proclamation had come—registration, the clerks said, merely registration, a census of the gifted for their own protection—and forty-one names had been entered in the parish book by the reeve, who was a careful man and believed in law. By autumn, thirty-nine of those names had been summoned to the mountain. Two had fled. The reeve himself had gone in the winter, though his name had never been in the book, and Sister Aldith thought that told its own story.

"They didn't drag anyone," she said. She had begun turning her spoon in her cooling stew without eating it, her eyes fixed on the middle distance. "That's what I want understood. Whatever you imagine—chains, wagons, burning—it wasn't like that. They came with carriages. Velvet seats. An officer with a kind face who thanked each family for their service to the realm. And the families *went*. Walked up the mountain road on their own feet, carrying their own bundles, waving goodbye to the neighbors who stayed."

"Why would anyone—" Pell began.

"Because they were told they'd be honored," Aldith said. "Because their children were promised schooling and their debts forgiven. Because the ones who hesitated were shown letters from people who'd gone before—letters saying the mountain was beautiful, saying the work was light, saying *write soon*." Her spoon stopped. "The letters were real. Written in their own hands. That's the part that broke the town, in the end. Not the taking. The writing."

Xanthe's skin crawled. "How? If they were—"

"I'm an old woman, not a scholar." Aldith shrugged. "But I buried enough of the gifted in my time to know that a soul can be borrowed the way a coat can. The court under the mountain has always had clever fingers." She said it flatly, a fact among facts, and Xanthe felt Ash go very still beside her, and knew he was thinking of Wren's charm, and the dying woman in this town who had spoken of souls sold to the court.

"The woman who made charms," Xanthe said. Her voice came out strange, thinned. "Dying. Last spring, maybe. She gave a charm away—took the trouble to knot it herself, even ill. Do you know her?"

Aldith's gaze sharpened. "Knot-work, you say? Sea-style knots, double-locked ends?"

"Yes."

"There was one like that. Came through in the spring, heading down-valley, already coughing blood." The old woman's brow furrowed. "Stayed a night in the chapel loft. Talked all night, half fever, half sense. Said her name was—" She paused, and Xanthe realized she had stopped breathing. "—Isolde. Isolde Fairweather."

The word *Fairweather* went through Xanthe like a hook through silk.

She heard it from far away, the rest—Aldith saying the woman had spoken of the court, of souls bought and sold, of a ledger; Ash's hand arriving gently on her shoulder, anchoring her to the room. She nodded at intervals. She kept her merchant's smile nailed in place, twenty years of practice holding her upright while something inside her tore loose and drifted.

Isolde. Alive last spring. Dying alone on a road, giving away charms knotted in the style only their mother had taught them both, spending her last strength warning strangers about the thing that had taken her.

*Why didn't you come to me?*

The question rose hot and useless. Twenty years of exile answered it: *Where would I have sent a letter, Xanthe? To what address does an exile live?*

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to check the perimeter and the vestry held only the two of them and the ticking stove, Sister Aldith poured something amber into two clay cups and pushed one across.

"For the shaking," she said. "You've been shaking since I said her name. Drink."

Xanthe drank. It burned all the way down and settled in her chest like a coal.

"She spoke of you," Aldith said.

The coal went cold. "What did she say?"

"Not much. Names mostly, and places, and none of them hers—she was careful, whoever she'd been before. But near the end, when the fever had her, she talked to someone who wasn't in the room." The old woman's voice gentled. "She said, *tell her it wasn't her fault, and tell her I chose.* Over and over. *Tell her I chose.*"

"Chose what?" Xanthe whispered.

"I asked her that." Aldith turned her cup slowly in her spotted hands. "And she said the strangest thing, in that calm way the dying have, like they've already paid the fare and are only waiting for the coach. She said: *the court doesn't steal souls, whatever the stories say. It buys them. It can't take what isn't offered freely—that's the covenant, that's the whole rotten law of it. So it makes people want to sell.*"

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the empty street, rattling a loose shutter in a house where no one remained to hear it.

"She said she'd sold hers," Aldith went on, watching Xanthe's face with those sharp old eyes. "Sold it to the court beneath the mountain. And when I asked what price they'd paid her—for a soul, mind, there must be a price—she smiled at me. Smiled! With blood on her lips. And she said: *a life. One life, spared. The only currency they accept and the only one I ever needed.*"

The room tilted. Or Xanthe tilted within it. Twenty years fell away and reassembled themselves around a single point of light, a signature on paper she couldn't remember signing, a burning room she couldn't remember leaving, a sister's face above her in the dark saying words she couldn't remember hearing—

*Tell her it wasn't her fault.*

*Tell her I chose.*

One life, spared.

Whose?

She knew. She had known since the ridge, since the first white sigil on the first latched door, since the moment her magic had begun to sing against the stone of this valley like a struck bell. She had known and had refused to know, and now Sister Aldith sat across from her with pity rising in her face like water in a well, and the refusal collapsed.

"Me," Xanthe said. The word barely made a sound. "She sold her soul to save me."

"I think," Aldith said gently, "that you already knew that."

Had she? Had some buried shard of her known all along—some splinter of memory the court had pried out and filed away, the way they pried out everything, leaving her twenty years of exile with a hole in the middle shaped exactly like one night? Her hands were trembling. The storm that lived beneath her breastbone stirred against its chains, restless, wanting weather, wanting to tear the roof off the world—and she smothered it, smothered it, the way she had smothered it in Greyharbor's market street, the way she had smothered it every day of her exile, because that was the whole of her life, wasn't it? Smothering. Hiding. Being the secret her sister had died—

*had sold her soul*—

to keep.

"The ledger," she heard herself say, from very far away. "Pell said the ledger records buyers' purposes. Debts. If Isolde's entry is in that ledger—if the court wrote down what she bought with her sale—then it will say it plainly. It will say who was spared, and why, and what really happened that night." She looked up. "It will give me back what they took out of me."

Aldith studied her for a long moment. Outside, the wind rose, dragging its sleeve through the empty cradles of Hollowmere, and the old woman glanced toward the window and then back, and her voice dropped low.

"Then you'll want to hurry, girl. Because I'll tell you one more thing, and it's the reason I'm still hiding in my own chapel instead of dead on the mountain road." She leaned close. "Three nights ago, riders came through. Crown colors. They weren't collecting souls—they were *delivering* something. Crates, iron-banded, carried down the miners' road into the mountain. And the officer—the pale-eyed one, the one they call Serel—stood in my street and told what was left of this town that the moon completes her thirteenth turn in eighteen days, and that on that night, the Thorn wakes fully, and everything owed will be collected."

She straightened, and drained her cup, and her old eyes were terribly calm.

"Everything owed," she repeated. "Including, I'd wager, the debt your sister paid. Souls aren't spent here, girl. They're *held*. Held until the harvest." She reached out and gripped Xanthe's wrist, her fingers strong as roots. "If you mean to buy your sister back from that court, you have eighteen days. And the mountain, I think, already knows you're coming."
