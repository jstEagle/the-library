## 9. The Ledger of Names

Pell had spent eleven years learning to read the crown's handwriting, and four years since learning to hate it.

The ledger lay open on the table before her, its cover cracked leather gone soft as old skin, and she hated it the way one hates a former lover—intimately, specifically, knowing exactly where it was weakest. The Saltwife's cabin aboard the *Drowned Lantern* smelled of tar and dried kelp and the tallow candle Maren had grudgingly surrendered, and Pell sat hunched over the book with her sleeves pushed past her elbows and a stub of graphite behind each ear, because some habits from the Chancery died harder than others.

The ledger had cost them. That was the thing nobody said aloud. It had come out of Hollowmere in Corvin's pack, pried from the dead hands of a court courier whose horse had foundered on the mountain road—a man who had bled out in the bracken with his pale livery darkening around him while Corvin knelt over him with shaking fingers, not from grief but from fury, saying nothing at all. Pell had watched him take the ledger and understood that he was looking for a name. She hadn't asked which name. Some questions you saved until you were ready for any answer.

Now she turned a page, and the candle guttered, and she read.

The entries were written in cipher—not a true cipher, nothing so honest. It was the Chancery's shorthand, the double-column notation used for grain tariffs and harbor dues, adapted for cargo that could not be weighed. Left column: acquisition. Right column: disposition. Pell's fingers moved down the left column, translating without thinking, the way she'd once translated tax assessments by candlelight in a cold office above the Hall of Records while snow piled against the shutters and clerks whispered about which families had stopped coming to register their gifted children.

*Acquired: one soul, female, age 34. Seller: self. Consideration rendered: pardon of kin, remission of sentence.*

Below it, in the right column, the disposition line was not an amount. It was a word.

*Thorn.*

Pell sat back. Her chair creaked. Outside the cabin walls, the ship groaned against the dock pilings, and somewhere above, boots crossed the deck—the night watch, Corvin probably, walking his rounds because sleep had become a country he could no longer enter.

She looked at the entry again. *Seller: self.*

That was the part the market rumors got wrong, she thought. The stories in the port towns spoke of souls stolen, snatched, dragged screaming into the mountain. But the ledger told a quieter story, and Pell knew from eleven years in the Chancery that the quiet stories were always worse. Nobody had taken this woman's soul. She had sold it. Sold it gladly, if the consideration was what Pell suspected—pardon of kin, remission of sentence—and the clerk who recorded it had noted the transaction with the same dry efficiency he'd have given a shipment of salt herring.

There was a marginal note beside the entry, small enough that Pell had missed it on first pass. She bent close, angling the page toward the candlelight. The handwriting was different here—older, more ornate, the hand of someone senior, someone permitted opinions.

*Buyer's purpose confirmed. Root accepts willingly given stock more readily than taken. Note for the Registry: encourage surrender where possible. Fear produces poor yield; gratitude produces abundance.*

Pell's stomach turned over slowly, like a bilge pump bringing up something foul.

*Encourage surrender where possible.* She thought about the proclamations nailed to every post in Greyharbor. *Registration is protection. Registration is mercy.* She thought about the painted thorn-sigils on thresholds in the empty villages, sigils the fleeing families had left behind—or been made to leave behind—as marks of houses already emptied. She thought about how the hunt had been framed, how the fear had been built brick by careful brick, so that when the offer came—register, surrender your gift, be spared—it would feel like rescue. Like gratitude.

*Gratitude produces abundance.*

"Gods below," she murmured, and her breath fogged in the cold cabin air. "It's a farm."

The word hung there. A farm. Not a prison, not a slaughterhouse—a farm, tended with all the patient husbandry the Chancery brought to everything, crops encouraged rather than forced, harvests scheduled by the moon. And the moon—

Pell flipped forward through the pages, fast now, her graphite scratching. The entries grew denser toward the back of the ledger, the acquisitions more frequent, and interspersed among them were notations in yet another hand, rounder, almost fussy, recording something called *turns*. She found the last one and read it twice.

*Turn twelve complete. Stock condition: restless. Root feeding increases. Turn thirteen anticipated within three weeks of this entry. Preparation of the court required. All holdings to be presented.*

Three weeks.

She did the arithmetic automatically, the way she did everything—three weeks from the date of the entry, corrected for the courier's travel time from Hollowmere, corrected again for the days they'd spent hiding in the chapel and walking the high road past villages with empty doorways. Three weeks, give or take two days, until the moon completed its thirteenth turn and the Thorn woke fully, and every soul in this ledger—every soul in whatever greater registry this was merely a copy of—would be *presented*, like plate at a banquet, like daughters at a marriage market.

Pell closed the ledger. Then opened it again immediately, because closing it felt too much like obedience.

She was a former clerk. She knew what documents did. Documents made monstrous things navigable, made them rows and columns, made them *work product*. For eleven years she had helped the crown turn people into entries, and she had told herself it was honest work, that the numbers had to live somewhere, that a well-kept record was a kindness compared to chaos. She had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold. And then one day she had processed a seizure order for a weaver's son in Duncarrow, a boy of nine whose gift was making thread sing, and she had seen the disposition column, and the disposition column had said *Thorn*, and she had understood that the floor had never been a floor at all—that she had been standing on the root itself the whole time, one of its fine white tendrils, drawing nourishment upward.

She had burned her seal two nights ago, in the galley, alone. It had smelled of wax and copper and it had felt like nothing, which frightened her more than grief would have.

Now she pulled fresh paper toward her and began to write, because translation meant nothing unless it was shared, and sharing meant clarity, and clarity was the only weapon she had ever trusted. Her hand moved steadily. *Souls are recorded as debts,* she wrote. *The court holds them as assets against the Thorn. Willing surrender preferred. Thirteenth turn: three weeks.*

Then she paused, graphite hovering, and wrote the last line slowly, pressing hard enough that the point snapped and she had to reach up to the other stub behind her ear:

*Isolde Fairweather — sold her own soul. Buyer's purpose: the Thorn. Consideration: unknown, likely pardon or exile of kin.*

She stared at that line for a long moment. Fairweather. Xanthe Fairweather, who slept in the forepeak with her boots on, who flinched from open sky, who had tied a luck-knot for Pell's wrist on the second day out and told her, with a merchant's easy smile, that it would keep her bearings true. Xanthe, whose sister had vanished from all records twenty years ago—not fled, not died, *sold*, and sold by herself, and the consideration almost certainly a pardon, almost certainly an exile, almost certainly—

Almost certainly Xanthe.

Pell set down the graphite. Her hands were steady—they were always steady, that was the joke of her, steady Pell, dry Pell, the clerk who never wept even when they'd confiscated her brother's instruments along with his freedom—but inside, something cold and precise was assembling itself, the way figures assembled on a page, row upon row, until the total could no longer be disputed.

Xanthe didn't know. Xanthe had spent twenty years believing her sister had simply gone, and that the exile was punishment, arbitrary weather from an arbitrary crown. But the ledger said otherwise. The ledger said Isolde Fairweather had walked into the court beneath the mountain and paid for her sister's life with the only coin she had, and the court had accepted, and the clerk had recorded it, and somewhere in the margins a senior hand had noted with satisfaction that willing stock yielded better.

And the moon would complete its thirteenth turn in three weeks, and Isolde Fairweather's soul would be *presented* to the Thorn, and Xanthe would never know what her sister had bought, or what it had cost, or that forgiveness might have been hers for the asking all along.

Pell blew out the candle. Sat in the dark. Listened to the ship breathe.

Then she picked up the papers, squared their edges precisely, and went to find the crew.

* * *

They gathered in the hold, because Maren refused to discuss treason above decks where gulls might listen—"Gulls are court birds," she'd said once, entirely seriously, and no one had laughed, because after Hollowmere no one laughed easily anymore.

Corvin sat with his arms folded, a wall of a man carved down to essentials. Ash lounged against a crate with the studied ease of a man performing relaxation, though Pell noticed his eyes kept going to the ledger in her hands. Wren perched on a coil of rope near the lantern, the girl they'd collected in Greyharbor, clutching her sister's charm-knot like a rosary. Maren stood apart, smoking a pipe that had gone out an hour ago. And Xanthe sat closest to the lantern, because even now, even below decks, she positioned herself where the light was worst, half her face in shadow—an instinct, Pell realized, of a woman who had spent twenty years practicing invisibility.

"The ledger's decoded," Pell said, without preamble. She had learned long ago that bad news delivered gently was still bad news, and gentleness just wasted everyone's time. "It's a copy of the court's registry. Souls are entered as debts. Acquisition on the left, disposition on the right."

"Disposition meaning what?" Ash asked. His voice was warm as ever, but Pell heard the wire under it.

"Meaning where they go." She turned the ledger open to the marked page and held it toward the lantern. "Every entry ends the same way. One word. *Thorn.*"

Silence. The lantern flame leaned in the draft from the hatch, and shadows swung across their faces like slow bells.

"There's more," Pell said. "The court prefers willing sellers. There's a note in the margin—senior hand, someone with authority. *Fear produces poor yield; gratitude produces abundance.* They've built the whole hunt to make surrender feel like mercy. Register, renounce, be spared. Every soul that walks in grateful feeds the root better than one dragged in screaming."

Maren took the pipe from her mouth. "I've moved gifted folk across that border for thirty years," she said quietly. "Half of them thanked me for getting them somewhere they could give themselves up clean. I thought I was saving them from the hunters." Her jaw worked. "I was delivering stock."

"No," Corvin said. It was the first word he'd spoken, and it landed like a dropped anchor. Everyone looked at him. He was staring at the middle distance, at some fixed point in the dark beyond the lantern. "You were saving them. What the court does with mercy isn't yours to carry." His voice didn't rise, but something in it made the hull timbers seem to lean away. "I carried my own share long enough. Put it down, Saltwife. You'll need both hands for what's coming."

Pell saw Maren look at him—really look, the way you look at a man you've sailed with for years and suddenly realize has a wound you never dressed. She filed it away. Later. Everything was later now.

"How long?" Xanthe asked.

Her voice was level. Too level. Pell recognized the tone from the Chancery—from the families who came to hear seizure orders read aloud, the ones who'd learned that composure was the only armor issued to the powerless.

"Three weeks," Pell said. "Maybe less. The last entry notes the thirteenth turn—when the moon completes it, the Thorn wakes fully, and all holdings are to be presented. Every soul in the registry. Gathered and given."

"To the root," Wren whispered. In her hands, the charm-knot had gone tight enough to whiten her knuckles. "To the thing under the mountain."

"To the throne," Pell corrected, and hated herself for the precision. "The root grows through it. The throne *is* the root, as far as I can tell. Weather, harvest, memory—the covenant buys dominion, and the souls are the interest payments."

"And mine?" Xanthe asked.

Pell had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it three times in the dark cabin, choosing words the way she chose column headings—for accuracy, for minimal damage. All of it evaporated now. So she did what she'd done for eleven years: she read from the record.

"Isolde Fairweather," she said. "Sold her own soul. Twenty years ago, autumn term. The consideration isn't itemized, but comparable entries from that period—" she made herself keep going, made herself be the clerk, because the clerk was the only person she could bear to be right now— "comparable entries list pardons of kin. Remission of sentences. Exile in place of execution."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, the tide knocked the hull against the pilings, a slow wooden heartbeat.

Xanthe didn't move. That was the terrible thing, Pell thought. She didn't flinch, didn't cry out, didn't do any of the things a person does when the ground opens. She just sat very straight in the bad light with half her face in shadow, and Pell watched twenty years of a carefully constructed story die behind her eyes without disturbing a single feature.

"She bought me," Xanthe said at last. Flat. Testing the words like coins bitten for gold. "My exile wasn't a sentence. It was the *price*. She walked into that place and paid it, and I—" Something moved through her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "I spent twenty years being angry at her for leaving."

No one spoke. There was nothing to say. Pell knew the shape of that silence intimately; she had sat in it across from a hundred families, holding papers that explained everything and consoled nothing.

Ash shifted against his crate. "Then we have three weeks to get her back," he said, and his voice had lost its performance entirely—all the charm stripped off it, leaving something raw and certain underneath. "The vault, the registry, whatever they call it. We go in, we find Isolde Fairweather's entry, and we take it out of their books."

"You can't take a soul out of a book," Maren said. "You take it out of the *root*. Different animal entirely."

"Then we learn the animal," Ash said. "Pell. The ledger—does it say where they're held?"

Pell hesitated. This was the part she'd saved, the part she'd turned over and over in the dark cabin, because it was either the best news or the worst news depending entirely on courage. "There are references," she said. "A holding. The entries call it the Vault of Held Breaths. It's beneath the court proper—deeper than the throne, if the tunnel diagrams in the back matter mean anything. And—" she met Ash's eyes, then Xanthe's, then Corvin's, one by one, because this needed to land on all of them— "the thirteenth-turn preparations require the holdings to be *moved*. Presented to the root. Which means, for a window of days, maybe hours, the vault will be open. The souls will be in transit. Between the vault and the throne."

"In transit," Corvin repeated softly.

"A caravan of souls," Maren said, and her laugh had no humor in it at all. "Gods preserve us. You're proposing to rob a funeral procession."

"I'm proposing," Pell said, "to intercept a debt before payment comes due. It's what I did for eleven years—only in reverse, and for the right side this time." She closed the ledger and held it against her chest, feeling its weight, the weight of every name inside it, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a life reduced to a line in a fair hand. "We have three weeks. We need the tunnel diagrams memorized, we need a way into the court, and we need—" she looked at Xanthe, really looked, at the woman sitting rigid in the shadowed light with her sister's sacrifice finally legible at last— "we need to decide what we're willing to spend. Because the court buys loyalty cheaply, Maren said that from the start, and everything in this ledger says they'll make us an offer. Each of us. Whatever we want most. That's how they farm."

The lantern guttered. In the silence, Wren unclenched her fingers from the charm-knot and held it up to the light, and Pell saw the knot-work clearly for the first time—the fine looping pattern, the maker's signature hidden in the weave, the hand of a woman who had tied luck into string for strangers right up until the day she tied her own soul into a ledger.

"My sister made this," Wren said quietly, to no one in particular. "Before she went into the mountain. She said if anything happened, follow the knots." She looked up, and her young face was set in an expression far too old for it. "So let's follow them all the way in."

Corvin nodded once, slow as a door closing. Maren knocked out her dead pipe against her boot. Ash pushed off from the crate and stood, and for a moment his eyes met Pell's, and she saw in them something she couldn't name—something bright and desperate and already halfway to a decision she hadn't witnessed yet.

"Three weeks," Xanthe said. She rose. The lantern light caught her fully for once, both halves of her face, and Pell saw that the exhaustion of twenty years had burned away and left something harder underneath, something with edges. "Then we stop hiding in harbors. Tomorrow we sail for the coast road, and after that—" she looked down at the ledger in Pell's arms, at the names sleeping inside it— "after that, we go under the mountain, and we settle accounts."

Outside, far above the hatch, through timber and tar and distance, the moon was rising—waxing, patient, beginning its thirteenth turn. Pell felt it the way she'd once felt due dates approaching in the Chancery: a pressure behind the ribs, a clock starting in earnest.

She hugged the ledger tighter and followed the others up toward the light.
