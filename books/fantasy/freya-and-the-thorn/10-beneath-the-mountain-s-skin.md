## 10. Beneath the Mountain's Skin

The entrance to the old miners' tunnel was a wound in the hillside, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble and the kind of dark that seemed to have weight. Xanthe stood at its mouth with a hooded lantern in one hand and her own heartbeat loud in her ears, and she thought: *this is how a throat looks before it swallows.*

"You can still turn back," Maren said behind her. The Saltwife's voice was flat as slate, but her eyes—when Xanthe glanced back—were not. They were fixed on the tunnel mouth with an expression Xanthe had seen on fishermen's faces when they spoke of a particular stretch of sea that took boats and gave nothing back.

"No," Xanthe said. "I can't."

It was the truest thing she'd said in weeks. Behind them, three days' hard travel from the coast, the villages had grown emptier with every mile—doors swinging on leather hinges, thorn-sigils painted in white clay above thresholds like warnings or invitations, Xanthe couldn't decide which. Ahead of them lay Hollowmere's other face: the court beneath the mountain, where souls hung like fruit and her sister's name sat in a ledger written in debts.

Corvin went first. He always went first; it seemed to be the whole of his personality distilled into a single habit. His broad shoulders filled the tunnel for a moment, then the dark took him, and his voice came back out of it like something dredged up: "Low ceiling. Watch your heads. And don't touch the walls more than you have to."

"Why?" Pell asked, ducking under a lip of stone.

"Because the walls remember," Corvin said, and did not explain.

Xanthe touched the wall anyway. She couldn't have told anyone why—only that some part of her, the part that had been calling storms since she was six years old and hiding them since she was sixteen, leaned toward the stone the way a compass needle leans north. The rock was cold and damp and utterly ordinary, and yet beneath her palm something *hummed*. Not sound. Pressure. The way the air felt ten breaths before lightning, when the sky goes the color of a bruise and every hair on your arms stands up to listen.

She snatched her hand back.

* * *

They walked for hours. The tunnel descended in long switchbacks, following what had once been a tram-line for ore carts—the rotted sleepers still visible under centuries of silt—and the air grew warmer and stranger the deeper they went. It wasn't mine-warm. It was body-warm, blood-warm, as though the mountain itself had a pulse and they were walking down its veins toward the heart.

"Old stories say the miners broke into something," Ash said. He'd fallen into step beside Xanthe somewhere past the second hour, carrying his lantern low so its light pooled around their feet instead of blinding them. He was good at that—making himself small, unthreatening, easy company. Too good at it, maybe. "Before the court came down here. Something already living in the deep rock. The miners sealed it up and the crown opened it again, two hundred years later, because crowns can't leave locked doors alone."

"And you believe that?"

"I believe the mountain is warm," Ash said, "and I've never been anywhere warm underground that didn't want something." He smiled at her, quick and crooked, and Xanthe found herself smiling back before she caught herself and made her face neutral. Trust was a coin she'd stopped spending freely. But coins wore smooth with handling, and Ash had been steady since the storm at sea—steady when he'd had every reason not to be.

The others strung out along the line behind them: Pell muttering distances into a wax tablet, counting turns with a clerk's precision even now; Maren bringing up the rear with a knife loose in her belt and her eyes on everything; Wren—no, Wren had stayed behind in Hollowmere with the old woman in the chapel, which was safer, which was right, though Xanthe had hated leaving her. The girl carried Isolde's charm against her heart. It would be waiting when this was done. *If* this was done.

At the third hour, the humming grew louder.

Or not louder—*closer*, as if it had been outside her all along and was now moving inside, settling behind her sternum like a second heartbeat slightly out of rhythm with the first. Xanthe stopped walking. The line stopped with her, lanterns bobbing.

"What is it?" Maren asked.

"I don't—" Xanthe pressed her knuckles to her breastbone. "My magic. It's doing something."

"Doing what?"

"I don't know." That was the truth and it terrified her. For twenty years her gift had been a simple thing, brutal in its simplicity: a storm lived in her chest, and she kept it leashed, and the keeping cost her constant effort, like holding a door shut against a gale. She knew every trick of it, every pressure point. This was different. This was the storm going quiet—not calmed but *attentive*, the way a dog goes still when it scents another animal through a fence. Her magic was listening to the mountain. Or the mountain was listening to her.

"Keep moving," Corvin said quietly. "Standing still down here is worse than walking."

They kept moving.

* * *

It began with the smell of smoke.

There was no smoke. The air was mineral-damp and warm, tasting faintly of iron, and there was no fire within fifty miles that Xanthe knew of. But the smell arrived anyway, sudden and specific: pine pitch burning, linen burning, *hair* burning, and underneath it the sweet resinous reek of a house giving itself up to flame.

Xanthe's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She was eleven years old. No—she was thirty-six, standing in a tunnel under a mountain, and she was also eleven years old, sitting on the back steps of a house with her knees drawn up, watching fireflies over the garden, and the door behind her was open and her mother was singing something low about the moon and—

"Xanthe." Ash's hand on her arm. "You've gone grey. What's wrong?"

"Nothing." Her voice came out wrong, thin. "I'm fine. Keep walking."

But the smell stayed with her, and worse than the smell came the images, surfacing like drowned things rising to break the surface of a lake. They came without warning and without order, each one complete and vivid and *impossible*, because Xanthe did not remember these things. She had never remembered them. She knew—for she had always known, the way you know a room exists behind a locked door even if you've never been inside—that her childhood before sixteen was mostly weather. Grey weather. A few bright days: learning knots from her father's big hands, the smell of her mother's herb chest, Isolde laughing. Everything else was fog, and she had assumed the fog was just what time did to ordinary years.

These memories were not foggy. These memories burned.

*A kitchen at night. Isolde, fifteen and furious, standing between Xanthe and their parents, arms spread wide. Their mother crying. Their father's voice, very calm, saying words Xanthe could not hear—as if the memory had been filed with the sound stripped out—and Isolde shouting back, and the shout arriving silent too, only the shape of it, her sister's mouth working, her sister's eyes wet and blazing.*

*Xanthe's own hands, wrapped in wet cloth, blistered. Why would her hands be blistered?*

*A signature on paper. Ink drying. A man's ring pressing into wax—a seal she almost recognized, a shape like a root, like a thorn—and beside the paper a cup of tea gone cold, and Isolde's hand coming down to sign, steady, so steady, while Xanthe watched from somewhere far away and could not move, could not speak, could not—*

"Stop." Xanthe said it aloud, to the tunnel, to herself. "Stop it."

The crew had halted. Lantern light wavered across four faces turned toward her, and in each of them she read a different version of the same question. Pell's was clinical: *is she going to be a liability.* Corvin's was flat and patient. Maren's was knowing, and that was worst of all, because Maren had warned her, hadn't she, back in Greyharbor: *the mountain road does things to people who carry things.*

"The stone," Xanthe managed. "Something in the stone is—it's showing me things. Things I don't remember."

"Memories don't work like that," Pell said. Then, slower: "Do they?"

"They do," Maren said, "if someone took them out and left the doors open behind them." She came forward, took Xanthe's chin in a rope-hard hand, and studied her eyes the way she'd study a chart. "Who sold your memories, girl?"

"I don't—" Xanthe started, and stopped, because the answer rose in her like water in a well: *I did. Someone signed for me. I watched it happen.* "I don't know. That's the point. There are years missing and I always thought—I always told myself it was just childhood, everyone loses childhood, but this isn't childhood, this is—" Her voice cracked. "This is the night my family ended. I know it is. I can feel the shape of it and I can't see inside."

The tunnel hummed. Warm. Patient. Like a lock waiting for the right key.

"We're close to the court's skin," Maren said, releasing her. "This deep, the Thorn's roots run through everything. If it holds your memories, it knows you're coming. It may be showing you these on purpose."

"Why would it do that?"

"To make you want to reach the throne room," Maren said simply. "Grief is the best bait there is. I've baited hooks with lesser things and caught bigger fish than you."

Xanthe looked down the tunnel, into the dark where the tram-sleepers vanished, where the air pulsed warm as a held breath. Somewhere down there was a ledger with her sister's name in it. Somewhere down there, apparently, was also a locked room with Xanthe's own name on the door.

*Isolde's face, furious and wet-eyed, standing between Xanthe and their parents. Standing between Xanthe and something. Protecting her from what—or protecting them from her?*

The question slid into her like a splinter and sat there.

"Walk," Corvin said, and they walked.

* * *

An hour later—though time had gone soft and unreliable, stretching like taffy in the warm dark—they reached the place where the miners' works ended and something else began.

The tram-line simply stopped. Beyond it the tunnel continued, but the walls changed: the rough-hewn granite gave way to stone that was smooth and dark and veined, and the veins were not mineral. They were black, branching, fine as hair near the edges and thick as a man's wrist at the center of the walls, and they moved—not quickly, not obviously, but if you looked away and looked back, the pattern had shifted, the way clouds shift, the way roots grow when no one watches.

Pell touched one and jerked her hand back as if scalded. "It's warm."

"Don't touch it," Corvin said, for the second time that day, and this time nobody argued.

Xanthe stood before the veined wall and felt her storm rise to meet it. Not violently—there was no violence in it at all, which frightened her more than any surge ever had. Her magic pressed outward against its leash like a child pressing its face to a window, and on the other side of the glass, the black veins pressed back. Recognition. Kinship. Two currents of the same deep water finding each other through stone.

*Oh,* she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Whatever feeds the court's power—it speaks the same language I do.*

And with the thought came the last fragment, the one she'd been circling all day the way a tongue circles a broken tooth:

*A burning room. Her own hands raised, and the storm pouring out of her—not called, not wanted, *bursting* from her like blood from a cut artery—and the roof beams catching, and her mother's voice cut off mid-word, and her father turning to run toward her instead of away, and Isolde dragging Xanthe bodily out into the rain, both of them screaming, the house roaring behind them—*

Xanthe came back to herself on her knees. She didn't remember falling. Ash was crouched in front of her, both hands on her shoulders, saying her name, and beyond him the others had formed a loose circle, lanterns high, watching the dark as much as watching her.

"I killed them." The words came out of her flat and strange, like someone else's voice played back through a shell. "My parents. The night of the storm. It wasn't bandits, it wasn't sickness, it wasn't any of the stories I built to fill the hole. It was me. My gift. I burned our house down with them inside it, and I didn't remember because—" Because someone had taken it out. Because someone had paid to have it taken out. Because Isolde had stood in a kitchen, arms spread wide, shouting silently at their parents, and then had gone somewhere cold and quiet and signed her name on paper beside a seal shaped like a thorn.

Twenty years ago. Twenty years of exile bought with her sister's soul, and Xanthe had spent those twenty years thinking herself merely unlucky.

"Xanthe." Ash's voice, gentle. "Breathe. Whatever you saw—"

"It's true." She got her feet under her. Her legs shook. She made them hold. "That's the worst part. It doesn't feel like a vision. It feels like remembering." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and found it wet. "Serel offered me a memory of Isolde. Back at the safehouse, before we came down. I thought she was lying. I thought it was bait." A laugh came out of her, awful and small. "It wasn't even a lie. She was offering me back pieces of myself the court has been keeping in a drawer."

Maren's expression had not changed, but something in it had settled, the way a ship settles when it takes on ballast. "Then you understand what you're walking into. The Thorn doesn't just hold souls, girl. It holds *leverage*. Every secret, every grief, every buried thing. That's how a throne keeps dominion—not with soldiers. With drawers full of other people's nights."

"Then we go faster," Xanthe said. She picked up her lantern. Her hand only trembled a little. "Every hour down here, it's showing me more. Teaching me. Softening me up. I'd rather get to the vault while I'm still angry enough to be useful."

Pell consulted her wax tablet, all business, mercifully incurious. "By my count we're perhaps two hours from the junction marked on the stolen charts. Past that, the galleries of the court proper. Air gets better—ventilated. Guarded."

"Guarded by what?"

"By bells," Pell said, and when they all looked at her: "The charts call it the Court of Quiet Bells. Nobody writes down why."

They went on. The tunnel sloped downward through the veined stone, and the black branches thickened overhead until they walked beneath a canopy of slow-moving dark, like traveling through the inside of a heart. Xanthe kept her eyes forward and her storm leashed and her hands fisted at her sides, and behind her closed eyelids, unbidden, Isolde's face waited—fifteen years old and furious, sixteen and signing, twenty and dying, all of it hers now, all of it returned, none of it asked for.

*You sold yourself for me,* she thought at the memory, at the sister inside it. *And I spent twenty years not even knowing I owed you.*

The tunnel hummed. The mountain breathed. And far ahead, faint as a pulse through a wrist, Xanthe heard—for the first time, unmistakable, impossible to unhear—the sound of bells ringing in a place where no wind could reach them.
