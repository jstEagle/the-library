## 11. The Court of Quiet Bells

The bells were the first wrong thing.

Xanthe had expected the mountain's heart to sound like a mine—hammer-fall, cart-rattle, the cough of men in the dark. Instead, as the miners' tunnel opened into a gallery of pale veined stone, she heard bells. Small ones. Silver-toned, patient, ringing in no pattern she could name, from no source she could find. They came from everywhere and nowhere, the way her mother's voice had come from everywhere and nowhere in the last dream Xanthe could remember having.

"Where are they?" Pell whispered at her shoulder. The former clerk had gone grey around the mouth since they'd passed the last checkpoint, her old seal burned to slag in a brazier two days behind them. "I catalogued this court for six years. There were no bells."

"Maybe that was the point," Corvin said.

They stood at the mouth of a stair that spilled down into light—not torchlight, not lamplight, but something cooler, like moonlight filtered through water. The gallery below opened wide as a cathedral, its ceiling lost in a haze of glow, its walls carved with reliefs of harvests and coronations and storms bending obediently before crowned figures. And moving through it all, dressed in silk the color of deep water and candlewax, were the courtiers.

Xanthe had sold charms to fishermen for eleven years. She knew how to make her face do pleasant things while her mind did arithmetic. She put on the merchant now, smoothed it over the storm coiled tight beneath her ribs, and descended the stair with Pell's forged papers in her satchel and Ash's arm resting lightly—too lightly—against hers.

*Agent Vessa Thornhale,* the papers said. *Broker's representative, licensed under the Seal of Weights and Measures.* A soul-broker named Odo Vess operated out of Greyharbor's back streets, buying small magics from desperate people and reselling them upward. His signature on their letters of introduction had cost Maren three favors and a cask of aged brandywine. If anyone in this court checked—and someone would check—the trail would lead to Vess, and Vess would be found very drunk, very confused, and very far away by then.

*Smile,* Ash had told her that morning, adjusting the collar of her borrowed coat. *But not too much. Brokers' agents are paid to be bored.*

She was bored. She was bored the way a drawn bow is still.

* * *

The courtiers noticed them without seeming to notice, which was itself a kind of noticing. As Xanthe crossed the gallery floor, conversations dipped and resumed around her like water closing over a stone. She caught fragments—a woman laughing at something a man had said, a pair of nobles discussing the vintage of a wine that could not possibly grow this far underground—and it took her perhaps thirty steps to understand what was wrong with the laughter.

It repeated.

Not exactly. Not mechanically. But the same laugh, the same rise and fall, the same little catch at the end, came from three different mouths as she passed through the crowd. A young man in silver laughed with an old woman's catch in his throat. A duchess in moth-grey silk turned her head with a gesture Xanthe had seen a fishwife make on the docks of Greyharbor, the exact tilt, the exact half-second pause before speaking, as though the duchess were borrowing the motion the way one borrows a coat.

"They wear them," Pell breathed. She had stopped pretending to walk; she stood frozen beside a fountain of black stone where no water moved. "The mannerisms. The voices. They're *wearing* them."

"Wearing what?" Ash asked, and his voice was easy, but his hand had found the small of Xanthe's back, steadying her, or himself.

"The souls." Pell's clerk's voice had come back, flat and precise, the voice she used for inventories. "When the court holds a soul, it doesn't just keep it in a jar. It—it circulates them. Little pieces. A laugh here. A way of standing there. Look at the Lady in blue—she's got a soldier's posture. Look at how she holds her shoulders. That's not hers."

Xanthe looked. The lady in blue stood like a man who had spent twenty years carrying a pike, weight balanced for a weapon that wasn't there, head turning in the small economical sweeps of someone trained to watch horizons. Her gown was worth more than Greyharbor's fish market. Her body remembered a war.

A courtier drifted near them—young, beautiful, wearing a green doublet embroidered with thorn-work—and greeted them in a voice like warm honey. "Agents of Master Vess? You're expected. The Chamber of Accounts keeps its own hours, but the Undersecretary is generous with guests who bring inventory." He smiled. It was a lovely smile, practiced, and somewhere inside it Xanthe heard—faintly, the way you hear your own name across a crowded market—a girl's giggle underneath, a child's giggle, worn smooth from use.

Her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

"We're honored," Ash said for both of them, bowing just deeply enough.

The courtier led them through the crowd, and Xanthe made herself look at everything, because looking was her job now, and because not looking would have been a mercy she couldn't afford. Nobles wore other people's griefs like jewelry. An old lord gestured with a stonemason's hands. A general wept quietly at the edge of a dance with a washerwoman's rocking motion, the sway of someone who has cried over tubs for decades and knows how to do it without stopping work. None of them seemed to notice what they carried. Or worse—they noticed, and had learned to enjoy it. One young noble caught Xanthe watching him and winked, then performed a little sailor's rolling gait across the marble, laughing, showing off the soul he'd been lent like a boy showing off a stolen knife.

*Horror dressed in silk.* That was the phrase that arrived in her mind, unbidden, and stayed.

And beneath it all, the bells went on ringing, patient and sweet, and Xanthe finally understood where they came from. They weren't bells at all. They were voices, hundreds of them, thousands, held so long and rung so gently that they'd become music. The court had tuned its stolen souls until the screaming sounded like song.

* * *

The Chamber of Accounts was smaller than she'd expected, which somehow made it worse. Wealth on this scale should have required cathedrals. Instead: a round room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, a desk of black wood, and an Undersecretary who was mostly desk—a small, precise person folded into robes the color of dust, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that never blinked quite enough.

"Vessa Thornhale." The Undersecretary did not look up from the ledger open on the desk. "Master Vess sends his regards, I trust. And his inventory."

"His regards, and his list." Xanthe laid the forged papers down with a broker's agent's bored precision. Inside her chest, the storm pressed against its cage. *Bored. Be bored.* "Three lots. Minor gifts, as specified. A weather-sense, a hearth-keeper's touch, a finder's knack. All surrendered willingly, all documented."

"Willingly." The Undersecretary said the word the way a priest says amen. A finger traced down the page. "The court prefers willing stock. It keeps better." Those not-quite-blinking eyes rose at last and fixed on Xanthe, and something in them weighed her—not her papers, not her cover, but *her*, the way a butcher weighs a carcass by sight alone. "You've a strong presence yourself, Mistress Thornhale. Weather-work, isn't it? Faint. Suppressed. Very disciplined." A thin smile. "You'd fetch well, if you ever grew tired of brokering."

"I'm paid to appraise, not to be appraised."

"Mm." The Undersecretary returned to the ledger. "Your lots will be verified against the manifest. Standard procedure. You'll wait in the Antechamber of Petitioners. Refreshments will be brought." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Do not wander. The court is hospitable, but the mountain is not."

They were escorted out by a guard whose footsteps made no sound at all.

* * *

In the corridor outside the Chamber, Ash exhaled for the first time in what might have been an hour. "You were good," he murmured. "The boredom. Very convincing."

"I wasn't acting."

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't suppose you were."

Pell had gone the color of tallow. "Did you hear it? In the Undersecretary's voice? When it said *keeps better*—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "There was a man in that voice. An accent. Northern. My father talked like—I catalogued souls for six years, Xanthe. Six years of entries. *One unit, willing. Two units, forfeit. Three units, purchased.* I thought I was doing accounting." Her breath hitched. "I was doing *inventory*. Of *people*."

"You didn't know," Ash said.

"I signed the ledgers." Pell stared down the corridor, toward the distant glow of the great gallery, where the bells sang their patient song. "I want to see the vault. I need to see what I was counting."

"That's not tonight," Corvin rumbled. He had appeared from the shadow of a pillar with the silence of a man who'd spent thirty years learning to be unnoticed, and his hand rested on Xanthe's shoulder, heavy as an anchor. "Tonight you're an agent waiting on verification. Tonight you learn the halls. The vault comes when the moon does." His jaw worked. "My boy would be twenty-six this winter," he added, so quietly it was almost not speech at all. "If they kept him, he's in those bells. So we do this right. We do this *once*."

* * *

The Antechamber of Petitioners was a long hall hung with tapestries depicting the covenant: crowned figures kneeling before a great dark root, offering up small glowing lights in cupped hands, and the root—generous, paternal—showering down rain and grain and long life. Woven in gold thread, the Thorn looked almost kind. Almost a god. Xanthe studied the tapestries the way she'd once studied weather, reading pressure and pattern, and what she read made her skin crawl. In every panel, the offered lights grew fewer. In every panel, the crown knelt lower. By the final tapestry, the king was prostrate, and the root filled half the sky.

"They think it's worship," Ash said beside her. "Look at the faces of the petitioners. Gratitude."

"People are grateful to the thing that eats them, if it eats them slowly enough."

"That's rather the whole art of power, isn't it?" He smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. Since the vault plans had been drawn, Ash had been watching her too closely again—that first-mate attention, weighing, measuring—and Xanthe had told herself it was protectiveness. Standing in the antechamber of a court that ate souls, she let herself wonder if it was something else entirely, some price being calculated behind his charming face, and hated herself for wondering, and wondered anyway.

Servants brought refreshments—wine dark as garnets, bread that smelled of honey—and none of the crew touched any of it. Time crawled. Through a colonnade at the hall's far end, Xanthe could see into a greater space beyond, and gradually, as the petitions of the evening proceeded, she understood that the throne room lay open to approved eyes, and that approved eyes were meant to be awed.

She was meant to be awed. She made herself go and look.

* * *

The throne room of the court beneath the mountain was lit by a thousand hanging lanterns, and every lantern was a soul.

That was the thing no tapestry showed. Lights floated in the high air of the vaulted chamber like a skyful of slow stars, each one pulsing gently, gently, in rhythms almost like breathing. Some burned white-gold. Some flickered blue. One near the ceiling pulsed a soft sea-green that Xanthe's body recognized before her mind did—storm-light, gift-light, the color of her own suppressed magic—and she had to grip the cold rail of the viewing gallery until the stone bit her palms.

And below them, grown up through the floor of the chamber, through the dais, through the seat of the throne itself, was the Thorn.

It was black. Not the black of absence but the black of depth—oiled, gleaming, shot through with veins of faint violet light that pulsed in time with the lanterns above, drawing them, feeding on them, the way a heart draws blood. Roots thick as temple pillars erupted from the stone floor and braided themselves into a trunk, and the trunk climbed the throne's back and spread across its arms, and from the throne's headrest, branches arched upward and outward like a canopy, like antlers, like a crown. Where root met stone, the rock had not been broken. It had been *grown through*, seamlessly, as if the mountain itself had been hollowed and seeded long ago and had spent centuries quietly becoming furniture.

Courtiers drifted below, making offerings. Xanthe watched a noblewoman kneel at the base of the dais and hold up a small clay vessel, and watched the nearest branch dip—a movement gentle as a heron drinking—and touch the vessel's mouth. Something tiny and bright poured upward out of the clay and into the black wood, and the violet veins flared, and every lantern in the chamber pulsed once, all together, like a congregation inhaling.

And the Thorn *thanked* her. Xanthe felt it even from the gallery, a wash of warmth, of approval, of terrible parental love rolling through the stone and up through her boots and into her bones. For one vertiginous moment she wanted to kneel. Wanted it the way the drowning want water. Every ache she owned—the exile, the hiding, the smothered storm, the sister-shaped hole twenty years wide—every ache leaned toward that warmth like a cold hand toward a fire, and a voice that was not a voice said, in the register of a lullaby: *I could carry all of that for you, little storm. I have room. I have always had room.*

Xanthe bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper, and the warmth receded, satisfied, indulgent, patient as roots.

*It doesn't force anyone,* she thought, shaking. *That's the horror of it. It doesn't have to. It just makes the kneeling feel like rest.*

Beside her, Ash had gone very still. He was staring at the lanterns—not at the throne, not at the courtiers, but at the lights themselves, searching them, one by one, with an expression Xanthe had last seen on Corvin's face in the tunnels. The expression of a man counting stars for one that's missing.

"Ash," she said quietly.

"Hm?"

"What are you looking for?"

He turned, and the charm was back in place, seamless, effortless, and that seamlessness—which she had trusted for weeks—now looked, in this place, like the courtiers' borrowed laughs. Like something worn.

"Just admiring the view," he said. "Come on. The Undersecretary's clerk is waving. Your lots are verified, Agent Thornhale. We're free to go."

*Free.* The word sat strangely in the bell-hung air.

As they turned to leave, Xanthe looked back once at the throne of root and bone, at the thousand patient lanterns, at the violet pulse keeping time like a heartbeat the size of a mountain. Somewhere in that black canopy, or in the vault beneath it, a sea-green light was waiting. Isolde. Twenty years sold. Twenty years worn, perhaps—a laugh here, a way of tilting the head there, some noblewoman on the gallery floor below wearing her sister's smallest gestures like perfume.

The bells rang on, sweet and endless, and Xanthe Fairweather walked out of the throne room with a broker's bored face and a storm screaming silently behind her ribs—

—and stopped dead in the corridor, because a courtier in dove-grey silk was approaching through the gloom, smiling, and greeting her by name.

Not *Thornhale*.

"*Xanthe,*" the courtier said warmly, in a voice she had not heard in twenty years, a voice that used to sing her to sleep. "Little storm. You've grown so tall."
