## 12. A Voice Not Her Own

The bells of the court beneath the mountain did not ring. They *hummed*—a low, patient vibration that lived in the stone and in the teeth of everyone who walked upon it. Xanthe had been in the Court of Quiet Bells for two days now, wearing the borrowed face of an agent for a soul-broker named Vessic Corrane, and she had learned to hate the humming most of all. It got into her dreams. It got into the spaces between her thoughts, filling them like water fills a hull.

*Smile,* she reminded herself, as she crossed the Gallery of Borrowed Voices toward the Hall of Petitions. *You are Corrane's woman. You are here to negotiate rates on three souls from the coastal holdings. You are bored, slightly cold, and mildly offended by the wine.*

She was none of those things. She was a storm in a silk dress, held behind her ribs by twenty years of practice, and every hour she spent in this place made the holding harder.

The courtiers glided past her in their pale silks, and she made herself look at them the way Pell had taught her to look at ledgers: not at what they pretended to be, but at what the numbers said. The numbers said these people were wrong. A young man with a merchant's laugh that belonged to someone older. A woman whose gestures repeated too precisely, like a clockwork figure wound by a careful hand. They wore other people's voices the way she wore this dress—fitted, flattering, and not theirs.

Because the Thorn let them. That was the truth under all the silk. The black root grown through the throne held thousands of souls, and the court drank from them in sips: a mannerism here, a turn of phrase there, a whole personality worn like a coat until it frayed.

Xanthe kept her eyes down and her breathing even, and she told herself she would not hear it. She would not hear Isolde's voice in any of those borrowed mouths. She had prepared for everything except that.

She heard it anyway.

"Excuse me—you've dropped your petition-slip."

The voice came from her left, warm and low and threaded through with a laugh that had always lived just under the words, and Xanthe's whole body went still as a struck bell.

She knew that voice. She had known it before she knew her own name. She had known it across a shared bed in a drafty house in Hollowmere, whispering stories about the sea; she had known it raised in anger once, only once, the night their parents died; she had known it last of all through a door, saying goodbye to a sister who was already half-gone.

Isolde's voice. Coming out of the mouth of a courtier in dove-grey silk—a man perhaps fifty years old, with a face like a folded map, holding out a slip of paper she had indeed dropped without noticing.

"Your slip," he said again, in her sister's voice, and smiled with a mouth that did not know how to smile the way that voice needed it to.

Every instinct Xanthe had screamed at once. Her hands wanted to close around his throat. Her magic wanted to rise up out of her chest like a gull off water and crack the gallery's vaulted stone open to the sky. Twenty years of smothering storms, and she had never once wanted to let one go so badly.

*It isn't her.* She gripped the thought like a rail in high seas. *It is a fragment. A sip. The Thorn letting its courtiers wear pieces of the dead.*

But knowing a thing and surviving it were different countries, and she stood on the border between them with her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

"Thank you," she managed. Her own voice sounded foreign in her ears—flat, careful, Corrane's-woman flat. She took the slip. Their fingers did not touch. She made sure of it.

"You seem troubled," the courtier said, tilting his head with a small, curious grace that was nothing like Isolde and everything like a thing studying how humans tilted their heads. "Forgive me. I only meant—the mountain makes strangers melancholy. It does that. All that weight above us." He paused, and then, gently, in her sister's voice, in her sister's cadence, with her sister's old habit of dropping the end of a sentence into softness: "Do you have family, mistress...?"

He left the name hanging. An invitation. A hook dressed as courtesy.

*Xanthe Fairweather,* said the part of her that was still a girl standing outside a burning room. *Say it. Say my name and see if she's in there somewhere, if any piece of her knows me, if—*

"No family," she said. "I'm Corrane's agent. Nothing else."

"A pity," said the courtier, in Isolde's voice, and something in his borrowed face flickered—not sympathy, not quite. Appetite, maybe. Or the memory of appetite. "The court believes no one should be without family. We are very generous that way. If you ever find yourself missing someone..." He produced, from his sleeve, a small card of grey vellum, blank except for a sigil pressed in wax: a thorn coiled like a sleeping serpent. "...the court keeps excellent records. Names, dates, debts. Even old ones. Even ones sold long ago, by people who loved you very much."

The gallery hummed. Somewhere above them, impossibly far above, the moon was climbing toward its thirteenth turn, and Xanthe felt it in her chest like a second heartbeat.

"They sold willingly," the courtier added, softly, kindly, in her sister's voice. "That's what no one understands. No one is taken here. Everything beneath this mountain was given."

*Given.* The word landed like a slap. Because she had read the ledger entry in Pell's careful transcription—*Fairweather, Isolde. Purpose: the Thorn. Consideration rendered: exile preserved, execution commuted*—and because some locked room in her own mind had rattled at those words, had tried to open, and she had not let it.

"I'll keep that in mind," Xanthe said, and walked away with her spine straight and her hands shaking inside her sleeves.

She did not drop the slip again. She held it so tightly it cut a line into her palm, and she focused on that small bright pain all the way down the gallery, because it was the only clean thing in her body.

* * *

They had a signal for *get me out*, Ash had told her aboard the Drowned Lantern, tapping the stem of his pipe against the rail. Left hand to the collarbone, twice. *Don't use it unless you mean it. Serel's people watch the galleries like hawks over a henhouse, and if you run, you'd better be ready to tell me why.*

Xanthe found an alcove between a fountain of black glass and a tapestry depicting the coronation of some king whose crown was woven through with roots, and she gave the signal to the nearest servant—an empty-eyed boy carrying wine—and then she stood in the alcove and shook.

Not visibly. Visibly she was composed, a broker's agent waiting on her principal, examining the tapestry with professional indifference. Inside, she was twenty-two years old again, standing in the rain outside a magistrate's office in Hollowmere while a clerk read out the terms of her exile, and Isolde was nowhere in sight, and no one would tell her why, and the storm in her chest was so quiet after that day. So terribly quiet. She had thought grief had burned it out of her. Now she wondered if something else had reached into her and turned it down, like a lamp trimmed low to make oil last.

*Even ones sold long ago, by people who loved you very much.*

What had Isolde given? What had Xanthe paid without knowing? The ledger named the consideration for Isolde's soul—exile instead of execution—but the ledger also recorded other transactions, Pell had said. Debts of memory. The court bought memories the way it bought everything else, and somewhere in the vault below hung not only souls but the missing hours of Xanthe Fairweather's life, filed and shelved like jars of preserves.

A burning room. Her sister's face. A signature on paper. The fragments surfaced when the stone hummed, unbidden, half-formed, and each time they slipped away before she could hold them, like fish through fingers.

"There you are."

Ash's voice, easy and warm, arriving at her shoulder like he had simply been looking for her, which was exactly what he was supposed to be doing. In the two days since they'd entered the court, Ash had become someone else entirely—or rather, he had become effortlessly, seamlessly, whoever the court expected him to be. A trader in relics. A man with money and no history. He wore a courtier's smile better than the courtiers did, and Xanthe had begun to understand that this was not a skill he had learned for this mission. It was a skill he had learned long ago, for reasons he hadn't yet told her, and the not-telling sat between them like a stone in a shoe.

"The wine here is criminal," Ash said loudly, for the benefit of anyone listening, and then, quieter, close to her ear: "Talk. Now. What happened?"

"That courtier," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The one in dove-grey, by the third fountain. He spoke to me."

"Half the court speaks to you. You're memorable."

"In *her* voice, Ash."

His face didn't change. That was the first terrible thing—that it didn't change at all, that whatever he felt moved behind his eyes like weather behind glass. "Serel's work?"

"Or the Thorn's. Does it matter? They let them wear—" Her breath caught. She mastered it. "They let them wear pieces of the dead. My dead. They know, Ash. Somehow they know who I am, or they're fishing for it, and either way they handed me my sister's voice like a gift and watched to see what I'd do."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing. I was Corrane's woman. Bored, cold, offended by the wine."

"Good," Ash said. And then, softer, with something underneath the smoothness that almost sounded like pain: "Gods, Xanthe. Good."

"It nearly wasn't." She looked at him straight on. "If he says one more word in her voice—if they put her in front of me, actually in front of me—I don't know that I can hold. You should know that. Before we go further. You should know what you're sailing with."

For a moment the mask thinned, and she saw the man beneath it: tired, frightened, carrying something heavy in the hold of himself that he never spoke about. His hand rose as if to touch her arm and stopped halfway, folding instead around the stem of a pipe he wasn't smoking.

"I know what I'm sailing with," he said. "I chose it."

Before she could ask what that meant—before the question could even finish forming—the humming of the bells changed pitch.

It was subtle. Most of the court wouldn't notice. But Xanthe had spent two days learning this place the way a sailor learns a sea, and she felt the shift the way you feel a wind turning: the hum deepened, gathered, acquired a note of attention. Down the gallery, conversations paused mid-word. Courtiers turned their heads in unison, all together, like a field of flowers following the sun.

And through the parted crowd came the Inquisitor.

Serel moved the way winter moves—without hurry, without malice, arriving regardless. Her pale eyes went across the gallery and found Xanthe in the alcove as easily as a lighthouse finds a boat, and she smiled the small courteous smile of a woman who has never once needed to raise her voice.

"Mistress Corrane's agent," Serel said. "How fortunate. I hoped to find you."

* * *

Later, Xanthe would try to reconstruct those minutes and find gaps in them, holes where fear had eaten the record. She remembered the quality of the light—cold, sourceless, rising out of the stone itself. She remembered the tapestry king's root-crown seeming to follow her with its stitched eyes. She remembered Serel stopping an arm's length away, close enough that Xanthe could smell her: ink and cold iron and, faintly, inexplicably, rain.

"You dropped something," Serel said.

"I don't think—"

"Not the slip." Serel opened her hand. On her pale palm lay a knot of braided cord, sea-grey and salt-stiffened, worked in a pattern Xanthe knew the way she knew her own heartbeat. A luck-knot. Her pattern. Her knots—the ones she sold in Greyharbor for coppers, the ones she'd taught no one living.

Except one person. One person alive had learned that knotting from her hands, twenty years ago, in a kitchen in Hollowmere, laughing at her own clumsy fingers.

"This was found near the third fountain," Serel said. "After your conversation with Master Ilvane. Perhaps you dropped it earlier in the day. Perhaps not." Her pale eyes were terribly kind. "It's fine work. Old-fashioned. The kind of knotting they do in the mountain towns, in Hollowmere especially. You'd know the style, I imagine, being from the coast."

"I buy charms wholesale," Xanthe said. "Corrane deals in souls, Lady Inquisitor, not luck-knots. Ask him."

"Oh, I intend to ask a great many questions." Serel closed her hand around the knot, and Xanthe felt the loss of it like a physical thing, like watching someone pocket a piece of her sister. "But first—the court wishes to thank you. Your principal's business is of interest to us. Three souls from the coastal holdings, yes? The Thorn is hungry this season. The moon turns soon." She said it lightly, the way one mentions weather. "There will be a reception. You and your associate will attend. Wear something warm; the throne room is colder than people expect."

"We're honored," Ash said smoothly, stepping into the space beside Xanthe as if he'd been there all along. "Though I should say—my colleague has been unwell. The mountain air. If she seems pale at the reception, I hope the court won't read anything into it."

"Pale?" Serel's gaze slid to Xanthe, lingered, and Xanthe understood suddenly, completely, that Serel knew. Had probably known from the moment they entered the court. Was playing with them the way a cat plays with something it intends to eat eventually, unhurried, because the moon had not finished turning and there was time. "She looks well to me. Strong, even. There's a particular vitality to the gifted, you know. I can always tell." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Can't you, Mistress Corrane's-agent?"

The silence stretched. Xanthe felt her magic stir against its chains, a slow roll of thunder far out at sea, and she bore down on it with everything she had.

"She's joking," Ash said, and laughed—bright, easy, a laugh that filled the alcove and broke the tension like sunlight breaking cloud. "Court humor. Terrible stuff. Come along, my dear, we've kept Lady Serel long enough, and I saw the steward with the good brandy—" He steered her out of the alcove with a hand at her elbow, bowing to Serel as they passed, chattering about vintages and import duties, and it was flawless, it was perfect, it was the performance of a lifetime.

It cost him. Xanthe saw it as they walked away: the small hitch in his breath, the sweat at his temple despite the cold, the way his hand trembled once against her elbow before he steadied it. Whatever game Ash was playing with the court, whatever price he had quietly arranged to pay, Serel's attention was a debt coming due, and he had just stepped in front of it for her.

At the gallery's end, he murmured, barely a breath: "Don't ask me what that costs. Not yet. Please."

Then the dove-grey courtier—Ilvane—was somehow ahead of them again, materializing out of the crowd with his folded-map face and his borrowed warmth, and he inclined his head to Ash politely before turning back to Xanthe.

"One more thing, mistress," he said, in Isolde's voice, gentle as a hand smoothing hair from a fevered brow. "The lady asked whether you have family. I thought you might want to correct your answer. Because we checked the records, you see—we check everything, it's rather our passion—and there *is* a Fairweather in our books. Sold twenty years ago. Lovely knot-work, they say. She used to sign her charms with a little double loop, here, at the end—" his fingers sketched it in the air, precisely, perfectly, the mark Xanthe had tied into ten thousand luck-knots and seen in her dreams, "—and she asked about you, at the end. Right up until the last breath. She asked whether the storm was safe."

The gallery hummed. The moon turned, somewhere far above, another degree toward full.

Xanthe's control cracked—not outwardly, not where Serel's pale eyes could see, but somewhere deep in the sealed room of her chest, a seam split with a sound only she could hear, and the storm behind it pressed its face against the gap and *waited*.

"Yes," she heard herself say, in a voice gone quiet and strange. "The storm is safe."

Ilvane smiled with Isolde's warmth and none of Isolde's love, and bowed, and dissolved into the court like sugar into dark water.

Beside her, Ash said nothing at all. But his hand found hers in the shadow between their sleeves and held on—tight, trembling, an anchor thrown to a drowning woman by another drowning woman—as the bells beneath the mountain began, very softly, to sing her sister's name.
