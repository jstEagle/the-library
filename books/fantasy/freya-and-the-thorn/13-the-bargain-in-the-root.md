## 13. The Bargain in the Root

Serel had learned, in her twenty years beneath the mountain, that the most dangerous lies were the ones told kindly.

She stood at the edge of the guest chamber's threshold and watched Xanthe Fairweather sleep—or pretend to sleep, which amounted to the same thing for Serel's purposes. The woman lay curled on a narrow bed of grey silk, her back to the door, her breathing arranged into the careful rhythm of someone who wanted to be left alone. Her hands were what betrayed her. They were clenched in the coverlet like a drowning woman's fists, knuckles white as river stones.

*You are afraid,* Serel thought, not unkindly. *Good. Fear is honest. It is the only thing down here that still is.*

The chamber had been prepared with care. Not a cell—Serel had insisted on that, over the murmured objections of the Chamberlain, whose borrowed voice always sounded faintly surprised by its own consonants. A guest room, then: warm lamplight, a ewer of water sweetened with mountain honey, a window of thick glass looking out onto nothing at all but polished stone and the slow amber glow of the deep veins. Kindness, arranged like flowers on a table. Every kindness in this court was arranged; nothing grew wild here except the Thorn, and even it was pruned.

Serel let the door whisper shut behind her and crossed the room without hurry. Twenty years had taught her patience the way other people learned prayers—by repetition, until it lived in the body without needing the mind.

"Your friend Ash is being fed," she said quietly. "In case you were wondering. Actual food. He asked about you twice."

Xanthe's breathing did not change. But her shoulders drew up a fraction, tight as bowstrings, and Serel smiled at the small honesty of it.

"You can keep pretending," she continued, drawing the room's single chair away from the wall and sitting down where Xanthe could see her if she chose to open her eyes. "Most people do, at first. Pretending is a skill, and you've clearly practiced. That merchant's smile you wear—I watched you use it on my patrol in Vellin Ford. You smiled at Corpora Dace like she was a customer buying luck-knots, and she never once looked at your hands." She paused. "Storm-callers always have restless hands. Did you know that? The gift sits in the palms like water in a cup. You hide it well. But I was a weather-witch once myself, before I gave it up, so I know where to look."

That did it. Xanthe turned over—not all the way, just enough to show one eye, sharp and grey-green as the sea before weather, fixed on Serel with the flat wariness of an animal deciding whether the hand reaching toward it held food or a snare.

"You were gifted," Xanthe said. Her voice was rough with disuse, or with the effort of not shouting. "The Saltwife said the Inquisitors hunt their own kind first."

"Maren talks too much for a woman who smuggles people for a living." Serel folded her hands in her lap. She wore no gloves; she had stopped wearing them years ago, after she understood that hiding her own scarred palms was a form of lying she could no longer afford. The scars were thin white lines across both hands, old burns from lightning she had once called down herself, back when the sky above Hollowmere answered her name. "Yes. I was gifted. Rain, mostly. Small storms. I could make a dry harvest or drown a rival's field, and for a while I thought that made me important." She laughed softly, without much humor in it. "It made me useful. There is a difference, and the court taught it to me very thoroughly."

"And now you hunt us instead."

"Now I *find* you," Serel corrected gently. "There is a difference there, too, if you'll let me show it to you. What happens after I find you—" she spread her scarred hands, "—that depends entirely on what you choose. And choice, Xanthe Fairweather, is the one thing this court has never taken from anyone. Remember that. It matters more than anything else I'm going to say tonight."

The lamplight burned low and gold. Somewhere far above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of stone, the world went on turning toward the thirteenth moon, and Serel felt it the way she felt weather in her old life: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat. Three weeks. Perhaps less. The Thorn was waking faster than the almanacs promised, drinking deeper, and the court's quiet bells had begun to ring at hours when no one had ordered them rung.

She spoke, and she made her voice gentle, because gentleness was the only language the frightened ever truly heard.

"Do you know why the crown hunts the gifted? Truly?" She didn't wait for an answer. "They tell the criers it's for safety. Uncontrolled magic, fires, floods, children hurt. Some of it is even true—I saw what an untrained storm-caller can do, once. I've seen it twice." She watched Xanthe flinch, and noted it, and stored it away. "But safety is the story they sell the villages. The truth is simpler and older. The throne needs feeding."

"The Thorn," Xanthe said.

"The Thorn." Serel nodded toward the ceiling, toward the deep places beyond it. "You've seen it. Don't lie to me—you went pale in the audience gallery when the Chamberlain led you past the throne. Everyone does, the first time. It looks like a root, because that's what it is. Older than the kingdom. Older than the mountain, some say; the miners found it growing *through* the rock, as if the mountain grew around it rather than the other way round. Black wood, silver sap. And it grants—" she counted on her fingers, an old clerk's habit she'd never lost, "—three things. Weather, first. The realm has not known famine-storm or killing drought in two hundred years, not one season out of joint, because the throne holds the sky by the throat. Harvest, second—the same bargain, rooted in soil instead of cloud. And third—"

"Memory," Xanthe whispered.

"Memory." Serel let the word sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "The rarest gift, and the cruelest. The Thorn can take a memory out of a living head as cleanly as you'd lift a splinter. And it can give them back. Whole. Perfect. You could live inside a returned memory and never know you'd ever been without it." She leaned forward. "And what does it ask in payment? Only souls. Only the surrendered ones. No one is dragged to the vault, whatever the border ballads say. The covenant is precise—it cannot take what is not freely given. Every soul in that vault below us was sold, willed, or traded by someone who loved them or hated them or needed bread. The court simply keeps the ledger."

"That's monstrous."

"It's *honest*," Serel said, and heard the edge come into her voice before she could soften it. "Forgive me. But I have stood in villages where mothers wept over daughters the hunters took, and I have also stood in those same villages in the years before, when the blight came and the wells went sour and children died of things a charm-seller's knot couldn't touch. The realm eats well, Xanthe. It has eaten well for two hundred years. You have eaten from that table your whole life. We all have. The only question the Thorn ever asks anyone is whether they're willing to know what's on the menu."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Xanthe had sat up now, drawn into herself, her arms wrapped around her knees, and Serel saw the argument happening behind her eyes—the argument between horror and hunger, between what she'd been taught and what she'd seen. Serel knew every turn of that argument. She had run it against herself for ten years before she surrendered her gift, and she could recite both sides from memory like a catechism.

"I don't believe you," Xanthe said at last, but her voice shook, and they both knew it was a lie, and Serel mercifully did not say so.

"You will. Or you won't, and you'll die believing something cleaner, and the realm will go on eating either way." She rose, crossed to the ewer, poured honeyed water into a glass, and set it on the table within Xanthe's reach—an offering, small and deliberate. Then she sat again, and lowered her voice, and came to the true business of the night, the reason she had requested this audience alone, without the Chamberlain, without the bells.

"But none of that is why I'm here," she said. "I'm here because I can give you something no one else in this court can. Something you've wanted since the moment you woke up under this mountain and felt the hole in your head where a piece of your life used to be."

Xanthe went very still.

"Your memories," Serel said. "The ones you sold. Yes—we know you sold them. The ledger records every transaction, and yours is written in a clerk's hand twenty years old, witnessed and sealed. Two entries. One large, one small. The small one is yours." She paused, letting the words find their depth. "A memory of a night in Greyharbor. A burning room. Your sister's face. And a signature on paper."

"Stop." The word cracked out of Xanthe like a whipcrack, and for one heartbeat the lamps guttered, every flame in the room leaning sideways as though a wind had passed through solid stone—and Serel felt the answering pressure in her own scarred palms, the old sympathy, the sky's ghost remembering her name. She kept her voice level. She had faced storms before. This was only another kind.

"You felt that," Xanthe breathed. "You felt me."

"I told you. I was a rain-witch." Serel flexed her fingers. "The gift doesn't leave people like us, Xanthe. It goes dormant. It waits. Sometimes, down here, close to the root, it stirs." She let that settle, then pressed on, soft as snowfall. "Here is my offer. Not the court's offer—mine, though the court will honor it. Swear yourself to the covenant. Give your storm willingly, as I gave mine, and take a place among us. Power, if you want it—I began as an Inquisitor's clerk and I ended running the hunt, because the gifted trust their own, and the court knows it. Safety, certainly; no hunter's hand will ever touch you again, and the world above is about to become a very dangerous place for people like you when the moon turns. And the memory." She met Xanthe's eyes and did not look away. "Yours, returned whole. The night your sister died—or didn't die, which is the question eating you alive, isn't it? You want to know what you forgot. You want it more than you want air. I watched you try to dig for it in the tunnels until Corvin pulled you off the wall like a woman clawing at a grave."

Xanthe's face had gone the color of ash. "Why would you give it to me? If the memory is terrible—if it would break me—"

"Because broken things can be rebuilt honestly," Serel said. "And because I took this road myself, twenty years ago, and I remember standing exactly where you're sitting, hating everything in this room, certain I would never become what I became." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and human, and that was the most dangerous thing about it. "The Thorn is not a monster, Xanthe. Monsters chase you. The Thorn *waits*. It lets the world grow hard around you until surrender looks like rest. I am not here to threaten you. I am here because in three weeks, when the moon completes its turn, everyone in this mountain is going to have to decide what they serve—and I would rather you decided knowing the whole truth than half of it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse, and I will grieve it, and the court will do what courts do." Serel shrugged, lightly, terribly. "But you should know one more thing before you answer. The soul-vault holds eleven thousand four hundred and six lights. One of them is registered under the name Isolde Fairweather. Sold, twenty years ago, by a seller recorded only as *kin*." She watched the blow land, watched it land exactly where she'd aimed it, and hated herself with the old familiar weariness even as she did the work. "When the Thorn wakes fully, every light in that vault goes into the root. Forever. Unless someone on the inside—" she tapped her own chest, once, "—chooses otherwise. Think about what a place at this court would be worth to a woman who wants her sister's soul back. Think about what access means. Think about how many locks a key opens, versus how many a storm breaks."

She rose. Smoothed her grey coat. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around, because some truths were easier said to a back.

"For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much coming from me—I read your sister's entry once, years ago, when I still read the ledger for grief instead of duty. The purpose field wasn't blank, the way most are. Someone wrote a line in it. Four words." She opened the door. The amber glow of the deep veins spilled in across the floor, across Xanthe's bare feet. "*For my sister's life.*"

Behind her, she heard the sound Xanthe made—a small, ruined noise, half breath, half wound—and the lamps flared high and gold, all of them at once, the storm in the woman's chest pressing up against the walls of her like floodwater against a door.

Serel stepped through and closed the door softly behind her, and stood a moment in the corridor with her scarred hands pressed flat against the cold stone, feeling the Thorn's slow pulse rise up through the mountain to meet her, patient as roots, patient as winter.

Three weeks. The bells would ring soon. And somewhere below, in the dark beside the throne, the root was already dreaming her name.
