## 14. What Xanthe Forgot

They did not chain her.

That was the first cruelty of it, Xanthe thought as she followed Serel down the corridor of pale stone—no irons, no guards, no door locked behind her. The Inquisitor simply walked ahead with her lantern held low and her pale eyes forward, trusting that Xanthe would follow, because where else was there to go? The court beneath the mountain had no walls because it had never needed them. It had doors, and doors opened onto more of itself, and every corridor curved gently back toward the throne room like water finding its level.

The galleries here were older than the court proper. The stone was rough-hewn in places, veined with seams of glittering ore that caught the lantern light and threw it back in small, cold stars. Old miners' marks still scored the walls—tally strokes, names scratched by men who had died believing they were digging for silver. Now the tunnels had been dressed in silk. Banners hung from iron hooks driven into rock, deep green and gold, and along the floor ran a carpet so dark it seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it.

Xanthe's magic stirred against her ribs with every step, restless as a bird in a covered cage. The mountain did something to it. It had been doing something since they'd entered the miners' tunnels days ago—a low resonance, a hum beneath her skin, as though her storm recognized this place the way a child recognizes a room it was born in. She hated that. She hated that some part of her felt *at home* here.

"You keep touching your wrist," Serel said without turning. "The knot."

Xanthe looked down. Her fingers had found the frayed luck-knot at her wrist again—the one Wren had carried, the one Isolde's hands had tied twenty years ago. She hadn't noticed she was doing it. She forced her hand flat against her thigh.

"It's nothing."

"Nothing is rarely worn for twenty years." Serel stopped before an unremarkable door of black wood, banded in iron gone soft with age. She set her lantern on a bracket beside it. "In here, Fairweather. And I would ask you to sit. What I'm going to give you goes better sitting down."

"What you're going to give me," Xanthe repeated. "You make it sound like medicine."

"Most truths are." Serel opened the door. "Bitter, but measured. A dose at a time. The court learned that lesson long ago, at great cost." Something moved behind her face then—something old and tired that didn't belong on a hunter's face—and was gone before Xanthe could name it. "Sit. Please."

*Please.* The word sat strangely in the cold air. Xanthe stepped past her into the room.

It was small and round, carved directly from the mountain's heart, and empty except for two chairs facing each other across a low table. On the table sat a single object: a shallow bowl of black glass, filled with what looked like water but wasn't. The surface didn't move when the draft from the door touched it. It simply waited, reflecting the ceiling, reflecting nothing.

Xanthe knew vessels like this. Every charm-seller knew them, or knew their cheap cousins—the scrying bowls sold to gulls who wanted to see lost lovers or buried coin. But those were toys. This was the real thing, and the real thing was not for seeing. It was for *holding*. Memory could be poured out of a person like wine from a cask. She had always known that the way you know fire is hot—abstractly, uselessly, until you are burned.

Her hand went to her wrist again. She let it stay this time.

"The ledger says four memories," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Sold under my name. Four entries, Fairweather, F., buyer's purpose withheld. Pell decoded three of them. Dates. Prices. But the fourth—" She stopped.

"The fourth is the largest," Serel said. She took the other chair, unhurried, folding her long frame into it with the ease of someone who had sat in many uncomfortable rooms waiting for many frightened people. "And the first. They are often the same memory, in cases like yours. The wound everything else was cut around." She gestured at the bowl. "What I am offering tonight is a taste. One memory, returned whole, for one hour. You will feel it as if it were happening now, because for your mind, it will be. At the end of the hour, the court takes it back—unless you choose to stay, and serve, and have all four returned permanently. That bargain stands. It has not changed."

"And if I refuse the taste?"

Serel almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile—it made her look young, and human, and very far from home. "Then you'll walk out of here still carrying the question, and questions weigh more than answers, Fairweather. I've carried both. Trust me on the arithmetic."

Xanthe looked at the bowl. The black surface looked back.

Twenty years ago, she had woken in a cart heading south, alone, with no memory of how she'd come to be in it, and a paper in her pocket bearing a seal she didn't recognize and a single line: *By order of the Crown, the gifted woman Xanthe Fairweather is remanded beyond the borders, never to return.* No crime named. No sentence explained. Just exile, clean and total, like a door closed while she slept.

She had spent twenty years building a life in the gap of that missing night. A merchant's smile. Luck-knots and weather charms. A careful, careful existence with the storm folded up small inside her chest. And underneath it all, the question had grown like a pearl around grit: *what happened?* What had been so terrible that the crown exiled her instead of executing her—and why couldn't she remember any of it?

"I want to know about my sister," Xanthe said quietly. "Not the court. Not the Thorn. Isolde. If this memory has her in it, pour it. If it doesn't, we're done here."

Something flickered across Serel's face—pity, maybe, or its close cousin, recognition. "It has her in it," she said. "From beginning to end. I'm sorry. I say that honestly. Sit down."

Xanthe sat.

Serel leaned forward and placed two fingers against the rim of the black glass bowl. Where she touched it, the surface bloomed with faint silver light, like moonrise on a windless sea. "This will feel like drowning for a moment," she said. "Don't fight it. Fighting makes it worse. Breathe when you can."

She lifted her fingers, and the light rose off the bowl like smoke given a mind, and slid across the table, and entered Xanthe through her open eyes—

* * *

Rain.

Rain on a burning roof, hissing where it struck the flames, filling the world with steam and smoke and the smell of wet ash. Xanthe was fourteen years old and standing barefoot in the dooryard of the house she had lived in all her life, and the rain was hers. She knew that the way you know your own heartbeat. The sky was hers. The storm had come up out of her chest like something escaping a cage, and now it stood over the house with all its teeth out, and she could not call it back. She had never been able to call anything back. That was the whole shape of her life: things left her hands too fast, too hard, and broke.

"Mama!" Her own voice, cracked with screaming. "Papa!"

The house groaned. Through the kitchen window she could see the table where they'd eaten supper an hour ago—an hour, only an hour, there had been bread and salt and her father laughing at something Isolde said—and now the window was a mouth of orange light, and the roof beams were glowing like coals in a forge.

Isolde's arms came around her from behind, dragging her backward, away from the heat. "Don't look. Xanthe, don't look—"

"They're in there—" Sixteen years old, Isolde, stronger than Xanthe had ever been, hauling her sister bodily across the mud. "We have to get them out, Iso, we have to—"

"There's no getting them out." Isolde's voice broke on it, and she said it again, harder, the way you drive a stake into ground: "There's no getting them out. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

Xanthe looked. Her sister's face was streaked with soot and rain, and her eyes—her steady, brown, unbearably kind eyes—were already somewhere else. Already working. Xanthe would understand that later, much later, standing in a room of black glass: that even in this moment, holding her burning family behind her, Isolde was already calculating. Already bargaining in her head with powers that had no interest in mercy.

"Horses are coming," Isolde said. "Do you hear them? The crown's wardens. Someone sent a beacon-signal—they'll take you, Xanthe. Unregistered gift, destruction of property, and if anyone died in that house—" She swallowed. "They'll hang you. Or worse. Do you hear me?"

"I didn't mean—I couldn't stop it, I *couldn't*—"

"I know." Isolde cupped her sister's face in both hands, and her palms were shaking, and her voice was not. "I know you couldn't. Listen to me now, because we don't have long. When they come, you let me talk. Whatever I say, whatever I agree to—you don't argue. You don't cry out. You get on that cart and you go south and you live. Swear it."

"I'm not leaving you—"

"*Swear it.*"

Hoofbeats through the rain. Lantern-light swinging up the lane. Isolde pressed her forehead to Xanthe's, one breath, two, and whispered something so fast and so fierce that the memory blurred it—Xanthe would spend the whole hour straining toward those words and never catch them—and then the wardens were there, six of them in oilskin cloaks, and a seventh figure in grey who dismounted slowly and looked at the burning house and then at the two girls in the mud with eyes like chips of winter glass.

"A storm-caller," the grey figure said. Not a question. "Uncontrolled. This is a hanging matter, girl. Both of you, likely—concealment of a gifted sibling carries the same weight."

"She's not gifted," Isolde said. "I am."

The words dropped into the rain like stones into a well.

"My sister has no gift," Isolde went on, and her voice was calm, was *level*, was the voice of someone reciting a lesson learned by heart in advance, because of course it was, of course she had rehearsed this in the space between the first flame and the hoofbeats, because that was who Isolde was, that was who she had always been. "The storm was mine. I lost control of it. My sister tried to pull me clear and couldn't. Punish me. Take me. She's innocent, and I'll prove it—in writing, under seal, however you require."

The grey figure studied them both for a long, long moment. Rain ran off its hood.

"The court doesn't take executions lightly anymore," it said at last. "Wasteful. There are... other arrangements available. For the right consideration." Its gaze moved to Xanthe, and Xanthe felt it like a hand pressing on a bruise she hadn't known she had. "Exile, remanded beyond the border, in perpetuity. In exchange—"

"In exchange for payment," Isolde said. "Yes. I know what you buy. I'll pay it."

"You don't know what you're offering, girl."

"I know exactly what I'm offering." Isolde turned and looked at her sister one last time, and smiled—that smile, the crooked one, the one that meant *it's all right, I've got you*—and said, "Take care of her. That's part of the price too. She goes somewhere the sun comes through, and no one hunts her, and she gets to grow old. All of that. Or no bargain."

"All of that," the grey figure agreed.

And Isolde laughed—a small, broken, real laugh, there in the rain, with their parents' house collapsing behind her—and pulled Xanthe into her arms and held her so tightly that Xanthe could feel her sister's heart hammering against her own, the only thing in the world not yet destroyed, and Isolde said into her hair:

"This isn't goodbye. It's just a long errand. You go be safe, little storm. I'll find you on the other side of it. I promise. I *promise*—"

* * *

Xanthe came back to herself gasping, doubled forward in the chair, hands braced on her knees, the taste of smoke still in her throat and her cheeks soaked through. For a moment she didn't know which rain was real—the remembered one or the tears—and then she did, and it was somehow worse.

The hour was not yet half spent. The memory held her anyway, replaying itself in fragments behind her eyes: the bread and salt on the table. The tally of everything the storm had taken in under sixty seconds. And Isolde's arms. Isolde's voice. *A long errand.*

She had believed, for twenty years, that she had been stolen from her sister. That some nameless cruelty of the crown had torn them apart in the night. She had built her whole exile on that foundation—every careful charm, every smothered storm, every year of hiding had been load-bearing brick on top of the belief that she was the victim of a theft.

There had been no thief.

There had been a sister, standing in the rain, selling the only thing she had.

"No," Xanthe said aloud. Her voice sounded scraped raw. "No, that's not—she wouldn't. She wouldn't give them *that*. Not her soul, not to this place, you lie, this is a trick, this is the Thorn wearing her face again—"

"The entry is dated three days after the fire," Serel said quietly. She had not moved from her chair. She watched Xanthe the way one watches a storm cell on the horizon—with respect, and readiness, and no fear at all. "Hollowmere parish record, cross-filed with the court ledger. Seller: Isolde Fairweather, aged sixteen. Consideration received: one exile commuted from execution; one guarantee of passage and protection; one sum paid to rebuild the village granary the storm destroyed." She paused. "Your sister fed half the town that winter, Fairweather. With the price of herself. The court drives a hard bargain, but it honors its contracts. It's the only thing it honors."

Xanthe pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. The storm in her chest was rising—not the wild thing of the memory, but its grief-stricken cousin, pressure building behind her sternum, static crawling along her arms, the lantern flame bending toward her as though leaning in to listen. She wrestled it down the way she had wrestled it down ten thousand times in ten thousand market squares. Smother it. Fold it small. Smile.

But there was no market here. There was no one to hide from except herself.

"Our parents," she whispered. "I killed them."

"You were fourteen," Serel said. "Untrained. Unwarned. Whoever should have taught you to hold your gift chose fear instead of instruction—that's the story of most gifted children, and it's the story the court tells itself when it needs to feel like a rescue rather than a harvest. But no. You didn't kill them. A storm killed them. There's a difference, and the difference matters, though I know it won't feel like it does tonight."

"You don't know what it feels like."

"No," Serel said. "But I know what it costs to carry a night you can't remember and can't put down. I told you I've carried both the question and the answer. Twenty years ago I sat in that chair where you're sitting, and I tasted my own worst night, and I made my choice." Her pale eyes were steady. "That's why I'm telling you plainly, Fairweather, because no one told me plainly: the guilt is real, and it is also a lie. It will tell you that you owe a debt that can only be paid in service. It will tell you that staying here, feeding the Thorn, is justice. It is very reasonable. That's what makes it dangerous. Guilt is the true inheritance your sister left you—but you don't have to *keep* it. An inheritance can be refused."

Xanthe lowered her hands. Her face was wet and she didn't care. "You're trying to recruit me with my own grief."

"I'm trying to save you twenty years," Serel said. "You'll make your choice either way. But hear the rest of the arithmetic first. Your sister's soul hangs in the vault below us. On the thirteenth turn, the Thorn wakes fully, and everything it holds becomes root—sustenance, strength, permanence. Nothing rises. Nothing is released. Ever. Unless—" she tilted her head, "—someone breaks it. And breaking it kills everything inside it. Those are your options, storm-caller. Serve and have her forever in the Thorn's keeping, or break it and lose her truly. There is no third door. I have looked. I have looked for eleven years."

The hour was ending. Xanthe could feel the memory thinning at its edges, the rain of it receding, Isolde's arms dissolving—and she clutched at it, greedy, shameless, hoarding every detail: the crooked smile, the fierce whisper, the laugh in the rain. *A long errand. I'll find you on the other side of it.*

She had kept the promise, Xanthe realized. In her way. Isolde had crossed the whole country in knots and charms and a stranger's dying hands to reach her sister after twenty years. *I'll find you.* She had.

The last of the memory drained away, and the room was only a room, and the bowl was only black glass, and Serel was watching her with something that in a kinder life might have been called kindness.

"So," the Inquisitor said softly. "Now you know what you forgot. The question weighs less. The answer weighs more. That's always how it goes." She rose, took up the lantern, and paused at the door with her hand on the iron latch. "The thirteenth turn is nineteen nights away. My offer stands until then. Think on which debt you'd rather pay—and to whom."

The door closed behind her.

Xanthe sat alone in the round stone room with the dead bowl and the bent lantern-flame, and slowly, deliberately, she untied the frayed knot from her wrist and laid it on the table between her hands. Isolde's knot-work. Tied by those hands, in that life, before the rain.

*A long errand,* her sister had called it. Nineteen nights left on the road.

Xanthe stared at the little knot, and the storm rolled quiet and enormous beneath her ribs, and for the first time in twenty years she was not afraid of it.

She had one debt to refuse, and one soul to steal, and she was going to need a crew to do it—which meant finding out why Ash's signal flare had sealed the vault behind her, before the man she'd trusted became the reason she failed.

She picked up the knot, retied it tight, and went to find Pell.
