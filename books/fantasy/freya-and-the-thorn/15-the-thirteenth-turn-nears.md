## 15. The Thirteenth Turn Nears

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above a shuttered chandlery in the low town of Hollowmere, and it smelled of old fat, wet wool, and fear that had soaked into the beams so long ago it had become part of the architecture. Pell had slept in worse places. She had slept in the court's own clerical dormitories, which were cleaner and colder and smelled of nothing at all, because a place where no one was permitted to weep eventually lost even the smell of grief.

She sat now at a scarred table with the stolen ledger open before her, and she did not read it. She had read it eleven times. She knew its contents the way she once knew the catechism of the Exchequer: *every soul is a debt; every debt is an entry; every entry is a signature.* What she could not stop doing was touching the corner of the page where Isolde Fairweather's name sat in faded iron-gall ink, as if by pressing her thumb there she might feel a pulse.

Three weeks. The moon above Hollowmere was a coin with one bite taken from it, and each night it would grow fuller, rounder, more complete—thirteen turns since the Thorn last woke, and on the thirteenth full turn it would wake entirely, and everything in the vault below the mountain would be spent like coin at a market stall.

"Read it again," Corvin said.

It was not a request. It was never a request with Corvin. He stood at the loft's single window, which looked out over the chandlery's yard and the crooked lane beyond, and he had been standing there for two hours in the manner of a man who had made standing there his entire profession. His hands rested on the sill. They were enormous hands, rope-scarred, steady as cleats, and Pell had watched those hands tie off a wheel in a gale without a tremor. They were not steady now. She pretended not to notice, because that was the courtesy they paid each other.

"I've read it again," Pell said. "Eleven times. The vault is beneath the throne room, accessed through the flooded galleries, sealed by a warden's key, and the entries are indexed by—" she stopped herself, because she had said all of this before too, and saying it again would only make the numbers realer, and the numbers were already too real. "There are four hundred and six names."

Corvin said nothing.

"One of them is a boy named Tammas Corvin," Pell went on, quietly, because someone had to say it aloud eventually and it might as well be her, "entered fourteen years ago. Debtor: the Crown Beneath. Purpose of purchase: *continuance.*"

At the window, Corvin's hands curled slowly into fists against the sill, and the wood creaked.

Pell had known Corvin for six years and had never once heard him speak of his son. She had seen him look at young sailors with an expression he probably believed was neutral. She had seen him give away his share of prize-money twice without explanation. She had seen him stand watches he didn't need to stand, take the coldest hammock, eat last. Grief, in Corvin, was not a wound you could see. It was ballast. It sat deep in the hull of him and kept him upright in weather that would capsize other men, and no one ever asked what it weighed.

"He'd be twenty now," Corvin said, to the window, to the lane, to no one. "If they hadn't taken him. Tall, maybe. His mother was tall." A pause, long enough for the wind to move something in the yard below. "He had a laugh that got him in trouble. Laughed at funerals. Laughed when the reeve fell in the harbor. Couldn't help it. I used to box his ear for it and he'd just laugh harder." His voice did not break. It simply got quieter, the way water gets quieter as it goes deeper. "I signed him over to a court clerk's apprentice for a harvest apprenticeship. Good wages. Good prospects. I thought—" 

He stopped. Pell waited. Waiting was also a courtesy they paid each other.

"I thought the court was the country," he finished. "That's what they want you to think. That the crown and the land are the same thing, so if you serve one you serve both." He turned from the window then, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man who had decided something a long time ago and was only now announcing it. "It isn't. The country is the people in it. The court is a thing that eats them. And I'm done confusing the two."

Pell closed the ledger.

"We'll need the warden's key," she said. "Serel carries it. Which means either we steal it from her or we convince her to use it, and I don't love our odds on either front."

"Xanthe thinks she can reach her."

"Xanthe thinks everyone can be reached. It's why she sells charms." Pell rubbed her eyes. "I was a clerk, Corvin. Eleven years. I know what Serel is. She's not fallen. Falling implies she was standing somewhere better. She's *invested*. People who are invested don't turn. They double down."

"You turned."

The words landed with the particular precision of a man who spoke perhaps forty sentences a day and therefore wasted none of them. Pell looked at him across the lamplit table and felt the old shame rise in her throat, familiar as heartburn.

"I embezzled a conscience," she said. "Different thing. I copied names out of the ledgers and sent warnings to families before the collectors came. When they caught me, I ran. I didn't stay and make the system work for me. There's a difference between a deserter and a convert, and Serel is the second kind, and the second kind are the dangerous ones, because they've already told themselves the story where they're the hero."

Corvin crossed the room and sat across from her, and the chair groaned under him the way honest furniture does. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them, faintly, they could hear Ash laughing at something in the chandlery yard—Ash laughed the way other men breathed—and Xanthe's voice answering, low and warm, teaching knot-work to Wren by lantern-light as though the world were not ending on a schedule.

"They're getting loud down there," Pell said.

"Aye."

"Hunters in the town. Two patrols today. Serel's sigil on the church door."

"Aye."

"If we're still here tomorrow night, we're caught."

"Aye." Corvin leaned forward, and the lamplight found the grey in his beard, the deep lines around eyes that had watched a great deal of sea and sky and had stopped being impressed by either. "So we go tomorrow night instead. Before they close the net. Xanthe's storm won't wait for the moon, and the moon won't wait for us. We move when the tide turns, same as always." He held out one huge hand across the table, palm up. "Give me the ledger."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to read every name in it," Corvin said, "and I'm going to remember them. All four hundred and six. If we get the vault open, those souls go up into the moonlight, and somebody on this crew should know their names when they pass. Somebody should say them." His jaw worked. "Nobody said my boy's name for fourteen years except me. I know what it does to a soul, going unsaid."

Pell's throat tightened. She picked up the ledger—and paused, because there was one more thing, and she had been carrying it for three days like a stone in her shoe.

She reached into her shirt and drew out the chain, and from the chain the seal: a disc of green bronze the size of a sovereign, stamped with the Exchequer's device—a hand holding scales, the scales holding nothing, because the court's justice had never needed weights. Her clerk's seal. Her name was engraved on the reverse. With it she had opened ledgers, sealed writs, authenticated the transfer of human beings from one column to another. Eleven years it had hung against her heart, warm from her skin, and she had told herself she kept it because it was useful, because a forged writ might save them, because sentiment was a luxury.

All lies. She kept it because putting it down meant admitting what she had done with it.

"Corvin," she said. "I need you to witness something."

* * *

They went up to the roof, because some things should be done under open sky. The moon hung over Hollowmere's broken rooftops, nearly half-full now, sharp-edged and white, and the mountain loomed black against the stars to the north with its crown of old mine-works like a skull wearing a rusted circlet. Somewhere up there, under tons of stone, Isolde Fairweather's soul hung in the dark like a lantern nobody had lit.

Pell knelt at the roof's edge, on the cold slates, and set the seal on the lead flashing between her and Corvin.

"Pell of the Exchequer, eleventh register, clerk of the second table," she said formally, because the words had power whether or not the magic did, and she wanted them said properly, in order, out loud, witnessed. "By this seal I authenticated one hundred and twelve transfers of persons. I recorded debts against lives. I wrote the names of children in columns beside sums." Her voice stayed level through sheer professional discipline, and she was grimly proud of it. "I am done being the instrument. Witness me."

"I witness," Corvin said.

She took out her knife, worked the seal loose from its chain, and set the blade's point against the bronze. Then she hesitated—one breath, two—and Corvin put his hand over hers, not guiding, just present, heavy and warm as a mooring line.

"It doesn't unwrite the names," she said. "I know that. Breaking it doesn't free anyone."

"No," Corvin agreed. "But a ship doesn't sail on yesterday's wake."

She struck. The knife drove through the soft bronze and split the seal in half with a crack that sounded obscenely loud in the quiet, and the two halves skittered apart on the lead flashing, the scales broken, the empty hand severed at the wrist. Pell stared at them, breathing hard, feeling—not lighter, exactly. Nothing so clean. More like a splint removed from a bone that had healed crooked: painful, necessary, and leaving her forever changed in shape.

Then Corvin did something he almost never did. He reached out and pulled her against his side, briefly, hard, one-armed, the way you hold onto a crewmate in heavy seas, and let go.

"Family does stupid brave things together," he rumbled. "Come on. We've a heist to plan and a storm-caller to lie to about the odds."

* * *

They gathered below as the moon climbed—the whole strange crew of them crammed into the tanner's loft among hanging coils of wick and bundles of tallow-scraps. Xanthe sat sharpening nothing in particular with a whetstone, her hands moving in the old soothing rhythm. Wren had fallen asleep against her shoulder mid-lesson, the girl's fingers still looped in half-finished knot-work, and Xanthe had not moved for an hour rather than wake her. Ash leaned in the doorway wearing his charm like armor, making jokes with a brightness that fooled no one anymore. Maren stood over a chart of the mountain's mine-works, her salt-grey hair bound back, marking approaches with a stub of charcoal.

And Pell laid out the plan, and Corvin backed her, and somewhere in the middle of arguing about the flooded galleries' third junction—whether the water would be chest-deep or neck-deep, a debate conducted with the passion of scholars—Pell looked around the lamplit room and understood, with a clarity that nearly undid her, what they had become.

Not a crew. Crews were hired. This was something older and stranger. A widow's son, a deserter's guilt, a smuggler's code, a betrayed sister, a stolen child, a liar's charm—all of it lashed together into one hull, taking water, refusing to sink. Every one of them had lost someone to the thing under the mountain, and every one of them had chosen, tonight, in a smelly loft in a dying town, to walk toward it rather than away.

"—and if the galleries are neck-deep," Pell concluded, "we rig the lines from the junction and Corvin goes first, because he's the only one of us who swims like something that enjoys it."

"Noted," Corvin said.

"And if Serel catches us in the tunnels—"

"She won't," Xanthe said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She had gone still, the whetstone motionless in her hand, and her eyes had the faraway shine they got when her gift stirred, like weather gathering behind glass.

"The stone's been talking to me since we came under its shadow," she said. "And tonight it's telling me the mountain is *listening back*. Something down there knows we're coming. Maybe it's always known." She looked up, and her gaze found Corvin, then Pell, and held. "Whatever happens tomorrow night—I need you both to promise me something. If the vault opens and the souls start rising, you don't stop for anything else. Not treasure, not answers, not me. You get them out into the moonlight. Promise."

Corvin promised first, in his way—just a nod, slow and absolute, the nod of a man signing articles.

Pell promised with words, because words were her weapon and she meant to spend them well: "Every name," she said. "I'll read them all out if I have to. Four hundred and six. They'll leave this mountain knowing somebody counted them."

Xanthe smiled then—tired, grateful, afraid—and outside, far to the north, above the black shoulder of the mountain, the clouds began, very softly, to turn.

Below them in the dark of the town, a bell began to ring. Once. Twice. Then a pattern—three and two, three and two—the hunters' call, the sound that meant doors were being knocked upon in the night, that meant the net was drawing closed hours ahead of any plan.

Maren snuffed the lamp with two fingers.

"So much for tomorrow night," she murmured into the sudden darkness. "They're here. Everyone to your places—and pray your storm-caller's ready, because the mountain's come down to meet us early."

In the blackness, Pell felt Corvin's hand find her shoulder—steady, heavy, certain—and heard him breathe one word, calm as a helm in a gale:

"Now."
