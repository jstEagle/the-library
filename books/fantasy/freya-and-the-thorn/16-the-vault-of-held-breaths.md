## 16. The Vault of Held Breaths

The water came up to their knees before they had walked a hundred paces into the mountain, and Xanthe understood that the court did not fear drowning. It had simply never bothered to drain the old miners' galleries when it moved in below them. Why would it? Water kept things quiet. Water kept things honest. A person wading through black floodwater could not sneak, could not run, could not pretend to be anything other than what they were: small, and cold, and afraid.

"Mind the third step," Ash murmured behind her. "There's a gap where the timber's rotted. Corvin marked it with chalk on our way down."

She found the chalk mark by touch, a pale smudge like a ghost's thumbprint, and stepped wide. The water swallowed her boot to the ankle and gave it back colder than it took it. Somewhere ahead, Pell's stolen ledger lay folded against her ribs back at the safehouse, but its map lived now in Xanthe's memory, traced over and over until she could walk it blind: down through the flooded galleries, past the cistern where the miners once cooled their drills, along the vein-road called the Long Throat, and then—up. Always up, at the end. The vault sat above the throne room like a heart above a stomach, because everything the court loved, it kept close to its mouth.

They went by feel and by the thin grey light of Ash's shuttered lantern, which he carried low so that only their boots were lit, two pairs of feet walking on the drowned floor of the world. He had not spoken much since they'd left the others at the tunnel mouth. She had thought it was concentration. She would think about that later, in the years after, how she had mistaken his silence for care.

* * *

The Long Throat was a corridor tall enough to stand in and narrow enough that their shoulders brushed the walls, walls slick with mineral weep, streaked white and rust-red like something bleeding slowly upward. The air changed as they climbed. It grew warmer, and sweeter, and wrong—the smell of beeswax and cut flowers drifting down from somewhere far above, the court's perfume seeping through stone.

"Do you feel that?" Ash whispered.

Xanthe did. Her magic had been restless since they entered the mountain, tugging at her like a dog at a door, but here it pulled differently—not outward toward open sky, but *down*, toward some vast slow thing beneath them. Toward the Thorn. The root ran through the whole mountain; she could feel it now the way you feel a train through a station floor, a deep rhythmic attention, patient and enormous.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"It knew we were coming," Ash said. "The whole country knows. That's rather the point of a country." He smiled at her in the lantern light, that easy crooked smile that had gotten them past three checkpoints and one very suspicious ferryman, and for a moment—just a moment—she let herself lean on it.

They rested at the top of the Long Throat, pressed into an alcove where the miners had once stored blasting oil. Above them, through a grate crusted with lime, they could hear music. Faint, sweet, stringed—and beneath it, if you listened the way Xanthe listened, dozens of voices singing harmony without any words at all. Courtiers. Or what passed for courtiers here.

"The vault is two galleries east," Ash breathed. "Then the stair Serel's key opens. Then—" he tapped his own chest, twice, the crew's signal for *heart of it, the thing itself*.

"And then Isolde," Xanthe said.

Ash looked at her for a long moment. In the shuttered light his face was all shadow and one bright line across the eyes, and she could not read what moved there. Later she would replay this moment a thousand times, hunting for the tell, the flicker, the guilt. She never found one. Whatever he was feeling in that alcove, he had buried it deep enough that even twenty years of hindsight couldn't dig it out.

"Then Isolde," he agreed softly. "Let's go get your sister back."

* * *

The flooded galleries ended and the dry ones began, and the difference was worse. Dry meant maintained. Dry meant the court came here. The corridors widened, floored in fitted stone, lit at intervals by lamps that burned without flame—a soft pearlescent glow that made no shadows, so that everything looked flat and far away, like figures seen through ice.

Xanthe counted doors. Pell's map said eleven between the stair and the vault antechamber. At the seventh door she heard something that stopped her mid-stride: humming. A woman's voice, tuneless and contented, coming from inside.

Ash raised a hand. They froze. The humming continued, wandered, faded—as if the singer had drifted off down some inner corridor—and did not return. Xanthe let out a breath she'd been holding since the sixth door.

"They don't guard it," she whispered. "Why don't they guard it?"

"Because you can't steal from a vault that wants to be opened," Ash said quietly. "Serel told us that herself. The souls aren't prisoners, Xanthe. They're *deposits*. The court doesn't think anyone would be mad enough to make a withdrawal."

"Mad enough," she repeated. "Is that what we are?"

"Ask me again when we're rich." But his smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes this time, and she was too tired, too deep in the mountain, too close to her sister to notice.

The eleventh door was iron, banded in bronze, and set into it at eye height was a lock unlike any Xanthe had seen: no keyhole, but a rose of interlocking petals, silver, each petal etched with a sigil she almost recognized. Serel's key was a plain iron thing, ugly as a nail, and when Ash produced it from inside his coat it seemed absurd against such a door—a sparrow's feather offered to a fortress.

It turned anyway. The petals unfolded one by one with a sound like a held breath released, and the door swung inward onto darkness that was not dark at all.

* * *

The Vault of Held Breaths was full of light.

Not lamplight. Not moonlight. A light that came from nowhere and belonged to no one, a pale gold radiance with no source, filling a chamber vast as a cathedral and shaped like nothing built by human hands—the ceiling arched and ribbed like the inside of a shell, the walls curving away into distances the eye refused to measure. And hanging in that sourceless glow, from hooks of black root that grew down through the ceiling like the fingers of a drowned god, were the souls.

Lanterns. There was no other word for them. Each one a soft-burning vessel of glassy light, round or teardrop or long as a finger, thousands upon thousands of them, tier upon tier rising into the arches, and each one burning with a color that was somehow also a voice, a name, a whole life compressed to a flame. Some burned steady. Some guttered, faintly, like breath gone shallow. One near the entrance flickered in a rhythm Xanthe recognized with a lurch as laughter—it laughed, softly, over and over, a soul that had died—or been taken—mid-joy.

"Oh," Ash said, beside her. Just that. Just *oh*, in a voice stripped of everything charming, and Xanthe understood that whatever else he was, whatever he had done or would do, in this moment he was as hollowed out as she was.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Does it matter?"

"No," she said. "But I want to know the size of the crime."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The chamber answered for him: *more than you can mourn in a lifetime*. That was the arithmetic of the place. That was why the court slept soundly.

There was no order to the tiers that Xanthe could see—no alphabet, no geography, no rank. And yet the ledger had given her one thread: entry number, date of deposit, and a notation in Pell's careful decoded hand: *Fairweather, I. — purpose: the Thorn — holding: root-tier, western aspect.*

Western aspect. She turned west, and the light turned with her, and she began to walk the aisles of the dead.

They hung close enough to touch. That was the cruelty of it. She could have reached out and put her palm against any of these little flames, and she caught herself flinching away from them as she passed, the way you flinch from strangers' eyes. An old man's soul, amber, steady as a hearth. A child's, no bigger than a plum, burning blue-white and quick. A pair hung side by side, gold and green, leaning toward each other like two people who had found each other even here, even now.

And then, on the lowest tier of the western wall, on a hook of black root worn smooth as if by long handling—

She knew her before she saw her. That was the truth of it. Some thread in Xanthe's chest, some sister-thread twenty years slack, pulled tight all at once, and she turned, and there it was.

Isolde's soul-light was white. Not the white of paper or bone but the white of lightning, of the heart of a storm, and it burned brighter than any lantern around it, steady and fierce and utterly still. Of course it did. Of course her sister's soul looked like weather. Even sold, even spent, even fed upon for twenty years by a root of ancient hunger—it had not guttered. It had waited.

"Isolde," Xanthe said aloud, and her voice broke the silence of the vault like a stone dropped into a well, and every soul-light in the chamber trembled at the sound.

She reached for it.

Her fingers were inches away—she could feel the heat of it now, or something like heat, something like being known—when Ash spoke behind her.

"I'm sorry, Xanthe."

The words landed before she understood them. She turned. He stood ten paces back, near the door, and in his hand was not the iron key but a signal flare, the court-issue kind, red-wrapped, already cracked and hissing like a snake. Red light bloomed up his face and made a stranger of it.

"Ash—"

"Don't touch it yet," he said, fast, low, pleading. "Please. If you take it now, before they come, they'll know the vault can be beaten and they'll move them all, and my daughter is in here somewhere, Xanthe, she's in here and I—" His voice cracked clean through. "I sold the plan. Weeks ago. Before we ever left Greyharbor. It was the only coin they'd take. Her soul for your plan—I didn't—they said no one would be hurt, they said—"

The flare spat. Somewhere far above, bells began to ring—quiet bells, the court's bells, sweet as a lullaby and terrible as a landslide.

"You lied," Xanthe said. It came out very small. "Every mile. Every storm. Every time you—" She thought of the night on the Drowned Lantern, his hands steadying hers on the wheel, the oath sworn in saltwater. "You swore."

"I swore true," he said desperately, backing toward the door as boots began to hammer in the corridor beyond, as the pearlescent lamps outside flared from pearl to white to a hard surgical glare. "I meant all of it. That's the hell of it, Xanthe, I meant every word, I just—I'm someone's father before I'm anyone's friend. You'd have done the same. For her." He nodded at the white light burning at her shoulder, inches from her outstretched hand. "You *would*."

And the worst of it, the part that would keep her awake in the years after, was that he was right.

The vault door boomed open behind him. Red-lit figures filled it—inquisitors in their grey, and at their center, pale-eyed and calm, Inquisitor Serel, who looked at Xanthe with something that was not quite pity and not quite respect.

"Step away from the lantern, Storm-caller," Serel said gently. "You've come all this way. Let's not spoil the ending."

Above them, through miles of stone, the thirteenth moon cleared the mountain's shoulder, and the Thorn, far below, began—very softly—to sing.
