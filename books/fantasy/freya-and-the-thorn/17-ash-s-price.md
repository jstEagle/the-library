## 17. Ash's Price

The flare was still burning in his mind.

Ash had lit it with hands that did not shake — that was the worst of it, the part he would carry longest. Not the moment Serel's pale eyes found him in the flooded gallery, not the sound of the vault doors grinding shut around Xanthe and her sister's light. It was before that: the steadiness of his own fingers striking the signal-flare against the wet stone, the small orange bloom of it reflected in the black water, and the thought that had risen in him, calm as a tide: *this is the price, and I have known the price since the first night she came aboard.*

Now they were taking him up through the mountain's skin, and the price was still being counted.

They marched him through galleries that had once been miners' roads — he could see it still, the old tool-marks under the court's gilding, the soot ghosts where lamps had hung for a hundred years of men who dug for iron and found instead the root of something older than crowns. The courtiers who lined the way did not jeer. That would have been easier. They only watched him pass with borrowed faces, wearing smiles that belonged to other people, dead people, sold people, and one of them — a woman in dove-grey silk with a mouth that moved half a beat behind her eyes — wore a smile Ash knew.

He had kissed that smile at a wedding on the Drowned Lantern's deck, eleven years ago, when Sera had been alive and round-bellied and laughing at Pell's toast. The courtier could not have known. The smile was just a smile, harvested from some soul or other, and it happened to be shaped like the one Ash had loved. But his chest seized anyway, and he stumbled, and the guards' grips on his arms tightened like roots.

*Everyone is someone's price.* Maren had said it to him once, years back, over cards in the hold while Corvin slept sitting up. She'd meant it as smuggler's wisdom: know what a man loves, know what he'll trade. Ash had laughed then. He wasn't laughing now. He was learning that the saying cut both ways — that every man is also someone's *price*, paid out by someone else, spent without his consent, and the ledger never closes clean.

They brought him to a cell that was not a cell.

That was the court's cruelty done properly, he understood now — not chains but comfort. A room carved warm from the living stone, hung with lamps that burned without smoke, furnished with a bed and a basin and a chair, and on the chair, folded neat as an apology, his own coat, the one he'd left aboard the Lantern. As if they expected him to stay a while. As if he were a guest whose visit had merely gone on longer than planned.

Serel closed the door behind them both. The Inquisitor's guards withdrew. For a long moment neither spoke. Water dripped somewhere far off, patient as a clock.

"You want to hate me," Ash said finally. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Go ahead. It's faster."

"I don't want anything from you." Serel crossed to the lamp and turned its wick down, and the room went amber and small. "I've seen men sell each other before. I've seen them sell themselves. What I haven't seen—" she turned, and her pale eyes caught the light like frost catching sun, "—is one who wept about it beforehand."

"I didn't weep."

"No," she agreed. "You bargained. You sat across a table from me in a tavern in Lowmark and you named your terms like a grain merchant. And your hands were steady." She tilted her head. "So tell me why you're shaking now."

He looked down. His hands were shaking. He hadn't noticed.

"Because it's done," he said. "And because it worked. Those are different shames, Inquisitor. Don't mix them."

* * *

He told her because there was no reason left not to, and because some part of him — the part that had been first mate of the Drowned Lantern, the part that had sworn the storm-oath with salt water and blood on the deck in the teeth of a squall — needed one witness who was not the Thorn.

"Her name was Lark," he said. "My daughter."

The name came out of him like a splinter coming out of flesh, slow and whole.

"She was six when the hunters came to Cindermarsh. This was before the proclamations, before registration — they didn't announce it then, they just... took. Quietly. A boat at night, a family gone by morning, neighbors pretending not to notice because noticing was dangerous." He wrapped his arms around himself, an old habit, holding his own ribs together. "Her mother had the gift. Small things — she could sweeten sour milk, coax a fire, find lost things. Lark had it too. Bigger. She'd stand on the dock and the gulls would come sit on her shoulders like she was telling them secrets, and maybe she was, I don't know, I never learned how it worked, I'm not gifted, I'm just—"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"I'm just the man who wasn't home that night. I was running cargo up the coast. I came back to an empty house and a neighbor who wouldn't meet my eyes, and a scorch-mark on the doorframe where someone had burned the household charm off the lintel." His laugh was terrible, thin, a wire pulled too tight. "You know what the worst part is? For two years I thought Sera had run. Taken the girl and gone, left me. I hated her for it. I built a whole hatred, brick by brick, fed it every day. And then I found out — a clerk's record, a bribe, a name in a column — that Sera died at the gate of the mountain town resisting, and Lark was carried inside, and her soul was entered into a ledger as an asset of the crown."

Serel said nothing. She had gone very still, the stillness of someone listening with more than ears.

"They don't kill the gifted children," Ash said. "Did you know that? They keep them. The bodies live in the nurseries below, tended, healthy, *empty*. Walking sleepers. Because the Thorn likes young souls best — bright ones, unspent. And the bodies..." He made himself say it. "The bodies are useful. They can be worn. Courtiers borrow them when they need a face nobody will question. A child begging at a gate opens doors that armed men can't."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

"For nine years I've been looking for a way in," Ash said. "I joined Maren's crew because she used to move the gifted across the border — I thought, someday, someone will go the other direction, into the mountain, and I'll be on that boat. I charmed my way into every job, every port, every dark corner where court business leaked. And then Xanthe Fairweather walked up the gangplank with storm in her bones and a sister's soul in her sights, and I thought: *there. There's the key. There's the storm big enough to crack this mountain.*"

"And instead you cracked the crew."

His flinch was involuntary and honest. "I went to Lowmark to scout the vault approaches. Serel's people were already watching me — they'd been watching me since Hollowmere, they knew what I was asking after in the records houses. And she sat down across from me with a cup of wine I hadn't ordered and said, 'First Mate Ash of the Drowned Lantern. We know about Lark.'" His voice frayed. "She said the Thorn holds forty thousand souls. She said most of them are past saving — held too long, thinned out, woven into the root itself. But she said the newer entries, the last decade's takings, those could be *released*. Returned. If the covenant were amended. If someone inside cooperated."

"And the price."

"The price was the crew's plan." He said it flatly, because dressing it up would be worse. "The route through the flooded galleries. The timing. The vault keys' rotation. Xanthe's true purpose — though they knew that already, mostly. In exchange: Lark's entry marked for release. Her body freed from the nursery. Passage out for both of us, papers, a new coast, a new name." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "Inquisitor, I have spent nine years learning exactly what my daughter's soul-light looks like. I've seen it in dreams. Gold, with a green thread through it, like sunlight through sea-glass. And Serel put a drawing of it on the table in front of me. From the ledger. *Verified.* Do you understand? They showed me my daughter's soul the way you'd show a starving man bread. What would you have done?"

It was not a rhetorical question, and Serel knew it. She considered him for a long time, head slightly bowed, and when she answered, her voice was quieter than he had ever heard it.

"I was offered my own memory once," she said. "A single afternoon, from before the court took my gift. My mother's kitchen. Bread rising on the sill. I refused." A pause. "Then I spent thirty years wondering if I'd been wise or only a coward, and I still don't know. So no, First Mate. I won't tell you what you should have done. I'll only tell you what happens next."

* * *

What happened next was Xanthe.

Ash heard her before he saw her — the guards' boots in the corridor, the jingle of restraint-chains, and beneath it a hum he felt in his fillings and the backs of his knees, the sound of a storm held in a jar of skin. They brought her in past him without ceremony, wrists bound in lead-threaded cord that dimmed her gift to a sullen glow, and set her on the bench across the cell, and withdrew.

For a moment they just looked at each other.

He had imagined this meeting a hundred times on the walk up through the mountain. In some versions she screamed at him. In some she wept. In one or two — the coward's versions — she simply nodded, because she'd always known, hadn't she, that everyone aboard the Lantern had a price, and his had merely come due first.

In none of the versions did she look at him the way she looked at him now: tired, and old, and searching his face for something she was not finding.

"Ash." Just his name. It landed like a stone in still water.

"Lark," he said, because she deserved the truth before anything else, even if the truth was a knife. "My daughter. Six years old. The court took her nine years ago and the Thorn has held her soul ever since, and I sold your plan to buy her back. That's all of it. There's nothing underneath."

Xanthe's bound hands flexed in her lap. The lead-cord smoked faintly where her gift pressed against it, a thread of ozone smell, the smell of the squall off Greyharbor the night she'd saved the ship and shown them all what she was.

"Wren told me about you," she said. "On the road. She said you gave her your blanket the first cold night and taught her dice so she'd stop being afraid of the dark. She said you were the one who made the crew laugh." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse than rising. "Corvin's son is in that vault, Ash. Did you sell him too, or does the ledger only discount children you happen to love?"

"Xanthe—"

"Isolde is in that vault." Now it rose, just a little, a wave gathering. "Twenty years. Twenty years I didn't even know I'd lost her — they took the memory of losing her, do you understand, they took my grief away and left me a smooth place where it used to be, and I got it back three days ago and it has been sitting in my chest like swallowed glass ever since. And you—" she stood, chains clinking, and the lamps guttered, "—you lit the flare. You stood in that gallery and you watched the doors close on her light and you *lit the flare*, and I want to know if you looked at me. While it closed. I want to know if you could."

"I looked." His voice broke on it. "God help me, Xanthe, I looked, and I did it anyway, because that's what the mountain does, that's what it's *for* — it makes you choose which beloved thing dies, and then it keeps the receipt. I am not a villain. I am a father. I am a father and they had my daughter's soul on a table between us like a purse of coin, and I—"

"—and Isolde was somebody's daughter too." Xanthe's voice dropped to almost nothing, and somehow that was the loudest thing in the room. "And Corvin's boy was somebody's son. And every soul hanging in that vault like a gutted lantern was somebody's everything, and you knew that, Ash, you'd *seen* them, you stood in that gallery with me and felt what I felt, and you still chose the one soul you could carry." She shook her head slowly. "I understand it. That's the horror of it. I understand it completely, and I will never forgive it, and both of those things are true, and I don't know yet which one is bigger."

He reached for her — an old reflex, the first mate's reflex, hand out to steady, to hold, to fix — and she stepped back, and the space between them was suddenly the width of the whole drowned world.

"Don't," she said. "Not with those hands."

Serel chose that moment to open the door, and Ash would remember, afterward, that he was grateful to her for it — grateful for the interruption the way a drowning man is grateful for the wave that finishes him.

"It's time," the Inquisitor said. "Both of you. The moon begins its final turn within the hour, and the court wishes witnesses presentable before the throne."

* * *

They went up through the heart of the mountain, and Ash catalogued everything, because cataloguing was all he had left — the way a man falling counts the floors.

The corridors widened as they climbed, or as they descended — he had lost all sense of direction in this place; the mountain kept its own geometry. Gilding crept up the walls like lichen, then like ivy, then like floodwater. Courtiers gathered along the way in silence, dozens of them, scores, standing in alcoves with their borrowed faces turned toward Xanthe, and Ash realized with a lurch that they weren't guarding her. They were *looking*. The souls in the Thorn could see through these eyes, however dimly, and something in the deep root had woken at her approach and was watching the storm-caller come with the hunger of a long fast.

Beside him, Xanthe walked with her chin level and her cuffed hands quiet, and the air around her tasted of lightning. Even leashed, even led, she filled the corridor the way weather fills a valley. Ash had seen her call the squall off the Lantern's bow; he had thought that was the size of her. He had been wrong. This was the size of her. This was what twenty years of smothering had compressed, coal into diamond, and the mountain had just invited the diamond into its softest chamber.

Serel walked ahead of them both, her pale eyes forward, her key-ring silent at her belt. Once, passing a junction where a guard-post stood empty — abandoned, Ash noted, as if the court had drawn all its strength inward toward the throne — Serel's hand brushed her belt, and one key shifted position, and their eyes met for half a heartbeat, hers unreadable, his pleading.

*What are you,* he wanted to ask her. *Jailer, turncoat, or something stranger?*

But the corridor opened, and there was no more asking.

The throne room was a cavern the size of a cathedral's corpse, and the throne was grown, not built — a seat of black root wound through with bone, rising out of the mountain's floor like a tree inverted, branches downward, buried heavenward. And through it, everywhere, ran the faint gold-green shimmer of held souls, thousands upon thousands, threaded through the wood like light through amber, and the whole vast chamber breathed with them, a slow tidal pulse, in and out, in and out, the sleeping breath of the Thorn.

Above it all, impossibly, hung a shaft of moonlight, falling through some fissure in the rock, silver-white and cold.

The thirteenth moon's light, reaching down into the dark to touch the thing that feared it.

Xanthe stopped beside him. The lead cords at her wrists smoked. Somewhere in the root, in a voice like her mother's, like anyone's mother, the Thorn began to speak.

And Ash, standing in the house of his betrayal, felt his daughter's soul-light stir somewhere in that golden weave — felt it, the way you feel a hand take yours in the dark — and did not know, anymore, which side of the bargain he was standing on.

The moon began its final turn.
