## 18. Throne of Root and Bone

They took her chains off in the antechamber.

Xanthe noticed that first—the absence of iron at her wrists, the strange lightness of her own hands—and understood it as a message before Serel spoke a single word. Chains were for prisoners. She was not being brought before the court as a prisoner. She was being brought as a guest, and guests were offered things.

"Compose yourself," Serel said. The Inquisitor's pale eyes caught the light of the bell-lanterns lining the corridor, and for a moment they looked less like ice and more like glass—something you could see through, if you knew where to press. "They will be kind to you. That is the worst thing I can tell you."

"I've been threatened by better than kindness," Xanthe said.

"No." Serel's voice was quiet, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened Xanthe more than any blade could have. "You haven't. Not yet. Threats harden the spine. Kindness finds the crack already there and pours itself in." She turned, her grey robes whispering against stone worn smooth by centuries of feet. "I only ask that when it happens, you remember that I told you the truth of it. That is all any of us can do down here. Remember we were told."

The doors opened without being touched.

* * *

The throne room of the court beneath the mountain was not what Xanthe had prepared herself for.

She had imagined darkness. She had imagined the wet gleam of root-flesh, the smell of loam and rot, courtiers hunched like ghouls around a black and hungry thing. What she found instead was light—hundreds of bell-lanterns hanging from a vaulted ceiling so high its apex vanished into shadow, each lantern glowing with a soft gold warmth that made the whole chamber feel like the inside of a struck note. The floor was white marble veined with silver. Courtiers moved through that light in silks of every color, laughing softly, conversing in voices that harmonized too perfectly, and everywhere—everywhere—the scent of something flowering.

It smelled like spring. It smelled like the orchard behind the house she had grown up in, the one Isolde used to climb when their mother wasn't looking, blossoms catching in her hair like small white stars.

That was the first cruelty, and Xanthe recognized it as such even as her chest tightened. They had made the monster's den smell like home.

And there, at the far end of the hall, beneath an arch of living wood that had grown up through the marble and split it like frost splitting a windowpane, stood the throne.

The Thorn grew through it. That was the only way to describe what she saw—a root vast as an ancient oak, black-barked and gleaming faintly, rising from the mountain's deep stone and winding itself around a seat carved from the same dark wood until it was impossible to say which had come first, the throne or the thing enthroned upon it. From its branches hung no leaves but small pale buds, closed tight, thousands of them, pulsing with a light that was almost—but not quite—the same gold as the lanterns. Warmer. Hungrier. The light of a hearth that wanted you to come closer and closer and never leave.

Souls. She knew it now, having seen them hang in their hundreds in the vault below. Each bud was a held breath. Each bud was someone.

Courtiers knelt along the aisle as she was led forward—not forced, not commanded, simply folding down one after another like wheat before wind, their faces serene, their eyes fixed on her with an attention that made her skin crawl. She counted the borrowed mannerisms as she passed: a woman who laughed with a fisherman's bark; a man who walked with a child's skipping rhythm; a young lord whose smile belonged, she was suddenly certain, to someone dead twenty years. The court wore its dead the way other courts wore jewelry.

At the foot of the dais, Serel stopped and inclined her head. "They will receive you now," she said, and stepped back into the crowd, and Xanthe climbed the seven steps alone.

Up close, the Thorn was worse. Its bark was not bark at all but something between wood and skin, and it breathed—slowly, deeply, the whole vast root swelling and settling like the flank of a sleeping animal. The buds nearest her trembled as she approached, turning on their stems the way flowers turn toward sun.

Toward her storm. Toward the gift coiled tight in her chest, the one she had spent twenty years smothering under merchant's smiles and luck-knots and lies.

"Daughter of Fairweather."

The voice came from everywhere—from the lanterns, from the marble, from inside her own skull, warm and resonant as a bell struck in a small room. The assembled court went still. Even the laughter died.

"You have traveled far," said the Thorn, "and been hurt much. Sit. Be welcome. No harm will come to you here that has not already come to you elsewhere."

There was a second chair beside the throne. She hadn't seen it from below—an armchair of pale wood, cushioned in blue, placed at the Thorn's right hand as though it had always been waiting. As though it had been grown for her.

Xanthe did not sit. But she did not run either. Running would have told them everything about how close she was to breaking.

"I came for my sister," she said. Her voice sounded thin in all that golden light. "Isolde Fairweather. Sold to this court twenty years ago. I want her back."

A murmur ran through the kneeling courtiers—not scandal, but delight, the soft rustle of an audience pleased that the play has reached the scene it promised.

"We know," said the Thorn gently. "We have always known. Why do you think your road here was so long, and yet never truly barred? Why do you think the vault opened for you as far as it did?" A pause, and then, softer: "We have been waiting for you a very long time, Xanthe."

* * *

The voice changed.

It happened between one breath and the next—one moment the great resonant nothing-voice, and the next a woman's voice, low and warm, with a laugh buried in it the way a riverbed holds water. A voice Xanthe had not heard in twenty years and had never once stopped hearing.

*Xanthe-love,* said her mother. *Come away from the door, sweetheart. You'll catch your death standing in the draft.*

Xanthe's knees nearly went. She locked them. Her hands, hanging at her sides, curled into fists so tight her nails bit crescents into her palms, and she used the pain the way she'd once used it to hold storms down—anchor, anchor, anchor.

"You're not her," she managed.

*No,* the Thorn agreed, sorrowful, patient, wearing her mother's voice the way the courtiers wore their stolen laughs. *No, love. She is gone, and I am sorry for it—I am sorry in the way that only something very old can be sorry, having watched ten thousand daughters lose ten thousand mothers. But I kept her voice. Of all the souls that have fed me, hers sang sweetest, and I do not waste what sings.* The buds nearest Xanthe pulsed brighter, and in their glow she could almost—almost—see a face. *Would you like to hear more? I have years of her. I have her singing you to sleep. I have the argument you two had the week before the fire, the one you've spent two decades wishing you could take back. I have her last thought, and it was of you.*

"Stop." The word came out cracked.

*I will never stop,* said her mother's voice, tenderly. *That is what I am offering.*

And then the court rose, all together, silent as snowfall, and the offer began.

* * *

It did not come as a contract. It came as a memory.

The Thorn reached for her—not with branch or root, but with something subtler, a pressure against the sealed door inside her mind, the locked room where her missing years lived. And gently, so gently, it turned the key.

Xanthe was nine years old again, standing in the orchard, and her mother was alive, and the light was gold, and for three full heartbeats it was so real that she forgot she had ever lost it—

Then she wrenched herself free, gasping, and the throne room reassembled around her, and the court was watching her with expressions of profound compassion.

"Do you see?" A new speaker now—a figure gliding forward from among the courtiers, tall and robed in silver, with a face so beautiful it had clearly been borrowed from several people at once. The court's herald, or its high steward, or whatever silk-wrapped name they gave the thing that spoke for the root. "This is what we hold. Not suffering, Xanthe Fairweather. *Keeping.* The world above takes everything from everyone—mothers, sisters, years, names—and gives back nothing but forgetting. We alone remember. We alone keep what the world discards." The beautiful face tilted. "Your sister is not lost. Your sister is *here*, whole, every moment of her preserved as perfectly as the day she was sold. And she can be yours again."

"Her soul isn't a keepsake," Xanthe said. "It's a person."

"It is both," the herald said, "and we are generous with both. Hear our terms."

And the terms unfolded like a flower opening, each petal more reasonable than the last.

Her sister's soul, released from the Thorn unharmed—restored to her, or given flesh again, the court had power enough for either. Her memories, all of them, the locked room thrown open, every stolen year returned. Her exile undone—pardon signed, name cleared, the storm-hunt called off, the gifted of Greyharbor and beyond freed to walk in daylight. All of it. Everything she had crossed a kingdom and betrayed and bled for, laid at her feet in a single breath.

The price was spoken last, and softly, in her mother's voice:

*Only stay. Only open your hands and let me have the storm. You have hated it since you were a child—it burned your family, it exiled you, it has made you hide behind charms and knots and a merchant's smile for twenty years. Give it to me, and be free of it forever. Give it to me, and feed it to me willingly, and your sister goes free, and you will sit at my right hand in a chair I grew for you before you were born, and you will never again have to be afraid of what you are.*

The court sighed, all together, like a tide going out.

Xanthe stood very still.

Because here was the terrible truth, the one she would carry to her grave: for one long moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—she *wanted* it. She wanted it with a hunger that scared her more than the Thorn ever could. To put the storm down. To set down the weight she had carried since she was nine years old, the thing that had killed her parents, that had cost her sister everything, that had made her a smuggler of luck-knots and a liar in every market from Greyharbor to the coast. Twenty years of smothering, and here was permission—sanctified, golden, offered in her mother's voice—to finally, finally stop.

And Isolde would live. Wren would be safe. Ash's daughter, Corvin's son, every soul in every bud—they would go free, wouldn't they, if she only said yes. The math was clean. The bargain was kind. That was the horror of it. There was no cruelty anywhere in the offer except the offer itself.

"You killed my sister," Xanthe said quietly. "You know that, don't you? Whatever you're keeping of her—you killed her to keep it. My mother's voice in your mouth, and my mother would have spat on you."

The golden light dimmed, just slightly. Just at the edges.

*Everything feeds something,* said the Thorn, and the warmth in the voice thinned, and beneath it Xanthe heard—for the first time, unmistakably—the vast slow hunger that had been underneath all along, patient as geology. *The ox feeds the farmer. The farmer feeds the king. The king feeds me, and I feed the world its weather and its harvests and its remembered dead. You have fed me your fear for twenty years, daughter of Fairweather, whether you willed it or no. I am only asking you to feed me gladly.*

"And if I refuse?"

The silence that followed had weight. The buds along the great root stirred, all of them at once, thousands of small lights turning toward her like eyes.

*Then I keep what I have kept,* the Thorn said, and now it was only the great voice again, and the cold in it was the cold of the mountain's bones. *Your sister sleeps another century in my wood. Your memories stay locked. The hunt above continues, and the gifted burn, and the moon completes its thirteenth turn, and I wake fully, and none of it—not one bud, not one soul—will ever see daylight again. Refusal costs everything, little storm-caller. Acceptance costs only you.*

"That's not true," Xanthe said.

Her heart was hammering. Her palms were bleeding where her nails had bitten through. And somewhere beneath the terror, beneath the ache of her mother's voice still echoing in the vaulted dark, something else was rising—the old familiar pressure in her chest, the storm she had been strangling since Greyharbor, since childhood, since always. Except that for the first time in her life she was not strangling it.

She was listening to it.

"The price isn't only me," she said. "It never is. That's the whole rotten covenant, isn't it? Everyone downstairs pays, and the ones upstairs call it order." She lifted her head and looked at the great black root, at the thousand trembling buds, at the throne it wore like a wound wears a crown. "My sister sold herself to save me. Do you honestly think I'd buy her back by becoming you?"

The Thorn was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, all the warmth was gone, and what remained was almost—almost—respect.

*Then you will do it the hard way,* it said. *As they all do, at the end.*

Above them, impossibly, through miles of stone, the thirteenth moon began its final turn—and every bud on the black root opened one petal wider, and the light in the throne room shifted from gold to a color Xanthe had no name for, and the court beneath the mountain drew one long, collective breath.

Xanthe reached into her chest, past twenty years of locks and lies, and took hold of the storm.
