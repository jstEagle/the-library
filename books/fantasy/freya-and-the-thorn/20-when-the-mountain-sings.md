## 20. When the Mountain Sings

### Xanthe

The storm had been waiting in her chest for twenty years, and now it came up like a tide.

She stood before the throne of root and bone with her arms open and her palms bleeding where the black thorns had pricked them, and she did not speak the refusal Serel had warned her to prepare. Words were the court's currency. The Thorn traded in bargains, in ledgers, in names signed on paper. Xanthe gave it none.

She breathed in.

The mountain's air tasted of iron and old water, of the flooded galleries below and the deep galleries above, of every tunnel the miners had carved when this place was honest work instead of hunger. She pulled all of it into her lungs. And beneath it she felt the other thing—the pressure that had been building since the vault sealed, since Ash's flare went up red against the dark, since Isolde's soul-light had slipped through her fingers like a fish made of moonlight. The storm did not rise in her so much as *remember itself*.

*You will die,* said the Thorn, in her mother's voice, in her father's voice, in a voice like rain on a warm roof. *The mountain will fall on you. Your sister's soul is still in my keeping. I can give her back whole—*

"You never had her," Xanthe said quietly. "You only held what she gave. And she gave it for me. That's not yours. It was never yours."

The root at the heart of the throne shuddered—a slow, vegetable movement, like something turning over in its sleep—and the courtiers along the walls stopped their murmuring all at once, hundreds of borrowed voices falling silent as one throat.

Xanthe called the storm.

It did not come from the sky. There was no sky down here, only stone and the memory of stone, and so the storm came from *her*—from the girl who had drowned a kitchen in lightning at nine years old, from the exile who had knotted charms in a market stall while thunder sat caged behind her ribs, from the woman who had walked into a mountain to take back a soul and found her own guilt waiting instead. Twenty years of smothered weather tore loose through her spine and out through her hands, and the mountain's bones rang like a struck bell.

Lightning does not behave underground. It should not have been possible. But the Thorn was a root, and roots drink, and everything the mountain held—its black water, its silver veins, its buried heat—was already threaded through the Thorn's hungry wood. The storm found it the way water finds a crack. The first bolt went *into* the throne and kept going, racing down through the root's endless branching body, lighting the whole court from within until every courtier stood outlined in white fire, their stolen faces flickering between the faces they wore and the faces they'd been.

Xanthe heard screaming. Some of it was hers.

### Corvin

Corvin had carried his son's name folded in his coat pocket for six years, written on the registration slip the hunters had left nailed to his door, and he had promised himself he would say it aloud only once more in his life: at the moment it mattered.

The moment was now.

He had the vault key—Serel's key, pressed into his hand by a woman whose pale eyes had finally stopped looking through people and started looking *at* them—and he had the wheel-lock that held the floodgates of the upper galleries, and he had perhaps four minutes before the mountain came down. He put his shoulder to the gate mechanism and thought about how Tam had laughed, gap-toothed, the last morning. He thought about nothing else. Grief was a tool if you held it right; it had a weight and an edge like anything else.

"Go," he told Pell, who was dragging two soul-lanterns by their chains, her clerk's hands blistered raw. "I'll hold the wheel."

"The wheel won't hold you, you stubborn old barnacle—"

"It doesn't have to hold me." He looked at her, and let her see all six years of it, because she had earned the truth and there wasn't time for kindness. "It has to hold the door. Go get them out."

Pell's face did something complicated and then went hard, which was her way of weeping. She ran.

Corvin turned the wheel. The counterweights groaned. Above him, through a hundred feet of splitting rock, he could hear the storm singing—not thunder exactly, though there was thunder in it, but something older, the sound the world might have made the first time weather happened. His son would have loved it. His son had loved storms, before the court taught everyone to be afraid of them.

The vault doors stood open behind him. Souls were rising out of it like a flock startled off water—hundreds of small lights, streaming upward through the cracks the storm was widening, pouring toward the distant ceiling and whatever thin thread of sky the mountain still remembered. Corvin braced the wheel with his back and his boots and his whole stubborn remaining life, and watched them go, and said, aloud, just once: "Tam Fairweather's neighbor's boy. Tam. Look at that, lad. Look at that."

One light broke from the stream. It circled him twice, close enough to warm his cheek, and then it rose.

He smiled until the end, which came quickly, and gently, under a great deal of mountain.

### Ash

Ash had done the arithmetic weeks ago, alone in the dark of the Drowned Lantern's hold, and the sum had never changed: one daughter's soul against a crew, a stranger's sister, a country's worth of strangers. He had sold the plan because love makes cowards of careful men, and he had hated himself cleanly and completely ever since, and hatred, too, has a weight and an edge.

Now the vault was collapsing and his daughter's light was somewhere in the rising flood of souls, and the lintel above the doors was coming down in slabs the size of coffins, and there was no arithmetic left in the world except this: he could hold the gap open, or he could look for her, and he could not do both.

He chose the gap. He wedged himself beneath the failing lintel with his shoulders and his raised arms, and he became, for the last minutes of his life, a doorpost. Stone ground against his spine. Something in his left arm cracked and stopped answering. He held anyway.

They streamed past him—all of them, the taken ones, the bought ones, the surrendered and the seized, souls he had helped condemn with a flare and a signature. None of them looked at him. Why would they? He was furniture. He was architecture. He was the price everyone else had paid.

Then a light slowed.

It was small—that was the thing that undid him, how *small* it was, no bigger than a candleflame, when he had spent six years imagining her as everything she would have grown into. The light drifted down through the chaos and hovered before his face, and he felt it the way you feel sun through a window on a winter day: warmth with no source, recognition with no reason.

"Pip," he whispered. "Pip, sweetheart."

The soul-light touched his hand—the broken one, the useless one—and where it touched, there was no pain at all. It rested there for three heartbeats. Four. Long enough. Long enough for him to feel, unmistakably, a child's forgiveness, which is the most terrible substance in any world, because it is given freely to people who have done nothing to earn it.

"I know," he said. "I know. Go up. Go up and don't wait for me."

The light rose. It joined the stream. Through the widening crack overhead, Ash caught one glimpse of the thirteenth moon—full, enormous, impossibly near, as if the whole sky had leaned down to receive them—and then the lintel finished its long argument with gravity, and he was not sorry, and he was not afraid, and the last thing he thought was that his daughter had always been warmer than anyone knew.

### Pell

Pell had spent eleven years as a court clerk, and she knew better than anyone alive that empires are made of paper. So when the Thorn began to die, she did not watch the lightning. She watched the ledger.

She had it under her arm—the Ledger of Names, stolen, decoded, its columns of debts and purchases now worthless—and as the court's power failed, the book began to change in her hands. Ink faded. Entries dissolved. Page after page of souls recorded as assets went blank, and Pell stood in the shaking gallery and read the erasure happening in real time, line by line, name by name, and she understood that she was watching the largest bankruptcy in history: every account closed, every debt forgiven, every asset returned to its rightful owner.

Isolde Fairweather's entry went last. Pell watched the ink lift off the page like smoke, and she said, to no one, "Paid in full," and then the gallery floor tilted and she ran, clutching a book full of blank pages like a relic, because someone had to carry the proof out. Someone had to tell the world what had been written here, even if the writing itself was gone.

Behind her, the stolen voices screamed free.

That was the sound she would describe for the rest of her life, badly, to anyone who asked: not pain, though there was pain in it, but *relief*, a thousand voices released from borrowed throats all at once, each one saying its own name for the first time in years, decades, centuries. The courtiers collapsed like puppets with cut strings, and what lay inside them was ordinary—old men, tired women, frightened clerks like she had been—and they wept on the marble in their own voices, and the sound of it was worse and better than any scream.

Above, the mountain sang. Pell ran toward the song, because it was the only exit left, and because for the first time in eleven years she was not afraid of loud things.

### Xanthe

At the end, there was no court, no throne, no bargain. There was only the root and the storm, locked together in the mountain's heart like two hands that could not let go.

The Thorn fought the way rot fights: patiently, whisperingly, offering her her mother's voice, her father's laugh, her sister's forgiveness in advance, her magic preserved, her exile undone, her guilt excused. Xanthe heard all of it and poured the storm harder. She was past hearing. She was the place where weather happens. Every suppressed year burned as fuel—every charm knotted small, every thunder swallowed, every smile she had sold in Greyharbor's market while the sky knocked at her ribs asking to be let out.

*Let go,* she told it, or it told her, or the mountain told them both. *Let go.*

The Thorn shattered.

There was no other word for it. A thing that ancient should have gone out slowly, guttering like a lamp—but it had been alive in the way a tumor is alive, and when the storm reached its heart, the whole vast root system, threaded through miles of stone, burst apart in a single silent instant of black light. Then the sound arrived: a crack that was not a sound but a *decision*, the mountain deciding after ten thousand years to be a ruin instead of a prison. The throne split. The galleries sagged. Somewhere far above, dawn was arriving early through a hole that hadn't existed yesterday.

And the souls came up.

They came through the broken stone around her like water finding its level, hundreds upon hundreds, streaming toward the wound in the world's skin and the full thirteenth moon hanging beyond it. Xanthe stood in the middle of the ascent, drenched in lightning-afterglow, her hair floating as if underwater, and watched the dead of twenty years go home.

Her magic went with them.

She felt it leave without drama, without pain—the way a fever breaks, the way a hand unclenches after being clenched so long the clenching had become the hand. The storm poured out of her and did not come back. What remained was just a woman standing in a collapsing room, mortal, exhausted, thirty-some years old, empty in the way a cup is empty after it has finally been drunk.

Something touched her cheek. Warmth with no source.

*Xanthe.*

Not spoken. Not quite remembered. A voice like her own childhood, like a shared bed during storms, like someone braiding her hair with clumsy loving fingers.

*Oh, little storm,* said Isolde's soul, passing through her on its way to the moonlight. *Look what you grew into. Look what you did. It was never your fault—it was never your fault—it was never—*

"I know," Xanthe whispered, and found that she meant it, and found that meaning it was the hardest thing she had ever done. "I know. Go up. Don't wait for me."

A breath of laughter, there and gone. *I never waited for anything in my life. Ask anyone.*

And then the warmth lifted from her cheek and rose, joining the stream, joining the others, climbing toward the enormous patient moon, and Xanthe Fairweather stood alone in the ruin of the court beneath the mountain with empty hands and an empty chest, and wept—not the way she had wept in grief, which was a breaking, but the way water weeps, which is only the shape of release.

The mountain settled around her with a long sigh, its dying gentler than its living had been. Through the dust and the early dawn, she could see figures moving on the upper gallery—Pell, with a blank book hugged to her chest; Serel, standing very still beside an open vault, her key finally turned; and no Ash, and no Corvin, and she marked both absences the way you mark graves when there is nothing else to mark.

She wiped her face. She picked her way toward the light.

Above her, the last of the souls reached the moon, and the thirteenth turn completed not in darkness, as the court had planned, but in radiance—and somewhere in the world above, every hunt, every registration list, every painted thorn-sigil on every threshold went suddenly, inexplicably cold.

The mountain sang until sunrise. Then it was quiet, and it stayed quiet, and the quiet was the kindest sound Xanthe had ever heard.

But in the dust of the ruined throne, half-buried, one fragment of the Thorn had survived—no longer than a finger, black as a closed eye—and as the first true daylight in centuries fell through the broken crown of the mountain, it twitched, very slightly, like a root remembering spring.

Xanthe did not see it.

Wren would.
