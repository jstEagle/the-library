## 21. Souls in the Moonlight

The silence after the storm was the loudest thing Xanthe had ever heard.

It pressed into the ruined throne room like water filling a broken hull—through the shattered galleries, through the split seams of the mountain's bones, through the ragged hole where the Thorn had been. Dust hung in the air in slow golden spirals, lit by a light that had no business existing this far underground: moonlight, pouring down through the wound her storm had torn in the world above, falling in a single vast column onto the place where the throne had stood.

The throne itself was gone. In its place, the black root lay in pieces across the floor like the ribs of some enormous slaughtered beast, still twitching at their severed ends, still trying to remember how to grow. Sap wept from them—not sap, she corrected herself, not anymore; it was only resin now, dead and ordinary, smelling of pine and cold iron—and where each drop fell, it hissed and steamed against the stone.

Xanthe knelt in the middle of it all, and could not stand.

Her hands were burned. Not the clean burn of fire but something stranger—the skin of her palms and forearms marked with pale branching lines, like frost-ferns, like lightning frozen mid-bloom, tracing the paths the storm had taken out of her body. She turned her hands over and watched the marks fade even as she looked, draining away like color from a dying leaf, and understood what they were. Receipts. The storm had written its price on her in its own hand, and now it was collecting.

*Oh,* she thought, with a strange floating calm. *So that's what it costs.*

She had known, of course. Somewhere beneath the fury and the grief and the twenty years of swallowed thunder, she had known that no one calls a storm like that—one that sings through a mountain's marrow and unmakes a root older than kingdoms—and walks away whole. The gift did not forgive that kind of spending. It did not forgive anything. That had always been the joke of it, hadn't it? The court fed souls to the Thorn to buy dominion over weather and harvest and memory, and here she had spent the whole of hers in a single night, freely, gladly, and bought nothing but an ending.

She tried to reach for it anyway. Habit, more than hope. She reached down into her chest, into that place behind her sternum where the storm had lived since she was small enough to think everyone carried weather in their ribs—

—and found quiet.

Not the strained, sweating quiet of suppression, the quiet she had performed for two decades, teeth gritted, charm-knots tied over her pulse like bandages. This was different. This was the quiet of an empty room. The quiet of a house after the family has gone. She reached deeper, the way she'd reached a thousand times before, groping for the first warm stirring of pressure that meant rain coming, and there was nothing there at all. No pressure. No warmth. Just her own heartbeat, mortal and unremarkable, tapping away in the dark like a clerk counting out the last coins of a bankrupt estate.

Xanthe Fairweather began, very quietly, to laugh.

It wasn't a good laugh. It came out cracked and half-sobbed, bouncing off the broken walls, startling a crow—*a crow? down here?*—off a fallen beam. But she couldn't stop it, because it was either laugh or lie down among the root-shards and never rise, and Isolde had not paid twenty years of soul for her sister to lie down in the dark.

"Isolde," she said aloud, and the name fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

The vault had opened when Serel turned her key. The crew had fought through the galleries—Corvin's sacrifice still a raw red absence in her chest, Pell screaming orders in her old clerk's voice, Ash holding the collapsing doorway open with his shoulders and his back and whatever was left of him—and the souls had risen. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Lantern-lights streaming up through the mountain's wound like reversed snowfall, like a river deciding to flow home to the sky.

But Isolde's light had lingered.

Xanthe felt her before she saw her—a warmth against her cheek, faint as breath on a winter window. She turned, kneeling in the ruin, and there in the moonlight column, drifting down through the dust and the gold, came a soft white radiance no larger than a lantern-flame, moving the way a person moves when they are in no hurry and know exactly where they're going.

"Izzy." The childhood name came out of her before she could stop it, small and stupid and true.

The light settled in front of her face, close enough that its glow touched her skin without warming it, and within it—if she unfocused her eyes, if she let herself believe the way she had believed as a girl watching storms build over the sea—there was a shape. A suggestion of shoulders. A tilt of the head she knew better than her own reflection. The particular way Isolde used to lean when she was about to say something devastating and gentle.

"You're so *old,*" said a voice like wind through a summer field, and Xanthe broke.

There was no dignity in it. Twenty years of exile and guilt and careful merchant smiles came apart all at once, and she sobbed like the child she had been on the night the burning room filled with smoke, reaching up with her scarred hands as if she could cup that light the way you cup water, knowing even as she did it that it would run through her fingers.

And it did. The soul-light drifted forward and passed *through* her—through her cupped hands, through her chest, through the empty room where her storm had lived—and for one impossible moment Xanthe was not alone inside her own body.

It was not warmth, exactly. It was recognition. It was the feeling of being *known* completely and found worth loving anyway, which is the only definition of forgiveness that has ever mattered. Memories that were not hers moved through her like fish through clear water: a kitchen with flour on the table; a younger sister shrieking with laughter as a harmless little whirlwind spun her braids; a night of smoke and screaming and a terrible calm descending; a candle; a pen; a signature; a door closing on a sister's face for the last time. Xanthe had carried the guilt of that night like a stone in her chest for two decades, and now she felt it lifted—not erased, never erased, but *carried*, briefly, by someone who had chosen the same weight willingly and called it love.

*"I never blamed you,"* said the voice, everywhere and nowhere, inside her ribs and outside her skin. *"You were a child and the storm was bigger than both of us. I only ever wished you'd stop blaming yourself. It was such a heavy thing to carry, Xanthe. You can put it down now. You can put it all down."*

"I killed them," Xanthe gasped, to the light, to the ruin, to the moon. "Izzy, I killed our parents, I—"

*"No. A storm killed them. And then my sister spent her whole life punishing herself for weather."* The voice frayed at the edges, going thin, going upward, going home. *"I have watched you from inside that root for twenty years, do you know that? Every charm you sold, every knot you tied, every storm you swallowed down until your teeth ached. I was so proud of you. I am so proud of you. Look what you grew in all that salt and hiding. Look what you built out of almost nothing."*

"Don't go." It was shameful, how young she sounded. "Please. I just got you back."

*"You never lost me. That's the whole point, you dear fool. You never once lost me."*

The light rose out of her chest slowly, reluctantly, the way dawn leaves a valley—lingering on every height it passes. And in the last moment, as it gathered itself into the moonlit column above, Xanthe heard it one final time, closer than a whisper, softer than memory:

*"Tie one for me. With the blue thread. The way I taught you."*

Then it was rising, rising, joining the long bright river of souls pouring up through the mountain's wound, and Xanthe knelt alone in the golden dust with her empty chest and her empty hands and watched her sister go home.

* * *

They came for her eventually—she didn't know how much later; time had gone strange, measured now only in the drift of dust through moonlight. Footsteps on broken stone. Pell first, limping, her clerk's coat scorched, her face streaked with soot and something else. Then others, crew and freed prisoners alike, moving carefully through the wreckage like people walking through a cathedral whose god has just died.

Ash came last, and he came leaning on the wall, because he had held a collapsing vault open with his body and his body had opinions about it now. His left arm hung wrong. His face was gray. But his eyes went straight past the shattered root, past the dead throne, past everything, up to the column of light where the last few soul-lanterns were still climbing toward the thirteenth moon.

One of them paused at the top of the column. Small and bright and stubborn. It hovered there a long moment, the way a child hovers at a gate, and then it dipped—once, twice, unmistakably, deliberately—before soaring up and away.

Ash made a sound that was not quite a word.

"She waited for you," Xanthe said. Her voice was wrecked, sanded raw by smoke and storm and weeping. "At the end. She wanted you to see."

He slid down the wall beside her, gracelessly, gratefully, and for a while neither of them spoke. Around them the freed souls' passage had left the air changed—lighter, somehow, the way a house feels after a funeral is finally over. The great wound overhead showed a smear of paling sky. Dawn was coming. The thirteenth moon, full and fat and finally finished with its turning, was setting in the west, and the first honest sunlight in twenty years was sliding down through the broken mountain to touch the floor where the Thorn had ruled.

"It's gone," Ash said at last. He wasn't talking about the Thorn. He was looking at her hands—at the fading frost-fern marks, at the way she flexed her fingers like a woman testing whether a limb still answered. "Your magic. Isn't it."

It wasn't a question. He was gifted himself; he knew the shape of another person's power the way sailors know weather.

"Yes."

"How does it feel?"

Xanthe considered the question honestly, turning it over like one of her charms, examining the knotwork of it. She thought of the exhaustion of hiding. Of luck-knots sold with a merchant's smile while real thunder sat caged behind her teeth. Of the raid in Greyharbor, smothering a storm in her chest until her vision went white. Of twenty years of being a vessel pretending to be a shelf.

"Light," she said finally, and was surprised to find it was true. "It feels light. Like setting down something I forgot I was carrying."

Ash nodded slowly. "My daughter's light touched my hand," he said, very quietly. "Before it went up. Just—touched it. Like she used to when she was small and wanted to check I was still there." His jaw worked. "I sold all of you for that. I'd sell all of you again. And I will be sorry for both of those things for the rest of my life, and both of those things are true, and I don't expect—" 

"Ash." She reached over and gripped his good hand with her scarred one. "I know what the court does to people who are desperate. I've spent a year learning exactly what it does." She looked up at the growing daylight. "Isolde forgave me for killing our parents. I imagine I can manage to forgive you for wanting your daughter back. It might take me until spring. But I'll get there."

He laughed—a wet, broken sound—and pressed his forehead briefly to her shoulder, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing else that needed saying, and because above them the sun was rising properly now, and the mountain around them was beginning, very faintly, to groan.

Pell appeared at the mouth of the gallery, sharp-tongued even through exhaustion. "Much as I hate to interrupt the feelings," she said, "the mountain is coming down. The Thorn was apparently load-bearing, which nobody thought to mention, and the tunnels are flooding. We have perhaps an hour, if we're lucky, and we are not, historically, lucky people. Move."

* * *

They climbed out through the wound in the world, up the shattered throat of the old mine-vent, hauling one another over fallen beams while the court beneath the mountain collapsed behind them—its beautiful terrible galleries folding in on themselves, its quiet bells silent forever, its borrowed voices gone home. Xanthe climbed last, mortal and magicless and slower than she had ever been, and no one left her behind. Corvin's rope, coiled and waiting at each ledge, did the work his hands could no longer do.

When she finally pulled herself over the lip of the vent and out onto the mountainside, the dawn hit her like a blessing.

Below, Hollowmere lay in the early light, its empty cradles and painted thresholds gilded gold. Beyond it, the road ran down and down toward the lowlands, toward Greyharbor, toward salt and market-noise and ordinary mornings. The air smelled of pine resin and rain-washed stone. On the horizon, the thirteenth moon was setting into the sea, and all along its path across the water, the fishermen would be telling stories for a hundred years about the lights that rose out of the mountain and scattered like a flock of silver birds.

Xanthe stood in the ruins of her old life and breathed.

Somewhere down in the dark, the last of the court was finishing its collapse, and somewhere up here in the light, Wren was waiting—Wren with her quick hands and her inherited knot-work, Wren who had started all of this with a charm in a market street. There were knots to tie. There was a charm owed, with blue thread, the way a sister had taught her, twenty years late and right on time.

Xanthe Fairweather rolled up her sleeves, took out her cord, and began.
