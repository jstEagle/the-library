## 22. A Charm for Greyharbor

The gulls had learned to steal from charm-sellers.

Xanthe watched one wheel down over the market stalls of Greyharbor's Salt Row, snatch a ribboned luck-knot right off a display line, and rise again into the pale spring light with its prize trailing like a pennant. The stall's owner—a round-faced woman Xanthe half-remembered from the years when such things were sold in whispers and hidden sleeves—shook her fist and laughed. Laughed. A year ago that theft would have been a hanging matter twice over: once for the stolen goods, once for what the goods meant.

Now it was just Tuesday.

"Your knot-work is crooked," Wren said beside her, holding up the cord she'd been worrying at for the better part of an hour. The girl was fourteen now, or thereabouts—she'd never been certain of her own age, which was the sort of thing the court's ledgers made ordinary—and her fingers were quick and impatient and stained blue at the tips from the woad-dyed thread she favored. "You said three crossings before the anchor loop. I did four."

"I did," Xanthe agreed, and took the cord from her. It was good work anyway. Better than good. There was a faint warmth in the fibers, a hum like a struck bell heard through a wall, that Xanthe's hands could no longer feel but her memory could not forget. "And it's still holding. That's the thing they don't put in the pattern-books, Wren. The knots aren't the magic. They're just where the magic decides to sit."

Wren squinted at her the way she always did when Xanthe said something that sounded like wisdom but might have been nonsense. She'd been doing it for eleven months, ever since she'd turned up on Xanthe's doorstep in the rain with a bundle of charms and no explanation except *you told me once you'd teach me if I ever came back*, which Xanthe had no memory of saying and every reason to believe anyway. She'd said a great many things to people in the last year. Some of them she remembered.

She set the crooked charm back in the girl's hands. "Four crossings makes it a storm-knot. Old fisher pattern, from before the crown ever bothered counting us. It calls weather instead of luck."

"Can you feel it?" Wren asked, and then went pink, because it was the question she'd promised herself she wouldn't ask anymore. She asked it anyway, sometimes. Xanthe had decided a long time ago that she preferred the asking to the careful silence.

"No," Xanthe said. "But I know exactly what it feels like. Like standing in a doorway with rain coming. Like the air before the first drop." She smiled, and found—to her mild, ongoing surprise—that it didn't hurt to say it. "That's yours now. The doorway. I'm just the one who knows where the doors are."

* * *

Salt Row had changed its sound.

For twenty years the market had spoken in two registers: the loud commerce of fish and rope and salt, and beneath it the low murmur of the hidden trade, charms passed palm to palm, prices named in glances, the word *gifted* never spoken above a breath. Xanthe had spent most of her adult life inside that second register, smiling her merchant's smile while a storm sat coiled behind her ribs like a caged bird she fed on silence.

Now the whole street rang. Charm-sellers' stalls stood open-fronted in the sun, their wares hung out like festival bunting—weather-cords and hearth-luck and birthing knots and the little woven eyes fishermen still swore by even though half of them worked and half didn't and nobody could reliably tell which was which. A registration office had opened near the harbor bell, and it did what its name said and nothing more: names taken, gifts noted, a stamp of grey wax, and a pamphlet about responsible practice that everyone mocked and some people actually read. The hunters were gone. Serel's pale-eyed patrols were a story told in taverns now, told wrong, told with the Inquisitor variously dead, redeemed, or vanished into the mountain along with everything else that had come down when the Thorn shattered.

Xanthe knew which version was true. She'd been there. But she'd learned that a city needs its stories more than it needs its accuracy, and besides—the true version was hers, and she guarded it the way she'd once guarded her gift. Carefully. Privately. Only taking it out on nights when the moon was full and the thirteenth turn felt close enough to touch.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're looking at the market but you're actually looking at something else." Wren nodded across the square, toward the corner where the old crier's post still stood, its wood scarred where proclamation boards had been nailed up season after season. Someone had carved a small thorn-sigil into it and then someone else had tried to sand it off, and the ghost of both acts remained together, which seemed to Xanthe about as honest a monument as any. "Is it the mountain?"

"It's usually the mountain."

They'd pulled the court's ruins down through the summer and autumn, carting the black root-fragments out into the sunlight where they smoked and curled and died like things that had never been meant to see morning. The miners' tunnels had been sealed with the crown's own masons and the crown's own coin, which Corvin would have found funny, if Corvin had lived to find anything funny. His name was on the new memorial wall by the harbor gate, along with Pell's clerk-seal, mounted in glass, its wax face deliberately left blank. *For the debts we stopped keeping*, the plaque read. Pell had argued about the wording for six weeks. She'd won, mostly.

Ash's name wasn't on the wall. Ash's name was nowhere, because Ash had asked—begged, really, in the letter he'd sent from the coast town where he'd gone to be no one—that they let his daughter's soul-light be the only record of him. It rose with the others on the thirteenth turn, and that was the whole of his monument, and Xanthe thought about him sometimes without anger, which had taken nearly the full year to manage, and considered it a kind of craft, like knot-work. You tied the anger off. You anchored it. You let it hold something instead of letting it hold you.

"Come on," she said. "We'll lose the morning light, and your storm-knots want tying before noon. Everything does. Even the ones that aren't storms."

* * *

Her shop—if a rented room above a chandler's with a table in the window could be called a shop—sat two streets back from the water, where the smell of tar gave way to the smell of bread. Xanthe had chosen it for the light. Maren the Saltwife had chosen it for her, back in the winter when Xanthe had come back to Greyharbor with empty hands and emptier chest and no idea what a storm-caller did when the storms were done with her. Maren had looked her over the way she'd once looked over cargo worth stealing, and said, "Teach, then. The gifted are coming out of their cellars and half of them don't know a ward-knot from a hangman's noose. Teach."

So she taught.

The morning's lesson was anchors—how to finish a charm so it stayed finished, how to bind intent into fiber so that time and salt air couldn't unpick it. Wren had four other students now, younger, drawn from the tide of families who'd crept back to Greyharbor once the hunting stopped: a mason's boy whose gift was for stone and who sulked because knots weren't stones; twin sisters who finished each other's charms without meaning to; a quiet girl named Bette who hadn't spoken since her parents were taken in the last bad month of the hunt and returned this spring with memories missing like teeth, and who tied the neatest anchor loops Xanthe had ever seen.

Xanthe moved among them, correcting grips, untangling thread, telling the old stories that were also the lessons. *This crossing is for the wind. This one is for the person you're making it for. Never make a charm angry—it keeps.* The room filled with the small sounds of work, and sunlight crawled across the floorboards, and outside a fish-cart cried its wares in a voice that cracked on the high notes, and Xanthe stood in the middle of all of it feeling the particular peace of a woman whose hands are busy and whose chest is quiet.

It had taken her months to stop listening for the storm.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, losing magic. Not the power itself—that went like a tide going out, and she'd been too broken open on the mountain's floor to mourn it properly—but the *listening*. Twenty years of holding a storm behind her ribs had built a constant vigilance into her body, a braced readiness, and long after the storm was gone her body kept bracing, kept listening, kept waiting for the pressure to build. She would wake at night reaching for weather that wasn't there. She would stand at the harbor edge in a rising wind and feel, for one terrible heartbeat, absolutely nothing where everything used to live.

Then, slowly, the nothing had started to feel less like absence and more like room. Space. A cleared field. She was learning to plant things in it that didn't need lightning to grow.

"Wren." She caught the girl's eye across the table. "Show them the four-crossing again. Slowly. Bette's watching your anchor hand."

Wren demonstrated, tongue between her teeth, and Bette watched, and the mason's boy pretended not to watch and watched, and the twins tied their cords in perfect unison without noticing they'd done it, and Xanthe stepped back to the window and let the lesson run itself for a moment, because this—this exact moment—was the inheritance. Not the gift. The gift had been Isolde's first, and then hers, and then the storm's own, and the storm had carried it away into the thirteenth moon with everything else. But this passing-on, hand to hand, patient and unforced and *chosen*—this had never belonged to the magic at all. It had belonged to her sister, who had taught Xanthe her first knot at six years old on the doorstep of a house that no longer existed, guiding her clumsy fingers, saying *slower, little storm, slower*, laughing.

Slower, little storm.

She was, at last. She was learning to be.

* * *

At midday she sent the students out with bread and instructions not to tie anything ambitious until tomorrow, and Wren lingered, the way she always lingered, shuffling her cords.

"Spit it out," Xanthe said.

"The harbor festival. Next full moon. They want charm-sellers to do the blessing-line on the seawall. All of us, out in the open, together." Wren turned a storm-knot over in her fingers. "I said we'd come. I probably should have asked."

"You should have," Xanthe agreed. "And we'll come."

"They asked if you'd lead it. The line. Because—" Wren hesitated, and Xanthe watched her decide whether to say the rest, and loved her a little for hesitating. "Because of what you did. Everyone knows. The souls in the moonlight, the mountain singing—they sing a song about it already, it's terrible, the words don't scan."

"There's a song?"

"There are three songs. One of them is very rude about the crown." Wren grinned, then sobered. "Will you? Lead it?"

Xanthe looked past her, out the window, down toward the water where the seawall curved gray and patient around the harbor, where next full moon there would be a line of charm-sellers standing in the open with their knots held up to the light, no sleeves to hide in, no smiles worn as armor. A year ago the idea would have been unthinkable. Five years ago it would have been a death sentence. And here it was, being offered to her by a fourteen-year-old with blue-stained fingers, as casually as an errand.

"I'll think about it," she said, which meant yes, and Wren knew it, and rolled her eyes the way the young do when the old insist on pretending to deliberate.

After she'd gone, clattering down the stairs two at a time, Xanthe sat alone in the warm room with her threads spread out before her, and reached for the box under the table.

It was a plain box, cedar, unremarkable, and it held the only object she had carried out of the mountain besides her own life: a single charm, salt-crusted, sea-faded, its knot-work fine as spider silk despite twenty years of wear. Isolde's work. The charm that had come to her through a dying woman in Hollowmere and a girl named Wren, the thread that had pulled her whole life apart and rewoven it into whatever shape it was now. She had hung it in the vault's darkness once, briefly, beside the soul-light that had been her sister, and the light had flared toward it like a moth toward a lamp, and Xanthe had understood in that moment that the charm was not a trinket. It was a door. It had always been a door. Her sister had knotted a doorway into string and sent it out into the world to wait for her.

She took it out now and held it in the window light. The fibers were soft with age. The warmth she'd once sensed in Wren's crooked copies was here too—or she believed it was, which amounted to the same thing, and had for a year.

There was one knot left in her that she'd been saving. Not a storm-knot, not a ward, not any pattern in any book. She'd been working it out slowly all winter, a few crossings at a time, the way you tell a story you're not sure you're allowed to finish. It was a charm for the dead, which was a foolish thing to make, because the dead need nothing and buy nothing and the market has no stall for them. It was a charm for one dead woman specifically, and it was tied from the memory of her hands.

Xanthe finished it now. Three crossings, then four—for the storm, for the sister who'd bought the storm's exile with her own soul—then the anchor loop, slow and sure, *slower, little storm*. She breathed on it, the way Isolde had breathed on every charm she'd ever made, giving it the warmth of a living lung. Then she climbed the narrow stair to the roof, where the chandler let her keep a line strung between two posts, and the harbor lay below her bright and loud and alive, gulls thieving, bells ringing, songs with unscannable words drifting up from the taverns.

She hung the charm on the line, alone at the end, where the wind off the sea could get at it.

"For you," she said. Not a prayer. Just a report, the kind sisters give each other. *The market's loud. The girl is gifted and stubborn and I think you'd like her. I'm slower now. I'm learning.*

The wind took the charm and swung it, and the light caught the faded thread and ran along it silver, just for a moment, just the way moonlight catches a soul rising—and Xanthe laughed, once, wet-eyed, and called it coincidence, and knew better, and went down the stairs to her students with the smell of salt and cedar on her hands, into the loud and living afternoon, where the work waited and the doors were open and the storm, wherever it had gone, was finally, finally at peace.
