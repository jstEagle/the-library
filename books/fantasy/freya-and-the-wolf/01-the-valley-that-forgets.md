## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goat was the last straw.

Not the goat itself — Paloma had made her peace with goats years ago, in the way one makes peace with weather or taxes. It was what Maren wanted done with the goat that finally tipped the scales.

"You'll need to kiss it," Maren had said the evening before, not looking up from her mortar, as though announcing supper.

"Kiss it."

"On the nose. A proper kiss, mind, not a peck. The rheumatism charm won't take otherwise."

"Maren, I have ground nettle for you every morning for three years. I have stirred potions with my own finger when we ran out of spoons. I once held a screaming chicken upside down over a cauldron while it cursed me in whatever language chickens curse in. But I am not kissing the goat."

Maren had looked up then, and her eyes — pale as pond water, sharp as fishhooks — had held Paloma's a moment too long. "No," she'd said softly. "I don't suppose you will." And she'd kissed the goat herself, muttering, and the goat had looked smug about it for the rest of the evening, and Paloma had lain awake in the loft listening to the wind come down off the hills and thinking: *three years, and I still flinch when she looks at me like that.*

So it was the goat, in the end. Or rather, it was everything the goat stood for, which was a life spent kissing things she didn't want to kiss.

She left before dawn.

* * *

Packing took no time at all, because Paloma owned almost nothing. A spare dress, patched at both elbows and one knee. A comb missing four teeth. A bundle of dried herbs she'd picked herself, which was less a supply than a habit — her hands simply did it now, the way other people's hands drummed on tables. Half a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth. And the small wooden bird Maren had carved for her the first winter, when Paloma had cried herself sick from homesickness for a home she couldn't remember.

She'd hesitated over the bird. She hesitated over it still, standing in the grey light of the doorway with her bundle under her arm, looking back at the cottage with its crooked chimney and its garden gone gloriously to weed.

"Take it or leave it," she told herself sternly. "But decide, because the sun's coming up and you look like a burglar."

She took it. She told herself this meant nothing.

The note she left on the table read: *Gone. Don't follow. The nettle bed wants cutting. — P.*

Then, feeling this was perhaps too curt for three years of roof and board, she added: *Thank you. I'm sorry. Both are true.*

Then, feeling *that* was too honest, she very nearly crossed it out, but the sky was going pink at the edges and cowardice is a poor use of good ink, so she left it and walked out into the dawn with her heart hammering like a woodpecker with an opinion.

Maren slept like the dead. Everyone said so. Everyone also said Maren had buried two husbands and outlived three tax collectors, and that no one who slept that soundly did so innocently. Paloma had checked, more than once, for wards on the doorframe. There were none. There had never been any. Which meant either Maren trusted her completely —

— or Maren had never needed wards at all.

Paloma chose not to think about that, and was extremely successful at it for nearly half a mile.

* * *

The road east wound down through gorse and bracken, following a stream that chattered to itself like an old woman doing accounts. Paloma knew these hills the way she knew her own hands: the lightning-split oak where the crows held their parliament, the flat stone where she'd eaten her lunch every fine day for three years, the hollow where she'd once found a fox kit and been bitten for her trouble.

She would miss them. That was the thing nobody warned you about leaving — you didn't miss people only. You missed stones.

By midmorning she could see it: the pass between two shoulders of hill, and beyond it, the Stillwater Valley, lying green and broad as a held breath. Travelers spoke of it the way they spoke of deep water — respectfully, and not too near mealtime. Nothing grew strange there, they said. No charms held. No curses stuck. Hedge-witches gave the valley a wide berth and muttered about "dead air," and kings' men rode around it rather than through, though no king's edict required it.

Magic could not follow you into Stillwater. That was the whole of its fame, and it was exactly why Paloma was walking toward it at a pace just short of running.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, not even Maren — *especially* not Maren:

Paloma Thackeray had never cast a spell in her life. Not one. Not a cantrip, not a charm, not so much as a luck-knot.

Three years apprenticed to a hedge-witch, and her magic had never once answered her. Oh, it was *there*. She could feel it the way you feel a tooth that hasn't quite decided to ache — a low, waiting hum behind her breastbone, worse some days than others. When Maren worked charms, Paloma's skin prickled and the candle flames leaned toward her like sunflowers after the sun. Once, aged nineteen, she'd gotten angry enough to shout, and every glass jar in the cottage had cracked in the same instant, neat as a line of soldiers fainting.

Maren had swept up the glass without a word and never mentioned it again, which was somehow more frightening than a scolding.

"It doesn't answer me," Paloma had finally confessed, in her second winter, staring into the fire. "Maybe I haven't any. Maybe you've wasted your bread on me."

Maren had poked the fire and said, "It answers. Just not to *you*." And when Paloma pressed — because of course she pressed; she was young and the fire was warm and pressing was what she did — Maren had only said, "Some doors open from the other side, girl. Stop rattling the handle before you break something."

Paloma had broken several things since. Hence the valley.

Because if magic couldn't follow her into Stillwater — if the whole valley was warded by whatever old, patient power kept it silent — then maybe the hum would finally stop. Maybe she could walk through those boundary stones and be, for the first time in her remembered life, simply a woman. An ordinary woman with an ordinary pulse and no low waiting thunder in her chest, no leaning candles, no cracking jars, no door rattling from the other side.

Freedom. That was the word for it. She turned it over as she climbed, testing it like a coin bitten for gold.

* * *

The boundary stones stood where all maps agreed they should: a ring of five grey monoliths across the neck of the pass, older than any kingdom, furred with moss, each carved — if carving was the word — with a spiral worn nearly smooth. Locals called them the Five Fingers. Nobody local would touch them. Paloma had asked why, once, at a market stall, and the fruit-seller had looked at her as if she'd asked to lick the moon.

"They're just stones," Paloma had said.

"Aye," the fruit-seller had agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing that the sea is just water.

Now she stood before them with the morning wind at her back and her bundle under her arm, and they were just stones, grey and patient and faintly ridiculous, like five old men who'd fallen asleep standing up and been too embarrassed to mention it.

"Well," she said aloud, because talking to yourself is free and no one out here to hear it. "Here goes nothing."

She stepped between the second and third fingers.

Nothing happened. That was, in fact, precisely what happened: nothing, and then more nothing, arriving in waves.

She felt it at forty paces. The hum behind her breastbone — her constant, uninvited companion, the tooth-ache of the soul — went quiet. Not faded. *Quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone leaves it. One moment the low waiting thunder; the next, a silence so complete she stopped walking altogether and stood in the middle of the road with her mouth open, startling a sheep.

"Oh," she said.

And then, because relief is a strange animal that often arrives wearing grief's coat, she burst into tears.

* * *

She cried for a shamefully long time, sitting on a milestone with the sheep judging her from a distance. She cried for the hills behind her, and the crooked chimney, and Maren's terrible nettle tea, and the fox kit, and the lightning-split oak, and the fact that she could not remember why any of it hurt, only that it did, the way an old scar aches in rain. She cried because the silence in her chest was the most beautiful thing she had ever felt and she could not enjoy it without wondering what it had cost, and whom.

Then she blew her nose on her sleeve, informed the sheep that they'd seen nothing, and walked on into the valley with her chin up, because a woman can do anything for ten minutes if she lies to herself convincingly enough.

The Stillwater Valley was, in fairness, lovely. Rolling green country, hedged and fielded, with a river running down its spine so slow and clear it seemed less to flow than to *consider*. The villages were tidy. The people were round-faced and unhurried. Everything smelled of cut grass and woodsmoke and bread, and nothing anywhere leaned toward her, crackled near her, or whispered behind her ribs.

It was perfect. It was peaceful. It was, she realized somewhere around noon, the exact texture of exile, dressed up in Sunday clothes.

"Cheer up," she muttered. "You wanted ordinary. Ordinary is what's on offer. Take it before the shop closes."

* * *

The Waystation inn sat where the east road met the river road, a broad, sagging building with a sign depicting a horse that might, generously, have been a large dog. The paint read THE TIRED TRAVELER, though someone had amended it in charcoal to THE VERY TIRED TRAVELER, and Paloma felt this was the most honest advertising she'd ever seen.

Inside smelled of stew, tallow, and decades of pipe smoke soaked into the beams. Behind the bar stood a woman built like a well-stocked pantry, arms folded, surveying Paloma with the frank appraisal of someone who has fed many strangers and been cheated by at least nine of them.

"You're not from here," the woman said.

"No one from here is from here, eventually," Paloma said. "I'm looking for work. I can cook, clean, mend, keep accounts, and I make a nettle tea that'll cure anything except honesty."

A man at the end of the bar snorted into his ale.

The woman's eyes narrowed. "What's wrong with you, then? Girls who run usually have a reason. Husband? Debt? Baby?"

"Just a mistress," Paloma said. "The magical kind. I've retired."

That got a laugh from the bar, and something in the woman's face loosened by a degree. "Hedge-witch, were you? Well. We're none of us fancy here." She stuck out a hand like a ham. "Bett Halloway. My cook ran off with a tinker in spring, my pot-girl's got the ague, and my regulars eat like it's their last night on earth every single night. Can you start now?"

"I can start yesterday."

"Yesterday's taken. Start now."

And that was that. Paloma Thackeray, fugitive from nothing anyone could name, tied on an apron in the Very Tired Traveler and began hauling bowls of stew to men who thanked her by nodding, which she was beginning to understand was local for *deep gratitude*.

* * *

The work was good. That surprised her. Her hands knew labor the way they knew herb-picking — without being asked — and the rhythm of it filled the hours: chop, stir, carry, wipe, carry, laugh politely at the joke about the tinker (told nightly, always funnier to the teller), bank the fire, carry, wipe, sleep.

Best of all was the quiet. Every night she lay in the little attic room under the eaves and listened to her own heartbeat, plain and unaccompanied, and fell asleep smiling.

Every night, that is, except she didn't quite. Because in the space between waking and sleeping, in that border country where the mind stops minding, she sometimes heard it: a faint, far hum, like a song played in the next house over. Not in her chest anymore. *Underneath.* Under the floorboards, under the fields, under the slow considering river. As if the valley hadn't silenced the music so much as muffled it — drawn a blanket over a cage and called it quiet.

She told herself it was the river. Rivers hum. Everyone knows this.

She told herself lots of things, that first fortnight. That Maren wouldn't come looking (true, probably; Maren had kissed the goat and said nothing, and Maren's silences were long as sermons). That the ache behind her breastbone was homesickness and would fade (it didn't, quite). That she was free.

She was free. She was also, she admitted one evening while scrubbing pots, a woman who had fled three hundred miles to escape a door, and had arrived to find the door had come with her, folded up small and hidden under the floorboards of her sleep.

"That's cheerful," said Bett, passing through the kitchen. "You've got the face of a woman arguing with God and losing."

"Just arguing with the pots," Paloma said.

"Pots win," Bett said. "They always win. More stew?"

There was always more stew. It was, Paloma thought, handing out bowl number forty of the evening, a strangely bearable life. Ordinary. Quiet. Safe.

She believed it right up until the storm.

* * *

It came in off the western hills on the seventeenth night, the kind of storm that seems personally offended by roofs. Rain hit the windows like thrown gravel; the Very Tired Traveler's beams groaned; the fire spat and sulked. Bett barred the door early and grumbled about the state of the thatch, and the regulars drank one more round than wisdom advised, and Paloma banked the ovens and thought, not for the first time: *this is fine. This is my life now. Pots and stew and storms, and nothing under the floorboards but mice.*

Which was when the pounding started at the door.

Not knocking. Pounding — the fist of someone past politeness, past fear, running on the last spoonful of himself. Bett frowned. The regulars went quiet. The wind howled, and beneath it came a voice, ragged and cracking:

"Open — in the king's — open the door—"

"In the king's *what*?" muttered the tinker-joke man. "Man sounds half-drowned."

Bett unbolted the door a hand's width, and the storm shoved it wide, and in staggered a man in a rain-black cloak — and beneath the cloak, glimpsed as he pitched forward, the grey-and-crimson livery of the usurper's household, torn at the shoulder and soaked through with something darker than rain.

He made it two steps. His eyes found Paloma across the room — found her, fixed on her, with an expression she had no name for yet and would spend many nights learning — and he said one word, hoarse as a crow:

"*Thackeray.*"

Then his knees quit, and he collapsed at her feet in a spreading pool of rainwater and blood, and outside, far off down the east road, thunder rolled — or hoofbeats, it was impossible to say which, and getting less possible by the minute.

Paloma stood very still, holding a soup ladle, and thought with perfect clarity: *I came here to be no one.*

The man bled on her clean floor, and the storm screamed, and somewhere in her chest — impossibly, in the valley where nothing answered — something knocked.
