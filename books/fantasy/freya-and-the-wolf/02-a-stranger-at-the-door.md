## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The storm came up the valley like a debt collector.

Paloma felt it first in her teeth — a pressure, a wrongness in the air that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with the particular way Stillwater weather announced itself. She was hauling water from the well when the wind changed, and by the time she'd latched the shutters on the inn's east side, the sky had gone the color of a bruise and the first fat raindrops were striking the flagstones hard enough to bounce.

"Bar's closed early," Hesk announced from behind his counter, to no one in particular, because there was no one in particular left to hear it. The last carters had rolled out an hour ago, bound for the low farms before the ford swelled. Now it was just the two of them, the fire, and a storm that sounded increasingly like it intended to come inside whether invited or not.

"You say that every time it rains," Paloma said, setting the bucket down.

"And every time it rains, I'm right." Hesk was a broad man built like a door that had been left out in all weathers, with a beard he claimed held secrets and everyone else claimed held crumbs. He squinted at her over the counter. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to tell me something I don't want to hear."

"I'm going to tell you the well cover needs mending."

"That's not what I don't want to hear, but fine. Tomorrow."

She liked Hesk. That was the trouble with staying anywhere longer than a week — you started liking people, and liking people was a door, and doors let things in. She'd been at the waystation inn six weeks now, scrubbing pots and hauling water and learning exactly how much personality a man could wring from the phrase *bar's closed*. Six weeks was too long. She knew it. She kept not leaving anyway, and told herself it was because winter was coming, which was true, and not because the valley's silence felt less like exile every day, which was also true, and more dangerous.

Here was the thing about Stillwater Valley that no one put on any map: magic didn't work here. Not *wouldn't* — *couldn't*, the way a candle couldn't burn underwater. Paloma had crossed the boundary stones on a gray dawn eight weeks ago with Maren's voice still ringing in her ears and three years of hedge-craft coiled under her skin, and the moment her boot passed the last stone, all of it had guttered out like a wet wick. The little warmth she carried behind her breastbone — the witch-warmth, Maren called it, the spark that could coax a fever down or find water under stone — simply wasn't there. She'd stood between the stones and pressed her palm to the earth and felt nothing but mud.

She had cried, a little. Then she had laughed, a lot. Then she had walked into the valley and gotten a job.

Because here was the other thing: if magic couldn't follow her into Stillwater, then neither could anything sent *by* magic. No scrying. No tracking charms. No whispering hexes riding the wind to find the runaway apprentice who'd stolen — borrowed, *borrowed* — a certain book. In the valley, Paloma Thackeray was just a girl with good arms and a suspicious talent for knowing when bread was about to turn, which she attributed to "a good nose" and never, ever to anything else.

Invisibility through mundanity. It was working beautifully.

Which is why, when the knock came, she should have done what Hesk did — folded her arms and said *bar's closed* and meant it.

* * *

The knock arrived between one thunderclap and the next, three blows so heavy they rattled the latch. Hesk looked up from his ledger. Paloma looked up from the pot she'd been scouring.

"We're closed," Hesk called, without moving. "Come back when the sky's finished its tantrum."

The knock came again, weaker. And then — a sound Paloma would hear in her sleep for years afterward — a body sliding down the other side of the door, cloth scraping wood, ending in a dull, final thump.

Hesk sighed the sigh of a man whose evening has just been murdered. "If that's Tolly drunk again—"

He hauled the door open, and the storm reached in with both hands, snuffing two candles and flinging rain across the floorboards. On the step, half in and half out, lay a man.

Not Tolly. Tolly was a beanpole shepherd who fell asleep standing up and had once been found in the pig pen explaining himself to no one. This man was broad, muddy to the eyebrows, and dressed — Paloma's stomach dropped before her mind caught up — in the dark green and black of Aldric's usurper's livery, though the tabard was torn nearly off him and soaked past identifying. There was blood, too. A lot of it, and not all of it washed on by rain. It had come from somewhere under his left arm, and it was coming fast.

"He's bleeding out on my step," Hesk said, in the tone of a man cataloguing inconveniences. "On my *new* step."

"You said the step was forty years old."

"It's new to *him*."

Lightning lit the yard white. In the flash, Paloma saw the road beyond the gate — empty, churned to porridge — and something else, further off, that might have been riders or might have been trees arguing with the wind. Her skin prickled. Old habit. Except here, in the valley, the prickling was only prickling; there was no craft behind it, no second sight, just a girl's nerves doing a witch's job.

"Hesk." She crouched by the man. Up close he was younger than the livery suggested — twenty-five, maybe, with a farmer's calluses under the soldier's dirt, which made no sense at all. His lips were gray. "He'll die on your step. That's worse for business than mud."

"Mud dries. Corpses linger." But Hesk was already grunting his way down to take the man's other side, because for all his grumbling, Hesk had never once in six weeks let anyone go cold or hungry under his roof, and Paloma suspected he never would. "Get his legs. Mind the — *hells*, that's a lot of blood."

They got him inside and laid him by the fire, and the storm slammed the door behind them as if pleased with itself.

* * *

The argument happened while Paloma cut the ruined sleeve away.

It went like this: Hesk wanted him out. Out into the barn, at least, or the woodshed, or failing that, propped against the step he'd bled on, since he seemed fond of it. The man wore Aldric's colors, and Aldric's men meant Aldric's patrols, and Aldric's patrols meant questions, and questions meant trouble, and Hesk had come to Stillwater Valley specifically because trouble had a famously poor sense of direction here.

"He's dying," Paloma said.

"People do that. It's very common."

"Not on my floor."

"*Your* floor?" Hesk's eyebrows climbed into territory his hairline had abandoned years ago. "Whose name's over the door?"

"Nobody's. It's painted on a board that blew down in spring. You nailed it above the privy by mistake." She tied off the tourniquet — a strip of the man's own cloak, boiled clean because Paloma boiled everything, a habit Maren had beaten into her alongside the herb-lore — and pressed down hard until the man groaned. Under her hands, his blood was hot. Too hot. Fever-hot, and rising, and beneath the heat she could feel something else, something wrong, a wrongness in the wound itself that her fingers recognized even though her valley-silenced gift couldn't reach for it.

Poison. Or something wearing poison's clothes.

Her hands knew what to do. That was the horror of it. Three years of Maren's training lived in her fingers independent of any magic: yarrow and moss for the packing, willow bark for the fever, and for whatever was crawling in the blood — she didn't know. Couldn't know. Here in the dead air of Stillwater, she was just a girl with herbs and hope, and hope was running out of the man faster than the blood was.

"So we agree," Hesk was saying. "Barn."

"He won't survive the barn."

"He won't survive my taproom either, if a patrol rides through that door and finds me harboring a deserter in usurper's green. You know what they do to houses that shelter runaways? They do it to the house, Paloma. The *house*."

She did know. Everyone in the valley knew. It was the price of the valley's peace: stay out of it, and it stays out of you. Neutral ground only stays neutral if nobody picks up a sword on it.

And Paloma Thackeray had spent eight weeks being nobody, doing nothing, wanting nothing but to be left alone by kings and witches alike. She had a system. The system worked. The system required that when a bleeding soldier in enemy colors collapsed at her feet, she stepped over him and went back to her pots.

She looked down at him. He'd opened his eyes — gray eyes, unfocused, swimming — and his hand had found her wrist with the grip of a drowning man finding driftwood. His lips moved. No sound came out. He tried again, and this time she leaned close enough to catch it, one word, cracked and desperate:

"*Please.*"

Not *help me*. Not *don't let them take me*. Just — please. Aimed at her like she was someone specific. Like he'd been looking for someone specific, and the storm had blown him to the wrong door, or the right one.

Later, Paloma would try to explain to herself why she did what she did next, and she would fail, because there wasn't a reason. There was just a crack, running through eight weeks of careful neutrality, thin as a hair and sudden as lightning.

"Hesk," she said. "The cellar."

"The cellar is where I keep the beer."

"The cellar is where you keep the beer *and* where no patrol will look, because you'll swear up and down he was never here, and Hesk — you lie beautifully. I've watched you tell farmers their ale isn't watered. You have a gift."

"My ale *isn't*—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands. Outside, the thunder rolled long and low, like the sky clearing its throat before making an announcement. "You understand what you're asking. If they find him, they find us. Both of us. They don't ask whose idea the cellar was."

"They won't find him."

"How can you know that?"

Because magic can't follow me here, she almost said, and swallowed it. Because I'm invisible. Because I've spent eight weeks becoming furniture, and furniture doesn't get interrogated. Instead she said, "Because I'm going to be very boring, and you're going to be very rude, and between us we'll bore them into leaving."

Hesk stared at her for a long moment. Rain hammered the shutters. The man by the fire shivered and bled and burned.

"You're a strange girl, Paloma," he said finally. "I've thought so since the day you walked in asking for work and flinched when I said the word 'witch' like I'd thrown something at you." He hauled himself upright with a groan that was mostly theater. "Cellar. Behind the casks. And if he dies down there, you're digging the hole, because I've got a bad back and a reputation."

"You have a bad back and a heart, is what you have."

"Don't spread that around." He was already fetching the lantern. "I've got a reputation for that too."

* * *

Getting him down the cellar stairs took the two of them and cost Paloma a wrenched shoulder and most of her dignity. They laid him on the pallet usually reserved for Hesk's cousin's occasional drunken visits, wedged in the deep corner behind the ale casks where the lantern light barely reached. Paloma packed the wound with yarrow and moss, forced willow-bark tea between gray lips, and did everything her hands remembered and her magic couldn't help with.

The poison — if it was poison — didn't care. The fever climbed.

"Stay with me," she muttered, wringing out a cloth. "Come on. You didn't crawl all the way through that storm to die behind a beer barrel. That's a stupid place to die. Even Tolly would say so, and Tolly once slept in a trough."

The man's eyes fluttered. Found her. Focused, this time — focused on her face with an intensity that raised the hair on her arms, because it wasn't the look a dying man gives whoever happens to be nearest. It was the look of a man checking a map against the territory.

"You," he whispered.

"Yes, me. Drink this."

"No." His hand crawled across the blanket, caught her sleeve. "Saw you. At the stones. Weeks — weeks ago. Knew it was — knew—"

Ice walked down her spine. She had crossed the boundary stones at dawn, alone, certain no one saw. Certain *nothing* could see, not here, not through the valley's blind silence.

"You're confused," she said firmly. "Fever talking."

"Fever's honest," he breathed, and something that might have been a laugh broke apart in his chest. "That's why they — that's why Vane uses—"

Boots overhead. Heavy ones. Several sets.

Paloma blew out the lantern so fast she singed her fingers.

Through the floorboards came the muffled percussion of the front door opening, Hesk's voice pitched to its maximum register of aggrieved inconvenience — "*closed, closed, can't you read, oh, the board's fallen down, well, it's CLOSED*" — and then another voice underneath it, smooth and dry as paper, saying words Paloma couldn't make out but whose shape she understood perfectly.

Questions.

The wounded man had gone rigid beside her. In the dark, his hand found hers and gripped it, and she felt him trembling, and she realized she was trembling too, and that some traitorous part of her — the part that had crossed those stones and cried and laughed and hired herself out to a beer-stained innkeeper — was whispering that this was what she'd run from, this exact moment, this knocking and these boots and this voice, and that hiding in a cellar with a dying stranger was not neutrality at all.

Neutrality was a door. She'd just picked a side of it.

Above them, the smooth voice said something short. Hesk's answer boomed through the boards, indignant, magnificent, a masterclass: "*Soldiers? Here? Haven't seen a soldier since the war, and HE was lost—*"

Then footsteps. Crossing the taproom. Stopping directly overhead.

Paloma held her breath. Beside her, in the dark, the dying man pressed the map-shaped thing he'd been clutching since the doorstep — she'd noticed it, assumed it was a pouch, thought nothing of it — into her hands, folding her fingers over it with terrible, deliberate strength.

"Keep it," he whispered. "When I'm gone. Don't let them — don't let *anyone*—"

The floorboards creaked. The boots turned. Went back toward the taproom, unhurried, patient as winter.

The man exhaled against her ear, one word, soft as ash falling:

"*Freya.*"

And then the fever took him under, and upstairs, the smooth voice began, gently, to ask Hesk about his cellar.
