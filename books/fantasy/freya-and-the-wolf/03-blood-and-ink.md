## 3. Blood and Ink

The storm came in off the western hills like a debt collector — loud, insistent, and impossible to ignore.

Paloma felt it in her teeth before she heard it, which was a new and unwelcome development. In the Stillwater Valley she wasn't supposed to *feel* anything. That was the whole point of the place. Magic couldn't follow past the boundary stones; it pooled outside the valley like rain against a window. For three weeks she had been gloriously, suspiciously ordinary, and ordinary people did not ache when weather turned.

She was hauling a second bucket of water from the well when the first thunderclap rolled over the inn, and the ache in her jaw sharpened into something that felt almost like a warning. She stood in the courtyard getting soaked, bucket forgotten in her hand, and thought: *Maren would say this is an omen.*

Maren said everything was an omen. Maren had once declared the omen of a sparrow landing on the woodpile to be "moderately inconvenient, financially." Paloma missed her with a ferocity that surprised her every time it surfaced, the way a bruise surprises you when you bump it.

"Girl!" Brannock's voice bellowed from the inn door. "Are you watering the yard or yourself? Either way, inside!"

Brannock was the innkeeper of the Drowned Goose, a broad red-faced man who treated his staff the way he treated his stew — with a great deal of salt and occasional unexpected tenderness. Paloma ducked inside, dripping, and set the bucket by the hearth.

"That's the last of the water," she said. "The well handle's sticking again."

"I'll oil it tomorrow." He squinted at her. "You look pale."

"It's the storm."

"It's always the storm with you." He jerked his chin toward the common room. "Go mind the taproom. Half the valley's come in out of the wet, and Hesta's already poured three wrong orders."

The taproom of the Drowned Goose on a storm night was a study in cheerful misery. Farmers sat steaming by the fire, complaining about their crops in the satisfied tones of people whose crops were, in fact, fine. A wool merchant argued with his own reflection in the window. Two children chased each other under the tables until their mother caught one by the collar without looking up from her ale.

Paloma wove through them with her tray, and for a little while the ache in her teeth faded under the comfortable noise of it all. This was what she'd wanted, she reminded herself. Warmth. Noise. Work that ended when the candles guttered. No hedge-witch mistress waking her at midnight because the moon was in the wrong house. No potions gone sour. No knowing, too-long looks from women who could smell magic like smoke.

Just a girl named Paloma Thackeray, serving ale in a valley where nothing strange could ever find her.

She was so busy believing this that she nearly dropped the tray when the door blew open.

* * *

It didn't open so much as surrender. The wind took it clean out of Hesta's hands and slammed it against the wall, and the lamplight bent sideways, and into that sideways light staggered a man.

He wore grey and black — the livery of the usurper's household, cut fine even soaked through, though the fine cut did nothing to hide the way he moved. Wrong. Lurching. One hand pressed to his side, and between his fingers, black in the lamplight, something that was not entirely blood.

He made it three steps into the room.

Then his knees went, and he came down like a felled tree, right at Paloma's feet, close enough that she felt the floor shudder through her boots.

The taproom went silent in the particular way rooms do when something dramatic happens — everyone frozen mid-chew, mid-sip, mid-lie. The wool merchant stopped arguing with himself. One of the children peeked out from under a table and said, with the frank delight of the very young, "He's dead."

"He is not dead," Paloma said, kneeling, because apparently her hands had decided this before her brain got a vote. "Someone fetch Brannock. And hot water. And — brandy, if we have it."

"We don't have brandy," Hesta said from behind the bar. "We have gin. Terrible gin."

"Gin, then."

"And I'll thank you not to order my stock around, girl—"

"Hesta." Brannock filled the doorway, took in the scene in one long look: the livery, the wound, the spreading dark on his clean floor. His face did several things in quick succession, none of them hospitable. "That's a king's man."

"That's a dying man," Paloma said.

"Dying men in that livery bring questions. Questions bring soldiers. Soldiers bring—" He waved a hand that encompassed taxes, requisitions, burned barns, and other misfortunes too numerous to list. "Out. Both of you. Drag him back into the storm he came from."

"He'll die."

"People die in storms. It's what storms are for."

Paloma looked up at him, and something in her face must have changed, because Brannock's bluster faltered. Later she would try to work out what he'd seen. She only knew that underneath her fear there was a hard little knot of certainty, and certainty has a look to it that even innkeepers respect.

"He crawled here," she said quietly. "In this. Nobody crawls somewhere by accident."

The silence stretched. Outside, thunder walked across the hills like something enormous changing its mind.

"Cellar," Brannock said at last, disgusted, as if the word were being extracted from him with pincers. "The old cellar, behind the casks. If anyone asks, we've never seen him. If he dies down there, you're digging the hole yourself."

"Fair," said Paloma, who had already gotten her shoulder under the man's arm.

* * *

The old cellar was cold and smelled of stone and years. Paloma got him onto a pallet of sacking near the far wall, and by the time she'd fetched the terrible gin, a lamp, and every clean rag the inn possessed, she was able to look at him properly.

Younger than the livery suggested. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair plastered to his forehead, a face that might once have been handsome in an official-portrait sort of way, now grey as ash. The wound was a slash along his ribs, deep, and the flesh around it had gone an ugly bruised violet threaded with black lines, like ink spilled under skin.

Poison. Maren had taught her poisons in her third year — mostly so Paloma would know which mushrooms not to put in soup, but also, in one memorable lesson, "so you can recognize the work of someone better than you, which will be most people." The black lines spread outward from the cut like roots. Whatever was in him, it was winning.

"You're going to tell me you can help me," the man rasped.

His eyes were open. Grey eyes, fever-bright, fixed on her with an intensity that made her want to step backward up the stairs.

"I'm going to tell you I can keep you comfortable," Paloma said. "There's a difference, and lying to dying men seems rude."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "Honest. Good. Honest people are rare in my line of work." He tried to push himself up and failed, breath whistling. "Listen to me. There isn't much time. Not because of this—" he touched the wound, matter-of-fact as a man noting the weather "—but because they're coming. Riders. Grey cloaks. You'll hear the horses before you see them, and by then they'll be in the village."

"The usurper's men? Here?" Paloma frowned. "This is the Stillwater Valley. Nothing follows anyone in here. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," he agreed. "Which is why I came. Which is why *she* sent me here." He caught her wrist. His grip was fever-hot and shockingly strong. "I need you to listen, because I'm going to start forgetting words soon, and I can't afford to forget the important ones."

"Who's *she*?"

But he was already rummaging at his chest with clumsy fingers, dragging something out from inside his sodden coat. A leather case, flat, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord. He pressed it into her hands with both of his, folding her fingers over it like a man closing a book he never wanted opened again.

"Take this. Don't read it tonight. Don't show anyone. When you understand what it is — and you will understand, sooner than you'd like — take it east. Out of the valley. To the free cities. Someone at the river ford will be looking for it. A scout. Red hair, bad temper, answers to Tamsin."

"This is theft," Paloma said faintly. "Or treason. Possibly both."

"It's a delivery," he said. "The last one I'll ever make." He sagged back against the wall, and for a moment she thought he'd lost consciousness, but his eyes found hers again, and there was something in them that frightened her more than the poison did. Recognition. Not of her face — of something behind it.

"What?" she said. "What are you looking at?"

"You have her hands," he whispered.

"Whose hands?"

"Your father's hands. The cartographer's hands." His breath caught, rattled. "He drew it. He drew all of it. Every road, every gate, every name. He hid the truest thing he ever made inside a lie about a map, and then he hid *you* inside the valley, because he knew — he *knew* — that one day the ink would need a keeper, and the keeper would need to be someone the magic couldn't touch until it was time."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "You're raving. Fever. Poison does this."

"He used to hum when he worked," the man went on, relentless, sinking. "Off-key. Some song about a wolf. And he'd say — he'd say, *the map remembers what the world wants to forget.*" His hand slid down her wrist, caught her fingers one last time. "Freya."

The word went through her like a needle finding a nerve she hadn't known she had.

Not *Paloma*. Not any name she'd been called, not Maren's pet names, not Brannock's bellowed *girl*. Something older, buried deeper, in a place in her that had no words but recognized this one the way a lock recognizes a key. For half a heartbeat she was five years old and someone was lifting her onto a workbench scattered with pens and paper, and there was humming, off-key, and the smell of ink —

And then it was gone, and she was kneeling in a cold cellar holding a package she suddenly couldn't feel her own fingers around.

"My name is Paloma," she said. Her voice sounded very far away.

"Is it?" He smiled, and there was blood on his lips now, thin and dark. "Funny. So was mine, once. Names are coats. We wear what keeps us warm." His eyes drifted toward the ceiling, toward the storm above. "They're coming. Grey cloaks. The tall one — Vane — he doesn't shout, he doesn't hurry, he just... arrives. Like rot. Don't let him touch the map. Don't let him touch *you*. There's a relic — he carries it — it doesn't kill you, it's worse, it takes the—"

He coughed. The cough went on too long.

"Hey." She shook his shoulder. "Hey. Takes the what?"

His eyes came back to hers, and for a moment they cleared, sharp and urgent and terribly young.

"Takes the *remembering*," he said. "Guard your memories, Freya. They're the only door he hasn't found."

Then the light in him guttered — she watched it go, the way you watch a candle drown in its own wax — and his head tipped back against the stone, and the man who had crawled through a storm to die at her feet was simply not there anymore.

* * *

Paloma sat in the dark for a long time.

She was aware, distantly, of doing sensible things. Closing his eyes. Taking the lamp and checking, twice, that no one else was hiding in the cellar's shadows. Wrapping the leather case in the oilcloth again and then, after a moment's thought, stuffing it down inside her shirt, against her skin, where it lay cold and heavy as a second heart.

Upstairs, the storm still roared. She climbed the stairs on legs that belonged to someone else.

The taproom had emptied somewhat — farmers gone home between gusts, children carried off asleep — but Brannock and Hesta were still there, and they both looked up when she came in, and she saw the answer arrive on their faces before she said a word.

"Well?" Brannock asked.

"He's dead."

Hesta closed her eyes briefly. Brannock swore, softly, thoroughly, with the fluency of a man who'd been swearing since before Paloma was born.

"Right," he said. "Right. Back cellar, then, like I said. Digging's easier after rain, silver lining. We'll tell folk he staggered off into the night, mad with fever, poor devil. Nobody'll question—"

The knock came at the door.

Three knocks. Unhurried. Evenly spaced. The kind of knocking that had never once needed to break anything down, because everything always opened.

Nobody in the taproom moved. The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof like a thousand small fists.

"Expecting anyone?" Paloma whispered.

"No," Brannock breathed. "No one rides in this."

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Same patience.

Brannock looked at Hesta. Hesta looked at the ceiling, invoking whatever gods innkeepers keep. And Paloma stood very still in the middle of the room with a dead man's package pressed against her ribs and a dead man's word lodged under her tongue like a fishbone.

*Freya.*

She didn't know the name. She had never known the name.

So why, when the door began to open, did her whole body lean toward it — the way you lean toward a door when someone you love is standing on the other side?

Through the gap came wind and rain and lamplight, and the silhouette of a man in a grey cloak, tall and unhurried, water running off him as though the storm were merely a formality he'd chosen to observe.

"Good evening," said the man in the doorway, in a voice like a drawer sliding shut. "Forgive the hour. I'm looking for a traveler. He would have arrived... recently."

Behind the bar, Hesta's hand found Brannock's sleeve. By the fire, the last farmer stared into his ale with the studied blankness of a man memorizing the grain of the table.

And Paloma Thackeray — who had crossed the boundary stones to become no one, who had spent three weeks being wonderfully, safely nobody — felt the leather case shift against her skin, warm now, unmistakably warm, like something waking up.

"Yes," she heard herself say, before terror or wisdom could stop her. "Won't you come in out of the rain?"
