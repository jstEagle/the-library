## 4. What Magic Cannot Follow

Paloma had always assumed that if she ever ran for her life, she would be better at it.

She had imagined, in the idle way one imagines things while weeding Maren's garden or stirring a poultice that smelled of boiled socks, that flight would feel swift and purposeful — her cloak snapping behind her like a banner, her wits sharp as a whetted knife. Instead, three days out from the waystation inn, she was soaked to the ribs, lost on a sheep track that the sheep themselves seemed to have abandoned as a bad idea, and carrying a dead man's map pressed against her chest like a second heartbeat.

The rain came down in the flat, patient way of Stillwater Valley rain, as though it had all the time in the world and knew it. Paloma pulled her hood lower and kept walking.

Behind her — she checked over her shoulder more often than was dignified — the world was quiet. Too quiet, perhaps. Inquisitor Vane's riders had reached the village the night the stranger died; she had seen their torches from the loft window, a slow orange procession winding up the valley road like a string of embers someone was carefully laying toward her door. She had gone down the cellar steps with shaking hands, taken the map from the dead man's stiffening fingers because some voice deeper than fear told her she must, and climbed out through the smoke-house while the innkeeper argued loudly and unconvincingly about strangers and weather.

That had been three days ago. The riders had not found her. More unsettlingly, they did not seem to be *looking* for her, not properly — she had watched two of them at a distance that morning, standing at the boundary stone where the valley road forked, and they had stood there like men reading a signpost that said nothing, turning their horses in slow circles, and riding back the way they'd come.

The valley, she thought, was hiding her.

Or something in it was.

Her magic, what little of it Maren had been able to pound into her — a knack for finding lost things, a touch of mending, the small green gift of coaxing herbs past their season — had guttered out the moment she crossed the boundary stones, like a wet wick. She had grieved that once, briefly, on the first night: grief for the last thread tying her to Maren, to the crooked little house, to the life she had fled. But now, walking, she tested the silence inside herself again and again, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, and found that the silence had changed texture. It no longer felt like absence.

It felt like shelter.

"Stop that," she told herself aloud, because talking to oneself was better than listening to the thoughts underneath. "You're a runaway scullery girl with a stolen map and a dead man's blood on your sleeve. Chosen ones don't smell of tallow."

The map, naturally, said nothing. It never said anything while she was awake.

* * *

She found shelter that evening in the ruin of a shepherd's bothy, half-swallowed by a hillside, its roof mostly honest turf and its walls mostly optimism. There was an old hearth, and dry bracken, and — this was the miracle — a flint and steel in a niche by the door, left by some shepherd who had either died or been promoted to a life with better roofing.

Paloma built a fire with the competence of a woman who had lit ten thousand cook-fires, and then, fed and warming, she made the mistake of looking at the map.

She had avoided studying it properly. That was deliberate. A thing you don't look at can't make demands of you. But the fire was warm, the rain was loud, and the leather case sat against her ribs with the patient weight of a cat that intends to be fed.

"Oh, fine," she said.

She unrolled it across her knees.

The leather was old — older than old, the kind of old where you stop being able to guess and start feeling vaguely disrespectful — but the ink upon it was black as the moment it was set down. It showed the Stillwater Valley entire, every stream and fold of hill rendered in fine, sure lines, and at its heart a mountain she did not know, drawn with a care that made the rest of the map look like a courtesy. Around the mountain's peak, in a script she could not read, ran a ring of characters like thorns.

The stranger had called it a map of the Wolf. He had called *her* —

No. She wasn't going to think about that yet. She was going to think about practical things, like whether the ink would burn.

This was, she would maintain afterward, a perfectly reasonable experiment. Vane's riders were hunting the map; the map was the dangerous thing; therefore it was sensible to know whether the map could be destroyed. If it burned, she could burn it and go home to Maren and pretend none of this had happened. That was the whole argument, and it was airtight, and she held a corner of the map to the flame with a steady hand.

The leather did not scorch. The ink did not so much as darken. The flame lay against it like a dog refused its dinner, licking and finding nothing, and after a long moment Paloma snatched her hand back and sat there breathing hard while the fire crackled on, entirely ordinary, entirely indifferent.

"All right," she whispered. "All right. You win."

The map, smug in its indestructibility, showed her the mountain.

* * *

She dreamed, that first night, of a tower.

It rose from the mountain's shoulder like a needle, pale stone shot through with veins of something darker, and she knew it the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why — not recognition exactly, but a hook set somewhere below thought. In the dream she stood at its base and looked up, and the tower went up and up into cloud, and somewhere high above, a window glowed with lamplight, and she understood with absolute certainty that the light was waiting for her.

Then the tower was gone and there was snow underfoot, and a wolf.

Not a wolf like the wolves of stories, gaunt and wicked-eyed. This one was vast and grey and calm, lying in the snow like a hill that had decided to have opinions, and when it turned its head and looked at her, its eyes were the exact brown of her father's — the father she could not remember, the hole in her childhood that Maren had always stepped around like a well.

"You're late," the wolf said, in a voice like her father's, or like a voice remembering her father's.

"I don't even know where here is," Paloma said.

"You do," said the wolf. "You drew it."

And she looked down and her hands were small, child's hands, holding charcoal, and the snow around her was covered in lines — streams, hills, the tower, the mountain — and she was drawing the map, she had always been drawing the map, and the wolf watched her with her father's patient eyes and waited for her to finish.

She woke with tears drying on her face and dawn grey through the bothy's doorway, and the map rolled neatly beside her, though she had fallen asleep with it open across her knees.

"That," she informed the ceiling, "was a lot of weather for one night."

* * *

For two more days she moved deeper into the valley, and the valley continued its strange, quiet conspiracy on her behalf.

She learned its tricks quickly, because she had grown up learning Maren's tricks and recognized the family resemblance. Rain fell ahead of her and cleared behind, so that her tracks washed away within the hour. Mist gathered along the ridgelines whenever she heard hoofbeats on distant roads, folding the hills closed like hands. Once, cutting cross-country above a river gorge, she slipped on wet slate and slid twenty feet toward the edge, and a hawthorn root — a root she would have sworn had not been there — caught her ankle and held until she got her feet under her.

"Thank you," she said to the root, and then felt foolish, and then decided she didn't care. Good manners cost nothing, and if the valley was going to be peculiar, she intended to be polite about it.

The riders, meanwhile, might as well have been hunting smoke. She saw them twice more: once a pair on the far bank of a stream, casting about like dogs that had lost the scent mid-stride; once a larger party on the valley road, and among them a man on a black horse who did not cast about at all. He simply sat, very still, in the rain, with his face tipped slightly upward, as though listening to something no one else could hear.

Paloma got herself flat behind a boulder and stayed there until her knees ached, and even after the party moved on she stayed, because the stillness of that man had frightened her more than any amount of searching. Searching meant doubt. Stillness meant certainty. And certainty, aimed at her, was a thing she had no plan for.

That night, in a hollow beneath a leaning rowan, she ate the last of the innkeeper's bread and had the conversation she had been avoiding since the cellar.

"He gave me the map," she said to the fire. "He was dying, and he had maybe ten words in him, and he spent them on my name. Not 'take this to the free cities.' Not 'burn it.' My name." She poked the fire. "Which means he knew me. Which means somebody told him about a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice, which means I was known, which means I was never invisible at all, I just hadn't been noticed *yet*."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl asked its one question.

"And the other option," she went on, because honesty was a discipline like any other and she was out of practice, "is that he knew me the other way. From before. From whatever Maren won't talk about." Her hand drifted to the map case. "From whoever taught me to draw."

She had no memory of drawing. She had no memory of a father beyond a shape — broad shoulders, ink-stained fingers, a hummed tune she could not name — and no memory of a mother at all, and no memory of arriving at Maren's door at six years old with a bundle of clothes and a name stitched into her collar. Maren had said, in her one and only statement on the subject, *Your people wanted you forgotten, child, and forgetting is a kindness I agreed to provide.* And Paloma, being six, and then twelve, and then nineteen, had accepted this the way one accepts weather.

But the wolf in the dream had her father's eyes. And the map was in her father's hand — she had known it the moment she unrolled it, the way you know handwriting before you read a word, the confident downstrokes, the little hooks on the terminals — and maps do not draw themselves, and children do not forget everything without help.

"Somebody helped me forget," she said quietly. The owl, if it was listening, offered no comment.

She slept, and dreamed of the tower again, and this time the dream took her inside it: a round room lined floor to ceiling with drawers, thousands of drawers, each labeled in that thorned script, and in the center of the room a door of dark wood with no handle, only a keyhole shaped like a wolf's print. She stood before it in the dream knowing — the way the dream made everything knowable — that behind the door was the thing the whole map pointed to, the thing the stranger died carrying, the thing Aldric believed he had killed.

And the door was not locked. It had never been locked. It was only shut, and shut doors wait.

"Wake up," said a voice behind her — her own voice, but younger, six years old, impatient. "You're the key, silly. You've always been the key. Why do you think they hid you in the one place magic can't follow?"

She woke before she could answer, heart hammering, and lay watching the rowan branches move against the stars.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Everyone knew the Stillwater Valley was dead to magic — every hedge-witch, every village granny, every smuggler who used the boundary stones to cool a hot cargo. It was common knowledge, the sort of knowledge nobody examines because it has always been true. Magic cannot follow you into Stillwater. Run there and be safe.

Run there and be *hidden*, said the child's voice in her memory. Hidden from what? Or hidden from whom? Or — and this was the thought that sat up in her chest and refused to lie back down — hidden *for*, the way you hide a letter in a book, the way you bury a seed in winter soil. Safe from magic. Safe *as* magic. Waiting.

"I'm not a seed," Paloma told the stars. "I'm a person. I'm a person who is going to walk out of this valley, find Tamsin's contact at the ford or whoever else the poor dead man meant me to find, hand off this map, and go back to pulling weeds. That is the plan. That is a good plan. Plans are how normal people survive."

The stars, like the owl, declined to commit.

* * *

On the fifth morning she reached the high shoulder of the valley where the land began to gather itself toward mountains, and stopped, because the map had begun to whisper while she was awake.

Not in words. In warmth. The leather case against her chest had grown gradually warmer as she walked northeast, cooler when she turned aside, warmer again when she corrected — a compass made of temperature, guiding her with the gentle insistence of a hand on the small of her back. She had been following it for an hour before she admitted to herself that she was following it.

She sat down on a flat stone, took out the map, and confronted it directly.

"Here's how this goes," she said. "You're going to show me where you want me to go. Then I'm going to decide. Me. The person. Whatever you are — and I have theories, and none of them end with 'ordinary map' — you do not get to steer."

The warmth pulsed once, almost apologetic.

"And another thing," she said, gaining momentum, because momentum was all that stood between her and terror. "If you're what I think you might be — if you're connected to the wolf, to the mountain, to whatever my father — " her voice caught on the word and she pushed through it — "to whatever my father was protecting, then I want you to know that I am a hedge-witch's apprentice whose greatest magical achievement to date is keeping Maren's parsley alive through a frost. I am not chosen. I am not special. I am a girl who is good at finding lost keys, and the fact that you seem to disagree does not make you right."

The map lay open on her knees, its ink black and patient, the mountain at its heart drawn in lines her father's hand had set down before she was born — or possibly lines *she* had set down, small hands, charcoal, snow — and the wind moved the rowan leaves overhead with a sound like pages turning.

Slowly, gently, the warmth spread from the case into her palms, up her wrists, into the hollow place where her magic used to live. It did not feel like power. It felt like being remembered — thoroughly, specifically, by someone who had been waiting a long time to do it.

Paloma looked northeast, where the peaks stood white against the morning, and somewhere among them a tower waited with its lamp lit, and a door with no lock stood shut, and a wolf with her father's eyes was dreaming of her, patiently, the way valleys dream of rain.

"Oh," she said, very small. "Oh, it's *that* kind of story."

She rolled up the map, tied it close, shouldered her bundle, and started walking toward the mountain — hunted, exhausted, terrified, and chosen, and unable to shake the suspicion that of all the feelings fighting for space in her chest, the strangest one, the one she'd deny under torture, was relief.
