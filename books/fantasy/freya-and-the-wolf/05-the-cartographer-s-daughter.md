## 5. The Cartographer's Daughter

The map had not spoken to her in three days.

Paloma told herself this was a mercy. She had taken a room above a cooper's shop in the village of Nethermere — paid for with the last of the innkeeper's coins and a lie about a widowed aunt in the south — and for three days she had done nothing but sleep, eat bread and hard cheese, and watch the rain stitch silver seams down the window. The valley did this to a person. Stillwater was not a place that hurried you. It was a place that held you, gently, like water holds a leaf, and after two weeks of running she had begun to feel less like a fugitive and more like something set down carefully on a shelf.

But on the third morning, the map spoke again.

She woke with her father's voice in her ears — *count the rivers, little wren* — and sat up so fast she cracked her head on the low beam of the attic ceiling. The curse she muttered would have made Maren proud. The thought of Maren made her chest ache in a way she refused to examine.

Below her, the cooper's hammer rang its steady rhythm against staves. Rain on the roof. A dog barking somewhere near the mill. Ordinary sounds, all of them, and yet Paloma sat in the grey light with her heart going like a rabbit's, because the voice had been *his*, unmistakably his, warm and dry as autumn leaves, and her father had been dead for thirteen years.

She unrolled the map on the floor between her knees.

It had been quiet since the night she'd fled the waystation — quiet in the way a sleeping animal is quiet, if an animal could be made of ink and calfskin and something older than either. By daylight it looked like what it pretended to be: a map of the Stillwater Valley, beautifully drawn, the mountains hatched in fine parallel strokes, the river rendered in a blue that seemed faintly wet no matter how long it sat rolled up in her pack. She had studied it a hundred times by now. She knew where every ford crossed, every shepherd's hut stood, every boundary stone leaned at its drunken angle.

What she had never been able to find was the maker's mark.

Every mapmaker signed their work. It was one of the first things Maren had taught her, back when Paloma was small enough to sit on the herb-drying racks and swing her feet: *A map is a claim, child. Whoever draws it is saying I have seen this place, I have walked it, I stake my name upon its truth. So they sign. Always.* And then Maren had gone strange and silent in a way she only ever went about one subject, and had changed the topic to comfrey, and Paloma had learned not to ask.

She had looked for the signature in all the usual places — bottom margin, cartouche, the curl of the title. Nothing. The map simply began, as though it had drawn itself out of the ground.

This morning, though, with her father's voice still ringing in her skull, she did something she hadn't thought to do before. She stopped looking *at* the map and started looking *as* him.

*Count the rivers, little wren.*

There were five rivers drawn in the valley. She counted them. Five. Then she counted again, slower, tracing each blue line from source to mouth with her fingertip — and found the sixth. It wasn't a river at all. It was a hairline crack in the vellum, or so she'd always assumed, running from the western peaks down toward the heart of the valley. But now, following it, she saw that it bent the way water bends. It forked the way water forks. And where it met the true river, the two blues were not quite the same blue.

Her skin prickled. The magic-silence of the valley pressed close around her, that familiar deadness where her power should have been — and yet something stirred anyway, deep under her breastbone, like a fish turning over in a frozen pond.

She fetched the cooper's good lamp from downstairs — he was at his workshop, and she left a coin on his bench by way of apology — and held the map up to the flame, tilting it until the light raked across the surface at a low angle.

The sixth river caught the light differently. Not ink at all. Something raised, something written *into* the skin of the vellum, invisible except when the light lay along it like a hand laid along a cheek.

Letters. Tiny ones, worked into the riverbed itself, flowing downstream letter by letter the way a message might be floated down a stream in a bottle.

Paloma's hands shook so badly she had to set the lamp down twice.

She copied the letters onto a scrap of paper, one by one, sounding them out the way she'd sounded out words as a girl. They were in cipher — of course they were; her father had never once in his life written anything plainly if he could help it, had labeled the jam jars in cipher, had once left a coded note about mending the fence that took Maren two days and a bottle of elderflower wine to break — but it was a cipher she knew. The one he'd taught her when she was seven, as a game, walking her along the hedgerows behind Maren's cottage. *Every secret needs a key, little wren, and the best keys are the ones people carry without knowing.*

The key had been her own name.

Not *Paloma*. The other one. The one nobody used anymore, the one that lived in the fogged-over country of her missing childhood, the one Maren had called her exactly once, on the worst night of both their lives, before catching herself and never saying it again.

Paloma didn't say it aloud now either. She wrote it instead, in the corner of her scrap of paper, in letters that wobbled because her hand wouldn't hold still. Then she matched the key to the cipher, letter to letter, the old childhood game, and the hidden river resolved into words.

*Drawn by Edric Vale, cartographer to the free cities, who walked every mile herein and loved most of them. To my daughter, if she is reading this: you found the river. Good girl.*

The lamp guttered. Or perhaps it didn't; perhaps it was only that everything in Paloma went briefly dark and ringing, the way the world does when a floor gives way beneath you.

*Edric Vale.*

She had known her father by another name. Everyone in Maren's orbit had. He had been plain Tam Fairweather to the village, a quiet man who mended fences and drew pictures nobody was allowed to see, who had died — or vanished; even now the memory slid away from her like a fish — when she was nine. Maren had burned his papers. Paloma remembered that much: the bonfire at the edge of the garden, the smell of hot ink, Maren's face lit orange and grim as a gargoyle's, and Paloma herself crying and being told, not unkindly, *some names are too heavy for a child to carry, so we put them down.*

She had thought, all these years, that the burning was grief. That Maren had destroyed a dead man's leavings because keeping them hurt.

Now, sitting on the floor of a stranger's attic with her father's handwriting swimming before her eyes, Paloma understood that the burning had not been grief at all.

It had been hiding.

* * *

The rain stopped around noon, and Paloma walked.

She didn't decide to walk; her legs simply took her, out of Nethermere along the mill lane, past the duck pond where two boys were sailing a raft made of barrel staves, up the sheep track toward the western hills. Toward the headwaters of the sixth river. She carried the map inside her coat, against her heart, where she had taken to carrying it without noticing she'd begun doing such a thing.

The valley opened around her as she climbed, green and patient. That was the thing about Stillwater that visitors never understood, she thought — the way it watched without watching. Magic couldn't live here. Every hedge-witch within fifty miles said so, said it the way farmers say *the soil's poor here*, a fact of the land, nothing personal. Her own gift had gone out like a wet wick the day she'd crossed the boundary stones, and she had been grateful, oh how grateful, because Maren's mistress-craft could not follow her into that silence.

But climbing now through the bracken with her father's words folded over her heart, she felt the silence differently. It didn't feel like absence. It felt like a held breath.

*You found the river. Good girl.*

He had known she would come here. Not hoped — *known*, the way he'd known things, with that infuriating cartographer's certainty, as if the future were just territory he'd already surveyed. He had drawn this map, hidden his name in a river, keyed the cipher to the name he'd given her before anyone thought to take it away — and then he had let himself be erased, let Maren burn his life down to ash, let his daughter grow up thinking he'd been nobody, because —

Because the valley's silence was not natural. Because someone had wanted a place where magic could not follow, and her father had built it, or warded it, or *drawn* it — she didn't know which, and the not-knowing made her want to scream at the sheep — and then he had put his daughter inside it, behind the walls, the way you put the last seed of something precious in a cold cellar to keep it through a long winter.

And now the winter was ending. Now there was a dying man's blood on the map and an inquisitor's riders in the passes and a name whispering to her in dreams, and the seed had felt the thaw.

Paloma stopped at the top of the sheep track, where the land should have shown her the whole western valley spread out below, and found that it didn't. There was a fold in the hills she had never noticed, a shallow vale tucked between two shoulders of rock, and standing at its lip she felt the same wrongness she'd felt at the sixth river — the sense of something drawn rather than grown. Someone had made this fold. Someone had folded the landscape like a letter, and hidden something in the crease.

She stood there a long time, wind pulling at her hair, and let herself remember.

* * *

She was six years old, maybe seven, sitting on her father's workbench with her heels drumming the drawer-fronts, watching him draw. This was in the before-time, in the house whose layout she could no longer recall, in a room full of lamplight and the smell of ink and tallow. His hands moved the way water moves, sure and unhurried, and the lines came off the pen like thread off a spindle.

"Papa," she had said, "why do you draw the same mountain over and over?"

He hadn't looked up. "Which mountain?"

"The one with the door in it."

His pen had stopped. Just for a heartbeat — a hesitation so small that any other child would have missed it, but Paloma had spent her whole short life learning to read her father's silences the way other children learned letters. Then the pen had moved again, smooth as ever, and he had smiled at her over the lamp.

"You've sharp eyes, little wren." He turned the sheet toward her. There it was: the mountain, hatched in fine strokes, and set into its flank a door — tall, arched, banded with lines that might have been iron or might have been script. "Tell me what you see."

"A door."

"What else?"

She had squinted. "A... wolf? Behind it? Underneath?" Even at six she had heard how foolish it sounded, and had flushed. "I don't know. It feels like a wolf."

Her father had set down his pen. He had lifted her off the bench and held her up in the lamplight, and his eyes — brown, ordinary, the eyes of a fence-mender, a nobody — had been shining and terribly sad.

"Then listen to me," he had said, "because this is the important lesson, more important than your letters. Some doors are kept shut not because what's behind them is wicked, but because the world isn't ready to look at it. And some people are doors too, little wren. Do you understand me? Some people are doors."

"I'm not a door," six-year-old Paloma had said, indignant. "I'm a girl."

"No," her father had agreed softly, setting her down, folding the map of the mountain away into a drawer that locked. "Not yet."

* * *

Paloma came back to herself kneeling in the bracken with wet cheeks and no memory of having knelt.

The wind moved through the valley below, bending the grass in long silver waves, and it looked — she could not unsee it now — like fur. Like the pelt of something vast lying asleep under the green, breathing slow, its ribs the ridgelines, its flanks the rolling meadows. All those years she had thought Stillwater was empty. It had never been empty. It was *occupied*, in the oldest sense, and the silence of it, the magic-deadness, was not a wall to keep danger out.

It was a blanket. Drawn up over a sleeper.

"Oh," Paloma said aloud, to the wind, to the hills, to the father who had folded the land like a letter around his sleeping daughter. "Oh, you clever, terrible man."

The map shifted against her heart. Not physically — the vellum stayed where it was — but she felt it the way you feel someone turn toward you in a dark room. And in the privacy of her own skull, in a voice that was half her father's and half something much, much larger, the map said:

*You found the river. Now find the ford, little wren. You are not the only one who reads me.*

Paloma got to her feet. Far below, at the bend of the true river where the willows grew thick and the water ran shallow over pale stones, she saw a figure on horseback pause at the crossing — a rider in travel-stained greens, hood back, scanning the far bank with the particular patience of someone who has been looking for a very long time.

The rider's gaze swept uphill and stopped, Paloma would have sworn, precisely on the fold in the hills where she stood.

The map warmed against her breastbone like a hand closing over hers.

*Run,* it suggested, almost kindly. *Or don't. Either way — she has already seen you.*
