## 6. Tamsin of the Ford

Tamsin had been tracking the girl for three days, and for three days the girl had been doing everything wrong.

She walked the riverbank instead of the ridge, where a scout could have spotted followers from a mile off. She built fires — small ones, banked ones, but fires, with smoke that rose up like a polite little flag saying *here I am, please come rob me*. She'd bought bread in Millbrook without haggling, which marked her as an outsider faster than any wanted-poster face could have, and she'd paid with a coin so old it had to have come out of a hoard, which marked her as something stranger still.

And yet here Tamsin was, three days behind her, having lost her twice in open country and found her again only by accident of weather. The Stillwater Valley did things to tracks. Everyone knew that. You crossed the boundary stones and your magic went quiet as a held breath, but apparently your *tracking* went quiet too, because Tamsin had followed a clear trail to a streambed and watched it simply stop, as if the girl had been lifted into the sky by helpful birds.

"Or as if the valley doesn't want you finding her," said Wren, perched on her shoulder.

"I'm not talking to you today."

"You're always talking to me. That's the tragedy of you."

Wren was a wren in the way that Tamsin's captain was a captain — technically true, spiritually debatable. The bird had been bound to her family for four generations, and mostly used the arrangement to offer commentary. Right now he sat with his feathers puffed against the drizzle, radiating smugness.

"The map," Wren said. "That's what matters. Corvin is dead, the map is loose, and the Council said *any cost*, Tamsin. Any cost."

"I remember what they said. I was there."

"Then why are we sitting in a bush?"

Tamsin shifted her weight, trying to find the part of a bush that wasn't personally offended by her presence. Below, where the river shallowed over pale stones into the ford, the girl had stopped. She stood at the water's edge with her hood back, dark hair plastered to her cheeks, staring across at the far bank like it owed her money.

Corvin had described her once, years ago, drunk on plum brandy and homesickness: *There's a girl in the valley who mended my boot and told me the hole was my own fault.* He'd laughed until he coughed. He'd been dying even then; Tamsin just hadn't known to listen for it.

*Paloma,* he'd called her. A hedge-witch's apprentice who'd run from her mistress into the one place in the world where nothing could follow her. Corvin had gone to her because of exactly that. Because the valley forgot things, and he'd needed somewhere to forget him before Vane's riders arrived.

He hadn't counted on the map being stubborn.

The Council's diviners had sworn the map couldn't be taken from her — that it clung like a burr, that hands that tried to pull it away came back empty. Tamsin had assumed this was diviner poetry for *we don't actually know*. But Corvin's last message had been specific: *It won't leave her. I don't know why. Find out why before Vane does.*

Below, the girl waded into the ford.

"Now," Wren said.

"It's raining."

"You've fought in worse."

"I've *complained* in worse. There's a difference." But Tamsin was already moving, because the truth was she'd been waiting all morning for the girl to do something predictable, and a ford was about as predictable as geography got. Open ground. Water on both sides. Nowhere to be clever.

She came down through the alders with her hood up and her staff drawn, and stepped into the shallows ten paces behind her.

"Don't," Tamsin said.

The girl went very still. Water swirled around her knees. Slowly, she turned, and Tamsin got her first proper look at her face, and felt something in her chest lurch sideways, because Corvin had done her an injustice in his description. He'd made her sound small. She wasn't small. She was watchful — the kind of watchful that meant she'd already counted the distance to both banks and decided which one she could reach before Tamsin could.

"You're free-city," the girl said. Her eyes flicked to the knot on Tamsin's shoulder-cord. "Scout cord. Grey-and-green."

"And you're observant. Good. That'll save time." Tamsin kept her voice level, friendly, the voice she used on farmers whose dogs were deciding about her. "My name is Tamsin. I knew Corvin Vale. I'm sorry about how he died. I'd like to talk about the thing he gave you."

The girl's hand drifted toward her satchel, then stopped — deliberately, Tamsin noted, the way you stop when you know the gesture gives you away and you want your opponent to know you know. Clever. Annoyingly clever.

"He didn't give it to me," Paloma said. "He died holding it. I'm just the one who couldn't pry his fingers loose."

"That's not what our diviners say."

"Your diviners say a lot of things. They also said the war would end in a season, if my mistress is to be believed, and she believed them, and look how that went."

Despite herself, despite three days of rain and bush-living and grief she hadn't let herself feel yet, Tamsin almost smiled. Corvin would have liked her. That was the problem. Corvin had liked dangerous people; it was practically a medical condition.

"I'm going to ask you plainly," Tamsin said. "Give me the map, walk back into the valley, and live a long unremarkable life. The Council will protect the valley's neutrality. Nobody comes after you. You go back to whatever inn was unlucky enough to hire you."

"No."

"No?"

"No." The girl's chin came up. "I don't know what's on it. I don't know why he chose me. I know a man died in front of me with poison in his blood and riders at the door, and the last thing he did was push this into my hands and say my name like he was *surprised* by it. So no. I'm not handing it to the first armed stranger who says a dead man's name at me. Forgive me if my trust has taken damage recently."

The river ran cold around Tamsin's shins. Somewhere upstream a heron complained about the weather, or possibly about politics.

"Then I'm sorry about this too," Tamsin said, and moved.

Later — much later, in the retelling, usually over ale, always with embellishment — Tamsin would describe the fight at the ford as brief and dignified. What actually happened was that she lunged, the girl sidestepped with the ease of someone who'd spent years dodging a hedge-witch's thrown crockery, grabbed Tamsin's staff-arm, and very nearly put her face-first into the river. Tamsin recovered, caught a fistful of cloak, felt the girl twist free with a practiced shrug that spoke of real training — *Maren taught her more than herbs, then* — and finally got both hands on the satchel strap.

She pulled.

The strap didn't budge. It wasn't that the girl was strong enough to hold it — though she was holding it, white-knuckled, braced against the current. It was that the strap itself seemed to refuse, the leather going rigid under Tamsin's fingers like a root grown through stone. She pulled harder. The current pushed. And then the map, inside the satchel, did something Tamsin had no word for except that every hair on her arms stood up and the river went silent — not quiet, *silent*, the way the valley was silent, the way the boundary stones made the world hold its breath —

— and let go of her.

Not the girl. *Her.* Tamsin's hands opened as if they'd been politely asked, and she stumbled back into deeper water, and the world's sound came rushing back all at once: rain, river, the girl breathing hard, Wren shrieking overhead in a register she'd never heard from him before.

"*What,*" said Wren, "was that."

Tamsin didn't answer. She was looking at the girl, and the girl was looking at her own satchel with an expression Tamsin recognized from mirrors, from the moment after you realize the ground isn't where you left it.

"It won't leave me," Paloma said quietly. "You felt it."

"I felt something."

"So did I. Every night since he died. It talks in my sleep." She looked up, and there was terror in her face, yes, but underneath the terror — worse, and better — there was a terrible kind of relief. Like someone who'd spent weeks thinking she was haunted finally getting confirmation the ghost was real. "I thought I was losing my mind. Thank you for being insane enough to grab it."

"You're welcome?" Tamsin waded back to shallower water, dignity dripping off her in sheets. "For the record, I'm supposed to take it at any cost. Those were my orders. *Any cost.*"

"What does that mean, exactly?"

"It means the Council was worried I'd come back without it."

"And now?"

Tamsin looked at her hands. They ached faintly, the way they did after touching the boundary stones too long. Whatever lived in that map — whatever had looked at her, because she'd have sworn under oath something had *looked* at her — it had made its preference extremely clear.

"Now," she said slowly, "I'm going to stand here in a river feeling like an idiot, and you're going to tell me everything that happened at that inn, starting with why Corvin said your name like that. And then—" she held up a hand before the girl could object, "—and *then* we discuss the fact that Vane's riders crossed the southern stones two days ago, which means the valley has stopped protecting you, which means whatever that map wants, it wants it *out of here*. With you. Quickly."

The rain thickened. The heron gave up and flew off. On Tamsin's shoulder, Wren had gone uncharacteristically quiet, head cocked toward the satchel like a dog listening to a door.

"You want to travel with me," Paloma said flatly.

"I want to *not fail Corvin twice in one month*. Whether that means traveling with you or dragging you back over the stones by your collar depends entirely on the next hour of conversation." Tamsin pushed her wet hood back and sighed. "Also, I'm told you mend boots and assign blame for holes. I have a hole. Consider it a test of character."

Something flickered across the girl's face — suspicion wrestling exhaustion wrestling, briefly and unwillingly, amusement. Exhaustion won, but only just.

"The ford's shallowest on the east side," Paloma said at last. "If we're crossing anyway. And if you're walking behind me where I can see you."

"Naturally."

"And if you tell me who Corvin Vale was. Really was. Because the man who died in that cellar was wearing usurper's livery under a free-city courier's coat, and I've been chewing on that for days."

Tamsin felt that one land, right where it was meant to. Trust, she thought, wading toward the east side of the ford, was going to be like this river: cold, awkward, and crossable only one careful step at a time.

"He was a lot of things," she said. "Most of them illegal in at least three cities. Start walking, and I'll tell you about the first time he stole a horse from Aldric's own stable — and why he never once regretted it."

On the far bank, the valley waited, grey and green and forgetting. Behind them, somewhere south, two days away and closing, rode the man who remembered everything.

Paloma shouldered her satchel — and the map inside it pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, like something patient turning over in its sleep.

Neither of them saw it. But both of them, without discussing it, started walking faster.
