## 7. The Road Out of Stillness

**Paloma**

The boundary stones of the Stillwater Valley did not look like much. That was the first thing Paloma had thought when she'd crossed them going in, half-starved and terrified, and it was the last thing she thought now, standing on the eastern side of the ford with her pack on her back and Tamsin of the Ford beside her, both of them looking at a pair of mossy granite lumps like they were deciding whether to jump off a cliff.

"They're smaller than I remembered," Paloma said.

"Everything is," said Tamsin. "That's how you know you've grown."

"I don't think that's wisdom. I think you just made that up."

"A little of both." Tamsin rolled her shoulders, checked the straps of her sword belt for the fourth time — Paloma had counted — and squinted eastward, where the road climbed out of the valley through birch woods still holding their autumn gold. "Once we cross, your magic comes back. All of it. At once."

"You keep saying that like it's a warning."

"It *is* a warning. You've been living in a house with no fire for months, and we're about to hand you a lit torch and lock you in a barn full of hay."

Paloma looked down at her hands. In the valley they were just hands — chapped from dishwater at the waystation inn, nicked from chopping onions, ordinary as bread. Beyond those stones, they had been something else once. She could barely remember what. A warmth behind the knuckles. A hum in the teeth. Maren's voice saying *you're a kettle, girl, not a teacup; stop pretending you can't boil.*

She missed Maren suddenly and fiercely, which was inconvenient, since she'd fled the woman at dawn not so long ago.

"Paloma." Tamsin's voice had gone quiet, the scout's quiet, the one that meant she'd noticed something. "We can wait. There's no shame in it. The valley will keep you safe."

"That's exactly why I have to go," Paloma said, surprising herself. "I spent my whole life being kept somewhere safe by someone who wouldn't tell me why. I'm done being a secret. I want to be a person who knows things."

Tamsin studied her for a long moment, then huffed out something that was almost a laugh. "You sound like Corvin."

"Corvin being the dead man whose map is currently whispering to me in my sleep?"

"The very same. Stubborn as a mule with opinions." She shouldered her pack. "Right. On three?"

"On three," Paloma agreed, and then, because she was nervous and because Tamsin had already started walking, she crossed the boundary stones on two.

* * *

**Tamsin**

Tamsin heard the crossing before she saw anything — a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, low and wrong, and then Paloma made a noise Tamsin would remember for the rest of her life.

It wasn't a scream. It was worse than a scream. It was the small, strangled sound of someone trying very hard not to scream because screaming would cost breath they needed for something else. Paloma went to her knees in the mud of the road, and every hair on Tamsin's arms stood up, and the air itself seemed to lean toward the girl like wheat bending under wind.

Magic coming home. Tamsin had seen soldiers take an arrow and make less fuss.

"Paloma!" She dropped her pack and crouched, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Breathe. Look at me. Breathe."

"I'm — " Paloma's eyes were wide and streaming, and light was coming off her skin. Not metaphorically. Off her skin, along her forearms, tracing the old scars from kitchen work in thin gold lines like someone had redrawn her in illuminated script. "It's — everything — I can hear the *river*, Tamsin, I can hear it thinking—"

"Rivers don't think."

"This one does, and it's *bored*—"

"Okay." Tamsin took her hands anyway, because the alternative was watching her shake apart alone, and Paloma's grip crushed down hard enough to grind bone. Gold light flared where their palms met, and Tamsin felt something pass through her — a warmth, a pulse, a sense of enormous attention turning briefly toward her like a lighthouse beam — and then, slowly, mercifully, it ebbed. The light sank into Paloma's skin. Her breathing steadied. The river went back to being a river, presumably.

Paloma sat in the mud, gasping, and started to laugh. It was a slightly unhinged laugh, but Tamsin had served with scouts long enough to recognize relief when she heard it.

"That," Paloma managed, "was *awful*."

"And yet you're laughing."

"Because it's *mine*." She held up her hands, turning them over, watching faint embers of light chase each other across her fingertips. "Do you know how long I've been told to be small? Maren never let me practice. Said the valley would eat it anyway. And she was right, it did, it just — sat there, all this time, waiting." Her face did something complicated. "I feel like I've been given back a limb I didn't know I was missing."

Tamsin retrieved the pack and held out a water skin. "Drink. Then walk. We've got twelve miles to the ridge and I'd rather not explain to the free cities' border patrol why their newest asset is glowing."

"I'm not glowing."

"You were glowing."

"I was *transitioning*."

"Corvin used to say things like that too," Tamsin muttered, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Paloma's face went careful and sad, the way it always did when the dead cartographer came up. But she drank the water, and she stood, and they walked.

They'd made perhaps a mile when Tamsin noticed the map.

* * *

**Paloma**

The map had been quiet since the ford. Quiet in the way a cat is quiet when it has decided to sit on something.

Paloma felt it against her ribs inside her coat, warm through the linen of her shirt, warmer than body heat had any right to be, and by the time they stopped at midday under a leaning birch, she couldn't pretend otherwise anymore. She drew it out. Corvin's leather case, blood-stained at one corner, and when her fingers closed around it the leather began to glow.

Not gold this time. A deep, patient amber, like lamplight through honey, and beneath her fingers she could feel the ink moving — actually moving, the lines of rivers and ridges rearranging themselves with the slow confidence of something that had been waiting years to be read properly.

"Tamsin."

"I see it." Tamsin had gone very still, hand on her knife, scanning the treeline out of pure habit even though no enemy had ever been frightened off by a glowing map. "Does it hurt?"

"No. It feels like..." Paloma searched. "You know when you come home and the whole house smells like bread, and you realize you were hungry the entire time and hadn't let yourself notice? It feels like that. Like being *expected*."

The ink settled. Where before the map had shown only the Stillwater Valley and its blank, unmarked borders, new lines had surfaced: roads running east, a river forked like a wishbone, and far to the northeast, a mountain drawn in heavier strokes than everything else, with a symbol at its heart.

A door. A tower above it. And curled around the tower's base, rendered in a single flowing line, a wolf.

Paloma's mouth went dry. "That's where he wanted me to go."

"That's the Hollowmount," Tamsin said quietly. "And that mark—" she leaned closer, and her breath caught, "—that's the old sigil of the free cities. Nobody's flown it in twenty years. Aldric burned every banner, every book, every—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Every archive."

Paloma looked up. "What do you mean, burned?"

Tamsin sat down slowly on a root, the way people do when the thing they're about to say is heavy and they'd rather be lower to the ground for it.

"You have to understand what the Wolf was. Before the war. Before Aldric." She turned her hands over, studying them as if instructions were written there. "He wasn't a weapon. Everyone always says weapon, but that's wrong. He was a *witness*. The oldest one. He remembered everything — every treaty, every betrayal, every name. He remembered the kings before Aldric. He remembered what Aldric was *before* Aldric took the throne." Her jaw tightened. "You can't rule honestly over a country that remembers you honestly. So the first thing Aldric did after the war wasn't raise taxes or garrison the cities. It was burn the archives. All of them. Ashford's great library burned for nine days. My grandmother watched the smoke from the ford and said it looked like the sky was grieving."

Paloma's hands had gone cold around the warm map. "All of it? Everything written?"

"Everything about him. Everything about the guardians. Everything about the bloodlines." Tamsin met her eyes, and there was something in her face that Paloma had never seen there before — not suspicion, not the wary assessment of a scout measuring an asset, but something rawer. Pity, maybe. Or fear wearing pity's clothes. "Paloma. If the map leads where I think it leads, and if what Corvin believed about you is true... then you aren't carrying a document. You're carrying the last copy of twenty years of burned history. There are people who died to get that map out of the capital. Corvin was one of maybe six who knew the route, and now he's the second one dead."

The birch leaves rattled overhead. Somewhere behind them, miles back, the Stillwater Valley lay silent and forgetting, and Paloma understood for the first time that its silence had never been peace. It had been a vault. She had been stored there, like the map, like the name, waiting.

"Wonderful," she said, because her voice would do something worse if she let it. "So I'm not just hunted. I'm *irreplaceable*."

"That's usually worse, yes."

"And the mountain — the wolf on the map — that's real? Not a symbol?"

Tamsin was quiet a moment too long.

"Tamsin."

"My mother sang a song about it," Tamsin said finally. "A lullaby. About a wolf under the mountain who keeps the names of everyone who ever lived, so that no one is ever truly forgotten. I thought it was a lullaby." She looked at the glowing sigil, and her voice dropped. "Everyone thought it was a lullaby. That was rather the point, I suppose."

Paloma looked down at the map, at the single unbroken line of the wolf, and felt the warmth of it pulse against her palms like a heartbeat keeping time with hers. Wonder rose in her — genuine, dizzying wonder, the feeling of standing at the edge of a story much larger than herself.

And underneath the wonder, cold and patient as water finding a crack, came dread.

Because if the wolf kept the names of everyone who ever lived, then it knew hers. It had always known hers. And whatever it wanted from her, it had been willing to wait her whole life to ask.

"We should move," she said, and stood, and folded the glowing map carefully against her heart like something alive, which, she was beginning to suspect, it was.

* * *

**Tamsin**

They made the ridge by dusk, and Tamsin called the halt mostly because Paloma had gone grey-faced and silent for the last hour, and because Tamsin's own nerves had begun conducting a full military review without her permission.

She built a small fire — small, screened, the kind you could put a boot through in two seconds — and shared out dried plums and the last of the valley cheese, which Paloma declared tasted like home, and then looked startled at her own words, and then looked like she might cry, and then firmly ate more cheese instead. Tamsin decided she liked her. Which was a problem, professionally speaking. Liking assets complicated extraction. Liking assets who glowed complicated it considerably.

"Ask me," Paloma said suddenly, across the fire.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking since the ford. You do this thing where your mouth opens and then you reorganize your face. It's very disciplined. It's also incredibly obvious."

Tamsin chewed a plum to buy time. Fair enough. "Why you?" she said at last. "Out of everyone. Corvin bled out in front of a dozen people in that village, and he gave the map to the scullery girl from the waystation inn. He was precise. He didn't do anything by accident. So either he knew something about you, or the map chose, and I don't know which answer frightens me more."

Paloma poked the fire. Sparks climbed. For a moment Tamsin thought she wouldn't answer, and then:

"When he pressed it into my hands, he said a name." Her voice was careful, the way you walk on ice. "My name. But not — not *Paloma*. Another one. One I almost knew. Like a word in a language I dreamed in as a child." She looked up, firelight in her eyes, and the map, tucked inside her coat, glowed faintly through the fabric as if agreeing with her. "And the worst part, Tamsin — the part I haven't said out loud — is that when he said it, I wasn't confused. I was *homesick*. For a place I've never been. For a door I've never opened."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the dark slope, a night bird called, and Tamsin found, to her considerable professional annoyance, that her hands had gone cold.

Because she knew the song. *Sleep, little wolf, beneath the stone; the door remembers; you are known.* Her mother's voice, twenty years gone, singing to a child at the ford while the archives burned on the horizon.

And she knew, with the certainty of a scout reading sign — broken twig, bent grass, the particular silence where a rider should be — that whatever waited for them at the Hollowmount had been waiting for Paloma Thackeray since before Paloma Thackeray had a name anyone dared speak aloud.

"Get some sleep," Tamsin said gently. "I'll take first watch."

Paloma curled up near the fire, the map against her chest, and within minutes her breathing slowed into the rhythm of dreams. Tamsin watched the coals and turned her whetstone over in her fingers and did not sleep at all.

Because in the last light before true dark, she had seen it: the map's glow pulsing through Paloma's coat, steady as a heartbeat.

Steady as a second heartbeat.

And far to the northeast, past the ridge, past the burned cities and the patrolled roads, the Hollowmount stood black against the stars — and Tamsin would have sworn, then and ever after, that the mountain was breathing too.
