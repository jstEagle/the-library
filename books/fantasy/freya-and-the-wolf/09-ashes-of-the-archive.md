## 9. Ashes of the Archive

The city had been called Vellum once, Tamsin said, because its walls were papered with books the way other cities were papered with proclamations and tax notices. Now it was called Ashfall by the people who still lived in its bones, which was a smaller number than Paloma would have guessed, and a braver one.

They came down out of the hills on the third morning after the crossroads, when the map's whispering had grown so insistent that Paloma stopped pretending she wasn't listening. It didn't speak in words. It spoke the way an itch speaks, or a name on the tip of the tongue — *this way, this way, you are closer than you know* — and it had led them through a pass full of wind and down into a valley where the air itself smelled faintly of smoke that had finished burning a generation ago.

"Mind where you step," Tamsin said at the edge of it. "The streets here are made of stories."

She meant it literally. The roads of Vellum were paved with compressed ash and pulped pages, pressed flat by rain and feet until they'd hardened into something like slate. Here and there a word survived, ghosted up through the grey: half a line of poetry, a merchant's ledger entry, someone's grocery list from before the war. Paloma found herself reading them as they walked, picking her way between *two measures of barley* and *and the sea answered* and *forgive me*.

*Forgive me* appeared three times in the first hundred paces. She decided not to think about why.

"You've been here before," she said.

Tamsin walked with her hand resting easy on her bow, the way she did everywhere now, but her eyes were soft. "Twice. Once as a scout running messages, once to — help with the evacuation, what there was of it." She paused. "It burned in four days. Aldric's men didn't even loot it properly. They just wanted it gone."

"Why?"

"Because archives remember things people want forgotten." Tamsin shrugged, but the shrug had weight in it. "Same reason he burned the ones in Corvale and Merrow's Landing. Same reason, if we're honest, my own city council let him get within ten miles of ours without raising so much as a strongly worded petition."

Paloma touched the map through her shirt, where it hung against her ribs like a second heartbeat. It had been quiet since they entered the valley — not silent, exactly, but *settled*, the way a dog settles when it finally smells home.

"And the archivist?" she asked. "The one your contact mentioned?"

"Sera Ilvane. Blind as a stone and twice as stubborn, according to everyone who's ever met her. She stayed when the city burned. She's been cataloguing the ashes ever since."

"Cataloguing *ashes*?"

"You'll see," Tamsin said, and there was something in her voice — wonder, maybe, or the memory of it — that made Paloma walk faster.

* * *

Sera Ilvane's archive occupied what had once been the Great Reading Room, a hall so vast that their footsteps came back to them a heartbeat late, like shy companions. The roof had mostly fallen in, and daylight fell through the gaps in shafts so thick with drifting ash that they looked almost solid, columns of pale light standing in the gloom like the ghosts of bookshelves.

And the shelves themselves — the real ones — were full.

Not books. Paloma saw that as they drew closer, following the sound of a small bell being rung somewhere ahead, patient and unhurried. The shelves were full of *trays*, thousands upon thousands of them, each holding a shallow layer of grey powder, each labeled in a careful hand.

"She sorts it," Tamsin murmured. "By street. By building. Sometimes by shelf, if she can work out which shelf."

"That's impossible."

"Mm," said Tamsin, in the tone of someone who had already lost this argument.

The bell-ringing led them to a desk near the center of the hall, a fortress of lamplight and magnifying lenses and trays arranged with geometric precision. Behind it sat a woman so old that her age seemed less a number than a geological era. Her eyes were closed, and stayed closed as she spoke.

"Two sets of boots," Sera Ilvane said. "One heavy on the heel, one trying to be quiet and failing. You're either thieves or pilgrims, and thieves don't usually announce themselves by ringing my bell on the way in."

"We rang your bell?" Paloma asked.

"The door chime. Everyone rings it. I put it there myself, forty years ago, so I'd know who was coming." The old woman's head tilted. "You're the one trying to be quiet. And you're carrying something that hums."

Paloma's hand went involuntarily to her chest.

"Not magic," Sera said quickly, and something in her voice changed — softened, or perhaps steadied. "Nothing so crude. Paper remembers, child. Good paper, inked with intent, keeps humming long after the hand that wrote it is dust. I've spent thirty years listening to ash. I know a singing document when one walks into my reading room."

Tamsin glanced at Paloma. Paloma took a breath.

"We were told you might help us," she said. "We're looking for — for whoever made this." She drew the map out and held it, uncertainly, toward a blind woman.

Sera Ilvane did not reach for it. Instead she extended one hand, palm up, and waited, and Paloma understood: not to take, but to *listen*. She laid the map across the old woman's palm, and Sera bent over it as though over a sleeping child, her lips moving slightly, her fingers hovering above the leather without touching.

For a long moment the only sound was the drift of ash through the light-shafts, settling on everything, gentle as snow.

Then the archivist laughed.

It was a startling sound — warm, cracked, delighted — and it echoed around the great ruined hall and came back to them from all directions at once.

"Oh, *oh*," she said. "I know this hand. I have known this hand since before you were born, girl, and I would know it in the dark, and I am always in the dark." She lifted her face. "Corvin Thackeray. Master cartographer of the Royal Survey. The finest mapmaker this kingdom ever produced, and the only one who ever treated his paper like a living thing. Where did you get this?"

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "He was my father," she said, and heard how strange the words sounded, spoken aloud for the first time in years. Maren had never forbidden her to say them. She had simply never given her anything to say them *with* — no stories, no details, no face. Just a silence shaped like a man.

Sera Ilvane went very still.

"Sit down," she said at last. "Both of you. No — not there, that chair wobbles, it has wobbled for twenty years and I refuse to fix it on principle. There. Good." She folded her hands on the desk. "Now. Tell me your name."

"Paloma."

"*Paloma.*" The old woman repeated it slowly, tasting it, and then shook her head. "That's not it. That's a hedge-name, a hiding-name — no offense meant, and none taken, I hope, because whoever chose it chose well. A dove. Something soft and harmless to tuck a dangerous little bird inside of." She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "But you asked about your father, and your father left portraits. Shall I tell you what I see when I listen to your voice? Because I can't see your face, child, but voices carry faces in them, if you know how to listen, and yours —"

Her breath caught. Actually caught, an audible hitch, and for the first time the archivist sounded her age.

"Yours has her chin," she whispered. "The way it lifts at the end of a sentence, like you're asking a question you already know the answer to. Corvin's wife had that chin. And the little girl in the portraits —"

"What portraits?" Paloma's voice came out smaller than she intended.

Sera rose, unhurried, and beckoned them deeper into the hall, past shelf after shelf of labelled ash, to a narrow alcove where the fire had never reached — some accident of stone and luck. On the walls hung frames. Most held nothing; the canvases had been cut out and carried off or burned. But three still bore images, dim under decades of ash-dust, and Sera reached up and wiped one clean with her sleeve.

A family. A tall man with ink-stained fingers and a smile that crinkled his whole face, seated at a drafting table. A woman standing behind him with dark hair and a chin that lifted at the end of her smile. And between them, perched on the table itself, sitting on a half-finished map as though it were a cushion, a small girl of perhaps five, laughing, her hands smeared with ink, one of the man's pens clutched in her fist like a scepter.

Paloma did not remember it.

That was the thing — that was the terrible, simple thing. She stood in front of her own laughing face and felt *nothing*, no echo, no ache of almost-remembering. Just the flat recognition one gives a stranger. She had assumed her memories were lost, drowned, scattered like everyone said childhood memories were. Standing here, she understood for the first time that they had not been lost at all.

Someone had taken them. Carefully. Deliberately. With love, maybe — Maren's face swam up unbidden, grim and tender all at once — but taken nonetheless, the way you take a knife from a child's hand.

"They hid you in Stillwater," Sera was saying softly. "After the first purges. Corvin came here, near the end — I remember his last visit. He was frightened, and Corvin Thackeray was never frightened of anything that bared its teeth. He gave me a sealed letter and told me that if a young woman ever came asking about him, with a certain map, I was to give it to her." She turned. "I have waited thirty years to give it away. Do you know what it's like, keeping a promise with no one to keep it *to*?"

Paloma couldn't answer. Tamsin's hand found her shoulder and stayed there, warm and steady, saying nothing, which was somehow exactly right.

The letter was small, wrapped in oilcloth, buried beneath a tray of ash labelled *West Wall, Poetry, Uncatalogued*. The seal had cracked with age. Inside was a single page, and Paloma knew the handwriting the way you know your own reflection — not from memory, but from the map, from the labels on the map, from every careful line of it.

*My little dove,* it began.

She read it aloud, because Sera could not, and because some part of her understood that this was what the letter wanted — to be heard, finally, after thirty years in the dark.

*If you are reading this, then the valley kept you, and the map found you, and I am sorry — sorry for the birthday you won't remember, and the lullaby, and the smell of ink, and every ordinary morning I stole from you so that Aldric's men couldn't steal anything worse. Forgive me. Magic cannot follow you across those stones, and neither can your past, and I know now that those are the same thing.*

*You were not hidden from the world, my dove. You were hidden *for* it. The Wolf sleeps in our blood, and it will sleep until one of us chooses, freely, with open eyes, to wake it. That choice must be made whole — with every memory intact, offered willingly — or not at all. So I had yours taken, all but the deepest roots, and I gave the key to the only person I trusted more than myself. When the time comes, the valley will give back what it kept. Until then, be happy. Be small. Be a dove.*

*I love you. I have always loved you. Even the parts of you I made sure you'd never miss.*

*— Your father, who was also, once, the Wolf's friend*

Paloma lowered the page. Her hands were shaking. The great hall was absolutely silent except for the ash, falling and falling, settling on everything, gentle as snow, patient as grief.

"I don't understand," she said finally, and hated how young her voice sounded. "If he wanted me to wake it — why take the memories at all? Why make it harder?"

"Because a forced sacrifice isn't a sacrifice," Sera said gently. "It's just another theft. He needed you to come to it *choosing*, with open eyes. He needed you to grieve first." The old woman's sightless eyes seemed, impossibly, to look right through her. "I'm sorry, child. He was a wise man, and wise men are cruel in ways fools never manage."

Tamsin made a small sound beside her — half laugh, half sob — and when Paloma looked over, the scout was wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretending she wasn't.

"He sounds," Tamsin managed, "like he would have been completely unbearable at dinner parties."

"He was," Sera said warmly. "He once corrected the king's own geographer. At court. With diagrams."

And despite everything — despite the shaking hands and the stolen childhood and the letter going soft and damp where her tears had fallen on it — Paloma laughed. It came out ragged, but it came out real, and it felt like the first true thing she'd done in weeks.

* * *

They stayed the night in the alcove, among the surviving portraits, because Sera refused to hear of them leaving before dawn and because, Paloma admitted privately, she wasn't ready. She sat a long time by the lamp with the portrait of the family, studying the laughing girl on the drafting table, trying to find herself in it.

"You have her hands," Tamsin said quietly, from across the alcove. She was cleaning her bowstring, not looking up. "Ink-stained. You always fidget with things when you're thinking. Pens, twine, that poor spoon at the inn."

"You noticed that?"

"I notice everything about you," Tamsin said, and then went very red, and became extremely interested in her bowstring, and Paloma decided, generously, to let her live it down.

Later, Sera brought them tea brewed from pine needles and ash-filtered snow, and told them stories — Corvin stories, dozens of them, each one a small lamp lit in the dark of Paloma's missing years. How he'd mapped the Stillwater Valley himself, decades ago, and come back shaken and wouldn't say why. How he'd argued with the Royal Survey about the mountain at the valley's northern edge, insisting there was a door in it that no survey instrument could find. How he'd laughed at his own funeral arrangements, reportedly, and planned them down to the flowers.

"He talked about the Wolf," Paloma said, near midnight. "Did he ever say — what it actually *is*?"

Sera was quiet for a moment.

"He said it was older than the kingdom. Older than names, almost — that 'Wolf' was just the shape people gave it because a guardian needs a shape or it frightens folk." The old woman's hands folded around her cup. "And he said the strangest thing, once, when he thought I wasn't listening. He said: *it doesn't guard the mountain. It guards the remembering.*" She sighed. "I thought it was a cartographer's poetry. Now I'm not so sure."

Outside, far off in the hills, something howled.

It was a long sound, low and rising, and it was not any wolf Paloma had ever heard in the valley — it was too vast, too deliberate, too *patient*. The map, hanging against her ribs, warmed suddenly through the cloth like a stone left too long in the sun.

Tamsin was on her feet with an arrow nocked before the echo faded. "Vane?"

"No." Paloma pressed her hand flat against the map, feeling it pulse. "No, it's — it's pointing north. Into the mountains." She looked up, and found that she was smiling, and didn't know why. "There's a door in that mountain. My father said so. And I think it just heard me coming."

Sera Ilvane poured herself more pine-needle tea, entirely unbothered, and said:

"Well. Then you'd best rest, child. Doors that wait thirty years hate to be kept waiting longer."
