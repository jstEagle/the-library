## 10. The Wolf Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had been watching them for two days before either of them admitted it.

Paloma noticed first — or rather, she noticed that Tamsin kept glancing back at it, the way a person glances at a stranger who has been walking behind them for too long to be coincidence. The peak rose out of the valley's northern shoulder like a bent old man in a grey cloak, and no matter how many switchbacks they climbed or how far the road curved east, it stayed exactly where it was: ahead of them, patient, enormous.

"It's not moving," Tamsin said on the second morning, around a mouthful of hard cheese. "I checked."

"Mountains generally don't."

"This one might. I've decided not to trust anything in this valley." She swallowed and squinted up at it. "The birds don't sing here, did you notice? And the streams run quiet. It's like the whole place is holding its breath."

Paloma held the map against her knee and tried not to think about why her hands were steady when everything else in her was not. Since the ford, since Tamsin's knife had hovered over her heart and then, miraculously, sheathed itself, the map had grown bolder. In daylight it was only leather and ink — beautiful ink, her father's ink, though she still couldn't say those words aloud without her chest tightening. But at night, when she slept, it whispered. And each morning the route drawn across its surface had shifted slightly, as if some careful hand were redrawing it while she dreamed, shortening the way, correcting her errors, leading her north.

Leading them both north, because Tamsin refused to leave, and Paloma had stopped pretending she wanted her to.

"The mark is close," she said. "Look." She turned the map so Tamsin could see. Where the mountain's peak should have been, the cartographer had drawn instead a small circle — not a summit but a seal, a wax-stamp of ink, and beneath it, in letters so fine she'd needed firelight and squinting to find them: *the door remembers what the world forgets.*

"That's cheerful," said Tamsin. "Very reassuring. Doors that remember. What does a door have to remember that's so important?"

"I don't know. But my father thought it worth dying over."

Tamsin went quiet at that, which she'd been doing more often lately. She had a whole vocabulary of silences, Paloma was learning — the guilty one, the stubborn one, the one where she was chewing on something true and hadn't yet decided whether to spit it out. This was the third kind.

They walked on. Behind them, the valley fell away in folds of green and silver, Stillwater spreading its slow rivers like spilled milk. Ahead, the mountain waited, and the map grew warm against Paloma's ribs, and somewhere under her skin, in the place where Maren's magic had guttered out at the boundary stones all those weeks ago, something else stirred — older than hedge-craft, quieter than any spell, patient as stone.

That night they made camp in the last stand of pines below the treeline, and Paloma dreamed.

* * *

She was standing in a hall she had never seen and knew perfectly.

It was vast and low-ceilinged, carved from the mountain's own bones, and every surface was covered in names. Not writing — names. They moved like fish under ice, silver and quick, thousands upon thousands of them, and she understood without being told that each one had belonged to someone, and that none of them had been forgotten, because this was the place where forgetting came to die.

In the center of the hall stood a wolf.

It was too large for the room and fit inside it anyway, the way a river fits inside its bed. Its fur was the grey of winter dusk, its eyes the amber of lamplight through honey, and when it looked at her, Paloma felt a sensation she had no word for — as if someone had opened a door in her chest she hadn't known was there, and behind it was a small girl in a sunlit kitchen, laughing at nothing.

"You came," the wolf said.

And it was her father's voice.

Not an imitation. Not an echo. His voice, warm and rough-edged, the voice that had sung her to sleep in a life she could not remember, and Paloma's knees nearly went out from under her. She gripped the nearest pillar — the stone hummed faintly under her palm, full of sleeping names — and stared.

"You're dead," she said. Her voice sounded very small in the vastness. "Maren said — everyone says —"

"My body died, little map-maker. Bodies do." The wolf's ears tilted, and there was something almost wry in the tilt, something that made her throat ache. "But you are confusing me with my coat. I am older than coats. I am older than the shape I wear. Ask me what I am."

"What are you?"

"A witness." He stepped closer, and the floor did not tremble, because he was careful; she understood that suddenly, that this enormous creature moved through the world the way a good nurse moves through a sickroom. "When your kings were shepherds, I watched. When Aldric took his throne, I watched. When they burned the archives and unmade the records and called it peace, I watched, and I remembered, and remembering is the one thing in this world they cannot burn."

"But they killed you. The stories say Aldric slew the Wolf beneath the mountain."

The wolf laughed, and it was such a familiar laugh — low, delighted, the laugh of a man who has just seen a joke hidden inside the world — that Paloma laughed too, wetly, helplessly, before she could stop herself.

"Oh, he believes he did. That's the mercy of it. You cannot kill a story by killing its teller, child, and you cannot kill a guardian by killing its body. We learned that lesson long ago, we who guard. So we stopped keeping our lives in bodies." He lowered his great head until his eyes were level with hers, and the amber light in them flickered like candleflame. "We keep them in bloodlines. In daughters. In memory."

The hall went very quiet. Even the swimming names seemed to pause.

"No," Paloma said.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"I don't need the rest. No." She backed up a step, and the pillar hummed against her spine. "I'm a hedge-witch's apprentice. I can cure warts and find lost goats and I once set a broken arm so badly the farmer thanked me anyway out of politeness. I'm not — I'm not a *guardian*. I'm nobody. That was the whole point of me. That was what I ran away to be."

The wolf regarded her with terrible gentleness.

"Do you know why the valley forgets?" he asked. "Why magic dies at the boundary stones? Your mother's people built it that way, long before you were born — a silence, a hiding place, a pocket of the world with its name sewn shut. Not to hide a weapon. To hide a *child*. Every warding stone in Stillwater was laid by hands that loved you before you existed."

"I don't believe you."

"You do. That's why you're angry."

She hated him for saying it, and loved him for the voice, and both at once was more than she could carry. She turned away, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the drifting names on the walls. One of them swam closer to her, silver and bright, and she caught a glimpse of it — *Freya*, it said, or almost said, and then dove back down into the shoal before she could be sure.

"Why now?" she asked the wall. "Why wake up now? Twenty-odd years of sleeping. Why pull me out of the only life I have?"

"Because the man who burned the archives has begun to fear what he cannot burn. Because his inquisitor carries a relic that unmakes memory, and relics like that do not stop with kings' enemies. Because the free cities sleep, and their sleep is deepening into something that looks a great deal like death." A pause. "And because you are ready, though you will argue with me about it."

"I'm not ready. I'm *hungry* and I'm scared and my boots leak."

"All true readiness begins with leaking boots."

"Don't be funny at me. My father doesn't get to be funny at me. I don't even—" Her voice cracked. "I don't even remember him. Do you understand that? You have his voice and I have nothing. I have a hole where he should be. Maren wouldn't talk about him. Nobody would talk about him. And now a wolf tells me I've been a locked box my whole life and I'm supposed to just — open?"

The wolf was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, the humor was gone, and what remained was so tender it hurt worse than anger would have.

"I watched him draw your first map," he said. "You were three days old and he drew the shape of your hand and labeled it *here be everything*. He hid you in silence because he loved you louder than he feared the dark. And when the riders came for him, he sent the map out ahead of you like a lantern, and he died knowing you would curse him for it someday." The amber eyes closed briefly. "He was right, and he would be proud of you for it. Cursing the dead honestly is a kind of loyalty."

Paloma sat down on the humming floor, because her legs had finished negotiating, and put her face in her hands.

"What happens if I say yes?" she asked, into her palms.

"You wake me fully. The valley's silence breaks. The free cities remember their own history, and Aldric's lie starves in daylight." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And the vessel that holds me must give up what she carries. Memory is the coin, little one. There is no other price."

"How much?"

"All of it."

The word landed in the hall like a dropped stone. All of it. Maren's crooked cottage and the smell of feverfew. The boundary stones at dawn. Tamsin's scowl, Tamsin's terrible campfire coffee, Tamsin's hand pulling her up the riverbank at the ford. The innkeeper's grumbling kindness. The storm. The dying stranger's blood on her hands. Her own name.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I sleep on, and you walk away, and live a small warm life, and the world outside the valley grows colder year by year. No one will blame you. Least of all me." The wolf lay down, folding his enormity neatly, the way her father — the way *someone* — used to fold a coat over a chair. "I have waited centuries. I can wait longer. Guardians are patient. It is girls who are brave, and girls should never be rushed."

"I'm not brave."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Brave is what they call you afterward."

Paloma lifted her head. The names on the walls had begun to move again, swirling, and among them she saw shapes now — faces, kitchens, storms, a lullaby half-remembered — her whole life swimming in the silver like fish coming up to feed. All of it, offered up. All of it, taken.

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." The wolf's eyes crinkled, and it was so exactly the expression of a man watching his daughter argue with a market vendor that she nearly wept. "Dreams are generous with time. Take all you need. But little map-maker — one thing, before you go."

"What?"

"He didn't only send the map ahead of you. He sent a name. You'll find it at the door, when you're ready to knock. And whatever you decide—" the great head dipped, "—know that you were never a locked box. You were a letter. And letters are meant to arrive."

* * *

She woke with frost on her eyelashes and Tamsin shaking her shoulder, and the first thing she saw was her friend's face wearing an expression Paloma had never seen on it before: naked, undisguised fear.

"You were talking in your sleep," Tamsin said. "Not muttering. *Talking.* Full sentences. And the map—" She jerked her chin toward Paloma's pack, and Paloma turned, and felt her breath go out of her.

The map lay on the frozen ground between them, glowing. Not whispering, not hinting — glowing, a soft gold seeping up through the leather like dawn through a shutter, and the ink had redrawn itself one final time. The route ended now at the mountain's root, at a place where the artist had drawn, in lines fine as hair, a door.

And above the door, new letters, still cooling:

*She knows the way in. Ask her.*

Tamsin's hand had found her knife hilt, not threateningly — reflexively, the way another person might grab a friend's sleeve. "Paloma. What did it say to you?"

Paloma looked at the mountain. Somewhere under all that stone, something enormous and gentle was waiting on her answer, patient as morning, wearing her father's voice like a borrowed coat. She thought about leaking boots. She thought about letters arriving. She thought about a small warm life, and how the cold outside the valley would come for it eventually anyway, the way winter comes for every garden.

"I'll tell you on the way," she said, and stood, and picked up the map.

Behind them, far down the valley road, so distant it might have been a trick of the frost-light, something glinted — metal, moving, deliberate. Tamsin saw it too. Her jaw tightened.

"Riders," she said quietly. "Coming fast. Coming *here*."

Paloma closed her hand around the map's warmth and started toward the door that remembered.

"Then we'd better knock."
