## 11. A Bargain in Bramblewick

Bramblewick smelled of bread and river mud, which Tamsin had always considered the two most honest smells in the world. Cities that smelled of perfume were lying to you about something. Cities that smelled of bread and mud were only telling you what they were.

She left Paloma at the tanner's yard on the edge of town — the girl had taken one look at the crowded market square and gone the color of old porridge, so Tamsin hadn't pushed. "Buy us a cart," she'd said. "And don't talk to anyone charming."

"I never do," Paloma had said, with the small dry flicker of humor that was starting to surface now that she wasn't actively being hunted through a storm. "Charming people make me nervous."

"Good instinct. Stay nervous."

Now Tamsin walked into Bramblewick proper with her hood up and her coin purse heavy, and let herself do something she hadn't done in three weeks: relax by exactly one degree.

It was a mistake. She knew it was a mistake even as she did it. But there were turnips in the market, actual fresh turnips, and a farrier who didn't ask questions, and a chandler selling tallow candles that wouldn't gutter in wind, and for one golden hour Tamsin of the Ford got to be an ordinary woman running ordinary errands instead of a scout escorting the last living vessel of an ancient guardian god through usurper-held country.

She haggled over the cart. She won, because she always won, because the carter made the error of glancing at her sword and assuming she was stupid enough to be bluffed by a man holding a mallet. She bought dried plums, hard cheese, a sack of oats, a jar of honey that she told herself was for Paloma's tea and not at all because she had missed honey like a missing tooth. She bought a second blanket, because Paloma shivered in her sleep and pretended not to.

It was while she was loading the cart, feeling absurdly pleased with herself, that she heard her name.

Not *Tamsin*. Her full name. Spoken in a voice she hadn't heard in four years, from behind her, quiet as a knife going back into its sheath.

"Tamsin Ellery Vane-Ford."

No. Not Vane. Her mother's family name, the one they'd stopped using after the war went bad. Nobody alive used that name except —

She turned around slowly.

Corren stood between a barrel of salt fish and a stack of empty crates, wearing clothes too fine for Bramblewick and too plain for court, which meant he was dressed to be overlooked, which meant he was here on business. He was twenty-three now. He'd been nineteen when she last saw him, all elbows and loud opinions, and the intervening years had sanded him down into something leaner and quieter, with their mother's dark eyes and a new scar cutting through one eyebrow.

"Hello, Tam," he said.

The cart pin dropped out of her hand. She watched it roll under the salt fish barrel rather than pick it up, because picking it up would mean moving, and moving might mean this wasn't happening.

"You're supposed to be in Aldric's capital," she said. Stupid thing to say. Obvious thing to say. But her mouth had gone ahead without her.

"I was." He glanced down the street, unhurried, a man checking the weather. "I have leave. Three days. I told them I wanted to see the river towns before winter closed the passes." A pause. "That was even mostly true."

"Corren." His name came out cracked down the middle. She crossed the distance between them before she decided to, and hugged him, and felt him hug her back — hard, both arms, no court-trained restraint at all — and for maybe ten seconds Tamsin of the Ford, who did not cry, cried into her little brother's shoulder in front of a barrel of salt fish in Bramblewick market.

Then she stepped back, wiped her face with her sleeve, and said, "Who's watching you?"

"No one yet. That's the point of dressing boring." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that used to get them both in trouble at the ford when they were children, and then it faded, and underneath it was something else. Something careful. "Tam. We need to talk. Not here."

* * *

He took her to a room above a dye-house, up three flights of stairs that creaked in protest, into a space that smelled of mordant and wool and had one window looking out over the river. He checked the street below it twice before he sat down.

"You're scaring me," Tamsin said. "You should know that. You're my baby brother and you're scaring me."

"I'm not your baby brother right now. Right now I'm a hostage with a message, and you need to hear the whole thing before you decide what I am." He folded his hands on his knees. Their mother used to fold her hands like that when she was about to say something difficult. "How is your charge?"

The floor tilted. Tamsin kept her face still through long practice, but inside, everything dropped an inch, like a stair that wasn't where you remembered.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Tamsin." Gently. "I'm not Vane. I'm not one of his people. But I know what I know, and what I know is that a girl matching the description of the Stillwater vessel came south out of the valley three weeks ago with a free-city scout, and that every garrison from here to the capital has been given that description, and that the inquisitor himself has moved his headquarters to Harrowgate, which is two days' ride from where you're standing." He paused. "And that our family's protection expires at midwinter unless someone gives him more than descriptions."

The room was very quiet. Down in the dye-house, someone was beating wet wool against a stone, a slow rhythmic thud like a heart that couldn't decide whether to keep going.

"How much do they pay you?" Tamsin asked. It came out flat and cold, and she hated it, and she said it anyway, because if she didn't say something cruel right now she was going to say something true instead, and the true thing would break her.

"They don't pay me anything," Corren said. "That's the part you don't understand. Hostages don't get paid. Hostages get *kept*." He unfolded his hands, refolded them. "Do you remember what happened to the Delmarre family? After the elder daughter ran messages for the river league?"

Tamsin remembered. Everyone remembered. The Delmarres had been a shipping family, respectable, careful. The daughter had been careless once. The family had been given a choice, publicly, in the square: the daughter, or the rest of them. The father had chosen the rest of them. They said he'd been right to. They said it very quietly, and only ever indoors.

"Papa signed the wardship papers himself," Corren said. "Did you know that? Four years ago. He sat at a table with a man from the chancery and signed me over, and Mama stood behind him with her hand on his shoulder so he wouldn't stop halfway through, and afterward Papa went out to the workshop and worked until dawn on a chair leg he sanded down to nothing. I saw it. Before they took me. I looked out the window and the light was on all night." His voice stayed level. That was the worst part. He'd learned levelness somewhere, in those years, and Tamsin could imagine exactly where. "They treat me well, Tam. I eat at good tables. I've read half the library. But I am a chair leg that hasn't been sanded yet, and everyone in that palace knows it, including me."

"Corren—"

"Let me finish. Please. Because I practiced this speech the whole ride here and if you interrupt me I'll lose it, and I can't afford to lose it, because the next part is the part where I betray either you or everyone else I love, and I'd like to do it without weeping in a dye-house."

So Tamsin sat on the windowsill, where she could watch the river, because she couldn't watch his face for this, and she listened.

"Vane doesn't want her dead," Corren said. "I've been close enough to him to read his correspondence — that's not boasting, it's just what happens when you're furniture; nobody minds furniture seeing things. He wants her delivered. Alive, unharmed, willing if possible. There's a reward attached, and the reward isn't money. The reward is wardships dissolved. All of them. Every family in the free cities holding a hostage gets their people back, sealed by the king's own seal, if the vessel is delivered by midwinter." He looked at her. "Do you understand what that means? Not just me. The Ostreler boy. Little Maren Delmarre's granddaughter — yes, *that* Delmarre line, they kept a cousin. Eleven families, Tam. Eleven children come home for Wintermark."

"And all you have to do," Tamsin said to the river, "is sell her."

"All I have to do is write a letter," Corren said. "That's all anyone's asking. A letter saying where you'll be. You wouldn't even have to lift a hand. Vane's riders would do the rest, and they'd be gentle, because gentle is the instruction. And you'd be gone before it happened, and no one would ever trace it to the Ford family, and at midwinter I'd walk through our front door and Mama would—" He stopped. For the first time, the level voice wavered. "Mama makes that barley soup. With the caraway. You know the one. I dream about it. Isn't that stupid? Four years of palaces and libraries and I dream about soup."

"It's not stupid," Tamsin said.

"I wrote the letter already." He reached into his coat and set it on the table between them. Sealed. Addressed in a neat clerk's hand to Harrowgate. "I wrote it before I ever found you. That's the coward's version — write first, find later, tell yourself the finding was fate. And then I saw you in the market buying honey, and you had flour on your sleeve, and you were arguing with a carter about a wheel, and you looked—" His breath caught. "You looked like you. And I thought, oh no. Oh no, I wrote a letter against *her*."

Tamsin looked at the letter. Small thing. One page. Eleven children on one side of the scale and one sleeping girl with flour on her sleeve on the other, and the scale balanced so neatly, so terribly neatly, and the whole trick of it was that the letter didn't even require her to do anything. Just to look away. Just to be a few miles off at the wrong moment. Evil, she thought, wore such comfortable clothes these days.

"What are you asking me, Corren?" she said finally. "Because if you're asking my permission, the answer's no, and if you're asking my forgiveness in advance, the answer's also no, and if you're asking me to take the letter and burn it, then hand it over, because I'll enjoy that."

"I'm asking you to tell me what to do," he said, and his voice broke clean through, twenty-three years old again and nineteen again and nine again, standing on the bank of the ford. "Because I can't. I've turned it over every mile of the road and I can't. If I burn it, I choose her over Mama. If I send it, I choose Mama over you. And you're the one who taught me that choosing people was the whole point of being alive, and now the choosing is a knife, and I—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have come. I should have sent it and ridden home and never—"

Tamsin got up off the windowsill and crossed the room and put her arms around her brother, the way she had when he was small and the ford flooded and he was sure the water would take the house. She held him while he shook, and she looked past his shoulder at the letter on the table, and she thought about Paloma asleep in the cart that afternoon outside Harlow Mill, one hand curled near the map even in sleep, like a child holding a promise.

*What would she do,* Tamsin thought. *If she knew. What would she do.*

And the answer arrived immediately, horribly, and it was: *she'd give herself up.* That was the whole shape of the girl. Paloma Thackeray, who hid a wounded stranger against every survival instinct she owned, who flinched from her own magic and carried a dead man's map across half the world anyway — she would stand up in whatever room they brought her to and say *take me, let the others go*, and she would mean it, and that was precisely why Tamsin could not, would not, ever let it happen.

"Listen to me," she said into Corren's hair. "Burn the letter."

"But Mama—"

"Burn the letter, and listen. Midwinter is eight weeks away. Vane thinks the vessel is a rumor with legs. Every day she stays unfound, his masters doubt him a little more, and doubtful kings cut budgets, and cut budgets mean fewer riders, and fewer riders mean she moves faster. Your letter doesn't save eleven children, Corren. It saves eleven children *if it works*, and if it works it puts a girl who's done nothing wrong into the hands of a man who unmakes memories for a living, and if it fails — if she slips his net, and she will, because I'll make sure she does — then Vane has failed twice, and failing men get cruel, and cruel men don't honor bargains. You'd trade eleven hostages for twelve, and the twelfth would be you."

He pulled back and looked at her, red-eyed. "You've thought about this."

"I think about everything," Tamsin said. "It's exhausting. Ask anyone." She picked up the letter. Heavy little thing, one page, eleven futures. She held it over the dye-house brazier, and hesitated — one honest heartbeat of hesitation, because love is not a clean thing and she would not pretend otherwise — and then she let it go.

The paper caught, curled, and was gone. Eleven children stayed hostages. One girl stayed free. The arithmetic sat in Tamsin's chest like a swallowed stone, and she understood, with terrible clarity, that she would carry it the rest of her life, whichever way she'd chosen.

"Now," she said, when the ash had settled. "Vane moved his headquarters to Harrowgate, you said. Two days' ride."

"Yes. Why—"

"Because I need to know how many riders he keeps, and which roads they watch, and whether he's put men on the valley passes." She was already counting supplies in her head, already re-planning the route north. "And you're going to tell me, because you're furniture, and furniture sees things."

Corren laughed — a wet, broken laugh, but a real one. "You're terrifying. You know that? Mama says I get it from her side."

"You get everything from her side. Now sit down. We have until your leave runs out, and I intend to use every hour." She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back at him. "Corren. Thank you for not sending it."

"I nearly did."

"I know. Nearly counts for something. Nearly is where all the important living happens." She opened the door onto the stairwell and the smell of dye and the ordinary evening noise of an ordinary town. "Stay here. I have to go lie to my friend about why I was gone all afternoon, and then tomorrow we ride north fast, because starting tonight, we're the ones being hunted."

Below the window, the river went on toward the sea, patient and indifferent, carrying everything with it eventually.

Two days later, at a crossroads north of Bramblewick, Tamsin would wake to hoofbeats and torchlight and the sound of Paloma screaming her name — and she would know, before she even opened her eyes, whose handwriting had guided the riders there.

But that night, in a room above a dye-house, she only held her brother's hand and memorized the number of Vane's riders, and told herself the letter was ash, and ash was the end of things.

She was wrong. Ash, it turns out, remembers where it came from.
