## 12. The Sold Name

The crossroads had a name, according to the leaning post at its center: Bramblewick Junction, though there was no bramble in sight and no wick either, unless you counted the crooked signpost itself, which leaned like a drunk uncle telling the same story for the fortieth time.

"Charming place," Tamsin said, chewing on a strip of dried apple. "Very atmospheric. If we're ambushed here, at least we'll die somewhere with character."

"Don't say that," Paloma said.

"What, dying?"

"Ambushed."

Tamsin raised an eyebrow. "You'd rather be surprised?"

"I'd rather not think about it. Thinking about it makes it more likely."

"That is not how anything works," Tamsin said, "but I admire your commitment to magical thinking. It's very on-brand for a witch."

"I was barely a witch. I boiled herbs wrong for six years."

"You escaped a hedge-witch mistress, crossed an anti-magic valley, carried a map that whispers to people in their sleep, and outran an inquisitor. At some point, Paloma, you have to accept that 'barely' is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Paloma almost smiled. Almost. The map rode against her ribs where she'd sewn it into her coat lining, and it had been quiet for two days — which should have been a relief, but felt instead like a held breath. Maps, she had learned, did not go quiet out of courtesy.

They had left the free city of Bramblewick at dawn with fresh horses, bread, and — though neither of them said it aloud — the knowledge that Vane knew they were this far south. The plan was simple: cut west along the old drover's road, avoid the king's road entirely, and reach the mountain gate before the first snows. Simple plans, Maren used to say, are how the world apologizes before it breaks your heart.

They watered the horses at the stone trough while the afternoon light went long and gold. A cart rattled past toward the free cities, driven by a woman who didn't look at them twice. Somewhere a lark was making an absolute spectacle of itself.

"Right," Tamsin said, swinging back into the saddle. "West. No stopping until—"

The arrows came from the hedgerow.

Not many — three, four — and none meant to kill. They thudded into the trough, the signpost, and one into the dirt between Paloma's horse's hooves, and the horse did what horses do, which is everything at once. Paloma hit the ground hard enough to see stars, rolled under the cart-track's low wall, and heard Tamsin shouting her name over the sound of riders coming down both roads at once.

Grey cloaks. Usurper's grey, with the black sun of Aldric's house stitched at the shoulder. Six of them, fanning out with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and never once been surprised by the outcome.

And at their center, riding forward as if arriving slightly late to a dinner party he'd organized himself, came Inquisitor Vane.

He looked exactly as Paloma remembered, which was somehow worse. Neat. Patient. A thin man with a thin smile, the kind of face that would apologize beautifully while it ruined you.

"Paloma Thackeray," he called, pleasantly. "Or do you prefer the valley name? We can discuss it. I have all afternoon, and you have nowhere to be."

Paloma pressed herself against the wall, heart hammering, and did the only thing her body knew how to do after six years of Maren's drills: she reached for magic.

It came. That was the horror of it — it came easily now, flooding up through her hands like water finding a crack, wild and bright and loud after the valley's long silence. She threw it without shape or plan, a burst of raw force that knocked two riders from their saddles and set the hedgerow on fire, which she hadn't intended, and which the lark, presumably, objected to strongly.

"Run!" Tamsin screamed — and then, strangely, screamed something else. "*Not toward me!*"

But Paloma was already running, because running was the plan, and the plan was simple, and simple plans—

A rider cut her off. She ducked his grab, felt fingers close on her collar and tear free, stumbled, kept going. Behind her she heard steel ring against steel — Tamsin, fighting, and fighting *badly*, or fighting *carefully*, and Paloma had seen Tamsin fight enough times now to know the difference.

She made the treeline. She turned, map burning against her ribs, magic still crackling off her fingertips like static —

— and saw Tamsin lower her sword.

Saw her raise her hands.

Saw Vane ride close, lean down from his saddle, and speak to her quietly, the way you speak to someone you've already paid.

* * *

Paloma ran until the trees closed behind her and her lungs burned and her legs gave out, and then she sat down against a fallen oak with her arms around her knees and shook.

The map had gone hot against her ribs. Not whispering now — *insistent*, pulsing like a second heartbeat, as if it too had understood what she'd seen.

"No," she told it. "No, you don't get to be right about things."

It pulsed again, gently, almost kindly.

She made herself think. That was the discipline Maren had actually taught her well: when everything burns, count what's left. She had the map. She had a knife, half a loaf of bread, and a horse that was, at last report, bolting in the opposite direction with admirable commitment. She did not have Tamsin.

She did not have Tamsin because Tamsin had lowered her sword.

Because Tamsin had shouted *not toward me* — not *run*, not *this way*, but *not toward me*, as if Paloma were the danger, as if Tamsin needed her to stay away from something. From some arrangement. From some *letter*.

Paloma's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking on water.

Two nights ago, outside Bramblewick, Tamsin had gone into the city alone to resupply. She'd been gone four hours. She'd come back with bread, oats, and a strange, brittle brightness, laughing too easily, sleeping facing away from the fire. Paloma had noticed. She'd told herself it was nothing. She'd told herself a great many convenient things, because trust, once given, defends itself like a mother bear.

And before that — before that, all the way back at the ford, Tamsin had been sent to recover the map *at any cost*. Those had been her own words, confessed in the firelight, back when "any cost" had apparently seemed like a figure of speech.

Paloma put her forehead against her knees.

The sun crawled. The lark, having followed her, resumed its performance somewhere overhead, tireless and oblivious. Eventually — she didn't know how long — she heard footsteps in the leaves. Unhurried. Familiar. Deliberately audible, the way you walk when you want to be heard approaching.

"I know you're there," Paloma said.

"I know you know." Tamsin came around the oak and stood at the edge of arm's reach, and Paloma made herself look at her. There was dirt on Tamsin's cheek and her knuckles were split and her eyes were red-rimmed and wrecked, and she looked, Paloma thought, like someone who had been crying for hours and had run out of tears and arrived at something worse, which was clarity.

"They let you go," Paloma said flatly.

"They let me go."

"Why?"

"Because I'm more useful to them loose than tied." Tamsin's voice cracked on *useful*. "Because Vane thinks — Vane *hopes* — that you'll take me back. Because a friend at your side is worth ten riders in a hedgerow."

"And are you? Useful to them?"

"Yes." Tamsin said it simply, and it was the simplicity that cut. "Yes, I am. I was. Paloma—"

"The letter." Paloma stood. Her legs held, mostly. "There was a letter. In Bramblewick. You wrote to them."

Tamsin flinched as if struck. "How—"

"*How do I know?*" Paloma laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Because you shouted *not toward me*. Because you dropped your sword like a good little asset. Because you've been strange since the city, and I noticed, and I *excused* it, because I trusted you, which — congratulations, Tamsin, you found the one weapon that works on me."

"I wrote one letter." Tamsin's hands were shaking; she shoved them behind her back like a child caught stealing. "One. Before I met you at the ford — no. After. After the ford, when I let you keep the map, I sent word ahead to my handler in Bramblewick. It's protocol. You don't break protocol, Paloma, you don't understand, they hold—"

"Hold what?" Paloma stepped closer. "What do they hold?"

And Tamsin broke. All at once, like a dam, like the whole careful structure of her coming down in a single breath.

"My family." The words came out ragged. "My brother Denny is a ward of Aldric's court, which is a polite word for hostage, and my parents' farm sits on land the crown has wanted for nine years, and every free-city scout knows the rule: cooperate, and your people sleep safe. Refuse—" She swallowed. "Refuse, and you find out what the crown does with farms. With brothers. I've cooperated for six years. Six years of small things, safe things, names of smugglers, routes nobody uses. And then you fell into my river with a map that could burn the whole kingdom down, and my handler wrote to me and said, *deliver the vessel, and your brother comes home.*"

The woods were very quiet. Even the lark had stopped.

"So I wrote back," Tamsin whispered. "I told them the road. The crossroads. The hour." She lifted her chin, and her eyes were streaming now, and she didn't wipe them. "And then I stood in that hedgerow for two days watching the road, hating myself, and when it happened — when I saw them close around you — I couldn't. Paloma, I *couldn't*. I shouted at you to run. I fought them. Badly, yes, on purpose, yes, but I fought them, and then I stood in front of Vane and I told him to his face that if they touched you I would spend the rest of my life making his job impossible, and he smiled, because he doesn't need me to help him anymore. He just needs me to make sure you don't trust anyone ever again. Which — look at us. Look at this. He's already won that."

Paloma stood very still.

Everything in her wanted to walk away. Everything in her wanted to believe. Both wants sat in her chest like stones, and between them, small and stubborn, was the memory of a woman at a river ford who'd had every reason to take the map and hadn't; who'd shared her bread and her watch-fires and her terrible jokes; who had slept, night after night, with her back to Paloma's back, which is a thing you cannot fake. Trust isn't built in grand gestures. It's built in the thousand small surrenders of letting someone stand behind you in the dark.

But she'd also slept soundly the night after Bramblewick.

"I believe you're sorry," Paloma said at last. Tamsin's face flickered with hope, and Paloma held up a hand before it could bloom. "I believe you're sorry, and I believe you love your brother, and I believe you chose him over me the way anyone would choose breathing over drowning. I even believe you fought Vane. What I don't believe — what I *can't* believe right now — is that it won't happen again. Because next time the price will be higher, and you'll pay it, because he's your brother and I'm a girl you've known for a season."

"He's my brother," Tamsin said, "and you're the only person alive who can wake the Wolf, and those stopped being different sizes of thing the moment I watched you throw fire out of your bare hands." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, angrily. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to keep me where I can see you. Because if Vane takes you, my letter stops mattering, my bargain dies with your usefulness, and Denny stays a hostage forever. Our interests are aligned, Paloma. Horribly, humiliatingly aligned. Let me fix this."

"The map says otherwise," Paloma said. "The map says I go to the mountain alone."

"The map," Tamsin said, "is a piece of leather that talks in your sleep. Forgive me if I don't take its side in an argument."

Despite everything — despite the burned hedgerow and the arrows and the sight of Tamsin lowering her sword — something in Paloma's chest cracked, and it wasn't breaking. It was the other thing. The worse thing. The thing where you laugh when you absolutely should not.

"You're infuriating," she said.

"I'm loyal," Tamsin said. "It presents as infuriating. Ask anyone. Ask my handler, who currently has a very detailed letter describing my insubordination."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. Then she pulled open her coat, drew out the map, and held it where Tamsin could see it — held it, notably, and did not hand it over.

"One condition," Paloma said. "From now on, no letters. No handlers. No protocol. If your family needs saving, we save them ourselves, after the mountain. Together. That's the price of walking beside me, and it's not negotiable, and if you agree to it and break it, I will not run from you next time. I will fight."

Something shifted in Tamsin's face — relief and terror in equal measure, the expression of a woman who has just been handed a rope and told not to hang herself with it.

"Together," she said. "After the mountain. I swear it on Denny's head, which is the only oath I've got left that means anything."

"Then we move. Now. Vane let you go because he expects you to lead him to me, which means he's watching the obvious roads, which means we take the drover tracks north and we don't stop until—"

The map moved.

Not fluttered — *moved*, the ink crawling across the leather like ants, lines rearranging themselves, and both women stared down at it as a new mark bloomed near its western edge: a small, sharp symbol like an eye, and beneath it, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh as a wound, a single line of writing that had not been there a moment before.

*He carries the relic,* it said. *He is three hours behind you.*

Paloma looked up. Tamsin had gone white.

"He showed it to me," Tamsin whispered. "In the hedgerow. A little box of black glass. He said it could unmake a memory like snuffing a candle, and he said—" Her voice failed, and she steadied it. "He said he was saving it for you."

The lark started up again somewhere overhead, tireless, absurd, singing its heart out over a world that was about to change.

Paloma folded the map against her heart.

"Three hours," she said. "Then we'd better make them count."
