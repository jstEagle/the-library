## 13. Alone with the Truth

The wound in her side had opinions.

Paloma discovered this somewhere past midnight, when she shifted against the roots of a fallen beech and the pain spoke up — not a scream, nothing so dramatic, just a low, insistent commentary, like an innkeeper muttering about the state of your boots. *You ran six miles on this,* it said. *Six miles, on a leg that was already complaining about the last three.*

"Thank you," she whispered to it. "Very helpful."

The forest around her did not answer. The forest had not answered anything since the crossroads.

She lay still and took inventory, because Maren had taught her that a witch who panicked died of panic before whatever else got her. One: she was alive. Two: she had the map, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, warm as a sleeping cat. Three: Tamsin's face when the riders came out of the dark — Tamsin's face, which Paloma kept trying to look at from every angle, hoping one of them would show her something other than what she'd seen.

There was nothing else to see. She had watched her friend hand her name to Vane with her own eyes. Well — not her name exactly. A letter. A description. *The girl who carries it travels light, favors her left side on hills, will follow water if she can.* Small betrayals, itemized. The kind you could fit in an envelope.

Paloma pressed her palm flat over the wound and breathed through it until the muttering faded to a grumble. The cut was shallow but long, a rider's blade catching her as she'd gone over the fence into the bramble field, and it had stopped bleeding hours ago, which meant either she was healing or she'd simply run out of blood worth losing. With her luck lately, both seemed plausible.

*Alone with the truth,* she thought, and almost laughed. That was the whole of it, wasn't it. No Tamsin to argue with. No Vane to run from, for the moment — though he was out there, patient as winter. Just her, a dead tree, a dying fire, and a map that would not shut up.

She sat up. The world tilted politely and settled.

"All right," she told the darkness. "Let's read you properly, then."

*

She had been carrying the thing for weeks and had never once read all of it.

That sounded like cowardice when she put it plainly, so she made herself put it plainly, sitting cross-legged by the small fire she'd dared only because the canopy was thick and the night was wet. She had read its edges. She had read the parts that were useful — the mountain gate, the hall behind it, the route that wound toward the free cities like a thread pulled through cloth. She had read the maker's mark a hundred times, her father's mark, the little compass rose with a wolf curled in its center, and every time she touched it something behind her ribs turned over like a sleeper.

But the middle of the map — the dense, strange country between the gate and the cities, where the ink shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it — she had avoided. The way you avoid a door in your own house that's suddenly warm to the touch.

Tonight there was no reason left to avoid doors. Tonight she had nothing left to lose except the things she'd already lost.

She unwrapped the oilcloth and spread the leather across her knees.

The map woke under her hands. It always did that — a faint warmth, a sense of attention, like a dog lifting its head when you said its name. Ink crawled along lines that had been blank an hour ago. Rivers unspooled. And there, in the country between, words surfaced slowly, the way drowned things surface, one at a time, patient as anything her father had ever drawn.

*The Wolf is not woken,* the map said. *The Wolf is remembered.*

"I know that part," she murmured. "Tamsin told me. The archives burned, the names burned, everyone forgot—"

But the ink kept coming, and she stopped talking, because the next line was not about the Wolf at all.

*A guardian sleeps in a bloodline, not a body. To wake it, the vessel must open every door in herself and let the guest walk through. All of them. There is no partial waking. There is no keeping back a room or two for yourself. What the vessel surrenders, she surrenders wholly: every memory, every name, every face she has ever loved. The Wolf takes the past entire, and gives back only the present, and the strength to hold it.*

Paloma read it four times.

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which struck her as very relatable.

"That's it?" she said finally, to the map, to her father, to the night in general. "That's the great price? Not blood, not death — just—" She gestured, helplessly, at her own head. "Everything."

Her voice cracked on *everything*, and she was annoyed at it for cracking, and then she was annoyed at herself for being annoyed, and then she sat with the map in her lap and let the annoyance burn down to what was underneath, which was fear, plain and cold and enormous.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not even to herself, not even in the valley where magic couldn't hear her: she liked her memories.

Not the big ones, particularly. Her childhood was a locked room Maren had never given her the key to, and everything since had been porridge and herb-sorting and running. But the small ones — Maren humming off-key while she ground feverfew. The taste of the first apple of autumn, eaten sitting on the waystation fence with her feet swinging. Tamsin's laugh, which started in her shoulders before it reached her mouth. The way rain smelled on hot stone in Stillwater Valley, the only place in the world where she had ever felt safe.

All of it. Gone. Not lost the way her childhood was lost — stolen, hidden, taken without her consent — but *given*. Handed over willingly, door by door, room by room, until some stranger wearing her body walked out of the mountain with no idea why her hands knew how to bind a wound or brew a poultice or love a person.

"No wonder nobody volunteers," she whispered.

The ink shimmered, and more words came, quieter somehow, as if the map had heard her.

*Yet consider, daughter of the compass: the alternative.*

And beneath that, in letters that glowed a slow, sullen red, like coals:

*Burn this map, and the Wolf sleeps on. Burn this map, and Aldric keeps his throne, and Vane keeps his patience, and the free cities keep their comfortable forgetting. Burn this map, and no one ever has to know what was traded for their peace. You may live. You may remember. You may grow old telling yourself that a war unlived is a kindness, and perhaps it is, and perhaps you will never learn, because you will have chosen never to find out.*

Paloma stared at it for a long time.

"You're lecturing me," she said. "My father built a lecturing map."

It should have been funny. It was, a little — she could picture him, this man she didn't remember, bent over his desk with his tongue between his teeth, deciding that his daughter would need not just instructions but *persuasion*, because he had known her, hadn't he? He had known her well enough to guess she'd sit in a dark wood someday weighing the whole world against her own small hoard of memories, and he had known which way she'd lean.

He'd known she'd want to burn it.

* * *

She got the fire built up before she let herself think about it too closely. That was the trick of it, she realized — you couldn't make a decision like this while shivering. Cold made cowards of everyone; Maren used to say that despair was mostly a temperature problem. So she fed the flames until they stood up straight and threw real light, and she laid the map on a flat stone beside her, and she looked at it the way you look at a thing you are about to do.

One corner of the leather would catch easily. She'd seen the ink resist flame before — *the map's ink will not burn*, she'd learned that much fleeing the valley — but the leather itself, the body of the thing, that was just skin. Dead thing. Things that were dead once could die again.

"If I do this," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them realer, and realness was a kind of medicine, "then Tamsin's brother stays a hostage. Then Vane keeps hunting whoever he thinks has the map, which is me, forever, because I'll always be the girl who might have known. Then Aldric goes on being king, and everyone who remembers what he was — everyone the Wolf would remember — stays forgotten." She paused. "And I get to keep the apples."

The fire crackled. The owl asked again.

"And if I don't," she went on, "then I walk into that mountain and come out as nobody. Some poor hollowed-out girl who doesn't know why she's crying when a stranger with kind shoulders laughs. Who doesn't know Tamsin sold her and Tamsin saved her and both of those were true at once. Who doesn't know that she was ever afraid, or brave, or tired, or — " her voice did the cracking thing again — "or anyone at all."

She picked up a stick from the edge of the fire. It was burning at one end, a little orange comet, and she held it over the corner of the map and did not move it closer, and did not move it away.

Here was the trap her father had built her, and it was a good one, and she hated him for it with a love so fierce it startled her: there was no clean choice. That was the whole lesson, written in shifting ink across half a kingdom. Loyalty to the world meant disloyalty to herself. Loyalty to herself meant letting the world go on lying. Every road out of the crossroads cost someone everything, and the only question — the only one, the whole of it — was *who deserved to pay*, and whether she had any right to answer that on anyone's behalf but her own.

"You knew I'd sit here," she told the map softly. "You knew I'd hold the fire over you and talk to you like you're him. Did you also know what I'd choose?"

The ink moved. Slowly, reluctantly, the way a tired man turns over in bed:

*I hoped. Hoping is all a map can do. The walking is yours.*

Paloma laughed — a wet, broken sound, but a laugh — and lowered the burning stick, and held it there, trembling, an inch above her father's last work.

An inch. That was all the distance between the two lives. An inch of smoke and leather-smell and then no more Wolf, no more war, no more choosing. Just Paloma Thackeray, hedge-witch's runaway, keeper of apples and lullabies, alive and whole and small.

The stick trembled.

A drop of fire fell —

— and she snatched it back, heart slamming, because in the instant before it fell she had seen, rising off the heated leather, a ghost of ink that hadn't been there before. Faint. Fresh. Four words, surfacing like a face under ice:

*Tamsin is coming back.*

Paloma sat very still.

"She can't be," she said. "I saw her. I saw her face, I saw the letter, I saw—" But even as she said it, she was remembering the rest of it, the part she'd been refusing to look at from every angle: that Tamsin had fired the arrow that killed the second rider. That Tamsin had screamed *run* before the ambush broke, a half-second early, a half-second that had been the whole difference. That the letter had described a girl who favored her left side on hills — and Paloma favored her right, and always had, and Tamsin knew that better than anyone alive.

The description had been wrong on purpose. Or it had been right, and the wrongness was wishful thinking, and Paloma had no way to know which, and that was the cruelest arithmetic of all.

The fire hissed. The map lay quiet, its new words fading even as she watched, sinking back beneath the older ones, leaving only the choice glowing red where it had been.

*Burn me,* said the map, in her father's careful hand. *Or carry me. But decide soon, daughter of the compass. Because the man with the relic does not wait on your arithmetic — and I can smell him on the wind, and he is two valleys closer than you think.*

Paloma looked east, where the trees stood black against a sky just beginning, very faintly, to bruise gray.

Then she folded the map along its old creases, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tied it tight against her chest — unburnt, unreadable, and heavier than it had ever been.

"Carry," she said. "For now."

She stood, gasped at what the wound thought of standing, and began to walk north through the wet grass — toward the mountains, toward the gate, toward whatever waited behind the door her father had built her.

Behind her, far off but not far enough, something in the dark gave a single low note, like a horn learning patience.
