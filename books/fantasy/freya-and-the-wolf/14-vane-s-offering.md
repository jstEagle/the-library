## 14. Vane's Offering

Inquisitor Vane had a theory about fear, and it was this: fear was a door. Most men hammered on it. The clever ones learned to knock politely and wait.

He sat across the cell from Paloma Thackeray and knocked politely.

The cell was not, in fairness, much of a cell. He had chosen it himself — a guest room in the garrison house at Colder's Ford, stripped of its furniture except for two chairs, a table, a candle, and a window that looked out on an orchard going gold with autumn. No chains. No irons. Chains were for people who needed to be reminded they were caught. Paloma Thackeray had needed no reminding. She had walked out of the bramble thicket with her hands up before his riders had even finished dismounting, which had disappointed him slightly; he had been looking forward to the chase.

"You took the thorn path," he said now, pouring tea from the pot between them. "Through the blackberries. Clever. It cost you your left sleeve and, I'd guess, a fair amount of blood."

"It cost me my favorite coat," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, wrapped around the cup he'd given her, were not quite. "You could have just asked at the crossroads."

"I did ask at the crossroads. The scarecrow was unhelpful." He slid her cup toward her. "Chamomile. You've been running for eleven days; you'll sleep better."

"I'm not drinking anything you give me."

"Of course not." He drank from his own cup to demonstrate, then set it down. "I poisoned the other pot anyway. The one back at camp. For morale."

That earned him something — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, quickly strangled. Good. Humor was also a door, and she had opened it a crack without meaning to. Vane catalogued the crack and moved on.

He let the silence sit while she drank — because she did drink, eventually, thirst being honest in a way people rarely were — and studied her the way he studied everything: patiently, twice, and then once more from an angle she wouldn't expect.

She was thinner than the portraits. The archivist's descriptions had prepared him for the face — the cartographer's jaw, the mother's eyes — but not for the way she held herself, shoulders curled inward like a letter someone was trying to keep private. Eleven days of flight. A burned village behind her, a dead man's map against her ribs where she thought he couldn't see it. She kept touching her side when she thought he wasn't looking, a small unconscious gesture, the way a woman touches a locket.

The map was under her shirt, against her skin. That was interesting. People hid documents in packs and pockets. Skin meant something else entirely.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Miss Thackeray?" he said.

"My winning personality."

"Your arithmetic." He leaned back. "Most people in your position would have burned the map by now. Chapter three of every ballad: the heroine destroys the terrible secret and saves the world. But you haven't. Do you know why?"

"Because I don't know how," she said. "It doesn't burn. Believe me, I tried. I tried knives, too, and a river, and asking it very nicely."

"Yes." He nodded as if she'd confirmed a delivery schedule. "Because it isn't really a map. It's a memory. And memories can't be destroyed by anyone except the person who owns them." He watched that land. It landed. Her cup paused halfway to her mouth, then continued, deliberately, defiantly. "Which brings me to why we're here."

"We're here because your riders are faster than my legs."

"We're here," Vane said, "because I am going to offer you a third path. And I want you to understand, before you laugh at it, that I am offering it honestly. I have offered it twice before in my career. Both times the person took it. Both times they lived long, comfortable lives and died in beds, surrounded by grandchildren who never knew what they'd given up."

"And the times you didn't offer it?"

"Those people are in the ground outside Harrowgate," he said pleasantly. "There's a plum orchard now. Very productive soil."

* * *

He laid it out the way he laid out all his arguments: slowly, in order, with no flourish, because flourishes were for men who doubted their own case.

"The King believes the Wolf is dead. He killed it himself, or so he tells himself every night, and the telling has become the truth, which is how truths usually get made. But the Wolf is not dead. It is sleeping, and it is sleeping in you — in your blood, in your forgotten childhood, in that scrap of leather you're keeping warm against your ribs like a sick bird. If you wake it, there will be war. Not a battle. A war. The free cities will rise, Aldric's veterans will answer, and the Stillwater Valley — which cannot hold magic, note, only keep it out — will become a border again instead of a sanctuary. Tens of thousands dead. Perhaps more. The last war's graves are still fresh enough to plant turnips in."

"You've counted them?"

"I count everything. It's a failing." He refilled her cup. "Now. The second path. You run. You wake the Wolf. Maybe you win. Maybe the guardian rises through you and Aldric falls and the free cities sing songs about the cartographer's daughter for a hundred years." He paused. "They won't sing them about you, though. They'll sing them about the Wolf. Vessels don't survive the waking, Miss Thackeray. Whatever is left afterward won't remember why any of it mattered. You've read the map. You know what it asks."

Her face went very carefully blank, which told him she had read that part, and recently, and alone, probably at night, probably more than once.

"And the third path?" she said quietly.

"The third path." He reached into his coat — slowly, letting her see every movement, because trust began with predictability — and set a small lacquered box on the table between them. He did not open it. "Inside this box is a relic older than either of us. It unmakes memory. Not erases — unmakes. There's a difference, and it matters. An erased memory leaves a hole. An unmade memory leaves a choice. With this, the King could open the map without waking anything. He could take the Wolf's true name out of the world the way you'd take a splinter out of a foot — cleanly, with the patient asleep."

Paloma stared at the box. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because the King doesn't want the Wolf, Miss Thackeray. He wants the *name*. Give him the name, and the guardian can sleep forever in whatever girl happens to be carrying it, and nobody ever needs to dig her out. Give him the name, and you walk away." He let that breathe. "Rich. Safe. Pardoned — you've committed no crime worth the word, but formally pardoned regardless, because parchment is cheap and kings like handing it out. A house. An income. Your memories — all of them, including the ones Maren stole from you as a child, including your father's face, including whatever happened the day they carried you over the boundary stones. We can give those back. The relic works both ways. Did you know that? Everyone assumes it only takes."

He watched her hands. They had gone still around the cup, absolutely still, the stillness of a woman holding onto something by force.

"That's a lie," she said. "It has to be. Why would Aldric leave the vessel alive? I'm the proof the Wolf exists. Dead, I'm nothing."

"Dead, you're a martyr with a mystery attached," Vane said. "Mysteries breed zealots. Also, the King tried killing the Wolf once already, and look how well that went. He is a great many things, Aldric, but he learns. Besides—" and here he allowed himself the smallest smile, the one he saved for closing arguments— "you're forgetting the most persuasive clause. Your memories. Every single one. The ones you've spent eleven days running toward. Don't tell me you haven't wondered what's behind that door. Don't tell me you haven't lain awake in hedgerows trying to remember your mother's voice and gotten nothing, nothing, nothing, and hated the world for it."

* * *

She cried, eventually. He had expected it around this point and had the decency to look out the window at the orchard while it happened, which was not kindness exactly, but was adjacent to it, and in his experience adjacency was usually enough.

When she had finished, she wiped her face with the sleeveless arm and said, "How do I know the memories come back? How do I know you won't take the name and then unmake everything anyway — the Wolf, me, all of it?"

"You don't," Vane said. "That's the honest answer, and I promised you honesty. What I can offer is this: I will swear it on the relic itself. The thing inside that box is bound by older law than the King's. If I break an oath sworn on it, it unmakes *me*. I have seen that happen. It is not quick." He turned the box over in his hands. "Also — and weigh this however you like — I don't want you dead. Killing you creates problems. Problems are work. I am a very lazy man wearing a very industrious coat."

"You're not lazy."

"No," he admitted. "But I contain laziness, the way a river contains mud. Now." He stood, stretched, and gathered the tea things. "I'm going to leave you alone with it. The offer, I mean. Not the box — the box comes with me, I'm not a fool. Think until morning. Eat what they bring you. The stew is genuinely good; the cook here won a ribbon at the Colder's Ford fair two years running, though between us, the second year was rigged."

"Vane." Her voice stopped him at the door. "Why do you care which path I take? Truly. You serve Aldric. Any path ends with him getting what he wants, doesn't it? Even if I refuse — you'll take the map off my body."

He considered lying, found he didn't want to — she'd earned the truth, or a slice of it, and slices were cheaper than loaves — and said:

"When I was a boy, my village stood on the old border. Before the war. I remember the Wolf's peace — not the Wolf itself, I was too small, but the *peace*. Nobody locked their doors. Do you understand? Nobody locked their doors, in a valley full of wolves, real ones, the ordinary kind. Then the war came, and the doors got locks, and then bars, and then nobody had doors worth locking, and everyone agreed this was normal." He adjusted his cuffs. "I have spent thirty years making the world safe for men like Aldric, Miss Thackeray, and I have begun to suspect I made it safe for no one at all. So yes. Part of me hopes you refuse. Part of me hopes you burn the whole rotten arrangement down. But a larger part of me has seen what wars do to orchards, and that part wants you to take the money and the memories and the house with the good light, and let the world go on sleeping."

He left before she could answer. Answers given too fast were worth nothing.

* * *

The night was long, and Vane did not sleep, because he never slept well on the eve of a decision that wasn't his to make. He sat in the guard room downstairs with a candle and a ledger he wasn't reading, and listened to the house settle, and thought about doors.

Once, near midnight, he heard her moving overhead. Pacing. Once, near the hour after that, he heard her stop pacing, which was worse.

His sergeant, a broad patient woman named Osk, came in at some point with bread and the question he'd been dreading. "If she says no?"

"Then we ride for the capital at dawn with a prisoner and a map, and I spend the next month listening to the King talk about legacy."

"And if she says yes?"

Vane turned the little lacquered box over in his hands. Through the wall, faintly, he could hear the girl upstairs begin to pace again — or perhaps it was the wind in the orchard, or perhaps it was something older than both of them turning over in its sleep.

"Then I'll have bought the world another ten quiet years," he said, "and sold a girl her own past at a discount, and I'll write it up in my ledger as a success, because that's what ledgers are for." He closed the box. "Go to bed, Osk."

Dawn was maybe two hours off when the pacing stopped for good, and the house went so quiet he could hear the candle burning, and Vane understood — with the particular dread of a man whose careful plans have just begun working — that whatever she had decided, she had decided it hours ago, and everything since had been performance.

For whom, was the question. For him?

Or for whoever might be watching through the window, from the direction of the mountain?

He snuffed the candle and went to find out.
