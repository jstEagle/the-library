## 15. The Relic and the Knife

They kept me in a wagon.

Not a cell — Vane was too clever for cells. Cells made prisoners desperate, and desperate prisoners did stupid, memorable things. A wagon, on the other hand, made a prisoner *cargo*, and cargo had no dignity to lose and therefore nothing to fight for. I understood the logic even as I hated it. For three days I lay on straw that smelled of old onions, my wrists bound in front of me — considerately, so I could eat — while the wheels groaned beneath me and the road carried us toward the capital and whatever Aldric intended to do with the last living vessel of the Wolf.

Vane rode beside the wagon most days. That was the worst part, in a way. Not the ropes, not the onions. The company.

"You're sulking," he said on the third morning, reining his grey mare level with the wagon's barred window. He said it the way a tutor might note that you'd failed to complete your sums.

"I'm *captive*," I said. "There's a difference. Sulking is voluntary."

"Is it?" He handed down a strip of dried apple without being asked, which was its own kind of insult — kindness from a man who had burned a village's worth of memory out of an archivist's mind. "You've been voluntary about most of this. You could have run at the ford. You didn't. You could have let Tamsin take the map. You didn't. You could have taken my offer."

I chewed the apple slowly. It was good apple. That annoyed me too.

"Your offer," I said, "was to hand over the last hope of every free city in exchange for a house and a stipend."

"And a title. Don't forget the title. Aldric is generous with titles; they cost him nothing." Vane adjusted his gloves. He had beautiful gloves, soft grey leather, and hands underneath them that had done things I tried very hard not to imagine. "You're going to say no again. I can tell. You have a face for it."

"What kind of face?"

"The kind your father had." Something flickered behind his eyes when he said it — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has spent years hunting one family and finds the hunt strangely lonely. "He also thought stubbornness was a virtue right up until it wasn't."

"He's dead because of men like you."

"He's dead because he drew a map no one was supposed to be able to read, and then he had a daughter who could." Vane turned his horse away. "Eat your apple, vessel. We make the river crossing by nightfall."

I watched him go and pressed my bound hands against the map, hidden flat against my ribs beneath my shirt, where it had lived since the crossroads. It was warm. It was always warm now, like a sleeping animal, and sometimes — only when I was nearly asleep myself — I could feel it thinking.

*Patience,* it seemed to say. Or possibly that was me. By the third day in a wagon, the line between my own thoughts and the map's whispers had worn thin as onion skin.

* * *

Tamsin rode at the rear of the column, where they put her: close enough to watch, far enough to grab. She'd been given back her weapons after the ambush — Vane believed, correctly, that she would behave — but nobody had given her back anything else. Not her brother's safety, which had been the price of her letter. Not my trust, which had been the cost.

We hadn't spoken properly since the crossroads. There had been the confession itself, weeping and terrible, and then two days of silence so thick the soldiers started making jokes about it, and then, on the second evening, Tamsin had appeared at the wagon window with a bowl of stew and said, "It's venison. Probably. It might be boot," and I had laughed before I could stop myself, and hated her a little for knowing that I would.

That was the thing about betrayal between friends. It didn't cut clean. It left all these frayed ends, and every time you moved you felt them catch.

"Paloma." Her voice came low through the bars that night, when the camp had gone quiet except for the sentries and the horses. "Are you awake?"

"No," I said.

"Good. Then you won't hear this." A pause. In the dark I could just make out her shape against the firelight — broad shoulders, cropped hair, the scar on her jaw that she'd gotten falling out of Maren's pear tree at fourteen and lied about ever after. "The transfer changes tomorrow. Vane's taking the relic off his own belt."

My heart did something complicated. "Why would he do that?"

"Because Aldric ordered it. The relic goes ahead by fast rider — Vane doesn't trust himself with it near you, or doesn't trust you near it, I couldn't tell which, and I wasn't going to ask." She glanced over her shoulder. "It'll be in the satchel of the man riding point. Young one. Ferret face. He's terrified of Vane and terrified of the dark and right now he's terrified most of all because he heard the sentries talking about wolves in the hills."

"Tamsin." I sat up as far as the rope allowed. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you what the sentries told each other," she said, "because I happened to be standing nearby. What you do with it is your own affair." But her hands had found the bars, and her knuckles were white around them, and her voice dropped until it was barely breath. "I sold you once. I'm not — I can't do it twice. Whatever happens tomorrow, whatever it costs, I'm not doing it twice. Do you understand me?"

I looked at her for a long moment. Trust, I was learning, isn't a door you open once. It's a bridge you rebuild plank by plank, and some of the planks are rotten, and you walk across anyway because the alternative is drowning alone.

"Then don't get caught," I said.

Her laugh cracked in the middle. "Wouldn't dream of it."

* * *

The transfer happened at dawn, and it went wrong immediately, which was how I knew Tamsin had arranged it perfectly.

Here is what I had learned about Vane in our weeks of acquaintance: he was patient, thorough, and almost never surprised. Almost. The almost was a hairline crack running through all that marble, and everything depended on finding it.

The crack, as it turned out, was named Brannick. The ferret-faced courier took the relic's satchel at first light, mounted up, and rode point exactly as instructed — and exactly forty minutes later, his horse shied at a heron rising out of the reeds, Brannick overcorrected like a man who had never met a horse he trusted, and the satchel went into the river.

I want to be clear that I watched this happen from the wagon window and it was, briefly, the finest moment of my life. Brannick floundered into the shallows after it. Two soldiers waded in to help. Vane stood on the bank with his jaw tight as a drumhead, and I saw him look at the water, and then at the treeline, and then — slowly, terribly — at Tamsin.

But the satchel came up dripping. Brannick clutched it. Everyone laughed with relief, the ugly relieved laughter of people who had imagined explaining a lost relic to Aldric. And Vane said, quietly, "Ride on," and the column moved, and the plan proceeded exactly as designed.

Because here was the part Tamsin and I had built together in whispers through the bars, plank by rotten plank: the heron was real. The river was real. But the *satchel* Brannick fished out of the shallows was not the satchel that had gone in, because Tamsin had been in the water before dawn, and what Brannick recovered was a decoy weighted with river stones and padded with the shape of a box, and the true relic was currently wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the stew pot in the supply cart, six feet from where I sat eating breakfast.

Gentle humor aside — and there was very little of it that morning — my hands shook so badly I spilled the porridge.

* * *

The escape, when it came, was less dramatic than I'd planned and more terrifying than I'd feared, which I believe is the general rule.

We made our move at the noon halt, in a stretch of road where the forest crowded close and the column strung out single file. Tamsin's job was the guards on my wagon; mine was the supply cart and the stew pot; and the third element — the one neither of us had spoken aloud, because speaking it might break it — was the map itself, which had grown warmer every hour since the river, and which I was beginning to suspect had opinions about our prospects.

I got the relic first. That part went eerily well: I slipped from the wagon under pretense of stretching my legs — Vane allowed it, roped, with two guards, because cargo needed exercise — and while the guards' eyes followed Tamsin's loud argument with the farrier about a loose shoe, I opened the supply cart, found the pot, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle.

It was smaller than I expected. Smaller than something that could unmake a person should be. A disc of black glass set in a ring of pale metal, cold to the touch in a way that had nothing to do with temperature — cold like a well, like looking down into somewhere deep and lightless. When my fingers closed around it, the map against my ribs went hot as a brand, and the relic went colder, and the two of them *hated* each other. I felt it like holding a magnet to a magnet, a shuddering refusal, and I understood suddenly why Vane kept it on his belt and not in his hand.

"Put it down," said a voice behind me, "and step away from the pot."

Brannick. Of course. The universe has a sense of humor and it is not gentle.

He had his sword half-drawn and his face full of the particular misery of a young man who has finally worked out that the stew pot was too heavy. "I knew it," he whispered. "I knew something was wrong. The pot weighed — I *carried* it—"

"Brannick," I said, and did the only thing I could think of, which was the truth. "Your name is Brannick, yes? Listen to me. That thing in my hand can take every memory you have. Every one. Your mother's face. Your own name. Vane carries it because Aldric wants a world where nobody remembers anything inconvenient — including you. Including your family. You've seen what it does. Haven't you?"

His sword arm wavered. "I haven't — they don't tell couriers—"

"They don't tell anyone. That's rather the point." I held his eyes. My heart was slamming. Somewhere behind him, Tamsin's argument with the farrier was reaching operatic volumes, buying me seconds. "I'm not going to hurt you. I'm going to walk past you, and you're going to count to fifty, and when Vane asks, you're going to say I had a knife. Which I don't. But he'll believe it, because it's tidier than the truth, and Vane loves tidy."

"That's mutiny," Brannick said faintly.

"It's counting to fifty," I said. "Fifty is very loyal."

And then — I will wonder about this for the rest of my life — the boy looked at the relic in my hand, and at me, and stepped aside. Just stepped aside, into the shadow of the cart, eyes fixed on the middle distance, mouth moving silently. Counting.

I went.

* * *

Tamsin was waiting at the treeline with two horses she had absolutely stolen, because Tamsin did not borrow things during escapes. She hauled me up onto the bay one-handed, took the reins of both, and put her heels in, and the forest swallowed us in a crash of branches and shouting behind.

We rode hard for an hour. Two. When the shouting faded and the ground rose into rocky hills where hoofprints died on stone, she finally slowed, and we walked the horses down a fern-choked hollow, and stopped, and breathed.

Then she slid off her horse, walked three paces away, and was sick.

I dismounted more slowly and stood there, holding the reins, giving her the privacy of pretending I wasn't watching. When she straightened, wiping her mouth, she wouldn't look at me.

"Well," she said. "That's done, then."

"That's done."

"I turned on my handlers. Actual handlers, Paloma. Sworn oaths, signed papers, my brother's life hanging on my good behavior. I committed treason in a stew pot." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "If they catch us now, they won't ransom me back to my family. They'll hang me next to you."

"Yes," I said.

"And I did it anyway." She turned at last, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened, and underneath all of it, shining through like a coin at the bottom of a stream, relieved. "I need you to know that I know what I am now. I'm the friend who sold you. That doesn't wash off. I'll carry it. But I'd rather carry it than carry what I'd be if I'd let them take you to the capital."

I wanted to say something gracious. Something forgiving and large-spirited. What came out instead was: "You stole horses?"

"Requisitioned."

"You stole horses and committed treason and faked a relic drop in a freezing river before dawn, and you're standing there shaking like a wet cat, and you're worried I'm going to grade your technique."

"A little," she admitted. "Was the river bit too much? It felt theatrical."

"The river bit was perfect. The heron was inspired."

"The heron was a coincidence, actually, but I'm taking credit."

And then we were both laughing — ragged, unhinged, tears-and-shaking laughter, the kind that comes out of you when terror leaves the body and takes the last of your composure with it. I sat down on a rock. She sat down on the ground. The horses grazed. The hills held their green silence around us.

When it passed, she crawled over and sat beside me, shoulder to shoulder, and said quietly: "Show me your wrists."

I showed her. The rope burns were raw. She took a flask from her coat — of course she had a flask — poured a measure over the raw skin, and wrapped them with strips torn from her own sleeve, her big clumsy careful hands working in silence.

"This doesn't mean I forgive you," I said.

"I know."

"It means I've decided to find out who you are when you're not terrified. Apparently it's someone who commits treason competently."

"Competently," she repeated, wounded. "I faked a relic recovery in a river."

"In a *stew pot*. The river was just delivery." I flexed my bandaged fingers. "Tamsin. Why? Truly. Not the speech — the reason. Your brother was safe if you delivered me."

She was quiet a long time. A jay scolded somewhere upslope.

"Because when I wrote that letter," she said finally, "I told myself it was arithmetic. One girl against my whole family. Any sensible person does the sum the same way." She picked up a twig and snapped it. "But arithmetic is what Vane does. It's what Aldric does. They add people up and the total always comes out the same: whatever keeps them comfortable. And I looked at you in that wagon, eating their apples, refusing their title, and I thought — she's doing the other sum. The one where you don't get to be safe if the price is everyone else forgetting." She shrugged, awkward. "I want to be the kind of person who does that sum. Even badly. Even late."

I looked at her — my betrayer, my rescuer, my friend, all three at once, none of them canceling the others — and I understood that this was what trust actually was. Not the clean faith I'd had in her at the ford. This was dirtier and heavier and worth more: two people who had seen the worst of each other and chosen to keep walking side by side anyway.

"Do the other sum," I said. "Out loud. Both of us. From here on."

"Deal." She stood, wincing, and offered me a hand up. "First entry: we have a relic that unmakes memory, a map that wants something from you, two stolen horses, no food, and the most patient man in Aldric's service somewhere behind us with a very long memory."

"Not for long," I said, and pulled the relic from my coat.

The black glass caught the light and gave none of it back. Up close, in daylight, I could see movement inside it — slow, deep, like sediment in still water. The map burned against my ribs, hotter than it had ever been, and for a moment the whispering resolved into something almost like words:

*Bring it home.*

"Tamsin," I said slowly, "when we crossed into Stillwater Valley — did the magic leave you all at once, or did it drain?"

"Drain. Like water through sand. Why?"

"Because this thing drains too. I can feel it pulling — not at me. At the *map*. At the names in it." I wrapped the relic carefully in its oilcloth, layer over layer, until the cold was muffled and the map's heat settled to a simmer. "Vane thinks he's carrying a weapon to the capital. He's carrying it exactly where it needs to go. And I just stole the one thing that might unmake the Wolf's name before we ever reach the mountain."

Tamsin stared at me. "So we've rescued it."

"We've *kidnapped* it," I said. "For its own good."

She rubbed her face with both hands. "Right. Other sum. Food first, then distance, then we work out how to deliver a stolen relic to a mountain guarded by a sleeping god, before Vane catches us." She swung up onto her horse and paused. "Any chance the map has opinions on the route?"

Against my ribs, warm as a heartbeat, the map whispered — and this time it was unmistakably a direction, and unmistakably north, and unmistakably *home*.

"North," I said, and turned my stolen horse toward the mountains.
