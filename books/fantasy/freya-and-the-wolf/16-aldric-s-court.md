## 16. Aldric's Court

The trouble with usurping a throne, Aldric had learned over eleven long years, was not the taking of it. Taking was easy. Taking required only nerve, a sharp knife, and a brother who trusted you — and Aldric had possessed all three in generous measure.

No, the trouble was the *keeping*.

Kings kept things. That was, he had come to understand, most of what kingship actually was. They kept borders, which were lines drawn on paper by men who had never walked them. They kept peace, which was a rumor maintained through careful rationing of bad news. They kept ledgers, granaries, garrisons, secrets, and — above all — they kept appearances.

Aldric kept his appearances very well indeed.

He sat that morning at the head of the long council table in the Hall of Amber, where the light came slantwise through windows his grandfather's glassmakers had dyed the color of honey and old wine, and he listened to his treasurer explain, for the third time, why the harvest tariffs from the eastern provinces had arrived short.

"Bandits," said Treasurer Corvel, a round man with the mournful dignity of a basset hound. "Organized ones, sire. Possibly several hundred."

"Several hundred organized bandits," Aldric repeated, "operating exclusively along tariff roads, and never once touching the grain itself."

Corvel's jowls quivered. "They are *principled* bandits, sire."

"Principled." Aldric turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "How refreshing. In my day we called them tax collectors with better manners." A ripple of nervous laughter went down the table — the laughter of men who laughed when the king spoke because not laughing had historically been unwise. Aldric let them have it. He had learned early that a king who made his council laugh was a king whose council told him true things between the jokes.

It was one of perhaps four pieces of wisdom he'd earned honestly. The rest he'd taken, like everything else.

He waved Corvel off to negotiate with the bandits — or hire them; whichever proved cheaper — and the council moved on to a dispute about fishing rights on the Serle, two barons glaring at each other across forty feet of polished table as though the distance were the river itself. Aldric settled it with an arbitrary ruling that pleased neither man, which he considered the mark of a fair judgment. Then came the petitioners, then the seal-stamping, then the interminable business of the winter garrison rotations, and it was well past noon before the hall emptied and only one figure remained, standing where he always stood: near the door, slightly apart, wrapped in grey.

Inquisitor Vane did not sit at tables. Aldric had never decided whether this was discipline or theater. With Vane it was usually both.

"You look tired," Aldric said.

"I don't sleep much, sire."

"No. I imagine you don't. What does a man dream about, I wonder, who spends his days unmaking other people's nights?" He said it lightly, almost fondly. Vane was the closest thing Aldric had to a friend, which said something bleak about friendship, or about Aldric, or possibly about both. "Sit down, Vane. You make my neck ache, looking up at you."

Vane sat — at the far end of the table, naturally, so that they spoke the length of twenty empty chairs. It was their ritual. Neither acknowledged it as such.

"The valley," Vane said. "Stillwater. The girl is real. She took the map from a dying courier in usurper's livery — mine, though he'd traded the colors off by then. Clever boy. Dead now regardless." He said it without inflection, reporting weather. "She crossed into the valley proper. My trackers lost her at the boundary stones."

"And your trackers are the best money can buy."

"They're the best anything can buy. Magic doesn't follow past those stones, sire. You know that better than anyone."

Aldric did know it. He knew Stillwater Valley the way a man knows a scar — intimately, unwillingly, and always with the sense that it might yet open again.

* * *

Here is what the histories would say, if Aldric permitted histories, which he did not: that King Aldric the First had slain the Wolf at the Battle of the Ash Ford, single-handed, in single combat, and that the beast's death had broken the old order forever and ushered in the Age of the Crown.

Here is what actually happened, which Aldric had told no living soul:

There had been no battle. There had been a *conversation*.

Eleven years ago, in a mountain hall lit by no torch he could name, Aldric — then a prince, then a traitor, then something in between that didn't have a word — had stood before the guardian of the free cities and asked it, in effect, for permission. Because that was the thing no one understood about the Wolf. It was not a weapon. It was not even, properly speaking, a soldier. It was a *witness*. It had watched the free cities for nine hundred years, and it remembered everything, and its memory was not a blade but a mirror, and mirrors, Aldric had discovered, are the only weapons that cannot be parried.

*I know what you were,* the Wolf had said to him, in a voice like his father's, like his mother's, like his own at seven years old. *Before the ambition. Before the knife. I remember the boy who wept at his hound's grave and swore he'd never let power decide what was true.*

And Aldric had said — gods help him, he could still hear himself saying it — *"That boy was a fool."*

And the Wolf had looked at him with ancient, sorrowful patience and said, *Then you will spend the rest of your life making certain no one ever asks me.*

So Aldric had done the only thing left. Not slain it — you couldn't slay a memory — but *unmade* it. Scattered it. There had been a vessel, there had always been a vessel, some bloodline chosen to hold the guardian sleeping inside ordinary flesh, and Aldric's scholars had found the bloodline and his soldiers had burned the house and his inquisitors had reported the line ended, and Aldric had believed them because believing them was the only way he could sleep.

And now a girl had walked out of Stillwater Valley with a map inked in blood, and the Wolf was waking, and Aldric had not slept properly in three weeks.

* * *

"You've read the archivist's deposition," he said to Vane.

"I wrote half of it, sire."

"The portraits. The cartographer's daughter. Confirm it."

"The face confirms. The age confirms. And the map's maker's mark—" Vane paused, and Aldric noted the pause the way a hunter notes a snapped twig. Vane did not pause. "The mark is Halvard's. The cartographer. Your old—"

"My old friend," Aldric finished, when Vane declined to. "Yes. Say it. Everyone else in this palace has learned to talk around the dead; I find it tiresome."

"Halvard," Vane said obediently, "who drew the maps that hid the vessel's bloodline from your own scholars for six years. Who smuggled the child into the valley. Who died — how did we record it?"

"Fever. In the records." Aldric smiled without warmth. "In truth, my own hand, in a room in this palace, after he refused — for the fourth time — to tell me where the girl was." He turned his cup again. The amber light caught it and threw a small wobbling coin of gold onto the ceiling. "You want to know something absurd, Vane? I liked him. Best of all of them. He used to beat me at cards and cheat while doing it, badly, so I'd notice, so I'd know he trusted me enough to let me catch him." His voice did something he hadn't authorized. He stopped it. "And when I put the knife to him he wasn't even angry. He just looked *sorry* for me. That's the part I can't get out from under. Eleven years, and I can survive the hatred of every free city, but I cannot survive being pitied by a dead mapmaker."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a clerk dropped a stack of papers and swore with impressive fluency.

"That," Vane said at last, carefully, "is not a sentiment I expected to hear from the throne."

"No. Well. The throne hears very little worth hearing." Aldric stood, and Vane rose with him, and they regarded each other down the length of the empty table like two chess players surveying a board neither had wanted to play. "Everyone believes I fear the Wolf because it's a weapon. Let them. Weapons can be countered. Armies can be met. But a witness, Vane—" He crossed to the window, to the honeyed light, to the city sprawling below with its ten thousand small lives going about their evenings in perfect ignorance of what kept their peace intact. "A witness cannot be fought. It can only be *contradicted*, and contradiction requires proof, and all the proof sleeps in one girl's head."

"Then we reach her first. The relic—"

"Will work. I know it will work. I had it built." He had, too — commissioned it himself, years ago, from a sorcerer he'd later hanged, because that was the other thing about keeping a throne: everyone who helped you build it became evidence. "Wipe the vessel clean and the Wolf wakes into emptiness. No memories. No witness. Just a great dumb loyal beast with nothing to testify about." He should have felt triumphant saying it aloud. He mostly felt tired. "Bring me the girl, Vane. Alive, unharmed, and — this is important — *before* she reaches the gate. Once she speaks her father's name at that door, no relic on earth will matter."

"And if she refuses to come quietly?"

Aldric watched the light die off the western roofs, one street at a time, like a candle being pinched out across the city.

"Then offer her whatever she wants," he said. "Her life. Her memories. Wealth, safety, a title. People think tyranny is made of terror, Vane. It isn't. Terror is cheap. Tyranny is made of *offers* — good ones, honest ones, the kind a sensible person would take. That's how you keep a throne. You make the truth more expensive than the lie, and then you wait."

Vane bowed — a shallow, precise bow — and moved toward the door.

"Vane."

The inquisitor paused.

"When you find her. When you stand in front of her." Aldric kept his eyes on the darkening window, on his own reflection ghosted faintly against the glass, a king-shaped shadow wearing a crown. "Look at her face for me. The cartographer's daughter. Tell me if she looks like him."

"Sire?"

"He had kind eyes," Aldric said, "and I have spent eleven years trying to remember whether that was a weakness in him or in me, and I genuinely no longer know." He laughed — a short, real sound, surprised out of him. "Gods. Listen to me. Confiding in my inquisitor. Halvard would be insufferable about it, wherever dead men go."

Vane said nothing. Vane was very good at saying nothing; it was most of his craft. But at the door he stopped, and for a moment — Aldric would think about this later, in the bad hours, turning it over like a stone — the grey man's shoulders seemed almost to soften.

"She'll look like herself, sire," Vane said. "They always do. That's rather the problem with vessels."

Then the door closed, and Aldric was alone with the amber hall and the twenty empty chairs and the ledger of everything he had done, which no clerk recorded and no archive held — except, perhaps, one, sleeping under a mountain, waiting for a girl with a map to come and read it aloud.

He poured wine he wouldn't drink and stood at the window a long time, watching the lamps of the capital bloom in the dusk, each one a small promise that tomorrow would resemble today.

Three hundred miles east, he knew without knowing how, a door in a mountainside was beginning to listen.

"Come on, then, little cartographer," he murmured to the dark. "Let's see what your father taught you about drawing lines."

Somewhere below, the clerk dropped his papers again.
