## 17. The Gate of Names

The mountain had been waiting for them long before they arrived.

Paloma felt it first as a stillness in the air — not the dead silence of the valley, which had always felt to her like a held breath, but something older and more deliberate. The kind of quiet a house makes when someone inside has heard your footsteps on the path and is deciding whether to open the door.

"You're doing the thing again," Tamsin said behind her.

"What thing?"

"The talking-to-a-mountain thing. Your lips are moving."

"They are not." They were. Paloma pressed them together and kept climbing. The path had narrowed to a shelf of stone above a valley of pines, and the wind up here carried the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it. Three days from Bramblewick. Two days since the crossroads where Tamsin had knelt in the mud and confessed, and one whole day — nearly — since Paloma had stopped flinching every time Tamsin handed her something.

Progress, she supposed, came in strange units.

"There." Tamsin pointed with her chin, because her hands were full of rope, rations, and the particular burden of being trusted again only provisionally.

The gate was not what Paloma had imagined through all those whispering dreams. She had pictured iron, or black oak banded in bronze, something you could batter and break. Instead it was simply a wall — a sweep of mountain face so smooth it might have been poured rather than carved, rising out of the scree without seam or crack, taller than any tower in any city she'd ever seen in a book. And set into its center, at the height of a person's heart, was a single hollow: a handprint-shaped depression no bigger than a bread loaf.

Around it, faint as breath on cold glass, lines of script curled in a spiral. The same hand as the map. The same hand as everything.

Her father's hand.

"Well," Tamsin said. "It's very dramatic. I'll give it that."

"Tamsin."

"I'm just saying. If you're going to seal an ancient guardian behind a mountain, you could at least put up a sign. *Knock.* Something."

Despite everything — despite Vane's riders somewhere behind them, despite the relic wrapped in oilcloth against Paloma's ribs, despite the fact that she had not slept properly in a week — Paloma laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, the way laughter does when it's been rationed for too long.

She stepped up to the wall.

Up close, the script resolved into words she almost knew. Not the trade-tongue, not the high speech of Aldric's court, not even the hedge-cant Maren had muttered over poultices. This was older, rounder, a language shaped for names rather than sentences. She had seen it once before, she realized — scratched into the underside of the workbench in Maren's cottage, hidden beneath thirty years of wax drippings, in a child's wobbly imitation of an adult's sure strokes.

*She had written it herself,* Paloma thought. *When I was small enough to hide under tables.* Her father must have taught it to her the way other fathers taught counting songs.

"Paloma." Tamsin's voice had gone careful. "Your eyes are doing something."

"What?"

"Glowing. A little. Like candlelight through skin."

Paloma raised her hand before she could talk herself out of it, and pressed her palm into the hollow.

Nothing happened.

She waited. Counted to ten. Pressed harder, until the stone bit cold into her fingers.

"Maybe it wants a knock," Tamsin offered.

"It doesn't want a knock." But doubt was already crawling up her throat, cold and familiar. *What if I'm wrong? What if the archivist misread the portraits? What if the bloodline thinned, or broke, or skipped me entirely and went off down some cousin's line to a farm girl who will never know why her dreams smell of pine?* She thought of the map whispering to her night after night — *a tower, a wolf, a door* — and wondered if it had been calling the wrong daughter home all along.

"No," said a voice, and it came from everywhere — from the stone under her palm, from the snow-heavy sky, from somewhere behind her own breastbone. "It does not want a knock."

Tamsin's sword cleared its sheath with a sound like a gasp.

The voice was her father's. That was the unbearable part. Not similar, not reminiscent — *his*, warm and dry and patient, the voice that lived in the single memory Paloma had salvaged from her stolen childhood: big hands steadying small ones on a sheet of leather, ink, a needle, the smell of tallow.

"Papa?" she whispered. And then, hating herself for the hope even as she felt it bloom: "No. No, you're not him. You're wearing him."

"I am what he gave me," the mountain said gently. "A name, spoken true, is a seed. He planted me in you before he planted me here. Speak it, little cartographer. The gate does not open to hands. It opens to truth."

"I don't—" Paloma's voice caught. "I don't remember his name. That's the whole — that's the entire *point* of everything, they took—"

"Not the name he answered to," the voice said. "The name he *was*. There is a difference. You have known it since you were six years old, scratching letters under a bench while Maren pretended not to see. Children keep what adults think to burn."

And Paloma stood very still, and let herself go back.

Not far. Just down. Past the fear and the road-dust and the ache of Tamsin's betrayal, past the inn and the storm and the dying stranger's blood on her hands, past Maren's lullaby and the boundary stones and the wet-wick death of her magic, down to the bottom of herself, where a six-year-old girl hid beneath a workbench with a nail and a scrap of leather, copying letters she wasn't supposed to know, sounding them out in a whisper because they made her feel *warm*, the way a hearth feels warm, the way being *known* feels warm—

"Aldous," she breathed. "His name was Aldous. *Freya-Aldous.* The name and the true name. He told me never to say them together unless I meant it."

The wall did not groan or grind. It simply *agreed*.

Light ran along the spiral script like dawn running along a ridge line, and the smooth stone softened, and opened — not outward like a door but inward like an eye, unfolding layer upon layer into a corridor of pale gold, and air rolled out of it smelling of cedar and old paper and, underneath, something wilder. Fur. Snow. Green things growing in the dark.

Tamsin grabbed her arm. "Paloma. Wait. We don't know what's in there."

"Yes we do." Paloma's heart was hammering, but her feet were already moving. "We've known since the archive. Since the map. It's been waiting longer than we have."

"That's exactly what worries me."

They went in together anyway. Paloma noted, in some small stubborn corner of herself, that this was what trust looked like now — not the old easy kind, but the kind that walks forward with its sword drawn and its stomach in knots and comes anyway.

* * *

The hall of memory was not a hall.

It was, at first glance, a library — shelves receding into golden distance, books without spines, their covers showing moving images instead of titles: a wedding, a harvest, a woman laughing in a kitchen, a boy falling out of a tree and getting up again. Paloma reached toward the nearest shelf and understood, with a lurch, what she was looking at.

"These are people."

"Lives," said the voice, closer now, and no longer coming from everywhere. "Every life the Wolf has witnessed. Every promise kept and broken within its sight. Kings forget what they were. The Wolf does not. That is why your usurper fears it. Not the teeth, child. The *witness*."

"The books are memories?"

"The books are *proof*."

Tamsin had gone quiet in the way she did when she was cataloguing exits. "How many?"

"More than either of you can carry. Fewer than there should be." Something in the voice darkened, like a cloud crossing a field. "Aldric burned three halls before this one. His inquisitor carries a relic that unmakes what my shelves hold. Come. You did not climb a mountain to browse."

The corridor opened into a chamber that was somehow both vast and intimate — a round room lit from nowhere, floored in moss soft as confession, and in its center stood the Wolf.

Paloma had dreamed it a hundred times by now: enormous, silver-grey, eyes like winter stars. So the shape was right. But the scale was wrong — the Wolf standing before her was barely larger than a large hound, and it sat with its tail curled around its paws in a way that was almost sheepish, and when it lifted its head and looked at her, Paloma forgot how to breathe.

Because it was wearing her own face.

Not literally — the muzzle was a muzzle, the fur was fur — but the expression, the tilt of the head, the exact quality of attention in those pale eyes, was hers. It was like catching her reflection in a window and having the reflection look back with opinions.

"Oh," said Tamsin faintly. "That's unsettling."

"You see yourself," the Wolf said, and its voice was still her father's, though softer now, worn in like a favorite chair. "All vessels do. I am not a body, Paloma Thackeray. I am a keeping. I sleep in bloodlines, and I wear the face of whoever wakes me, because a witness must be able to say: *I was there. I saw. I am no different from you.*"

"You're smaller than I dreamed."

The Wolf's ears twitched. In any other creature it would have been embarrassment. "Dreams exaggerate. Necessity does not. I am exactly as large as the last vessel's courage, and no larger. Your grandmother woke me tall as a barn. Her son — your father — kept me lean and quick. What I will be when you wake me depends entirely—"

"On what I'm willing to give." Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "I know. I read the map. All of it. Every memory. Willingly surrendered. That's the price."

"That is the gift," the Wolf corrected, gently. "There is a difference, and the difference is everything. A price is taken. A gift is chosen. Aldric's whole reign is built on taking. If you come to me as a payer, I cannot wake. If you come as a giver—" the great head inclined, "—then we will see what I become."

"And if I don't?" Paloma heard her own voice go thin and sharp. "If I turn around and walk back down the mountain? If I burn the map and let the world forget, like you said I could?"

"Then I sleep, and the free cities keep their comfortable lie, and Aldric grows old on a stolen throne, and in a hundred years his grandson burns whatever halls remain, and the forgetting becomes permanent." The Wolf's eyes held hers, unblinking, unjudging. "You would live. Long, perhaps. Safe, certainly. Hollow, inevitably. I do not say this to sway you. I say it because a witness does not flatter."

Tamsin shifted beside her. "For what it's worth," she said roughly, "I sold you once already. To save people I love. I know exactly what that bargain costs, and I know exactly what it pays, and it isn't worth it. It's never worth it. You pay forever for a thing you buy in minutes."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't. Don't make it a moment. I'm just stating facts." But her voice cracked on *facts*, and she stared hard at the middle distance, jaw working.

The Wolf watched them both with an expression that was painfully, precisely Paloma's own — the look she wore when someone was hurting and she didn't know whether touching them would help or wound.

"There is one more thing," the Wolf said quietly. "Before you choose. A vessel may ask me anything, and I must answer truly. One question. It is the oldest courtesy between us."

Paloma's heart was loud in her ears. She had climbed a mountain carrying a hundred questions — about her father, about the war, about the stranger who died with her name in his mouth, about why Maren's lullaby had verses in the old tongue, about whether the girl under the workbench had been happy —

But what came out, in the end, was none of them.

"When I give you my memories," she asked, "the ones I keep — the ones I chose, in the map's final page, the three small ones — will they be *mine*? Truly mine? Or will they be like your face on you — a copy, a costume, something worn?"

The hall was silent. Even the books seemed to lean in.

"Child," the Wolf said, and her father's voice had never sounded so much like her own, "a memory you have *chosen* is the only kind that was ever truly yours. Everything else was given to you by other people — your mother's stories, your mistress's rules, your king's history. Choice is the only authorship there is. Keep your three. Choose well. And know this—" the Wolf rose, and though it remained small, the room seemed suddenly too small to hold it, "—what you surrender, I will carry. Nothing you give me will be lost. It will be *witnessed*. There is no safer place in all the world for a life than inside a witness."

Paloma looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked back, and nodded once — not encouragement, exactly. Permission. *This is yours to do. I won't take it from you, and I won't make you do it alone.*

Paloma turned to the Wolf, and found her own face looking back at her, patient as stone, hungry as spring.

"All right," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "Then ask me the question everyone asks, so I can answer it properly. Ask me if I'm ready."

The Wolf tilted its head — her head — and smiled, insofar as a wolf can smile.

"No," it said. "I ask a different question. The vessels before you were all asked *are you ready*, and they all lied, and the lying cost us dearly. So instead, Paloma Thackeray, daughter of Aldous, keeper of the gate of names — tell me:

*What will you keep?*"
