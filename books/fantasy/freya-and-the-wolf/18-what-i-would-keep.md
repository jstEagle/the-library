## 18. What I Would Keep

The hall of memory had no ceiling that Paloma could find, only a soft grey light that came from everywhere and nowhere, like the sky on a day when it couldn't decide whether to rain. The Wolf stood before her — wearing her own face, her own crooked nose, her own stubborn chin — and waited with the patience of something that had been waiting for three hundred years and could wait three hundred more.

"You said I could choose," Paloma said. Her voice sounded small in all that light.

"I said you could choose," the Wolf agreed. It was strange to hear her own voice coming from another mouth, stranger still because the Wolf's version of it was calm where hers was not. "That is what waking me means, Paloma Thackeray. Not a door opening. A trade. Every memory you carry becomes mine to hold, so that I may rise and remember what the world has paid kings to forget."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we both sleep on, and Aldric wins by default, which is the way most tyrants win." The Wolf tilted its head — *her* head — with a wry expression Paloma recognized from every mirror she'd ever avoided. "You would live. You would keep everything. Your childhood in the valley. Maren's kitchen. Tamsin's terrible singing. And the world would go on forgetting, comfortably, until it forgot how to be free at all."

"That's not fair."

"No," the Wolf said. "It rarely is. Fairness is for stories. This is a choice."

Paloma sat down. There was no floor she could see, but when she thought about sitting, sitting happened, and there was a floor under her, smooth and cool as river stone. She pulled her knees up the way she used to as a child, when Maren's lessons went too long and the herbs needed sorting and the world outside the hedge-witch's cottage felt too large to hold.

"All of them?" she asked. "Every memory? Even the bad ones?"

"*Especially* the bad ones. Pain is the ink names are written in. If you kept your wounds and gave me only your joys, I would wake hollow, and hollow things make poor guardians." The Wolf crouched across from her, and for a moment its face flickered — not hers anymore, but a great grey shape with eyes like winter stars — and then it was her face again, kinder about it. "But I am not cruel, little vessel. My maker built one mercy into me, and you have earned it. Before the last of you pours into me, you may keep a handful. Three things. No more."

"Three?"

"A number old enough to be trusted. Choose well. Once the hall closes behind you, what remains is all you will have. Everything else will be mine, and I will carry it so you don't have to — but you will not get it back. Not tomorrow. Not in fifty years. Perhaps never."

Paloma looked down at her hands. They were shaking. She made them stop, mostly through stubbornness, which was, she suspected, the only inheritance she'd ever gotten honestly.

"When you say memories," she said slowly, "do you mean everything? Or—"

"Everything. Your mother's face. Your father's hands on the map. The first time magic answered you. The taste of Maren's burnt porridge. The sound Vane's boots make on stone. The weight of Tamsin asleep against your shoulder on the road out of Stillness." The Wolf's voice gentled. "All of it goes into the keeping. Except three."

"And after — I'll still know *how* things? How to walk, how to read, how to—"

"You will be yourself, emptied. Skills remain; stories leave. You will know how to bake bread and throw a knife and swear in three languages, and you will not know why you learned any of it. You will meet people who love you and feel nothing but a stranger's warmth." The Wolf paused. "You will meet Tamsin and not know her name."

The word landed in Paloma's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"That's the part that frightens me," she admitted. "Not losing my father. I never really had him. Not losing my childhood — Maren stole that years ago, this is just the theft being made official." She laughed, and it came out wet. "It's Tamsin. She sold my name to save her brother, and I forgave her, and I don't — I can't explain what that forgiveness cost, or what it's worth, or why it matters that it exists. If I forget that, then the forgiveness forgets itself. It'll be like it never happened."

"It did happen," the Wolf said. "I will remember it. That is rather the point of me."

"But she won't know that I—" Paloma pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "She'll look at me and see someone she betrayed, and I won't even know enough to tell her it's all right. She'll carry it alone. Again."

"Yes," the Wolf said simply. "Love is like that. It is almost always the one who stays who carries the heavier pack." It reached out — her own hand reaching toward her — and stopped just short of touching. "So choose carefully, Paloma. What do you want to keep?"

* * *

She thought it would be hard. Everyone always said choosing was the hard part, that the mind balked at sacrifice the way a horse balked at fire. But sitting in the endless grey light, Paloma found that her mind did no such thing. Her mind went straight to work, sorting, the way it always had — Maren had called it her curse, an apprentice who could not stop cataloguing — and within moments she had a list, and the list was wrong, and she threw it away, and made another, and that one was wronger.

Her father's face. Surely that. He was the reason for all of this; his mark was on the map; his name opened doors. But when she reached for his face she found she barely had one — a blur of brown hair and big hands, assembled from other people's silences more than from anything she'd actually seen. Keeping a blur seemed pointless. Grieving a blur seemed worse.

Maren, then. Twenty years of porridge and potions and cold-eyed lessons. But here the trouble ran the other direction: there was too much. Which morning? Which lesson? Which of the thousand small cruelties and smaller kindnesses? To pick one was to say the others mattered less, and they didn't, they all mattered, they'd made her—

They'd made her. And that was the thing, wasn't it. She could keep the making without keeping the maker. Whatever Maren had built in her would still be standing after the architect's name was gone.

"Tamsin's face," Paloma said aloud, and the words came out steady, which surprised her. "That's the first one."

The Wolf nodded slowly. "Explain your reasoning. I like to know what I'm carrying."

"She found me at a ford with orders to take the map off my corpse if necessary, and instead she walked four hundred miles beside me and lied to her handlers and finally turned a knife on her own people to save my life. And then she confessed the worst thing she'd ever done because she couldn't stand to have me trust her under false pretenses." Paloma's throat tightened. "If I keep her face, I'll know. I won't know *how* I know, I won't know the ford or the road or the crossroads ambush, but I'll look at her and I'll know she's safe. That she's home. Whatever else is empty in me, that won't be. That's worth one of three."

"A good choice," the Wolf said. "Held."

Something shifted in the grey light, subtle as a page turning. Somewhere far off, or very near, a memory detached itself from her — she felt it go the way you feel a splinter leave, a sting and then a strange lightness — except it hadn't left yet, it had only been *marked*, set aside, promised. Tamsin's face, windburned and freckled, glaring at her over a campfire, laughing at her over a map, crying at her at a crossroads. Held.

"The second," the Wolf prompted.

Paloma closed her eyes. She let herself drift backward through twenty years of mornings, past the inn at the waystation, past the boundary stones, back to the cottage at the edge of the hedge, and she listened.

There. Underneath everything — under the lessons and the scoldings and the long silences — a lullaby. Maren had sung it exactly twice that Paloma could recall: once when Paloma had fever at seven and once, unaccountably, on the night before Paloma fled, as though the old woman had known and had nothing else to give. It had no words Paloma understood. It was in some older tongue, or no tongue at all, just a rising-falling line of notes like a bird deciding whether to sing.

"Maren's lullaby," she said. "Not Maren. Just the song."

"Not the woman who stole your childhood?"

"No. Because I've spent two years hating her and two months understanding her, and if I keep either feeling I'll spend the rest of my life doing one or the other. But the song — the song was the only true thing she ever gave me. Songs outlive their singers anyway. Let it outlive her properly." She smiled despite herself. "Besides, I'm told I hum it when I'm nervous. Future-me should at least know where it comes from."

"Held," the Wolf said, softer now. "One remains."

And here Paloma stalled.

Because the third slot held a war inside it. Her father's name — the true name, the one that opened the gate, the one the dying stranger had gasped with his last breath. If she kept that, she'd carry the key to everything, the answer to the question that had eaten her whole life: *who was I before the valley?* But the Wolf had already heard the name spoken at the Gate of Names; it lived in the mountain now regardless. Keeping it would be greed, not need.

Vane's face, then — no. Why would she keep the face of the man who hunted her? So she could flinch at strangers forever?

Aldric on the field, unmade by remembered truth — she wouldn't see that yet, it hadn't happened, and besides, she was beginning to suspect that what she chose here would shape what she became there, and she did not want to become a person organized around a king's ruin.

The smell of the valley. That was it. That was the thing.

She'd known it since chapter one of her own life, though she hadn't had the words for it until now: the Stillwater Valley smelled like nothing else in the world. Cold water over slate. Wet moss. Woodsmoke from the waystation inn, and underneath it all a faint green sweetness like the air itself was remembering spring. Magic couldn't follow her there, and neither could the smell of anywhere else. It was the smell of the one place she had ever been safe, and safety, she was learning, was rarer than love and harder won.

"The smell of the valley," she said. "Cold water and woodsmoke. That's the third."

The Wolf was quiet for a moment. "Not your father. Not your name. Not the truth of what you are."

"I'm giving you the truth of what I am. All of it. That's rather the arrangement." Paloma unfolded her legs and stood, and the Wolf stood with her, and for a moment they were mirror-close, close enough that she could see the hall of memory reflected in her own eyes looking back at her. "Everyone keeps telling me who I was. The map. The archivist. You. My dead father, via wolf-dreams. Fine. Take the whole story. But let me keep three small true things — a face, a song, a smell — and I'll build myself a new story out of them. Maybe it won't be the right story. But it'll be *mine*, and I'll have chosen it, which is more than anyone in this whole cursed war can say."

The Wolf smiled with her mouth, and it was, unexpectedly, a beautiful smile — the smile she'd never managed in any mirror, unguarded and glad.

"There she is," the Wolf said. "The cartographer's daughter. You know what he drew on the back of every map he ever made? A single line, in the corner, small enough to miss: *the blank spaces are where you begin.*" It extended her own hand, palm up. "He'd have liked your three. Small, true, portable. Very much his daughter's choices."

Paloma took the offered hand.

"Now," the Wolf said, and its voice deepened, became the great grey voice of the mountain-thing beneath, vast and gentle as an avalanche deciding to be kind, "it will hurt for exactly as long as it takes. Hold the three. Say them as you go. Face, song, smell. Say them until there is nothing left to say them with."

"Tamsin's face," Paloma whispered.

The light began to move. Memories rose around her like snow falling upward — Maren's kitchen, the fevered night, the boundary stones, the storm, the dying man's blood on her hands, the ford, the archive, the gate — thousands upon thousands of moments lifting out of her, each one a small cold ache as it went. She gripped the three and said them like a rope thrown into dark water.

"Tamsin's face. Maren's lullaby. The smell of the valley."

The snow rose faster. Her mother — she hadn't known she'd had a memory of her mother until it left, a flash of brown hair and humming, gone. The first time magic answered her, green sparks off her fingertips, gone. The map whispering in dreams. Vane's patient voice. Tamsin weeping at the crossroads — *gone*, and that one tore, that one took something with it, and she said the rope again, louder:

"Tamsin's face! Maren's lullaby! The smell of the valley!"

The hall filled with light. The Wolf filled with her. And at the very end, as the last of her poured upward and outward into the great grey keeper of names, Paloma felt something press against her palm — brief, warm, unmistakable — a hand holding hers, freckled and windburned, saying without words what she would no longer have the history to understand.

Then: white. Then: quiet. Then: a girl standing alone in a hall with no ceiling, holding three small true things and nothing else, blinking at the world like a newborn crow.

Somewhere above, or below, or everywhere, a mountain rumbled — a long, slow, waking sound, like the earth clearing its throat after three hundred years.

The girl looked up. She did not know where she was. She did not know who she had been.

But she knew, with a certainty that needed no reasons, that somewhere out there was a face she loved, a song she knew, and a valley that smelled like coming home.

Far away, at the edge of the free cities, King Aldric's war-horns began to sound.
