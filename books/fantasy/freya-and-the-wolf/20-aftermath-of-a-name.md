## 20. Aftermath of a Name

The armies stood down on a Tuesday, which Tamsin thought was the most absurd part of the whole affair. Wars, in her experience, ended with fire and weeping and the signing of documents that smelled of tallow and bad faith. This one ended because a girl who could no longer remember her own name walked out onto a field between two hosts, looked at ten thousand armed men as though they were a mildly confusing market crowd, and asked — genuinely asked — if anyone knew where she might get something to eat.

The Wolf had risen through her at dawn. By noon it was done. Aldric had not been killed; he had been *remembered*, which by all accounts was worse, and his soldiers had watched their king come apart under the weight of what he actually was rather than what he'd made himself into. Vane had broken like dropped crockery. And then the great presence that had filled Paloma Thackeray like wine fills a cup had poured itself away into the mountain, patient as winter, and left behind a small woman with empty eyes standing in the trampled grass.

Tamsin had reached her first. She would always be proud of that. Not of the battle — she'd spent most of it hiding behind a supply wagon with a crossbow she never fired — but of getting there before the priests, the generals, the chroniclers, and the one Bramblewick alderman who was already sketching ideas for a statue.

"Paloma," she'd said, taking her hands. "It's me. It's Tamsin."

And Paloma — Freya now, though Tamsin's tongue kept stumbling on the new name the way a cart horse stumbles on a rutted road — had looked at her with perfect, courteous blankness and said, "That's very kind. Do I know you?"

"You will," Tamsin had promised, and meant it as a vow, and had been terrified for three days straight ever since.

* * *

The free cities did not handle peace gracefully. They were, Tamsin reflected, rather like cats presented with an unattended fish: suspicious, offended, and determined to investigate whether it was a trick.

She sat in the Council Hall of Bramblewick — a room built for arguing, with acoustics designed so that every faction could hear every other faction being wrong — and listened to them do it. The question of the hour was not how to feed the displaced, or how to disarm the usurper's garrisons, or even what to do about Aldric, who sat in his own dungeons now, lucid and quiet and asking no one's forgiveness. The question of the hour was *her*.

"The vessel," said Alderman Corvus, who had a voice like a hinge and used it often, "is a strategic asset."

"She's a person," said Magistrate Ellery.

"She is the living gate to a guardian capable of unmaking kings," Corvus persisted. "The cities survived this war because of her. The next war will be survived the same way, if we are wise enough to keep her where we can—"

"Keep her." Ellery's laugh had no warmth in it. "You make her sound like a document. Lock her in an archive, post guards, bring her out when the wax needs breaking."

"If the alternative is losing her—"

"To *what*? She gave up everything. Every memory. Her childhood, her mother's face, her own name — she poured it all out through that gate so the rest of us could keep ours." Ellery stood, and Tamsin noticed her hands were shaking. "And your proposal is to thank her by putting her in a box."

Tamsin had been quiet for two days, which for her was a personal record. But she'd watched Paloma — Freya — sit in the guest quarters of the Council House, polite and lost, while a procession of officials came to look at her the way farmers look at a famous bull. She'd watched Freya smile at each of them, gracious and vacant, and ask afterward, "Who was that?" And Tamsin had answered, every time, "Nobody important," until the lie wore grooves in her throat.

So now she stood up.

"I'll tell you what she is," Tamsin said, and the hall went quiet, because Tamsin of the Ford had a reputation, and part of that reputation was knowing exactly when to use it. "I found her in Stillwater Valley with a dead man's map in her hands and inquisitors on her trail. I hunted her, at first. Sent to take the map at any cost — those were my orders, and I want that on the record, because I nearly cost us everything twice over." She let that land. Several aldermen developed sudden interests in their notes. "I've since learned she forgives easier than I do, but then, she has less to forgive *with* these days, doesn't she?"

"Tamsin—" began the Chair.

"No. You'll hear it." She planted both hands on the table. "You're talking about her like a fortress. A well. A thing with a function. I watched her choose, in that hall under the mountain, what to keep and what to give up. She kept a lullaby. She kept the smell of a valley. She kept *my face* — mine, of all the faces available, and I had sold her name to save my family not a month before. That's what she is. Not an asset. The bravest person any of you will ever meet, and she doesn't even know it, because knowing it was part of the price."

Corvus cleared his throat. "The sentiment does honor to us all. But guardianship requires—"

"Here's what guardianship requires," Tamsin said. "A roof that isn't a cell. Food she likes — she's discovered she likes honey on everything, which I mention so you understand I'm paying attention. And people around her who see her as herself, not as the door the Wolf walked through." She looked around the hall, slow and deliberate. "Because I promise you this. The day she becomes a monument, the day you put her behind glass and call it protection — that's the day you prove Aldric right about everything. He wanted to own the past. Don't you dare build him a shrine out of her."

Silence. Then old Magistrate Ellery, dry as autumn: "And what do you propose, Scout Thackeray?"

"I propose," said Tamsin, "that she comes home with me. To the valley. Where magic can't follow and neither can politics. And I propose that anyone who wants to visit asks nicely, brings bread, and remembers she's a person and not a treaty clause."

Someone laughed. It broke the spell, mercifully. The vote, when it finally came after another four hours of argument — because free cities would debate the color of the sky if given the opportunity — went Tamsin's way, narrowly, with conditions, and with a budget line for "the custodial maintenance of the vessel" that Tamsin crossed out and rewrote as "Freya's keep" in letters large enough to be seen from the gallery.

* * *

Packing took an afternoon, mostly because Freya kept picking things up and asking what they were.

"This is yours," Tamsin said, holding out a worn leather band. "You wore it every day. You said it was from your father."

Freya turned it over in her hands. Her expression was the one Tamsin had come to know in the weeks since the mountain — open, attentive, and utterly without recognition, like a window lit in a house where nobody lives yet. "Did he? That was kind of him."

"He was kind, from what I can gather. Stubborn too. You get the stubbornness honestly."

"Do I?" Freya smiled. "You talk about me as though you've known me forever."

"I have. Nearly a year." Tamsin busied herself with the strap of a satchel that didn't need adjusting. "Ask me anything. That's our arrangement, isn't it? You ask, I answer, and if I don't know, I say so."

"Our arrangement." Freya tried the words like a foreign dish. "You make it sound very formal."

"It's the most important contract I've ever been party to, so yes, formal." Tamsin shouldered the pack. "Come on. The valley's three days' walk if we don't dawdle, and there's an innkeeper at the waystation who owes you his life and expresses it through enormous breakfasts."

They walked. Autumn had come down into the lowlands while the world was busy saving itself, and the roads were loud with returning soldiers and refugees and peddlers already selling commemorative ribbons, which Tamsin found obscene and Freya found pretty, and bought one, green, because it matched her eyes, which was somehow worse than the obscenity.

On the second evening, camped under a leaning oak, Freya asked the question Tamsin had been dreading.

"What was I like? Before."

Tamsin fed the fire another branch and considered lying, considered softening, and settled — as always — on the truth, because Freya always knew when she was being handled, memory or no memory. Some things survived the pouring-out.

"Frightened," she said. "Brilliantly, stubbornly frightened. You'd spent years being invisible on purpose, and you were *good* at it, and then the whole world decided to look at you anyway." She poked the fire. "You were also funnier than you have any right to be. You once talked your way past an inquisitor's checkpoint by pretending to be a cheese merchant with strong opinions about rinds. I have never respected anyone more."

"A cheese merchant."

"With opinions, Freya. About rinds."

Freya laughed — really laughed, head back, and Tamsin felt something in her chest unknot that had been tied since the battlefield. "I wish I remembered that."

"No you don't, not yet. Wishing for it is enough for now." Tamsin lay back against the pack. "That's the deal. I remember for both of us until you can carry your half."

"And if I never can?"

"Then I carry it forever. I'm told I'm stubborn too."

"Where do I get the stubbornness?" Freya asked, grinning.

"Your father. And Maren, your mistress — teacher. Hedge-witch, terrifying, secretly fond of you. She used to make you copy herb-lore until your fingers turned green."

"Tell me about her tomorrow," Freya said, settling down. "Tell me one thing every day. That can be the arrangement."

"One thing a day," Tamsin agreed. "Starting tomorrow with Maren, who once turned a tax collector into a temporary newt."

"That's not true."

"It's absolutely true and there are witnesses."

Freya's laughter followed Tamsin down into sleep, and it was the best sound she'd heard since before the war, maybe ever.

* * *

The waystation inn still stood, which surprised no one more than its owner. Hobb — wide, flour-dusted, prone to crying — took one look at Freya in his doorway and burst into tears so violently that a visiting wool merchant asked if someone had died.

"Worse," Hobb managed. "She don't remember me."

"She remembers your breakfasts," Tamsin said. "I've been building it up for three days. No pressure."

What followed was, by Tamsin's account and the sworn testimony of six travelers, the largest breakfast ever served in Stillwater Valley. Freya ate her way through it with the solemn delight of someone discovering food for the first time — which, in fairness, she effectively was — and pronounced honey on porridge to be the single greatest invention of civilization. Hobb had to leave the room twice.

Afterward, Freya wandered out to the boundary stones, and Tamsin let her go alone, watching from the doorway. The girl stood where the valley's silence began, where magic guttered out like a wet wick, and Tamsin saw her tilt her head as if listening to something just below hearing.

When she came back, her eyes were bright and puzzled. "There's something here," she said. "Under everything. Like a song you almost know the words to."

"The valley forgets," Tamsin said carefully. "That's why it's safe. Why it was always safe — for you, for the map, for all of it. Your father built the silence on purpose."

"My father." Freya touched the leather band at her wrist. "Tell me about him. Is that today's thing?"

Tamsin hesitated. Some truths were heavy freight, and Freya had only just started walking again. But she'd promised no handling, no softening.

"He drew maps," she said. "Beautiful ones. He hid the truest map he ever made inside his daughter's childhood, and then he hid his daughter in the one place in the world where no one could follow — including, I think, her own magic. He wasn't running from something, Freya. He was keeping something. Keeping *you*."

Freya was quiet a long moment. Then: "He sounds tired."

"He was," Tamsin said, startled. "By all accounts, he was."

Freya nodded slowly, filing it away wherever she filed things now — some new country inside her, uncharted, growing its own towns. "Tomorrow," she said, "tell me about the cheese merchant."

* * *

Evening came down gold over the valley. Inside the inn, Hobb was arguing happily with his cook about whether Freya's return warranted a festival (it did not, per Freya, who had hidden in the cellar until the matter was settled). Outside, Tamsin found her sitting on the mounting block, turning the green ribbon over in her fingers.

"The council sent a letter," Tamsin said. "Official seal and everything. They want to discuss 'ongoing arrangements.' Corvus wants quarterly inspections."

"Inspections of what?"

"You. Us. Whether the Wolf is comfortable." Tamsin sat down beside her. "I told them where they could file their quarterly inspections. Politely. Mostly politely."

Freya smiled, but it faded into something more thoughtful. "Do you think they're right to worry? About what sleeps in the mountain?"

"I think the Wolf waited a thousand years without eating anyone. I think it can manage patience a while longer." Tamsin bumped her shoulder. "Besides, it left you the best parts. Honey opinions. Cheese nerve. Terrible taste in ribbons."

"Green suits me."

"Green suits everyone, that's the problem with green."

They sat in the quiet, the good kind, the valley kind. And then Freya said, softly, "Tamsin. When you tell me the story — our story — every night. There's a piece you always skip. Right at the end. After the battle, before the walking home."

Tamsin's stomach dropped. "There's nothing—"

"There's a piece," Freya repeated, gentle and immovable, "and you skip it every time, and tonight I'd like to know what it is."

Tamsin looked out at the darkening stones, at the boundary where the silence lived, and understood with terrible clarity that the girl beside her was going to be a person who noticed things, monument or no monument, and that some prices could not be paid in advance — only daily, honestly, one thing at a time.

"All right," she said. "But not tonight. Tonight I'll tell you about the newt."

Freya laughed, and let it go — for now. But as they went in to supper, Tamsin caught her glancing back once toward the mountain, head tilted, listening to that song beneath the world.

And Tamsin wondered, with a tenderness that felt a lot like fear, what words Freya was almost remembering.
