## 21. The Stranger Who Remembers

The valley had not changed, which was the cruelest and kindest thing about it.

Paloma stood at the boundary stones — she knew they were called that because Tamsin told her so every time they passed them, in the same patient voice, as though reciting a prayer that had to be said exactly right or it wouldn't count — and felt nothing where magic should have been. There was no ache of leaving power behind, no wet-wick guttering out. There was simply the wind coming down off the mountain, smelling of pine and cold stone, and Tamsin beside her with two horses and one enormous pack and an expression she wore whenever she was deciding whether to say something.

"You're doing the face," Paloma said.

"What face?"

"The face you make before you tell me something I already know."

Tamsin swung down from her horse. "I don't make a face."

"You absolutely do. Your eyebrows go all—" Paloma demonstrated, drawing her own brows together and slightly upward, like a man bracing for rain.

"That's not a face, that's concern."

"It's a face. You've been making it since the ford."

"I've been making it since the ford," Tamsin agreed, and something in her voice went soft and strange, the way it always did when their story touched a part of itself she hadn't gotten to yet. "Come on. The innkeeper will want to see you before the light goes."

* * *

Marta at the waystation inn had gone gray around the muzzle since — well, since whenever it was. Paloma wouldn't have known either way. That was the arithmetic of her life now: everything was either since-when-ever-it-was or since-Tamsin-said-so, and the second category was growing fat while the first starved.

"By the old gods," Marta said, dropping a ladle into the stew pot with a clang. "It's you. It's actually you. I'd heard—" She stopped herself, which was kind, and then didn't stop herself, which was human. "I'd heard you didn't remember anything."

"Not anything," Paloma said. "I remember how to ride. I remember how to make bread rise without magic, which Marta-the-other-Maren taught me, apparently, and which I'm told is more useful anyway." She paused. "Did I just call her Maren? Maren. The hedge-witch. Sorry. Tamsin tells me about her too."

"Maren." Marta's eyes went distant and complicated. "She still lives up past the birches. Sends down for flour and complains about my prices. She doesn't know you're back, does she."

"No," Tamsin said, in the tone of someone closing a door gently but firmly.

"And you're staying?"

"At the waystation," Paloma said. "Through winter, probably. Through longer than winter, if you'll have me. I can work. Apparently I'm good with my hands."

"She's insufferable about it," Tamsin said. "First week on the road she re-sewed a saddlebag seam and looked at me like she'd invented thread."

"I had invented thread. As far as I knew."

Marta laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, and then put them both to work, because that was the way of waystations: grief and homecoming were both just chores waiting to happen.

* * *

The room they shared was small and smelled of cedar and lamp oil, and every night, without fail, Tamsin told the story.

That had been the bargain — though Paloma hadn't made it, couldn't remember making it, and sometimes suspected Tamsin had made it with a version of her who no longer existed, which raised questions neither of them liked to poke at. But the shape of it was simple. Every night, lights out, blanket up, Tamsin would settle against the headboard and begin:

"Once there was a girl who ran away from a witch—"

"Was I brave when I ran away?" Paloma asked, the first night. "Or stupid?"

"Both. Bravely stupid. Stupidly brave. Keep still, I'm telling this."

And Tamsin would tell it: the dawn flight across the stones, the storm, the wounded stranger in usurper's livery collapsing at the girl's feet. The cellar. The blood-inked map pressed into her hands by a dying man who gasped a name — hers, or almost hers. The flight deeper into the valley. The cartographer's mark. The scout at the ford who'd come to take the map at any cost and found she couldn't pry it from the girl's fingers, and so, being a sensible woman, had pried nothing and followed everything.

"Was the scout pretty?" Paloma asked, third night in.

"The scout was muddy, exhausted, and holding a knife."

"But pretty."

"...The scout was having a very bad week."

"So yes."

"Freya."

"Sorry. Go on."

And Tamsin would go on: the road out of Stillness, the magic flooding back like water into a dry well, painful as being born. The ruined library city and the blind archivist who'd recognized her face from portraits painted before the war. The mountain gate and the wolf beneath it, sleeping not in a body but in a bloodline — in her, in the hollows of her, in the memories she'd carried without knowing they were cargo.

Then the hard part. Tamsin never rushed the hard part. The sold name. The crossroads ambush. Vane with his relic and his patience. The offered third path — keep your memories, live rich, let the world sleep on. One long night of temptation, honestly rendered, because Tamsin refused to make her past self a saint.

"You thought about it," Tamsin said once, quietly, in the middle of that chapter. "Really thought about it. I want you to know that's all right. Anyone would have."

"Did I almost say yes?"

"You asked him what Aldric was afraid of. And he told you — the Wolf remembers him as he truly was. And you went quiet for a long time, and then you said, 'So the truth is a weapon after all,' and stole his knife two days later."

Paloma lay in the dark listening to this stranger describe a woman she'd never met, this Paloma Thackeray, this Freya, this vessel, this thief of relics and keeper of names — and felt what she always felt, which was a tenderness so acute it was nearly grief. Like hearing about a twin sister who died young. Like reading a book whose last pages had been torn out, except the last pages were her.

"Tamsin."

"Hm?"

"Why do you tell it every night? You could tell me once. I'd remember. I remember things now — the road, the cities, the archivist's hands. My memory works fine going forward."

Tamsin was quiet long enough that the fire ticked twice.

"Because the first week," she said finally, "you woke up screaming, and you didn't know why, and neither did I, and I realized — you shouldn't have to learn your own life like a lesson. It should come to you like a story. Stories are kinder. Stories you can fall asleep inside of."

* * *

Winter came down off the mountain and settled over the valley like a hen over eggs, and Paloma learned the shape of her new days.

Mornings: bread, and the inn's endless small repairs, and the discovery that her hands remembered skills her head did not — she could mend a latch, splint a chair, set a bone in a farmer's thumb with a confidence that startled everyone including herself. Her body was a house full of furniture left behind by the previous tenant. She kept finding rooms.

Afternoons: she walked. Always toward the mountain, never quite to it. There was a track that switchbacked up through the pines toward a shoulder of granite where, Tamsin said, a gate stood sealed, and beneath the gate a hall, and in the hall —

"In the hall," Paloma prompted, one white afternoon, breath steaming.

"The Wolf sleeps," Tamsin said. "Patient. It agreed to sleep, after. After everything. It said—" and here Tamsin always hesitated, because this part she'd witnessed herself and it sat on her differently, "—it said the world had earned its quiet. It would wake only if the world forgot again. Truly forgot. Then it would rise and be witness, whether the world wanted witnessing or not."

"Do you think it dreams?"

"I think it dreams in you, sometimes. You talk in your sleep."

"I do not."

"You said 'the ink won't burn' four times last Tuesday. Very firmly. Like you were arguing with someone."

Paloma laughed, and the laugh went up through the pines and scattered crows, and for a moment the mountain seemed to lean in and listen, the way old animals do — not alarmed, not interested, exactly. Just keeping accounts.

Evenings: the story. Always the story. Tamsin had begun varying it, Paloma noticed — adding details, testing different emphases, the way a musician tries a song in new keys. Some nights the stranger at the door was tragic; some nights he was funny, stumbling in and knocking over a chair and apologizing to the chair. Once Tamsin told the ford scene entirely from the scout's point of view, and Paloma had listened with her chin on her knees and said, at the end, "The scout loved her already, didn't she. At the ford."

Tamsin had looked at the fire for a while.

"The scout didn't know what love was yet," she said. "She knew the map wouldn't leave the girl's hands. She told herself it was duty."

"And now?"

"Now the scout thinks duty was the excuse love used to get close."

Paloma reached over and took her hand, and neither of them said anything else, because some chapters don't need telling to be true.

* * *

There were hard nights, of course. Nights when the story wasn't enough.

Once — deep midwinter, snow to the sills — Paloma woke at the hour when even the fire seems asleep, and lay staring at the ceiling beams, and felt the terrible smoothness of herself, the blankness where a childhood should be. Not pain. Worse than pain: absence, polished clean. She got up without waking Tamsin and stood at the window watching snow come down over the valley, over the boundary stones, over the whole soft forgetting country her father had built to hide her in.

A hand on her shoulder. Tamsin, awake after all, hair a disaster, eyes sharp even half-blind with sleep.

"Tell me a piece I haven't heard," Paloma said. "Not from the story. A piece nobody tells. Something small."

Tamsin thought. Snow ticked against the glass.

"You hated porridge," she said. "Before. Maren made it with honey and you still complained. You said honey was just sweetness lying about being medicine."

Paloma huffed a laugh. "That sounds like me."

"It is you. You said it Tuesday. About the cough syrup."

And that was the thing, Paloma was learning — the thing that kept the aching hope from tipping over into plain ache. She was not a stranger wearing Paloma's face. She was Paloma, continuous, the same person underneath the scraped surface, the way a river is the same river though every drop has changed. The memories were gone. The grain of her remained. Tamsin saw to it daily, pointing out the grain like a carpenter proud of the wood.

* * *

In the spring, Maren came down from the birches.

Paloma saw her first from the inn yard — a small, stooped figure with a walking stick and a basket, arguing with Marta's prices from forty paces away, audibly. And Paloma felt her heart do something complicated, because here was a woman who had raised her, hidden her, lied to her, grieved her, and whom she did not know at all.

Maren stopped when she saw her. The basket handle creaked under her grip.

"Well," Maren said. "You've got her stance. The way she used to stand in my garden, daring the beans to grow."

"I'm told I'm insufferable with my hands," Paloma offered.

"You always were." Maren's eyes were wet and furious, which Paloma would learn was simply how Maren's eyes worked. "Do you want the lullaby? I sang it to you for eleven years. It's yours if you want it. No strings."

Paloma considered. Somewhere under her ribs, something leaned toward the offer like a plant toward light.

"Yes," she said. "But sing it wrong first. So I can learn it properly myself."

Maren barked a laugh that turned into a sob halfway through, and sang it wrong, gloriously wrong, every note in the wrong place, and Paloma hummed along badly, and between the two of them they built something new out of something old, which was, Paloma was beginning to understand, the only way anything was ever built.

* * *

On the last mild evening of the year's turn, they climbed as far as the shoulder of granite. The gate was there — vast, seamless, carved with names worn shallow by weather — and Paloma laid her palm against it the way she did most weeks, a habit she'd invented for herself and defended fiercely as tradition.

The stone was warm.

It was always warm. Tamsin swore it hadn't been, before. But Paloma chose to believe the warmth was for her, specifically, the way a sleeping dog stays warm for whoever leans against it.

"He's still there," she said. "Underneath. I can feel it. Like a heartbeat through a wall."

"The Wolf sleeps in the mountain," Tamsin said, "patient."

"No." Paloma kept her hand flat on the stone, and smiled at the view — the valley below, gold-green in the low sun, the waystation's chimney smoking, Marta's laundry, Maren's birches, all of it small and stubborn and worth waking for, if it ever came to that. "The Wolf sleeps in *me*. The mountain's just where I left the door."

They stayed until the sun went, and walked down in the blue dark, and that night Tamsin began the story as always — *"once there was a girl who ran away from a witch"* — but Paloma held up a hand.

"Tomorrow," she said, "tell me the part where the stranger dies. The one who gave me the map. You always go fast there."

Tamsin went very still.

"There's a reason I go fast there."

"I know. Tomorrow anyway." Paloma pulled the blanket up, and in the dark she said the thing she'd been carrying all day like a stone in her shoe: "He whispered a name to me. Before he died. Everyone keeps saying it — the archivist, Maren, even you, once, when you thought I was asleep. A name that isn't Paloma." She turned her head on the pillow. "Tomorrow, Tamsin. Tell me the name. I think I'm ready to hear it."

Outside, far up the mountain, something old and patient turned over in its sleep — and, very faintly, through the stone and the snow and the years, it sounded almost like approval.
