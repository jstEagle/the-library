## 22. Freya and the Wolf

The valley had a way of marking time that had nothing to do with calendars. It marked it in mud.

One year to the day since Tamsin had hauled Paloma out of the Stillwater shallows with the map clutched to her chest like a second heartbeat, and the waystation inn's yard was a churned brown sea of hoofprints and cart ruts, because spring in the valley meant everyone remembered at once that they owned things with wheels.

"You're doing it again," Tamsin said from the doorway, arms crossed, one boot propped against the frame the way she always did when she wanted to look casual and never quite managed it. "Standing in the rain watching geese."

"They're not geese. They're ducks."

"They're a menace, is what they are. One of them bit Old Hewitt this morning."

"Old Hewitt stole its bread. That's not menace, that's justice."

Tamsin snorted and came out under the eaves beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. The rain came down soft and steady, the kind of rain that didn't so much fall as settle, as if the sky had decided the valley deserved a long, unhurried drink. Beyond the yard, the boundary stones stood grey and patient in the mist, and beyond them — invisible, but Paloma felt it anyway, the way you feel a wall behind wallpaper — the mountain where the Wolf slept.

Where *she* slept, if you believed what the archivists said. Paloma preferred not to think about it too hard before breakfast.

"A year," Tamsin said.

"Mm."

"You don't remember any of it."

"No." Paloma watched a duck terrorize a chicken for no discernible reason. "But I'm told I was there."

"*There* is an understatement. You were insufferable. You argued with a war council. You were nineteen and you told General Corvane his battle plan had 'the strategic elegance of a dropped pie.'"

"That doesn't sound like me."

"It sounds exactly like you. You're insufferable now. You just have less material."

Paloma laughed, and the laugh did what it always did — startled her a little on its way out. She still caught herself waiting for feelings to arrive with their histories attached, like guests who'd forgotten their luggage. Joy came, but without the memory of why joy was safe. Grief came, thin and clean, without the weight of what she'd lost. It was a strange way to live, inside a self with all the furniture removed. But the rooms were hers. She'd swept them herself.

Mostly. There was the matter of the Wolf curled up in the cellar of her, so to speak. But the Wolf was patient, and mostly quiet, and on good days Paloma could almost pretend she was simply a woman who worked at an inn and was unreasonably fond of ducks.

Almost.

"Tamsin." She turned. Rain dripped off the eaves between them. "Why are you looking at me like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you've got a frog in your pocket and you're deciding whether to tell me."

"That is a very specific accusation."

"I've learned to read you. It only took eleven months."

Tamsin looked out at the rain for a moment. When she spoke again, the joke had gone out of her voice, replaced by something careful. "I've been carrying something for a year. Waiting for today."

"The anniversary?"

"The anniversary. And because I kept hoping I'd find the right moment, and then I realized there isn't one. There's never a right moment for anything that matters. You just pick a muddy Tuesday and open your hand."

She reached into her coat and drew out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft from handling. Whatever was inside had been carried a long way, and often.

Paloma didn't take it. Not yet. "Is it going to make me cry?"

"Probably."

"Then give it here before I lose my nerve."

* * *

Inside, by the fire, with the inn empty between the breakfast rush and the noon carts, Tamsin unwrapped the bundle on the table.

It was a book. Small, water-swollen at one corner, its leather cover cracked like old riverbed. No title. Just a maker's mark stamped into the cover: a compass rose with a wolf's head at its center.

Paloma's breath went strange.

"I know that mark," she said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "It's on the map. It's—"

"Your father's. Yes." Tamsin pushed the book across the table. "This belonged to him. It came back to us after — after everything. With the archives being rebuilt, things surface. Things people hid during the war, in walls, in wells. A clerk found this in the rubble of the old capital and traced the mark. He brought it to the council. The council sent it to me, because apparently I'm the person who decides things about you now, which is a responsibility nobody asked me to take and which I have discharged with characteristic excellence."

"Tamsin."

"Open it."

Her hands weren't quite steady. She opened the book.

It was a journal — her father's, unmistakably, though she had no memory of him at all. The handwriting was small and slanted, crowded into margins, full of arrows and corrections, the writing of a man whose thoughts moved faster than his pen. Sketches filled the spaces between entries: the valley's boundary stones, the mountain gate, a tower she almost recognized from dreams she no longer had. And names. Pages and pages of names, crossed out and rewritten, each one annotated in that same restless hand.

"What is it?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"He was choosing," Paloma said slowly. The understanding rose in her like water finding its level. "Names. He was choosing a name."

"For you?"

"For—" She stopped. Turned a page. Another. The ink had smeared in places, water-damaged, but near the end of the book the entries grew shorter, more urgent. The last written page held a single name, written over and over, larger each time, as if he'd been practicing it, or praying it.

*Freya. Freya. Freya.*

The room tilted gently. Paloma put her palm flat on the table.

"He whispered it," she said. "At the end. To the stranger who brought me the map. That was the name the dying man gasped — my name, or almost mine. Everyone thought it was a message, a key, a piece of the puzzle." She laughed, and it came out wet. "It wasn't a puzzle. It was a father naming his daughter. He was telling the stranger what to call me. What I should have been called all along."

Tamsin reached across the table and covered her hand. "Paloma was Maren's name for you. The one she gave you to hide you. It kept you safe — I'll never say otherwise. But it was a disguise. This one—" she tapped the page, gentle, "—this one was chosen. By a man who knew exactly what he was doing, right up until he couldn't anymore. He didn't leave you a duty, Paloma. He left you a gift. There's a difference. I've been thinking about it for a year."

Paloma couldn't speak. Outside, the rain went on settling, patient as everything in the valley. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under the innkeeper's round and comfortable tread.

"So," Tamsin said, when the silence had stretched as far as it could bear. "Happy birthday, sort of. Or naming day. Or whatever we're calling it. Freya."

The name landed in her chest and sat there, warm, like a stone left too long by a fire.

It didn't fit yet. That was the thing — it didn't fit the way a borrowed coat doesn't fit, and also the way a real coat doesn't fit the first winter you wear it. But underneath the strangeness was something else. A rightness. As if some part of her, the part that had been listening all along, heard the name and stood up.

"Say it again," Paloma whispered.

"Freya."

And the world broke open — gently, the way ice breaks on a river in spring, in long bright seams.

*Hands. Big hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, smoothing a sheet of leather across a scarred wooden table. Lamplight. The smell of tallow and iron-gall ink and, faintly, of pine smoke. The hands dip a pen and draw — a line that becomes a river, a river that becomes the valley, the valley that becomes a map. A voice, low and tired and amused, humming half a tune — Maren's lullaby, the same lullaby, older than Maren's singing of it. And the hands pause over the map's heart, over the place where the mountain gate will be, and press there once, softly, the way you'd press a hand to a sleeping child's back.*

*Then the voice says — to someone off in the dark of the room, or maybe to the map itself, or maybe, somehow, across years and death and forgetting, to her:*

*"Freya. There. Now it's true."*

The vision lasted three heartbeats. Then it was gone, and Paloma was crying at an inn table in the Stillwater Valley, holding her father's journal, laughing through it because — because of course. Of course it was the hands. Of all the things a year of stories could have given her back, the first thing her own mind chose, unprompted, unbidden, was her father's hands on the map.

Not the war. Not the Wolf. Not the terrible choice at the gate.

The hands. The lamplight. The name, made true.

"Tamsin." She wiped her face with her sleeve, graceless, sniffling. "I remembered something."

Tamsin had gone very still. "What? What did you see?"

"His hands. Drawing the map. He named me while he drew it — he said it made it true." She pressed her fingers to her mouth. "Tamsin, the doctors said nothing would come back. They said the surrender was total, that memories don't grow back."

"The doctors," Tamsin said, "also said you'd be bedridden for a month after the gate, and you were up arguing with General Corvane in four days. The doctors don't know you." She leaned forward, eyes bright and fierce and a little afraid. "What else? Did you see anything else?"

"No. Just that. Just—" Paloma breathed out. "Just enough."

"Just enough," Tamsin repeated softly. She sat back. And then, because she was Tamsin, and because grief and wonder in equal measure made her unbearable, she added: "You realize this means you're going to be insufferable about it. 'When I was Freya, I remembered my father's hands.' You'll work it into every conversation. You'll tell the ducks."

"I will absolutely tell the ducks."

* * *

They walked up to the boundary stones that evening, when the rain had thinned to mist and the western sky had gone the color of hammered copper. It had become a habit over the year — the evening walk, the two of them, the stones. Tamsin claimed it was exercise. Paloma knew it was pilgrimage, though neither of them said so.

The mountain stood dark against the last light. Somewhere in its deeps, the Wolf slept — the guardian, the witness, the thing that wore her face and kept every memory she'd surrendered, patient as stone. The free cities had debated for a year about what to do with it. Monuments had been proposed. Paloma had vetoed them all through Tamsin, on the grounds that she refused to be a statue and refused even more strongly to let a mountain become one. Let it sleep. Let it keep what it kept. Some things should be remembered by something older than people.

At the stones, Tamsin stopped, as she always did, and looked back down the valley — the habit of a scout, checking the road behind. Nothing there. Nothing had been there for a year. Vane was ash and testimony; Aldric was a cautionary tale told in three cities with varying degrees of accuracy; the war was a thing that lived in books now, in the rebuilt archives, in Tamsin's brother's careful histories.

"Freya," Tamsin said, trying the name out in the open air for the first time.

It sounded different out here. Bigger. It sounded like it had room to grow into.

"It's strange," Paloma said. "I've been Paloma for a year. It's the only name I remember answering to. It feels like giving something up."

"You're not giving anything up. That's the whole point." Tamsin turned to face her. In the copper light her expression was unguarded in a way she almost never allowed — the scout's mask set down entirely. "You spent a year being the girl who lost everything. The vessel. The sacrifice. The story everyone tells has you handing over your whole self at that gate, and it's a beautiful story, and it's true, and I hate it, because it ends with you empty and everyone treating you like a miracle instead of a person." She took a breath. "So here's how I see it. Paloma was the name that kept you hidden. Freya is the name that lets you begin. Not inherit. Begin. Your father didn't hand you a finished self — he handed you a door. You get to walk through it however you like."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. "You've been rehearsing that."

"For weeks. On the walk. Out loud. The ducks have heard it four times."

"I knew it. The ducks and I discuss you, you know."

"I assumed as much."

Paloma laughed — and then, before the courage could evaporate, she did the thing she'd been circling for months the way a duck circles bread. She took Tamsin's hand. Tamsin, veteran of a dozen skirmishes, survivor of betrayal and forgiveness and one genuinely catastrophic pie-related argument with a general, went pink to the ears.

"Well," Tamsin said. "That's new."

"Is it?" Paloma said. "Because I'm told I used to look at you like this before, too. I'd hate to break continuity."

"You're told correctly," Tamsin managed. "For the record."

"For the record," Paloma agreed, and squeezed her hand, and turned to look at the mountain one last time before the light went.

The first star was out above the peak. The valley below them was lighting window by window — the inn, the forge, Old Hewitt's crooked farmhouse — small warm answers to the gathering dark. Somewhere in the yard, a duck was committing some fresh outrage. Tomorrow there would be mud and carts and travelers wanting breakfast, and a journal to read properly, cover to cover, a page at a time, learning her father the way you learn a friend: slowly, with tea, with interruptions.

A year ago she had woken with nothing. Now she had a name with roots, a friend with steady hands, a first memory like a single lamp lit in a great dark house — and the sense, growing stronger every day, that the house had many rooms, and the lamps would come on one by one, in no hurry, in their own order.

"Come on," Tamsin said. "It's cold, and Hewitt promised us pie, and you can tell me everything you saw. Every detail. I want all of it."

"All of it?"

"All of it. We've got time." She smiled, and started down the hill toward the lights. "We've got nothing but time, Freya."

And Freya — who had been Paloma, who had been the vessel, who had been, briefly and terribly, a god's empty cup — followed her down through the grass toward the inn, toward the pie, toward the long slow work of becoming, smiling at the sound of her own name like a woman hearing music she'd always known and was only now allowed to dance to.

Behind them, the mountain kept its silence. But if either of them had looked back — and neither did, because neither needed to — they might have seen, high on the dark slope where the gate stood sealed, a faint warmth in the stone, brief as a heartbeat, patient as morning.

Something down in the deeps had heard the name too.

And it, too, had begun again.
