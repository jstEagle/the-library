## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The frost came to Corvane three weeks early that year, and it came hungry.

Astrid Halloran knew this because she could feel it in her fingers before she felt it anywhere else—the old guild scar on her left forearm aching like a struck bell whenever the cold meant business. She had learned to read the weather through the mark the way sailors read the sea, and what it told her this morning, as she knotted the last of her luck-cords by candlelight, was: *bundle up, girl, and don't linger.*

She bundled up. Lingering was another matter. Lingering was how you starved.

By the time the sun hauled itself over the slate roofs of the Tanner's Quarter, she had claimed her usual corner of Gullmarket Square—a stretch of wall beneath a baker's awning where the ovens' warmth leaked down and made her wares look almost respectable. She laid them out on a scrap of grey cloth with the care of a jeweler and the honesty of a woman who had long since stopped lying to herself about what she was selling.

Luck-knots. Fever-charms. A small jar of "blessed salt" that was, in fact, ordinary salt she had talked to nicely.

None of it worked. That was the point. That was the whole desperate, careful point of Astrid Halloran's life: everything she sold was harmless, which is to say, empty, which is to say, legal. The Lanternwright Guild had once filled knots like these with true light—wards against fever, blessings on the harvest, songs stitched into thread. The Guild had been outlawed six years ago, its masters hanged from the Bridge of Sighs, its apprentices scattered or worse. What remained was Astrid, twenty-three years old, selling string to the frightened at two coppers a knot.

"Morning, love," said the baker's wife, leaning out with a heel of yesterday's bread. "You've got frost in your eyebrows again."

"It's fashionable," Astrid said. "The northern ladies pay a fortune for powdered brows."

"The northern ladies are mad." The bread landed in her lap anyway, warm as an apology. This was the arrangement: Astrid kept the corner swept, the beggars moved along, and Maren fed her breakfast. In Corvane, kindness survived the way weeds did—in cracks, quietly, where nobody official thought to look.

Her first customer came before the market bells finished ringing.

He was a fisherman's son, maybe sixteen, with chapped hands and the particular gray face of someone who hadn't slept. His mother had the lung-fever. He didn't say so; he didn't have to. They never did. They just stood there holding their coins like they were ashamed of them.

"For fever," he said, nodding at the little braided charms of blue thread and river-stone. "Do they... I mean, people say—"

"People say a lot of things," Astrid said gently. She picked one up and turned it in the light so he could see the knotwork, tight and even, made by hands that had once made *real* ones and grieved the difference every single day. "What I'll tell you is this: my grandmother swore by them, and my grandmother lived to ninety-one, and she was a stubborn old goat who'd have argued with death itself at the door. Maybe the charm helped. Maybe the stubbornness. Two coppers either way, and you get both."

He bought two. She threw in a third and told him it was for luck, and watched him go with his shoulders a little straighter, and hated herself in the small, familiar way she hated herself every morning before the sun got high enough to burn it off.

*It's not nothing,* she reminded herself. *Hope isn't nothing. Hope is the only thing that ever was something.*

It was a nice thought. It usually lasted until midmorning.

* * *

Gullmarket filled as the bells died away: fishwives shouting prices, a knife-grinder working his wheel, children weaving between legs with stolen apples. Astrid sold a luck-knot to a carter whose axle had been singing a bad song for a week. She sold a "ward against ill neighbors" to a widow with a gleam in her eye that suggested the ill neighbor had better watch himself. She sold nothing at all for an hour, and spent it watching the sky.

The sky was wrong. She'd been telling herself it wasn't for months, but there was no getting around it anymore: the clouds sat too low and too still, like a held breath. The frost didn't melt by noon the way frost should. And last week, the sparrows—Corvane's fat, shameless, eternal sparrows—had gone quiet. All of them. At once.

Astrid did not let herself think about what that might mean. Thinking about what things meant was a luxury for people without a hanging offense tattooed into their skin.

Because that was the other thing about the mark. It wasn't just an ache in the cold. It was a brand, in the old way, in the guild way—a lantern-and-key inked in silver-threaded ink along the inside of her left forearm, visible only when she wanted it to be, which was never. To the Inquisition, that mark was a confession. To Astrid, it was the last living piece of everyone she had loved, and she wore it under three layers of sleeve and wool the way other people wore mourning rings.

"Girl. Girl! Are your ears full of wax?"

Astrid blinked. Old Bess Tarrow stood before her stall, wrapped in a shawl that had been mended more times than the shawl had any right to permit, glaring at her with the ferocity of the very old and very fond.

"Bess. You're supposed to be home. Your hip—"

"My hip can mind itself. Give me a fever-charm." Bess slapped down four coppers, twice the price, and when Astrid tried to give two back, the old woman closed her hand over hers with surprising strength. "For the extra, you'll tell me why you look like a cat in a room full of rocking chairs today. Something's wrong with the air. Don't tell me there isn't. I'm eighty-one; I know air."

Astrid hesitated. Then, because Bess Tarrow had been buying her useless charms since her first week in Corvane, and because some lonelinesses were heavier than caution: "The birds have stopped singing. Have you noticed?"

Bess went still in a way that old people shouldn't be able to go still. Her eyes drifted up, past the awning, to the pale and silent sky.

"Aye," she said quietly. "I noticed." She tucked the charm into her shawl and patted Astrid's hand once, hard, like a blow and a blessing in one. "You keep your sleeves down, love. And you keep your head. Whatever's coming—and something's coming, the air's been wrong since the equinox—it comes for the careless first."

Then she shuffled off toward the fish stalls, and Astrid stood there feeling the words settle over her like snow.

*Whatever's coming.* Wonderful. As if the list weren't long enough already: the Inquisition, the rent, the winter, the fact that she had eaten bread and guilt for breakfast and called it a meal.

* * *

The raid came at half past noon, and it came the way raids always came in Corvane: politely.

That was the worst of it. Six years of purges had taught the Inquisition that fear worked better than violence, so they arrived without horns or torches—just eight gray-cloaked wardens walking into Gullmarket at an unhurried pace, spreading out through the crowd like ink through water, and a crier with a pleasant voice announcing that the market would pause while "a routine inquiry concerning unlawful charms" was conducted.

The market did not pause. The market *died*. Stalls folded in seconds. Vendors became statues. Somewhere a child started crying and was hushed with a hand over its mouth, and the silence afterward was the kind of silence Astrid imagined lived inside tombs.

She had done this drill a hundred times in her head. Hands steady. Pack the cloth, fold the corners, charms into the false bottom of her basket, basket under the awning's loose board—no, no time for the board, basket under her cloak, walk, don't run, *walk*, head down, bored face, bored face, *bored face*—

"You there. Charm-seller."

The warden was young. That was somehow worse. His gray cloak was new, his boots were new, and his eyes had the terrible earnestness of a man who still believed in his work. Behind him, two more wardens had begun turning over the abandoned stalls of slower vendors, methodical as clerks.

"Me?" said Astrid, with the exact note of bewildered innocence she had practiced in front of a cracked mirror until she could have taught a class. "I sell buttons, mostly. Thread. A bit of ribbon."

"Show me."

She showed him. Buttons, thread, ribbon, and—because hope is a gambler's disease—one luck-knot she'd failed to pack fast enough, sitting on the cloth like a confession.

The young warden picked it up. Turned it over. And here was the thing, the awful thing, the thing that made Astrid's heart slam against her ribs: he looked at the knotwork *carefully*. Too carefully. His thumb found the pattern of the braid, and his brow furrowed, because the knotwork was good—too good, guild-good, the kind of even tension that no button-seller's grandmother should be able to manage—and somewhere behind his earnest young eyes a question was beginning to form.

"This is fine work," he said slowly.

"My gran's," Astrid said. "I copy her. She was from the north; they braid everything up there. Sheep, hair, grudges—"

"Hold out your arms."

The world went narrow and cold. Astrid heard her own heartbeat. She thought, with strange clarity: *three layers of sleeve, he'll have to roll them, there's time, there's no time—*

"She's with me."

Bess Tarrow materialized at Astrid's elbow like a vengeful spirit in a mended shawl, and thrust her wrist out at the warden before Astrid could move.

"And *I'm* the charm-seller, sonny, if you're hunting charm-sellers. Go on. Roll up my sleeve. Mind the bruises; I bruise like a plum these days. There's a fever-charm in my shawl pocket, bought fair off this girl here, and if that's a crime then arrest me, but do it quick, because I'm eighty-one and I haven't got all day, and neither, I'd wager, have you—you've got the look of a lad whose sergeant counts his arrests, and I promise you on every saint I've outlived, an old woman's charms aren't worth the paperwork."

The warden stared at her. Bess stared back, magnificent, immovable, her chin up and her watery eyes blazing with a fury that Astrid realized—with a lurch—was not entirely performance. The fury was real. It was simply being *aimed*, the way a skilled archer aims a thing that already burns.

Somewhere across the square, another warden shouted that they'd found charms at the tallow-booth, and the young man's attention snapped toward it like a compass needle. He dropped the luck-knot back onto the cloth.

"Move along," he said, to both of them, and hurried off toward the easier catch.

Astrid did not remember packing her basket. She remembered Bess's grip on her arm, iron under the thin skin, steering her through the frozen crowd, past the gray cloaks, out of Gullmarket and down Tanner's Lane and into the shadow of the aqueduct arch where the beggars slept and no one official ever looked.

Only there did the old woman stop. Only there did she let go.

And only there did Astrid see that Bess was shaking—not with fear, though there was fear in her face, but with something else. Her hand had gone to her chest. Her breathing had gone thin and whistling.

"Bess—"

"Don't fuss." The old woman waved her off, gasping a little, and lowered herself onto the beggars' bench with the dignity of a queen taking a throne. "Heart's old. It complains. It's been complaining since the coronation and it'll complain at my funeral." She patted the bench beside her. Astrid sat.

For a while neither of them spoke. Under the arch, the city sounded far away: cart wheels, a distant bell, the ordinary noise of a city pretending nothing had happened.

"Why?" Astrid asked finally. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "You could have been arrested. You could have—" She gestured at Bess's chest, at the whistling breath. "You saw the wardens and you walked *toward* me."

Bess looked at her for a long moment. Up close, in the shadow of the arch, her eyes were pale as ice and just as clear.

"I've bought forty of your charms, girl. Forty, over three winters. Luck-knots, fever-charms, a 'ward against ill neighbors' that I gave to my horrible son-in-law, gods rest him." A dry chuckle, which cost her; she pressed her hand flat to her sternum and waited it out. "And in three years of selling me your gran's pretty string, do you know what I've learned?"

"That they don't work?"

"That they *don't work*," Bess agreed, "and that you hate it. Every time. You flinch when you take the coin. You look at the knots like they've disappointed you personally. Nobody sells worthless goods with that much grief unless the worthlessness is a lie they're telling to stay alive." She reached over and, before Astrid could pull away, tapped one finger—once, lightly—against Astrid's left forearm. Against the sleeve. Against the hidden place.

Astrid went absolutely still.

"I was a girl when the Guild still sang," Bess said softly. "My mother took me to a lantern-blessing on the Winterfair, before you were born, before the war, before all of it. I remember the light coming off those knots like honey. I remember thinking the world couldn't possibly run out of that kind of kindness." She sighed, long and slow. "They were wrong to hang them. All of them. Even the guilty ones—they were wrong in the *manner* of it, and the city's been colder ever since, and I don't mean the weather. Though now that you mention it, the weather too."

"Bess—"

"Hush. I'm not asking what you are. I'm telling you what I am: an old woman who's decided how she means to spend what's left of her money and her courage, and today I spent both on you, and I'd do it again tomorrow, so spare me the speeches." She struggled upright, waving off Astrid's help, and settled her shawl. "Go home. Eat something that isn't bread. And Astrid—" she pronounced the name carefully, having clearly extracted it from some earlier transaction and hoarded it since, "—whatever's coming, whatever the air's been trying to say since the equinox—don't you dare be careless with yourself. The city's short on kindness as it is."

She shuffled off down the lane, small and crooked and utterly unbowed, and Astrid sat alone under the aqueduct arch with her heart doing something complicated and painful in her chest.

*Don't be careless with yourself.* When had anyone last said that to her? When had anyone last *seen* her clearly enough to say it?

She should move quarters. She should leave Corvane entirely—go south, go anywhere, start over in a town where nobody knew her face. That was the sensible thing. That had been the sensible thing for three years running, and every year she stayed, and every year she told herself it was because moving was expensive, and every year the lie got thinner.

She stayed because of Maren's bread. Because of Bess's forty worthless charms. Because some stubborn weed of belonging had rooted in the cracks of her, and pulling it out would hurt more than staying ever could.

Fear was a habit. But apparently, so was hope.

Astrid packed her basket, pulled her sleeves down over the ache in her arm, and headed for home through streets that were already going dark at half past three—as winter tightened its grip on a city that had forgotten, along with everything else, how to sing.

She did not see the blood on her own doorstep until she was nearly upon it.

She did not hear the man dying in her cellar until he whispered her guild name through the floorboards—a name that had not been spoken aloud in six years, spoken now in a stranger's ruined voice, like a key turning in a lock she'd buried.

But that, as the saying goes, is the next chapter's grief.
