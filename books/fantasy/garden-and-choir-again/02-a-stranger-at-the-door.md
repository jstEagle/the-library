## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with living in a cellar, Astrid had long ago decided, was that everything above you sounded like it was coming for you.

A cart rumbling over cobbles: soldiers. A shutter banging in the wind: the Inquisition kicking down doors. The neighbor's cat, Marmalade, conducting his nightly patrol across her ceiling of floorboards: the entire Corvane guard, armed and coordinated, which even Marmalade could not manage.

She had learned to sort these sounds the way other people learned to sort coins — by weight, by ring, by the particular music they made against the palm. Tonight, three days after the raid on Tallowmarket, every sound was suspect. She sat at her workbench with a luck-knot half-finished between her fingers and did not tie it. Her hands knew the pattern so well they could complete it blind, but tonight she made them wait.

Above her, footsteps.

Not Marmalade's. Marmalade traveled light and without apology. These steps were heavy, uneven, dragging on the left side, and they stopped directly over the trapdoor to her workshop.

Astrid put out her lamp.

For a long moment there was only silence, the kind that has weight, the kind you can lean against. Then something struck the trapdoor — not a knock, exactly. More like a body leaning its whole weight against wood and losing the argument.

"Hello?" said a voice through the boards. It was a man's voice, cracked and wet, the voice of someone who had been shouting or bleeding or both. "Is this — the sign said — there was a lantern sign—"

There was no lantern sign. There had been no lantern signs since the Guild fell, since the Lanternwrights were hanged or scattered or renamed themselves things like *honest chandler* and *itinerant tinker*. Whoever was up there was either mad, lost, or hunting her.

All three possibilities argued for staying very quiet in the dark.

But the voice came again, weaker now, and beneath it Astrid heard the sound she had been trained — three years ago, in another life, by people who were mostly dead — never to ignore. Not crying. Not begging. Just breathing, wrong, the wet rattle of air going where air should not go.

"Oh, hang it," she whispered to herself, because there was no one else to say it to. "Hang it all."

She lit the lamp again, climbed the ladder, and unbolted the trapdoor.

* * *

He fell into her arms rather than through her door, which saved them both a certain awkwardness. He was tall, or had been designed to be; pain had folded him down to something smaller. His coat had been fine once, grey wool with silver thread at the cuffs, and was now mostly blood, some of it his. She got him down the ladder in stages, apologizing under her breath each time his boots knocked the rungs, as though the ladder might complain to someone.

Her cellar workshop was small and warm and smelled of beeswax, dried feverfew, and the tallow candles she sold openly at market while selling rather less open things beside them. Shelves lined the stone walls: jars of knotwork charms, bundles of herbs, spools of colored thread sorted by intention — red for courage, blue for calm, green for luck, though Astrid privately thought all four colors worked about equally well, which was to say, mostly on the person doing the tying.

She laid the stranger on the cot in the corner, the one she kept for nights when the fear got too loud to sleep through. Up close he was younger than his voice had suggested — thirty, perhaps, with a courier's lean build and a face that had recently been introduced to a fist, or several fists taking turns.

"You're bleeding," she told him, uselessly, and went for her kit.

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry about your floor."

"It's a dirt floor."

"Then I'm sorry about your dirt."

Despite everything — despite the danger coiled tight in her chest, despite the memory of Tallowmarket, of old Marta being dragged away while the crowd studied its shoes — Astrid felt the corner of her mouth move. It had been a long time since anything down here had made her want to smile. She hadn't realized how much she'd missed the wanting.

She cut away his shirt and found the wound: a slash across his ribs, deep but clean, already bound with a field dressing that had soaked through twice. Someone had tried to keep him alive on the road. Someone had failed, or nearly.

"This needs more than a bandage," she said. "This needs—" She stopped.

They looked at each other. He knew what she'd been about to say. She knew he knew. The word hung between them like a spark over dry straw: *magic.*

In Corvane, magic was a hanging offense. Not a fine, not a flogging. A hanging. The Lanternwright Guild had been outlawed six years ago; its last master had died on the gallows at Wintersmeet, and its last apprentice had learned to keep her hands still and her sleeves long and her gift folded up small inside her like a letter she would never send.

"If you're going to do it," the man said quietly, "do it. I didn't come all this way to die of politeness."

"How do you know I can?"

"Your hands," he said. "You've got a guild mark under that sleeve on your left arm. You've been rubbing it since you opened the hatch. We all did that. Every one of us who survived."

Astrid went cold from the scalp down. Slowly, deliberately, she pulled her sleeve straight and stepped back from the cot.

"Who are you?" she asked. "Who sent you? Was it Veyra? Because if the Inquisitor thinks she can—"

"No." He caught her wrist — gently, for a dying man, but firmly enough that she understood he had strength left for exactly one thing, and this was it. "Listen to me. My name is Dannel Ferro. I carried dispatches for the Magistrate's court until four days ago. And four days ago I was given a package to deliver north, and I opened it instead, because I am a curious fool, and what I saw in it changed what side I'm willing to die on."

"Let go of my wrist."

"In a moment." His eyes were bright with fever and something fiercer underneath. "The Hollow Choir, apprentice Halloran. Do you remember the name?"

She should have said no. She should have laughed, called him mad, reached for the poker. Every survival instinct she had honed over three years of careful, cramped, quiet living screamed at her to say *no*.

Instead she heard herself ask, "How do you know my name?"

"The map knows it." He released her wrist and fumbled inside his coat, wincing, and drew out a scroll wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax — dark red wax, stamped with a sigil she had not seen since she was nine years old: a lantern with seven flames.

Her mouth went dry. That was the seal of the First Choir. That was the seal from the frontispiece of the guild primer Master Corren had burned in front of her, telling her it was kinder this way, telling her to forget.

"He said it was destroyed," she whispered. "Corren said all the seals were destroyed."

"Master Corren," said Dannel Ferro, "has said a great many things."

* * *

She healed him because she could not help herself, and hated herself a little for how much it helped *her*.

It was a small working, as such things went — guild-craft, hearth-magic, the humble stitching-together that Lanternwright apprentices learned before they learned anything grander. She warmed her palms at the lamp, hummed the low settling note that Corren had taught her first, and pressed her hands to the wound, and let the warmth go where it was needed. The flesh knit. The fever broke like a cloud passing off the moon. Dannel sighed the sigh of a man setting down a weight he had forgotten he was carrying.

"That's better than the court physicians," he murmured. "And considerably cheaper."

"Guild rates," she said. "One secret per healing. You've paid in advance."

His laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended she saw the truth she had been refusing to see since she laid him on the cot. The wound had been dressed too late. Whatever poison or rot rode in it had already done its traveling. Her craft could close a cut; it could not chase what the cut had invited in.

"How long?" he asked, reading her face the way couriers read weather.

"I don't know."

"Astrid." He used her name plainly, familiarly, the way no one had spoken it in years — everyone in Corvane who knew it called her nothing at all. "I have maybe an hour. Maybe two. Don't waste them being kind to me. Be kind to yourself instead. Open the map."

She unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that would not quite stay steady. Inside was a scroll of heavy paper, and the moment lamplight touched it, Astrid forgot how to breathe.

The map was drawn in ink that moved.

Rivers slid like slow silver worms. Mountains shrugged and settled. And across the whole face of it drifted washes of color — green fading to grey, grey bleeding to black — spreading outward from the north like frost across a windowpane. She watched a forest on the map's upper edge turn from painted green to the brown of dead leaves, watched snow fall on it in July, watched the ink itself seem to grieve.

"It's alive," she said. "This is living ink. Nobody's made living ink since—"

"Since the Choir fell silent," Dannel said. "Look at the marks. Seven of them."

Seven small lantern-sigils were scattered across the map like dropped coins. One glowed faintly in the drowned country east of the Ashfield fens. Another, further south, near the mining towns. A third sat almost on top of—

Astrid's finger stopped. The third mark sat squarely within the walls of Corvane itself, in the Hill district, where the magistrates kept their whitewashed houses and their respectable gardens.

"That one's wrong," she said. "That's the Hill. Nothing sings up there except money."

"Maybe," said Dannel. "Or maybe the map remembers something the city wants forgotten." He shifted, and the effort cost him; she saw the pain climb his face like water rising. "The Choir kept the covenant, Astrid. Between the human realm and the Faeheart. Their song was the hinge. When the purges scattered them, the song stopped — and the seasons started failing, and everyone blamed weather, or God, or the Fae, anyone but the men who broke the hinge. The seasons are dying because the Choir is scattered. And the world—" another cough, longer, wetter, "—the world is running out of time to be fixed."

"Why me?" she asked. It came out smaller than she intended. "I was an apprentice for two years before the fall. I tied knots. I sang warming-songs over bread ovens. I'm nobody."

"You're the last," he said simply. "The last Lanternwright. The map was made to find you. It's been looking for three years — that's why the ink is tired, if ink can be tired. It settled on you the night you burned your last true charm and kept the false ones to sell. Isn't that right? You couldn't bring yourself to sell the real ones."

She stared at him. Then, slowly, she crossed to the shelf behind her workbench and took down the jar she never touched, the one at the back, behind the tallow candles. Inside lay a dozen charms of genuine craft — knots tied with intent, songs sung into thread — that she had never once offered for sale. They were useless in hiding. They were everything.

"They were all I had left of them," she said. "Of the guild. Of—" Of Corren, she did not say. Of the man who had taught her every note she knew and then stood in the magistrate's square and voted, with the other respected citizens, to make everything he'd taught her a crime.

Dannel's hand found hers and pressed the scroll into it, folding her fingers closed around the living paper. It was warm. It pulsed faintly, like something sleeping.

"The Hollow Choir," he said. "Find them. Wake them. Take the song back to the Faeheart before the last season goes out." His grip tightened. "Promise me you'll—"

And then he stopped, because death arrived, quietly, the way it does — not a dramatic collapse but a simple absence, as though the man had stepped out of the room to attend to something elsewhere and would not be back.

Astrid sat with him for a while anyway. She closed his eyes. She covered him with the good blanket, the one without holes, because whatever else he'd been, he had bled out on her dirt floor carrying a message meant for her, and messages deserved courtesy.

It was only afterward, washing her hands at the basin, that the last of his words came back to her — the words she had been too shocked to hear properly, the ones that had slipped past in the tide of everything else.

He had died whispering. She leaned close at the end, the way you do, to catch it. And what he had whispered, over and over, soft as a lullaby, was not *find them*, not *save the world*, not any of the grand dying declarations the ballads promised.

He had whispered her guild name. Her full guild name, the secret one, the one given to her at her initiation by candlelight, the one known only to masters of the Lanternwrights and to the one initiate who bore it.

*Lantern-of-the-Low-Places. Lantern-of-the-Low-Places.*

No one alive should have known it. No one alive *could* have known it — unless the map had told him, unless the map had been carrying her name the way a lantern carries flame, waiting all these years for a wall to hang itself on.

Astrid looked down at the scroll in her lap. The living ink stirred at her touch, and far to the north, past the blackening forests and the frozen rivers, a shape began to surface in the paper — a mountain, hollow at its crown, drawn in lines that shimmered like held breath.

And beside it, in handwriting that faded in and out like a voice remembering itself, two words appeared:

*Come singing.*

Astrid Halloran sat alone in her cellar with a dead man, a living map, and a choice that had been waiting for her for three years without her knowing it.

She was terrified. She noticed, distantly, that she was also — for the first time since the gallows at Wintersmeet — awake.
