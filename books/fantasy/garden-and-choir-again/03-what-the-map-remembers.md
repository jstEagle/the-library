## 3. What the Map Remembers

The courier's blood had dried to a brown crust on the wax seal, and Astrid spent a full quarter hour deciding whether that was worse than breaking the seal itself.

She sat at her workbench with the map lying flat between two candle stubs, her hands hovering over it like a woman deciding whether to pet a strange dog. The cellar workshop was cold enough that her breath showed. It had been showing for three weeks now, which was wrong—Corvane winters came late and arrived politely, like merchants, not like this. Not like an army.

"Right," she said aloud, because the silence had gotten opinionated. "You're a map. Maps don't bite."

Maps didn't bite. But she'd once sold a charm to a man whose map *had* bitten him—a cartographer's joke gone sour, ink with teeth—and so she took up her bone-handled knife and broke the seal at arm's length, eyes half shut, ready to fling herself backward off the stool if anything hissed.

Nothing hissed. The wax cracked. The map unrolled with a sound like a sigh.

It was drawn on something that wasn't quite vellum and wasn't quite bark, pale as birchwood, soft-edged. And the ink—

Astrid leaned closer. Then closer still, until her nose nearly touched it, which any sensible person would have warned her against.

The ink was moving.

Not wriggling, not crawling—the way cheap enchanted inks moved, all show and no substance, bought by merchants who wanted their ledgers to impress. This was slower. Deeper. Rivers of dark green slid along lines that might have been roads or might have been veins. A mountain range near the top of the map breathed, very faintly, its peaks rising and falling like the flanks of a sleeping animal. And across the whole southern portion—the whole southern third of the map, where Corvane sat rendered as a small gray smudge of stubbornness—the color was draining. Slowly. Like blood leaving a face.

"Oh," said Astrid quietly. "Oh, you're not a map. You're a *diary*."

She knew living ink. Every apprentice of the Lanternwright Guild had ground it once, under Master Corren's supervision, from oak-gall and moonwater and one drop of sap from a tree that only grew where two seasons shook hands. Living ink remembered what it was shown. It kept drawing long after the artist set down the pen.

Which meant someone had drawn this map while watching the land fail. Or—or the ink had been given the land itself to look at, and had been faithfully reporting ever since.

She fetched her loupe, the good one with the cracked rim she'd never been able to replace, and bent over the map's edges. There were words there, tiny as lice, worked into the border flourishes. She read them twice before they made sense.

*Seven voices scattered. Seven hearths kept. Gather what remains.*

Below that, smaller, shakier: *If you are reading this, the courier reached you. Good. He was stubborn about it. — I.*

"I," said Astrid. "Very helpful. Thank you, mysterious I." Her voice wobbled on the last word, and she pretended it hadn't.

Because here was the thing she hadn't let herself think since the stranger died on her floor three days ago, since she'd dragged his body out through the coal chute at four in the morning and paid a grave-digger's widow six coppers to look the other way: he had known her guild name. Not *Astrid Halloran*, charm-seller, harmless, forgettable. He had whispered *Lanternwright* into the dark of her cellar with his last breath, the way a man says *home*.

Nobody knew that name. She had made very sure nobody knew that name.

And now somebody's ink did.

* * *

She spent the evening tracing the seven marks.

They were small—not X's, nothing so crude—but little circles drawn with a care that suggested each one had cost the mapmaker something. A drowned chapel in the eastern fens, its tower drawn tilted, half-swallowed by water that shimmered even in ink. A mining town wedged under a mountain's shoulder, marked with a drum symbol she recognized from guild notation: *percussion rests here*. A fen-crossing. A high pass. Two more she couldn't parse without the old cipher-books she'd burned years ago, gods forgive her, burned them herself to stay safe.

And the seventh mark sat almost in the center of the map, in a place with no town, no road, no name—just a spiral, drawn over and over, tight as a snail shell, until the ink there was thick and dark and seemed, when she wasn't looking directly at it, to be turning.

"The Hollow Choir," she murmured. Seven resting places. Seven singers, or what remained of them, scattered like seeds after a threshing.

She had been nine years old when the purges came. She remembered it in pieces, the way children remember disasters: the smell of smoke that turned out to be the guildhall library; Master Corren's hand gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise as he walked her out the back gate; the proclamation nailed to every door in Corvane within the month, MAGIC IS DEATH, in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse. She remembered thinking the letters looked frightened themselves.

She had been one of perhaps a dozen apprentices who survived, scattered or smuggled or simply overlooked. She'd stopped counting the others years ago. Counting was how you got caught—counting meant hoping, hoping meant searching, searching meant questions, and questions in Corvane were a slow way of hanging yourself.

But the Choir. The Choir was older than the guild, older than the city. Even the guild had treated it as legend, mostly—singers who walked between the human realm and the Faeheart, carrying the covenant-song that kept the wild magic on its own side of the fence and the human world turning properly on its own side of the year. Planting season followed harvest. Winter agreed, eventually, to end. That was the covenant's whole promise, and it had been so reliable that people forgot it was a promise at all, the way you forget the floor is holding you up.

Until the floor started creaking.

Three failed harvests. Frost in midsummer two years past, killing the vineyards overnight. Children in the river districts who'd stopped dreaming entirely—their mothers said it like a confession, ashamed, as though dreamlessness were a disease of bad housekeeping. And this winter arriving early and arriving *angry*, with birds falling dead from the sky and the harbor freezing in sheets thick enough to walk on, which had happened exactly never in the memory of anyone's grandmother.

Astrid had blamed the weather. Weather was safe to blame. Weather couldn't be hanged.

She looked down at the map, at the green draining out of the south like hope leaving a sickroom, and admitted, in the privacy of her own skull, that she had known. Some quiet cellar-room of her heart had known for a year and had been keeping its head down just like the rest of her.

"You could burn it," she told the map conversationally. "That's option one. Burn the map, sweep up the ashes, go sell fever-charms until the Inquisition finds me anyway, because a dying man shouted my secret name in my own cellar and the widow with the shovel heard every word."

Option one had a lot going for it. Longevity, chiefly.

"Option two," she said, and stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and she'd built three comfortable years on the principle that things stayed unreal as long as they went unspoken.

The candle guttered. Outside, above the grate that passed for her window, she heard the night watch calling the hour—two bells past midnight—and beneath it, faintly, the sound of ice settling on the rooftops like a large animal rearranging itself.

*Gather what remains.*

* * *

She didn't sleep. Instead she did the accounting, because Astrid Halloran had learned early that courage was mostly bookkeeping done honestly.

Her stock: forty-one luck-knots, braided from ordinary red thread but knotted with a guild pattern that genuinely did nudge fortune, gently, the way you'd nudge a sleepy ox. Nine fever-charms. Three salt-wards, nearly spent. One rain-blessing she'd never had the heart to sell because it had been the first working she'd ever done alone, and it smelled, faintly and forever, of Master Corren's workshop—of tallow and cedar shavings and the tea he drank too hot.

She laid everything out on the bench in rows, the way she'd inventory it for market, and stood looking at it.

"This is a stupid amount of magic," she observed. "This is a *laughable* amount of magic. Seven singers of the Hollow Choir, and I'm bringing forty-one lucky strings."

But it wasn't nothing. That was the terrible, seductive truth of it. The knots would nudge fortune. The fever-charms would keep someone alive through a bad night. Small magics, harmless magics, the kind she'd spent three years convincing herself were all she had left—all she deserved, maybe, after surviving when the guild hadn't.

She picked up the rain-blessing. It was a scrap of linen embroidered with a cloud that had come out looking more like a sheep, because she'd been eleven and bad at embroidery, and Corren had hung it in the workshop window anyway and told her it brought the finest drizzle he'd ever seen. He'd laughed. She could still hear exactly how he laughed.

She put it down again quickly, the way you put down a hot pan.

"Don't," she advised herself sternly. "Don't do that. If we're doing this, we're doing it with dry eyes and a packed bag."

We. Apparently it was *we* now. Apparently some part of her had decided while she wasn't voting.

She thought about the old woman from the market raid—Mother Ostrey, who sold turnips and lies in equal measure and had stepped in front of a guard's cudgel for a girl she'd known for two years of small purchases and smaller talk. The widow had taken the blow on her shoulder rather than let them search Astrid's stall, and had winked at her afterward through a watering eye, and had charged her double for turnips the next week, which Astrid had respected enormously.

If Mother Ostrey could spend her bones for a stranger's secrets, what exactly was Astrid saving hers for?

For nothing. For a warm cellar and a low profile and a life shrunk small enough to hide inside. She had mistaken survival for a plan. It wasn't a plan. It was a waiting room, and the world outside the door was coming apart at the seams, and somewhere in the fens a woman who used to sing the seasons into place had gone mute with grief, and somewhere under a mountain a drummer was drinking himself to death rather than touch his instrument.

The map shifted under her eyes. She watched the green retreat another hair's breadth from the southern coast. It was slow, the failing—glacial, almost, if glaciers still behaved—but it was constant, and constant loss is the most frightening kind, because it lets you pretend, right up until there's nothing left to pretend with.

"All right," Astrid said. "All right. Fine. *Fine.*"

She said it four times, which was three times more than conviction required and exactly the number denial needed.

* * *

Burning the charms took longer than she expected, and hurt more.

Not the luck-knots—those went quickly, thread catching flame with a cheerful little whump, forty-one small futures unwinding into smoke. She felt those go the way you feel a sneeze: noticeable, brief. The fever-charms were harder. Each one held a night's careful work and the memory of whoever she'd imagined clutching it—she always imagined someone, a fisherman's wife, a colicky infant, an old man in a drafty attic—and burning them felt, absurdly, like letting all those imaginary people die.

"That's ridiculous," she told herself, feeding the ninth one to the candle. "They were never real. You cannot murder hypothetical invalids."

But her throat was tight, so the point stood disputed.

The salt-wards crumbled to nothing; salt doesn't burn so much as surrender. And then only the rain-blessing remained, sitting on the bench with its sheep-cloud, smelling of cedar and too-hot tea and a life that had ended nine years ago in a courtyard full of smoke.

Astrid held it over the flame for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic of it, done honestly, as promised: if she carried this one scrap of linen across half a kingdom, she would carry him too. Master Corren, dead these nine years—or vanished, or fled, or whatever men became who helped write proclamations that killed their own students. Carrying him would be warm. Carrying him would also be a stone in her pack, and the road ahead, according to a map drawn in dying light, ran through fens and mountains and at least one place where the ink itself was afraid to go.

You can't bring the old world with you, her mother used to say—you'll only arrive tired. Her mother had died believing the guild would protect them both. The guild had not protected anyone.

"Sorry, Master," Astrid said, and touched the linen to the flame.

It burned fast and bright and blue at the edges, the way well-made things do, and for one heartbeat the firelight threw a shape on the cellar wall that looked so much like a broad-shouldered man with his sleeves rolled up that she laughed out loud, one startled note, half sob.

Then it was ash, and the cellar was just a cellar, cold and small and finally, completely empty of the past.

* * *

Packing took twenty minutes, which told her something about the size of the life she'd built. One blanket. One knife, plus the bone-handled one. Flint and steel. Dried apples, hard cheese, the heel of a loaf gone friendly with frost. Six coppers and a silver button she'd been saving against a rainy day, on the theory that every day lately was one. The map, rolled tight and wrapped in oilcloth. The loupe. And, after a moment's argument with herself that she lost, one single luck-knot—the forty-second, the one she'd braided for herself years ago and never worn, because wearing your own luck felt like cheating.

She knotted it around her wrist now, under her sleeve, next to the faded guild mark she'd spent three years hiding.

At the top of the stairs she paused with her hand on the latch and looked back at the workshop one last time. The bench. The candle stubs. The little scatter of ash where forty-two small magics had lived and died. Three years of safety, swept into a corner.

"Goodbye," she said, and meant the room, and the city above it, and the girl who'd hidden in both.

Then she opened the door onto the frozen dark, and the cold came in like a question, and Astrid Halloran—last apprentice of the Lanternwright Guild, charm-seller, coward, and apparently now a courier of her own—stepped through it, eastward, toward the fens and the first of the seven.

Behind her, on the bench, in the ash of the burned charms, something glowed briefly green—the living ink, remembering her—and then, patient as winter, went dark.
