## 4. The Inquisitor's Kindness

Inquisitor Veyra believed that cruelty was a confession of incompetence.

She had said so once, years ago, to a junior officer who had been overfond of the thumbscrews, and she had watched his face do the arithmetic: *she is not joking.* He had requested a transfer to the coastal garrisons by spring. She had signed the papers herself, with a small note wishing him fair winds. Kindness cost nothing and confused people wonderfully.

So it was that on the morning after the market raid, she sat in the back room of the Lamplighter's Rest — a tavern that smelled of spilled ale and wet wool — across from a woman who sold eels, and asked her questions in the manner of someone asking after a sick relative.

"Take your time," Veyra said. "There is no hurry. The tea is terrible either way."

The eel-seller, whose name was Doss and whose hands were permanently silvered with fish-scale, had been crying on and off since dawn. Not from fear, exactly. From the particular grief of a person who has seen something happen and does not know whether telling the truth will make her a good citizen or a bad neighbor.

"You're not in trouble, Mistress Doss," Veyra continued. "You witnessed a raid. You are a witness. Witnesses are the most protected class of person in Corvane this week. I have it in writing." She produced a folded document and set it on the table between them like a plate of biscuits. "Signed. Stamped. The stamp alone took me an hour to obtain; I would hate for it to go to waste."

Doss looked at the stamp. It was, indeed, very official-looking. Something in her shoulders came down half an inch.

"It was just a girl," Doss said finally. "Selling luck-knots. Red thread, little braided things. My granddaughter bought one last autumn for two coppers and hasn't dropped a single dish since, and Marta drops dishes *constantly*, so either the charm works or my granddaughter simply decided to be careful, and honestly I don't care which."

"Neither do I," said Veyra truthfully. "Tell me about the girl."

"Tall-ish. Thin. Hood up, but you could see she had that look — you know, the one where they watch the crowd sideways, counting exits. Kept her left sleeve pulled down past her wrist even when she was handing things over. Cold day, but everyone else had their hands out of their sleeves. Chilblains don't care about secrets." Doss wiped her nose with the heel of her hand. "She gave old Nan Tarrow a fever-charm for free. Nan's boy's been coughing all winter. And then your soldiers came through breaking stalls, and Nan — Nan stepped in front of her. Little old stick of a woman, stepped right in front of a soldier with a pike, and told him the girl was her niece."

Veyra waited. Patience was a tool like any other, and hers was well-oiled.

"They took Nan instead," Doss whispered. "The girl got away. Ran toward Tanner's Row. And now everyone's saying if they talk, we'll all be next, and Nan's in a cell somewhere, and her boy's still coughing, and—" She stopped, appalled at herself, hands flying to her mouth.

"And you have just told me everything anyway," Veyra said gently. "Because you are honest, and because you want Nan Tarrow home, and both of those things are to your credit. Here is what happens now." She counted on her fingers, unhurried. "Nan goes home today. Before noon. Her grandson receives a physician's visit, paid from the Inquisition's discretionary fund, which exists precisely so that I can spend money without explaining it to anyone, a privilege I guard jealously. And you receive no further visits from me unless I have very good news, which I intend to have."

Doss stared at her. "Why?"

It was the question Veyra liked best and answered least often, because the true answer made people uncomfortable. *Because I think we are doing this wrong,* was not a sentence an Inquisitor got to say.

"Because it works better," she said instead, which was also true, and stood, and shook the eel-seller's fish-scaled hand as though it were the hand of a duchess.

* * *

By noon, Nan Tarrow was home, complaining loudly about the quality of prison porridge to anyone within earshot, and her grandson had been examined by a physician who prescribed honey, rest, and a suspiciously generous payment "for the medicine." By one o'clock, Veyra had statements from eleven witnesses, and by three she had done what she always did with statements, which was lay them out on the floor of her office in a great paper mosaic and lie down among them like a woman reading stars.

Her office occupied the top floor of the Inquisition annex on Candler Street. It was a tidy room, mostly, except for the floor-mosaics and except for the cabinet.

The cabinet stood against the north wall, plain oak, locked, and inside it lived the thing Veyra did not discuss with colleagues, superiors, or her own reflection on difficult evenings.

Confiscated charms. Forty-one of them.

Regulations were unambiguous: seized magical objects were to be catalogued and destroyed by burning within seven days, in public, as instruction. Veyra catalogued them beautifully. Her ledgers were famous. The destructions she scheduled, rescheduled, and then performed with great ceremony using — she had verified this carefully — a brazier positioned directly beneath the office's single window, in a room with a truly remarkable draft.

What went into the fire were replicas. She had learned to carve soapstone and twist wire and braid red thread from the confiscated objects themselves, studying each one the way a scholar studies a manuscript before burning the library. The originals lived in the cabinet, wrapped in linen, labeled in her neat hand.

She did not fully understand why. That was the honest truth of it, and Veyra was honest with herself the way she was honest with witnesses: completely, and at inconvenient moments.

She unlocked the cabinet now and took out the newest acquisition, recovered from a stall in the market before the raid scattered everything: a luck-knot, red thread, two coppers' worth of craft and perhaps a copper's worth of actual magic, if that. It was clumsy work. The knotting was sound but the binding-verse stitched into the lining was misspelled — *ward* rendered with a doubled letter, the way a self-taught person spells a word learned by ear.

Someone had taught this girl by speaking, not writing. Someone had sat with her, probably in winter, probably by a bad lamp, and said the words aloud until she had them.

Veyra turned the knot over in her fingers. It hummed faintly against her palm, the way a struck bell hums after the sound seems gone. Harmless. All forty-one of them were harmless — fever-charms and luck-knots and a birthing-blessing and one small wooden bird that, when wound, turned its head toward whoever in the room was saddest, which Veyra had tested on three separate occasions and found infallible and deeply unsettling.

*Harmless,* she thought. *Forty-one arrests. Three hangings that I know of, in the early years, before policy softened to transportation. For this.*

She put the luck-knot back, wrapped in its linen, and did not let herself open the drawer with the wooden bird.

* * *

The child came to her at four o'clock, escorted by a harried sergeant and clutching a meat pie that Veyra had instructed the sergeant to purchase as a condition of cooperation. The child was a girl of about eight named Pip, gap-toothed, ferociously self-possessed, who looked around the Inquisitor's office with frank appraisal and said, "You've got a lot of papers on the floor."

"I do," Veyra agreed. "Would you like to help me with them? It's called work and it's terrible."

Pip considered this. "My da says you burn witches."

"My da would be wrong about several things, then," Veyra said. "We don't burn witches. Witches aren't real. We do arrest people who use magic, which is against the law, and I enforce the law, which is my job, and I am trying to find out whether I'm any good at it. Sit down. Eat your pie. Tell me about Saturday."

Pip sat. Pip ate. Pip talked.

She had been at the market with her mother, helping sell buttons, and she had watched the charm-seller for most of the afternoon because the charm-seller was interesting. "She did a trick," Pip reported, with crumbs flying. "With the thread. She'd hold it behind her back and say something quiet and when she brought her hand out the knot was already tied, and she never once looked at her hands while she did it. Marta can't even tie her boots without looking."

"What did she look at?"

"People." Pip shrugged, elaborate with eight-year-old wisdom. "That's how you sell stuff. You gotta look at people. But she looked at 'em funny. Like — like when our cat watches the door. Like she knew something was coming before it came."

Veyra felt the small cold click of a lock turning somewhere behind her ribs. "And when the soldiers came?"

"She knew first." Pip licked grease off her thumb. "Before the shouting even started, she was already putting her stuff away. Calm. Like she'd practiced. And then Nan got in the way, and the girl looked — " Pip frowned, searching. "Not scared of the soldiers. Scared *for* Nan. Like it was her fault, only worse than that. Like she'd known it would happen someday and hoped it wouldn't be Nan."

"Did you see her face? Under the hood?"

"Bits." Pip held up her hands to frame an invisible portrait. "Pale. Dark hair coming loose, kind of reddish in the light. Young. And she had a mark — right there—" Pip tapped the inside of her own wrist, "—when she reached up to fix her hood. A little circle with lines coming off it. Like a lantern. Or a sun. But lantern-ish."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones and faded away.

"Lanternwright," Veyra said, mostly to herself.

"A what?"

"A guild," Veyra said. "An old guild. Older than the law that broke it." She looked at the child, at the pie, at the afternoon light going long and gold across her paper mosaic on the floor. "Pip. You have been extremely helpful. You are going to forget this conversation entirely, partly because I am going to ask you nicely and partly because children are wonderful at forgetting things, and I am relying on that talent. Your mother will be paid for the lost selling-hours. There will be another pie."

"There'd better be," said Pip, with the gravity of state.

* * *

That evening Veyra did something she almost never did: she closed her office door, sat down at her desk, and wrote a letter she did not send.

*To the reader of this letter, being myself,* it began, because she had learned long ago that arguments with herself went better in writing.

*I identified a Lanternwright today. The last one, as far as anyone knows — the guild was nine hundred strong once, and now there is a girl in a hood selling two-copper luck-knots and giving fever-charms away free to grandmothers. Every witness description agrees on one point above all others: she never harmed anyone. Eleven statements. Eleven accounts of small kindnesses. One account of an old woman willing to face a pike for her.*

*The law says magic is the rot in the realm. I have spent twelve years enforcing that law, and I have believed it — I still believe it — because I have seen what unchecked power does to the powerless, and guilds were power, and power rots. This is the argument. It is a good argument. I have made it to councils and to crowds and to myself at three in the morning.*

*But I keep forty-one charms in a cabinet because I cannot burn them, and tonight I am asking myself why, and the answer keeps coming out wrong-shaped. It is not sentiment. I have tested it against sentiment and it survives. It is this: every one of those objects was made to help someone poorer than its maker. Show me the rot in that. Show me where the rot is supposed to be, because I have looked, and what I found instead was a girl who spelled her binding-verses the way you spell words you've only ever heard spoken in love.*

*Tomorrow I request warrants for Tanner's Row. I will find her. That is my job and I am, whatever else is true, good at my job.*

*But I find I want to ask her a question before anything else, and the question is not "where are your accomplices."*

*The question is: who taught you?*

She read it twice. Then she folded it, sealed it, wrote *Do not open until this matter is resolved* on the outside in her precise clerk's hand, and locked it in the bottom drawer of the desk, beside a small wooden bird that turned its head, very slightly, toward her.

Veyra blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the city settle — carts, dogs, a drunk singing three streets over with more conviction than accuracy.

Tanner's Row, tomorrow. A girl who watched crowds the way cats watch doors.

She did not yet know the girl's name. But names, Veyra had found, were like drafts under doors: sooner or later, everything cold came looking for warmth, and all you had to do was be patient, and courteous, and standing in the right room when it arrived.

She gathered her coat, checked the cabinet lock twice out of habit, and went home through the falling snow — pausing only at the corner of Candler Street, where a market-stall's abandoned red thread had snagged on a railing and caught the lamplight, bright as a small stubborn fire that refused, quite rudely, to go out.
