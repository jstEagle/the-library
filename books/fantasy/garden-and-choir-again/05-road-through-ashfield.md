## 5. Road Through Ashfield

The road north out of Corvane was called the Pilgrim's Way, though Astrid had never met a pilgrim on it. She'd only ever known it as the route the charcoal carts took, groaning south toward the city with their loads of blackened oak. Now she walked it alone in the opposite direction, with a pack on her back, a dead man's map against her ribs, and three years of careful invisibility peeling off her like old paint.

It should have felt like freedom. Mostly it felt like walking out of doors without checking whether the shutters were latched.

She had burned her charms two nights ago—all but one fever-knot, which she'd kept for reasons she refused to examine too closely—and the absence of them sat strangely in her hands. For three years her fingers had been never idle: knotting, braiding, whispering small harmless intentions into twine and thread. Luck-knots. Warming-charms. The little braided circles that eased a colicky baby when nothing else would. Work that kept her hands honest and her heart quiet, because as long as she was making something, she wasn't remembering everything.

Now her hands were empty, and they itched.

The first village was called Bracken Hollow, and Astrid knew something was wrong with it before she reached its edge, because there were no crows.

Crows followed harvests the way beggars followed funerals. A healthy autumn field should have been loud with them, strutting and squabbling over spilled grain. But the fields around Bracken Hollow stood silent under a sky the color of dishwater, and the crops—she stopped at the fence to look properly—were black. Not withered, not frost-bitten. *Black*, stalk and leaf and ear of barley alike, as if someone had drawn the fields in ink and then forgotten to add the world's colors back in.

An old man was standing at his gate, staring at the same fields. He didn't look at her when she stopped beside him. He looked like a man who had done all his looking already and found nothing worth the effort.

"Bad year," Astrid offered.

"Third bad year." His voice was flat as the road. "First the late spring. Then the blight came up from the south. Now this." He nodded at the black barley. "My grandfather farmed this land sixty years. He said you could set your calendar by the seasons. Now I don't set anything by anything."

"Have you sent word to the capital? Asked for aid?"

That earned her a glance, dry as seed husks. "We sent word. They sent an assessor. He looked at the fields, wrote in his book, and told us it was *unseasonable weather*." The old man smiled without any warmth in it. "Unseasonable weather. Three years running. In every direction."

Astrid's hand drifted, without her permission, to the map beneath her coat. *The covenant's silence is why the seasons are failing.* The courier hadn't lived long enough to explain how he knew. But the map did not argue with the old man. The map, drawn in living ink that shifted when she wasn't quite watching it, showed the north country graying at its edges like a page left out in the damp.

"Your children," she said carefully. "How are they?"

The question surprised him into turning fully around. "Why do you ask after the children?"

"No particular reason."

He studied her—a young woman, travel-worn, alone, asking odd questions—and whatever he saw made him decide she was harmless, or at least not worth the effort of suspicion. "Sleeping," he said finally. "All of them. Too much. My granddaughter is six years old, and she sleeps like the dead and wakes like one too. Doesn't play. Doesn't chatter. Just sits and stares until it's time to sleep again." He rubbed his face with a cracked palm. "The midwife says half the little ones in the village have stopped dreaming. She says it like it's a thing that can happen. Like weather."

*Like weather.* Astrid thought of the guild hall, of Master Corren's voice rolling through the lecture room: *Magic is not a power, apprentices. It is a conversation. The world talks; we answer.* And somewhere beyond the human realm, past veils grown thin and threadbare, something vast and wild had been talking for centuries—and the Choir had been the answering voice, holding the two worlds in harmony.

And now nobody answered. So the world had stopped speaking. Stopped dreaming. Stopped turning green.

"Thank you," she said to the old man, and meant it more than he could know.

"For what?" he asked, genuinely puzzled, and went back to staring at his ink-black fields.

* * *

Three days further north, the villages grew worse and the road grew emptier, and Astrid began keeping a tally in her head that she wished she weren't keeping.

Ashfield itself—the town that gave the whole region its name, though the name had once been Meadowfield before anyone had the honesty to change it—was the worst yet. The blight here had moved past the fields and into the orchards, past the orchards and into the hedgerows, so that the whole valley looked like a hearth the morning after a fire. Even the moss on the stones was gray. Even the river ran sluggish and dull, like water that had forgotten what it was for.

And the people. Astrid passed through the market square—it was barely a market anymore, four stalls where there had clearly once been forty—and felt something twist in her chest that she recognized, with uncomfortable precision, as guild training. Not the magic part. The other part. The part Corren had taught first, before any lesson in knots or songs: *Look at people, Astrid. Really look. Magic that doesn't begin with looking is just meddling.*

So she looked. She saw a woman mending a net with hands that moved automatically, eyes fixed on nothing. She saw a boy of perhaps ten sitting on a step, doing absolutely nothing, in the way of someone who has forgotten what doing things is for. She saw a baker selling bread to a line of customers, and not one person in that line—not the baker, not the buyers—said a single word to each other. The silence wasn't hostile. It was worse than hostile. It was *empty*, the way a house is empty, the way a bell is empty.

Astrid bought a roll she didn't want, just to put a coin in the baker's hand and say "thank you" out loud, and the baker flinched at the sound of her voice like a man waking from a nap he hadn't meant to take.

"You're not from here," he said.

"No."

"Where are you going?"

"North," Astrid said. It was true, and vague, and safe.

"Huh." He looked at her roll, then at her, then at the gray sky. "You'll want to eat it while it's warm. Nothing stays warm here long anymore." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes—something that might once have been humor. "Used to be famous, my rolls. Won a ribbon at the Harvest Fair, three years back. Last fair they ever held. You can't have a harvest fair without a harvest."

"I'm sorry," Astrid said.

"Don't be sorry. Eat." He waved her off, and as she turned away she heard him mutter, almost to himself, "Warm while it lasts. That's the whole world now, isn't it. Warm while it lasts."

Astrid walked to the edge of town and ate the roll sitting on a low wall, and it was good bread, and she nearly cried over it, which embarrassed her so much she pretended she had something in her eye.

It wasn't the bread. It was the ribbon. Somewhere in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, was a map that could tell her why the fairs had stopped, why the children slept like the dead, why the whole world was going quietly gray—and every mile north brought her closer to the drowned chapel marked on its second page, closer to a singer who had gone mute from despair, closer to answers that felt less like hope and more like a debt coming due.

She finished the roll. She brushed off her hands. She got up and kept walking, because that was the whole art of survival, really: finishing the roll, brushing off your hands, keeping walking.

* * *

The farm appeared at dusk on the fourth day, and it was dying.

Astrid saw it from the crest of a hill—a farmhouse, a barn, a scatter of outbuildings, and fields that were not merely blighted but *actively* blighted, the blackness spreading even as she watched, a slow tide of dark creeping across what remained of the green. This was no ordinary rot. Ordinary rot had texture, variety, a kind of terrible vitality. This was uniform. This was *tidy*. This was the world forgetting how to be alive, in neat rows, like unmade beds.

There was a figure in the nearest field. A woman, on her knees among the black stalks, pulling them up and throwing them behind her in a pile, pulling and throwing, pulling and throwing, with the mechanical desperation of someone who knows the work is hopeless and cannot stop doing it anyway.

Astrid should have walked on. Every instinct honed by three years in Corvane said *walk on, head down, invisible, alive*. The guild's last surviving apprentice owed the world exactly one thing, and that was to keep existing until she understood why.

But the woman pulled up another handful of black stalks, and her shoulders shook, and she made a sound—not a sob, something quieter and worse, the sound of a person trying very hard to make no sound at all—and Astrid's feet carried her down the hill before her caution could catch up and tackle them.

"Ma'am?" she called softly, from the fence line. "Ma'am, it'll be dark soon."

The woman looked up. Her face was streaked with dirt and older than it ought to have been. "I know how dark works," she said.

"I—" Astrid hesitated. *Look at people. Really look.* She looked. Behind the exhaustion there was fury, and behind the fury there was fear, and behind the fear, buried deep, there was still a stubborn coal of something that refused to go out. "May I see your field?"

"It's not my field. It's my father's. He's in the house. Bad lungs. He watches me ruin his farm through the window every day and pretends he isn't watching." The woman—her name, she said, was Marta—wiped her face with her forearm and left a new smear of dirt. "You a tax assessor?"

"No."

"Soldier?"

"No."

"Then what do you want with the field?"

Astrid opened her mouth to say *nothing*, which was the safe answer, the practiced answer, the answer that had kept her breathing for three years. What came out instead was:

"I want to help."

Marta stared at her. Then she laughed—one short, hard bark with no joy in it. "Help. Right. Everyone wants to help. The assessor helped. The priest helped. The traveling doctor helped, and charged us for the privilege. Do you know what helps, girl? Nothing. Nothing helps. The blight isn't a sickness. Sicknesses end. This is—" she groped for the word, and settled on, "—this is the ground giving up. You can't cure a thing like that."

"You're right," Astrid said quietly. "You can't cure it. It isn't a sickness."

"Then what is it?"

And there it was. The question, hanging in the cold evening air between them, and Astrid standing at the fence with three years of silence in her throat and a dead courier's map against her ribs.

Every rule she had survived by said: *lie*. Say you're a herbalist. Say you know a poultice. Keep the truth locked in the cellar of yourself where you'd kept it since the guild fell, since the hangings, since the day Master Corren stood in the magistrate's court and testified—she still couldn't finish that memory, she always swerved away from it like a cart swerving from a broken bridge—

But the field was black. The children weren't dreaming. The baker's ribbon-winning rolls had nowhere to go. And the woman on her knees in the dirt was making that sound, that terrible quiet sound, and Astrid Halloran had spent three years being careful, and she was so tired, and the world was so gray.

"It's a silence," she heard herself say. "The ground isn't sick. It's *silent*. There used to be... a song. Something that kept the growing things growing, the seasons turning, the dreams coming. And the song stopped. Three years ago. Everything you're seeing is what happens when the music stops and nobody starts it again."

Marta looked at her the way you look at a stranger who has just said something either profoundly wise or profoundly mad. "A song," she repeated.

"A song," Astrid confirmed, feeling slightly mad herself.

"And you're going to sing it back, are you?"

"I'm going to try to find the people who can."

Marta's eyes narrowed. "You're one of those. One of the—" she dropped her voice, glancing at the house, at the road, at the gray sky, as if the word itself might be listening. "—the magic folk."

Astrid's heart performed a complicated maneuver involving a lurch and a stall. "No," she said, on pure reflex.

"You hesitated."

"I didn't hesitate."

"You absolutely hesitated. You hesitated like a cat caught on a table." Marta stood, brushing off her knees, and there was something almost fierce in her tiredness now. "Listen to me, stranger. I don't care what you are. My father's dying, my farm's dying, and the priest says God has turned His face away, and the assessor says it's unseasonable weather, and I say somebody, *somebody* in this rotten gray world knows what's happening and won't say. If that somebody is you—if you know even one true thing about why my barley is black—then tell me, or help me, or get off my fence."

The wind moved through the black stalks with a dry rattle, like applause from a very bored audience.

Astrid took a breath. And then, because the moment demanded it, because the woman deserved it, because three years of lying had apparently rotted through at last like everything else:

"My name is Astrid. I was apprenticed to the Lanternwright Guild. I make—I *made*—charms. Small ones. Harmless ones. Luck-knots, mostly." She pulled her sleeve back, and there it was in the fading light: the guild mark on her forearm, the lantern-and-key sigil, faint as an old scar. "This is a hanging offense. I'm telling you because you asked for one true thing, and that's mine."

For a long moment Marta said nothing. Then she looked at the black field, and at the house where her father watched, and back at Astrid, and said, in a completely different voice, a voice stripped of all its armor:

"Can you fix it? The field. Can you actually fix it, or are you just going to stand there being honest at me?"

"I don't know," Astrid said honestly. "I've never tried anything this big. Charms are... small. Intentional knots in small things. A field is—" she gestured helplessly at the acres of dark. "A field is enormous."

"But you could try."

"I could try."

Marta looked at the sky, which was doing its best impression of a slate roof. "Well," she said. "Father's asleep by now. And I've spent four months pulling up dead barley, and I'd rather spend one night doing something stupid and hopeful than another night doing something sensible and dead inside." She stepped back from the field and spread her arms, an odd, exhausted gesture of surrender. "Go on, then. Try."

* * *

Astrid knelt in the black barley and discovered that she had no idea what she was doing.

That wasn't entirely true. She knew the theory. Every Lanternwright knew the theory: magic was a conversation, intention given form through craft, the world talked and you answered. But her entire practical education consisted of knots the size of a thumbnail. This was like teaching someone to swim by having them float in a washbasin and then pointing them at the ocean.

Still. She pressed her palms flat against the soil, closed her eyes, and listened.

Corren's voice again, unbidden, from a lecture hall that no longer existed: *Before you speak, apprentice, listen. What is the field saying?*

And the field was saying—Astrid's breath caught—the field was saying nothing. That was the horror of it. Not pain, not death, not rot. *Nothing.* Soil is supposed to murmur. Growing things are supposed to hum, faintly, ceaselessly, the way a hearth hums, the way a hive hums. This soil was silent the way a stopped clock is silent. All that latent life, all those seeds waiting under the black, and not one whisper of intent anywhere in it.

Except.

Except deep down, deeper than the blight, deeper than the silence, there was—Astrid concentrated, pressing harder against the earth, ignoring the cold seeping through her knees—there was a *seed*. One seed, one stubborn barley seed, that had not yet surrendered. It sat in its darkness holding onto the simple animal fact of itself, *I am a seed, I am a seed, I am a seed*, and it was the loneliest sound Astrid had ever heard.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you poor brave thing."

"What?" Marta called from the fence. "What is it?"

"There's one still alive." Astrid's eyes were stinging, which was ridiculous, it was a *seed*. "One seed. It's still trying."

"Then help it," Marta said simply.

So Astrid did the only thing she knew how to do. She took the seed's tiny stubborn intention—the *I am, I am, I am*—and she treated it like thread. She found its ends. She began, with the care of three years' practice, to knot.

Not with her fingers. With herself. With her breath and her attention and the small warm banked fire of intention that she had kept hidden so long it had nearly gone out. She wove the seed's *I am* outward, stitch by stitch, into the soil around it, and where the stitching touched, the silence broke. Not loudly. Quietly, the way ice breaks up in spring—first one crack, then another, then a shivering network of cracks, and under the cracks, murmurs. The soil remembered how to murmur. The neighboring seeds, hearing their brother seed's *I am* echoed and amplified, began answering: *we are, we are, we are.*

Astrid laughed. She couldn't help it. It burst out of her like a bird startled from a hedge, and she laughed and knotted and wove, tears freezing on her cheeks, and the warmth pouring out of her was *enormous*, it was the warmth of the guild hall, of her mother's kitchen, of every good fire she had ever known, and the black tide in the field stopped. Held. Began, at its edges, to recede.

Green came back to the barley like color flooding into a washed-out painting. She could feel it happening, could feel the stalks straightening, the ears filling, the whole field exhaling a breath it had been holding for three years. Exhilaration hit her like a wave breaking over her head—pure, staggering joy, the joy of a swimmer surfacing, of a singer finding the note, of a locked door swinging open—and she threw her head back and laughed at the gray sky, defiantly, triumphantly, *loudly*—

Which was, she realized some moments later, a mistake.

Because the exhilaration curdled even as she felt it, because in the middle of her triumph her own words floated back to her: *a hanging offense*. Because she had just performed forbidden magic, openly, at dusk, in a farmyard, with her sleeve rolled up and her guild mark bare to the air, and she had been *loud about it*.

She opened her eyes. The field was green. Half of it, anyway—a great swath of it, spreading, real, impossible. Marta was standing at the fence with both hands over her mouth.

And on the road above the farm, where it crested the hill against the last gray light, stood a man with a lantern.

He hadn't spoken. He was too far away for her to read his face. He simply stood there, silhouetted, lantern raised, looking down at the impossible green field and the kneeling woman with the bare mark on her arm, and the light of his lantern trembled very slightly, the way a hand trembles.

"Marta," Astrid said, without taking her eyes off the hill. "How far is it to the next town?"

"Eight miles," Marta whispered. "Why?"

"Because I think I need to be gone before morning."

The man on the hill lowered his lantern slowly. Then he turned, and walked away over the crest, and was gone—but not, Astrid's hammering heart insisted, gone for good. He had seen. Whoever he was—farmer, traveler, tithe-collector—he had seen a forbidden thing done openly, and seen it done well, and the world was full of people who reported such things, and full of worse people who hunted them.

Astrid got up on shaking legs. The green field rustled behind her, alive and loud with murmuring, the first singing thing in miles, and it sounded to her suddenly less like triumph and more like a bell tolling.

"I'm sorry," she said to Marta. "I've brought trouble to your door."

Marta was still staring at the field. When she turned, her eyes were wet and furious and shining. "Trouble," she said. "You gave us back our farm, and you're worried about *trouble*." She grabbed Astrid's arm—grabbed the marked arm, deliberately, and held it. "Listen to me, Astrid-who-was-apprenticed-to-the-Lanternwright-Guild. Whatever comes of this, you come back this way if you need to. There's a loose board in the barn, behind the feed bins. You remember that."

Astrid's throat was too tight for words. She nodded.

"Now go," Marta said. "Go north, go find your singers, and start that song again—because if this is what one seed can do, then whoever silenced the rest of it owes this world a debt, and I want it paid."

Astrid shouldered her pack. At the fence she paused, and looked back once—at the green sweeping slow and steady across the black, at Marta standing guard over it with her fists on her hips like a general reviewing a miracle.

Then she walked north, fast, into the dark, eight miles of night road ahead of her and the taste of exhilaration still on her tongue, gone sharp now, gone metallic, like fear.

Behind her, far behind, she thought she heard a horse.

She told herself it was the wind.

She walked faster.
