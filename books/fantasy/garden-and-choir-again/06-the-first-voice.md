## 6. The First Voice

The map had been leading her north for six days, and for six days it had been lying to her.

Not maliciously—Astrid was fairly sure maps drawn in living ink didn't have it in them to be malicious—but the way the road kept *shimmering* just ahead of her boots, promising a bridge that turned out to be a fallen tree, a village that turned out to be a memory of a village, made her suspect the thing had a sense of humor. A dry one. The kind that never laughed at its own jokes.

"You could just tell me where we're going," she told it, holding it up by its wax seal so the parchment dangled like a hanged man. The ink swirled, unrepentant, and settled into a shape she'd come to recognize as smugness. "Fine. Keep your secrets. Everyone in this country does."

She folded it away and looked out over the valley the stranger's dying directions had promised: a bowl of drowned land where a river had jumped its banks years ago and simply decided to stay. Roofs broke the water's surface like the backs of sleeping animals. A bell tower leaned at an angle that would have given a Corvane building inspector apoplexy, its spire crowned with a heron's nest instead of a cross. And around the tower—this was the part that stopped her breath, the part the map had been nudging her toward all day—a garden.

Not a garden growing *near* the flood. Not a garden on some spared hillock. A garden growing *in* the water, rows of green rising straight out of the flood like the river had agreed, politely, to grow around them. Roses with their feet in three feet of standing water, blooming in a season when even the pines had given up. Bean vines climbing the drowned chapel wall. Something orange and heavy hanging from a trellis that should have rotted a decade ago.

Astrid stood on the ridge with her pack straps biting her shoulders and felt something in her chest do a thing she hadn't let it do in three years.

It felt like hope. Small, green, ridiculous hope—the kind that grows in a crack in the pavement and gets stepped on eventually, but is still, while it lasts, unbearably alive.

"Well," she said aloud, because talking to herself was cheaper than talking to anyone who might report her, "either that's a miracle or a trap."

Her experience said the two were not mutually exclusive.

* * *

Getting down to the chapel meant wading. Astrid rolled her trousers to the knee, tied her boots around her neck by their laces, and hissed her way through water cold enough to make her reconsider every choice that had led here—which, granted, was most of her life. The garden rows rose around her as she went, and up close they were worse than impossible; they were *showing off*. Tomatoes fat as fists. Lettuce so crisp it crackled. Bees working the blossoms in lazy spirals, which shouldn't have mattered, except that Astrid hadn't seen a bee since Ashfield, and before that not since autumn, and everyone knew what it meant when the bees stopped.

The chapel door hung open on rusted hinges. Inside, the floor was flooded ankle-deep, and the water was warm.

That was the detail that undid her. Warm water, in a drowned church, in the death of winter. She stood dripping in the nave, boots in hand, and listened—and this was where the guild training woke up in her bones, the old Lanternwright discipline she'd practiced in secret every night for three years, lighting a single flame in her palm behind a locked door just to remember she existed.

Because the chapel wasn't silent.

Most people would have heard nothing. Most people heard nothing everywhere anymore; that was rather the whole problem. But Astrid had spent seven years learning to hear what the world sounded like underneath itself—the hum in a luck-knot, the whisper in a fever-charm, the long slow note a candle makes when it burns near someone telling the truth. And the chapel was singing.

Not loudly. Not in words. It was the sound water makes when it remembers being rain, layered under the sound green things make when they unfurl, threaded through with something older and rounder, a bass note like the earth humming to itself in the dark. The song came up through the flooded floor and out through the roses and it was *failing*, she could hear that too, fraying at the edges like a hem coming undone—but it was there. Alive. Here, in the middle of everything dying, something was still singing.

Someone was keeping it alive.

"Hello," Astrid called, and her voice cracked on the second syllable from disuse. She cleared her throat. "I'm not with the Inquisition. I'm—" *What*, exactly? A charm-seller. An outlaw. The last apprentice of a guild that officially never existed. "I'm looking for the Choir."

Silence. Then, from behind the altar, a woman stood up out of a patch of marigolds.

She was tall and gray-streaked, maybe fifty, wearing a coat that had once been fine and now served mainly as a home for mud. Her hands were earth to the wrist. She looked at Astrid the way you look at weather—not hostile, not welcoming, merely taking inventory.

"Choir," the woman repeated. Her voice was rough, like a door opening after years shut. "There's no choir."

"I think there is," Astrid said. "I think you're it."

The woman's face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, the way faces do when they've practiced blankness until it fits better than anything else. She wiped her hands on her coat. "You're trespassing."

"It's a chapel."

"It's my garden."

"The garden is in the middle of a lake."

"Yes," the woman said, with the first flicker of something that might, in better light, have been pride. "Isn't it lovely?"

And despite everything—the cold, the fear, the six days of lying maps—Astrid laughed. It came out rusty and surprised them both.

* * *

The woman's name was Sera Ilvane, though she offered it the way you'd hand over a coin you suspected was counterfeit. She would not say how the chapel had flooded or why the garden grew, and when Astrid asked directly about the Hollow Choir, Sera picked up a watering can and became profoundly interested in the marigolds.

So Astrid did what the guild had trained her for. Not the fire-work, not the knot-work—the listening.

She sat on a dry-ish pew with her hands open on her knees and let the chapel's song wash through her. It took an hour. Guild listening always did; Master Corren used to say patience was ninety percent of magic and the other ten percent was knowing when to run, and Astrid had thought it was a joke at the time and now recognized it as the single truest thing he'd ever taught her.

Underneath the water-song and the green-song, she found the third thread. The human one. It was thin as wire and it was coming from Sera—from her hands, from the rhythm of her work, from the way she moved among the rows. Sera couldn't speak it. Astrid understood that within minutes, the way you understand a limp: the singer's voice had been broken somewhere deep, and everything she couldn't say had gone into the garden instead. Every note of grief and stubbornness and love that had no mouth to leave by had rooted in the flood and *bloomed*. That was why the tomatoes were fat. That was why the water was warm. A Choir singer's voice doesn't die when it goes silent. It just finds somewhere else to live.

"You're singing right now," Astrid said quietly.

Sera's back stiffened. "I haven't sung in eleven years."

"I know," Astrid said. "That's what I mean. You've been singing anyway. Through the garden." She hesitated, then committed. "It's the covenant-song. Part of it. The ground-verse, I think—I only ever learned fragments, but I'd know it anywhere. My master—" She stopped. Saying Corren's name felt like stepping on ice. "My teacher made me memorize pieces of it when I was small. He said I'd never need them. He said a lot of things."

Sera set down the watering can very slowly. When she turned, her careful blankness had cracked along new lines, and what showed through was exhaustion so total it looked like anger.

"Go away," she said. "Whatever you want, whatever the map promised you—it's not here. There's no Choir. There's me, and there are vegetables, and there is a great deal of water. Singing got everyone I loved killed, and tending this garden has kept exactly one person alive, and I intend to keep doing it until I don't. Go away."

"Seasons are failing," Astrid said. "You know that. You can feel it from here—the song's fraying. I heard it before I ever saw you."

"I know." Two words, flat as stones.

"Then you know what happens if nobody mends it."

"I know what happens if somebody *tries*." For a moment Sera's voice climbed toward something, cracked, and dropped. She pressed her lips together. When she spoke again it was barely above the water's murmur. "Eleven years ago there were forty-one of us. Do you know how many are left? Neither do I. That's the answer. Nobody knows. We scattered like—like seed in wind, and the wind was the Inquisition, and I watched—" Her jaw worked. Nothing came out. Eleven years of silence, and it closed over her again like water.

Astrid didn't push. She'd learned that from charmselling, actually: you can't force a sale, and you can't force a grief. You can only be present, and honest, and occasionally useful.

So she took off her pack, pulled out her travel bread and a jar of honey she'd traded two fever-charms for in Ashfield, and said, "I'm going to eat lunch. You don't have to talk. But if you wanted to show me how you keep beans alive in a flood, I have questions, because as far as I can tell it violates at least four natural laws and I find that deeply appealing."

Sera stared at her for a long moment.

"There are five laws it violates," she said finally. "Sit down. Don't get honey on my pew."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and if it wasn't friendship, it was at least a truce, which in Astrid's experience was how all friendships started whether they admitted it or not.

Sera taught her the flooding trick—which was less a trick than a sustained act of persuasion, convincing the water it was soil and the soil it was safe—and Astrid, watching closely with her guild ears open, realized that Sera wasn't casting anything at all. She was *humming*. Without sound. The song moved through her hands and into the roots, wordless, constant, the way a heart beats without being asked.

"You don't even notice you're doing it," Astrid said.

"Do what?" Sera said, and then heard herself, and went still. "...Ah."

"That. Exactly that."

Sera sat down heavily on the chapel steps, water lapping at her ankles, and looked out over her impossible garden with an expression Astrid couldn't read. "When they came for us," she said, "I ran. That's the whole story. Others stood and sang and burned for it, and I ran, and I ran here, because my grandmother was baptized in this chapel and it seemed as good a place to hide as any. And the first spring, nothing grew. Everything drowned. And I was so angry—so *angry*—that I screamed at the water. All the words I'd ever sung, all at once, inside, where no one could arrest them." She flexed her fingers. "The beans came up the next day."

"And your voice?"

"Gone the same day. Like a trade." She shrugged, a gesture that tried to be casual and failed. "I've made my peace with it. The garden sings. I listen. It's more than most people get."

"It's not enough," Astrid said gently. "Sera. The covenant isn't a garden. It's a *choir*. It was always voices together—that's the whole architecture of it. One voice holding a fragment can keep a valley alive. I think that's what you're doing; I think this whole valley is the healthiest land I've walked through in a year. But the seasons aren't failing in a valley. They're failing everywhere. The song needs assembling. Seven voices, the map says. Maybe more." She pulled the map from her coat and unfolded it between them, and the living ink stirred, and—Astrid watched Sera's face as it happened—the parchment shifted, showing the drowned chapel, showing the garden, showing a small figure bent among the rows, and then moving on, northward, to a mark shaped like a drum.

Sera touched the ink with one dirt-caked finger. The ink rippled away from her touch and then, slowly, leaned back into it, like a cat deciding to trust.

"I can't sing," she whispered.

"You're singing right now."

"In the *choir*. With *voices*. In front of—" She stopped. Her hand closed. "If I stand up and open my mouth, nothing comes out. Eleven years, girl. Whatever they broke in me didn't heal. It *set*."

"Then we'll carry you," Astrid said. "That's what a choir is. It's not forty-one soloists. It's people covering for each other's cracks. My teacher used to say—" Again the name stuck. She pushed past it. "He used to say a voice alone is a candle, but a choir is a lantern. The whole point of the guild was that none of us carries the light alone. I've forgotten a lot of things in three years. I refuse to forget that."

The light was going gold over the water. Somewhere out in the flood, the herons in the bell tower were settling down for the night with prehistoric dignity. Sera looked at the map, and at the garden, and at her own hands.

"If I go," she said, "the garden dies."

"No," Astrid said, and pointed at Sera's chest. "*That* doesn't die. It'll just have to come out of hiding. Which, frankly, is what all of us are going to have to do, if any of this works."

Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. She stood, walked to the farthest row of marigolds—the oldest ones, Astrid noticed, the ones grown woody and wild—and knelt there for a long time with her forehead nearly touching the blossoms. Saying goodbye, or thank you, or both.

When she came back, she had a canvas satchel over her shoulder and seeds in her pocket, because she was the kind of woman who took seeds everywhere, and Astrid understood that she had just gained an ally the way you gain weather: completely, and all at once, and with no guarantee of mild conditions ahead.

"We go at dawn," Sera said. "The fen roads are faster than the map thinks, and I know them, and—" She paused at the chapel door, silhouetted against the last light, and her voice dropped. "Girl. If the Inquisition is following you—"

"They are," Astrid said. "An Inquisitor named Veyra. She nearly had me in Corvane. She's patient. She's *kind*, which is worse."

Sera nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprisable. "Then we take the fen roads, and we move quiet, and we pray the map keeps its jokes to itself." She looked north, where the drowned country gave way to darkening marsh, and something in her face—something long-buried, something green—flickered. "And Astrid Halloran. Last apprentice of the Lanternwrights."

Astrid went cold. "How do you—"

"The map," Sera said, tapping her temple. "It talks, if you hold it long enough. Yours has been muttering about you for days, apparently. Says you argue with it." A pause, and then, dry as August: "It says you're rude."

Astrid opened her mouth to defend herself, and that was when they both heard it—far off across the water, thin on the evening air: hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving at a trot along the northern ridge, where no honest traveler had any reason to be at dusk.

Sera's hand found Astrid's arm, grip iron-hard.

"Run first," she murmured. "Ask questions after. That order matters."
