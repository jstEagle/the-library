## 7. Old Debts

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Corren Halloran would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were when the magistracy handled small claims—disputed fences, unpaid debts, the petty arithmetic of neighbors. It was a day for old accounts coming due.

He was in his study at the top of the magistrate's house, working through a petition about a miller's rights to a stream that had, inconveniently for everyone, moved itself two fields east over the winter. The paper was dry in his fingers. The fire was well-built. Outside his window, Corvane lay under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the frost had come early again this year, and no one in the city said aloud what everyone was thinking: *the seasons are failing.*

Corren read the letter twice.

*Word from Ashfield. A woman selling charms on the north road. Guild-trained, by her hands. She healed a farmer's blight in open daylight, witnesses say, and walked on without hiding it. She carries something. A map, some say. She is headed toward the fen country.*

There was no signature. There never was, with these letters. But Corren knew the hand, because he paid for it, and because he had spent eleven years making certain he knew everything that moved along the roads out of Corvane like a man who checks a wound every morning to see if it has begun to bleed again.

He set the letter down on the desk beside the miller's petition and looked at it for a long time.

Then he got up, went to the cabinet where he kept the good brandy—the bottle he allowed himself one glass of on the anniversaries of things—and poured himself a glass on no anniversary at all.

"Astrid," he said to the empty room.

The name came out of him the way a splinter comes out: with relief, and with blood.

* * *

Eleven years ago, there had been a guildhall on Lanternmaker's Row with a crooked door that stuck in damp weather, and Master Corren had been its last cantor, and Astrid Halloran had been his last apprentice, and between them they had held more music than either of them knew what to do with.

She had come to him at nine years old, all elbows and stubbornness, brought by an aunt who could no longer feed her. He had tested her the way the guild tested all its children—not with questions but with listening. He had hummed the first three notes of the covenant-song, the ones every Lanternwright learned before their letters, and waited to see what she did.

Most children repeated the notes back, obedient as bells.

Astrid had listened, frowned at him with the full gravity of nine years old, and added a fourth note of her own—a small, bright, entirely unauthorized note that made the candle flames in the testing room lean toward her like flowers toward sun.

"You changed it," Corren had said.

"It wanted changing," said Astrid. "It was going to end too soon."

He should have known then. He told himself, later, in the long years of not sleeping, that he *had* known then—that the girl who heard where songs wanted to go was always going to be trouble for a man whose whole life was learning how to make songs stop.

But that morning, eleven years ago, he had only laughed, and written her name in the apprentice roll, and thought: *here is the future of the guild, and she doesn't even know it yet.*

* * *

The trouble with guilt, Corren had learned, was that it didn't look like anything from the outside. People expected it to be dramatic—wailing, hair-tearing, midnight confessions. In practice it looked like a man doing his job very, very well. It looked like Magistrate Corren arriving early to every session, ruling fairly on every fence dispute, remembering every clerk's name and every widow's circumstances. It looked like a man building so orderly and blameless a life that no one would ever think to look underneath it.

It looked, most days, almost convincing. Even to him. Especially to him.

He finished the brandy and went down to find his steward.

"Brannick," he said. "I'll need riders. Four, maybe six. Quiet ones."

Brannick, who had served him for eight years and had long ago stopped being surprised by anything except the weather, raised one eyebrow exactly a quarter-inch. "Riders, sir. For what purpose?"

"There's a woman on the north road. She may be carrying stolen property."

"Stolen property," Brannick repeated, in the tone of a man filing the phrase away for later examination.

"Guild materials." Corren kept his voice level, bored, administrative. He had gotten very good at bored and administrative; it was practically a second guild training. "Relics of the old order. You remember the statutes."

"I remember the statutes, sir." Brannick paused. "Begging your pardon, but the statutes also say what's done with folk who carry guild materials. I've seen the postings on the square. It's not a fine."

"I know what the statutes say, Brannick."

"Yes, sir." Another pause, longer, more dangerous. Brannick had the particular courage of servants, which was to say he had nothing left to lose and knew precisely where his master kept his conscience, if any. "Only I'd have thought, sir, that if you wanted the woman dead, you'd have sent soldiers weeks ago. Word of her has been moving up the roads since Ashfield. Everyone's talking about it. The blight-healing. So if you're only now sending riders, quiet ones, four or six—" he counted on his fingers with terrible patience— "then it occurs to me you're not sending them to catch her. You're sending them to warn her."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the kitchen maid dropped a pot and swore with impressive fluency.

"Brannick," Corren said, "if you ever have thoughts like that again, I recommend keeping them somewhere behind your teeth, where they can do less damage."

"Of course, sir. And shall I keep the riders' instructions behind my teeth as well?"

Corren looked at his steward for a long moment. Then, very quietly: "Tell them to follow her. Tell them to stay a half-day behind, always a half-day, and to be loud about their campfires. If anyone else asks, tell them the magistrate wants the woman taken alive for questioning."

"And if someone else catches her first?"

"Then we will hope," Corren said, "that the Faeheart's mercy is better than mine has been."

Brannick bowed—slightly deeper than protocol required, which was Brannick's way of saying *I heard all of it, sir, every word*—and went to arrange the horses.

* * *

That night Corren did not sleep. Instead he did what he did on such nights, which was to take down the box.

It sat at the back of his wardrobe, behind the winter coats, wrapped in oilcloth, locked with a key he wore on a cord around his neck and told people was for his correspondence. Inside were the relics of a dead life: his cantor's bell, its bronze gone dull with disuse; a tuning fork of guild-silver that still, when struck, produced a note that made the room feel briefly larger than it was; a bundle of sheet music in the old notation, the notation that had been burned by the cartload in the square while he stood on the steps of the magistracy and watched.

And beneath those, wrapped separately, a child's practice slate. On it, in chalk long faded to ghost-gray, a fourth note. Unauthorized. Bright. Added to the covenant-song by a nine-year-old who thought it ended too soon.

He had taken it off her slate himself, the week before the purges began, meaning to scold her for improvising on sacred material. He had never given it back. He had never been able to throw it away either. It sat in the box like a splinter of the man he might have been.

Because here was the thing Corren could not explain to anyone, not to Brannick, not to the other magistrates, not to the confessor he did not keep: he had not outlawed magic out of ambition. Ambition would have been simpler. Ambition could be lived with.

He had done it out of fear, and worse, out of a kind of love.

In the last year of the guild, the covenant had already been failing—he had felt it in his bones before anyone said it aloud, the way a cantor feels a song going flat. The singers were dying or scattering. The Faeheart had gone silent. And Corren, who had spent thirty years listening to the wild realm through the thin membrane of the covenant, had begun to hear something else underneath the silence. Something vast, and hungry, and *awake*. The wildness wasn't fading, whatever the histories claimed. It was gathering. And when it broke through—if it broke through—it would not come gently. He had seen what leaked through the cracks already: crops blackening overnight, children dreaming of teeth.

So when the Crown's men came asking which of the guildmasters would help them end magic forever, Corren had reasoned like a man bracing for a flood: better to break the dam ourselves, carefully, than let it burst. Better a controlled silence than a screaming wilderness. He had given them the guild rolls. He had shown them where the singers slept. He had testified, calm and reasonable and *sincere*, that the covenant was already dead, that magic was a corpse propped up by tradition, and that mercy lay in burying it properly.

They had believed him because he was believable. That was the worst of it. He had used everything the guild taught him about truth and harmony to argue the world into silence.

And Astrid—

Astrid had stood in the guildhall doorway the night the soldiers came, fifteen years old, holding her practice slate, and looked at him with a question in her face that he had spent eleven years refusing to answer. He had told her to run. He had told her the guild was over, that magic was over, that she must live small and safe and never sing. He had watched her go and told himself it was mercy. Told himself it was the only thing left he could give her.

He had never let himself ask whether what she saw in his face that night was a mentor saving his student, or a coward saving himself, or both wearing each other's coats.

* * *

Toward dawn he gave up on sleep and went to his window. The city was gray and frozen below, chimneys smoking, carts creaking on the early streets. Somewhere out there past the walls, past Ashfield, past the drowned chapel and the fen, a girl with an unauthorized fourth note in her ear was walking toward the Faeheart with a map in her hands.

If the map was real—if the Choir could truly be gathered—then everything Corren had built his penance upon would collapse. Not just the laws. The *reasoning*. Because his whole argument, the one that had hanged singers and emptied guildhalls, rested on a single claim: *the covenant is already dead.* And if a charm-seller with a borrowed map could walk out and prove otherwise—

Then it hadn't been mercy. It had been murder with good manners.

Unless she failed. Unless the riders found her, or Veyra found her—Veyra, whose courtesy frightened Corren more than any cruelty could have, because cruelty could be hated, and courtesy had to be *answered*—unless the whole mad errand died quietly in the fens, and the silence held, and the seasons kept failing, and Corren kept ruling fairly on fence disputes until the frost took the last of the harvest and everyone starved in perfect legal order.

He pressed his forehead against the cold glass.

"She'll forgive me or she won't," he said to his reflection, faint and gray in the window. "But she has to survive long enough to choose."

Behind him, in the wardrobe, behind the winter coats, inside the locked box, the cantor's bell sat in the dark. And though Corren did not touch it, did not strike it, had not struck it in eleven years—he could have sworn, in the hour before sunrise, that he heard it humming. One low note. Waiting.

By midmorning, four riders left the north gate of Corvane, carrying the magistrate's sealed orders and, folded privately into the saddlebag of the eldest among them, a second message in Corren's own hand:

*She rides toward the fen. Take the eastern causeway, not the road. Trust the witches more than the soldiers. And tell her—*

Here the ink had stopped, and started, and stopped again, three times, before settling at last on the only true thing he could send her across all those miles of failing country:

*Tell her the fourth note was right. It was always right. — C.*
