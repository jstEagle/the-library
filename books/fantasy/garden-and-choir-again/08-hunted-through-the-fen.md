## 8. Hunted Through the Fen

**Astrid**

The marsh did not want them.

Astrid had known that from the first step off the causeway, when the peat sucked at her boot with the enthusiasm of a child who has found something interesting in a puddle. The fen was not hostile exactly. It was simply indifferent, the way a sleeping bear is indifferent, and it made its opinion of travelers clear by swallowing every path they tried to take and offering, in exchange, water that looked like weak tea and smelled like old promises.

"Left here," Sera said.

Astrid stopped. "You can *talk*?"

"One word." Sera's face, usually as still as a pond in winter, showed the ghost of a smile. "I'm saving up."

They had been three days out of Ashfield, moving north through country that grew stranger by the mile. The blight followed them like a shadow — blackened wheat, orchards that bloomed and then dropped their fruit all at once, as if the trees had given up mid-thought. And now, behind them, dust on the southern road: riders. Veyra's riders. Astrid had counted six before the trees closed over the road, and she had spent the last day imagining them multiplying.

The word Sera had given her — her first word since the drowned chapel — had been *run*, delivered at dawn in a voice like a rusted hinge, and Astrid had obeyed without argument, because when a woman who hadn't spoken in years chooses that particular word for her debut, you listen.

Now they stood in knee-deep sedge while the light went amber and low, and Astrid admitted what she'd been refusing to admit since noon.

"We're lost."

Sera said nothing, which was easy for her, but her expression managed to convey *I noticed four hours ago and was being polite about it.*

"There's supposed to be people out here," Astrid pressed on. "Fen-folk. Witches, the map says. If anyone knows how to move through this place—"

"The map also says the covenant is a song," Sera said, finding a second word with visible effort, like a miser counting out coins. "Songs don't keep you dry."

"It kept us ahead of the riders so far."

"So far is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Astrid laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound, the kind of laugh she'd forgotten she owned. Three days of terror, and here was a mute singer making jokes at her expense. It was, she thought, the most hopeful thing that had happened since the courier died in her cellar.

Then the dogs bayed, far to the south, and hope went back in its box.

"They've crossed the causeway," Astrid breathed. "How—"

"Bribery," Sera said grimly, spending words like a woman who had decided survival was worth the expense. "Or luck. Or Veyra herself. Move."

They moved. The fen fought them every step — reeds that whipped their faces, pools that pretended to be ground, a heron that watched them flounder with an expression of profound editorial judgment. Astrid's lungs burned. Her guild mark, hidden under her sleeve, had begun to itch, which it always did when magic was near or danger was nearer, and she couldn't tell which, and both possibilities were bad.

It was Sera who saw it first. She stopped so abruptly that Astrid walked into her back, and raised one hand — not toward the riders, but toward a stand of willows ahead where the air itself seemed wrong. Warmer. Softer. Like stepping from a cold room into one where bread had recently been baked.

"Do you feel that?" Astrid whispered.

Sera nodded slowly. Then, wonderingly, she said a third word: "*Home.*"

* * *

**Sera**

The willows parted onto a village that should not have existed.

Houses of woven reed and black timber stood on stilts above the water, connected by walkways of planked rope, and smoke rose from chimneys in colors Sera had not seen since the Choir still sang — green smoke, blue smoke, one chimney exhaling what looked suspiciously like small golden birds that circled once and dissolved. Gardens grew on floating rafts. A child sat on a walkway fishing with a line that ended not in a hook but in a small silver bell, and whatever the bell rang for, the child pulled up wriggling things that glowed faintly and were dropped into a bucket without ceremony.

Witches. Real ones. Old covenant-keepers, hiding in the wet places of the world the way seeds hide in winter soil.

Sera felt her throat tighten around words she couldn't spend. She had been mute for eleven years — not voiceless, never voiceless, but *silent*, by choice, because after the purges every note she owned felt like a weapon pointed at someone she loved. She had tended the chapel garden and let it sing for her. That had seemed like enough. That had seemed like all she could bear.

And now here was a whole village of people who remembered, and her chest hurt with the effort of not weeping, because weeping was nearly singing and singing was—

"Halt there."

An old woman stood on the nearest walkway, and she was not what Sera expected. Fen-witches in the stories were crooked and cackling. This one was straight-backed and silver-haired, with a face like a river stone — smooth, hard, worn into shape by long argument with the water. Her eyes went from Sera's face to Astrid's sleeve, where the guild mark had slipped into view, and her expression cooled by several degrees.

"A Lanternwright," she said. "In my fen. The last time your guild came through here, they took Maren's daughter for burning a fever-charm. They hanged her from the mill bridge and left her for the crows, and the crows wouldn't even touch her, because she'd done nothing wrong and even carrion knows innocence when it smells it."

"I'm sorry," Astrid began.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful, or be gone." The witch's gaze shifted to Sera, and something flickered in it — recognition, maybe, or memory. "You. You're one of theirs. One of the Choir. I can see it sitting on you like a bird on a wire."

Sera nodded.

"Then why is your mouth shut? Why do you come to us silent, with a guild-branded girl and dogs behind you, asking for shelter your kind forfeited when you let the law stand?"

The question landed like a stone in still water, and Sera had no answer that could be spoken, because speaking was the wound itself. She opened her mouth. Closed it. Felt eleven years of silence press down on her like floodwater.

It was Astrid who answered.

"Because she's been carrying more than any of us," Astrid said, and her voice shook, but she didn't stop. "Because she lost everyone she sang with and kept tending a garden anyway, in a dead chapel, alone, for eleven years, growing flowers that shouldn't live, because someone had to remember what beauty was for. Because I found her and she came with me — a stranger, a guild apprentice, armed with nothing but a map and bad planning — because she believes the song can be sung again. So yes, ask us why we're here. But don't ask why her mouth is shut. Ask what it cost her to keep walking anyway."

Silence. Somewhere across the water, the fishing child's bell rang, bright and small.

The old witch studied them both for a long moment. Then she stepped aside.

"Maren," she called, without turning her head. "Get the door open. And someone deal with those dogs — quietly. We are not murderers yet, whatever else we are."

* * *

**Astrid**

The witch's name was Odile, and she fed them eel stew in a house that smelled of herbs and lamp oil and, underneath everything, faintly of magic — that green-bread warmth Astrid had caught outside. The stew was excellent. The company was another matter entirely.

"You knew the covenant," Astrid said, when the bowls were empty. "Personally."

Odile snorted. "Knew it? Girl, my grandmother helped *make* it. Nine witches, seven singers, and the Faeheart's own heralds, standing in a ring on the night the worlds were stitched together. I was a child when the singing stopped. I remember the exact morning. The porridge tasted of ash, and my mother cried into her hands and said, 'They've killed it, they've killed the song,' and none of us understood yet what that would mean." She turned her cup in her fingers. "We understand now. Look at your seasons. Look at your fields."

"Why didn't the witches fix it? Rebuild it yourselves?"

"Because the covenant isn't ours to rebuild." Odile's voice went flat. "It was a bargain between the Faeheart and *human* voices. The singers were human. The guild trained them. And when the purges came, the guild burned, the singers scattered, and the humans who weren't singing stood by and let it happen. Some of them wrote the laws." Her eyes flicked to Astrid's sleeve. "So no, child. We owe your kind nothing. We shelter you tonight because Sera Ilvane's grandmother pulled my mother from a flood, and debts outlive grudges. Barely."

Astrid absorbed this. It should have stung more than it did; instead it settled into her with the heavy rightness of a truth she'd half-known. The guild hadn't only been victims. The guild had been *absent*, in the end — hiding, surviving, selling charms in alleys while the world came apart.

"And the riders?" she asked instead. "You'll really turn them away?"

"I'll do better than turn them away." For the first time, Odile smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "The fen decides who finds whom. Your Inquisitor's dogs will chase their own tails until dawn, and by then you'll be gone. There's a man you need, two days north. A drummer. Tam Breck, he calls himself now; he had a grander name once. He drinks in a mining town called Greyhollow and pretends his hands don't remember anything."

"You know where all of them are."

"We know where all of them are. We've watched them for eleven years, grieving and waiting and hating humans, and doing precisely nothing, because grief is comfortable and hope is work." Odile rose and began stacking bowls with sharp little clinks. "Sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow you'll need your legs."

But sleep didn't come easily. Astrid lay on a pallet of reeds in the loft, listening to the fen breathe, and thought about Odile's words. *Grief is comfortable and hope is work.* She had spent three years telling herself that selling charms was enough — that small magics, harmless magics, were all the world would allow and all she could risk. But the courier had died to bring her that map. The old woman in the market had taken a beating to shield her. Sera had broken eleven years of silence one hard-won word at a time.

Hope was work. Fine. She was tired of resting anyway.

She was nearly asleep when the shouting started.

* * *

**Sera**

They came at moonrise, and they did not come the way Odile promised the fen would prevent.

Later, Sera would learn how: a fisherman, paid in silver a week ago to watch the waterways, a traitor with a boat and no loyalties. But in the moment there was only firelight on the water — torches, six of them, spreading in a line across the mere — and the sound of boots on walkways, and Odile's voice cracking across the village like a whip: *"The back channels! Take the reed-door, GO!"*

Hands pulled Sera upright. Astrid was beside her, wide-eyed, gripping the map case to her chest. An old man — Maren, presumably, though Sera had no time to sort names — shoved them through a door woven of living reeds that shouldn't have been a door at all, and then they were in a narrow channel between walls of sedge, poling a flat-bottomed skiff into darkness while behind them the village lit orange.

"They're burning the walkways," Astrid whispered. "Odile said they weren't murderers—"

"They're not burning houses. They're burning *exits*." Sera heard her own voice and marveled at it — words coming easier now, the dam cracked and leaking. "Veyra doesn't want the village. She wants us cornered."

They poled. The channel bent and split and bent again, and Sera steered by instinct and by the garden-sense that had never left her — feeling where the water ran clean, where the ground firmed beneath the muck. Behind them, shouts. Ahead, a fork: left toward open water and the northern shore, right into a blind maze of islets where the reeds grew tall as sails.

"Left," Astrid panted. "Open water, we can outrun—"

"No." Sera was already turning the skiff right. "Open water is where lanterns look. The maze is where fen-children play. Trust the maze."

They trusted the maze. For a quarter hour it worked — the torches spread along the left shore, calling to each other, hunting emptiness. Sera let the skiff drift among the islets, breathing hard, and allowed herself one full second of relief.

That was when the rider appeared on the nearest islet.

He must have cut cross-country; his horse stood blowing and mud-lathered at the reed's edge, and he himself stood with a crossbow, level, steady, thirty feet of black water between him and them. Young, Sera noted distantly. Nervous. His hands were better than his eyes.

"Off the boat," he said. "Slow. The Inquisitor wants you alive. Don't make me improvise."

Astrid's hand crept toward her sleeve, toward the small hot spark of magic she carried, and Sera saw the arithmetic happening behind her eyes — a shield-charm against a bolt, odds, distance, the dark water waiting to swallow whoever guessed wrong.

*Sera,* Astrid's eyes said. *What do we do?*

And Sera, who had spent eleven years teaching herself that silence was safety, that a voice raised was a voice that could be cut out, looked at the boy with the crossbow and at Astrid with her hand halfway to courage, and understood that this was the moment the fen-witches had meant. Loyalty tested by survival. Not loyalty to a cause — loyalty to a person, standing next to you in a leaking boat, willing to burn herself to buy you a chance.

No. Not tonight.

"*Run*," Sera said.

Not a whisper. Not the rusted hinge of dawn. Her full voice, eleven years deep, cracked and rusty and enormous, rolling across the black water like a bell — and the boy flinched, the bolt went wide and high, splashing somewhere behind them, and Astrid needed no second invitation. She seized the pole and drove them into the deepest channel as Sera's voice, freed now and apparently unwilling to return to its cage, kept going:

"*Run, run, run*" — not panic, she realized as the skiff shot forward; it had become rhythm, it had become *music*, the first notes of a song she'd thought buried — and the reeds themselves seemed to lean aside for them, the water to run smoother, the whole fen briefly remembering what it was to be part of a song.

They burst out of the maze on the far side, into shallows, into reeds, into running — up the bank, through sedge, legs screaming, until the torchlight was a memory and the northern stars wheeled overhead and Astrid collapsed laughing and gasping against a fallen tree.

"You *sang*," Astrid managed. "Eleven years of nothing and you sang us out of a crossbow."

Sera leaned against the tree, shaking, tears cutting tracks through the fen-mud on her face, and found that the words were still there, waiting, patient as seeds.

"Not sang," she corrected. "*Started.*"

Behind them, far to the south, the village lights had gone out one by one — spared, perhaps, or merely searched. Odile's fate sat in Sera's chest like a swallowed stone. They owed that woman everything, and they had repaid her by leading ruin to her door, and there was no undoing it, only going forward, only making the debt mean something.

Two days north. A mining town called Greyhollow. A drummer who drank and pretended his hands had forgotten.

"Come on," Sera said, and held out her hand to Astrid in the starlight. "We have a choir to finish."

Astrid took it, and they walked north together into the dark, one voice newly woken and one mark freshly itching, while somewhere behind them the fen closed over their wake like water over a skipped stone — and somewhere ahead, in a town built on coal and grief, a man was about to discover that his hands had never forgotten anything at all.
