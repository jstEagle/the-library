## 9. The Second Voice

The mining town was called Grumblehole, which Astrid felt was at least honest about itself.

"It's not called that," Sera said. She had been finding words again, slowly, the way a frozen stream finds cracks in the ice—one syllable here, two there, each one seeming to surprise her as much as it surprised Astrid. "It's *Grimsby Hollow*. The miners just call it—"

"Grumblehole," Astrid said, "because everyone in it is miserable. I read the sign."

The sign, in fact, said GRIMSBY HOLLOW in letters that had once been gold and were now the color of old teeth. Below it someone had painted, in a fit of civic despair or dark humor, WELCOME TO THE END OF THE WORLD — LAST ONE OUT TURN OFF THE LIGHTS.

The town clung to the side of a mountain like a burr to a dog's coat. Mine shafts pocked its slopes, their timber frames leaning like tired old men telling the same story over and over. The air tasted of coal dust and woodsmoke and something underneath both—a sourness Astrid had been noticing for weeks now, ever since Ashfield. The taste of land forgetting how to be alive.

Sera pulled her hood lower. Three days out of the fens, her voice had returned in fragments, but her face still did most of her talking. Right now it said: *I hate towns.*

"I know," Astrid murmured. "But the map says he's here, and the map hasn't lied to us yet."

The map, tucked inside her coat against the cold, gave a faint shiver against her ribs—the living ink stirring, she'd learned, when they drew near one of its marked places. It had done this outside the drowned chapel too, trembling like a compass needle finding north. Somewhere in this grim little town was a drummer of the Hollow Choir.

They found him where the map's ink had darkest: the Drowned Rat, the only tavern on Coal Street that was still open, its windows fogged with breath and argument.

* * *

The Drowned Rat smelled of spilled ale and wet wool and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the beams. Half the tables sat empty. The other half held men whose faces had the gray undertone of people who spent their lives underground and never quite got all the way back out of it.

And at the corner table, alone, sat a mountain of a man with a drummer's forearms and a drunkard's eyes.

Astrid knew him before she knew why she knew him. It was the hands. Even wrapped around a mug, even unsteady, they had a rhythm to them—fingers tapping the wood in patterns too complex for idle habit. *Da-dum-da-da-dum.* A heartbeat with opinions.

She slid onto the bench across from him before her courage could reconsider.

He looked up. His eyes took her in—the travel-worn coat, the fen-mud still on her boots—and dismissed her entirely. "Table's taken, girl."

"It's not. I checked."

That earned her a flicker of attention. Up close, Tam Breck was younger than his bulk suggested—forty, maybe, with a face that had stopped bothering to age gracefully somewhere around thirty-five and had settled into sullenness instead. A beard going gray at the chin. And around his neck, half-hidden by his collar, a cord.

Astrid's heart stuttered. She knew that cord. Every guild apprentice learned to recognize it: a drumstick hung on leather, worn smooth by years of grip. Choir members wore their instruments' tokens even when the instruments themselves were ash.

"You're Tam Breck," she said quietly.

The mug stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a long moment the tavern noise seemed to bend around them—the conversations dimming, the fire popping, some instinct in the room knowing that something was about to happen near the corner table.

Then Tam set the mug down with exaggerated care, the way very large men do when they want you to understand how much restraint is costing them. "Get out."

"My name is Astrid Halloran. I was apprenticed to the Lanternwright Guild, last of them, and I have a map that shows seven resting places of the Hollow Choir, and yours is the second—"

"*Get out.*" Not shouted. Worse than shouted—flat, final, the voice of a man closing a door on a room with himself inside it.

"No," Astrid said.

Sera, beside her, made a small sound. When Astrid glanced over, Sera was staring at Tam with an expression Astrid hadn't seen on her before—not hope exactly, but its raw material, the ore that hope gets smelted from.

Tam saw it too. Whatever he saw in Sera's face, it hit him like a fist. He stood up so fast the bench shrieked across the floorboards, and for one absurd moment Astrid thought he might actually strike them.

Instead he said, in a voice like gravel sliding, "You don't get to come here. Either of you. You don't get to sit in front of me with your *faces*—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Out. Now. Or I'll put you out."

"He can't," Sera said softly. It was the longest sentence Astrid had heard from her since the chapel. "He won't. I remember him. He carried the little ones across the ford at Silvermere when the water rose. He complained the whole time. About the water, the children, his boots." Her mouth trembled into something almost like a smile. "He complained beautifully. In rhythm."

Tam Breck looked at Sera Ilvane the way a man looks at a ghost he has spent ten years trying not to dream about.

Then he knocked back the rest of his ale, slammed down three coppers, and walked out into the rain without another word.

* * *

They found him an hour later at the edge of the mine slag, sitting on a fallen beam, letting the drizzle soak through a shirt that had clearly given up on protecting anyone from anything.

Astrid sat down beside him. He didn't tell her to leave, which she chose to interpret as progress.

"I'm not going to ask you to forgive yourself," she said.

Tam snorted. Rain dripped off his nose. "Generous of you."

"I'm not going to ask anything, actually. I'm going to tell you something, and then you can do whatever you like with it." She pulled her coat tighter. "Do you know what's happening to the seasons?"

"The seasons are the seasons. Bad year. Happens."

"Not like this. Ashfield's crops came up black. Children in three villages have stopped dreaming—they sleep like stones and wake like stones. The winters are getting longer and nobody's writing it down because everyone thinks someone else is keeping count." She turned to look at him. "The covenant between the human realm and the Faeheart is broken, Tam. It broke when the Choir was scattered. It breaks a little more every day. And the map says there are seven voices left who could sing it whole again, and I've found one, and the map says you're the other."

Silence, except the rain ticking on the slag heaps.

"You know what my music did?" Tam said finally. His voice had changed—lower, rougher, the gravel ground finer. "During the purges. You know what they had us do, we who wouldn't run? They marched us through Corvane singing the covenant-song, to show the people magic wasn't dead yet. A show of defiance. And a boy heard us. Sixteen, maybe. Heard the song and felt the covenant wake in him, felt the Faeheart answer, and he—"

His hands, which had been tapping the beam all along, went still.

"—he lit up. That's the only word for it. Lit up like a lantern, wild-light pouring off him, because the song called to whatever slept in his blood and it woke up hungry. And the Inquisitor's men saw. And they hanged him from the market bell-tower with the whole city watching, and they made us keep singing while he kicked, because stopping would have been worse, they said. Defiance, they said." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Astrid had heard in months. "My music killed him. My rhythm in his blood. So no, girl. No map. No choir. You can find your seventh voice somewhere else."

Astrid was quiet for a long time. The rain filled it up.

"That boy's name," she said at last. "Do you remember it?"

Tam's head came around sharply. "What kind of question—"

"Do you remember his name?"

"...Pell. Pell Arden." He said it like a wound reopening. "Of course I remember it. I say it every night. It's the last thing I—"

"Then you've already been keeping the count," Astrid said. "Ten years, one name, every night. That's more remembering than the whole rest of Corvane does for anyone." She stood, brushing rain from her coat, and looked down at him—not with pity, which she knew he'd hate, and not with judgment, which he'd expect. Just with the truth, laid out plain. "I'm not asking you to sing for forgiveness. I'm not sure there is any, for either of us—I sold charms to terrified people for three years and told myself it was harmless, and maybe it was, and maybe it was just me hiding. I don't know anymore. What I know is the world is dying quietly, Tam, and there are five voices after you, and every one of them will have a reason not to come. You're allowed yours. But the seasons don't care about our reasons, and neither does the Faeheart, and somebody has to stand up and make the sound anyway. Somebody always has to. That's the whole job."

She turned to go.

"Astrid Halloran."

She stopped. She hadn't told him her surname.

Tam Breck was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—something between recognition and dread. "Halloran," he said again. "There was a Halloran in the Choir. Before my time. A singer they wrote songs *about*, back when writing songs about singers didn't get you hanged."

"My mother," Astrid said quietly. "Maerin Halloran. I know."

The big man stared at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—but it was a different laugh this time, cracked and wondering, and he dragged a hand down his wet beard.

"Of course you are," he said. "Of course the last one's a Halloran. The Faeheart always did have a sense of humor." He pushed himself up off the beam, swaying slightly, and stood there in the rain looking at the mine entrances pocking the dark hillside. "I haven't touched a drum in eleven years. There's one in my house under the floorboards. I look at the boards every night. Some nights I take the plank up and look at it and put it back."

"And tonight?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Tonight I'll take it up," he said finally, "and I'll leave it out. That's all I'm promising. Leaving it out." He turned and started toward the town, then paused. "Your friend. Ilvane. Is she eating? She looks like a strong wind would file a complaint about her."

"She's eating," Astrid said, smiling despite everything. "Slowly."

"Hm." He grunted—an actual rhythmic grunt, *da-dum*, though Astrid suspected he didn't know he'd done it. "Tell her... tell her the ford at Silvermere dried up two years ago. Tell her the river's gone. She'll want to know."

"Why?"

"Because she sang that river awake when she was nine years old," Tam said, "and if it's dead, she'll want to grieve it properly. We're Choir, girl. Grieving properly is half the job."

He walked away through the rain, a big man moving with a strange new carefulness, as if the ground beneath him had begun keeping time.

* * *

They left Grimsby Hollow at dawn, three travelers instead of two, and the map against Astrid's ribs burned warm as a second heartbeat.

Tam rode behind them on a borrowed mule, the drum strapped across his back under oilcloth, saying nothing, complaining about nothing—which Sera whispered was the loudest thing he'd done in eleven years.

By midday the mountains fell away behind them and the road opened east, toward the lowlands, toward the third mark on the map. Astrid unrolled it across her saddle to check the ink, and frowned.

The third resting place had changed.

Where yesterday the living ink had shown a modest farmhouse in the countryside south of Corvane, it now showed a grand estate—walled, gated, familiar in a way that made her stomach drop. She knew those grounds. She'd grown up knowing them.

The third voice lived at the house of Master Corren.

Behind her, Tam's mule stumbled, and he swore in perfect four-four time.
