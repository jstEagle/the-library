## 10. Veyra Closes In

The fen did not want her there.

Veyra had interrogated men who swore they didn't want her in their houses either, and she had learned long ago that the trick was to behave as though you'd been invited. So she rode into the marshes at the head of six soldiers with the unhurried air of a woman arriving for supper, and she let the fen hiss and suck and complain all it liked.

"Cheerful place," said Sergeant Doss beside her. He was a broad, patient man who had followed her through nine years of arrests and had developed, she suspected, a private taxonomy of her moods. This one he would file under *bad*.

"It's honest," Veyra said. "Everything here is exactly what it appears to be. Mud. Water. Things that bite."

"That last one's true of Corvane too, ma'am."

"Yes, but in Corvane they wear better boots." She reined her horse around a pool that gleamed an unhealthy green. "Keep your eyes open. The witch's hut is supposed to stand on stilts above the deep channel. The locals call her Maren of the Reeds. They also call her *nothing to do with us, please don't burn our roofs*, which is the more useful name."

They found the hut an hour past midday, leaning over black water like an old woman peering down a well. Dried herbs hung from its eaves in bundles. A wind chime made of fish bones turned slowly in a wind Veyra could not feel.

"She knows we're coming," Doss murmured.

"Of course she does. Witches always know. It's practically the entrance requirement." Veyra dismounted and handed him her reins. "Wait here. If I'm not back by dark, burn nothing, arrest no one, and go home and tell the Provost I died of mud."

"And if you're not back by tomorrow?"

"Then be very polite to whoever lives here next."

She crossed the plank walk alone. The door opened before she knocked — of course it did — and there stood the witch: small, brown as a chestnut, with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the color of pond ice. She looked Veyra up and down with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a chicken.

"You're the Inquisitor," the witch said. "You've got the look. All patience on the outside and a ledger on the inside."

"That's the kindest description anyone's given me this year. May I come in? The alternative involves my soldiers, and they're much less interesting conversationalists than I am."

The witch stepped aside. "Wipe your boots. I just swept three hundred years ago and I've been meaning to get to it since."

Inside smelled of smoke, river water, and something sweeter underneath — crushed flowers, perhaps, or memory. Veyra took the offered stool and accepted tea she did not entirely trust, because refusing would have been rude and rudeness was a poor opening move. The tea tasted of honey and iron.

"You know why I'm here," Veyra said.

"I know why you *think* you're here. You think you're chasing a charm-seller who's run off north with two singers and a drum." Maren settled across from her, hands folded. "That's the small version of the story. There's a larger version. You won't like it."

"I rarely like anything before breakfast. Fortunately I ate."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because something in her face eased — not into warmth, exactly, but into a kind of weary respect, the way one soldier regards another across a hopeless position.

"You were at the purges," the witch said. Not a question.

"I was twenty-two. I held the lanterns while others did the arresting. Later I did the arresting myself." Veyra kept her voice level. "I have never pretended otherwise."

"No. That's what makes you dangerous. The ones who pretend sleep well." Maren leaned forward. "Ask me what the covenant does, Inquisitor. Ask properly. Because I think you've spent your whole career hunting magic without once asking what it was *for*."

Veyra set down her cup. "Very well. What does the covenant do?"

And Maren told her.

* * *

She told it plainly, without theater, which was somehow worse. Long ago — before Corvane had walls, before the guilds, before anyone thought to write the word *law* — the world had been one cloth, wild and human woven through each other. The Faeheart was the loom. And the covenant was the song that kept the weave taut: human voices and fae voices together, season answering season, dream answering dream. The Hollow Choir had been the mortal half of that song. Seven singers, then dozens, then generations of them, carrying the melody so the Faeheart would stay a neighbor instead of becoming a flood.

"When the Choir was scattered," Maren said, "the song didn't stop all at once. Songs are stubborn things. It frayed. Like rope. And everyone congratulated themselves on how quiet the world had gotten." Her pond-ice eyes fixed on Veyra. "You've noticed the quiet, haven't you? The winters that come late and leave late. The crops that grow black at the root. The children who've stopped dreaming."

"We assumed blight," Veyra said. "Bad seasons. Coincidence."

"Coincidence." Maren laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Three years of coincidence, spreading outward from the places where the singers used to stand. No, Inquisitor. Here is what your Provost's tidy laws never accounted for. The Faeheart isn't dying. It's *pressing*. A dammed river doesn't vanish; it rises. Every year the covenant holds weaker, the wall between the wild and the human grows thinner, and the wildness starts to seep through. Not gently. Not prettily. Wolves that remember being gods. Rivers that change their minds about which way is downhill. Forests that walk when they're insulted." She sipped her tea. "Your grandchildren, if you have any, will live in a country where the map has to be redrawn every morning. Unless the song comes back."

Veyra sat very still. She was aware, distantly, of the fish-bone chime turning outside, of the fire ticking, of her own heartbeat conducting its usual dull business. Inside her, something that had stood straight for fifteen years developed, quietly, a crack.

"The law says magic is a corruption," she said at last. "I have arrested people on that law. Hanged some of them."

"I know. I watched from the reeds, some years." Maren said it without accusation, which was the most accusatory thing possible. "You weren't wrong to keep order. Order matters. Bread matters. But you were handed a lie wrapped in a truth, child, and you carried it so long you forgot to weigh it. Magic can corrupt — so can fire, so can love, so can bread if you eat enough of it. The law didn't ban corruption. It banned the *song*. And now the bill's come due, and it's addressed to everyone."

"The woman I'm chasing," Veyra said slowly. "Halloran. The charm-seller. She believes all this?"

"She believes worse. She believes she can fix it." Maren smiled, and for a moment looked every one of her three hundred swept years. "Which is either madness or hope, and I've never learned to tell them apart at a distance."

* * *

Veyra walked out at dusk and stood on the plank walk looking at the water, which reflected a sky going the color of a bruise healing.

Doss waited by the horses, radiating the careful silence of a man who knew better than to ask questions first. He lasted four minutes, which for Doss was heroic.

"Ma'am? Do we arrest her?"

Veyra opened her mouth to say yes. It was the correct answer. It was the answer the law required, the answer she had given a thousand times in a thousand rooms, the answer that had built her career brick by patient brick.

"Maren of the Reeds," she said instead, "is an old woman who grows impossible flowers and tells stories. I found no evidence of unlawful practice. We'll note the interview and move on."

Doss blinked. In nine years she had never once declined an arrest within reach. "Ma'am —"

"She makes a decent cup of tea, Sergeant. Write that down under *mitigating circumstances*." She mounted. "We ride south. There's a mining town called Greyhollow on the singers' likely road. If Breck the drummer passed through, someone will have seen him, and drunk men are the easiest witnesses in the world to find. They're the only ones who talk to strangers on purpose."

They rode. But Veyra rode differently than she had come — slower, her eyes moving constantly to the edges of the fen, to the pools too green, to a stand of trees whose branches leaned, she could almost swear, a half-degree toward the road. Watching. Or listening.

At the fen's edge, where the solid ground began, she stopped and looked back. The witch's hut stood on its stilts above the dark water, and in its window a light burned steady, like a held note.

*If the song doesn't come back,* Maren had said, walking her out, *the wall doesn't fall all at once either. First come the dreams. Then the beasts. Then the maps stop working. You'll have time to see it coming, Inquisitor. That's the cruelest part. You'll have time.*

Veyra had spent her life believing that justice was a scale: crimes weighed, penalties measured, the world balanced. But scales presumed a stable floor beneath them. And if the floor itself was singing — or had stopped singing —

"Ma'am," Doss said cautiously, "you're doing the thing where you stare at nothing and the rest of us age a year."

"Hm." She gathered her reins. "Sergeant, a hypothetical. Suppose you learned that a law you'd enforced your whole life was protecting people from a knife — but that the knife was also holding up the roof."

Doss considered this with the gravity he brought to everything, including soup. "I'd want to know who sharpened the knife, ma'am. And why they stopped."

"Yes," Veyra said softly. "So would I."

She turned her horse south toward Greyhollow, toward the drummer, toward the trail of a charm-seller who was running headlong toward an answer Veyra was only beginning to understand was a question. Behind her the fen breathed out its green dusk, and somewhere far off — so far she might have imagined it, and told herself firmly that she had — something that was not quite a bird and not quite a bell sang three notes, and waited, as if expecting a reply.

Veyra did not reply. But she did not order it hunted down either, and that, she reflected as the dark came on, was how cracks began.
