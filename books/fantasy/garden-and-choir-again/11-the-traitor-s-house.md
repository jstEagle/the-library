## 11. The Traitor's House

The map had a sense of humor. Astrid was becoming sure of it.

For three days it had led them along the river road east of Corvane, its living ink crawling and rearranging itself each dawn like a spider redrawing its web, and for three days the destination it promised had grown more familiar and more impossible by degrees. First it was simply *north of the willow bend*. Then it was *the house with the blue shutters*. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, when the ink settled at last into something still and final, it was unmistakably, insultingly clear:

Master Corren Aldous's estate sat on the hill ahead of them, all pale stone and tidy hedges, its chimneys breathing gentle smoke into a sky the color of old dishwater.

"No," said Tam Breck.

"Yes," said Astrid.

"No. Absolutely not. The map's wrong. Maps lie. I once followed a map to a treasure and found a tax collector."

"The map doesn't lie." Astrid held it up so the ink could defend itself. The little silver line that marked their third voice pulsed patiently beneath the drawn windows of the drawn house, as if knocking. "It's never lied yet. It got us through the fen. It got us to Tam's mine before his shift started, which I maintain was a small miracle."

"That was luck."

"It was ink that knew where you'd be. That's not luck, that's *intent*."

Tam folded his arms over his drum, which he had not let out of arm's reach since the mining town, the way a man holds a thing he both hates and cannot put down. Beside him, Sera Ilvane stood very still, looking up at the hilltop house with an expression Astrid couldn't read—grief, maybe, or recognition. Since the fen, since her single recovered word, Sera spoke perhaps four times a day, but she communicated plenty. Right now what she communicated was: *I know this place.*

"You've been here," Astrid said softly.

Sera nodded. She touched two fingers to her own throat, then pointed at the house, then made a shape in the air—a slow arc, like a conductor drawing a phrase of music.

"He sang here," Astrid translated. "In this house."

Another nod. Then Sera's hand went to her throat again, and pressed there, hard, and her face did something complicated and closed, and Astrid understood without words: *he sang here, and then he stopped, and then he helped make sure no one else ever would.*

The anger came up in Astrid like water finding a crack. Three years of fear, three years of selling fever-charms out of a cellar with her sleeve pulled down over her guild mark, three years of watching the old woman from the market hang for nothing worse than kindness—and here was the house where one of the men responsible drank good wine behind tidy hedges.

"Stay here," she said. "Both of you. If I'm not back by dark—"

"We're coming," said Tam.

"It might be a trap."

"Then you shouldn't go alone into it." He shrugged, massive and immovable. "Besides. If he's really a cantor, if he's really one of ours—" his jaw worked around the word *ours* like a stone, "—then someone who isn't you should be there to see his face when you say it out loud."

Astrid looked at Sera. Sera looked back, and then, deliberately, took one step forward up the hill. It was the most eloquent argument she'd made in weeks.

"Fine," Astrid said. "But let me do the talking."

"Wasn't planning on talking," said Tam. "Was planning on glaring."

"You're very good at that."

"I've had practice."

* * *

The gate was unlocked.

That was the first wrongness. Corren Aldous, magistrate of the eastern circuit, keeper of a household that had survived three purges and a famine without a scratch, did not leave his gate unlocked. Astrid stood before it with her hand on the cold iron and felt her guild training wake up all along her spine—the old lessons, the ones about doors and silences and the stories houses tell. An unlocked gate was either carelessness or invitation, and Corren had never once in six years of apprenticeship been careless.

The garden inside was worse than the gate. It was beautiful. In a province where the orchards grew black fruit and the wheat came up gray as ash, Corren's garden was a riot of late roses and stubborn green, bees working the lavender with the drowsy industry of creatures who had not heard the news that the world was ending. Magic kept it, of course. Old magic, patient magic, the kind that seeps into soil over decades until the ground itself forgets how to be barren. Astrid recognized the work the way you recognize handwriting.

She had learned that handwriting. Some of it was hers.

"Oh," said Tam quietly, beside her. "Oh, that's foul."

"I know."

"He's been *watering his roses* while children stop dreaming."

"I said I know, Tam."

They crossed the lawn. The front door, too, stood ajar. Beyond it, a hallway lined with books, and at the end of the hallway, a study door thrown open onto firelight, and a voice—deep, unhurried, terribly familiar—saying:

"The kettle's just boiled. Come in out of the cold, Astrid. All three of you. And wipe your boots; Ilvane always tracked mud, and some habits deserve to outlive us."

* * *

The study smelled of tea and lamp oil and, underneath it, faintly, of bell-bronze. Corren sat in a wingback chair by the fire, and time had not been gentle with him in the way that matters—he was still broad, still upright, still possessed of the voice that could fill a lecture hall—but his hair had gone entirely white, and his hands, wrapped around his teacup, trembled in a way Astrid had never seen them tremble, not even the day the Guildhall burned.

He looked at her the way a man looks at a debt he has been avoiding.

"Astrid Halloran," he said. "Last apprentice of the Lanternwright Guild. Do you know, I used to say your name every night for the first year. Like a prayer. Or an apology. I stopped being able to tell which."

"You knew we were coming," Astrid said.

"The map told me." He gestured, and Astrid saw it lying open on his desk—the twin of hers, or rather the original, older, its ink faded to brown but still faintly moving, like something sleeping. "It arrived eleven months ago. Wrapped in oilcloth, left on my doorstep by no one I could find. I burned it twice. It came back three times. Eventually I understood it wasn't asking permission." His mouth twisted. "Living ink. The Choir's last joke. We always did have a cruel sense of humor."

"You were a cantor." It wasn't a question. She needed to hear him say it anyway.

Something moved behind Corren's eyes—relief, she thought, horribly. Relief that it was finally being said.

"I was the finest cantor the Hollow Choir ever produced," he said. "That is not vanity; it is inventory. I could hold the low line alone for the whole of the Vespers verse. When I sang the binding, birds would land on the chapel roof to listen. Sera can tell you. Sera stood next to me for nine years." He glanced at her, standing silent in the doorway with Tam, and his face aged another decade. "Ilvane. You look well. The garden at the drowned chapel—is it yours?"

Sera's chin lifted. Her silence was louder than anything Tam could have glared.

"No," Corren said quietly. "Of course it isn't. Nothing grows for me anymore. I only keep the habit." He set down his cup. "Ask me, Astrid. Whatever you climbed this hill to ask me. Ask it now, while my nerve is holding, because I promise you it won't hold long."

"Why?" The word came out of her rougher than she intended. Six years of rehearsed composure, gone in one syllable. "Why did you do it? You *knew*. You sang the covenant with your own throat. You knew what the silence would cost—the seasons, the dreams, the Faeheart bleeding wild into the world. You knew better than any magistrate in Corvane has ever known anything, and you stood up in front of the Assembly and you told them magic was a plague. You wrote half the statutes yourself. Children hang under laws you drafted, Master Corren. *I* nearly hung under them. So I am asking you, and I want the truth and not the comfortable version: why?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted a small eternity.

"Because it was already dead," Corren said.

Astrid blinked. "What?"

"The covenant. It was already dead, Astrid. Before the purges, before the statutes, before any of it." He rose, slowly, joints cracking, and went to the window overlooking his impossible garden. "You were seven when it began failing. Too young to remember the winter that didn't end. I was forty-one, and I stood in the choir loft of the Hollow Chapel and I sang the binding verse, and do you know what happened? *Nothing.* For the first time in eight hundred years, nothing. The Faeheart didn't answer. Not weakly. Not faintly. *Not at all.* Seven voices, then fourteen, then the full choir, singing until our throats bled, and the wild realm beyond the veil was as silent as a grave."

"That's not—" Astrid started.

"It is true," Sera said.

Everyone turned. It was the longest sentence she'd spoken since the fen. Her voice came out rusted, but steady.

"We thought it was us," she rasped. "We thought we'd failed. We tried everything. New harmonies. Blood-offerings. Tam drummed for three days without stopping." Her hand found Tam's arm. "And then the Inquisition came, and Corren—" her throat worked, "—Corren went to the Assembly and told them the truth. That the covenant was broken past mending. And they heard only the part they wanted. That magic had *failed*. That it was finished, and dangerous, and godless, and could be killed safely because it was already dying."

"They took my testimony," Corren said, still facing the window, "and they built gallows with it. Yes. I know. I have read every transcript of every trial my words enabled. I keep them in that cabinet." He pointed, and his finger shook. "Eleven volumes. I read one every year on the anniversary. It is my penance and it is not enough and I know that too."

"But the law," Astrid whispered. "You could have fought it. You could have—"

"With what?" He turned, and now she saw it, the thing under the white hair and the trembling hands: exhaustion so total it had calcified into calm. "Astrid. I was one man whose magic had just proven useless in front of the entire Assembly. They offered me a choice that night—I've never told anyone this—not Ilvane, not anyone. Stand with the condemned and burn with them, uselessly, or help shape the transition so that it was orderly instead of apocalyptic. So that hedge-witches got trials instead of mobs. So that the guild archives were *confiscated* instead of burned in the streets. So that when they came for the Lanternwrights, they came with warrants instead of torches." His voice cracked, just once, on the word warrants. "I told myself order was mercy. I told myself that for twenty years. I am telling you now, in this room, with whatever honesty I have left: I do not know anymore whether I saved anything worth saving, or merely arranged the furniture in a burning house."

The anger in Astrid's chest was still there—it would not die, it had earned its keep—but something else had grown up around it, unwelcome and enormous. Understanding. She hated it. Understanding was so much heavier than hatred.

"The seasons are failing again," she said. "Worse than before. Crops going black. Children not dreaming. And the map says the covenant can be reforged. The Choir can be gathered. Seven resting places, and we've found three—you're the third. So it wasn't dead, Corren. Whatever you heard in that chapel, whatever silence you sang into—"

"It wasn't dead," Corren agreed. "It was *listening*. There is a difference, and I have had twenty years to learn it, and I would give both hands to have learned it faster." He crossed to the desk, opened a drawer, and lifted out something wrapped in flannel. When he unwrapped it, the firelight caught bronze: a cantor's bell, small enough to fit in a palm, old enough to predate the guild, humming faintly with a note below hearing. "The covenant didn't die that winter. It withdrew. The Faeheart pulled back behind its veil and went quiet the way a hurt animal goes quiet—to see what we would do. And what we did, all of us, every soul who informed and testified and signed and looked away, was show it exactly why it had withdrawn in the first place."

He held the bell out to her.

"So the question is not why I betrayed the guild, Astrid. Ask me that on the road, if we live to have the road. The question is whether you can stand in this room, knowing everything I've told you, and still take the third voice with you to the Faeheart. Because you need it. Because the song needs a low line under it or the high voices tear themselves apart—Ilva­ne knows, ask her. Because I am the finest cantor the Hollow Choir ever produced, and I am also the man who built your gallows, and both of those things are true at once, and I have not the slightest idea how you carry that. I only know that I have carried it for twenty years, and it does not get lighter."

Astrid looked at the bell. She looked at his hands, trembling around the offering. Behind her, Tam had gone very still, and Sera had come forward to stand at her shoulder, and Sera was nodding—slowly, painfully, the way trees nod in wind.

And that was when they heard the horses.

Hoofbeats on the river road, many of them, moving fast, and beneath the sound of hooves a thinner sound that Astrid felt in her teeth before her ears caught it: the particular dissonance of warded lanterns, the kind the Inquisition carried, the kind that ate spells out of the air.

Corren closed his eyes. "Veyra," he said. "She requested my testimony three days ago regarding a fugitive charm-seller. I delayed her. I have run out of delays." He opened his eyes, and pressed the bell into Astrid's hand, folding her fingers over it with both of his. "Back door, through the garden, the wall gap behind the beehives—the bees are mine, they'll part for you. Go north. Don't look back."

"And you?" Tam demanded.

Corren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Astrid had seen since the old woman in the market square.

"I," he said, straightening his magistrate's collar with hands that no longer shook at all, "am going to answer my door."
