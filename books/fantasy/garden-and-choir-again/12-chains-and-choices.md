## 12. Chains and Choices

The letter arrived at supper, as bad news always does—slid under the door of Corren Halloran's study by a servant who knew better than to knock.

He read it twice. Then a third time, standing at the window while his mutton went cold on the desk behind him, because the words did not improve with repetition but he kept hoping they might.

*The girl who passed through Ashfield is your former apprentice. The Inquisitor Veyra requests your counsel regarding her apprehension.*

*Requests.* Veyra was not a woman who requested. She was a woman who informed you that something had already been decided and gave you the courtesy of being told before it happened to you. If she was requesting counsel, it meant she wanted him complicit. It meant she wanted the magistrate of Corvane's signature on the order, so that when the history books were written—and they would be written; Veyra thought in terms of history books—the arrest of the last Lanternwright would bear two names.

Corren set down the letter and looked at his hands.

They were good hands. He had been told so all his life. A magistrate's hands, a reasonable man's hands, hands that had signed the Act of Severance eleven years ago with a steady grip and a clear conscience—or what he had told himself was a conscience, which in the dark turned out to be nearly the same thing and in the light was nothing like it at all.

Because here was the truth he kept folded up small, the way one folds a letter one intends never to reread: Astrid had been right. Not about everything—she had been nineteen and furious and right the way the young are right, entirely and uselessly—but about the covenant. About the seasons. About the guild. He had known the song was failing before he helped outlaw the singing of it. He had stood in the Guildhall of the Lanternwrights, in the last winter the frost came honest, and heard the choir thin, and understood that the wild was going quiet, and made his choice: better an ordered dying than a lawless living. Better to bind magic in chains than watch it thrash and take the city down with it.

And then, to make the chains hold, he had given them the names of every member of his own guild.

His mutton was definitely cold now. He ate it anyway. Guilt, he had learned, was no excuse for waste.

* * *

Astrid came to him in chains, which was Veyra's idea of diplomacy.

"She walked into my cells without a fight," Veyra said, standing in Corren's study with her hands clasped behind her back, as though she were admiring his bookshelves. "Which interests me more than if she'd fought. People who surrender are people who believe they've already lost, or people who are buying time. I haven't decided which she is."

"Perhaps," Corren said carefully, "she is simply tired."

"Everyone is tired, Magistrate. Tiredness is not a defense." Veyra turned from the bookshelves. She had the kind of face that made kindness look like an accusation, which was unfair to her, since she was frequently kind. She had brought the prisoner water without being asked. She had ordered the manacles loosened when Astrid's wrists bled. And she had come here first, before the formal proceedings, because she wanted something from him and preferred to take it gently.

"I need your testimony," she said. "You trained her. You know her methods, her associates, her intentions. The court will want to hear it from you."

"And if my testimony is that she is no danger to anyone?"

"Then I will write it down in my neat hand," said Veyra, "and it will be read aloud at her trial, and it will change nothing whatsoever, and we will both have done our duty." She paused at the door. "You look unwell, Magistrate. You should sleep."

He did not sleep.

* * *

They kept Astrid in the holding cell beneath the courthouse—not the deep cells, not yet, though those waited below with their silence-wards like held breath. Corren went down at midnight with a lantern and a plate of food he had no business bringing and every intention of bringing anyway.

She sat on the cot with her back against the wall, and when the light reached her she looked up, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there are conversations that must be had entirely in silence first or not at all.

"You've got old," she said finally.

"You've got freckles."

"That's not new."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't." He set the plate on the floor and slid it through the bars. Bread, cheese, an apple. Her favorite apple, the small tart ones from the orchard at his country house, which meant he had sent a servant out at some ungodly hour, which meant she noticed, which meant it counted for less than nothing and everything at once.

"Corren." She picked up the apple but didn't eat it. Just held it, turning it over, the way she used to turn over difficult charms when she was trying to understand them. "Why did you do it?"

"The Act?"

"All of it. The names. You gave them Sera Ilvane's name. Tam Breck's. You sang with them, and then you—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it, and he hated that she had learned to master it somewhere he hadn't been to teach her.

"Because I believed the covenant was already dead," he said. It was the true answer. He had rehearsed the false answers for eleven years and found, arriving at this moment, that he could not stomach any of them. "I stood in the Guildhall and listened to the choir failing and I thought: this is a funeral, and funerals need order, and order needs law. I thought if I bound the magic, I could bound the grief too. Keep people from tearing each other apart over something already lost."

"And instead you killed it faster."

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long time. There was no hatred in it, which was somehow worse. Hatred he could have carried. What she gave him instead was the particular pity of a student for a teacher who has failed an examination everyone else passed.

"The map found me," she said. "The courier died getting it to me. Seven voices left, Corren. Seven, out of a hundred and forty. And the seasons are coming apart, and you sit up there signing grain tariffs."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Do you actually? Because I used to think you were a coward. That was easier. But cowards don't send apples."

He laughed—a short, broken sound, entirely involuntary—and pressed his hand flat against the cold bar. "Go to sleep, Astrid."

"I'm not tired."

"Liar," he said gently, using her own word back at her, and she almost smiled, and he climbed the stairs with his heart in pieces and began, for the first time in eleven years, to think about doing something rather than merely regretting it.

* * *

The relics lived in a locked cabinet in his cellar, where he had kept them the way a man keeps a snake he cannot bring himself to kill and cannot bring himself to release.

He had surrendered most of the guild's effects during the purges—the great songbooks, the resonance bowls, the choir robes with their stitched lanterns. But a few things had passed through his hands as magistrate, items entered into evidence and misfiled, and misfiling, he had discovered, required only a steady hand and a clear conscience, and he had both, or the things he called by those names.

He unlocked the cabinet now and laid them out on the workbench one by one, like a man setting a table for ghosts.

A tuning fork of white iron, its tines worn smooth by forty years of cantors' thumbs. His thumb among them. He struck it against the bench and it hummed, faintly, a note that seemed to lean toward something just out of earshot.

A scrap of choir robe, singed at the hem. Tam Breck's, torn off him the night of the arrest. Tam had fought. Tam had always fought, even as a boy, even at rehearsal, arguing about tempo with the fervor other men reserved for war.

A bell.

Corren's hand stopped above it.

It was a small thing, a cantor's handbell no bigger than a pear, cast in bronze gone green at the lip, with a handle wrapped in leather so old it had gone soft as skin. Every cantor of the Lanternwrights carried one. It was not for music, exactly—it was for *calling*. When the choir gathered to renew the covenant, the cantor rang the bell three times, and the bell remembered every voice that had ever answered it, and called them home across any distance, through any silence.

He had rung it for the last time eleven years ago, in the Guildhall, and no one had come, because he had already given the soldiers the schedule of the gathering. He had rung it knowing. He had stood there ringing and ringing into an empty hall, and the emptiness had been his own work, and he had put the bell in an evidence box and closed the lid and become, very efficiently, a magistrate.

He picked it up. It was lighter than he remembered. Grief usually is; it's the carrying that weighs.

"Well," he said aloud, to the empty cellar, because talking to oneself was the privilege of the sleepless, "this is either redemption or the stupidest thing I have ever done, and I once set fire to a rehearsal hall trying to demonstrate harmonic resonance, so the bar is high."

The bell did not answer. Bells are terrible conversationalists. But when he tilted it, very slightly, something inside it shifted—a soft, bronze whisper, like a sleeper turning over—and Corren felt, distinctly, the sensation of being *listened to*.

He sat down on the workbench stool and did not move for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic he could not escape. If he did nothing, Astrid would be tried, convicted, and hanged, and the seasons would continue to rot, and he would live out his days as a respected man in a freezing world, which was a sentence worse than hanging and longer. If he testified for her, she would still hang—Veyra had been quite clear, and Veyra was many things but never unclear—and he would have soiled his reputation for nothing, which was the worst of all available trades: paying full price for goods never delivered.

Or.

Or he could open the cellar door at the hour when even the guards drank, and walk down the corridor with a lantern and a key ring, and commit, at fifty-eight years old, his second great crime—which was really his first crime, corrected, and therefore not a crime at all except in the eyes of the law, and the law, he reflected, had been wrong before. He had made sure of it personally.

The trouble, he thought, turning the bell over in his hands, was that redemption purchased late arrives damaged. No one gives you credit for doing the right thing after you've done the wrong thing thoroughly and for a decade. Astrid would not thank him. She should not thank him. Gratitude for a belated decency is like thanking a man for returning your stolen horse lame.

He would do it anyway. Not because it would be forgiven—it would not, it must not—but because the alternative was to die respectable, and he had seen respectability up close for eleven years, and it smelled like cold mutton and sounded like an empty hall.

* * *

At half past three in the morning, Corren Halloran, Magistrate of Corvane, holder of the Seal of the Northern Bench, let himself into the holding cell corridor with a stolen key ring and the gait of a man walking to his own execution and finding, to his surprise, that he minds it less than expected.

Astrid was awake. Of course she was awake. She was twenty-two and condemned and awake, and when the cell door swung open she was on her feet with a sharpened spoon in her fist before he'd finished the third step, which pleased him obscurely. Good instincts. He'd taught her those.

"It's me."

"I can see it's you." She didn't lower the spoon. "This is a trick. This is a test. You're going to walk me into a squad of soldiers and call it mercy."

"If I wanted you hanged," Corren said, "I need only have stayed in bed. Come along. Quickly. The guard changes at four and Wexley drinks, which is the single reliable fact in municipal administration."

She searched his face for the trap and found, apparently, only an exhausted old man holding a lantern, because she lowered the spoon—though she kept it, tucked in her sleeve, which pleased him further.

"Why?" she asked.

He held out the bell.

She looked at it and went very still. "That's a cantor's bell."

"It's *the* cantor's bell. Mine." He pressed it into her hands and closed her fingers around it. "Ring it three times when you're ready to gather them. It remembers every voice that ever answered the covenant. Including mine." His voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and he cleared his throat and pretended it hadn't. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I want to be extremely clear about that, because if you forgive me I shall lose my nerve entirely, and I am relying on my nerve to get us both out of this building."

"Us both?"

"I'm staying," he said. "Someone must be sitting at this desk when they discover the empty cell, and it should be the man responsible. It's the first honest piece of paperwork I'll have filed in eleven years." He was already moving, guiding her down the corridor, past the snoring Wexley, toward the coal chute that opened onto the alley behind the courthouse—a route he had chosen at two o'clock and rechosen four times since. "There's a horse in the mews, gray, bad-tempered, answers to Duchess. She'll take you to the east road before anyone's the wiser."

Astrid stopped at the bottom of the coal chute and looked back at him, and in the lantern light her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Corren—"

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Whatever it is—don't. Save it. Sing it, if you must sing something. Ring the bell, find your seven voices, mend what I broke." He managed a smile, and it was crooked, and it was, he suspected, the truest thing on his face in a decade. "And Astrid—the apple. Eat the apple. You'll need the strength, and I paid a fortune for them out of season."

She laughed—one cracked, unwilling note of it—and then she was climbing the chute, and then she was gone, and the bell's faint bronze whisper faded with her, and Corren Halloran stood alone in the dark holding an empty lantern.

He climbed the stairs to his study. He sat at his desk. He took out a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and began, in the careful hand of a lifelong magistrate, a confession.

Outside, far to the east, faint as memory, a bell rang once.

Then again.

Then a third time—and somewhere in the sleeping city, in a fen, in a drowned chapel garden, in a mining town's cheapest room, seven scattered hearts lifted at once, hearing their names called home.
