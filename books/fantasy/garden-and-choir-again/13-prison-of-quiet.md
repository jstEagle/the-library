## 13. Prison of Quiet

The cell they put her in had once been a chapel.

Astrid knew this the way she knew most things—by listening. Beneath the smell of damp stone and old iron, beneath the wards that hummed in her teeth like a struck tuning fork she couldn't stop hearing, there was a faint residue of song. Hymns, sung by ordinary voices, badly, for years and years. The walls remembered them the way river stones remember water.

It was, she thought bitterly, the cruelest thing about the Inquisitor's cells. They didn't build them new. They took places that had held something holy and hollowed them out, then filled the hollow with silence so complete it pressed against your eardrums like deep water.

Her magic was gone. Not suppressed—*gone*, or at least unreachable, walled off behind whatever lattice of wards Veyra's people had threaded through the stone. She kept reaching for it anyway, the way you keep reaching for a tooth that isn't there. Her fingers would curl around nothing. The guild mark under her sleeve sat cold and dumb as a scar, which, she supposed, was all it had ever legally been allowed to be.

Three days. Or maybe four. Time moved strangely when you couldn't hear the seasons.

They fed her well. That was the part that unsettled her most. Bread still warm, soup with actual meat in it, an apple each morning polished to a shine. Once, a small pot of honey. Astrid had spent three years eating luck-knot money—thin broth, heel-ends of bread, tea brewed twice from the same leaves—and now her jailer was feeding her better than she'd fed herself since the Guild fell.

"It's not kindness," she told the wall on the second day. "It's strategy."

The wall did not disagree.

She slept. She counted stones. She recited the covenant-song to herself in her head, all seven movements, though without her magic the notes felt like reading a letter from someone who had died. And she waited for Veyra, because Veyra always came eventually. That much Astrid had learned about her even before the fen, even before the cells: the Inquisitor did not believe in leaving questions unanswered. It was, in its way, a kind of respect.

On the fourth day—or the fifth—the door opened without a key turning. Of course it didn't need one. The whole room was a lock.

Veyra came in carrying a wooden box.

Not shackles. Not instruments. A box, plain pine, worn soft at the corners from handling. She set it on the floor between them, folded herself down onto it with the unhurried ease of a woman settling into her own kitchen, and looked up at Astrid with those patient gray eyes.

"You've been talking to the walls," Veyra said.

"Someone has to keep them company. They seem lonely."

"They're chapels," Veyra said. "Or they were. I requested this one specifically." She said it simply, without apology, and somehow that was worse than if she'd been cruel about it. "I thought you might appreciate the acoustics."

Astrid sat on the narrow cot and said nothing. Silence was armor. She'd learned that in the market, wearing it like a second cloak under her coat.

Veyra waited too. She was very good at waiting. Astrid began to suspect it was the Inquisitor's actual weapon—not the riders, not the cells, but this: a woman who could sit in a quiet room longer than anyone alive.

Finally Astrid broke, because of course she did. "If you're going to interrogate me, get on with it. I'm told I'm very stubborn, but even stubbornness gets bored."

"I'm not going to interrogate you." Veyra rested her hands on her knees. "Interrogation assumes I want something from you that you don't want to give. I already know what you're doing. The map, the singers, the road east. You found Sera Ilvane. You found Tam Breck. You went to Corren Halloran's estate and left it in flames—metaphorically speaking; the man's reputation will never recover, which is a kind of fire."

Astrid's stomach dropped. "Then why am I in a cell?"

"Because you were seen doing magic in Ashfield in front of forty witnesses, and my job requires me to be seen doing my job." Veyra's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "Also because I wanted to talk to you, and you run very fast when you're not in a cell. Tam Breck gave us quite a chase through the mine tailings. He's heavier than he looks."

"He's *drunk* most of the time. How did you—"

"The miners love him. They'd sell their mothers for him, and they sold you for considerably less." Veyra shrugged. "Loyalty is expensive. Hunger is cheap. That's not a criticism of them. It's a description of the world we've made."

She lifted the box onto her lap and opened the lid.

Astrid's breath caught before she could stop it.

Charms. Dozens of them. Luck-knots in faded red thread—the same pattern Astrid had tied ten thousand times, her own knot-work, she recognized the little asymmetry in the loops, the tell of a right-handed tier working fast in cold weather. Fever-charms of knotted rowan. A salt-ring pendant. A birch-bark star, the kind sold to mothers for cradles. A cracked blue bead strung on leather that Astrid had made herself, four years ago, for a fisherman's daughter with bad lungs.

"You kept them," Astrid said. Her voice came out strange.

"I confiscated them," Veyra corrected, but gently. "That's the official word. Confiscated implies they're evidence. Evidence is supposed to be destroyed after trial." She turned the blue bead over in her fingers. "These are eleven years past their trials."

"Why?"

Veyra didn't answer right away. She looked down at the box the way people look at graves they visit more often than they admit.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "my brother had a fever. The sweating sickness. Our mother bought a fever-charm from a woman in the Saltmarket—a charm exactly like these ones, probably tied by someone trained in the same guild as you. She paid half a week's wages for it." Veyra's thumb moved over the bead, back and forth. "My brother died anyway. And my mother never stopped believing the charm had failed because *she* hadn't believed hard enough. She spent the rest of her life certain that her own faith was too thin to hold him. Do you understand what that does to a person? To watch her blame herself for a piece of string?"

Astrid's throat was tight. "The charms aren't string."

"No," Veyra agreed. "Sometimes they aren't. That's the problem. Sometimes they work, and everyone can see it, and no one can prove it, and the difference between a miracle and a fraud is invisible to everyone except the person who paid." She closed the box lid halfway. "I became an Inquisitor because I wanted to know which it was. I have spent twenty-two years trying to know. And do you know what I've learned, Astrid Halloran?"

"That magic is real?"

"That it doesn't matter." Veyra looked up, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Whether it's real doesn't matter. What matters is that a woman with a sick child will pay anything, believe anything, blame herself for anything—because the world gives her nothing else to hold. Your guild sold hope to people who couldn't afford bread. And when the purges came, the poor didn't rise up to defend you. Did you notice that? Where were the markets when they hanged the Lanternwrights? Buying bread. Keeping their heads down. Because hope that costs coin is just another tax, and the poor know a tax when they pay one."

The words went in like splinters, deep and clean, and Astrid hated how deep.

"My customers loved me," she said. It sounded thin even to her own ears.

"Some of them did. Mrs. Odell shielded you in the market raid. I interviewed her." Something flickered across Veyra's face—regret, quickly filed away. "She has a broken wrist now. She says it was worth it. I believe her. But ask yourself what she was protecting, Astrid. A person? Or the last person who ever treated her like her luck mattered?" Veyra leaned forward. "What has magic actually given the poor? Not the great houses with their hedge-witches on retainer. The poor. Name me one thing."

Astrid opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Warmth," she said finally. "In winter, when coal was dear, I charmed Mrs. Odell's grate to burn slow and long. One charm, three winters' worth of fuel saved. She ate meat that year. First time in a decade."

"And when the ward-stones ran out of song?" Veyra asked quietly. "Because they do. All charms fade. What did she eat the fourth winter?"

Astrid stared at her hands.

"She ate what she'd eaten before," Veyra said. "Less, because she'd learned to expect warmth and lost it. That's the arithmetic of charity that runs out. It's kinder never to promise." She stood, brushing off her skirts, and set the box on the cot beside Astrid. "Keep them. Consider it evidence of my failure to destroy things I should have destroyed. We all have our crimes."

* * *

After that, Veyra came every day.

Never with questions about the map, or the Choir, or the Faeheart—subjects on which, Astrid realized slowly, the Inquisitor already knew more than she let show. Instead she brought conversation the way other people brought bread: small, warm, offered daily. She talked about the weather, which was failing, and pretended not to notice that they both knew why. She talked about a case involving counterfeit relics in the Dockward. She talked, once, for nearly an hour, about her mother, in a flat careful voice that made clear she had never said any of it aloud before and was not entirely sure why she was saying it now.

And Astrid talked back. That was the trap of it, and she knew it was a trap, and she walked into it every single day with her eyes open, because a cell is a cell and Veyra was the only moving thing in it.

"You're making me like you," Astrid accused her on the ninth day. "This is a technique. There's probably a manual."

"There is a manual," Veyra said placidly, shelling walnuts onto the floor between them. She'd taken to bringing food that required work—nuts, oranges, peas in pods—because, she claimed, idle hands made prisoners melancholy. Astrid suspected the truth was that Veyra couldn't bear to sit still either. "Chapter Four. 'Kindness as Instrument.' I disagreed with it so thoroughly I wrote a rebuttal in the margin and was reprimanded for defacing Inquisitorial property."

"What did the rebuttal say?"

"*Kindness that is instrumental is not kindness.*" She handed Astrid a walnut. "I still think I was right. Which raises uncomfortable questions about what I've been doing here for nine days."

Astrid cracked the walnut against the cot frame. "You're doubting."

"I'm *considering*. Doubting is for people with time."

"The seasons are dying, Veyra. Spring came late and left early. The children in Ashfield stopped dreaming—I saw it, I *heard* it, the whole village was silent at night like a house where someone has just died. That's not weather. That's the covenant failing. The Choir kept it, and we killed the Choir, and now the wild is bleeding out of the world one season at a time." She leaned forward. "You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't verified it yourself. You're too careful not to."

Veyra was quiet for a long moment. A shell fell from her fingers.

"I rode to Ashfield," she said at last. "Before I came here today. I interviewed the midwife. Children who don't dream, crops that grow black, a well that tastes of iron." She met Astrid's eyes. "I also interviewed a woman whose son was hanged in the purges for singing a harvest song. She showed me his grave. She asked me—" Veyra's voice did something complicated and then steadied itself. "She asked me whether the law that killed him had saved anyone. I've been asking myself the same question for nine days, and I find that I don't have an answer, and I am *not a person who lacks answers*, Astrid. It's the only thing I've ever been sure of about myself."

"So help us."

"I can't." It came out fast, almost reflexive. Then slower: "Two hundred years of law. Thousands of sworn officers. If I decide the law is wrong, I don't get to just—set it down. It's not a coat. It's a spine."

"Spines can be mended."

"Spines can be *broken*," Veyra said, "which is what happens to people who turn. Ask Corren Halloran how it feels to live bent." She rose, straightened her coat, and paused at the door with her hand against the stone. "Your friends are looking for you. The drummer and the singer. They're not subtle. Tam Breck tried to bribe a guard with a harmonica."

Astrid's heart lurched. "Are they—"

"They're alive. They're incompetent, but they're alive." Veyra glanced back, and for one unguarded moment her gray eyes were young and terribly uncertain. "I'm telling you this because in a few days I'll have to file a transfer order moving you to the capital for trial, and once you're in the capital, no one will ever tell you anything again. Whatever happens next—happens soon. Sleep well, Charm-seller."

The door sealed behind her with a sound like a held breath.

Astrid sat in the quiet with the box of charms beside her and Veyra's words coiled in her chest like wire. *What has magic given the poor?* She picked up the blue bead, the fisherman's daughter's charm, and turned it in the light.

And then she noticed what she should have noticed days ago: the bead was warm.

Faintly, impossibly warm, in a room warded down to bare stone—warm the way a mark glows when someone nearby remembers you kindly, warm the way a guild-trained ear knows a voice is approaching before footsteps sound. Somewhere beyond these walls, someone was singing. Badly. Off-key, half-remembered, unmistakably a fragment of the covenant-song, getting closer.

Astrid smiled for the first time in nine days, and began, very quietly, to hum along.
