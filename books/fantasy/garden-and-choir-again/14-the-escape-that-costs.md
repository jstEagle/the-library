## 14. The Escape That Costs

Tam Breck had not held a drum in nine years, and his hands remembered it the way a dog remembers a door it was beaten behind: with a flinch first, and then a terrible longing.

He stood in the shadow of the Inquisitor's prison wall with the drum wrapped in sacking against his chest, and he told himself—for perhaps the fortieth time since dawn—that this was not singing. This was not music. This was a signal, nothing more. Three beats meant *the ward-line is open*. Two meant *run now, you fool, they've seen us*.

"You're muttering," Sera said beside him.

"I'm praying."

"To whom?"

"The god of cowards. He's a patient sort. Never asks anything of you until the very last moment, and then he asks everything."

Sera Ilvane did not laugh—she had not laughed in years, and Tam had stopped expecting it—but the corner of her mouth moved, which from her was practically a festival. She looked out at the prison yard through the gap in the wall where the fen-witches' briar spell had quietly rotted the mortar. Beyond it, torchlight. Beyond that, cells. And somewhere in those cells, Astrid Halloran, who had pulled two broken singers out of their ruins and asked them for nothing but the truth.

"She'll be in the third block," Sera murmured. "The quiet cells. Where the wards drink magic."

"Then we'd best be quick about it." Tam unwrapped the drum. His palms were sweating. "Because I'll tell you what I told the witches: I haven't played since Marlow died, and my rhythm's likely rusted all to pieces."

"It isn't."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've heard you tap your fingers on the table every night since we left the fen," Sera said. "In your sleep, too. The song never left you, Tam Breck. You just stopped listening to it."

He wanted to argue. He was, in fact, assembling an argument—it began with *you don't know what it's like to have your music kill a man* and went downhill from there—but the yard bell rang the half-hour, and there was no more time for arguments.

Tam Breck struck the drumhead once, softly, like a heartbeat. Then twice more.

The sound went out across the prison yard small and human and utterly unremarkable—a drunk beating time outside a wall, nothing worth a guard's attention—and yet the old guild-signal carried inside it the shape of something larger, the way a single note carries the chord it belongs to. Somewhere in the third block, he prayed, a charm-seller with a guild mark under her sleeve was hearing it and understanding.

*We came back for you. We actually came back.*

Gods help them all.

* * *

Astrid Halloran heard the drum through three feet of stone and thought at first she was dreaming.

She had spent four days in the quiet cells learning what silence truly was. Not absence of sound—the prison was full of sound, boots and bells and the coughing of other prisoners—but absence of *answer*. Her magic, which had been as constant as her own pulse since childhood, a low warm hum beneath everything, was simply gone. The wards drank it. She kept reaching for it the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, and kept finding the same hollow.

So when the drumbeat came—three soft strokes, spaced like a heart deciding to keep going—she felt it in her bones before her ears, because bones did not need wards to listen.

*Three.* The ward-line is open.

She sat up on the cot so fast her head swam. Four days of Veyra's courteous visits, four days of that unbearable question—*what has magic ever actually given the poor, Miss Halloran?*—four days of doubt planted in her like a seed in good soil, and now this: someone had come. Two someones. Idiots. Glorious, impossible idiots.

She pressed her palm flat against the cell wall and felt, faintly, the vibration still trembling in the stone. And beneath it—this was the strange part, the part that made her breath catch—she felt the wards themselves. They were drinking deep, gulping down magic poured into the wall-line from outside, and they were *sated*, sluggish, drunk on it. Someone was feeding the line more than it could swallow.

That took power. Real power. The kind the guild had trained into its singers and no one else alive still had.

"Sera," Astrid whispered. "You absolute madwoman."

The lock on her door was iron and honest; it yielded to the hairpin she'd spent four days pretending to pick at her own hair. She slipped into the corridor, past a snoring turnkey whose dreams the briar-spell had sweetened into something deep and dreamless, and followed the pull in her chest toward the yard.

She found Sera at the base of the outer wall, standing with both hands raised and her whole body arched like a drawn bow, pouring herself into the ward-line. Astrid saw it plainly even without her magic—the light of it, green-gold, running up the stones in veins like sap rising in spring. It was the most beautiful thing she had seen in years, and it was killing the woman making it.

"Sera!" Astrid caught her as she sagged. "Stop—you have to stop—"

"Holding it open," Sera whispered. Her lips were gray. Her eyes, though—her eyes were brighter than Astrid had ever seen them, bright as the drowned chapel garden. "Can't hold it and walk. So I'm holding it. Go."

"Not without you."

"Astrid." A ghost of the old Choir discipline in her voice, the cantor's tone that made novices stand straighter. "The line closes when I stop. If I stop before you're through, the wards wake, and everything we've spent is wasted. Everything *I've* spent." She smiled, small and terrible. "I've been silent for six years, girl. Let me spend the rest loudly."

"No." Astrid hauled Sera's arm over her shoulders. "New plan. We go together, and you pour it out slower, and if your knees buckle I'm carrying you, and I want you to know right now that I will complain about it the entire way east."

For one moment Sera simply looked at her. Then she laughed—one cracked, rusty, astonished syllable of laughter, her first in years—and let herself be half-carried through the gap in the wall while the green-gold light streamed after them like a ribbon unspooling.

They made the treeline as the ward-line snapped shut behind them with a sound like a great door closing. Somewhere in the prison, bells began to ring.

Tam was waiting with the horses, white-faced, drum still slung across his back.

"Tell me that was the plan," he said.

"That was absolutely the plan," said Astrid.

"Liar," said Sera, and fainted.

* * *

They rode east through the hours before dawn, and Tam Breck discovered that terror was an excellent cure for a nine-year silence.

Not that he sang. But he kept the drum against his chest as they rode, and whenever the pursuit-bells behind them swelled, he beat time against the sacking—soft, steady, the traveling-rhythm the old guild used on the long roads—and it steadied the horses, and it steadied Sera, who lay across the third horse with her head in Astrid's lap and her breathing gone thin as paper.

It also steadied him, which was the humiliating part. Every stroke of the drum was a small argument with the ghost of Marlow Fell. *This isn't music,* he told the ghost. *This is just keeping time.* The ghost, being dead, declined to answer, which was how most of their conversations went.

Dawn found them in the hill country above the Ashfield road, in the lee of a fallen watchtower, and there was no pretending anymore. Sera's wound was not bleeding, which was somehow worse. The ward-line had not cut her flesh; it had cut deeper than flesh. There was a mark on her side like frost-fern drawn in shadow, spreading slow as ink in water, and where it spread the skin went cold and quiet. Astrid laid her hands over it and her healing-craft slid off it like rain off glass.

"It's drinking my magic too," Astrid said quietly. "Whatever the wards took from her, they're still taking."

"Then we get her to the Faeheart," Tam said. "The garden. The map says—"

"The map says the entrance is three days' ride east, and she doesn't have three days." Astrid's voice didn't break, but Tam heard the crack in it anyway, the way you hear a flaw in a bell. "Look at her, Tam."

He looked. Sera Ilvane, who had kept a garden blooming impossibly through six years of despair, who had given a mute girl back the word *run*, who had poured six years of stored silence into a prison wall in one blazing act—lay with her eyes half-open, watching the sky above the ruined tower as if memorizing it.

"I can hear it," Sera said.

"Hear what?" Astrid leaned close.

"The seasons turning wrong." Her voice was faint but clear. "Out there. Past the hills. Winter won't loosen its grip—it's holding on like a hand that's forgotten how to open. Can't you hear it? The world's gone off-key, and nobody's been singing to it, and it's forgetting the tune."

Tam sat down heavily on a fallen stone. He had spent nine years telling himself the purges hadn't mattered much in the end—that the guild had been small, that the covenant was priest-talk, that the world got along fine without songs in it. He had built a life on that foundation, a shabby comfortable life of ale and odd jobs and never, ever playing.

And here was a dying woman hearing the world go off-key from a ruined watchtower, and he could not call her a liar, because he had heard it too. For weeks now. In the black crops. In the children who didn't dream. In the way winter clung past its season like a guest who'd forgotten the way out.

"What do we do?" he asked. It was not a rhetorical question. He genuinely did not know, and for Tam Breck, asking was a kind of surrender.

Astrid didn't answer at once. She was looking at Sera's wound, and then at her own hands, and Tam watched her arrive at something the hard way, step by step, like a woman wading into cold water.

"When Sera held the ward-line," Astrid said slowly, "she wasn't strong enough. Not alone. I could feel it—the line kept pulling, wanting more than she had. She gave it everything anyway." She looked up at him. "Why did it work at all?"

"Because she's stubborn?"

"Because she *meant* it." Astrid's eyes were fierce and wet. "Don't you see? Magic isn't just voices. The guild taught us that and we forgot it. The song needs a reason underneath it, or it's just noise. Sera held that wall because she'd decided you and I were worth dying for, and the magic believed her. That's why it opened." She gestured at the east, where the mountains waited under their burden of wrong winter. "And that's why the covenant failed. It wasn't just that the Choir scattered. It's that the realm stopped having a reason to sing to the Faeheart. We broke faith first. The song couldn't hold without it."

Tam turned his drum over in his hands. Under the sacking, the old guild-mark was worn nearly smooth by his thumb, from all the years of touching it and putting it away.

"So we need more than voices," he said.

"We need a reason. Something worth meaning." Astrid laughed, short and unsteady. "Which is a problem, because I've been asking myself Veyra's question for four days straight and I still don't have a good answer. What has magic given the poor? Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Small mercies. And look what it costs—" she touched Sera's brow, gentle, "—and tell me why any sane person wouldn't outlaw it."

"Because of her garden," Tam said.

Astrid blinked. "What?"

"At the chapel. The one that bloomed through the blight-year, when everything for twenty miles around was black." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I'm not a poet. But I stood in that garden and I thought: *this is what it's for.* Not the charms. Not the luck. This. One impossible green thing in a dying field, so the people walking past remember the world was meant to be kinder than it's being." He met her eyes. "That's a reason, isn't it? That's the reason. We don't sing to save the world. We sing so the world remembers what saving looks like."

Silence, except the wind and Sera's thin breathing.

Then Sera's hand moved—found Astrid's wrist, found Tam's sleeve—and drew them both down close.

"Listen to him," she whispered, and her gray lips curved. "Nine years silent, and he opens his mouth and it's *true*. That's the voice the Choir lost. That's the voice we need." Her grip tightened, sudden and urgent. "But hear me, both of you. The Faeheart takes a price. The old songs say so—I heard them before I went mute, I heard them and I didn't understand. Whatever kindling the covenant needs, it will cost you something dear. Both of you. Promise me you'll walk in knowing that. Promise me you won't hate the garden for taking."

"We promise," Astrid said.

"And Tam." Sera's eyes found his, and they were already far away, seeing something beyond the tower, beyond the hills. "When we reach the mountain—when it's time—you'll need to play. Really play. Not signals. Music."

His stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Sera, I can't. What I am—what happened to Marlow—"

"Marlow chose to stand between the soldiers and the novices," Sera said. "Your drum didn't kill him, Tam. Your drum called him brave, and he answered. There's a difference, and it's taken me six years of silence to learn it, and I'll not watch you waste another nine." She closed her eyes, spent. "Play for him, then. Play the song he died answering. Let it finally finish."

Tam Breck sat with his drum and said nothing, because there was nothing in him large enough to say it with.

* * *

They rode east as the sun climbed, and the land grew stranger with every mile.

Frost in the ditches in high summer. A river running slow and glassy, half-froze at the edges though the air was mild. A shepherd standing motionless in a field, watching his flock with the blank face of a man who has forgotten why he's watching. And always, underneath, that wrongness Sera had named—the sense of a great instrument detuned, strings slackening one by one.

Sera faded by degrees. By midday she could no longer sit a horse; they lashed a litter between two mounts and Astrid rode beside her with one hand pressed constantly to the cold fern-mark, feeding her own warmth into it, buying minutes at the price of her own strength. By late afternoon Sera had begun to murmur—not words, at first, and then words: scraps of the covenant-song, fragments the guild had recorded and the purges had scattered, surfacing in her like fish rising in a dark pool.

"...and the wild shall hold the hand of the ordered..." she breathed. "...and neither shall forget the other..."

"She's singing it in her sleep," Astrid said, wonder and grief tangled in her voice. "Even now. Even fading. Tam—she's *still singing*."

"Then we'd better make sure she reaches somewhere worth singing to." Tam stood in his stirrups and looked east.

And there it was.

The mountains had been visible since morning, blue-gray on the horizon. But now, as the sun lowered behind them, he saw what the map's final page had promised and he had not quite believed: a peak ahead split by some ancient violence, its hollow heart open to the sky like a cup. And from within it, faint as memory, faint as the first note of a lullaby you almost recall—

Light. Green-gold, the exact color of the ward-light Sera had poured out, glowing softly in the mountain's wound like an ember refusing to die.

Beneath his hands, without his permission, without his decision, Tam Breck's fingers had begun to tap the drumhead. Softly. Steadily. The traveling-rhythm, the road-song, the one Marlow had loved.

He didn't stop it.

"There," Astrid said, pointing, her voice shaking. "The garden beneath the mountain. Sera—Sera, look, we found it, we *found* it—"

Sera's eyes opened. She looked at the light in the mountain for a long moment, and then she smiled, and began, in a thread of a voice, to sing.

Behind them, far to the west, a horn sounded on the road they had left. Riders. Many riders, moving fast.

But ahead—ahead was the reason.
