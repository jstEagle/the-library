## 15. The Garden Beneath the Mountain

The map's final page had been folded so many times that the living ink had worn thin along the creases, like a face grown tired of being looked at. Astrid held it up to the grey morning light and watched the last of its secrets bleed through: a mountain with a hollow heart, drawn not as stone but as an open mouth, singing.

"That's it," she said. "That's where we're going."

Tam Breck squinted at it over her shoulder, the way he'd been squinting at everything for three days—suspiciously, and with one hand never far from his drum. "It looks like a throat," he said. "We're walking into a throat."

"Mountains don't have throats."

"Everything has a throat if you look at it long enough." He said it without humor, which was unlike him. Since Sera had taken the ward-line's bite at Veyra's prison, Tam had lost his taste for jokes the way a man loses his taste for food during a fever. He carried her now on his back when the ground allowed it, and on the bier they'd lashed together from hazel poles when it didn't, and he did both without complaint, which frightened Astrid more than any complaint could have.

Sera lay among the blankets with her eyes half-open, watching the sky through the hazel lattice. The wound in her side had stopped bleeding days ago, but the color hadn't come back to her face, and her breathing had developed a whistle at the top of each breath, thin as a reed. Once, in the night, Astrid had heard her humming—a broken little fragment of the covenant-song, three notes, then silence, then three notes again, like someone knocking on a door that wouldn't open.

"We're close," Astrid said again, mostly to herself. "The map says two days past the Ashen Fells."

"The map also said there'd be a bridge over the Greywater," Tam said. "There is no bridge over the Greywater. There is a river where the bridge used to be, and my boots are still wet from it."

"Your boots are always wet."

"My boots are *philosophically* wet. It's different."

Astrid smiled despite herself, and Tam almost smiled back, and for a moment the road felt less like a funeral procession. Then Sera coughed—that whistling, tearing cough—and the moment passed, and they walked on.

* * *

They climbed into the Ashen Fells on the second day, following a track that no cart had ever widened, that shepherds had perhaps never walked, that seemed to exist only because the map insisted upon it. The fells themselves were wrong in a way Astrid couldn't name at first. The rock was black and glassy in places, as though something had burned here long ago—not hot, but *old*, a fire from before memory. Nothing grew on the slopes except lichen the color of old silver, and the wind came down off the heights carrying a smell she knew from her guild years: ozone and honey, the smell magic made when it pooled somewhere deep and slow.

"It's here," she whispered. "Under us. All of it."

"How can you tell?"

"I can feel it in my teeth." She touched her tongue to her back molar, where the sensation gathered, a faint hum like a struck tuning fork pressed to her jaw. "Corren told me once that the oldest wards didn't keep things out. They kept things *in*. Or—" she hesitated, "—kept things *asleep*."

"And we're going to wake it up."

"We're going to ask it to sing."

Tam looked at her sideways. "Those aren't the same thing?"

"Not even slightly."

Sera stirred on the bier and lifted one hand—not toward Astrid, but toward the mountain ahead of them, where the track bent around a shoulder of black stone and disappeared. Her lips moved. No sound came out. But Astrid had spent enough weeks now learning to read the shape of Sera's silenced voice, and what she read there made the hair rise on her arms.

*Home,* Sera's mouth said. *Home, home.*

* * *

They found the entrance at dusk, and it was nothing like Astrid had imagined, which was itself a kind of shock, because she had imagined everything: a gate of iron, a door of carved stone, stairs descending into dark. Instead the mountain simply opened, the way a hand opens, along a seam in the rock wide enough for three people abreast, and from within came light—not firelight, not sunlight, but a green-gold glow like the last hour of a summer evening that refused, out of stubbornness or love, to end.

Warm air breathed out of it. It smelled of loam and growing things, of rain on warm stone, of every garden Astrid had ever loved compressed into a single exhalation.

"Oh," said Tam, very quietly. His drum, slung across his back, gave a soft involuntary thrum, as if something inside the mountain had plucked its skin.

They went in.

The passage sloped gently downward, and the walls changed as they descended—from black glassy rock to grey granite to something softer, veined with quartz that caught the green-gold light and scattered it into constellations. Moss grew in the crevices, luminous, damp. Small white flowers opened as Astrid passed them and closed behind her, so that she left a wake of darkness, like a boat leaving a trail on still water.

And then the passage opened, and Astrid Halloran forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

The mountain was hollow. Not empty—hollow, the way a bell is hollow, the way a heart is hollow, shaped to hold something rather than nothing. Its ceiling arched away overhead into darkness shot through with drifting motes of light, and its floor—its *floor*—was a garden.

It ran farther than her eyes could follow, terraced and tumbling and wild, roses climbing over apple trees, apple trees leaning against stands of birch that had no business growing underground, streams threading between beds of herbs she recognized from her mother's garden and herbs she recognized from nowhere at all. And everywhere, everywhere, the creatures.

They were small. That was the first thing. The stories had made them vast and terrible, the wild folk of the Faeheart, but these were small as children, small as wrens—round-shouldered figures with bark-brown skin and hair like moss and seed-down, moving through the garden rows with hoes of antler and baskets woven from their own shed hair. Some had too many joints in their fingers. Some had eyes like a goat's, horizontal-pupiled, gentle. One, passing close, had a face like a fox and hands like a woman's, and it looked up at Astrid with an expression of such weary recognition that she felt her chest ache.

"They know us," Tam murmured. "Or they know what we are."

One of them approached—an elder, by the silvering of its moss-hair, walking with a staff of rowan wood worn smooth by centuries of gripping. It stopped before Astrid and studied her with eyes the color of pond water, and then it did something that undid her entirely: it bowed. Deeply. To *her*, the charm-seller, the fugitive, the last apprentice of a dead guild.

"Lanternwright," it said. Its voice was dry leaves and running water. "You are late."

Astrid's throat worked. "I'm sorry. I—I didn't know I was expected."

"You were expected eighty years ago. Your master was expected before you. We have been keeping the garden ready for a long time." The creature's gaze slid to Sera on her bier, and softened. "Bring the singer. Bring her to the water. There is time yet, but not much, and none of it to waste."

* * *

They laid Sera beside a pool at the garden's center, a spring-fed basin ringed with stones carved in spirals, and the garden-folk brought salves that smelled of crushed violet and something older, something that hummed. Under their ministrations Sera's breathing eased, the whistle in it softening, and her eyes found Astrid's and held them with a fierce, exhausted question.

*Why did you bring me here?* the look asked. *What happens now?*

"Now," said the elder, who had followed them, "we must speak of the price."

Astrid turned. "The price. The map mentioned—the final page said the covenant asks a toll, but it didn't say—"

"No. Maps do not say. Maps only lead you to the place where you must be told." The elder settled onto a stone with the care of great age, folding its long hands atop the rowan staff. Around them, the garden-folk had gone quiet, pausing in their rows, turning their gentle faces toward the pool. Even the light-motes overhead seemed to drift lower, to listen.

"The covenant between your realm and ours is not a wall," the elder said. "It is a song. You know this. Your guild knew this. A song sung by mortal voices and answered by wild ones, verse and chorus, call and reply. While it is sung, the seasons turn, the dreams flow, the boundary holds—soft enough to let wonder through, firm enough to keep the wildness from drowning your fields and your minds."

"And the Choir sang it," Astrid said. "Until they were scattered."

"Until they were hunted," the elder corrected, without accusation, which was worse than accusation. "And the song fell silent. And now the covenant is a dying ember, and embers must be fed."

"Fed with what?"

The elder looked at her for a long moment. Pond-water eyes, ancient and kind and utterly without mercy, because kindness that flinches is not kindness at all.

"With memory," it said. "With the dearest thing a mortal owns. Any mortal who reforges the covenant must give the Faeheart their most beloved memories—all of them, whole and living—to burn as kindling beneath the new song. Not copies. Not echoes. Given. Gone. The song must be built of love, Lanternwright, or it will not hold. And love that costs nothing binds nothing."

The words went into Astrid like cold water into a wound.

She heard herself speak, from very far away. "Gone how? Forever?"

"There is no forever in the Faeheart. But gone as far as anything mortal goes. You would not remember that you had forgotten. You would stand in the world after, whole and hollow, knowing that something was missing and never knowing what."

Tam made a sound low in his throat. "No. No, that's—there has to be another way. Another singer. Someone who doesn't—"

"The covenant does not take volunteers' leftovers," the elder said gently. "It takes the truest thing. That is why it has waited eighty years. That is why it will wait longer, if it must. It is patient. The world is not."

Astrid looked down at Sera. Sera was looking back at her, and her hand had found Astrid's wrist, grip feeble but insistent, and her mouth was shaping words again, silent, urgent:

*Don't. Find another way. Don't.*

And Astrid thought of her mother. Of flour on her mother's hands, of the lullaby hummed off-key over the washbasin, of the exact weight of a small hand in hers on a winter market day. She thought of Corren, young, laughing, teaching her to hold a note the way you hold a bird—firm enough that it stays, loose enough that it lives. She thought of the guild hall burning, and of the years after, selling luck-knots to strangers, keeping the mark hidden, keeping herself hidden, keeping alive without quite knowing why.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, that's why.*

Her eyes were stinging. She became aware that the entire garden had gone silent around her—the folk standing motionless among their impossible roses, the streams running quiet, the light-motes hanging suspended like held breath. Waiting. They had been waiting eighty years, and they could wait a few minutes more, but not many.

"I need," Astrid said, and her voice cracked, and she steadied it the way Corren had taught her, from the diaphragm, from the root, "I need a night to think. One night."

"One night," the elder agreed. "The garden will keep. It has kept for eighty years." It rose, slowly, and its pond-water eyes were kind again, unbearably kind. "But I will tell you what I tell every soul who stands where you stand, so that you cannot say you were not warned. The Faeheart does not want your suffering, child. It wants your love. That is the horror of it. It will take the best of you, and it will make something beautiful, and the world will be saved, and you will walk out of here not knowing why you ever wanted to save it."

It bowed again, and shuffled away into the rose-rows, and the garden resumed its breathing around them.

* * *

Night, if it could be called night, came softly to the hollow mountain—a dimming of the green-gold light, a settling of the motes into the flowerbeds like fireflies going home. The garden-folk brought them bread and honey and a stew of roots whose names Astrid didn't know, and it was the best food she had ever tasted, which felt like cruelty.

Tam sat sharpening his drumsticks by the pool, though there was nothing to sharpen them against. Sera slept, truly slept, her color a little better, her hand still curled loosely around Astrid's sleeve.

Astrid sat apart, on a terrace above the roses, with the map open on her knees. The living ink had gone still at last, all its shifting done, all its roads walked. Only one figure remained bright on the final page: the mountain, the open mouth, and inside it, drawn in ink that glowed faintly warm, a single small note of music.

She stared at it until her eyes blurred.

Below her, Tam spoke without looking up. "You've already decided."

It wasn't a question. She didn't insult him by pretending otherwise.

"If I do this," she said, "you'll have to remember for me. Both of you. Everything I tell you tonight—my mother, the guild, all of it—you carry it. Because I won't be able to. Someone has to."

"Astrid—"

"Somebody has to, Tam. Otherwise it's like it never happened at all. And it happened. It mattered. *I* mattered, once, to people." Her voice caught. "I won't let the world be saved by a girl nobody remembers saving."

Tam set down his drumsticks. For a long moment he said nothing, and then he picked up his drum, quietly, and began to tap out a rhythm—slow, steady, the rhythm of a march, or a heartbeat, or a man keeping time while he thinks.

"All right," he said finally. "Start with your mother. Tell me everything. Leave nothing out."

And high above them, at the mouth of the mountain, where the black fells met the starless sky, a light appeared—small, bobbing, descending the track they had climbed. Lantern-light. Many lanterns, moving with the disciplined spacing of soldiers.

Astrid watched them come, and her heart did a strange, calm thing: it broke, and settled, and held.

"They found us," Tam said, rising, reaching for his sticks.

"No," Astrid said softly, watching the lights descend toward the garden, toward the pool, toward the last night of the life she knew. "Look at the spacing. That's not a raid formation."

The lanterns drew closer, and at their head walked a tall figure in a grey traveling cloak, and even at this distance, even after everything, Astrid knew the set of those shoulders, the deliberate unhurried stride.

Inquisitor Veyra had come to the Faeheart—and she was not alone. Behind her, limping on a borrowed stick, grey-faced and free in a way he should not have been, came a second figure that made Tam's drum fall silent and Sera sit bolt upright on her bier, one word finally tearing free of her ruined throat, raw and ringing through the hollow mountain:

"*Master.*"

Corren had come.
