## 16. What Astrid Would Lose

The garden did not sleep, and so neither could Astrid.

She had tried. She had wrapped herself in a cloak of moss that the small green creatures of the mountain had offered her with the solemn courtesy of innkeepers presenting fresh linens, and she had lain down beneath a tree whose leaves chimed faintly in a wind she couldn't feel, and she had closed her eyes and attempted, with the discipline of three years' practice, to be somewhere else.

But the Faeheart hummed. That was the only word for it. The whole hollow mountain was a struck bell that had been ringing for centuries without stopping, too low to hear with the ears and too constant to ignore with anything else. It came up through the ground and through her ribs and settled behind her sternum like a second heartbeat that kept slightly different time than her own.

So she gave up on sleeping and sat up instead, and the moss-cloak slid off her shoulders, and one of the little creatures — the round ones with bark-colored skin and eyes like dew — collected it again with an air of patient resignation, as though it had expected this from the beginning.

"All right," Astrid told it. "I'm up. You can stop judging me."

It had no expression she could read, but she felt judged anyway. It seemed to be a specialty of the place.

* * *

Sera slept, finally, on a bed of living ferns near the garden's heart, her wound bound in poultices that smelled of honey and something older than honey. Tam slept sitting up against a stone, his drum cradled in his lap like a child he didn't trust anyone else to hold. Even awake, Tam held it that way. Astrid suspected he would be buried holding it, whenever burial came, and that he'd have opinions about the shoveling.

And Astrid walked.

That was what you did, she was learning, when the mountain would not let you rest. You walked among the impossible beds of flowers — flowers that bloomed in colors that had names in no human tongue, flowers that turned to follow not the sun but the sound of footsteps — and you let the humming fill you, and you tried not to think about what the garden's keeper had told her at dusk.

*Any mortal who reforges the covenant must give the Faeheart their dearest memories as kindling.*

Dearest. Not dearest *one*. Not a person, not a name she could strike from a list and grieve cleanly. Memories. The moments themselves. And the Faeheart, the keeper had said, would know which were truly dearest, because the Faeheart accepted nothing false. No bargains. No clever substitutions. It weighed the heart itself.

Astrid had spent her whole life making charms out of almost nothing. A knot, a word, a breath of intention. She knew how much power lived in small things honestly given. She should have found the covenant's price beautiful.

Instead she wanted to throw up behind a topiary shaped like something that might once have been a deer.

* * *

"Inventory," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller, and because the dew-eyed creatures seemed to find human speech mildly entertaining and she needed an audience more than she needed dignity. "Let's do an inventory."

She climbed to a flat shelf of stone overlooking the central pool, where the water glowed faintly gold from below, and she sat down cross-legged the way Corren used to make apprentices sit when they were being foolish — spine straight, hands open, no fidgeting — and she began.

Memory one: her mother's kitchen.

It arrived whole, the way the best memories did, unsummoned and complete. The smell of bread and woodsmoke. The chipped blue bowl her mother stirred porridge in, the chip shaped like a sliver of moon. Her mother humming — always humming, some tuneless thing she'd invented herself, a song with no words that rose and fell like weather. Little Astrid on the stool by the window, feet swinging, watching frost ferns crawl up the glass and believing, with the total faith of a four-year-old, that her mother could keep the cold out forever.

Her mother had died when she was nine. Fever, the same winter the guild first whispered about failing seasons. Astrid remembered the funeral less clearly than the kitchen. She remembered the kitchen every day.

"That one," she said quietly. "Obviously that one."

The pool said nothing. The creatures below had stopped pretending to work and were watching her, a dozen small bark-colored faces tilted upward.

"No," she said, louder. "No, I mean — obviously it *would* be that one. That doesn't mean I've decided. There are rules. There has to be a process." She heard how it sounded and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I'm negotiating with a pond. This is fine. This is where three years of selling luck-knots got me."

Memory two, arriving without permission: rain on the guildhouse roof.

Not a dramatic memory. One of hundreds of identical evenings. She was fourteen, and it was raining hard enough to turn the world to static, and Master Corren had the apprentices gathered in the hall because the lesson was listening. *Magic is ninety percent listening,* he'd said, pacing, his boots leaving damp crescents on the flagstones. *Anyone can shout. The craft is hearing what the world is already trying to say.* And then he'd taught them their first true song — not a spell, just a listening-song, a way of opening the ear past the ear — and he'd sung it wrong on purpose, first, so they wouldn't be afraid of getting it wrong themselves.

She could still hear his voice doing it. Deliberately flat on the third note, then a wink down the row of benches, then the true note, clean as a bell, and forty children laughing and the rain coming down and the whole roof ringing like a drumhead.

Astrid opened her eyes.

"Oh, no," she said. "No, no. He doesn't get to be in the top two. He betrayed us. He helped outlaw all of it. He sent riders after me. He—" She stopped, because the list ran out there, or rather it didn't run out but everything else on it belonged to other people's chapters, and here in the dark of her own skull the accounting was brutally simple.

Corren had broken her heart. Corren had also taught her every song she knew. Both facts stood in her chest side by side like two people waiting in the same queue, refusing to acknowledge each other.

The Faeheart accepted nothing false. Which meant she couldn't sort him out of the ledger. Which meant—

"Fine," she told the pool, furiously. "Fine. Top two: my dead mother and my traitor mentor. What a life I've had. What rich material."

One of the dew-eyed creatures made a sound like a spoon tapping a cup. It might have been sympathy. It might have been laughter. With this place, it was genuinely difficult to say.

* * *

She tried, then, over the next hours — hours measured in the slow wheeling of light that came from nowhere and went nowhere — to build a case for the alternatives.

There was the market. Three years of it. Surely the market counted for something. The first morning she'd set out her tray with her sleeves pulled down over her guild mark and her heart hammering, and an old woman had bought a fever-charm and paid double and said, *You've kind hands, girl, whatever else you are.* The taste of the first coin she'd earned honestly in a city that would hang her for what she was. The winter she'd sold nothing for six days and then a farmer had come in desperate, his daughter burning, and Astrid had worked magic she barely had left and taken no payment at all, and the man had wept onto her counter, and she had gone home that night feeling — what? Useful. Real. Like a lanternwright still, in miniature, in secret.

Good memories. True ones. She laid them out before the glowing pool one by one, like a merchant displaying wares, and waited to feel the hum change, to feel the mountain lean toward them.

Nothing. The humming went on, even, indifferent, vast.

"It's not that they're shallow," she argued. "They're not shallow. They're the bravest things I ever did. I built a whole self out of them. I survived on them."

But even as she said it she understood the shape of the problem, the way you understand a knot by pulling it tighter. Those memories were dear because of what they proved. They were evidence. Evidence of endurance, of purpose, of the person she'd made herself into after everything was taken. And the Faeheart didn't want evidence. Kindling wasn't proof of anything. Kindling was the thing itself, the dry sweet heartwood, the part of you that caught fire easiest because it had loved hardest first.

The market memories were armor she'd forged. The kitchen was the skin underneath.

She thought about Sera, mute these many years, tending a garden she could no longer sing to. Sera hadn't lost her voice to any bargain. She'd lost it to despair — had locked it away herself, Astrid suspected, the way you lock away a knife you're afraid you'll use. And now Sera lay sleeping under ferns with a wound in her side taken holding a ward-line open for a stranger, and when she'd regained that single word in the fen — *run* — she'd said it like a woman handing over the last coin in her purse.

What would Sera say, if Astrid asked her which memories were dearest?

Sera would say: don't ask. Don't inventory. The heart isn't a ledger, girl. You'll only find what you already know.

* * *

Near what she guessed was dawn — the light did its slow gold-turning whether or not anyone called it morning — Astrid gave up on negotiation and simply let the memories come, all of them, unranked, a flood.

Her mother teaching her to knead bread, flour to the elbows, both of them laughing at the mess. Her mother's hand cool on her forehead during fevers, murmuring a charm so old it wasn't even illegal anymore, just forgotten. The last thing her mother ever said to her: *Mind the garden while I'm abed,* and nine-year-old Astrid watering the herbs faithfully for a week before anyone told her.

Corren's voice correcting her grip on a bell-rope. Corren drunk once, only once, after the first purge, weeping in the guildhall and saying, *We should have sung louder,* and young Astrid standing in the doorway not knowing yet what those words would cost the world. Corren years later, across his magistrate's desk, eyes sliding away from hers, saying *I did what was merciful* in a voice that begged her to believe it because he could not believe it himself.

Tam's drum rolling through the guildhall like summer thunder. Sera's voice, before it went, high and clear over the covenant-song, the note that had once held the seasons in place.

Her own name. The history of it. Halloran — her mother's family, dockworkers and netmenders, a name that meant nothing to anyone now except her, and soon, if she went forward, not even that.

She sat with all of it, and the pool glowed, and the creatures had drifted away to their duties, and the hum of the mountain filled her like water fills a cup.

"I don't want to lose them," she said at last. Simply. To the pool, to the mountain, to whatever listened. "I want you to know that. I'm going to do it, and I don't want to, and those are both true, and if your covenant runs on honesty then it can start with that."

The hum deepened. Just slightly. Just enough that she felt it in her teeth, and the gold light in the pool brightened the way coals brighten when breathed upon.

Acknowledged, then. Whatever that was worth.

* * *

She was still sitting there when the sound came down from the outer passages: boot-heels. Many of them. Disciplined, evenly spaced, unmistakably military, echoing through halls that had known no human feet but hers and Tam's and Sera's limping tread.

Astrid was on her feet before her mind caught up, one hand already reaching for magic that the garden allowed here — the wards that had stripped her in Veyra's cells did not reach into the Faeheart; the mountain ate such things — and she stood at the edge of the glowing pool with light pooling gold around her ankles and watched the passage mouth.

The creatures had gone still among the flowerbeds, dozens of dew-bright eyes turning toward the dark of the tunnel.

And out of that dark, into the impossible garden, flanked by soldiers who stared around them with the expressions of men walking into scripture, came Inquisitor Veyra — coat dusty, sword peace-knotted at her hip, hands conspicuously empty.

Veyra looked at Astrid across the glowing water, and she did not reach for her irons, and she did not reach for her writ.

"Astrid Halloran," she said, and her voice was not the courtroom voice. It was quieter. Almost humble. "I need to talk to you. Not as your Inquisitor." She glanced at the blooming, chiming, impossible dark around her, and something in her face cracked and reassembled. "As someone who has seen what's happening to the sky."
