## 17. Veyra's Turn

Inquisitor Veyra had spent nineteen years believing that the world was a ledger, and that her job was to keep the columns straight. Magic on one side. Order on the other. Every arrest a small balancing, every hanging a line drawn firmly beneath a sum.

The ledger had stopped adding up somewhere around the third frozen harvest.

She had gone north herself to verify it—refused to send clerks, refused to trust reports, because a woman who built her life on the integrity of records ought to at least look at them with her own eyes. What she found in the northern provinces would have shamed any honest accountant. Orchards budding in the wrong month and then dying in bud. Rivers that forgot how to freeze and then forgot how to flow. A village where every child under ten had stopped dreaming entirely, sleeping like stones all night and waking without words, their mothers holding them and weeping into hair that smelled of nothing at all.

And in her own strongbox, wrapped in oilcloth where she had hidden it from even her own clerks: forty-one confiscated charms. Fever-knots. Luck-threads. A little clay bird that, when she held it too long, seemed to want to sing.

She had arrested people for selling those things. She had watched them hang for worse. And the whole time, the seasons had been dying like an old dog no one wanted to admit was dying, and the law she served had been, at best, a very tidy way of arranging the furniture in a burning house.

So she rode east. Alone, which scandalized her escort until she ordered them home. Through Ashfield, where the blight had been healed—she saw it plainly, green standing in a black field like a single honest word in a forged letter—and through the fen, where a witch with one eye looked at her warrant and laughed until she had to sit down on a stump.

"You're the one," the witch said, wiping her eyes. "The kind one. The one who keeps the charms."

"I destroy confiscated materials," Veyra said. "Eventually."

"Mm." The witch looked at her the way a farrier looks at a horse with a bad shoe—not unkindly, but with the certainty of work ahead. "You'll want the hollow mountain. East past the drowned chapel, follow the singing river. And Inquisitor—" the old woman's voice dropped its humor entirely, which was somehow more frightening than the laugh, "—whatever you're carrying in that box, you'd best decide soon whether it's evidence or inheritance."

Veyra rode on through the night, and did not open the strongbox once, and thought about it the entire time.

* * *

The mountain announced itself before she saw it. That was the only way to describe it. The air changed first—a warmth like a hearth-room in a cold house, rising out of the ground itself—and then the birds, ordinary gray hedge-birds, began flying toward the peak in a slow, deliberate stream, as though summoned to a meeting they were late for. And then, faintly, threaded under the wind: voices. Many voices, badly coordinated, singing something old.

It sounded, Veyra thought, like a choir rehearsing in a barn. Which was either the most hopeful thing she had ever heard or the most damning evidence yet that the world had gone mad.

She left her horse tied at the treeline with its saddlebags full of oats and a note giving her name and rank, because if she did not come back, someone ought to know where to look. Then she climbed.

The entrance was a cleft in the mountainside hung with vines that should not have survived the altitude, let alone the season. She ducked through it with her hand on her sword out of pure habit, and immediately felt foolish, because the passage beyond opened into—

A garden.

Inside a mountain. Under stone, under winter, under every law of agriculture she had ever enforced, a garden spread out in terraces of impossible green. Fruit trees heavy out of season. Flowers she recognized from her grandmother's stories and flowers she did not recognize at all, some of them turning gently to watch her pass, which she decided firmly not to think about. Small creatures moved among the rows—creatures half-familiar, like animals remembered incorrectly. One of them, a fox with feathers instead of fur along its spine, regarded her with an expression of profound bureaucratic disapproval, as if she were late for an appointment made generations ago.

"Right," Veyra said quietly, to no one. "Ledger's crooked. Noted."

She followed the sound of the singing.

* * *

She found them in a natural amphitheater near the garden's heart, and her first coherent thought was that this was the saddest army ever mustered. A woman with a drum sat on a rock, drinking from a flask he clearly wasn't supposed to have. A tall singer lay on a bier, bandaged, propped up on cushions, conducting with two fingers when she had the strength. A handful of soldiers—Veyra's own soldiers, gods help her, men whose transfers she had signed—stood in an uncertain row looking like children asked to perform at a family gathering. And at the center, thin and travel-worn and unmistakable even from behind, stood Astrid Halloran, last apprentice of the Lanternwright Guild, wanted in three provinces, teaching a traitor magistrate to hold a note.

Corren. She knew his face from a hundred official functions. He looked terrible. He looked, she thought, like a man who had finally put down a weight and discovered it had been holding him upright.

They hadn't seen her. They were mid-rehearsal, if you could call it that.

"No, no," Astrid was saying, patient in the way of someone who has explained the same thing eleven times and expects to explain it twelve more. "Master Corren, you're rushing. The covenant-song isn't a march. It breathes."

"It breathed differently in my day," Corren said with dignity.

"In your day the covenant worked."

A silence. Tam Breck took a long pull from his flask. On the bier, Sera Ilvane—the mute singer, though rumor said she had a word back now, maybe two—pressed her fingers briefly to her lips, which Veyra somehow understood meant *be kind*.

"Again," Astrid said softly. "From the turn."

They began again, and this time Veyra heard it properly: the song underneath the song, the one the garden was humming, the one the birds had been flying toward. It was imperfect. It frayed at the edges. But it was real, and it was aimed at something vast and listening just beyond the garden's far wall, where the stone itself glowed faintly, like a banked fire.

Veyra stepped forward, and her boot crunched on gravel, and every head turned.

She had planned this moment for three hundred miles. She had drafted speeches. She had, at one low point outside Ashfield, composed a formal apology suitable for reading aloud to a magistrate, and then burned it out of embarrassment.

What came out was: "Don't stop on my account. You were getting somewhere around the second verse."

Tam Breck choked on his drink. One of the soldiers made a noise like a kettle.

Astrid stared at her. Up close, Veyra could see what the years of hiding had done to her—the watchfulness worn smooth by exhaustion, the guild mark half-visible at her sleeve. But mostly Veyra saw a young woman trying to decide whether to run, fight, or laugh, and losing to laughter.

"Inquisitor Veyra," Astrid said. "You've come a long way to arrest four singers, a drummer, and a magistrate."

"I've come a long way," Veyra said, "to ask a question." She unbuckled her sword belt—slowly, letting everyone see—and laid it on the gravel. Then, after a moment's consideration, she set her warrant case beside it. The fox-creature padded over and sat on the warrant case. Nobody moved to evict it.

"The question," Astrid said carefully.

"The seasons are failing. I've seen it myself, in the north, with my own eyes, which is more than the Council can say, since they've stopped leaving the capital except to attend each other's dinners. I've read the old records. I've interrogated witches." She paused. "One of them laughed at me for quite a long time, but eventually she talked. I know what the covenant is. I know what happens if it isn't reforged. And I know—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the honesty of it, "—that everything I've spent nineteen years enforcing was built on top of a lie told by a frightened man to save his own skin, and that I helped make the lie comfortable."

"That's several questions," Tam observed.

"It's one question with a long preamble," Veyra said. "The question is: what can I do?"

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that the feathered fox got bored and wandered off with the warrant case, which Veyra chose to interpret as a legal ruling.

"You could start," Sera Ilvane said from the bier, her voice rusty but present, "by explaining why. Truly. Not the speech."

Veyra considered lying, decided against it on professional grounds—she had always been a poor liar, which was inconvenient in an inquisitor and had made her excellent at catching them—and told the truth instead. About the northern villages and the dreamless children. About the orchards budding wrong. About the strongbox.

She walked to where she'd left her pack, and brought back the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped it in front of them. Forty-one charms, arranged in careful rows, each tagged with the date and place of confiscation in her neat clerk's hand. Fever-knots. Luck-threads. The little clay bird.

"I was supposed to burn these," she said. "Every one. It's regulation. Confiscated magical materials are destroyed within thirty days." She looked down at them. "I have had some of these for six years. I told myself I was preserving evidence." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself a great many things. But when the children stopped dreaming, I took out the fever-charms and held one, and I understood that I hadn't been preserving evidence. I'd been keeping a museum of everything I'd taken from people who needed it, because some part of me knew the arrests were wrong and couldn't make myself finish the crime."

Astrid picked up the clay bird. Turned it over in her hands. When she held it, Veyra saw her shoulders soften, the way a person softens hearing a familiar voice in another room.

"This was Marta Ellingsen's work," Astrid said quietly. "She sold these at the winter market. Three coppers. She gave them free to anyone who couldn't pay." She looked up. "You arrested her."

"I did. Two years ago, autumn market." Veyra met her eyes. "She was released after six months. The charge was reduced because she cooperated. I wrote the recommendation myself—it's the only mercy I ever managed to fit inside the law, and I've been ashamed of how small it was ever since."

"She died last winter," Astrid said. "Fever. There was no one selling fever-charms anymore."

The words landed the way true things land—heavy, clean, no argument possible. Veyra nodded once. "Then there is nothing I can say that unpicks that thread. So I won't try. I'm asking what I can do going forward, and I'll accept whatever answer you give, including none."

Astrid looked at her for a long moment. Then at Sera. Then at Tam, who shrugged elaborately and said, "Don't look at me, I'm just the drummer. Well. Allegedly."

"We need voices," Astrid said at last. "That's the plain truth of it. The covenant-song needs to be carried by more than four people and a handful of conscripted soldiers who'd rather be anywhere else." She glanced apologetically at the soldier row, and one of them, a big farm-boy private named Osk, had the grace to look sheepish rather than offended. "The Faeheart takes the song and weighs it. Last rehearsal, it weighed us and found us light. We need the song to mean something. To be carried by people who chose it."

"And you're asking whether I choose it," Veyra said.

"I'm asking whether you want to," Astrid said. "Not whether you think you should. Whether you want to. Those aren't the same thing, and the Faeheart knows the difference. It doesn't accept anything false." A ghost of a smile. "Believe me. I've checked."

Veyra looked at the garden. At the glowing wall of stone where something vast waited, patient as geology. At the ragged, stubborn, ridiculous collection of people who had walked further and lost more than she ever had, and were still here, still singing, badly, on purpose.

"Nineteen years ago," she said, "I joined the Inquisition because I watched a hedge-witch save my brother's life with a charm, and then watched the magistrates hang her for it anyway, and I decided the world would be safer if power like that answered to rules." She laughed, short and bleak. "I was twenty-two and certain. I'm forty-one and I've verified the failing seasons personally, so my certainty has developed complications."

"So that's a yes?" Tam said.

"That's a yes with a long preamble."

"He does that too," Sera murmured, nodding at Corren. "I'm beginning to think it's a magistrate disease."

"I prefer 'thorough,'" Corren said with great dignity, and then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone, added, "Inquisitor, your pitch will need work."

"My *what*?"

"The covenant-song. If you're joining the Choir, we should assess your range." He was already rummaging in his coat for a tuning fork, because of course he had a tuning fork; he was Corren.

"I am the most senior law enforcement officer in this province—"

"Sing the note, Veyra," Astrid said, and she was laughing now, really laughing, tired and bright, and the sound did something to the garden—the nearest fruit trees leaned almost imperceptibly toward her, the way plants lean toward sun.

Veyra closed her eyes. Drew a breath the way she'd drawn breaths in courtrooms for two decades, steady and controlled and absolutely certain.

Sang.

It was, everyone agreed later, not good. It was, however, honest—flat in places, wobbly in others, but carrying underneath it something that made Sera sit up on her bier and Tam set down his flask and the far trees lean in too. Something that sounded, faintly, like a woman who had spent nineteen years keeping order in a burning house and had finally, finally picked up a bucket.

The stone wall at the garden's heart glowed a shade warmer.

"Well," said Astrid, when the note faded. "We can work with that."

* * *

They rehearsed until the garden's false daylight dimmed toward rest. Veyra learned the first verse, then the turn, then fumbled the bridge twice and got it on the third attempt while Osk and the other soldiers—who had, it turned out, been secretly practicing harmonies in their tents for a week and were delighted to finally have permission—hummed beneath her. Corren conducted with his tuning fork like a general directing a battle he intended, this time, to win cleanly.

Afterward, while the others drifted toward the campfires the garden provided (the fires lit themselves, which Veyra elected not to have opinions about), Astrid found her sitting on a low wall, cleaning her boots out of habit, the strongbox of charms open beside her.

"You gave them away," Astrid said. "While we sang. I saw. The luck-threads—you passed them out to the soldiers."

"They'll need the luck more than my evidence locker does." Veyra scrubbed at a stubborn stain. "Halloran. Before tomorrow. I want to understand what it costs. The reforging. The witches said the Faeheart takes kindling—memories. Dearest ones. Is that true?"

Astrid was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved across her face, and Veyra saw her decide, again, to tell the truth.

"It's true. And I've already chosen what I'll give." She said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "My mother. My teacher's lessons. The shape of my own name's history. Everything that made me want to save this place." She smiled, and it was the bravest and worst smile Veyra had ever seen. "So tomorrow, when I forget you—and I will forget you, all of you—I'd like it noted for the record that tonight was worth it."

Veyra looked at her for a long time. Then she reached into her coat and produced, of all things, a small blank notebook and a pencil stub.

"Then we'd better write things down," she said briskly, in the tone she used for opening official proceedings. "Names. Dates. Who owes who an apology. I am, whatever else I am, an excellent record-keeper, and I am telling you now, Astrid Halloran: when this is over, somebody is going to sing your history back to you if I have to stand in a market square and do it myself. Consider it entered into evidence."

Astrid stared at her. And then, slowly, she laughed—wet-eyed, helpless, real—and took the pencil.

Across the garden, the stone wall pulsed gently, like a heart remembering its rhythm, and high above the mountain, the first true snow of a healing winter began, very softly, to fall.
