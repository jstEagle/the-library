## 18. The Hollow Choir Assembles

The garden beneath the mountain did not know what season it was, and so it chose all of them at once.

Snowdrops bloomed through drifts of autumn leaves. A cherry tree heavy with summer fruit stood hip-deep in a bed of crocuses, and above it all hung a ceiling of stone veined with light that moved like water. The air smelled of rain on warm earth and old honey and something else—something older than either realm, the smell of a promise kept for a very long time and then, lately, not.

Sera Ilvane was carried into that impossible garden on a bier of woven willow, because Tam Breck had insisted she be carried properly, "like a queen or a grandmother," and no one had the heart to argue with him. He had cut the willow himself from the garden's edge, apologizing to the tree as he worked, and the tree had—everyone agreed afterward—leaned slightly toward him while he did it, which was either encouragement or curiosity, and with fae things there was rarely a way to tell.

"Mind her feet," Tam said, though Sera's feet were wrapped in three layers of blanket and had been for two days. "Feet are where singers keep their luck. Everyone knows that."

"Nobody knows that," said Veyra's youngest soldier, a boy called Pell who had joined the Inquisitor's service to escape his father's turnip farm and now found himself carrying a wounded heretic through a magic garden, which was, he reflected, still better than turnips.

"I know it," said Tam. "Therefore somebody knows it."

The bier-bearers set Sera down beneath the cherry tree, where the ground was softest, and Sera opened her eyes. Her voice was still mostly gone—the wound in her side had taken more than blood—but her eyes were awake in the old way, the way Astrid remembered from the drowned chapel: eyes that listened.

"Well," Sera mouthed, looking up at the stone sky full of moving light. "It hasn't changed."

"It's gotten worse," Astrid said gently. She knelt beside the bier. "The seasons. You should see Corvane in winter now. Winter doesn't end. It just... sits there. Like a guest who's forgotten why they came."

Sera's mouth curved. *Like your cooking,* she mouthed, and Tam laughed so hard he nearly dropped the drum.

* * *

Corren Halloran arrived last, because Corren Halloran arrived last to everything now; his body had decided, somewhere between the prison cart he'd escaped and the mountain pass, that it would move at exactly the speed of consequence and no faster.

He came limping down the garden path with one boot missing—he'd lost it crossing a river and the river had declined to give it back—and when the half-forgotten creatures of the garden saw him, they did not flee, which surprised everyone. One of them, a small gray thing like a hedgehog that had been designed by someone working from memory rather than reference, waddled right up to him and sniffed his ruined boot.

"Hello," Corren said, because what does one say.

The creature looked at him for a long moment with its mismatched eyes, then sat down on his remaining boot and refused to move.

"He likes you," said Astrid, coming over.

"That seems like poor judgment on his part."

"He's a fae creature. Judgment isn't really their department." She hesitated. Then, quieter: "You came."

Corren looked at her—at the apprentice he had betrayed, who had freed him anyway, whose face held neither forgiveness nor accusation but something harder than both, which was simply the truth—and he nodded once.

"The covenant is dying," he said. "I helped kill it. It seemed only fair I help carry the body back."

"That's not how you carry a living thing, Master Corren."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm learning. Slowly. At the speed of consequence."

The hedgehog-thing made a sound that might have been a snort, and if a fae creature can laugh at a man, then this one did.

* * *

Veyra's soldiers gathered at the garden's edge and did not come further in.

There were eleven of them. They had marched out of Corvane under sealed orders to arrest a fugitive charm-seller, and somewhere over the last hundred miles those orders had curdled into something none of them could name. Their captain—a broad, weathered woman named Doss—had watched Veyra herself kneel in the snow outside the mountain and ask a witch for directions, and after that, Doss figured, the chain of command was more of a suggestion.

"They won't cross the threshold," Veyra said to Astrid. She stood with her hands clasped behind her back, watching her own soldiers the way a shepherd watches sheep near a cliff. "They're afraid."

"Of the garden?"

"Of being wrong." Veyra's voice was even. "They've spent their whole lives enforcing the idea that this place is poison. You don't unlearn that by walking through a door. You unlearn it slowly, or you never do." She paused. "I'm still unlearning it myself. Ask me again in ten years."

Astrid studied her. Three weeks ago this woman had sat across a cell table and asked, in perfect sincerity, what magic had ever actually given the poor. Astrid had hated her for it. Now she understood the question had been real—not a blade, but a hand reaching out in the dark, feeling for something solid.

"Bring them in anyway," Astrid said. "Not to fight. To listen. That's all the Choir ever needed most people to do."

Veyra raised an eyebrow. "You want my soldiers in your audience?"

"I want everyone in your audience. The covenant doesn't bind me to the Faeheart alone. It binds everyone who ever ate bread grown under its seasons." Astrid smiled tiredly. "Which is everyone."

Veyra considered this. Then she turned, walked to the edge of the garden, and said, in the voice she used for death sentences and tax assessments, which was somehow the same voice:

"Gentlemen. Ladies. We are going to stand among the flowers and we are going to listen to singing. Anyone who has a problem with that may report to me directly, and I will explain it to them at length."

No one reported to her directly.

* * *

They rehearsed at dusk, or what passed for dusk under a mountain—the light in the stone veins dimming to a deep amber, the garden settling like a cat.

Tam took his place on a flat stone near the center, drum across his knees. His hands shook. They had shaken for twenty years, since the night in the purges when his rhythm had driven a mob's march and a man named Aldous Ferrin had died under it, and every beat since then had been an argument with that memory. But he was here. Astrid had offered him neither forgiveness nor judgment, only the truth, and the truth had turned out to be heavier than guilt and easier to carry, because at least it went somewhere.

Sera lay propped against the willow bier, too weak to stand, her lips shaping words that made no sound. But the garden heard her anyway. Where her breath touched the grass, small white flowers opened, one by one, like ears.

Corren stood at the far edge with the cantor's bell in his hand. He hadn't rung it yet. He turned it over and over, this small brass thing that had once commanded choirs of forty voices, wondering if a bell could forgive its ringer, and deciding that probably it couldn't, and ringing it anyway when the time came, because duty and redemption look identical from the outside and only differ in the cost.

And Astrid stood in the middle of them all, guild mark bare on her arm for the first time in three years, and taught them the song.

It was not a complicated song. That was the first surprise. The covenant-song was old the way rivers are old—all of its complexity worn smooth, leaving only the current. Seven phrases. A rising line, a falling line, a hold in the middle where the wild and human realms were meant to clasp hands. The map had given her the notes. The garden itself gave her the rest: whenever she sang a phrase correctly, the nearest flowers turned toward her, and when she sang it wrong, they very politely turned away, which was the gentlest criticism she had ever received and also the most effective.

"Again," she said. "From the rising line. Tam, softer—you're not driving a mob, you're rocking a cradle."

"I know how to rock a cradle," Tam muttered. "I rocked a cradle in '89. Whole village slept through the raid because of me." He tapped the drum, soft as heartbeat, and the ground beneath them seemed to lean in.

"Sera—just breathe the third phrase. Don't sing it. Breathe it."

Sera breathed it, and the cherry tree dropped three blossoms out of season, and Tam swore softly and happily.

"Master Corren. The bell on the fourth. Just once. Let it ring."

Corren lifted the bell. His hand hesitated at the top of the arc—for one visible moment, the whole weight of twenty years hanging there, the law he wrote, the guild he buried, the girl whose name he'd traded away—and then he let it fall.

The note rang out clean and silver, and every creature in the garden stopped moving at once, and for a heartbeat the whole hollow mountain felt like a held breath.

Then they sang it together, all of them, the ragged remnant: a mute singer breathing, a guilty drummer keeping time, a traitor's bell, a charm-seller's threadbare voice, and around the edges eleven soldiers humming badly because they didn't know the words but couldn't bear not to join.

The song rose. The song fell. The song reached the hold in the middle—

—and nothing happened.

* * *

They tried again. And again. Four times, five, six, until Tam's palms bled and Sera's breathing went shallow and the amber light in the stone dimmed toward true dark.

On the seventh attempt, the garden itself answered—not with the blaze of renewal they'd hoped for, but with a single image, rising up out of the ground like heat-shimmer: the Faeheart's inner gate, the one at the garden's far end, wreathed in living root. And across the gate, plain as writing, the kindling-flame of the covenant burning low. Not out. Low. Fed, these long years, on whatever scraps of devotion still leaked through—from Sera's garden, from fen-witches' grumbles, from farmers who left bread out for no one in particular. Embers. Only embers.

And beneath the flame, a shape. A hollow, human-shaped hollow in the heart of the fire, waiting to be filled.

"Oh," said Corren quietly. He had read the old cantors' texts; he knew a price when the world drew him a picture of one. "Oh, child. No."

"What?" Tam looked between them. "What's it saying? I don't read garden."

"It's saying the song is right," Astrid said. Her voice was steady, which surprised her. "We're singing it perfectly. That's not the problem." She looked at the shimmering gate, at the hollow waiting in the flame, and felt the truth settle onto her shoulders like a coat she'd been fitted for all her life without knowing. "The problem is the fuel. The covenant runs on love. Dearest memories, freely given—it told me that when I first came here, and I thought—I hoped—" She stopped. Started again. "It wants everything that makes the singer want to save the world. That's the kindling. That's always been the kindling."

Silence in the garden. Even the flowers seemed to stop turning.

"Then we find another singer," said Veyra at last, practical as a hammer. "Someone with fewer dear memories."

"There's no such person," said Sera, aloud—one cracked, rusty, astonishing word, her first full spoken sentence in days, and everyone flinched at the sound of it. She glared at them all from the bier, daring anyone to make a fuss. "Everyone loves someone. That's the whole point of the covenant. That's why it works."

"Then we wait," said Doss from the soldier's line. "Feed it slow. Embers can grow."

"The seasons can't," said Astrid. "Spring didn't come to Ashfield this year. Next year it won't come to Corvane. There's no version of waiting that ends well."

She walked toward the gate. The hedgehog-thing trotted at her heel, and she bent and picked it up, and held it against her chest, and it let her, which fae creatures almost never do.

"Astrid." Corren's voice broke on the second syllable. "Whatever you're about to say—"

"I'm not going to decide tonight." She turned, and gave them all a smile that fooled absolutely no one, least of all herself. "Tonight we sleep. Tomorrow I'll inventory what I have. That's what the guild taught us—count your stock before you spend it." Her smile flickered. "I just wanted you all to know that the song works. We got that far. Whatever happens next, seven people sang the covenant-song in the garden under the mountain, and the garden heard it. Nobody's done that in twenty years."

Tam slung his drum under his arm. "Get some sleep, lass," he said gruffly. "Inventories keep."

But Astrid stood a moment longer at the gate, one hand pressed to the living wood, feeling through it the low burn of the flame and the hollow shaped exactly like a person who loved too much.

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone, the guild mark on her arm began—very faintly—to glow.
