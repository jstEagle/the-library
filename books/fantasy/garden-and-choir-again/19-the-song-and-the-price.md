## 19. The Song and the Price

The garden beneath the mountain did not know it was winter.

That was the first thing Astrid understood, standing at its threshold with Sera's bier behind her and Tam's drum silent at his hip. Above them, in the world of men, frost had eaten the orchards down to black lace and the rivers wore their ice like armor. But here, in the hollow heart of a mountain that had been singing to itself for three hundred years, roses opened their fists in the dark, and moss glowed green as spilled wine, and something vast breathed slowly beneath the soil — patient, wounded, waiting.

"It's beautiful," Veyra said beside her, and her voice cracked on the word like a girl's.

Astrid had not expected the Inquisitor to say anything at all. She had expected silence, or orders, or the particular flatness Veyra used when she was deciding how much truth a room could bear. Instead the woman stood with her hands loose at her sides — no chains, no writs, no soldiers' formation — and looked at the impossible garden the way Astrid imagined a person might look at a door they'd spent their whole life nailing shut, only to find it had never been locked.

"Don't get sentimental," Tam muttered. "It's about to ask us for everything we've got."

"It already has," said the garden.

Or rather: something spoke from within the garden, in a voice like wind through wheat, like water finding a crack in stone, like every lullaby Astrid had ever half-remembered sung all at once by a choir of leaves. The creatures tending the flowerbeds — small ones, furred and scaled and feathered, with too many joints in their fingers and eyes the color of old coins — set down their tools and turned toward her.

*You have brought voices,* the Faeheart said. *Bring also the kindling.*

Astrid's mouth went dry. She had known. The map's final page had told her in its shifting living ink; the fen-witches had told her in their bitter, sideways way; even Corren, limping into the garden two days past on a leg he'd broken escaping custody, had told her with his eyes downcast, unable to look at her while he said it. *Any mortal who reforges the covenant must give the Faeheart their dearest memories as kindling. Not the shallow ones. It will know. It always knows.*

Knowing a thing and standing inside the knowing were different countries entirely.

"How much?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "How much does it need?"

The wind-through-wheat voice considered her. Somewhere in the dark of the mountain, water dripped a slow count.

*All,* it said gently. *All of what is dearest. That is what dearest means.*

* * *

They gave her the night. It seemed absurd — a night, as if this were a wedding eve and not an execution of the self — but the Faeheart was old and courteous in the way of very old things, and so the Choir gathered around a fire of glowing moss-wood and ate bread the little tenders brought them, warm and faintly sweet, and pretended this was any other vigil.

Sera lay on her bier propped against a root, her wound bound in fen-witch salve that smelled of pine and iron. She could speak now — words came back to her in handfuls, like coins recovered from a well — but she saved them, spending only when necessary. Now she reached out and caught Astrid's wrist.

"Tell me," she rasped. "What you'll lose. Say it out loud. It helps."

"Does it?"

"No," Sera said, and her ruined laugh was almost a song. "But lying alone about it is worse."

So Astrid said it out loud, to the fire, to the Choir, to the listening dark.

"My mother." The words were stones in her mouth. "Her face first. I can feel which memories are deepest — the guild taught us to inventory ourselves, you know, before every working. Center yourself, name your anchors. My mother teaching me to knead bread, flour to her elbows, singing off-key on purpose to make me laugh. The shape of her hands. The sound of my name in her mouth." She swallowed. "And Corren. The lessons. Him putting my fingers on the cantor's strings, telling me the song doesn't live in the notes, it lives in why you sing them. He was — he was the closest thing to a father the guild ever gave me, and then he—" She stopped. Started again. "I hate him and I love him in the same breath, and the Faeheart will take both, because both are true."

Tam turned his drum slowly in his hands. "You could walk away," he said. "Nobody here would think less."

"Yes, they would," said Sera.

"Yes, I would," said Veyra, from the edge of the firelight, and there was no cruelty in it — only the terrible honesty Astrid had come, reluctantly, to trust. "I would think less of you, and I would deserve to think less of myself for it. Go on, Halloran. Finish the list. Name your own history too. You've been circling it all night."

Astrid looked at her. "You've gotten bold, Inquisitor."

"I've gotten tired," Veyra said. "There's a difference, and it took me forty years and one dying witch in a cell to find it. Finish the list."

"My name's history," Astrid said quietly. "Where it came from. Who gave it to me. Why Halloran, why Brightwater's daughter, why the guild mark sits where it sits on my arm. If I give that up, I'll be a woman with a name she can't explain, wearing a scar she can't read, loving people she can't remember loving." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'll be exactly what I was in Corvane. A charm seller with empty sleeves. Except this time I'll have done it to myself."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, thunder rolled — or perhaps it was the mountain clearing its throat.

"You won't be empty," Sera said. "Listen to me, apprentice-of-no-guild. The covenant isn't made of memories. Memories are just the kindling. What burns — what actually burns — is love. And love, once given, doesn't ask for receipts." She squeezed Astrid's wrist, hard, with a singer's strength. "I forgot my own voice for six years. Do you know what I learned? Nothing truly sung is ever wholly lost. It goes somewhere. It waits."

"That's either very comforting or very frightening," Tam said.

"Both," said Sera. "That's how you know it's true."

* * *

Dawn, if the garden kept such hours, arrived as a slow brightening of the moss along the walls, green light rising like tide. The tenders came for her without ceremony. One of them — a creature like a fox crossed with a moth, wings dusted silver — pressed its paw-hand into hers and led her down a path of white flowers toward the center of the garden, where the ground opened into a bowl of living stone, and the stone sang.

Astrid felt it through her boots before she heard it with her ears: the covenant-song's skeleton, still humming after three hundred years of neglect, worn thin as an old coin but unbroken. This was the thing she had been chasing since a dying man bled out in her cellar. This was the reason the seasons failed, the reason children stopped dreaming, the reason crops grew black in Ashfield and the wild places crept closer to the plowed fields every year. The bridge between realms, left unsung, was falling down — and the Faeheart's wildness was already bleeding through the gaps.

The rest of the Choir assembled at the bowl's rim. Tam with his drum. Sera carried down and settled among cushions, too weak to stand, her voice ready anyway. Corren, gray-faced, holding his cantor's bell in both hands like a man holding a confession. Veyra and her twelve soldiers, uncertain, unarmed, arranged in something that was almost — Astrid realized with a start — a choir formation. Someone had taught them to stand like that. Someone long ago.

"The song needs a lead voice," Corren said. His voice broke on *lead*. "It was always meant to—"

"I know," Astrid said. "You taught me. Apparently some things stick deeper than others."

He flinched. She hadn't meant it as mercy, but it landed as mercy, and she watched him accept it the way a starving man accepts bread.

She stepped down into the bowl.

The stone was warm under her bare feet — when had she removed her boots? The garden had its own opinions about preparation. The humming rose through her ankles, her knees, her spine, tuning her like an instrument, and Astrid understood suddenly that the Faeheart had been preparing her since the moment she entered, perhaps since the moment she was born, perhaps since the moment some ancestor of hers first sang a boundary into being between the wild and the tame.

*Are you afraid?* the garden asked, gentle as snowfall.

"Terrified," Astrid said.

*Good,* said the Faeheart. *False kindling burns cold. Yours will burn true.*

She began to sing.

It was the covenant-song, or its seed of it — the melody the map had shown her, the one Sera hummed in fragments, the one Tam's drum remembered in its bones. Her voice caught it up and the Choir answered: Tam's drum rolling in like summer thunder, Sera's thread of soprano weaving gold through the melody even flat on her back, Corren's bell tolling the deep harmonic, and then — hesitantly, crackingly, beautifully — twelve soldiers' voices, untrained and earnest, swelling underneath like a congregation finding its courage.

The garden blazed. Flowers burst open in waves. The little tenders began to dance, or pray, or both.

And the Faeheart came to collect.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. Astrid had braced for pain, for tearing, for the ripping-out feeling she'd imagined a hundred times on the road north. Instead it was like warm water rising — the memory of her mother simply *dissolving*, flour-white hands fading from her mind's eye even as she sang, and she sang harder because the song needed her grief and her grief was leaving, and she thought, wildly, *this is what it means to spend yourself, this is what mother would have wanted, mother who am I thinking of—*

Corren next. The workshop. His big careful hands over hers on the strings. *The song lives in why you sing it.* Gone. She felt the lesson leave and kept singing on muscle and mystery, because her body knew the song even as her mind lost the reasons, and Sera had been right, oh, Sera had been right, the love burned on without its receipts—

Her name. *Halloran.* The history of it, the who-and-why, draining away like color from a photograph. She held onto the word itself — names are stubborn things — but the story behind it went, and she was a woman named Astrid who could no longer say why.

The song crested. The covenant blazed.

Every soul in the garden felt it: a great ringing *yes* that passed up through the mountain, out through the stone, across the miles and the realms. Astrid heard the seasons turn — audibly, as promised by no one and true anyway — a groan and a shudder and then a rushing, like a frozen river remembering how to move. Snowmelt somewhere far away. A thaw cracking open. Wind changing its mind mid-gust. The wild realm and the human realm locked hands through her voice, through the Choir's ragged glory, through twelve soldiers singing badly and sincerely, and the bridge that had been falling for three hundred years rebuilt itself note by note, and the last thing Astrid gave — freely, gladly, without being asked — was the memory of how afraid she had been.

Then it was quiet.

She stood in the bowl of stone, breathing hard, tears on her face that belonged to no grief she could name. The garden was brighter than it had ever been. The tenders were cheering, or whatever passed for cheering among fox-moths. Tam was laughing and crying into his drum. Sera was singing — really singing, full-voiced, the despair gone out of her like a fever broken.

And Astrid Halloran — a name that now fit her like a borrowed coat — looked at the faces around the bowl's rim and felt warmth toward all of them, and recognition of none.

A gray-haired man with a broken leg was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read, holding a small brass bell, and when their eyes met he smiled like a man being forgiven, and Astrid smiled back politely, the way you smile at a stranger who seems to need it.

"Well," she said, to no one, to everyone. "Someone want to tell me what happens now?"
