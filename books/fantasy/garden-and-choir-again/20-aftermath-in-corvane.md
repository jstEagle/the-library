## 20. Aftermath in Corvane

The proclamation went up on the Guildhall doors on a Tuesday, because Magistrate Pelloran insisted that matters of state be posted on working days, when the most people would see them and the fewest would be drunk enough to argue.

*By order of the Provisional Council of Corvane,* it read, *the Edict of Silence is hereby struck from law. The practice of magic is no longer a crime. All sentences passed under the Edict are commuted. All pending warrants are void.*

Below the formal text, someone had nailed a smaller piece of paper with plainer words: *They're calling it the Covenant Restoration Act. It means you can stop hiding.*

By noon there was a crowd. By evening there was an argument, because this was Corvane, and by the following morning there was a woman standing at the doors with a bucket of whitewash and a brush, staring at the proclamation as though it were written in a language she had once known and forgotten.

Her name was Marta Quill, and three years ago she had bought a fever-charm from a girl with careful hands and a sleeve pulled down over her wrist. The charm had worked. Her grandson had lived. She had never told anyone, because telling meant hanging, and her grandson needed feeding more than he needed truth.

Now she dipped her brush and painted a small lantern on the wall beside the proclamation. Just a lantern. Nothing more. But half the crowd understood it, and by nightfall there were forty lanterns on the Guildhall wall, and by week's end so many that the Council sent a man to scrub them off, and the man looked at them for a long while and then reported that the mortar was somehow damaged and the paint could not be removed without structural risk.

Marta Quill slept better that night than she had in three years.

* * *

Corren Halloran watched the crowd from his study window and did not sleep well at all.

He had returned to the city four days after the mountain—limping, hollow-eyed, and carrying a cantor's bell that no longer rang for him alone. He had expected arrest. He had drafted his confession in his head on the road, polished it like a stone, rehearsed the parts that hurt.

Instead, the city had simply gone on around him, changed at its foundations, and left him standing in his own house like a ghost at its own funeral.

He sat at his desk now, before him the confession he had written out fair in his best magistrate's hand. Eleven pages. He had been thorough. That was the least mercy he owed anyone.

The hard part was not writing it. The hard part was knowing what Astrid had paid, and knowing that no confession, however thorough, would return one single memory to her. He had taught her her first song. He knew that much still—it was in the papers Veyra's people had gathered, in the testimony of the singers. And he knew that somewhere in the Faeheart's keeping was the version of Astrid who remembered learning it, and that she did not know him now, and never might.

He had outlawed magic to save his own skin, and told himself it was mercy. He had turned her over to Veyra to protect his position, and told himself it was duty. There was no mask left to wear. So he wore none.

On Thursday he walked to the Guildhall steps in broad daylight, mounted them slowly because of his leg, and read all eleven pages aloud in a voice that carried. He named names—his own first and last. He described the night he had testified against the Guild, the letters he had forged, the warrant he had helped draft for people whose songs he had sung beside for twenty years.

When he finished, the square was very quiet.

An old woman near the front—the one who had painted the first lantern, though he didn't know it—said, clearly, "And what will you do about it?"

"Whatever the law allows," Corren said. "And then whatever it doesn't."

In the end the law allowed exile. Some shouted for the rope; more, in the end, did not, because the confession had included the names of every person he had condemned, and their families stood scattered through the crowd listening to a man dismantle his own life brick by brick, and hatred is difficult to sustain when the hater keeps handing you the bricks.

He left the city at dawn on Sunday, on foot, with a pack and the bell. At the gate he paused and looked back once at the rooftops, at the market district where a girl used to sell luck-knots with her sleeve pulled down.

"I'm sorry," he said, to the city, or to her, or to no one. "I'll carry what I can."

Then he walked north, toward Ashfield, where the blighted farms were coming green again and they said a man with a good voice and a guilty conscience might be useful.

* * *

Veyra resigned on Monday.

She did it in writing, which disappointed the Council, who had hoped for a scene. The letter was two sentences long: *The laws I swore to enforce have been repealed, and I find I do not mourn them. I resign my commission effective immediately.*

What the letter did not say was that she had spent the past week cataloguing her private collection—two hundred and eleven confiscated charms, each wrapped and labeled in her own hand—and deciding, one by one, what to do with them.

She gave the fever-charms to the charity ward at Saint Ellowen's. She gave the luck-knots to the dockworkers' guild, who laughed and asked if they actually worked, and she said, "Better than the warrants ever did," and they laughed again and took them. The rest she distributed through the poor quarters with no explanation at all, going door to door herself, in plain clothes, and discovering to her considerable discomfort that people were glad to see her.

"You're the Inquisitor," said a cobbler's widow, taking a warming-charm from her hand suspiciously.

"I was."

"And now you're giving things away."

"It seems I'm retraining."

The widow looked at her a long moment. "My husband always said you weren't cruel. He said a cruel woman wouldn't have waited until the cell was warm before questioning him."

Veyra hadn't known anyone had noticed that. She found, to her surprise, that it mattered.

On her last day she cleared out her office and found, at the bottom of a drawer, a child's drawing—a market stall, a girl behind it, a lantern overhead—taken from a witness years ago and never destroyed, because destroying it had felt like a lie even then. On the back, in her own younger handwriting: *Subject may be guild-trained. Observe.*

She sat with it for a while. Then she folded it into her coat, because some evidence you keep, not for the case, but for the debt.

She did not go looking for Astrid Halloran. She assumed the woman would come to Corvane eventually—people come home, even when home has changed—and she wanted, when that happened, to be findable.

* * *

Astrid came home in the first week of spring.

She came on foot, as she had left, with Sera's farewell still warm in her ears and Tam's drum fading behind her, and she stopped at the top of the hill above the city and looked down and felt nothing she recognized.

That was the trouble. Not grief—grief she knew, grief was an old neighbor. It was the absence underneath. She looked at Corvane's smoke and spires and her heart did not quicken. She searched herself for the feeling of coming home and found a clean, smooth emptiness, like a room swept for a guest who never arrived.

She knew, intellectually, that she had loved this city. She had written it down herself, before the mountain, in a letter to herself that she now carried like a map to a country that had sunk: *You love the smell of the tanneries in rain. You complain about it constantly but you love it. You love the pie stall by the fountain. You love the way the bells sound wrong on foggy mornings.*

She descended into the streets and tested each item against the emptiness.

The tanneries smelled of rain and rot. She noted it, the way she'd note weather. The pie stall was gone—replaced by a flower-seller, because flowers were suddenly fashionable among people who no longer feared charms and wanted something gentler to buy. The bells sounded wrong in the morning fog. She supposed they always had.

The market was worst.

She had sold charms here for three years. She knew—she must have known—every crack in the cobbles, every vendor, the exact spot where the old woman had shielded her during the raid. She walked the rows and it was all new. Bright banners, because the Lantern Festival had been revived and expanded. Stalls selling actual magic now, legally, badly—a boy charging two coppers for sparks that fizzled, a hedge-witch with a line of customers waiting for warts. A man playing a fiddle with a charm on it that made the music drift, and children chasing the notes like butterflies.

It should have been hers, this memory. Three years of her life had happened on these stones and none of it rose to meet her.

She stood in the middle of the market square, in the spring sun, surrounded by joy, and felt like a stranger reading a letter addressed to someone with her face.

"Excuse me, miss." A flower-seller leaned over her stall. "You look lost."

"I lived here," Astrid said. "Once."

"Well, welcome back, then." The woman pressed a cut stem into her hand—a white flower, unremarkable, cheap. "First one's free. Everyone gets one. House rule since winter ended."

Astrid held the flower and did not know why her eyes stung, only that they did, and that the sting felt like something reaching for a door that wasn't there anymore.

"Thank you," she said. "Do you—did you know a girl who used to sell charms here? Luck-knots. Sleeve always down over her left wrist."

The flower-seller's face went soft. "Everyone knew her. Quiet thing. Good knots. Old Marta swears one saved her grandson." She pointed across the square. "See that wall? During the raid, Widow Anselm stood in front of her and let herself be taken. Died in the cells, poor soul, but the girl got away." She shook her head. "Funny. You've got her look about you, a bit. Around the eyes."

"Do I?" Astrid said.

She thanked her and moved on, and did not correct her, because how could she—*that was me, and I don't remember her either*—and because some part of her wanted, desperately, to hear the story of that girl from strangers, to be introduced to her own life secondhand, like a relative shown portraits of the dead.

Widow Anselm. She wrote the name down that night in her journal, under the letter to herself. *Someone died for me. Learn her. She deserves a remembering, even if mine is borrowed.*

* * *

She found her old cellar workshop on the fourth day.

The landlord had kept it sealed—"waiting for the tenant," he said, embarrassed, "though I don't recall her face, if that's not too strange"—and inside, everything was as she had left it: the workbench, the dried herbs, the scorch mark where she'd burned her remaining charms before leaving. Dust lay over it all like snow.

She stood in the doorway a long time.

There was a chair by the bench, and draped over the chair-back, a shawl she did not recognize and recognized completely—her hands knew its weight before her mind did. She picked it up and her fingers found the mended elbow without looking. *I mended this,* she thought. *I sat somewhere and mended this and I don't remember where or when or whether I was crying.*

She hung it back exactly as it was. Some things you leave as shrines.

Then she unpacked her kit, set out her knotting cord, and began, slowly, to make a luck-knot. Her hands remembered the pattern perfectly. Of course they did. Hands keep what hearts surrender.

Halfway through, the knot came out wrong—not badly, just differently, a variation she had no memory of learning—and she stared at it and laughed, a short wet sound, because it was the first new thing her body had shown her in weeks.

She finished it and set it on the bench, and opened the shutters, and let the market noise pour in.

* * *

Veyra found her there on the sixth day.

No knock—just footsteps on the stairs and then the Inquisitor, former Inquisitor, filling the doorway in plain clothes, holding her hat in her hands like a man at a funeral.

"I heard you'd come back," Veyra said. "I told myself I'd wait until you sent for me. Then I waited six days and decided patience was cowardice wearing a nicer coat."

Astrid looked up from her workbench. "You know I don't remember you."

"I know." Veyra came in and stood awkwardly, a woman built for interrogation rooms with nowhere to put herself in a workshop. "I've been trying to decide what I am to you. Enemy, probably. Former enemy. The woman who put you in a cell and asked you uncomfortable questions in a warm room."

"The warm room," Astrid said slowly, "is familiar. Isn't that strange? I don't remember you, but when you say that, something in my chest goes quiet. Like a dog hearing its own name in another yard."

Veyra swallowed. "I brought you something."

She laid the child's drawing on the bench. The market stall, the girl, the lantern. Astrid picked it up and studied it, hunting herself in the lines.

"This is supposed to be me?"

"A witness drew it. A child. I kept it because—" Veyra stopped, started again. "Because I couldn't destroy it. I told myself it was procedure. It wasn't. It was the first time in my career I looked at a warrant subject and saw a person, and I hated it, and I kept it anyway."

Astrid looked at the little drawn lantern for a long time. "You interrogated me kindly. People keep saying that. The fen-witches said it. Tam said it, grudgingly, which from Tam is practically a love poem."

"I tried to be decent inside an indecent job. It's not the same as being good."

"No," Astrid agreed. "But it's not nothing." She slid the drawing across the bench. "Keep it. You carried it this far; it's yours now. I'll make you a new one, if you like. I'm opening a shop."

Veyra blinked. "A shop."

"Charms. Legal ones. There's a stall available in the market—the flower-seller says the boy who does sparks is moving on, he's terrible at it, bless him." Astrid turned the luck-knot over in her fingers. "I don't remember why I started doing this. But everyone tells me I was good at it, and my hands agree, and these days my hands are the only witnesses I trust."

Veyra looked at her—at the guild mark showing openly on her bare wrist now, faintly luminous, as marks apparently did when the covenant ran strong—and something in her stern face loosened by a degree.

"I'll take two knots," she said. "One for me. One for—" She hesitated. "For a woman named Anselm. I signed her transfer order. She died in my cells."

Astrid went still. Then she reached for her cord. "Then we'll both owe her," she said. "Hers I'll make for free."

* * *

Spring deepened. The shop opened—no cellar this time but a proper stall, with a sign Astrid painted herself: *CHARMS, HONESTLY MADE.* Business was slow and then less slow. The boy who made sparks came by to learn knotwork and turned out to have real talent for it, and she took him on as an apprentice, because the world, apparently, needed Lanternwrights again, and someone had to be first.

People came who had known her. They told her stories—about the raid, about the fever winter, about the night she'd left with a packed bag and a burning hearth. She collected them the way she once collected charms, wrapping each one carefully, labeling it, setting it on shelves in her mind. *This is your life. It was witnessed. Try it on gently.*

Some fit. Some didn't. The pie stall by the fountain, she learned, had closed because the owner's daughter married a fishmonger from the coast, and Astrid found she could almost feel the shape of having cared about that news, and smiled at the almost.

And sometimes—only sometimes—a story would land somewhere deeper than the others. The flower-seller mentioned that the charm-girl used to hum while she worked, always the same tune, and Astrid's hands stopped mid-knot, because the tune rose in her throat unbidden, complete, a lullaby she had no memory of learning and could not stop singing.

She wrote it down that night. All eight bars. Underneath, in her journal, she added a line to the growing list:

*Something of mine is coming home. Slowly. One song at a time.*

She did not yet know how right she was.
