## 21. Garden Without a Gardener

The garden had never needed much from Sera Ilvane. That was the joke of it—the drowned chapel had been her exile, her tomb, her three years of silence, and the whole time the roses had gone on blooming as if despair were merely another kind of weather. She watered them now out of love rather than habit, which changed nothing about the roses and everything about her.

Her voice had come back in the spring, in the Faeheart's garden, when the covenant blazed and every scattered thread of the Choir felt itself pulled taut and singing. It had come back all at once, like a door flung open after years of being painted shut. The first thing she'd said was Astrid's name. The second thing she'd said was also Astrid's name, because the first one hadn't gotten an answer.

She said it again now, quietly, to no one, kneeling in the wet grass between the foxgloves.

"Astrid."

The word went out over the garden and came back unchanged. Somewhere beyond the chapel's broken roof, a wren made its opinion known, and the opinion was that Sera should stop moping and scatter some seed.

"I'm not moping," she told the wren. "I'm grieving. There's a difference. Moping is self-pity. Grief is—" She paused, trowel in hand. "Grief is just love with nowhere to put itself."

The wren did not appear moved. Wrens rarely were.

That was the trouble with getting your voice back after three years of silence: you discovered you'd become someone who talked to birds. And worse, you discovered you liked it.

* * *

Tam Breck arrived on the fourth morning of the week, as he always did, banging through the chapel door with all the grace of a man who had once kept time for the Hollow Choir and now kept time mostly for his own elbows.

"Your gate's sticking," he announced.

"Your manners are sticking," Sera said. "Also, it's a chapel, not a fortress. The gate sticks because the hinges are four hundred years old and half-drowned."

"Everything worth having is half-drowned." He set down the basket he carried—bread, cheese, a jar of honey that he pretended wasn't a bribe for her to keep tolerating him—and surveyed the garden with the critical eye of a man who had spent the winter learning, to his own horror, that he loved teaching. "Where do you want the new ones sitting?"

"New ones" meant students. Three of them this season: a fisherman's daughter named Odile who hummed like tide coming in; a stonemason's boy called Perrin whose voice cracked so spectacularly it could be heard across two fields; and a widow named Marta who had come late to singing and was making up for lost decades with a ferocity that frightened the bees.

"The shade, I think. The foxgloves make Perrin sneeze and he takes it personally."

"He takes everything personally. Good instinct in a singer." Tam lowered himself onto the chapel steps with a groan that was at least forty percent performance. His drum sat in its sling across his back, worn smooth now where his hands had worried it for months. He still didn't play for himself. But for others—for these clumsy, hopeful children of Corvane's outskirts—he played like a man paying off a debt one beat at a time.

Sera understood that arithmetic. She'd spent three years not singing because singing had once been the thing she did while the world burned. Tam had spent longer. Neither of them had been healed by the covenant so much as given back their tools, and tools, unlike wounds, required daily work.

"They're early," she said.

"Perrin slept here last night. Don't tell his mother. Actually do tell his mother, she'll box his ears, and honestly the boy could use the discipline." Tam dug the bread out of the basket and tore it in half, handing her the bigger piece without looking, which was as close to tenderness as he permitted himself before noon. "How is she?"

He didn't have to say who. There was only one *she* that mattered anymore, only one name that hung over this garden like weather.

"She came through on Tuesday," Sera said. "Sat by the well for an hour. Didn't know me." She kept her voice level, gardener-calm, the way you spoke about frost or blight. "I offered her tea. She thanked me very politely. Called me 'madam.' She asked if I'd lived here long."

Tam chewed. Swallowed. "And?"

"And I said yes. All my life, it feels like." Sera pressed her thumb into the bread until it dented. "She looked at the garden and said it was beautiful, and asked who tended it, and I said I did, and she said, *you must love it very much.*"

The words sat there in the morning air, small and unbearable.

"She remembers how to be kind," Tam said at last. "Just not why."

"That's it exactly. That's—it's like watching someone play our song with all the notes right and none of the heart. No. Worse. Like watching someone else wear her face and be *polite* in it." Sera stood abruptly, because sitting still had become impossible, and went to the rose bed where the blooms came up white as choir robes. "The Faeheart took her dearest memories as kindling. We knew that. We *watched* her choose it. And everyone tells me to be grateful—grateful!—because the seasons turn and the children dream and magic isn't a hanging offense anymore. As if gratitude and grief can't live in the same house."

"They've always lodgered together in my experience," Tam said. "Noisy pair. Never pay rent."

Despite herself—a small, treacherous despite—Sera laughed. It came out wet. Tam had that gift: he could make you laugh at the exact moment laughing was the only alternative to breaking something.

* * *

The students arrived in a clatter of bicycles and complaints. Odile first, always first, slipping through the gate like water finding its level. Then Marta, who greeted the garden with a nod usually reserved for equals. Then Perrin, emerging from behind the chapel where he had clearly, obviously, slept, hay in his hair and an expression of elaborate innocence.

"Good morning, Mistress Ilvane," he said. "Lovely day. Slept wonderfully. In my own bed. Far away."

"You have straw in your collar."

"That's from... my bed. Being straw."

Marta snorted. Odile hid her smile in her sleeve. Tam, who had appointed himself percussion master and general menace, rapped his knuckles on the drumhead twice—not playing, just marking time, the way he marked everything—and the lesson began.

Sera taught them the way she had been taught in the Choir, before the purges, before the silence: breath first, then tone, then meaning. *A note without meaning is a stone,* her own teacher had said. *A note with meaning is a seed.* She had forgotten the saying for three years and remembered it the moment her voice returned, whole, like everything else the covenant had shaken loose.

They worked on rounds today. Simple ones, old ones, songs the Choir had sung to keep the boundary between the realms supple as green wood. The students didn't know what the songs were for. They knew only that when they sang them together, the garden leaned in. Roses opened a fraction wider. The well water tasted sweeter afterward. Bees arranged themselves along the chapel wall in patterns that Perrin swore spelled something rude in Old Tongue.

Halfway through the second round, Sera stopped them.

"Hold. Hold. Odile—you're pushing. You don't need to push. The song carries itself; that's what a round *is*. You're not shouting over each other, you're weaving. Listen to Marta. Marta, sing your line again."

Marta sang it again, low and sure, a woman who had buried a husband and learned that the only honest response to loss was to make something. Her line went out over the garden like a hand laid gently on a shoulder.

And Sera felt it—that faint, familiar warmth at the edge of her awareness, the thing she had begun calling the *far-hum*. Every time someone in the world remembered Astrid Halloran kindly, somewhere the guild mark on Astrid's arm glowed. Sera couldn't see it from here. But she had been there, in the Faeheart's aftermath, when they'd first noticed it: Tam humming an old marching tune and Astrid's forearm lighting up like a lantern behind linen, and Astrid staring at it with polite bafflement, saying, *that's odd, does it often do that?*

Does it often do that. As if her own skin were a stranger's weather.

The far-hum passed through the garden now, faint as heat off stones, and Sera knew without seeing that Marta's singing had brushed against some memory of Astrid—the charm-seller, the girl who'd once sold luck-knots in Corvane's market, the stranger who'd walked into a drowned chapel and heard a song in a mute woman's garden. People remembered her in fragments now. A kindness here. A charm that had actually worked, there. Each fragment a small light on a woman's arm, in a city she no longer recognized, worn by a woman who no longer recognized them.

"Again," Sera said, and her voice only shook a little. "From the top. And this time, mean it."

* * *

In the afternoon, when the students had gone, Sera walked down to the well and sat where Astrid had sat, because grief made her sentimental and sentimentality, she had decided, was allowed.

Tam found her there an hour later. He didn't sit so much as collapse beside her, drum across his knees, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of music.

"She asked me yesterday," Sera said finally, "if the guild mark hurt. On her arm. She'd caught it glowing—someone in the market must have thought kindly of her—and she looked frightened of it. Of her own arm, Tam. And I said no, it doesn't hurt, it means people remember you fondly. And she said—" Sera's throat closed. She made herself finish. "She said, *what a strange thing, to be remembered by strangers.*"

Tam was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the roughness it got when he talked around things instead of through them.

"When we broke her out of Veyra's cells," he said, "she was more scared of us failing than of her dying. Did I ever tell you that? Lying on that cot with the wards chewing holes in her, and she's whispering, *go back, go back, don't let Sera get hurt.* Not save me. Not I'm frightened. *Don't let Sera get hurt.*" He turned the drum slowly under his hands. "That woman gave up her mother's face for us. For the whole soggy world. And now she sells charms in a shop on Bellwether Lane and calls her customers 'sir' and 'madam' and goes home to a room she doesn't remember choosing."

"I know."

"I'm not saying it to torture you. I'm saying it because somebody has to say it out loud or it turns to rot inside." He exhaled. "Veyra visits her, you know. Once a month. Walks all the way from the capital, sits in that little shop, buys a fever-charm she doesn't need. Former Inquisitor. Pays full price."

"I know that too."

"Do you know why?"

Sera looked at him.

"Because," Tam said, "every time Veyra walks in that door, Astrid lights up like a festival. Some corner of her remembers being afraid of that woman, and then remembers not being afraid, and then remembers—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—whatever it was. Trusting her. Dying next to her soldiers in a mountain garden. And the mark glows, and Astrid says, *there it is again, that funny light,* and Veyra stands there buying a charm she doesn't need with eyes like a kicked dog, and then she walks home." He shrugged. "It's the cruelest kindness I've ever seen. Or the kindest cruelty. I genuinely can't decide."

"It's both," Sera said. "Everything true is both. That's the first thing the Faeheart ever taught me."

* * *

Evening came down soft over the chapel. Sera lit the two candles that survived on the altar—the rest had drowned years ago—and did her rounds among the beds, pinching deadheads, talking to the plants because she was that sort of woman now and had stopped apologizing for it.

At the far border, where the wall had fallen open to the marsh, something new was growing. It had appeared the week after the covenant reforged: a plant none of her books named, silver-leaved, flowering in a color she could only describe as *remembered*. It smelled faintly of woodsmoke and river water and, impossibly, of a cellar workshop in Corvane where a lonely girl had once packed away her charms and burned the rest.

Sera knelt beside it. She did not touch it. Some things you tended best by leaving alone.

"You're hers," she murmured. "However you got here. You're hers."

The flower did not answer, but the far-hum stirred—one small glow, somewhere east, on a woman's arm. Someone, tonight, in some kitchen or street or shop, was remembering Astrid Halloran and smiling.

Sera stayed there until the stars came out, and then she went inside, and took out the letter she had written eleven times and burned ten of them, and began the twelfth.

*Dear Astrid. You don't know me, but I know you—*

She crossed it out. Started again.

*Dear Astrid. The garden is blooming. You would love it. You did love it.*

She stared at that sentence a long time. Then, slowly, she added:

*I am going to keep writing to you anyway. Not to make you remember—I know better than to argue with the Faeheart's prices. I'm writing so that when the memories come home, one song at a time, the way the map's ink used to move—they find a record waiting. Proof of who held your life while you were away. So here is what happened this week: Perrin slept in the chapel and lied badly about it. Marta has learned the third verse. Tam laughed twice, which for Tam is a season of miracles. And a flower grew by the fallen wall that smells like your old cellar, and I think it means the Faeheart is already beginning to give you back.*

*Come to the garden sometime. You won't know why it feels familiar. Come anyway.*

*—Sera, who tends it for you*

She sealed it, addressed it to a shop on Bellwether Lane, and set it by the door for tomorrow's post.

Outside, the silver-flowered plant turned its face toward the east, toward the city, toward the woman who would read these words without knowing why her arm glowed when she did—and in the dark, very softly, the garden began, on its own, with no one tending it, to sing.
