## 22. Choir Again

The lullaby came to Astrid on a Tuesday, in the middle of measuring out dried feverfew for a customer who could not stop sneezing.

She had been humming without noticing—she hummed a great deal these days, the way other people drummed their fingers or chewed the inside of their cheek—and then she realized she was humming something with words to it, actual words, in a language she did not speak and had never studied, and her hands went still over the scale.

*Sleep, little ember, the dark is a door.*

The melody was old. Older than Corvane's walls. It moved the way water moves in a place no one has dammed, bending around stones that had been stones since before stones were named. And it carried, underneath its sweetness, a second voice—a low green drone like roots humming in spring soil—and when she reached for that second voice her throat tightened and her eyes stung and she had to set down the feverfew entirely.

"Madam?" said the sneezing customer, a bricklayer's wife named Odette with flour on her sleeves. "Are you quite all right?"

"I don't know this song," Astrid said, which was not an answer, but Odette was the sort of woman who understood that some answers take years to arrive.

"Everyone knows that one," Odette said comfortably. "My gran sang it. Said her gran sang it before her. *Sleep, little ember,*" she warbled, badly, cheerfully, "though I only ever knew the one verse."

Astrid stood very still behind her counter while the shop bell jingled and Odette departed into the bright cold morning with her packet of feverfew.

*I don't know this song.* But it lived in her mouth as if it had always lived there, the way a scar lives in skin. She turned her left arm over and looked at the guild mark beneath her sleeve—the lantern-and-key of the Lanternwrights, faintly warm against her wrist, glowing the way it did now whenever someone nearby remembered her kindly. It glowed often these days. Corvane was learning to remember her.

But this was different. This was not someone else's memory warming her from outside. This was a song arriving *from within*, unbidden, unexplained, wearing the shape of her own voice.

She thought of the garden beneath the mountain. She thought of the price—the dearest memories given up as kindling, her mother's face gone soft as smoke, Corren's lessons dissolved like sugar in rain. She had walked out of the Faeheart hollowed and whole, seasons turning audibly overhead, and she had told herself: *that is done, that is spent, that is the shape of my life now.*

And now here was a lullaby she had never learned, sitting in her chest like a bird that had flown home to the wrong nest.

"Oh," she said aloud, to the empty shop, and laughed, and cried a little, and swept up the spilled feverfew, because even miracles do not excuse a messy counter.

* * *

It took her three more songs and most of a season to be sure.

The second came in midwinter, at Tam Breck's kitchen table, while he argued with Sera about whether new singers should learn percussion first or last. (Tam held that rhythm was the bones of all music; Sera held that Tam simply liked hitting things.) A tune rose in Astrid without warning—a round, three parts, braided like rope—and she began to sing the lowest line before she knew she was doing it, and Tam stopped mid-sentence with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

"That's a Choir round," he said. "That's *Wellspring*, that's—we sang that at my induction. Nobody's sung that in nine years. Where did you—" He set the spoon down. His face did something complicated. "Astrid. Where did you hear it?"

"I didn't," she said. "It arrived."

Sera, across the table, went very quiet in the way she went quiet when the garden spoke to her—which, these days, it did constantly, the drowned chapel garden having become something between a sanctuary and a school. Then Sera smiled, slow and certain, and said the thing Astrid had been afraid to say:

"The Faeheart is giving it back."

"Not all at once," Astrid said. Her voice shook. "Not even quickly. One song at a time. Across years. Maybe decades." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the round still resonated like a struck bell. "Why so slowly?"

"Because you gave your best memories as kindling," Tam said, "and kindling burns. You can't hand someone back ash." He rubbed his jaw. "But a fire leaves warmth in a room long after. Maybe... maybe what can't be returned whole gets returned as song. The shape of the memory, if not the memory itself. The Faeheart doesn't do anything straight, you know that. It does everything sideways and green."

"It's returning them as music," Sera said softly, "because music is how the covenant remembers. You didn't just give the Faeheart your memories, Astrid. You gave them to the *song*. And the song has been carrying them ever since. And now—" she spread her hands, "—now it's singing them home."

Astrid looked down at her hands. Hands that had sold luck-knots in alleys, that had healed a farmer's blight in open daylight, that had held a dying courier's map, that had gripped a cantor's bell in a dark cell. Hands that had surrendered everything that made saving the world worth saving, and were now, apparently, being repaid in installments.

"How many songs are there?" she asked.

"As many as there were memories," said Sera. "Which is to say: a lifetime's worth. Which is to say—" and here her smile went crooked and kind, "—you're going to be finding lullabies in your chest until you're an old woman. Possibly two old women."

Tam snorted. "Good. Gives us something to do at practice besides listen to you two argue about vowels."

* * *

Spring came to Corvane properly that year—the first proper spring anyone under forty could remember—and with it came the question of what Astrid Halloran was going to *do*.

The answer, when it finally settled on her, was almost embarrassingly small.

There was a shop on Bellwether Lane, two streets over from the market where she had once sold charms out of a basket with her sleeve pulled low and her heart hammering. It had been a cobbler's, then empty, then briefly a shop that sold nothing but pickled eels, which had failed for reasons the whole street still discussed. The rent was modest. The window faced east, so the morning light came in gold. There was a counter worn smooth by fifty years of elbows, and a cellar below that smelled of stone and old rain—the same smell, she discovered, as her first workshop, though she had no memory of her first workshop beyond what others had told her.

She signed the lease with a hand that only shook a little.

The sign took a week to paint, because she kept changing her mind. In the end she painted, in careful letters:

**HALLORAN'S CHARMWORK**
*Luck-knots • Fever-charms • Wards • Songs*
*(Songs by appointment)*

On opening day, half of Bellwether Lane crowded in out of curiosity and the other half out of loyalty, and Odette arrived with a basket of buns and announced to the room that Astrid had once cured her sneezing with nothing but feverfew and good manners, which was a generous retelling, and Astrid let her have it.

Magic was legal now. That still caught in her throat sometimes, like a laugh swallowed wrong. Legal. There was a registry office on Cordwainer Street with a cheerful clerk who stamped licenses, and Astrid had stood in line behind a nervous boy of nineteen who could make candle flames dance, and when her turn came the clerk had looked at her guild mark—visible now, worn openly, the first Lanternwright mark seen in Corvane in nine years—and had gone slightly pale and asked, in a whisper, whether she would consider teaching.

She had said yes before she knew she was going to.

So the shop sold charms in the mornings and taught in the afternoons: three students at first, then seven, then eleven, squeezed among the shelves of dried herbs and bottled moonlight and the one jar of genuine Faeheart honey that Sera sent every solstice and that Astrid refused, on principle, to sell. The luck-knots were honest work—no glamours, no bargains, just knotwork and intention and the old guild patterns her fingers remembered even when her mind did not. Sometimes her fingers knew things her mind had lost, and she had learned to trust them, the way you trust an old dog to find the road home.

And sometimes, in the quiet hours between customers, a song would arrive.

* * *

They came in all weathers, those songs.

A cradle-song in high summer, sweet and simple, that made her stop in the doorway of the shop and stand with her eyes closed while the light poured over her, and afterward she could not have said why her cheeks were wet, only that something in her had been *held*, briefly, by arms she could not remember.

A marching cadence in autumn that arrived while she was sweeping and turned her broom into a drumstick, tap-tap-tap against the floorboards, until Tam—who had come by to argue about vowels again—joined in on the counter with his knuckles, grinning like a man much younger than his regrets.

A grief-song in deep winter, on the anniversary of the night the courier died in her cellar, that came up through her like groundwater and left her kneeling behind the counter with her forehead against her arms, not sad exactly, but *full*, the way a cup is full, the way a grave is full, the way a bell is full of its own ringing.

And always, threaded through them, fragments of the two she had given away. She never got faces. She got edges. A smell of bread and lavender that must have been her mother's kitchen. The sensation of a big warm hand adjusting her elbow during a difficult interval—that must have been Corren, though Corren himself was three years in exile now, out past Ashfield somewhere, teaching farm children their letters and sending her a letter every month that always began *To the finest student I failed* and always made her cry and always made her write back.

Once, in early spring of the third year, a fragment arrived that was neither her mother nor Corren. It was a memory-shaped thing of lamplight and laughter and the smell of ink, and it felt like *home*, and she sat with it a long time trying to understand whose it was, until Sera visited, took one look at her face, and said gently:

"That one's yours, love. That's just you, in your workshop, happy. The Faeheart isn't only returning the people you loved. It's returning the life you loved. Bit by bit. It's building you back."

Astrid looked at her hands again. They were steady.

"Well," she said, when she could speak. "It always did everything sideways and green."

* * *

The mark on her arm glowed brightest on market days.

She had learned to read it the way sailors read weather. A faint warmth meant someone nearby was remembering her kindly—a customer walking home with a luck-knot, perhaps, or Odette telling the bun story yet again. A stronger glow meant a whole crowd of it, and on festival days, when the new singers performed in the square—Tam's students, Sera's students, the boy who made candles dance, a fisherman's daughter with a voice like clear water—the mark shone through her sleeve like a coal wrapped in linen, and strangers sometimes noticed and pointed, and she had stopped hiding it.

That was the strangest mercy of all: that the city remembered her better than she remembered herself. That she was held in hundreds of small kindnesses—*the charm woman who healed the blight, the one who stood up to Veyra, the one who sang the world back*—and that each kindness fed the little lantern on her wrist. She had given away her memories and the world had quietly taken notes. She was, in a sense, the best-documented stranger in Corvane.

Veyra understood this. Veyra, who resigned rather than enforce dead laws and now ran a school for warders out of a converted courthouse, came by the shop on the first Thursday of every month, bought nothing, drank one cup of tea, and sat in the chair by the window like a sword hung respectfully on a wall.

"You know what I can't get over," Veyra said once, watching a student botch a warding sigil with tremendous confidence. "I spent ten years confiscating charms. I had a whole system. Catalogued, boxed, sealed. And now I teach children to make them." She sipped her tea. "Some days I feel like a man who burned a library and got hired as a librarian."

"Some days I feel like a library that got burned," Astrid said, "and is slowly getting shelved again."

Veyra considered this. "One song at a time?"

"One song at a time."

"Hm." Veyra turned her cup. "You know, I checked, after. The mountain records. The covenant ledgers. There's a line in the oldest one—it's in the Faeheart's own hand, if wildness can be said to have handwriting. It says: *What is given in love is never spent, only lent.*" She glanced up. "I used to think that was sentiment. Now I think it's bookkeeping."

Astrid laughed, and the mark on her arm glowed, and outside the window the spring light lay along Bellwether Lane like a blessing nobody had to pay for.

* * *

The fourth year, on a grey-gold morning in late March, Astrid opened the shop early, put the kettle on, and began sorting the herb drawers, and the lullaby came back.

*Sleep, little ember, the dark is a door.*

But this time it came with more than a melody. It came with a warmth behind it, a specific warmth, the warmth of a particular room, a particular lamp, a particular pair of arms—and for one impossible, unrepeatable moment, Astrid Halloran stood in her shop holding a memory instead of a song. Not a face. Not a name. But the *weight* of being held as a child, the exact pressure of a chin resting on the top of her head, the exact pitch of a hummed note against her ear—

—and then it folded itself back into music, the way a wave folds back into the sea, leaving her gripping the edge of the counter with tears running freely and a smile she couldn't have stopped with both hands.

It was coming back. Not fast. Not all of it. But it was *coming back*, one song at a time, across years, the way spring comes—not all at once, but absolutely.

She wiped her face. She lit the lamp in the window, though the sun was already up, because the sign said *charmwork* and a charm-shop's lamp should be lit, that was simply correct. She unlocked the door and flipped the board to OPEN and stood a moment in the doorway breathing the cold sweet air of a city that had learned her name again.

Down the lane, Odette waved. Somewhere near the market, faintly, the new singers were rehearsing, and the wind carried scraps of their song to her like petals. On her wrist, the lantern-and-key glowed steady and warm, a small sun keeping its own counsel.

Astrid Halloran stepped back inside, put the kettle on again—for the first pot had gone tragically cold during the crying—and began, quietly, to hum.

*Sleep, little ember. The dark is a door.*

*And every door opens, and morning comes home.*

She did not know where she had learned it.

She was learning it still.
