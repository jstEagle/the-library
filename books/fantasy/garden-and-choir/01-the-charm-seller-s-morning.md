## 1. The Charm Seller's Morning

The sparrows woke Vesper before the bells did, which was how he knew it would be a bad day.

"Up, up, up," chattered the one who nested in the eave above his window, a scruffy hen he'd privately named Magistrate because she had the tone of someone about to issue a fine. "Up. The baker's lit his ovens. The fishwife's arguing with her husband again. Up."

"I'm up," Vesper said to the ceiling.

"You're not up. You're horizontal and lying about it."

He was, in fairness, both of those things. He hauled himself out of bed anyway, because a man who sold luck for coppers could not afford the luxury of mornings that began when he pleased. His room above the chandler's shop was small enough that two steps took him from bed to washstand, three more to the door, and if he stretched his arms he could touch both walls at once, which he sometimes did just to remind himself that the world had edges and he knew where all of his were.

Downstairs, past the smell of tallow and the chandler's cat — a vast gray creature named Duchess who regarded him with the weary tolerance of one professional acknowledging another — and out into Corvane's Gray Market while the sky was still the color of dishwater.

The Gray Market earned its name honestly. It sat in the crook where the Tanner's Ward met the Weavers' Stair, a warren of stalls and awnings and cook-smoke where everything sold was almost legal, or once had been, or would be again if the wind shifted. Charms were permitted here — *harmless* charms, the writs said, though nobody had ever satisfactorily defined harmless — and so the market filled each morning with people selling knotted thread against headaches, iron nails bent into horseshoe shapes for luck, dried lavender for sweet dreams, and other comforts priced somewhere between a prayer and a lie.

Vesper's stall was third from the fountain, under a striped awning gone pale with years of rain. He set it up the same way every morning: the folding table, the cloth, the little wooden tray of luck-knots arranged by color like a confectioner's display. Red for courage. Blue for calm. Green for harvests, yellow for journeys, white for griefs that needed easing. Each knot took him four minutes to tie, and he tied them with the particular attention of a man performing an act that was absolutely, definitely, on his mother's life, nothing more than knot-tying.

Because here was the thing about Vesper Winterbourne's luck-knots: they worked.

Not spectacularly. Not miraculously. A red knot wouldn't make a coward brave; it might make a frightened man's hands steadier for an afternoon. A blue knot couldn't cure sorrow; it could hold a weeping woman's heart together long enough for sleep to find her. Small workings. Quiet ones. The kind of magic that could pass for craftsmanship if you squinted, and Vesper had spent seven years making sure everyone squinted exactly as much as they wanted to.

Seven years, since the Choir fell silent. Since the gods stopped answering and the Crown decided that anything resembling divinity was either fraud or treason, and both were punishable, and only one was profitable for informants.

"You're brooding," said Magistrate, who had followed him down and now perched on the awning frame. "Brooding is bad for trade. Smile. You have the face of a man whose soup has been stolen."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is a closed shutter. Open it a crack. Buyers like a crack."

Vesper sighed and arranged his features into something approximating mild good humor, the expression he wore the way other men wore coats. It fit well. He'd worn it so long it had molded to him.

* * *

By midmorning the market had found its rhythm — the fishwife still arguing, now with a customer instead of her husband; the pie man ringing his bell; children weaving between legs with the impunity of the small. Vesper sold a green knot to a farmer's wife whose orchard had suffered three bad years running, a blue one to a young clerk with ink-stained fingers and shadows under his eyes, and a yellow one to a courier leaving for the coast road who bought it without ceremony, the way you'd buy bread.

And through it all, the sparrows talked.

They always talked. Sparrows were the gossips of the bird world, worse than magpies and twice as fast, and the rooftops of Corvane carried more news than any broadsheet. Most of it was dross — who was feeding whom, which chimney had a new cat, the eternal scandal of the fat pigeon on the bell tower — but Vesper listened anyway, half because information was survival and half because the alternative was listening to his own thoughts, which tended toward places he didn't care to visit before noon.

Today the gossip had a strange grain to it.

"The bell tower's quiet," reported a sparrow with a torn ear, dropping onto his table and eyeing the crumbs beside his lunch. "Quiet since yesterday. Old Rattlewing says the pigeons won't roost there anymore."

"Pigeons are fickle," Vesper said around a mouthful of bread.

"It's not just the tower." The sparrow hopped closer, feathers tight. "It's the whole east row. The chimneys by the tannery. The dovecote on Weaver's Lane. Empty, empty, empty. Birds don't go where the silence is."

"What silence?"

"That's the thing." The sparrow ruffled itself, and for a moment its black eyes held something Vesper had never seen in a sparrow before, which was gravity. "There isn't a silence. That's what I keep telling them. Silence is when sound stops. This is different. This is like — like a held breath. Like the moment before thunder, except it doesn't end. It just sits there. And it's spreading."

Vesper kept his hands moving on the knot he was tying. Red thread, over and under, over and under. Four minutes. He did not let his fingers shake, because his fingers never shook, because men whose fingers shook did not survive seven years of being small.

"Birds are strange this time of year," he said lightly. "Molt season. Half of them lose their sense along with their feathers."

"Molt season," the sparrow repeated flatly. "Right. And I suppose the rats acting strange is molt season too."

"The rats are always strange. It's their defining quality."

But when the sparrow flew off, Vesper found himself looking east, toward the rooftops beyond the Tanner's Ward, where the morning light lay ordinary and gold on ordinary tiles, and felt — faintly, like a draft under a door — something he couldn't name. Not a sound. Not a silence. A *waiting*, maybe. The city holding its breath the way a man holds his breath passing a graveyard.

He tied the thought a knot and put it away. Over and under. Four minutes. There was bread to sell luck for and no profit in flinching at sparrows' fancies.

* * *

The trouble came at midday, as trouble does, wearing an honest face.

She was a broad, comfortable-looking woman in a dyer's apron, hands stained indigo to the wrist, and she browsed his tray for a long while before choosing a white knot — grief-easing, for her sister, she said, who had lost her husband to the lung-fever in spring. Vesper wrapped it in brown paper and made change and was reaching for his practiced smile to send her off when she looked up at him with sudden, terrible directness.

"Do they really work?" she asked. "Or is it just — comfort? Thread and hope?"

Every seller of charms got this question eventually. Every seller had a ready answer. *As much as anything works, mistress. A knot holds what you put into it.* Something warm and meaningless, a coin of words accepted without examination.

Vesper opened his mouth to give it.

And then he looked at her properly — at the indigo hands and the tired eyes and the paper-wrapped knot clutched like something that mattered — and the lie caught in his throat like a fishbone.

Because the truth was that it would work. He had tied his worry into it, his attention, that quiet thing inside him that reached out along the threads and settled the world's rough edges down. Her sister would sleep easier. Not because of thread and hope. Because of him. Because of the gift he kept folded up small inside himself like a knife wrapped in wool, the gift that had gotten three neighbors taken during the Purge and had gotten him, by some mercy he'd never understood, overlooked.

"Comfort's not nothing," he said at last, carefully. "A rope bridge and a stone bridge both get you across the river. One of them costs less."

"But which one holds when the flood comes?"

"Madam," Vesper said gently, "if the flood comes, no bridge in Corvane will save you, and the smart money is on not living near rivers."

She laughed despite herself, and the dangerous moment passed, and she went off clutching her white knot. But Vesper watched her go and felt the familiar weariness settle over him like a coat two sizes too heavy. Seven years of bridges. Seven years of answers measured out in spoonfuls, of being exactly unremarkable enough, of smiling the smile that fit him like skin. Some days the smallness fit so well he couldn't feel the seams. Other days —

Other days a woman asked an honest question and he realized he hadn't answered anyone honestly in seven years, including himself.

Magistrate landed on the awning post and cocked her head at him. "You look like a man thinking about the seams."

"Don't you have a rooftop to gossip on?"

"Gossip finds me. I'm a public service." She hopped down onto the table, bold as a tax collector, and lowered her voice in the way birds do, which is to say not at all. "There's more. About the silence. The starlings came in from the Fenmarch this morning, the whole murmuration, early. Weeks early. And they're saying—" she paused, savoring it, then lost the savor, "—they're saying the marshes have gone quiet too. All of it. The herons, the frogs, everything. Just stopped. Like something walked through the country and told it to hush."

Vesper's hands had gone still on the table. He noticed, and made them move again. Over and under. Red thread.

"Sparrows love a dramatic season," he said. "First frost, last frost, the great silence of the Fenmarch. Next you'll tell me the moon's gone green."

"The moon hasn't gone green."

"There. You see how silly it sounds when I say it back to you."

The sparrow gave him a long look, the particular look of a creature deciding whether a man is stupid or pretending, and Vesper met it with the mild, pleasant blankness he'd polished over seven years until it could turn aside knives. Then she flicked her tail and went up into the eaves, and the market noise closed over the conversation like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

He stayed until the light went long and amber, longer than he needed to, selling the last few knots to stragglers and packing the tray with the slow deliberation of a man postponing something. Postponing what, he couldn't have said. The walk home was only ten minutes. The room was only four walls. Nothing waited there but cold supper and Duchess's judgment and the same tomorrow laid out neat as thread.

Except that tomorrow, the sparrows would talk again. And today they had used a word he hadn't heard in seven years, not from any beak, not once: *hush*. As in, something told the country to hush. As in, something with the authority to be obeyed.

Vesper folded his awning. The fountain behind him chuckled its idiot chuckle. Somewhere east, past the rooftops, the held breath went on holding.

"Evening, charm-seller."

He turned. A boy stood by the fountain — one of the pie-man's runners, freckled, ten or eleven, breathing hard from running. "Got a message. For the knot-man. From the sewer-gang lads." The boy wrinkled his nose. "They say the rats are all come up wrong. Gathering in the big junction under Cooper's Row, hundreds of 'em, sitting in rings. And the lads say—"

The boy hesitated, suddenly uncertain, aware he was carrying something strange.

"They say the rats are singing. Same note, over and over. And old Tam who works the drains swears on his mother he knows the tune. Swears he's heard it before, years back, in church, before the churches went quiet."

Vesper smiled the smile that fit like skin.

"Rats sing," he said pleasantly. "Well. Tell Tam the market sells rat-traps, two coppers each."

The boy grinned, pocketed his penny, and ran.

Vesper stood a moment in the amber light with his packed tray under his arm, watching the rooftops go rose and shadow, listening to the fountain and the fishwife and the ordinary evening noise of a city that did not know it was holding its breath.

Over and under, he thought. Four minutes.

Then he turned toward home, walking at exactly the pace of an unremarkable man, and did not run, and did not look east, and told himself very firmly that tomorrow he would sell knots, and the day after that, and the day after that, for as many days as a small life could be stitched together and made to hold.

Behind him, far beneath the streets, in the dark of Cooper's Row, the rats sang on.
