## 2. What the Rats Remember

The trouble with living above a sewer grate, Vesper Winterbourne had long ago decided, was not the smell. The smell was honest. It announced itself and asked nothing of you. No, the real trouble was the rats, who considered his cellar a kind of public square and himself its reluctant mayor.

They came at dusk, as they always did, pouring up through the grate in a rustling brown tide that any sane man would have drowned in boiling water. Vesper set out the crusts instead.

"You're late," he told the largest of them, a scarred female he'd named Duchess on account of her imperious whiskers. "And don't think I haven't heard about the bakery on Tallow Row. Six loaves. *Six.* You should be ashamed."

*The baker was asking for it,* said Duchess, around a mouthful of crust. Rat-speech was less words than impressions wearing words like borrowed coats—greed, warmth, the particular smugness of a successful crime—but after twenty years Vesper translated fluently. *He left the cooling window open. That's practically an invitation.*

"That is practically theft."

*Same thing, if your heart is open.*

Vesper sighed and broke off a corner of cheese for the youngest of them, a stuttering fellow called Pip who counted everything twice because once never felt finished. This was the arrangement: bread for gossip, gossip for nothing at all, because a charm-seller who traded in harmless luck-knots could not afford to be seen listening to vermin, and so every conversation happened in the privacy of his own damp cellar, between one candle and the next.

It was, all things considered, a good life. Small, careful, unremarkable—the three virtues Corvane rewarded most since the gods went silent and the Purge taught everyone what happened to remarkable men.

Tonight, though, the rats were not interested in bread.

*Listen,* said Duchess, and her whiskers went flat, and thirty small bodies went still at once, which was more frightening than any noise they could have made.

"I'm listening," Vesper said. "I'm always listening. It's my whole personality."

*No.* She crept forward onto the flagstone nearest his boot, and her black eyes caught the candlelight like wet stones. *Beneath. Listen beneath.*

So Vesper did something he hadn't done in seven years. He opened the deep ear—that part of him he kept folded away like a letter he couldn't bear to reread—and let the city's under-voice come up through the stone.

Sewers had a sound. Everyone who lived above them knew it without knowing it: water moving, brick settling, the vast slow digestion of a city. Underneath that, the rat-song, the cockroach-song, the busy little chattering of things that thrived where people failed.

Tonight there was something else.

A hum. Low, patient, rising and falling in a pattern too regular to be water and too alive to be pipes. It came from far down—from below the sewers, below the cisterns, below even the old foundations that predated Corvane's oldest maps—and it moved the way a sleeping man breathes.

Vesper closed the deep ear so fast his head rang.

"Rot-sickness," he said aloud, firmly, the way you'd slam a door. "Fungal bloom in the lower galleries. Gets into the brickwork. Makes vibrations. I read about it."

*You didn't read about anything,* said Duchess. *You hate reading.*

"I read pamphlets."

*You use them to wrap fish.* She edged closer. *Winterbourne. Man-of-charms. Friend-of-crumbs. It hummed a hymn tonight.*

"It hummed a *noise*."

*A hymn,* she repeated, with the terrible patience of the very old. *Three notes up, two notes down, hold the last one like a held breath. The old ones sang it before the Silence. My grandmother's grandmother sang it in the grain stores when the world still had gods in it. We remember songs, man-of-charms. Songs are how we know a place is ours.*

Vesper stood up, which ended conversations with rats by custom, and began blowing out candles with great efficiency.

"It's rot," he said. "Or gas. Or the tide. Go home. Eat your crusts. Steal fewer loaves."

But Pip, counting his crumbs twice at the edge of the grate, looked up with his small stupid brave face and said the thing that ruined Vesper's evening entirely:

*It knows the words now.*

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat by the dead hearth with a cup of chicory going cold in his hands and performed the ancient art of telling yourself you're fine.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done seven years ago, when the Choir fell silent and the Inquisition came through Corvane like a fire through dry grass: beasts had never spoken the god-tongue. Not once. Animals sang the *beast*-tongue, the low green language older than temples, and the god-tongue was a different river entirely—it ran through human throats or not at all. When the gods died, the hymns died with them, and the Inquisitors burned the songbooks, and Vesper had watched a woman on Gallows Hill recite the Lamentation of Thorns in a language that officially no longer existed, and he had learned the day's most important lesson:

Some silences are load-bearing. Do not dig beneath them.

So whatever was humming under Corvane, it was not singing hymns, because hymns were dead, and dead things stayed dead in this country, because if they didn't then everything Vesper had built—every small careful unremarkable year—was a house built on a lie, and houses like that have a way of—

There was scratching at the grate.

At midnight. Rats did not scratch at midnight. Rats had schedules.

Vesper lit the candle and looked, and the grate was crowded—Duchess, Pip, a dozen others packed shoulder to shoulder like mourners, and behind them, further back in the dark, shapes he didn't recognize. Sewer-rats from the deep runs. Pale ones. Big ones. Rats who had never come up to the mayor's cellar in their lives.

*We brought witnesses,* said Duchess quietly.

"Witnesses to what? A gas leak?"

*To the words.* Her voice had gone strange—hushed, the way animals go hushed before earthquakes. *Pip will say them. He has the best memory. Counting runs in the family.*

Pip crept forward. His whiskers trembled. And then, in a small rusty voice, in perfect, flawless, dead-for-seven-years High Liturgical—the tongue of the Choir, the tongue of the silenced, the tongue whose last living speaker had hanged on Gallows Hill while Vesper stood in the crowd doing nothing, saying nothing, being small and safe and unremarkable—

Pip recited the opening verse of the Hymn of the Waking.

*"Arise, arise, O buried light,*
*the door remembers, though the night—"*

"Stop." Vesper's voice cracked on it. "Stop. Stop *saying* that."

*—the door remembers, though the night—*

"*Stop!*"

The candle guttered. The rats flinched back. And in the ringing silence, Vesper became aware of two things at once: that his hands were shaking, and that somewhere far below his floor, far below the sewers, the humming had paused—as if it had heard him shout, as if it were listening back.

Then, gently, patiently, it resumed. Three notes up. Two notes down. The last note held like a held breath.

Like something knocking politely. Before it breaks the door down.

*It started learning the words three nights ago,* Duchess said softly. *First it only hummed. Then it hummed with meaning. Tonight it taught us. We don't know why it chose us, man-of-charms. But we know why we came to you.* She looked up at him, and her eyes were old and sorry. *Because you're the only one left in this city who would believe us. And because—*

She stopped. Rats, Vesper knew, did not hesitate. Hesitation was a human invention.

*Because the hymn has a name in it,* Pip whispered, counting the syllables twice, getting the same answer both times. *One name. At the end of the verse. It isn't a god's name.*

The hum rose beneath them, warm and slow and certain, and Vesper's blood went cold as well-water, because he already knew—he had known since the first note, some traitor part of him had known—

*It's yours,* said Duchess. *Winterbourne. It's singing about you.*

* * *

He made them repeat it four times. He checked Pip's translation against Duchess's, against the pale deep-runner who spoke an older dialect and got the same result. He did everything a careful man does when he wants the answer to change.

It didn't. *Arise, arise, O buried light, the door remembers, though the night—* and then the name, folded into the melody like a coin sewn into a hem: *Winterbourne. Winterbourne. Winterbourne.*

Something beneath Corvane was singing him to sleep. Or singing him awake. With hymns, he thought hysterically, it was honestly hard to tell the difference.

He paid the rats their crusts. His hands only shook a little. Duchess lingered at the grate.

*What will you do?* she asked.

"What any sensible man does," Vesper said. "Nothing. Brilliantly. For years, if necessary."

*And if it doesn't stay beneath?*

"Then I'll move. There are other cities."

*Are there?* said Duchess, in a tone he chose not to examine, and slipped down into the dark.

He blew out the candle. He lay down. He stared at the ceiling and rehearsed tomorrow: knots to tie, charms to sell, sparrows to gossip with, a perfectly ordinary morning in a perfectly ordinary life, and absolutely nothing underneath it except, perhaps, a faint hum that he would simply decline to hear.

The plan was excellent. The plan was airtight.

He was just beginning to believe it when the sparrows started screaming.

Not chirping—screaming, dozens of them, all along the rooftop outside his window, and threaded through their panic, rising up from the deep places of the city like smoke through floorboards, came the hymn again. Louder now. Closer. And beneath it, unmistakable, a new sound: footsteps. Measured boots on cobbles, many of them, moving through the midnight streets toward the Gray Market, and a voice calling the hour in a clear cold voice he knew better than he wanted to:

"Inquisition. Open in the name of the Silence."

Vesper sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and reached for the first thing his hands found—a half-finished luck-knot, its threads still warm from yesterday's work.

Seven quiet years, he thought. Seven years of being small.

And now the past wasn't knocking anymore.
