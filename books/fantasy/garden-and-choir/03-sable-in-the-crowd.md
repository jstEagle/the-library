## 3. Sable in the Crowd

The trouble with selling luck was that people believed in it.

Vesper had learned this the hard way, over seven years of careful, deliberate mediocrity. A charm that worked too well drew attention. A charm that worked exactly as well as chance would have anyway — that was a business. So he tied his knots with a flourish and no magic at all, murmured blessings that were really just good wishes wearing a costume, and made sure his luck-knots failed often enough to be respectable. A man whose charms always worked was either blessed or damned, and both kinds ended up on Inquisitorial lists.

He was mid-knot — a simple three-loop for safe travel, priced at two coppers because hope should be affordable — when the sparrows went quiet.

That was the first warning. Sparrows were never quiet. They were the Gray Market's gossip column, its town criers, its early-warning system, and they had spent the morning chattering at him about the strange hush spreading through the rooftops, how the pigeons near the cathedral had stopped cooing altogether, how old Mother Carrion's crows had flown inland and refused to say why. But now every sparrow on the awning above his stall lifted its head at once, like a congregation hearing the first note of a hymn they'd hoped never to hear again.

*Gray coat,* said the nearest sparrow, in the flat, feathered way it had. *Gray coat and iron buttons.*

Vesper's hands did not stop moving. That was the second lesson of survival: your hands must always look busy, because busy hands belong to someone, and belonging to someone is safety.

*How many?* he asked, without moving his lips much.

*One,* said the sparrow. *But she walks like ten.*

He knew who it was before he looked up. He knew by the way the market's noise changed shape around her — not quieter, exactly, but *tidier*, the way a room tidies itself when a schoolmistress enters. He knew by the particular rhythm of her boots on cobblestone, a stride he had once matched step for step down the Lantern Canal at midnight, back when matching strides was allowed and midnight belonged to them.

He looked up anyway. He couldn't not. Seven years, and he still hadn't taught himself that trick.

Sable Halloway came down the market row the way frost comes across a window: gradually, beautifully, and with the absolute certainty that everything green would be gone by morning. Her gray coat was immaculate. The iron buttons of her office caught the light and gave none of it back. Two junior Inquisitors trailed her at the correct deferential distance, holding clipboards they didn't need, because Sable Halloway did not need help remembering anything. She had once recited, from memory, the entire tariff schedule of the Corvane wool trade just to win an argument about whether he was overpaying for socks. She had been right. She was usually right. It was one of the many things he had loved about her and one of the very few things he could afford to remember fondly now.

She stopped three stalls down, at Old Pemmy's table of dried herbs and dubious tonics, and picked up a bottle of something amber.

"Madam," Pemmy began, "that's a restorative, perfectly legal—"

"It's willow bark and gin," Sable said. "Which is legal. I'm checking the seal." She turned the bottle. Whatever she saw satisfied her, or disappointed her; with Sable you rarely got to know which. She set it down precisely where it had stood, aligned with the edge of the table, and moved on.

*She's coming here,* said the sparrow, unhelpfully.

*Thank you,* said Vesper. *I had assumed she was inspecting the fishmonger out of a deep personal interest in mackerel.*

The sparrows tittered. Sparrows found everything funny; it was their only defense against being small and edible, and Vesper envied them for it.

He ran through the inventory in his head while she worked her way closer. Luck-knots: fine, luck was not magic, luck was commerce. Traveler's wards: fine, everyone agreed a knotted cord kept moths out of luggage, even if everyone was wrong about why. His little clay birds that whistled in the wind: fine, decorative, no more magical than a kettle. Nothing on his table so much as whispered. He had built this stall the way other men built alibis, brick by careful brick, and if he was honest — and he tried not to be, honesty being another luxury the Purge had taxed into extinction — he had built it with her in mind. Every item passed the test he imagined her applying. Would Halloway find anything? No. Because Halloway had taught him, without ever meaning to, what thoroughness looked like from the inside.

That was the cruel joke of it. The Inquisition had trained him better than any teacher, using her as the textbook.

She arrived at his stall the way weather arrives, and he arranged his face into the expression he practiced in the mirror some mornings: pleasant, slightly dull, the face of a man whose most exciting secret was the markup on his traveler's wards.

"Morning," he said. "Looking for luck?"

"Inquisitor Halloway," she said, to the junior clerk, who wrote it down though she had clearly already written it down. Then, to Vesper, in the voice she used for ledgers: "Name and license."

"Vesper Winterbourne. License 4-4-1-9, renewed last month." He produced the paper from under the counter before she asked. He had produced it before she asked for six years running. At this point it was almost a ritual between them, and he hated that he thought of it as *between them*, because between implied two sides of something, and there was nothing between them except a counter full of harmless knots and seven years of ash.

She took the license. Her eyes moved over it the way they used to move over his handwriting on grocery lists — fast, complete, missing nothing. Her gloved fingers held the corner of the page, and he watched those fingers and thought absolutely nothing, felt absolutely nothing, performed his indifference with the dedication of an actor who had rehearsed the role until the role had eaten the man.

"Your stock," she said. "Display it."

"Of course." He laid everything out with the ease of long practice. "Luck-knots, three styles. Traveler's cords. Wind-whistles — those are the clay birds, purely ornamental, though my aunt swears they keep headaches away, and my aunt also swears by boiled onions for grief, so I'd take her testimony lightly."

One of the junior Inquisitors made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Sable did not smile. But something happened at the corner of her mouth, a small muscular event, quickly suppressed, and Vesper felt it land in his chest like a coin dropped down a well. He counted the seconds until he heard it hit bottom. It took longer than it used to. Or maybe he was listening harder.

"These knots," she said, lifting a luck-knot and turning it over. "You tie these yourself?"

"Every one. Badly, according to my mother, but she's been dead nine years and can't be contradicted."

"What's in them?"

"String."

"In the knots generally. Charms are required to declare components."

"String," Vesper said again, gently, "and the sincere wish that the buyer has a good week. Which is not a component, it's a business model."

She set the knot down. She aligned it with the others. Of course she did. He watched her gloved hand make the small adjustment and thought: *she still does that.* And then he thought: *stop watching her hands.* And then he watched her hands.

"License is current," she said to the clerk. "Stock appears compliant." She said *appears* the way she said everything, with a precision that left room for later regret. Then her gaze came up and met his, and for one unguarded half-second — half a second, no more, he would swear it on whatever gods were left to swear things on, which was to say on nothing at all — her eyes were not an Inquisitor's eyes. They were tired. They were twenty-eight years old and carrying forty years. They were the eyes of a woman standing in a market looking at a man she had once loved enough to burn her whole life down around the absence of him.

Then the shutters came down, smooth as a shopkeeper closing at dusk, and she was procedure again.

"You'll receive notice if the quarterly inspection changes schedule," she said. "Keep your license visible. Report any customer offering you goods of unregistered origin."

"I always do," Vesper said. "Nobody ever offers me anything interesting. I think word's gotten around."

Something flickered behind her eyes — he refused to name it, naming it would cost him — and then she turned to go, and the market exhaled around her, and the sparrows resumed their gossip like a curtain rising on the second act.

*Well?* said the sparrow.

*Well what?*

*You did the thing with your face.*

*I don't know what you mean.*

*The thing where your face says nothing but your shoulders say everything,* the sparrow said, preening. *Birds see everything, charm-seller. You should sell shoulder-charms.*

Vesper did not answer, because answering would have meant agreeing, and agreeing would have meant admitting that his shoulders were currently carrying the weight of a woman walking away from him for the four-hundredth time, and that he had counted, and that counting was the problem.

* * *

The rest of the morning crawled. He sold a travel cord to a nervous grandmother, a wind-whistle to a child who immediately dropped it, and a luck-knot to a carter who asked, in the way customers sometimes did, leaning close and lowering his voice as though purchasing contraband:

"They say you're the real thing, friend. Real charm-work. My cousin's rooster wouldn't crow till you sold him a knot, and now—" 

"My cousin's rooster," Vesper said pleasantly, "was probably cold."

"But if a man wanted—"

"If a man wanted real magic," Vesper said, and here he deployed the speech he had given perhaps a thousand times, worn smooth as river stone, "he'd want his head examined, and shortly afterward removed. Magic's been dead seven years, friend. Everything since is string and good wishes. Two coppers."

The carter bought the knot. They always bought the knot. Hope was the one product that never suffered from disproof.

But the exchange had cost him something, the way these exchanges always did, because every denial was a small rehearsal for a larger one, and somewhere under his breastbone a question had begun to hum — the same question the rats had been humming at him for days, the same question the silent rooftops implied. *What if it isn't dead? What if it's only quiet?* He told the question to be quiet too. Questions, unlike sparrows, obeyed him occasionally.

It was late afternoon, the light going honey-colored and the market thinning out, when the sparrows spoke again.

*She's back,* they said. *Alone. She sent the gray coats away.*

His hands stopped. This time he let them.

She came down the row without the clerks, without the clipboard, and stopped at his stall, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was so familiar it was practically furniture — an old armchair they had both been carefully not sitting in for seven years.

"Master Winterbourne," she said. "A procedural matter. Your stall's registration lists a secondary address for correspondence."

"It does."

"The address is a bakery that closed two years ago."

"Is it?" He raised his eyebrows with genuine surprise, which was genuine, because he had genuinely forgotten to update it, thoroughness having limits, especially when the alternative was giving the Inquisition a map to his bed. "I'll correct it. Bureaucracy marches on."

"Mm." She looked at the luck-knots. She looked at the canal behind the stalls, where the water carried leaves and the occasional regretful boot. When she spoke again, her voice had changed key — lower, faster, the voice of a woman reading aloud in an empty room. "There were two speakers taken from the Tanners' Quarter last night. Both heard something calling. Both walked toward the cathedral on their own feet, smiling."

The market noise seemed to lean away from them.

"That's terrible," Vesper said, and meant it, and made sure the meaning sounded like the ordinary horror of a law-abiding citizen rather than the specific, crawling dread of a man who had heard something calling too — through rat-song, through sparrow-silence, through the hum beneath the city that he had been telling himself was rot-sickness for three days straight.

"It is," Sable said. "Whoever hears it next, we'll want to talk to them. Quickly." Her eyes came up. "Speakers hide well, Master Winterbourne. Beasts don't gossip to just anyone."

"Sparrows gossip to everyone," Vesper said. "It's mostly about breadcrumbs and grudges."

"Mm," she said again, that same precise syllable, and he understood suddenly that she was doing what he was doing: performing a part for an audience of empty air, saying the dangerous thing sideways where only the intended listener could hear it. Seven years ago she had caught him feeding a lame pigeon words of comfort and had said nothing, then or ever. Some mercies became habits. He wondered — he did not let himself wonder, he strictly forbade himself, and wondered anyway — how many reports she had filed that were slightly, invisibly incomplete.

"Anything else, Inquisitor?" he asked.

"No." She turned. Stopped. Did not turn back. "Your knots," she said. "The three-loop style. You've improved the tension on the final pass."

And she walked away, gray coat and iron buttons, leaving Vesper standing in the honey-colored light holding a length of string, understanding that she had just told him — in the only language they had left, the language of knots and licenses and things unsaid — that she remembered his hands.

Above the awning, the sparrows had gone quiet again. All of them. Every rooftop, every gutter, every chimney-pot in Corvane, silent as held breath.

And from far below the cobbles — so far below it might have been imagination, might have been his pulse, might have been the past knocking politely — Vesper heard the first clear note of a hymn.

He knew the tune. He had hoped he'd forgotten it.

He hadn't.
