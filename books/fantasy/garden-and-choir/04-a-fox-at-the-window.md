## 4. A Fox at the Window

Vesper Winterbourne had a rule about evenings: after the third bell of dusk, he stopped listening.

It wasn't that the beasts went quiet at night. Quite the opposite. Night was when the city's animals did their real talking—the rats held their councils in the walls, the cats conducted their territorial diplomacy from fence posts, and the moths, who were terrible gossips with nothing but opinions about light sources, droned on until dawn. If Vesper listened to all of it, he would never sleep, and a charm-seller with shadows under his eyes looked like exactly what he was: a man carrying something heavy.

So he had taught himself to close the door on it. The gift sat behind that door like a dog told to stay, and mostly—mostly—it stayed.

He was boiling barley over his little stove when the knocking started. Not at his door. At his window. Third floor. A soft, deliberate tap-tap-tap, polite as a tax collector.

Vesper turned down the flame and did not turn around.

"Go away," he said to the room in general. "Whatever you are, I don't buy after dark."

Tap-tap-tap.

"I mean it. I sell luck-knots and anti-moth sachets and a very respectable line in love charms that do absolutely nothing, which is why they're legal. Nothing else. Nothing strange."

The tapping stopped. Then, in a voice like dry leaves arranging themselves into words—a voice that came *through* the glass rather than around it—something said:

"Your barley is burning."

Vesper spun. On his windowsill, framed by the fogged pane, sat a fox.

Not a spirit-fox, not a shadow-fox, not one of the painted paper foxes Corvane's children folded for luck at midwinter. A red fox, actual and ordinary in every visible particular, with dust on its paws and one ear notched as if it had once lost an argument with a trap. It sat with its tail curled over its feet like a gentleman waiting to be announced.

And it was looking at him the way no animal had ever looked at him—not with the flat frankness of beasts, not with the hungry simplicity of creatures who wanted things—but with *recognition*. With something almost like apology.

"Your barley," the fox repeated. "Truly. It's burning."

Vesper rescued the pot, mostly by reflex, his eyes never leaving the sill. His heart was doing something complicated against his ribs. Because here was the thing, the impossible thing, the thing that made the floor feel slightly tilted under his stocking feet:

Animals spoke beast-tongue. All of them. Every sparrow and sewer rat and carthorse in Corvane spoke the same wordless, image-rich language of scent and instinct and need. It was the first law of his gift, learned before he'd had words of his own: *beasts do not speak Common.* They could not. Their mouths were wrong for it, their minds were shaped differently, and any speaker who claimed an animal had talked to him in plain human speech was either lying or mad or about to be arrested.

And yet.

"You're speaking Common," Vesper said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was a skill seven years of market-stalls had honed to a fine edge. "Fluent Common. Archaic Common, if my ear isn't rotted. You just used the old second-person familiar."

"Did I?" The fox cocked its head. "Forgive me. I have been asleep a long time, and languages settle into the bones like silt." Its amber eyes caught the lamplight. "You understood me anyway. That is the more remarkable thing."

"Is it." Vesper set the pot down. He kept the stove between himself and the window, an instinct he didn't examine too closely. "Because from where I'm standing, the remarkable thing is a fox on my third-floor windowsill conducting grammar lessons."

"The gutter was convenient," the fox said, as if this explained everything. "And your chimney is poorly swept, or I would have used it. One does prefer to arrive properly."

"One does," Vesper agreed faintly.

He should have shut the curtain. He knew that. Seven years of practiced smallness, of being unremarkable, of teaching his face to be a closed shop—and here he stood, barefoot, holding a scorched pot, having a conversation through his own window with an impossibility. Somewhere in the back of his mind, the part of him that had survived the Purge was screaming in a very small, very controlled voice.

But the other part of him—the part that had been a boy talking to hedgehogs before he knew it was forbidden—that part had gone quiet and bright, the way it always did when the world cracked open a little.

"What are you?" he asked.

"A fox," said the fox.

"That's not what I asked."

"It is entirely what you asked. Whether it is what you *meant* is another matter." The fox licked one paw with maddening calm. "I am a fox who remembers. That is the relevant portion. May I come in? The wind up here has opinions."

"No," said Vesper. "Absolutely not. Animals don't come inside. Animals especially don't come inside *talking*."

"I am not, strictly speaking—" the fox began, then stopped itself with visible effort, ears flattening. "No. You're right. Precautions are wise. We will conduct our business through the glass, if you insist on glass between us. Though I'll note that glass has never stopped anything that mattered."

*There it is again,* Vesper thought. *That archaic cadence. Nobody talks like that. Nobody has talked like that since—*

Since the gods went silent.

The thought arrived whole, cold as well-water, and Vesper set the pot down very carefully because his hands had decided to become unreliable.

"Seven years ago," he said slowly, "the Choir fell silent. Everyone says so. The priests say so, the Inquisitors say so, the history books they burned and rewrote say so. And when the gods went silent, something else happened too. Something nobody talks about."

"Vellichor," said the fox.

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Vesper had heard it exactly once before, whispered by his grandmother, who had died believing the world was larger than it was. *We live in Vellichor, little wren,* she'd said, rocking him, *and Vellichor is wide as the sky.* He'd been four. By six he knew better than to repeat it. There was no Vellichor. There was Corvane, and beyond Corvane there were the borderlands, and beyond those there were other nations on maps sold in proper shops, and that was the whole of the world, certified and stamped.

Except.

Except that Vesper had never actually met anyone who had traveled. Except that the roads out of the city all bent, somehow, the way roads in dreams bend, and travelers who left always arrived somewhere that was, on inspection, still home. Except that the maps in the proper shops never quite agreed with each other about what lay past the fens, and the cartographers got nervous if you asked twice.

"There is no Vellichor," Vesper said. "It's a story. A grandmother's tale."

"Yes," said the fox. "So are borders, if you look at them honestly. But mine is a story with teeth." It rose, paced the length of the sill—three steps each way, a prisoner's walk—and settled again. "Listen to me, charm-seller. I remember the country that was erased. Not conquered, not renamed—*erased*, unpicked from the world like a stitch pulled from cloth, the night the Choir went silent. Every road leading out now leads back in. Every map lies by omission. Every person born inside these borders since has been born inside a lie with edges, and none of them know it, because how would they? The lie is the horizon."

Vesper's mouth was dry. "You're mad," he said, without conviction. "Or I'm dreaming. Or this is some Inquisitor's trick—a trained beast, a ventriloquist—"

"Do I sound like a ventriloquist?"

"You sound like my grandmother's ghost, which is worse."

Something moved across the fox's face—an expression no fox should have been able to make, old and tired and fond. "Good," it said softly. "Then she remembered too. Some of us were permitted to keep our memories. It was meant as a punishment." A pause. "It has been a long punishment."

Outside, the city murmured its evening murmur. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones; somewhere above, pigeons muttered in their roosts about the day's crumbs. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. But Vesper heard them differently now, the way you hear a house differently after someone tells you it's haunted—as if the walls themselves might be in on something.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why my window? There are forty thousand people in Corvane."

"Forty thousand people who cannot hear a word I say in beast-tongue, and perhaps six in this city who can—and of those six, five are already answering summons you haven't heard about yet." The fox's ears swiveled, tracking something Vesper couldn't hear. "You I chose because you listened to sparrows this morning and told them to be careful, and because you charge a widow half price and pretend you don't, and because—" the fox hesitated, and for a moment looked genuinely, terribly weary, "—because you are the only speaker in Corvane who has spent seven years trying to be small. The others preened. The others *practiced*. You hid. Hiding is a skill, Vesper Winterbourne, and I find myself suddenly in great need of it."

"How do you know my—"

"Your sparrows gossip," said the fox. "About you, specifically. You are, I'm told, 'the tall one who listens,' which among sparrows is practically a title of nobility."

Despite everything—the dread crawling up his spine, the tilting floor, the name *Vellichor* sitting in his chest like swallowed ice—Vesper felt his mouth twitch. "They also complain that I only feed them stale bread."

"They mentioned that as well. At length. Sparrows hold grudges the way rivers hold water."

* * *

They talked until the barley was cold and the lamp had burned low.

The fox would not give its name. "Names are doors," it said, "and I am keeping mine locked for the moment," which Vesper thought was either profound or nonsense and suspected was both. But it told him other things, in that dry leaf-voice, while the night deepened and the fog pressed against the glass.

It told him that the silence of the gods had not been a death but a sleep, and that sleeps end. It told him that prayers had begun to be answered in the lower quarters—small miracles, a fever broken, a debt forgiven by a strangely softened creditor—and that answered prayers were not gifts but *grips*, hands reaching up through the dark to take hold of whoever prayed hardest. It told him that the humming beneath the city, the hymn the rats had begged him to hear two nights ago, was the Choir tuning itself.

And it told him about the Door.

"There is one way out of Vellichor," the fox said. "There has only ever been one way, even before the erasure, though in the old days we called it by gentler names. A Door made of unmaking, at the edge of the drowned places. When the gods slept, it sealed. When they wake—" the fox's tail flicked, once, sharply, "—it opens. And it is opening now. I can feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar."

"Out to where?" Vesper asked. "If the whole world outside is a lie, what's actually out there?"

The fox was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't know," it admitted. "That is the truth, charm-seller, and I dislike it as much as you do. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the true country, the one that was here before the gods built their garden and filled it with singers. Perhaps only sky. But I know this: a cage does not stop being a cage because it is beautifully made, and Vellichor is *very* beautifully made. The streets are kind. The bread is good. The borders are closing like a hand folding shut, and everyone inside is congratulating themselves on how safe they feel."

Vesper looked at his little room. The stove, the shelf of charm-components, the bed he'd slept in for seven years, the window with its fogged glass and its impossible passenger. Everything exactly where he'd left it. Everything, suddenly, a cell furnished by someone with excellent taste.

"I have a life," he said, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it. "A stall. Customers. A routine. I've spent seven years building a life that fits through the eye of a needle, and you want me to believe the needle was inside a cage the whole time."

"I want you to believe nothing," said the fox. "Belief is for priests and fools. I want you to *watch*. Watch the prayers being answered. Watch who answers them. Watch what happens to the people who get chosen." Its eyes held his, amber and ancient and sorry. "And when watching stops being enough—and it will stop being enough soon, sooner than you'd like—come find me. I'll be at the fen-gate at moonrise, every night, for as long as there are nights to keep count of."

"And if I don't come?"

The fox rose, stretched elaborately, and turned toward the open dark of the gutter.

"Then I will tell you stories about yourself until you change your mind," it said. "I am very patient. It is one of the few advantages of being old." It paused at the sill's edge, silhouetted against the fog, and added, almost as an afterthought:

"Oh—one more thing. Lock your window tonight. Not against me. Against what's coming up from under the city. It hums, and things that hum are looking for voices, and yours—" the fox glanced back, and its eyes were grave, "—yours is the finest I have heard in seven years. Sleep lightly, Vesper Winterbourne. The Choir is waking, and it remembers every voice it ever loved."

Then it stepped off the third-floor sill into empty air, and simply—wasn't. No fall, no flutter, no sound. Just the fog closing where a fox had been, and the faint smell of autumn leaves in a room that had no trees near it.

Vesper stood for a long time with his hand on the cold glass.

Then he latched the window, as instructed. Then he sat down on his bed in the dark, in his beautiful, kind, well-breaded cage, and listened.

At first: nothing. The pigeons settling. The city breathing.

And then, far below—beneath the cobbles, beneath the sewers, beneath the roots of the oldest buildings—so faint he might have imagined it, except that the rats' warning was still fresh in his ears:

A hum. Rising. Patient. Tuned, he realized with a chill that started in his spine and spread outward, to a melody he knew.

It was the song his grandmother used to sing.

In the morning, Vesper told himself, pulling the blanket over his head like a child. In the morning he would decide what to do. In the morning—

From the rooftops outside, the sparrows had begun to sing along.
