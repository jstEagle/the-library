## 5. The First Answered Prayer

Tamsin Cobb had three rules for living in Corvane, and she recited them to herself every morning the way other people said grace.

One: never look an Inquisitor in the eye. Two: never owe money to a man with a ledger and a grudge. Three: never, under any circumstances, pray out loud.

The first two she'd learned from her mother. The third she'd learned from her own good sense, because everyone knew the gods were dead, and talking to dead things was how you ended up on a cart headed for the Ash Steps. The Choir had gone silent seven years ago. The Purge had followed, swift and thorough as a fever, and now the only prayers permitted were the ones printed in the sanctioned liturgy—flat little verses about civic gratitude that nobody believed and everybody mumbled.

But rules, Tamsin had discovered, bent beautifully when your son couldn't keep down water.

* * *

Tam was six years old and had been sick since the first frost, some creeping lung-rot that the apothecary treated with tinctures that cost more than Tamsin earned in a week of boiling other people's linens. She worked at the Sudsrow wash-house from dawn until her hands went pruney and gray, and she came home each evening to find him smaller, paler, more like a candle burning down toward the socket.

On the night it got worst—the night his breathing went thin and whistling, like wind through a keyhole—Tamsin sat on the edge of his cot and broke rule number three.

She didn't pray loudly. She barely prayed at all, really. It was more of a leak than a prayer, the kind of thing that escapes a person when they've run out of everything else.

"Please," she whispered, into the dark, into the space between the cot and the wall where the damp lived. "Please. I'll give anything. I'll scrub floors till my knuckles crack, I'll—I don't care who hears it. Somebody. Anybody. Please."

She waited, feeling foolish, feeling like a woman shouting down a well.

And then the well answered back.

It wasn't a voice. It was warmth—a slow bloom of heat in her chest, right behind the breastbone, like swallowing a coal wrapped in wool. It spread down her arm and pooled in her palm, and her palm began to glow. Not brightly. Softly, the way bread glows when you tear it open fresh. Gold light, patient light, light that seemed to be waiting for her permission.

Tamsin looked at her hand. Her hand looked back at her, glowing, and somewhere in the back of her mind a small sensible voice said, *Rule three, Tamsin. Rule three.*

But Tam made a sound in his sleep—a small, cracked sound—and the sensible voice shut up fast.

She pressed her glowing palm against his forehead.

The heat poured out of her like water out of a tipped jug. It moved through him; she could feel it moving, could feel it finding the rot in his lungs the way a hound finds a rat in a wall, and she felt the moment it caught hold, because Tam's whole body arched and he coughed—once, hard, wet—and then breathed. Really breathed. Deep and easy, the way children breathe when nothing is wrong with them at all.

The glow faded. The warmth settled somewhere behind her ribs and stayed there, humming faintly, like a kettle just off the boil.

"Tam?" she whispered.

He opened his eyes. They were clear. He looked at her with the enormous, offended dignity of a six-year-old who has been ill past his tolerance for it and said, "I'm hungry."

Tamsin Cobb put her face in her hands and cried until the bed shook, and then she got up and fed her son bread and honey at midnight, and neither of them thought once about Inquisitors or rules or what a miracle costs, because hope is a very bad accountant.

* * *

By morning, half of Sudsrow knew.

This was not Tamsin's fault. It was Mrs. Pell's fault, because Mrs. Pell had come by with broth—which Tamsin hadn't needed, which was itself a marvel—and found Tam up and demanding second helpings, and Mrs. Pell had a mouth on her that traveled faster than most horses.

"A miracle," Mrs. Pell announced to the wash-house yard, wringing a sheet with the fervor of a woman testifying. "A true one. The boy was grey as ash and now look at him." She thrust Tam forward like evidence. Tam, enjoying his celebrity enormously, coughed experimentally. Nothing happened. The crowd murmured.

"He's better," Tamsin said quickly, hauling him back by the collar. "That's all. Good broth and rest. Mrs. Pell exaggerates."

"I do not," said Mrs. Pell, wounded. "Show them your hand, Tamsin."

"There's nothing to show."

There was something to show. That was the problem. Sometime in the night, while she slept beside her son, the warmth behind her ribs had cooled and set, and where it had set there was now a mark on her left palm: a small spiral, fine as a fingerprint, gold-brown like a leaf's vein-work. It didn't hurt. But when she flexed her fingers, it warmed, faintly, like a coin held too long in a fist.

The washerwomen crowded around. Old Bess took Tamsin's hand in both of hers, turned it over, and went quiet in a way that frightened Tamsin more than any shouting would have.

"That's a speaker's mark," Bess said. "My gran told me. Before the silence, folk used to get marked like that. Chosen, they called it." She looked up, and her old eyes were wet. "The gods are answering again, girl."

"They're dead," someone said from the back. "Everyone knows they're dead."

"Well, then somebody's answering," Bess snapped, "and I'd like to know who, because whoever it is just saved that boy's life, and I'll thank them either way."

The yard went strange after that. Tamsin felt it happen—felt the awe in the air thicken and curdle, slow as milk left out. Awe, she would learn, is a crowd's first emotion. Fear is its second. And fear always arrives asking the same question: *whose fault is this?*

Because here was the arithmetic every soul in Corvane carried: magic was outlawed. Magic had killed people—or so the proclamations said, posted on every corner in black ink. The gods' silence had been bought with blood during the Purge, and anyone who invited the old powers back was not a miracle-worker but a menace. The crowd loved Tam. The crowd had wept over Tam. And the crowd was already beginning to look at Tamsin's marked hand the way you look at a lit fuse.

"You should cover that," muttered Doss, the wash-house foreman, sidling close. He was a thin man whose loyalty changed direction with the wind, and the wind had shifted. "Cover it, and say nothing, and maybe nothing comes of it."

"Nothing *did* come of it," Tamsin said. "My son is alive."

"Aye." Doss wouldn't meet her eyes. "Funny thing, though. My cousin's boy died last winter. Same sickness. Nobody's palm glowed for him." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse, and then he walked away, and Tamsin understood that the story had already slipped out of her hands. It wasn't *a boy was saved* anymore. It was *why her? Why not us? Why not mine?*

Hope, Tamsin thought, watching the yard full of faces turn away from her, is a fire in a dry field. Everyone wants to stand near it. Nobody thinks about the wind.

* * *

They came at noon.

Tamsin heard them before she saw them—the particular rhythm of Inquisitor boots on cobbles, four men walking in step, unhurried, because hurry was for people who weren't certain of the outcome. The wash-house yard emptied the way water empties from a pulled plug, everyone suddenly remembering urgent business elsewhere, except the ones who stayed precisely because staying was safer than being seen leaving.

Sable Halloway led them.

Everyone knew Sable Halloway. She was the Inquisitor the ballads got wrong—too young, too quiet, too fair for the stories—and she walked through Corvane like winter walking through a room: no malice in it, just an absolute indifference to what it froze. She stopped in front of Tamsin and looked at her the way you look at a ledger entry.

"Tamsin Cobb?"

"Yes."

"We received reports of unlicensed thaumaturgy in Sudsrow. Healing works. You'll show me your hands, please."

Tamsin's heart was going like a drum in a parade, but her hands, traitors, came up obediently, palms out. Years of scrubbing had taught them obedience if nothing else.

Inquisitor Halloway looked at the spiral mark for a long moment. Something crossed her face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. If Tamsin hadn't been watching so desperately she'd have missed it entirely. It looked almost like grief.

"When did this appear?" Halloway asked.

"Last night."

"Were you praying?"

"No." The lie came out smooth, and Tamsin was proud of it, and then Halloway's eyes lifted to hers and Tamsin understood that the woman had heard ten thousand lies and graded them silently, and this one had received a poor mark indeed.

"Mrs. Cobb," Halloway said, and her voice dropped—not softer exactly, but quieter, meant for Tamsin alone, though the whole yard strained to hear. "I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to think very carefully, because your answer determines whether you walk home tonight or ride a cart. Were you praying?"

The yard held its breath. Somewhere a pigeon cooed, fat and indifferent.

And here is the thing about Tamsin Cobb: she was tired. She was tired down to the marrow, tired of rules, tired of smallness, tired of a city that let children die of lung-rot because the alternative to dying was illegal. She looked at the Inquisitor, and she thought of Tam eating bread and honey at midnight, and she said:

"Yes. I was praying. And somebody answered. And if that's a crime, then arrest me, but you'll do it knowing my boy is alive because of it, and so is every person in this yard glad of it, whatever they're pretending right now."

The silence afterward was total. Even the pigeon stopped.

Halloway studied her for a long, long moment. Then she closed Tamsin's fingers over the marked palm, gently, the way you'd close a book you intended to read later.

"Unverified report," she said, loud enough for the yard. "No evidence of unlawful practice. The mark may be a scald or a birthmark surfacing. We'll require the subject to present herself for examination within the week." Her eyes flicked up to Tamsin's, and there was a message in them, plain as print: *Go home. Cover your hand. Stop making my job impossible.* Then, quietly, almost beneath hearing: "You were seen. By more than me. Be careful who you pray in front of."

She turned and walked away, and her boots took their unhurried rhythm with them, and the yard exhaled all at once like a bellows.

* * *

That night, Tamsin sat by the fire with Tam asleep and whole beside her, and she turned her marked palm toward the flames and watched the spiral catch the light.

It was warm. Warmer than it had been at noon. And underneath the warmth—faint, far off, like music through a wall—she could feel something listening. Not menacingly. Patiently. The way a large animal waits outside a door it knows will eventually open.

"Thank you," she whispered, because manners cost nothing even when everything else does. "Whoever you are. Thank you."

The warmth pulsed once, pleased, like a cat arching under a hand.

Then it spoke—not in words, not yet, but in a pressure behind her ribs, a meaning that arrived fully formed the way meanings do in dreams:

*More soon,* it seemed to say. *You are the first. There will be others. And they will need to find each other before the ones in grey coats find them.*

Tamsin sat very still.

Outside, far across the rooftops of Corvane, a church bell rang the hour, and beneath its bronze note—if you had a marked palm, if you pressed it flat against your chest and listened—you could hear something else. A hum. Deep and old and rising, like a hymn remembered underground.

Somewhere in the Gray Market, a charm-seller with sparrows on his shoulder paused mid-sale and went pale, though Tamsin wouldn't learn his name for days yet.

All she knew, sitting in her small room with her sleeping son and her burning hand, was that the well she'd shouted down had not merely answered.

It was climbing up.
