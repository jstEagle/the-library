## 6. Speakers Are Summoned

Vesper Winterbourne had always believed that the worst way to wake up was cold water to the face. He revised this opinion at half past three in the morning, when every rat in Corvane began singing.

Not squeaking. Not chittering. *Singing* — a low, droning hum that rose through the floorboards of his rented room above the charm shop like steam through a kettle, and it was not one voice but hundreds, thousands, the whole gray underground of the city opened at once and exhaling a hymn.

He sat up in bed with his heart going like a trapped moth and listened.

The words were in no language he knew, and yet he knew them, the way you know a tune from your childhood without ever remembering learning it. The rats below were reciting something old. Something patient. Something that had been waiting under the city for seven years with its hands folded and its eyes closed, and had now, apparently, decided to open them.

"Stop that," Vesper said aloud, to the floor, in the firm tone he used on pigeons who wanted more than their share of bread. "It's the middle of the night. Some of us have stalls to set up."

The humming did not stop. If anything, it deepened, gathering harmonies from somewhere further down, somewhere older than sewers, and Vesper pressed his palms against his ears and discovered that it didn't matter, because the song wasn't coming through his ears at all. It was coming through the part of him he'd spent eleven years pretending wasn't there.

The beast-gift. The listening. The thing that had gotten three speakers hanged before he was old enough to shave.

"Oh," he said quietly, into the dark. "Oh, no."

* * *

By dawn the rats had stopped, but the sparrows hadn't.

They came to his window in a delegation of nine, which was unusual — sparrows rarely traveled in formal numbers — and perched along the sill in a row like a jury, regarding him with small black eyes full of terrible news.

"The quiet places are getting louder," said the eldest, a scruffy hen with a torn wing feather who had been gossiping on his sill since before the Purge. "The gutters are humming. The bell tower is humming. The *cathedral*, charm-seller. The cathedral is humming so loud the bats have moved out."

"Bats are sensible," Vesper said, knotting a luck-charm with hands that wanted very much to shake. "Bats don't pay rent either."

"This isn't funny."

"It's never funny," he agreed. "That's why I do it. If I stop making jokes, I'll have to start thinking, and if I start thinking, I'll have to admit what this is." He tied off the knot. His fingers knew the work; they could have done it asleep, and often had. "And I'd rather not."

The sparrow looked at him for a long moment with an expression he recognized, because he wore it himself most mornings: the look of someone watching a friend walk calmly toward a cliff and deciding, out of politeness, not to mention it again.

"The fox came by," she said finally.

Vesper's hands went still. "When?"

"Before dawn. It sat on your roof and watched the cathedral and made a sound I've never heard a fox make." She ruffled her feathers. "It sounded like grief, if grief had a vocabulary."

"Did it say anything?"

"Said to tell you: *when the second one walks, run.*" The sparrow cocked her head. "I don't know what it means. But the pigeons say two people left their homes last night without shoes, and the moles say the ground under Foundling Street is warm, and the dogs—" she paused, and lowered her voice as though the walls might be listening, which, in Corvane, they sometimes seemed to be, "—the dogs won't bark anymore. Anywhere in the city. They just watch."

Vesper looked down at the luck-knot in his hand, a harmless tangle of red thread that he sold for two coppers and which contained, if you squinted, absolutely nothing illegal whatsoever.

"When the second one walks," he repeated.

"Yes."

"And has anyone counted?"

The sparrow's silence was answer enough.

* * *

He heard about the first one at midday, from a mole who surfaced in the market's drainage grate with dirt on her nose and urgency in every syllable.

"A laundress," the mole reported. "Tamsin Oduya, off Sudsrow. Prayed over her boy when the fever had him, and the fever *left*, charm-seller, just poured out of him like bathwater, and she's got a brand on her wrist now that glows when—"

"I know," Vesper said. "I know how brands work."

"—and the Inquisitors took her this morning, and here's the strange part." The mole leaned close, as if the whole Gray Market weren't already leaning close around them, pretending to buy turnips. "She went *willingly*. Walked out of her own house smiling. Her neighbors say she kept saying *he asked so nicely.*"

"He," said Vesper.

"That's what they said. *He.*"

Vesper bought a turnip he didn't want, because the stallholder was staring, and walked home the long way, taking three unnecessary turns and doubling back twice, habits worn into him like grooves into a stair. The city felt different today. He couldn't have said how, exactly, except that it was quieter in the wrong register — the dogs silent, the cats sitting motionless on walls facing east, all of them, every cat he passed, facing east toward the cathedral like iron filings around a lodestone.

At his door, the fox was waiting.

It sat on his windowsill as though it paid rent there, red as a coal, tail wrapped neat around its feet, and its amber eyes held the particular patience of a creature that has waited out several empires and expects to wait out this one too.

"You're back," Vesper said, unlocking the door with hands steadier than he felt. "You vanish for a week, send messages through sparrows like some kind of furry spymaster, and now you're on my sill looking smug."

"I am never smug," said the fox, in that archaic, careful Common of its, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "I am *correct*, which is frequently mistaken for it."

"Come in before the neighbors see me talking to my windowsill."

* * *

Inside, with the shutters drawn and a kettle on — because Vesper firmly believed no apocalypse should be discussed without tea — the fox curled up on his one good chair and told him.

"There were twelve gods in Vellichor," it said. "You know this, or your grandparents knew it, before the maps were burned and the names scraped off the stones. When the Choir fell silent, the gods did not die. They slept. And sleep, charm-seller, is not death. Sleep ends."

"One laundress praying her son well doesn't mean—"

"One laundress is the first swallow," the fox said. "You know what follows swallows."

Vesper poured the tea. His hands were steady. He was quite proud of his hands. "Summers."

"Summers," the fox agreed. "The gods are waking. And a waking god needs a mouth. It cannot speak the world itself — not anymore, not after what was done to them — so it reaches for the ones who already know how to listen. The speakers. Every man and woman in this country born with an ear turned toward beasts is also, whether they like it or not, a door left unlatched." Its amber eyes fixed on him. "How many doors do you think there are in Corvane?"

"I don't know what you're—"

"Fourteen," said the fox. "There are fourteen. I have counted. Two have already answered."

The kettle screamed. Vesper took it off the heat with great care, as though sudden movements might cost him something.

"Two," he said. "Two answered. Out of fourteen."

"Tamsin Oduya was the first. She is in the Inquisitors' cells now, and the god in her does not mind; a cell is only a smaller temple, from its point of view. The second was a lamplighter named Colm Ferris. He walked out of his lodging house at moonrise, barefoot, smiling, and was found at dawn on the cathedral steps, standing perfectly still, with tide-water in his eyes."

"Tide-water."

"The sea the gods remember," the fox said softly. "The sea that was in Vellichor, before Vellichor was erased. They dream of it while they wake, and it spills out of whoever carries them." It stood, stretched, and paced the length of his small room, and Vesper noticed — not for the first time — that the fox moved like something much larger wearing a small shape uncomfortably. "Twelve remain. Twelve doors, still shut, still pretending to be walls."

"Fox." Vesper set down the teapot. "Say what you mean."

"What I mean," said the fox, turning, "is that hiding works beautifully right up until the moment someone starts calling your name. You have spent eleven years being unremarkable, and you are very good at it — truly, it is the finest unremarkableness I have ever seen — but the gods are not Inquisitors. They will not search for you. They will simply call, the way the sun calls to flowers, and every speaker in this city will hear it at once, and the only question is which of you loves your small life more than you love answering."

"I don't love anything about answering."

"No," the fox agreed. "But you love the sparrows. And the rats. And that dreadful mole. And the gods know exactly how to make a summons sound like a favor."

Outside, distantly, a church bell rang the hour — except it didn't, quite. The note bent halfway through, sagged into a lower tone that no bell in Corvane had ever been cast to make, and held it a moment too long before letting go.

Vesper and the fox looked at each other.

"They're tuning up," Vesper said. His voice came out flat and strange. "The whole city. They're tuning up."

* * *

He told himself he would stay indoors. He told himself this with real conviction, the way he told himself every year that he would save money, and then, at dusk, he found himself walking anyway — because the sparrows had come back in greater numbers, twenty of them now, thirty, lining his roof ridge like mourners, and because the dogs of the street had all come to sit outside his shop door in a silent congregation, and because Wren the mole's cousin came up through his floorboards to report that a third speaker, a fishwife off the canals, had stopped speaking entirely and only *hummed* now, and hummed in harmony with the drains.

So he walked. Because a man who has taught himself to be small learns that smallness is a kind of work, and work keeps the panic in its pen.

He was passing Tanner's Row when he saw them.

Two figures, walking side by side up the middle of the street, unhurried, barefoot on the cobbles despite the cold. A woman and a young man — the young man he recognized, dimly, as a stable-hand who used to whistle to the carriage horses in a way that made the horses weep. Their eyes were open. That was the worst of it. Their eyes were wide open and full of slow-moving gray water, tide-water, sea-water from a sea that had been deleted from existence, and they were smiling the mild, courteous smiles of guests arriving at a party.

People had come out of their houses to watch. Nobody spoke. A child started crying and was hushed.

"They're going to the cathedral," whispered the woman next to Vesper, a baker with flour to her elbows. "Third one today. Should someone—should we stop them?"

"Stop them how?" said her husband. "With what? A bread paddle?"

And Vesper understood, standing in the crowd with his collar up and his heart hammering like a fist on a door, that nobody would stop them, that nothing would stop them, that the summons moving through Corvane was not a sound but a tide, and tides are not argued with.

The stable-hand turned his head as he passed. Just slightly. Just enough.

His tide-gray eyes found Vesper in the crowd the way a compass finds north, and he smiled wider — a smile with too much welcome in it, the welcome of something vast and patient that had just confirmed an address — and he said, in a voice like water over stones:

"*Soon.*"

Then he walked on, and the woman walked with him, and the crowd exhaled all at once.

Vesper did not remember walking home. He arrived, somehow, with his shutters banging behind him and the fox already standing on his table, fur bristled to twice its size, amber eyes blazing.

"You felt it," the fox said. Not a question.

"It looked at me." Vesper's voice cracked in the middle and he hated it. "Fox, it *looked* at me. Eleven years. Eleven years of being nobody, of selling thread and knots and nonsense, of laughing at the right jokes and flinching from the wrong ones, and one look across a street and—"

"Now you understand." The fox leapt down, began to pace, quick and sharp. "Invisibility was never a refuge. It was a countdown. Every day you hid was a day closer to the waking, and the waking does not care how well you hid. Twelve doors left, charm-seller. Twelve. And tonight, when the hymn comes again — and it will come again, it will come louder — you will hear your name inside it. Not spoken. *Meant.* There is a difference, and you will feel it in your teeth."

"Then I'll leave." He was already pulling the satchel from under the bed, already stuffing charms into it with hands that shook now, properly shook, no more pride in them. "Tonight. Before the hymn. There are roads south, there's the river, there's—"

"The borders," said the fox quietly.

Vesper stopped.

"You've felt it," the fox said. "Don't pretend you haven't. The roads that bend back on themselves. The ferryman who swears the bridge was washed out yesterday and swears it again today. This country was drawn as a trap when the gods fell silent — a snare to hold them in their sleep — and now that they're stirring, the snare is drawing shut. There is exactly one way out of Vellichor, and it is not south, and it is not the river." Its eyes gleamed in the lamplight. "It is the Door. And we must reach it before the Choir finishes waking — or before the Inquisitors finish counting their fourteen speakers and come knocking on yours."

Somewhere across the darkening city, faint as breath, the humming began again.

Vesper stood in the middle of his small room with his half-packed satchel and his whole small life, and outside, on the roof ridge, forty sparrows lifted into the air at once, all together, all in the same direction — away from the cathedral, away from the sound, fleeing like birds before a storm — and the oldest hen, the one with the torn wing, dropped briefly to his windowsill on her way past.

"Run, charm-seller," she said. "When the second one walks, run. You're the twelfth door. Maybe the thirteenth. We've lost count, and the counting is the frightening part."

Then she was gone, and the hymn rose beneath the floorboards like floodwater, and Vesper Winterbourne — seller of harmless charms, master of being unremarkable, invisible man — picked up his satchel and ran.
