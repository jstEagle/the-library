## 7. Interrogation by Candlelight

The knock came at the ninth bell, which was wrong in three separate ways.

First, Inquisitors did not knock; they arrived, the way weather arrives, and you simply endured them. Second, no Inquisitor had ever visited Vesper's rooms above the charm shop, because visiting a charm seller implied he was worth visiting, and his entire life's work had been built on the principle that he was not. Third—and this was the one that made his hands go still over the luck-knot he was braiding—the knock came in threes. Two soft, one lighter. A rhythm he knew.

He had not heard that rhythm in seven years. He had thought about it more times than he would ever admit to anyone, including himself.

Vesper set down the knot, wiped his palms on his apron out of pure habit—he hadn't sold anything since noon—and opened the door.

Sable Halloway stood in the corridor holding an official lantern, though there was nothing official about the way she held it: slightly too low, slightly too far from her body, as if she'd forgotten it was a prop. Her gray coat was buttoned to the throat. Her hair was pinned back with the severity of someone who had decided, long ago, that beauty was a liability in her line of work and had executed the decision thoroughly.

"Inquisitor Halloway," Vesper said.

"Charm-seller Winterbourne." Her voice was flat as slate. "You're summoned for questioning regarding the matter of the answered prayers."

"Am I?" He leaned against the doorframe, aiming for bored and hoping he'd landed somewhere near it. "I sell knots, Inquisitor. The gods have never shown interest in my inventory."

"The gods," she said, "have recently shown interest in everything."

Behind him, in the shadows of the rafters, the fox—silent as furniture all evening—made a sound that was almost certainly a laugh.

* * *

Sable had prepared for this interview the way she prepared for everything: obsessively, and then by pretending she hadn't.

She knew his file by heart. *Vesper Winterbourne, thirty-one, licensed minor-charmer, no violations, no associations of note, no family.* She had written half of it herself, years ago, when a junior clerk asked why the market's most unremarkable man had no record before age twenty-four. She had invented a childhood for him. A village. A dead mother. It had been the first lie she'd told for him, and it had not been the last.

Now she climbed his stairs behind him and catalogued the rooms the way she was trained to: candle stubs, dried lavender, a chipped blue teapot, thread everywhere, thread on the chair, thread on the floor, a half-finished knot glowing faintly gold in the lamplight. A nest of small comforts. A life built to look like it could be burned down without loss.

She knew better. She had helped him build it.

"Sit," he said, gesturing at the one chair without thread on it, and then, catching himself, added, "Inquisitor."

"You may call me Inquisitor," she said. "It's cleaner."

"It always was." He put water on to boil, which was absurd—they both knew it was absurd, no one boiled water during an interrogation—but his hands needed something to do, and hers needed something to watch. That had been the architecture of their old conversations: his hands, her eyes, the words passing between like birds neither of them could catch.

"You understand why you're here," she said, opening her ledger.

"Because two speakers walked into the cathedral last week with their eyes full of ocean, and the Office is frightened, and frightened offices pull files." He set out two cups. "Mine is thin. I imagine that's why I'm here and not already in a cell."

"That's remarkably well-informed for a charm-seller."

"Sparrows talk, Inquisitor. It's the one magic I'm permitted."

And there it was—that flicker at the corner of her mouth, quickly suppressed, the ghost of the woman who used to laugh into her sleeve at his jokes so the other apprentices wouldn't hear. Sable watched him pour the tea and hated how precisely her body remembered all of this: the way he held the pot high, the way he always gave her the cup without the chip in the rim, the way the room smelled of lavender and hot metal and something underneath that was just *him*, unchanged by seven years of law and silence.

She opened her ledger anyway.

"Where were you on the night of the ninth?"

"Asleep."

"Witnesses?"

"My neighbors heard me snoring through the wall. I'm told it was very convincing."

Her pen paused. "You find this funny."

"I find it necessary," Vesper said quietly. "Humor is the only charm I've never been arrested for."

* * *

She asked about Merrin Cole next, and Vesper felt the question land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"Merrin Cole," Sable read, "laundress, speaker-class two, summoned on the fourth. Found walking toward the cathedral. You knew her."

"I bought soap from her sister."

"You were seen speaking with her. Twice. In the market."

"I speak with everyone in the market. It's called commerce."

"What did you discuss?"

"The price of wool. The weather. Whether her son's cough was improving." He met her eyes and lied with his whole face, warmly, fluently, the way she had once told him he did everything—with more care than the task required. "I discussed the price of wool with a woman who is now a vessel for something old and hungry, and I would discuss it again, because she was kind to me when kindness cost her nothing."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

Sable looked down at her ledger, at the neat columns of questions she'd drafted in her office by candlelight, each one designed to corner him gently, to give him every exit except the true one. And she realized, with a tiredness that went all the way down to the bone, that he was using every single exit. Beautifully. Gratefully. The way a man drinks water in a desert—not because he's fooled, but because he trusts the hand offering it.

She turned the page.

"Do you know what happens to speakers who are summoned and refuse?" she asked.

"They're found," Vesper said. "Eventually. Walking."

"Yes." Her pen hovered. "Do you know where they walk *from*, Master Winterbourne? What direction do they come from when we find them?"

He was quiet a moment. Outside, a sparrow landed on the sill and said, in its small gossiping voice, *the tall one knows, the tall one knows,* and Vesper wished, briefly and sincerely, that sparrows kept secrets.

"From the east," he said. "From the river districts."

"From your street," Sable said softly.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." She closed the ledger. For a moment—for one unguarded moment that lasted exactly as long as it took either of them to notice it—she was not an Inquisitor and he was not a suspect, and they were just two people in a small warm room who had once known each other completely and now knew each other only in the negative space of what they refused to ask.

Then she noticed it, and so did he, and the armor came back up on both sides with an almost audible click.

* * *

*Ask me,* Vesper thought, watching her repack her things with brisk efficiency. *Ask me directly, just once, and I'll tell you everything. About the fox. About the Door. About the hymn under the city and the rats reciting dead languages and the summons humming in my teeth every night like a second heartbeat.*

But she wouldn't ask. That was the mercy and the torment of Sable Halloway: she would circle the truth forever, giving him chances to hand it to her freely, because she understood—if she forced it out of him, she would have to act on it, and if she acted on it, she would lose him twice. Once to the pyre and once to memory.

So instead she said, "Your license comes up for renewal next month."

"Does it."

"There will be a new inspection protocol. Charms will be tested for resonance." She snapped her case shut. "Resonant charms are classified as instruments of summoning. You'll want to make sure your inventory is... quiet."

"That sounds like advice, Inquisitor."

"It sounds like procedure." She stood, and the lantern light caught her face for just a moment, and he saw what she'd been hiding behind the slate voice: fear. Not of him. *For* him. It was worse. He could survive being hunted. Being protected was going to break his heart.

"Thank you for the tea," she said at the door.

"You didn't drink it."

"No," she agreed. "I never do. You always make it too strong."

"You always complain about that."

"And you always make it that way anyway." Something moved across her face—grief wearing humor's coat—and then she was gone, boots measured and even on the stairs, the official stride, the one that meant witnesses might be listening.

Vesper stood alone in his little room full of thread and lavender, and after a while he picked up her untouched cup and drank it himself, and it was terrible, and he drank all of it.

* * *

The fox waited until the footsteps faded entirely, until even the sparrow on the sill lost interest and flew off into the dark, before it spoke.

"She loves you," it said, from the rafter, in its archaic voice that sounded like wind through a ruined orchard.

"Don't."

"She lies the way rivers lie—smoothly, and only to keep moving."

"*Don't.*" Vesper set the cup down harder than he meant to. "You don't know anything about it."

"I know what love looks like when it's been starving for seven years," the fox said. "I invented the look."

Vesper pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "She thinks I'm a suspect. She's building a case. Every question tonight goes in that ledger, and that ledger goes to her superiors, and if I'm ever foolish enough to be caught doing anything—" He stopped. Breathed. "She's not protecting me. She can't afford to. Neither can I."

"Mm," said the fox, in the tone of a creature who has watched empires rise and fall and has opinions about all of them.

From somewhere beneath the floorboards—beneath the cellar, beneath the foundations, beneath whatever the city pretended it was built on—came the hum again. Fainter than last night. Patient. A hymn holding its breath between verses.

Vesper went still, listening with the whole of his skin.

"What is it?" the fox asked, though it knew.

"The song changed," Vesper whispered. "There's a new verse. It's..." He swallowed. "It's in my voice."

The fox rose from the rafter, stretched, and dropped silently to the floor, and when it looked up at him its amber eyes held something that was not comfort and not warning but some third thing, older than both.

"Then we should hurry," it said. "Before the choir learns all the words."

* * *

Three streets away, in a cold office above the Inquisitorial Hall, Sable Halloway sat before her ledger with a fresh candle and wrote:

*Subject cooperative. No evidence of summoning activity. Charm inventory within tolerance. Recommend continued observation.*

All of it true. All of it a lie. She had learned, over seven years, that the best false reports were assembled entirely from accurate sentences, arranged in an order that concealed rather than revealed—a trick she suspected she'd learned from him, though she could no longer remember which of them had taught it to the other.

She turned to a clean page, hesitated, and wrote one more line, small, in the margin where no clerk would look:

*He makes the tea too strong on purpose. He always has.*

Then she blew out the candle, and in the dark she took from her breast pocket the pressed flower—dried to a whisper, seven years old, a sprig of white yarrow he'd given her on a bridge in another lifetime—and held it a moment before putting it away.

Below her window, far off toward the river, something beneath the city began, very softly, to sing.
