## 8. The Hollow Ones

The fox had said, *Go and see her. You'll want to know what's waiting for you,* in the tone of a physician recommending a very thorough amputation.

Vesper had argued, of course. He was quite good at arguing with the fox, mostly because the fox let him win the small arguments so it could lose none of the large ones. He'd argued that visiting a hollowed speaker was exactly the sort of thing an invisible man did not do. That Merrin Oakeshott lived on Candlewick Row, four doors from an Inquisitor waystation. That his face was known now—*named aloud*, the fox kept saying, as if that were a small detail and not the end of seven years of careful, painstaking smallness.

But the rats had come to him at dawn, a delegation of nine, sitting in a row along his windowsill like tiny gray magistrates, and told him that Merrin hadn't slept in six days. That she stood at her window all night, singing to the river. That the fish were listening.

And Vesper Winterbourne had never once, in thirty-one years, been able to refuse a creature that asked him for help. It was, he reflected grimly as he pulled his hood up against the drizzle, the single flaw in his otherwise flawless strategy of survival: he cared. Embarrassing, really. He should have had it seen to.

* * *

Candlewick Row smelled of tallow and wet wool, the way poor streets always did after rain—the smell of people burning cheap light to hold off cheap dark. Merrin's house was narrow and leaning, squeezed between a chandler's shop and a house that had given up on standing altogether and was resting against its neighbor like a drunk uncle. Laundry hung overhead between windows, dripping. Somewhere a baby cried with the tireless indignation only babies could sustain.

Vesper had known Merrin before. Everyone in the Gray Market had. She sold eels, which was a profession that required a voice like a foghorn and hands like knotted rope, and she had both, plus a laugh you could hear from three stalls away. She used to call him *lucky Ves* and slip him a smoked eel on feast days because, she said, a thin man needed feeding more than a fat one, whatever the physicians claimed.

He knocked the way neighbors knock—two quick raps, apologetic—and when no answer came, he tried the door. Unlocked. Of course it was unlocked. Locked doors were for people who still lived inside their houses.

Inside, the room was dim and tidy in the way of rooms whose owner had stopped noticing them. A cold hearth. A chair pushed back at an angle, abandoned mid-thought. Dishes washed and stacked with terrible precision. And by the window, framed in gray light, stood Merrin Oakeshott.

She was facing the glass, hands folded, and she was humming.

The sound stopped him in the doorway. Not because it was beautiful—it wasn't, not anymore—but because it was *layered*. Underneath her cracked laundress-and-eelwife voice ran something else, something vast and slow, the way you might hear the sea if you pressed a shell too hard against your ear. The windowpane buzzed faintly with it. On the sill outside, three pigeons sat perfectly still, heads cocked, drinking the sound.

"Merrin," Vesper said gently. "It's Ves. Lucky Ves. From the market."

She turned.

He had prepared himself for bad. He had spent the walk rehearsing bad: sunken cheeks, wild hair, maybe sores, maybe the vacant stare of the fevered. What he had not prepared for was her eyes.

They were full of tide-water.

Not water-colored. Not watery. *Full*—the irises gone, replaced by a slow gray-green movement, a shifting depth in which things drifted that were not reflections of the room. Once, he thought he saw the pale suggestion of a fish turn over in there, far down. Her face was still Merrin's face, lined and strong and freckled across the nose, but behind it, through it, something looked out that had never once bought eels or laughed at market jokes or worried about rent.

"Oh," said Merrin's mouth, in a voice that was half hers and half the hum. "Oh, but you came quickly."

"I—" Vesper's carefully practiced smallness failed him entirely. "I heard you weren't well."

"I'm extremely well." She smiled, and the smile was wrong—not cruel, just *wide*, wider than faces went, as though someone else was operating it and hadn't finished reading the instructions. "I've never been better. I've never been anything at all. Do you know what a relief it is, Ves? To stop being one small tired person? There's so much room now."

Every instinct he owned screamed at him to leave. He stayed. He was beginning to suspect this was simply what his life was going to be now: every instinct screaming, and him staying anyway.

"May I sit?" he asked, because manners were a rope he could cling to.

"You may stand where you are. It's easier to see you there." She tilted her head, and the tide-water in her eyes swirled. "You're frightened. That's sensible. I was frightened too, the first night. But then the fear went out of me like water out of a pulled plug, and honestly? Good riddance. Fear is *exhausting*. You must be so tired, Ves. Seven years of being afraid. I can hear it in you. It sounds like a kettle that never quite boils."

"The rats sent me," he said, grasping for solid ground. "They said you sing to the river."

"I sing what I'm given." She unfolded her hands and spread them, palms up, an offering or a surrender. "Would you like to hear today's portion? It's short. They're getting shorter. Or perhaps longer—I can't tell anymore; time is rather like soup in here." And without waiting for an answer, she began:

"*When the door stands open in the fen,
and the last song is carried on the last tongue,
then the sleeper who refused shall choose,
and the chooser shall forget the choosing.*"

The words landed in the room like stones dropped into still water. Outside, the pigeons shifted. In the walls, Vesper realized with a lurch, mice had gathered—he could feel them, dozens of them, pressed against the plaster, listening the way the devout listen.

"That's—" His mouth was dry. "*That's* about me, isn't it. The last tongue. The chooser."

Merrin's head tilted further, past comfortable, past possible, and the tide-water churned. For a moment something moved beneath the surface of her—something vast turning over in deep water—and when she spoke again, the hum underneath her voice had risen until the dishes rattled softly in their stack.

"There you are," she breathed. "There you *are*. We wondered where they'd hidden you."

"We?"

"The quiet ones. The ones who learned to whisper instead of sing." She took a step toward him, and her bare feet made no sound on the boards, which feet should do, feet should always make some sound. "You speak to beasts. Oh, don't make that face—it's written all over you, dear heart, it's in how you stand, always angled toward the door, always listening down. The sparrows gossip about you. Did you know that? They think you're the kindest man in Corvane. They're wrong, of course. You're the *loneliest*. But kindness and loneliness wear the same coat."

"Stop," Vesper said quietly.

"They've been saving you." Another step. The tide-water rose higher in her eyes, brimming, though it never spilled. "All this time, while you sold your little knots and your little charms and made yourself so wonderfully, so *wastefully* small—they were saving you. Because a choir needs more than voices, Ves. A choir needs someone to carry the last note home. And you—"

"*Stop.*"

He hadn't meant to shout. The word came out of him with seven years of pressure behind it, and every creature in the room flinched—the pigeons burst upward off the sill, the mice poured away through the walls like sand through fingers—and Merrin stopped. Stood still. And slowly, slowly, the tide-water drained from her eyes, and what was left looking out of them was just Merrin, just the eelwife, just a tired woman in a cold room, and she was crying.

"Ves," she whispered, and her voice was entirely her own, small and cracked and human. "Ves, I can't—there's gaps, there's hours gone, I wake up standing at the window and my throat hurts and I don't know what I've said. I saw my sister yesterday and I called her by a name nobody's used in a hundred years and she *ran*, Ves, she ran from me." Her hands came up and pressed against her own cheeks as if checking that her face was still hers. "Am I still in here? Tell me I'm still in here."

"You're in here," Vesper said, and crossed the room and took her hands, because what else was there, what else had there ever been. Her fingers were ice. "You're in here, Merrin. I know the difference. Believe me, I know exactly what you sound like when it's you."

"How?" she asked, and then her eyes widened, and he watched the understanding arrive, and watched her be afraid of it. "How do you know, lucky Ves?"

"Because I talk to animals," he said. "Properly. Beast-tongue, since I was six years old. And because two days ago the rats started reciting hymns in a dead language, and a fox started talking to me in archaic Common, and I have been pretending very hard that none of it is happening." He laughed, and it came out broken. "I'm apparently not very good at pretending anymore."

"No," Merrin agreed, wetly. "You're really not."

They stood like that a moment, two speakers holding hands in a cold room, and outside the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the row the baby finally stopped crying, which somehow felt ominous.

"Merrin," Vesper said carefully. "When it has you. When you're... full. Does it say why? Why now? Why speakers?"

Her face did something complicated—grief and terror wrestling for the same ground. "It says the country is a lung," she said, "and it's been holding its breath for seven years, and it can't anymore. It says everything that was buried is coming up for air. It says..." She swallowed. "It says the borders aren't borders anymore. They're a *hand*, Ves. Closing."

"The Door of Unmaking," Vesper said. "The fox mentioned—"

"The fox." Merrin went very still. "Red? Old eyes? Talks like a book nobody finished writing?"

"...Yes?"

"Don't trust it." The words came fast, urgent, and for a heartbeat the tide-water flickered at the edges of her irises, as if the thing inside her was leaning forward to listen to its own warning. "Don't— I don't know why I'm saying that, I don't know where it comes from, but Ves, *don't trust the fox*, it's the oldest thing in this country and old things want—"

The flicker spread. Her eyes flooded gray-green, top to bottom, in the space of a breath, and her spine straightened, and her voice changed mid-word into that layered vastness, and Vesper felt the temperature of the room drop as though a door had opened onto winter.

"*The vessel tires,*" said Merrin's mouth, in Merrin's voice that was not Merrin's voice. "*We return her shortly. She is useful. They are all useful. As you will be, little whisperer. Come to the cathedral when you are ready. Come and stop hiding. We have waited so patiently, and patience is nearly done, and the door in the fen will not stay shut forever, and neither will we.*"

"I won't come," Vesper said. His voice shook. He let it. "I've spent my whole life refusing things bigger than me. I'm rather good at it."

"*No,*" the god said, and the tide-water smiled. "*You haven't. You've spent your whole life refusing small things. Money. Comfort. Safety. You have never once refused a creature that needed you. And we are the largest creature there has ever been, and we need you, and you will come, because you cannot help it. It is the single flaw in your otherwise flawless strategy of survival.*"

Vesper went cold to the marrow. Those were almost his own thoughts, his own bitter joke, word for word, plucked out of his skull like a splinter.

"You shouldn't be able to read me," he whispered.

"*We are not reading you,*" said the god gently, almost kindly, which was infinitely worse. "*We are remembering you. There is a difference. Ask the fox.*"

Merrin shuddered, sagged, and would have fallen if he hadn't caught her. For a moment she was just weight and wet breath and the smell of river water in her hair, and then her eyes cleared, human and exhausted and mortified.

"Did I—" she croaked. "What did it say?"

"It offered me a job," Vesper said. "I declined."

"Good lad." She managed half a laugh, then gripped his sleeve with sudden fierce strength. "Ves. Whatever it offered—whatever any of them offer—remember that they only ever offer you yourself, magnified. That's the whole trick. That's the entire trick. They hold up a mirror and say *look how big you could be*, and you look, and while you're looking, you stop being anything at all." Her grip tightened. "I liked being small, Ves. I liked eels. I liked my sister. Don't let them take small from you. Small is where the people live."

Outside, boots on cobbles—several pairs, unhurried, official. Through the window Vesper saw gray cloaks moving down Candlewick Row, and the neighbors pulling their children indoors, and the chandler's shutter closing with the particular speed of a man who wanted no part of whatever came next.

Inquisitors. Four of them. Heading for this door.

"They've come for me," Merrin said, strangely calm. "Or for you. Possibly both. Possibly they can't tell the difference anymore either." She pushed him toward the back of the house, toward the scullery and its low window over the drainage ditch. "Go. Out the back. And Ves—" She caught his hand one last time, and her eyes were clear, and human, and terrified, and kind. "Whatever the fox told you about the Door—whatever it promised you—you check it twice. Promise me. Promise me you'll check it twice."

"I promise," Vesper said, because she was Merrin, and because she was afraid, and because he could not help it.

He went out the scullery window into the ditch, into the cold and the smell, and behind him he heard the front door open and the measured voice of authority saying a name—her name—and Merrin Oakeshott answering, calm as Sunday, "*I've been expecting you. So has he.*"

The fox was waiting at the edge of the Fenmarch road, sitting in the rain as though rain were a rumor it had declined to believe.

"Well?" it said. "Now do you understand what's waiting for you?"

"Yes," Vester said, wringing ditchwater from his sleeve, and hating how steady his voice came out. "And I have a question for you, fox. And you're going to answer it honestly, or so help me, I'll ask the rats, and they're *far* less polite."

The fox's ears went back, just slightly. Just enough.

"Ask," it said.
