## 9. Running the Gray Streets

The knock came at the fourth bell of the morning, and it was not a polite knock. It was the kind of knock that had a warrant behind it.

Vesper was already awake—he had not truly slept since Merrin had said his name in that tide-water voice—but he lay still on his cot and listened the way he'd taught himself to listen for twenty years: with his whole body, like a hare in a field of hawks.

Three sets of boots on the stairs outside his room above the charm shop. Heavy ones. Inquisitors wore boots with iron heel-plates so that people would hear them coming. Vesper had always thought this was a design flaw, personally, but then he supposed terror worked better when it announced itself.

"They're coming up," hissed the fox from the windowsill, where it had spent the night sitting like a gargoyle with opinions. "Through the door, most likely. Doors are traditional."

"Thank you," Vesper whispered. "Your tactical genius is noted."

"I also considered the window."

"The window is three floors up."

"And yet."

Vesper was already moving. He pulled on his coat, swept his luck-knots into a satchel—habit, even now, even fleeing for his life; a man did not leave stock behind—and eased open the shutters onto the gray light of Corvane's morning. Below, the alley ran behind the tanneries, slick with yesterday's rain and the general moral dampness of the city.

The door shuddered under the first blow.

"Vesper Winterbourne," boomed a voice he didn't recognize, which meant Sable hadn't come herself. Small mercies. Or large ones. He genuinely could not tell anymore. "By order of the Office of Inquiry, open—"

He went out the window instead.

* * *

The fall was shorter than it felt. He caught the drainpipe, slid, swore with a fluency that would have shocked his customers, who believed him a mild man, and landed in a heap beside a barrel of something that smelled like regret. The fox flowed down after him along a stack of crates with the insufferable grace of a creature that had never once fallen over anything.

"Left," said the fox.

"Right," said a pigeon overhead, from the eaves.

They looked at each other.

"Excuse me," said Vesper to the pigeon, "but whose side are you on?"

"The city's," said the pigeon, preening. "The gray streets belong to us. You're just borrowing them. Left goes to the Tanner's Cut, but there's a checkpoint there since dawn. Right goes down through the cisterns. Wet, but empty of boots." It cocked its head. "Also, you owe me bread. From the market. Tuesday. You gave crumbs to the sparrows and nothing to the pigeons, and we remember."

"We do remember," agreed the fox gravely. "Pigeon memory is a historical archive with wings."

Vesper went right.

Behind him, above, the door of his room gave way at last, and he heard the crash of an Inquisitor's boot on floorboards he'd mopped every week for six years. Something in his chest folded up small, like a letter. That had been his home. It had been small and cold and entirely unremarkable, and he had loved it precisely because no one else ever had.

Now it would be picked apart by men in gray coats looking for evidence of what he was.

Well. They'd find the charms. Luck-knots and traveler's blessings and little woven birds that were only ever just thread. Harmless. He'd made sure everything was harmless for twenty years.

Except, apparently, him.

* * *

The cisterns beneath Corvane were older than the city above them, if you asked the rats, and the rats were older than the cisterns, so their testimony carried weight. Vesper moved through dripping dark lit by the fox's eyes, which glowed faintly green, which was one more item on the long list of things about the fox that Vesper had decided not to think about too hard because thinking about it too hard made the world tilt.

The moles found them first.

Moles, as a rule, did not come to Vesper. Moles kept to themselves, underground, in the great blind nation of the earth, and regarded surface affairs with the weary disdain of farmers watching kings parade. But tonight—or morning, whatever it was down here where the sun had never been invented—a mole surfaced from a side tunnel, blinked its pinprick eyes, and said:

"You're the speaker everyone's whispering about."

"I'm a charm seller," said Vesper automatically.

"You're the speaker," the mole repeated, with the patience of a creature who had never once been fooled by anything, living as it did in permanent darkness where lies had no purchase. "We felt you coming. The ground told us. Listen, ground-speaker, because I've walked far to say it, and walking is not my favorite thing."

It sat back on its haunches. Even in the dark, Vesper could feel it gathering itself like a small brown professor.

"The purge is moving district by district. Not random anymore. Ordered. They have lists. Names of speakers, going back years—someone has been keeping records, someone inside, someone patient." The mole's claws flexed. "The fishmonger Doss, who spoke with eels. Taken two nights past. The widow Anselm, who sang the cellar spiders calm. Taken. The boy who whistles to the ferry-horses—"

"A boy?" Vesper's stomach dropped. "How old?"

"Small," said the mole. "Younger than your luck-knots are old. They came for him at dawn. But here is the thing, ground-speaker, here is why I walked: they missed him. He ran. He's in the storm-drains off Cooper's Row, hiding in the dark with the water, and he's calling for help in beast-tongue, and every rat in that quarter hears him and none of them can do anything except feel terrible, which rats are not built for."

Vesper was already standing.

"That's a trap," said the fox flatly.

"Probably," said Vesper.

"It's certainly a trap. A child speaker loose in the drains, crying loud enough for every ear in the city? Either the Inquisitors know and they're waiting, or they don't know and will know within the hour, because—" the fox gestured with its entire body at the concept of Corvane "—look around."

"Then we're fast," said Vesper.

"Why?" The word cracked out of the fox sharper than it meant, and for a moment something ancient looked out through those green eyes, something tired beyond the arithmetic of centuries. "Why, Vesper? Every instinct you have says run. Run for the Fenmarch, run for the Door, run for the country that isn't on any map. Every hour you spend being noble is an hour closer to a cathedral full of hollow-eyed vessels. I have watched speakers die for smaller causes than a stranger's child."

Vesper crouched down to the fox's level. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping time.

"When I was nine," he said quietly, "I talked a cart-horse out of bolting on Foundry Bridge, and a man heard me do it, and my mother put me on a barge that same night and I never saw her again. She saved me by sending me away alone. I have thought about that every single day for twenty years, and I have never once decided whether it was love or cowardice." He straightened. "I'm not deciding it today either. I'm just going to get the boy."

The fox held his gaze for a long moment.

"You're going to be insufferable about this right up until it kills you," it said. "Fine. I know the drains. Try to keep up, and if you die, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," said Vesper, and ran.

* * *

Cooper's Row announced itself before they reached it: smoke, and shouting, and the particular organized clatter of an Inquisitor raid, which sounded like a kitchen falling downstairs in formation.

Vesper pressed himself into the mouth of an alley and listened upward, because in Corvane the pigeons saw everything and charged accordingly.

"Two wagons," reported a pigeon from a gutter, dropping down to a ledge near his head. "Eight graycoats. They've got a sniffer—that's a dog, dearie, don't look at me like that, I know you talk to dogs, and let me tell you, dogs have *opinions* about the ones they leash. The sniffer's got the child's trail. They're working the drain mouths one by one. You've got maybe a quarter hour before they corner him in the big cistern off the tannery sluice."

"Is there another way in?"

"There's always another way in. Cities are mostly holes." The pigeon ruffled. "That'll be two breads. You're building debt, charm-man."

"I'm building a *reputation*," muttered Vesper, and followed the fox down through a grate into the roaring dark of the storm-drains.

The water was calf-deep and cold enough to make arguments unnecessary. The fox trotted ahead along the drain's edge, tail high, utterly unbothered by wet or dark or the distant sound of men with iron-heeled boots, and Vesper followed, satchel held over his shoulder, heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't let it hammer since the Purge.

And then, faintly, through the rush of water, he heard it.

Not crying. Whistling. A thin, wavering whistle in beast-tongue—the grammar all wrong, the accent terrible, the way children speak any language—and the words were simple and repeated, over and over, the way children repeat things when repeating is the only rope they have:

*Anyone. Anyone. Anyone please.*

Vesper answered.

He whistled back down the drain, three notes, the old traveling-call that hedge-witches had used before the Choir fell, the call that meant *a friend is coming, hold on.* The whistling stopped. Then, disbelieving, trembling: *Friend? Real friend? Not gray-coat pretending?*

"Real friend," Vesper called aloud, and then, because the child might be too frightened for speech, he whistled it too, and added the promise-note at the end, the one that meant *no tricks.*

From ahead, small and ragged, came a sound Vesper felt in his ribs: a child laughing and sobbing at the same time, in the dark, in a drain, in a city that had outlawed everything he was.

*Hold on,* Vesper whistled. *I'm coming.*

* * *

Wren was eight, maybe nine, small enough that the water reached his chest, soaked to the bone, with a rat perched on his shoulder like a tiny furry conscience. His eyes in the fox-glow were enormous and dry in the way of someone who had cried himself empty and gone past it into something harder.

"You talk," Wren whispered, staring at Vesper.

"So do you."

"Mum says—said—" His face crumpled and reassembled itself with terrifying speed, the way children's faces do, grief and practicality sharing a room. "Mum said never let anyone hear. Never. And then the gray-coats came anyway, and she pushed me down the cellar hatch, and I ran, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They took her?"

Vesper couldn't lie to him. It seemed suddenly, obscenely important not to lie to him.

"I don't know," he said. "But I know someone finding out. And I know getting you out of here first."

"The gray-coats are close," reported the rat on Wren's shoulder, in the bored tone of a professional. "Four at the sluice mouth. Two more on the street above. The dog is conflicted, for what it's worth. It keeps losing the scent on purpose and then feeling guilty."

"Dogs," said the fox, "are better than people, and it isn't close."

"Can you get us out?" Wren asked Vesper, and there it was—the whole terrible weight of it, the whole reason Vesper had spent twenty years being small, packed into a child's question asked without doubt. Because children didn't doubt adults could do things. Doubt was a skill you learned later, usually from adults.

"Yes," said Vesper, before he could stop himself.

The fox shot him a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

* * *

The way out was a maintenance ladder to a coal-yard two streets over, according to the pigeons, who had by now assembled a small parliament on the subject of Vesper's escape and were charging him rates that bordered on extortion. The problem was the sluice mouth, where four graycoats stood with lanterns, between the fugitives and the ladder.

"Distraction?" whispered Vesper.

"What kind of distraction," said the fox, "does a man with a satchel of thread and a talking fox produce in thirty seconds?"

Vesper opened the satchel. He looked at his luck-knots—little woven birds, harmless, harmless, harmless—and then at the rats gathering in the shadows of the drain, dozens of them, summoned by Wren's frightened whistling and Vesper's promise, all of them listening.

"Gentlemen," he said. "Ladies. I need a miracle of ordinary unpleasantness."

Rats, it turns out, require very little encouragement.

What happened next was later described in the Inquisitor report—which Sable Halloway would read twice, and which would make her press her lips together to keep from smiling—as an outbreak of vermin of unprecedented coordination. Rats poured from the sluice grates in a silver-brown flood, up trouser legs, into lantern housings, across the toes of iron-heeled boots. One entered a graycoat's helmet and expressed, in the universal language of small clawed mammals in confined spaces, its displeasure. Lanterns dropped. Men screamed in registers the Office of Inquiry would prefer not to discuss. And in the chaos, a soaked child and a charm seller climbed a rusted ladder into a coal yard while a red fox walked along the wall above them like a spirit overseeing its work.

At the top, gasping, black with coal dust, Wren grabbed Vesper's sleeve.

"The rats," he breathed. "You told the rats. All of them. At once. Like it was easy."

"It wasn't easy."

"But you *did* it." Wren's face had changed. The fear was still there—it would be there forever now, Vesper knew, a tenant that never left—but underneath it something else had kindled, something Vesper recognized with a lurch because he'd smothered it in himself two decades ago. Wonder. Pride. The wild belief that what you were might be worth something. "My mum always said our gift was a curse to hide. But you just used it to *save*—"

"Don't," said Vesper, more sharply than he intended.

Wren flinched.

Vesper crouched. Coal dust and drain water and exhaustion had stripped away most of his practiced smallness, and what was left underneath spoke plainly.

"I'm sorry. Listen to me. What you are is not a curse. Your mum was wrong to say it that way, and she was right to say it scared, because both things are true at once and that's the whole rotten trick of it." He took the boy's shoulders. "But you cannot admire what I did. Do you understand? If you admire it, you'll do it again, and louder, and one day the wrong person will hear, and there won't be a charm seller in a drain to come for you. Being hidden isn't shame, Wren. Right now, tonight, it's the only armor anyone's given us."

Wren's chin trembled. "Then why did you come for me?"

Because I couldn't not, Vesper almost said. Because the moment I heard you whistling, twenty years of being small ended, and I don't know yet whether that's salvation or suicide. Because hiding, it turns out, was never a refuge—it was a countdown, and the clock just rang.

"Because someone came for me once," is what he said instead. "Late. Imperfectly. But they came."

Above the coal yard, bells began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the cathedral summons, deep and slow, rolling across the rooftops like a hand pressing down on the whole city. Every pigeon lifted at once, a gray explosion against the darkening sky. Down in the drains, Vesper felt rather than heard the rats go silent, all of them turning their heads toward the sound.

And the fox stood very still on the wall, ears flat, and said, in a voice gone quiet and old:

"It's starting. They're calling all of them in. Every speaker in Corvane." Its green eyes found Vesper's. "We have until tomorrow. Maybe less. The Fenmarch, Vesper. The drowned chapel. We move tonight, or the Door closes and the song begins without us."

Wren slipped his small, freezing hand into Vesper's, and did not let go.

Vesper looked at the child, at the fox, at the burning bells, and understood that the man who had sold luck-knots that morning no longer existed. There was no path back to smallness. There was only forward, into the fen, carrying everything he'd sworn never to carry: a gift, a child, a god in a fox's body, and a name that half the city was already whispering.

"All right," he said. "Tonight, then. The Fenmarch."

And far off across the roofs, the bells rang on, sweet and terrible, counting down.
