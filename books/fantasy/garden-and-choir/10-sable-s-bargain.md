## 10. Sable's Bargain

The order came at dawn, written in the Provost Marshal's own hand, which meant it could not be ignored and could not be forgiven.

*Inquisitor Halloway: the speaker Winterbourne is to be taken by any means necessary. His gift is confirmed. Failure to deliver him will be treated as complicity. You have three days.*

Sable read it twice, standing in her office above the Inquisition barracks while the morning bells — the wrong bells, the sweet terrible ones — rang across Corvane. Then she folded the letter into precise quarters and burned it in the candle flame, because that was what one did with orders like this: one obeyed them, and one destroyed the evidence of having been given them.

"Any means necessary," she said aloud, to the empty room.

The room did not answer. The pressed flower under the blotter said nothing either, though she was aware of it the way a person is aware of a bruise — constantly, privately, and with a certain grim tenderness.

It was a cornflower. Blue as a lie, flattened to a wafer of itself, seven years old. He had picked it off a cart of winter greens in the Gray Market because he'd claimed it was the only honest thing for sale that day, and he'd tucked it into her collar and said, *There. Now you're contraband too.* She should have burned it during the Purge with everything else. Instead she had pressed it between the pages of her mother's psalter, and then between the pages of her own case files, and now it lived beneath her blotter like a heart she kept forgetting to remove.

She was aware of the irony. She had made a career of being aware of ironies and doing nothing about them.

* * *

"You look terrible," said Junior Inquisitor Doss, sliding into the chair across from her desk without being asked. Doss was twenty-three, earnest, and shaped like a well-meaning turnip. He was also, Sable had long ago concluded, the only person in the building who liked her, which made him both her most valuable asset and her most dangerous liability.

"I slept fine," Sable said.

"You slept at your desk. There's ink on your cheek." He leaned forward, lowering his voice to the register he used for gossip rather than treason. "Is it true about Winterbourne? The charm-seller? Half the barracks say you knew him before."

"Half the barracks should spend less time talking and more time sharpening things."

"That's not a no."

"It's not anything. It's an instruction about whetstones." She pulled the day's docket toward her and began reading it with the deliberate slowness of a woman who wished to be left alone. It did not work. It never worked.

"They found another one," Doss said, quieter now. "A speaker. Down by the tanneries. Hollow-eyed, walking toward the cathedral like he was late for his own funeral. Alderman Vetch's stableboy, if you can believe it. The alderman's furious. Says if the gods want his staff, they can pay wages like everyone else."

Despite herself — despite everything — Sable laughed. It came out cracked and startled her more than the bells had.

"The alderman said that?"

"Word for word. He wants the Inquisition to bill the gods for lost labor." Doss grinned, then sobered. "Sable. Three days isn't much. If Winterbourne's half as good at hiding as people say—"

"He's better," she said, before she could stop herself.

Doss looked at her. She looked at the docket. Somewhere below them, the bells rang on, and beneath the bells, if you held very still, you could hear the other sound — the hum from under the city, the old hymn rising through the sewers and cellar-stones like water through chalk. Everyone in Corvane heard it now. Pretending otherwise had become a civic duty.

"I'll need the Fenmarch maps," she said. "And the raid rosters. And Doss—" she looked up, and put every year of her authority into it, "—when we move on the Gray Market, I lead the entry team. Personally. That's not negotiable, and it's not favoritism. I know the market. I know which stalls have back doors."

"Do they all have back doors?"

"Every single one. Charm-sellers are pessimists. It's why they're still alive."

* * *

The false lead took shape over the next two hours, and Sable built it the way she'd once built luck-knots beside Vesper at his stall — patiently, with small deceits nested inside larger ones, each knot holding because every strand believed it was doing something else.

She wrote a report. In it, she stated that reliable informants placed the fugitive Winterbourne in the river districts, seeking passage south, and recommended the Inquisition concentrate its forces on the ferry landings and the southern gates. Every word of it was a lie, and every word of it was verifiable-seeming, because she attached to it the name of an informant who did exist — a smuggler named Quill whom she had arrested twice and released twice, and who owed her exactly one large favor and would understand, when the inevitable questions came, that this was it.

Then she sat with the report for a long time.

This was the part no one warned you about, she thought. Not the betrayal itself. Betrayal was easy; she'd watched men betray their mothers for a warmer cell. It was the increments. You never fell. You stepped down, one stair at a time, each stair reasonable, each stair defensible — *I'm buying time, I'm keeping him out of worse hands, I'm managing the investigation* — until you looked back up and couldn't see the door you'd come through.

She thought about the hollow-eyed stableboy walking toward the cathedral. She thought about Merrin Oduya, the seamstress, whose file Sable had read four times now, whose eyes witnesses described as full of tide-water. She thought about what "any means necessary" would mean when they caught Vesper, and what the gods wanted speakers for, and which fate was actually the mercy.

That was the thing she could not write in any report: she wasn't protecting him from the Inquisition anymore. She was protecting him from the alternative.

She sealed the report with the Provost's black wax and sent it down with a runner she trusted, and then she unlocked her bottom drawer and took out the psalter and looked at the cornflower for a while, the way other people prayed.

"You absolute fool," she told it gently. "You're going to get us both unmade."

The flower, being seven years dead, declined to comment. It was, she reflected, the last piece of him that had ever been entirely honest with her.

* * *

At midday she walked the Gray Market herself, alone, out of uniform, which was against approximately six regulations and customary practice besides. An Inquisitor learned more in plain wool than in scarlet; fear closed mouths, but curiosity opened them.

The market was wrong. That was the first thing. She had known these stalls since she was a girl buying ribbon with her mother, and she knew their music — the haggling, the laughing, the particular rhythm of a place where everyone was slightly cheating everyone and nobody minded. Today the rhythm limped. Sellers watched the rooftops. A sparrow sat on the awning of the pie stand and did not move for the better part of ten minutes, and Sable would have sworn, would have staked her commission on it, that it was watching the crowd the way a guard watches a gate.

She stopped at the luck-knot stall because she could not help it.

It was untended. The little knots hung in rows on their pegs, red thread and green, each one tagged with a scrap of paper in a neat, careful hand: *For safe roads. For honest weather. For finding what is lost.* She touched one. The thread was warm, which was impossible in this cold, and she snatched her hand back and glanced around like a girl stealing plums.

"He's gone, then," said a voice at her elbow.

An old woman stood there, selling nothing Sable could see, wrapped in so many shawls she resembled a friendly landslide. Her eyes were sharp as awls.

"Gone where?" Sable asked, and heard how badly she asked it.

The old woman smiled with most of her teeth. "Gone where the wind goes, dear, and the wind's been strange lately. Blowing backward. My grandmother said that happens when the country remembers something it was made to forget." She leaned closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "You've got the look of someone asking for two reasons."

"Do I."

"Mm. One reason wears a uniform. The other one doesn't." She patted Sable's arm with a hand like a bundle of kindling. "Tell the second reason this: the fen road floods at moonrise, but the eel-catchers' path stays dry, and it passes close enough to the drowned chapel to hear the water singing. If your second reason has business out that way." She straightened, suddenly deaf and harmless and ancient again. "Ribbons, dear? Lovely ribbons."

Sable bought a ribbon she did not want, in a blue that hurt to look at, and walked away with her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

The old woman knew. Of course the old woman knew — half the market probably knew, the way animals know before storms. Which meant the Inquisition would know soon too, unless—

Unless the flood reports stayed buried. Unless the eel-catchers' path went unmentioned. Unless the southern gates swallowed every soldier in Corvane tonight while one dry path through the Fenmarch lay open and unguarded, like a word left deliberately out of a sentence.

One more stair down. She was beginning to lose count.

* * *

She spent the evening in the records room, ostensibly reviewing the Fenmarch surveys. Actually she spent it removing pages. The 1189 hydrographic survey went into her satchel entire — the one showing the drowned chapel, the one with the annotation in some dead surveyor's hand: *structure predates city charter; foundation stone bears no mason's mark; recommend further inquiry.* No one had ever made the further inquiry. Bureaucracy was a blessing that way.

Doss found her there near midnight, which was the worst possible timing, because her face was tired and tired faces tell truths.

"The Provost's asking about your report," he said. "The southern deployment. He wants to know your source."

"Quill. He'll confirm it."

"And will he?"

"Yes, Doss." She met his eyes and gave him the truth wearing its best uniform: "He owes me, and he knows what happens to people who don't pay."

Doss nodded slowly. He was a turnip, but he was not a stupid turnip. "Sable," he said carefully, "if this goes wrong — if the speaker slips through, and they trace it back —"

"Then it traces back to me, and only me. That's an order."

"That's not what I was going to say." He shifted, miserable. "I was going to say — whatever it is you're doing, whoever he is to you — I hope it works. People are walking into that cathedral with empty eyes, and nobody upstairs seems to care why. They care about orders." He shrugged, enormous and awkward. "Somebody ought to care about why."

He left her alone with the lamps and the missing pages and the humming under the floor, which was louder tonight, or perhaps she was simply listening harder.

She opened the psalter one last time. The cornflower lay in its crease of paper, blue as the day it was picked, blue as a boy's impossible promise. *Now you're contraband too.*

"Yes," she murmured, closing it. "I suppose I finally am."

* * *

The bells rang midnight, and then they rang wrong — off-key, urgent, all twelve towers at once, though no hand had pulled them. Sable stood at her window and watched the sky over the Fenmarch, far to the east, where a light was rising that was not dawn and not fire but something older than either: a seam of brightness splitting the dark like a crack in porcelain.

Below her window, in the barrack yard, the horses were screaming. And along the gutters, in the torchlight, she saw them moving — rats, dozens of them, hundreds, flowing east in a silent gray river, every animal in Corvane running the same direction, toward the drowned chapel, toward the singing water, as if summoned.

As if carrying something.

She grabbed her cloak, her satchel, the psalter she had no right to and no intention of leaving behind, and took the stairs three at a time.

Three days, the Provost had given her. The gods, it appeared, had given her one night.
