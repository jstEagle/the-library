## 11. The Map That Isn't There

The cartographer's name was Odo Finch, and he had been dead for eleven years.

Vesper learned this from the pigeons, who considered the whole affair a matter of civic record. *Old Finch, up on Chandler's Row, third floor, the one with the crooked weathervane. He stopped coming down in the Year of Quiet. His sister took the furniture. Nobody took the maps.*

"Nobody took the maps," Vesper repeated aloud, ducking under a sagging clothesline as the fox trotted ahead of him through the gray morning crowd. "That's not ominous at all."

*Maps are heavy with looking,* said the fox, whose name — if it was a name — was Ash, and who spoke Common the way old priests spoke it, all rolled vowels and forgotten grammar. *And this one does not like to be looked at.*

Wren walked between them, small hand hooked in the fox's ruff because she had decided somewhere in the last two days of flight that this was simply how one traveled now. She was seven years old, she had been pulled out of an Inquisitor raid by a stranger who talked to rats, and she had adapted to her new life with the terrifying flexibility of children and cats. She had asked, exactly once, whether they were going to die, received the answer "not today," and found it sufficient.

"What does that mean, doesn't like to be looked at?" she asked now.

"It means," Vesper said, "that we're about to climb three flights of stairs to look at something that will stop existing the moment we do."

"That's silly."

"Yes," Vesper agreed. "Most true things are."

The stairs to Finch's attic were narrow and smelled of dust and pigeon and, faintly, of ink that had gone to sleep decades ago. The door was locked, which mattered to no one present; the fox sat before it and made a sound that was less a bark than a word worn smooth, and the lock clicked open with the sheepish air of a mechanism remembering an older master.

Inside, the attic was a country of paper.

Maps hung from every rafter like laundry — coastlines, trade routes, the great spiral of the Corvane aqueducts, districts Vesper had never heard of and suspected did not exist, or existed only because someone had drawn them first. Dust lay over everything in a soft gray pelt. In the corner stood a desk with a chair pushed back at the angle of a man who meant to return in a moment, and on the desk, a lamp, and beside the lamp, a cold cup of tea gone to stone.

Vesper felt the particular ache of interrupted lives. Eleven years. The man had probably just stepped out for more milk.

"He died," Wren said, reading his face with the blunt accuracy of children. "Didn't he? A long time ago."

"A long time ago," Vester confirmed, and then caught himself: "Yes. He did."

The fox crossed the attic floor without disturbing the dust — Vesper had stopped questioning this around day two — and stopped beneath the largest map of all, a grand survey of Corvane and its provinces that dominated the far wall, all gilt edges and confident borders. The nation spread across it like a sleeping animal: Corvane proper, the Fenmarch lowlands to the south, the Grayreach hills, the sea beyond. Every border drawn twice, in ink and then in gold, as if the cartographer hadn't quite trusted the first line to hold.

"This is it?" Vesper asked. "This is just... a map of the country."

*Look away,* said Ash.

"I beg your pardon?"

*All of you. Look away. Then look back — but not directly. Look at it the way you look at a thing you are trying not to spook.*

Vesper looked at Wren. Wren looked at Vesper. The fox looked at both of them with the patient contempt available only to animals and schoolteachers.

So they turned around. Vesper studied the rafters. Wren studied her shoes. Somewhere below them, a cart rattled past on Chandler's Row, and a vendor began shouting about eels, eels, finest eels, and the ordinary noise of the city rose up through the floorboards like water through sand.

"Now," said the fox.

They turned back — obliquely, as instructed, eyes angled like people pretending not to stare at a fight.

The map had changed.

Where the nation of Corvane had sprawled in gilt certainty, there was now — underneath, or behind, or perhaps simply *elsewhere* — another shape entirely. Coastlines that matched nothing Vesper knew. Rivers running backward. Mountains named in a script that hurt slightly to almost-see, letters that resolved only in the periphery and dissolved when pursued. And across the whole of it, written in a hand both careful and grieving, a single word:

*VELLICHOR.*

"Oh," said Wren.

"Oh," Vesper agreed, because there was nothing else. His heart was doing something complicated against his ribs. He had spent seven years selling luck-knots and telling himself the stories about magic were stories, and here was a country that had been erased so thoroughly it could only exist when nobody believed in it hard enough to look.

"It's beautiful," Wren whispered.

It was. That was the terrible part. The erased land glowed at the edge of vision like a lantern seen through fog — green where Corvane was gray, gold where Corvane was brown, its rivers bright as spilled light. Magic remembered as beautiful. Vesper hadn't let himself think of it that way since he was young enough to talk to sparrows without checking the windows first.

*Finch mapped it from memory,* the fox said quietly. *He was Vellichori. One of the last. When the gods went silent and the borders closed, the country did not vanish — it was folded. Tucked inside what you call Corvane, the way a letter is tucked inside an envelope. And the maps were burned, every one, except the ones that could hide themselves.*

"Why hide?"

*Because a thing that is remembered can be found. And a thing that can be found can be opened.* The fox's tail moved once, slowly. *Or closed.*

* * *

They tried, over the next hour, to actually read the thing, and this was where the map earned its reputation for difficultness.

Vesper would approach with a candle, squint at the region around the Fenmarch, and find only Corvane there — honest, dull, taxable Corvane, its lowlands marked with drainage ditches and eel-farms. He would step back, glance sideways, catch the flash of the other country, lunge forward — and it would be gone again, smug as a fish.

"It's mocking me," he said.

*It's shy,* said the fox. *There is a difference. Barely.*

"You said it shows itself when no one looks directly. How is anyone supposed to use a map like that? You'd need to navigate an entire country out of the corner of your eye."

*I never said it would be easy. I said it would be possible. Those are different promises.*

Wren, meanwhile, had given up on looking altogether. She had climbed onto Finch's dead chair, curled up with her chin on her arms, and was simply gazing at the wall with the unfocused patience of a child waiting out an adult conversation. Her eyes had gone half-lidded. Her breathing slowed.

She was listening. Vesper realized it with a small cold drop in his stomach. Listening the way he listened — the way he had listened since before he knew it was a gift, before he knew it was dangerous — except that Wren had never been taught to be small about it. No one had ever told her to stop.

"Wren," he said carefully. "What do you hear?"

"The map's talking," she said, without opening her eyes. "Not words. More like... humming. Like when you hum with your mouth shut." Her brow furrowed. "It's sad. It wants to be looked at so bad and it can't stand being looked at. Both at once." She tilted her head. "There's a hole in it."

The attic went very still. Even the dust seemed to pause mid-settling.

*A hole,* the fox repeated.

"A place where the humming goes down instead of around. Like a drain." Wren opened her eyes and pointed — not at the map, but slightly left of it, into the space where the hidden country lived. "There. Under the wet church. The one that's mostly underwater. The humming goes down through the floor and keeps going."

Vesper followed her finger. Of course he saw nothing — Corvane's Fenmarch, drainage ditches, eels — but he felt it anyway, or imagined he felt it, a faint pull behind his eyes like the moment before a sneeze.

"The drowned chapel," he said slowly. "Saint Aldhelm's. It flooded when they rerouted the Fenmarch canals — forty years ago, maybe more. It's a ruin. Fisherman's landmark. Children dare each other to swim in the nave." He turned to the fox. "You already knew. Didn't you. That's why we came south."

*The Door of Unmaking opens where the country was folded thinnest,* Ash said. *A drowned church is a good fold. Water remembers better than stone.* The fox sat, wrapping its tail over its paws, and for a moment looked older than any animal should. *But knowing where a door is and opening it are separated by a distance I cannot measure for you. Doors like this one ask questions.*

"What kind of questions?"

*Price questions.* The fox's amber eyes held his. *We will speak of it when we must. Not before.*

Vesper knew a deferral when he heard one. He had sold enough charms to know that everything worth buying came with terms read quickly at the end. But Wren was yawning, boneless with exhaustion, and the light through the attic window had gone honey-colored, and there would be time — a few hours, maybe — before the city finished closing its fist.

* * *

They made camp in the attic, because it was the last place in Corvane anyone would look for fugitives: above a dead man's life's work, in a room that officially contained nothing. Vesper found biscuits in a tin that had outlasted its owner and pretended not to do the arithmetic on how long ago that tin had been sealed. Wren ate hers with the solemnity of a duchess.

Later, while the fox dozed on the windowsill — gods apparently napped; this was somehow the least surprising thing Vesper had learned all week — Wren crept over to sit beside him against the desk.

"Mister Vesper."

"Just Vesper."

"Vesper." She considered the map, which was currently being only a map, gilt and dull and safe. "Is it true the gods are waking up? The fox says it, and the rats said it, and the lady with the water eyes said it."

Merrin. Vesper's chest tightened at the memory — Merrin's eyes full of tide, Merrin's mouth full of someone else's prophecy, Merrin naming his gift aloud in a voice like a bell rung underwater. *Speaker,* she had called him. *The last one. The one who sings.*

"They're waking," he said. "I think that's true."

"And they take people. People who talk to animals. Like us."

"Yes."

Wren nodded, absorbing this the way she absorbed everything: completely, and without visible panic. Then she said, "My gran talked to bees. Before the Quiet. She said the bees used to sing back, proper songs, and after the Quiet they only ever said work-work-work, and she cried about it sometimes." She picked at a biscuit crumb. "Do you think if the gods go back to sleep, the bees will sing again?"

Vesper opened his mouth. Closed it. He thought about the hymn the rats had recited in a language dead for seven years. He thought about the summons he had felt in his own bones, patient as a hook. He thought about a door under dark water that asked price questions.

"I think," he said finally, "that songs don't really die. They just wait for somebody to remember them."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Gran would've liked you," she decided, and fell asleep against his arm, and Vesper sat very still in a dead cartographer's attic holding a sleeping child and a country's secret, and let himself, for exactly one minute, feel wonder instead of fear.

The map, in the corner of his eye, glowed softly — as if it had noticed, and approved.

* * *

Dusk came down over Chandler's Row like a lid.

Vesper woke from a shallow doze to find the fox standing rigid on the windowsill, ears forward, hackles a dark ridge along its spine. Below in the street, torchlight moved in formation. Not the loose scatter of a night watch — the ordered geometry of an Inquisitor sweep, moving door to door along the row.

*They have the child's description,* the fox said. *And yours. And they are asking after a red fox, which flatters me and inconveniences us equally.*

Vesper gathered Wren without waking her, slinging her onto his back where she clung like a burr. On the wall, the great map waited. He gave it one last sideways glance — the hidden country flashing green and gold, the drowned chapel's secret humming in the place where the humming went down.

One question, though, before they ran. He had earned one.

"Ash. The Door. Whatever it asks — whatever the price is—" He swallowed. "Would it be something I'd miss?"

The fox looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in its amber eyes that Vesper would spend a long time, later, trying to name. Pity, maybe. Or apology.

*Everything worth giving,* it said, *is something you'd miss. Come. We reach the Fenmarch by midnight — and the tide won't wait, and neither, anymore, will the gods.*

Below, the first fist hammered on the cartographer's door.

Vesper went out the window.
