## 12. Midnight at the Drowned Chapel

The Fenmarch did not want visitors.

Vesper had known this within the first hour of leaving the cartographer's attic, when the reeds along the old towpath had begun whispering to each other in that rustling way reeds had — *strangers, strangers, tell the water* — and the water had answered with a long, unimpressed sigh. By the third hour, when Wren had slipped hip-deep into a bog hole and come up wearing a crown of pondweed and an expression of profound betrayal, Vesper had stopped pretending this was a journey and admitted it was a negotiation.

"The marsh says we're rude," he translated.

"We're *fleeing for our lives*," Wren said, wringing out her sleeve. "Rudeness is a luxury."

"It says fleeing for your lives is no excuse for trampling its orchids."

The fox trotted ahead on the raised causeway stones, tail high, entirely unbothered by mud, as though the Fenmarch were a country it had personally commissioned. Which, Vesper was beginning to suspect, it might have been.

"Does it ever get tired?" Wren asked, nodding at the fox. "Walking like that? Like it's being painted?"

"Gods don't tire," Vesper said, then caught himself. "I mean — I don't know what it does. It hasn't told us what it is. Only what it remembers."

"That's worse," Wren said seriously. "My gran always said the ones who remember everything are the ones you should worry about. The forgetful ones forgive you."

The fox's ears flicked backward toward them but it said nothing, which was itself a kind of answer, and Vesper filed it away in the growing drawer of things he would think about later, when later existed.

They walked until the moon stood straight overhead and the causeway gave out, and there — exactly where the map-that-wasn't-there had promised, which is to say nowhere and slightly to the left of it — they found the chapel.

It had drowned a hundred years ago and never gotten over it. Its bell tower leaned out of the black water at a drunken angle, and the roofline broke the surface in green-slicked ribs, like the back of some enormous patient animal. Windows glimmered below the waterline, glass gone opaline with age, and through them the moonlight fell in slanted columns into the flooded nave, so that from the bank it looked less like a building than like a lantern someone had sunk and never retrieved.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, it's *sad*."

"Chapels usually are," Vesper said. "It's part of the design."

"Not sad like people. Sad like a dog left at a market stall." She hugged her elbows. "It's been waiting for somebody to come back."

Vesper felt that one under his ribs. He crouched at the water's edge and listened the way he'd learned to listen — not with his ears, which were ordinary enough, but with the attention underneath them. And the chapel *did* speak, in its slow stone way: not words, but a kind of held breath. A congregation of eels going about their business in the pews. Carp drifting through the transept like latecomers to a service that had ended a century ago. And beneath all of it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well—

*singing.*

Not the hymn. Not the waking gods' summons that had hollowed out Merrin and two others whose names Vesper refused to learn because learning names made it harder to run. This was older. Smaller. A seam of sound rather than a shout.

"It's here," he said. "Under the water. The Door."

The fox sat down at the edge of the bank, wrapped its tail over its feet, and looked at him with those amber eyes that were far too old for the face around them.

"Then go and knock," it said.

* * *

Getting in was the easy part, if you didn't mind drowning a little.

The chapel's doors had rotted to lace, and behind them the water stood chest-high in the nave, cold enough to make Vesper's teeth file a formal complaint. Wren swam; she was small and quick and treated the whole enterprise as a grand adventure, which Vesper found either admirable or alarming depending on how much water he'd recently inhaled. The fox walked across the surface.

"You could have mentioned that was possible," Vesper said, hauling himself onto a submerged pew.

"You didn't ask," said the fox.

"I'm asking now."

"Too late. The moment has passed." It picked its way along the pew-backs, dry as an opinion. "Besides, miracles are wasted on the unwilling. You'd only have spent the rest of the night wondering what it cost."

"Everything costs," Vesper muttered. "That's the whole problem with everything."

Moonlight came down through the broken roof in pale columns, and where the light struck the water, the flooded nave turned to hammered silver. They waded down the center aisle — the aisle, Vesper realized, where mourners would once have walked, where brides, where children carried too soon — and the singing grew. Not louder. Closer. The way a memory gets closer when you stop trying to avoid it.

And then they reached the altar, and Vesper saw the Door.

It was not a door. That was the first thing. It was a *seam* — a line of light standing in the water where no light had any business standing, thin as a knife's edge, tall as a man, and utterly still. The water around it bent, the way air bends above a candle flame, and the moonlight that touched it did not pass through but gathered, pooling against it like rain against glass. Through the seam, if he tilted his head, Vesper could see — not another place, exactly. A suggestion of one. Green hills under a sky the color of a word he'd almost remembered. A country with no name on any map, because the maps had been burned, and the country with them.

Vellichor.

Wren surfaced beside him, hair plastered to her forehead, eyes enormous. "Is that—"

"Yes."

"It's *beautiful*."

It was. That was the terrible thing. It was beautiful the way the first day of spring is beautiful, the way a hand reaching down in the dark is beautiful, and Vesper stood chest-deep in a dead chapel looking at salvation and felt, beneath the wonder, a cold little voice that sounded remarkably like seven years of survival saying: *nothing free has ever been offered to you before. Ask the price.*

He asked the price.

The seam did not answer in words. It answered the way the chapel answered — in pressure, in presence, in a slow turning of attention like a lighthouse beam coming around. And then, gently, with the terrible courtesy of very old things, it spoke. Not aloud. Into the water, into the stone, into the marrow of anyone listening:

*A door opens outward only. What stands in a doorway must be of one side or the other. Bring nothing that holds you here, and you may pass. Bring what anchors you, and the anchor holds.*

Vesper waited for more. Fine print. A list. Anything.

"That's it?" he said finally. "Give up what anchors me? What does that — I mean, specifically. Is there a form? A ceremony? Do I burn something? Because I've got a whole satchel of charms and honestly most of them are decorative—"

"Vesper." The fox's voice was quiet. "Stop talking."

He stopped talking. The silence filled back in around the seam of light, and in that silence he understood, the way you understand a debt when the collector arrives — not the amount, but the fact of it.

What anchored him to Corvane.

His stall in the Gray Market, with its crooked awning and its luck-knots that worked maybe one time in ten, which he'd always considered honest advertising. His room above the tallow-chandler's with the window that stuck and the view of nothing. The sparrows who brought him gossip. The rats who'd brought him the hymn. Seven years of practiced smallness, of being nobody so thoroughly that nobody looked twice — every charm sold, every morning survived, every careful, cowardly, hard-won inch of the life he'd built inside the trap.

And underneath all of it, holding it together like a keel—

Sable.

Sable in the crowd with her inquisitor's gray coat and her eyes that used to laugh and now only measured. Sable saying *step aside, citizen* in a voice that had once said *stay* in the dark. Sable, whom he had lied to beautifully by candlelight, and who had lied beautifully back, and whom he still measured his days against like a man checking a wound to see if it had healed. It hadn't. He'd simply gotten good at not pressing on it.

If he stepped through the Door, he left her on the other side of it. Forever. In a country whose borders were closing like a fist.

"There's no version," he said slowly, "where I keep it. Is there. No version where I walk through carrying — everything."

The seam's attention rested on him, patient as tide.

"No," said the fox.

"How do you know? Have you been through?"

A pause. The smallest pause. If Vesper hadn't spent seven years reading animals the way other men read ledgers, he'd have missed it entirely.

"I know what doors ask," the fox said. "It is my business to know."

"Well, your business is grim." Vesper laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, the laugh of a man standing on a ledge deciding whether to look down. He pressed his palm flat against the water, feeling the seam's warmth radiating through it like a hearth through a wall. "It's right *there*. Do you understand? Seven years I've been small and quiet and careful, and it's right *there*, and all I have to do is give up the only parts of my life that were ever actually mine."

"Those aren't the parts that anchor you," Wren said.

He looked at her. She was treading water beside a drowned pew, moonlit, matter-of-fact, eleven years old and wise in the way children are wise — accidentally, devastatingly.

"The charms don't anchor you," she said. "You could sell charms anywhere. Even somewhere else. It's—" She hesitated, and for once the fearless child looked afraid. "It's people, isn't it. Anchors are always people. My gran was mine. That's why I ran when they took her. Nothing held me down after that."

Vesper opened his mouth to say something comforting and found nothing there. Because she was right, and they both knew it, and the seam of light knew it, and the whole drowned chapel seemed to lean in to listen.

"I can't," he said.

"You haven't tried."

"I don't need to try. I know what it'll ask for." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the crack, hated the honesty, hated the moonlight making everything visible. "There's a woman. There's — we were — before the Purge, we were—" He gestured helplessly at the water, at the light, at the entire impossible situation. "She hunts people like me now. Professionally. And I still — when I see her in a crowd, everything in me goes *there*, like a compass needle, like it doesn't care that the needle's pointing at a knife. That's an anchor. That's the anchor. And I am not giving that up. I'm not giving up the one thing in this whole rotten country that was ever good, just to — to be *free* somewhere else without it. What kind of freedom is that? That's just a bigger cage with nicer weather."

The chapel was silent. The carp hung in the moonbeams like ornaments. Somewhere in the tower, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"Good," said the fox.

Vesper turned. "*Good?*"

"You flinched," the fox said. "You stood in front of the way out and you flinched, and you named the reason, and the reason was love. Do you know how many come to a door like this ready to abandon everything, relieved to be rid of their anchors, and the door takes them anyway — or worse, lets them through, light as paper, because there was nothing in them worth weighing?" Its tail curled around its feet. "A man who won't pay that price is a man worth following. Even if he's a fool. *Especially* if he's a fool."

"I'm glad my cowardice meets with your approval."

"It's not cowardice. Cowardice runs *from* something. You're refusing to run *to* something. Different animal entirely." The fox rose, shook the nonexistent water off its coat, and looked at the seam with an expression Vesper couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition, or the particular weariness of someone who has stood before this exact door many times and knows precisely what it asks because it has paid it, or refused to, or both. "But understand this, Vesper Winterbourne. The Door will wait. Doors like this always wait. The gods who are waking will not."

As if summoned — and Vesper's stomach dropped with the sudden certainty that it had been — the water changed.

It began far off, out in the Fenmarch: a shudder through the reeds, a rising murmur among the eels, the carp scattering from the moonbeams like startled thoughts. Then the hymn. Not the faint seam-song of the Door, but the *other* one, the waking one, rolling across the marsh in waves, and this time it wasn't distant. This time it had direction. This time it was coming *toward* them, and threaded through it, thin and clear and horribly familiar, a human voice — a woman's voice, reciting, chanting, *prophesying*—

"*the singer comes to the water, the singer comes to the door, the last song will be sung by the one who hides his face—*"

"Merrin," Vesper whispered.

"She walks," the fox said, ears flat, all its ancient calm gone tight. "They let the vessels walk, now. They're using her eyes." It spun toward Wren. "Out. Now. The way we came."

"But the Door—"

"The Door waits. *We* cannot." The fox leapt from pew to pew toward the ruined entrance, swift and suddenly urgent. "Vesper! *Move!*"

One last time Vesper looked at the seam of light — the green hills, the wordless sky, the whole erased country standing open like a held breath, waiting for him with the patience of stone. So close. One confession away.

*I'll come back,* he promised it, knowing even as he thought it that promises made while running are the cheapest currency there is.

Then he turned and plunged into the cold water after the fox, and behind him, out in the dark marsh, Merrin's voice sang closer, and closer, and closer.
