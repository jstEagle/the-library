## 13. The God Who Speaks Fox

I have worn this body for seven years.

Seven years of fleas. Seven years of eating things raw that I once would have had roasted with rosemary and served on silver. Seven years of being scratched behind the ears by children who should have been kneeling.

Do not pity me. Pity is for creatures who did not choose their circumstances. I chose mine the way a drowning man chooses the rock he clings to — gratefully, and with full knowledge that it will scrape his hands bloody.

My name — my true name, the one that used to make rivers change course and orchards bloom out of season — was Vell. Yes. The same as the country. We were not named after the land; the land was named after me. It is a long story involving a very flattering farmer and an even more flattering harvest, and I will tell it someday if we both live, which is increasingly uncertain.

For now, I am a fox. Red as rust, small as regret, and currently crouched in the rafters of a tannery in Corvane's undercity, watching Vesper Winterbourne sleep badly on a pallet of sacking while a child called Wren snores against his side like a small, damp bellows.

He sleeps the way he does everything: carefully, apologetically, one ear open for trouble. Even unconscious, the man is vigilant. It is exhausting to watch. It is also, I confess, rather endearing, and I say that as someone who has watched emperors sleep and found them merely damp.

* * *

Let me tell you what actually happened seven years ago, because everyone else's version is wrong, including the maps', including the Inquisition's, including — most grievously — Vesper's.

The gods did not fall silent.

We went to sleep.

There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Silence implies death, or absence, or at minimum a failure of nerve. Sleep implies choice. And choice implies that someone, somewhere, might wake up.

We were tired. You must understand how tired. Imagine hearing every prayer ever prayed in a country for a thousand years — every plea for rain, every petition for a son to come home from a war you personally ended three centuries ago, every desperate bargain struck over a sick goat. Imagine answering all of them, forever, because you can, and because no one taught you how to stop. We were not worshipped so much as *used*, the way a well is used, and no one thanks a well either.

So when the last true believer died — a beekeeper in the eastern fens, a woman named Odile who talked to her hives the way Vesper talks to sparrows — we saw our chance. One by one, we folded ourselves down into the deep places of the world. Into roots and riverbeds and the slow mineral dreams of mountains. We let our names be forgotten. We let the maps be redrawn. When the Purge came, when the Inquisitors burned the speakers and outlawed the old words, we did not resist, because resistance would have meant waking, and waking meant listening again, and we had finally, *finally* learned to close our ears.

All of us but one.

I could not sleep. That is the humiliating truth of it. My siblings sank into the earth like coins into a well, and I stood at the edge of that darkness and found that I was afraid — not of sleeping, but of what I might dream. I had spent too long inside other people's heads. Every prayer I had ever answered was still in there, layered like sediment. If I slept, I would dream them all at once. A thousand years of other people's desperation, playing behind my eyes until the stars burned out.

So I stayed awake. And being awake in a country that was erasing itself around me, I did the only sensible thing.

I got small.

A god cannot hide as a god; divinity has a smell, and Inquisitors are trained to it like hounds. But a fox? A fox is beneath notice. A fox is vermin, scenery, a flash of red at the edge of a field. I poured myself into this body the way you would pour a lake into a thimble, and I lost most of myself in the process — my height, my radiance, my ability to be in two places at once, my singing voice (oh, my singing voice, you have no idea) — and kept only what mattered: memory, cunning, and just enough of the old tongue to speak to the one creature in Vellichor who could hear me.

Which brings us, inevitably, to the man snoring on the sack.

* * *

Vesper Winterbourne was not supposed to matter.

When I first marked him — three years ago, in the Gray Market, selling luck-knots to gullible clerks — he was simply a speaker, and there were others then. A woman who read omens in pigeon flights. An old man who could quiet dogs with a word. Speakers were useful to me the way candles are useful: sources of warmth, briefly lit, easily extinguished. I listened through them. I nudged them. I kept a low fire of the old knowledge burning in the only way left to me.

But Vesper was different, and it took me a shameful length of time to see why.

It was not his gift, though his gift is considerable. Beasts adore him, and beasts are honest judges of character in a way humans have never managed. No — what made him different was that he *refused*. Every other speaker I had ever known wanted something. Power, purpose, an answer, an audience. Vesper wanted none of it. He heard the summons of the divine humming under the skin of the world, and he pulled his collar up and walked the other way, whistling.

Do you know what that is like, for a god? To meet a mortal who looks directly at you and asks you to keep your voice down?

I followed him for a year before I spoke to him. I told myself it was strategy. I told myself many things that year. Mostly I told myself them at two in the morning, sitting on his windowsill, watching him mend charm-strings by candlelight and murmur apologies to the moths he caught and released instead of killed. Apologies. To moths. I had once parted a sea — all right, a very large marsh, but the principle stands — and this man apologized to moths.

When I finally spoke, he dropped a spool of thread, knocked over the candle, and said, "Either I've finally gone mad or you're about to ruin my life."

Both, as it turns out. He has always been quick.

* * *

Here is where I must be honest, and honesty, I find, sits in a god's throat like a fishbone.

I lied to him.

Not about everything. About Vellichor, I told the truth — the erased country, the borders that closed like a hand closing, the Door of Unmaking that waits beneath the drowned chapel in the Fenmarch. All true. About the gods waking, about the summons, about the hollow-eyed speakers walking toward the cathedral — true, all of it, every word chosen with care but none of it false.

What I did not tell him was why I needed him to reach the Door.

He believes — believed, perhaps still believes, though the look he gave me tonight suggests the belief is dying — that I want out. That I am a trapped thing seeking escape, and that he, with his gift and his stubbornness and his infuriating decency, is my key. It is a tidy story. Tidy stories are the best lies; they require so little maintenance.

The truth is that I do not want out.

The truth is that I want my family back to sleep.

They are waking, one by one, and they are waking *wrong*. Seven years of silence have curdled in them. They surface into a country that forgot them, and they are hungry — not for worship, exactly, though worship will do in a pinch, but for *voice*. For speakers. For mouths to pour themselves through. Merrin Oakes, the laundress's neighbor, the girl with the tide-water eyes — she was a person four days ago. Now she is a glove, and something vast is wearing her, and she smiles with a smile that belongs to a being older than the river, and every hour she wears it, less of Merrin floats back up.

That is what waking means now. Not return. Occupation.

And I am the only one of us who stayed conscious through the long dark, which means I am the only one who remembers how the sleep-song goes. The hymn we sang each other down into the earth, verse by verse, the lullaby of gods. I remember it. But I cannot sing it.

This body has no voice for it. A fox's throat was not built for hymns that fold mountains into bed. I have tried — oh, how I have tried, alone on rooftops at midnight, producing sounds that alarmed several cats and achieved nothing else. The song requires a speaker's throat. A human throat, tuned by the gift, carrying a god's melody.

It requires Vesper.

* * *

He woke an hour past midnight, the way he always does — all at once, no drowsy middle ground, as if sleep were a debt he resented paying. Wren stirred against his side. He eased the boy flat again with the practiced gentleness of a man who has moved sleeping animals off his bed for years, and then he looked up into the rafters, directly at me, because of course he knew I was there. He always knows. It is deeply unnerving.

"You're staring," he whispered.

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're staring *at* me. There's a difference. Sparrows do it too. It's very rude in every language."

I dropped from the rafters — I am graceful even in a borrowed body, thank you — and landed on the pallet's foot. Up close, in the tallow-light, he looked terrible. Hollowed. Three days of flight will do that to a man built for hiding in plain sight. There was flour still in his hair from the miller's loft, two nights ago, and a bruise going yellow along his jaw from the Inquisitor raid, and his hands, wrapped around his knees, had the tremor of a man who has run out of ways not to think about things.

"Vesper," I said. "We need to talk about the Door."

"We've talked about the Door."

"We've talked about *reaching* the Door. We haven't talked about what happens when we get there." I sat back on my haunches. It is a posture that makes humans trust me; I have never understood why, but I exploit it shamelessly. "And I find I can't carry the next part anymore. So I'm going to tell you the truth, and you're going to be angry, and then — if you're still speaking to me — we're going to save this country. Or end it. Possibly both. The paperwork is unclear."

His eyes narrowed. In the candlelight they were the exact gray-green of the fen-water where the chapel drowns, which I have decided is coincidence, because the alternative keeps me up at night, and I have done quite enough of that for one eternity.

"The truth," he repeated flatly. "From you. Willingly. Should I sit down?"

"You're already sitting down."

"Then I'll lie down. This feels like a lying-down sort of truth."

"*Vesper.*"

"All right." He didn't laugh, quite, but something in his face loosened by a degree, the way it does when humor slips under a door that fear has barred. "Go on, then. Fox. Truth."

So I told him.

I told him my name was Vell, and I watched him go still in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. I told him about the sleep, the choosing of it, the thousand-year weariness that made it mercy and not abandonment. I told him about the fear that kept me awake — and here my voice did something embarrassing, cracked in a register no fox should own, because it has been seven years since I said any of this aloud and loneliness is a pressure like deep water; hold it long enough and it finds every seam.

I told him about the waking. About Merrin. About what waited in the cathedral, wearing his neighbors like festival costumes. And then I told him the part I had buried deepest:

"I don't need you to open the Door so I can leave. I need you to stand at the Door and *sing*. The counter-hymn. The sleep-song. The one that will fold my brothers and sisters back down into the dark before they finish hollowing every speaker in Vellichor." I made myself hold his gaze, which is difficult; guilt weighs more than a fox. "I brought you here under false pretenses. I let you believe you were my escape. You were never my escape, Vesper. You are my lullaby."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the tallow to gutter twice.

Wren, mercifully, slept through it. Children can sleep through anything; it is their superpower, and I envy it more than I have ever envied flight or foresight or the turning of seasons.

"You used me," Vesper said at last. Not shouted. That was worse, somehow. His anger, when it comes, comes quiet, like frost.

"Yes."

"You let me run. You let me save Wren, you let me nearly die in the mill fire, you let Sable—" he stopped on the name, the way he always stops on it, a stone in the shoe of his sentence, "—you let all of it happen, on a lie."

"On an *incomplete* truth," I said, and immediately wished I had not, because it is precisely the sort of thing a god would say, and he flinched as if I'd shown him my real shape for a moment — all vastness and no apology.

"Is that what divinity teaches you? Bookkeeping?"

"Divinity taught me nothing. Divinity is what I'm trying to *unlearn*." I stepped closer, across the sacking, close enough that he could see — really see — how little of me was left, how much I had poured out of myself to fit this shape. "You think I don't know what I did? I picked the one man in Corvane who built his whole life on refusing exactly this, and I asked him for the largest favor ever asked of anyone. I dressed it up small because I knew if I showed you its true size you'd run, and I would have deserved it, and the speakers would have emptied out one by one until there was nothing left of anyone." My tail curled tight around my feet. Old habit. It is what this body does when it has nothing left to hide behind. "I am not sorry I lied. I am sorry you're the one who has to pay for it. Those are different sorrows, and I carry both, and neither one fits."

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, the city murmured — cart wheels, a drunk singing, and underneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the cathedral's hum, growing nightly. Somewhere in that hum, Merrin's mouth was reciting prophecies about a door and a last song, and every word was true, and every word was a countdown.

"If I sing your song," he said slowly, "what happens to me?"

Ah. There it was. The question underneath the question, the one he had been circling since the chapel, since the Door showed him its price and he flinched from naming what anchored him. I could lie again. I could soften it. I have softened truths for a thousand years; it is practically my trade.

"A song that puts gods to sleep," I said, "is sung by giving something up. That is the price of the Door, and the price of the verse, and I will not pretend otherwise. What you give, and how much, and whether anything of you remains on the far side of the last note — that, I genuinely do not know. I have never sung it from your side of it." I let my ears fall, which in this body is as close as I come to bowing. "I can promise you this much: I will stand beside you for every note. And whatever is left when the song ends — man, memory, or echo — I will not abandon it. I have been the only one awake in a sleeping country for seven years, Vesper Winterbourne. I would rather be unmade than do it forever."

He was quiet a long moment. Then he reached out and scratched me behind the ears, which is a liberty, and presumptuous, and familiar beyond all bounds between a mortal and a god.

It was also the kindest thing anyone had done to me in seven years, and I leaned into it before I could stop myself.

"Liar," he said softly. "Traitor. Manipulative flea-bitten deity."

"Yes."

"Fine." He looked toward the window, toward the east, toward the drowned chapel waiting in the Fenmarch like a held breath. "Teach me the song."

I opened my mouth to begin —

— and every dog in Corvane started howling at once.
