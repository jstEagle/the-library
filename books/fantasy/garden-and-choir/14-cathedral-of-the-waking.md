## 14. Cathedral of the Waking

The bells of Corvane had not rung in seven years. They had been melted down, by decree, along with every other instrument that might carry a tune too far — a law Sable Halloway had always found faintly absurd, as though music were a contagion and silence a cure. And yet here she stood at dawn on the cathedral steps, listening to the great cracked bell above her swing all by itself, tolling a note that no rope pulled and no hand guided, and thinking: *of course. Of course it was never about the bells.*

She had been summoned before sunrise by a courier who would not meet her eyes. The summons had said only: *Come. Bring your records. Come quickly.* The records — seven years of interrogations, confiscations, burnings — sat under her arm now like a dead thing she was being asked to present at its own funeral.

Below the steps, the plaza filled.

It filled the way water fills a bowl: from every side at once, without hurry, without any single direction you could point to afterward. Bakers still in their aprons. Clerks with their ledgers tucked under their arms like shields. A fishwife who had once spat at Sable's boots during a purge now stood with her hands folded, her face tilted upward, listening to something Sable could not hear and was grateful she could not hear.

And they were all facing the same way. That was the part that made her spine go cold. Not toward the cathedral doors — toward the *sky*, or rather toward some point past the sky, as if the whole city had become one enormous eye trying to focus on something just out of frame.

"Speaker," said a voice behind her, and Sable turned to find Lord Chancellor Aldous Vane descending the steps toward her, his chain of office catching the early light. He looked, she thought, like a man who had dressed for a coronation and arrived at an execution, or possibly the reverse; with Vane it was often difficult to tell which he considered worse.

"Lord Chancellor." She bowed. "You sent for me."

"I did." He stopped two steps above her, which put them nearly eye to eye, a piece of architecture he had chosen deliberately and she pretended not to notice. "Inquisitor Halloway. How many speakers did we take last year?"

"Eleven. All processed according to statute."

"And the year before?"

"Nineteen."

"And in all those years, in all those files—" he gestured at the dead weight under her arm— "did you ever once record anything like *this*?"

Sable followed his gesture. Above the cathedral's broken spire, the morning air had begun to move. Not wind — she knew wind, wind had manners — but a slow spiraling brightness, like heat-shimmer given ambition, gathering itself into shapes that almost resolved and then, mercifully, didn't. She heard someone in the crowd below gasp and then hush their neighbor, and the hush spread outward like a ripple until ten thousand people stood in perfect silence, and the only sound in all of Corvane was that self-swinging bell.

"No, my lord," she said. "Nothing like this."

"Good," said Vane, and there it was — the thing she had come to dread more than the gods themselves. Not fear in his voice. *Relief.*

"Good?" she repeated.

"The Church of Silence has governed this city for seven years on the promise that silence is safety." He smoothed the front of his coat, a man preparing a speech he'd been composing since before dawn. "But promises age, Inquisitor. Fear ages faster. If these... presences can be *addressed*. Negotiated with. Formalized." His eyes had gone bright and distant, the way men's eyes went when they counted. "Then perhaps what we outlawed was never evil. Perhaps it was merely *ungoverned*."

Sable looked down at the crowd — at the fishwife, at the clerks, at a child on her father's shoulders reaching up toward the shimmer with the unselfconscious delight of the very young — and felt the ground tilt beneath a conviction she had built her whole life on.

They hadn't been wrong about magic, she realized. They had simply been waiting for it to apply for a license.

* * *

Three streets away, wedged into the doorway of a shuttered chandlery with a fox pressed against his ribs and a child pressed against his other side, Vesper Winterbourne watched the same light over the cathedral and understood it completely differently.

"It's pulling," Wren whispered. The girl's small hand had found his sleeve without either of them deciding it should. "I can feel it. Like when you're asleep and someone says your name and you come up out of the dream before you're ready."

"I know," Vesper said. Because he did know. It had started an hour ago as a tickle behind his teeth, the sensation he got when a beast wanted to tell him something urgent — but this came from no sparrow, no rat, no pigeon. This came from underneath everything, from the floor of the world, and it was saying his name in a voice made of every voice at once.

Around them, the gray streets were emptying. And not only of people. Sparrows poured off the rooftops in rivers. Cats sat in doorways staring east with their pupils blown wide. In the gutter, a line of rats marched in perfect formation, tails lifted like candles, and Vesper caught fragments of their chatter as they passed — not words anymore, not even hymn-words, just a single sustained note of *yes, yes, coming, yes* — and every hair on his arms stood up.

"They're all going," he murmured. "Every speaker in the city. Every animal that ever listened."

"Not every animal." The fox's tail curled around his ankle, warm and deliberate. "I am not going. I have been to this particular gathering before, little speaker, and I left early last time too."

"You *knew* this would happen." Vesper kept his voice low, though the street was empty enough. "You told me the Door was opening. You didn't tell me the whole house would start walking toward it."

The fox's amber eyes reflected the strange light, and for a moment something moved behind them that was much older than a fox. "Would it have changed what you did this morning?"

Vesper opened his mouth. Closed it. Thought about the hours since midnight — the flight through the undercity, the mole-tunnels, the raid, the small warm weight of Wren's hand in his as they'd run — and admitted, silently, that it would not have.

"That's what I thought," said the fox, with the gentle insufferability unique to foxes and certain elderly aunts. "Now. The girl is humming."

Vesper looked down. Wren was indeed humming — a thread of melody, thin and wavering, her eyes fixed on the light above the spire. And the horrible thing, the thing that turned his stomach to lead, was that he recognized it. Everyone in Corvane would have recognized it, if they'd had seven years to forget it. It was the first line of the old hymn. The one the Choir used to sing. The one that had gone silent the day the gods did.

"Wren." He crouched to her level, took her shoulders. Her skin was warm, fever-warm. "Wren, look at me. Not at the light. At me."

Her eyes came back from very far away. For a heartbeat they were her own again — frightened, nine years old, missing her mother — and then the tide rose in them, that same tide-water look he'd seen in Merrin's hollowed gaze, and her mouth opened and a voice came out that was not hers, that was vast and patient and terribly glad:

"*We hear the small ones singing. We are coming to collect our choir.*"

Then she blinked, swayed, and was a tired child again, asking him in a whisper whether they could sit down soon.

Vesper looked at the fox. The fox looked at Vesper. Somewhere across the city, the bell tolled on, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the hymn continued in a thousand small throats.

"We have to get her away from here," Vesper said.

"Yes," said the fox. "And I must tell you, little speaker, that I do not think 'away' exists anymore."

* * *

From the cathedral gallery, where the city's ruling council had been arranged in rows like exhibits, Sable watched the doors open.

No one had opened them. She was quite sure of that; she had posted six of her own Inquisitors at those doors at midnight, and all six now stood at the base of the steps looking like men who had been asked to hold back the tide with brooms. The doors simply swung inward, both together, with the unhurried grandeur of a host admitting guests long expected, and the light inside was gold, and warm, and utterly wrong for a building that had stood empty and cold for seven years.

The crowd did not surge. That was the detail Sable would remember afterward, the one that haunted her: they did not surge. Ten thousand people walked, calmly, in the manner of parishioners late for a service they still intended to catch, and the council members around her began, one by one, to stand.

"Sit down," Sable said, to no one in particular.

Chancellor Vane was already moving. So was Magistrate Corvel, who had signed more burning orders than anyone alive. So was the entire bench of aldermen, adjusting their chains and collars as they descended toward the doors, and Sable stood frozen at the rail above them, watching the government of Corvane walk into the dark on its own feet, and understood with terrible clarity that no order she could shout would stop them. You cannot arrest a man for kneeling. There was no statute for it. She had checked, in another life, in another context, and there was no statute for it.

Inside, the light gathered.

She saw it happen from above: the congregation flowing into the nave, filling the pews, overflowing into the aisles, faces upturned. And at the altar — where the plain stone block of the Church of Silence still stood, austere and bare — the air thickened into presence. Not one shape. Several. They overlapped like figures seen through frosted glass, immense and indistinct, and each time Sable tried to fix the outline of one, her eyes slid off it the way a cartographer's slid off Vellichor.

A voice spoke. It did not come from the altar so much as from the walls, the stones, the bones of the building, and it said:

"*You kept our house. We are pleased.*"

And Chancellor Aldous Vane — who had outlawed a hymn, who had melted bells, who had signed the death warrant of the last woman who sang in public — dropped to his knees on the cathedral flagstones and said, in a voice wet with gratitude:

"My lords. My gods. We hoped — we *prayed* —"

"*Yes,*" said the walls, and the warmth in it was the worst part, the unbearable, paternal warmth of it, "*we heard you. We hear everyone now. Every voice that was ours. We are calling them home.*"

Sable's hand found the railing and gripped it. *Every voice that was ours.* Speakers. Every speaker in Corvane, summoned like iron filings to a lodestone, and somewhere in the crowded streets below — she scanned the plaza desperately, uselessly — somewhere out there was a charm-seller with kind tired eyes and a fox, walking toward this building whether he willed it or not.

She had spent seven years protecting him with false reports and misfiled evidence, and it had all worked, and none of it mattered, because you could hide a man from Inquisitors but you could not hide him from gods.

"Inquisitor Halloway."

She startled. One of her own junior officers stood beside her, pale, sweating, holding out a slate. "Report from the Gray Market quarter, ma'am. The, ah. The speakers. We've counted forty-one answering the summons so far. Walking. All walking the same direction." He swallowed. "There's a list forming. Cross-referenced with the old registries. Your name is on the distribution."

She took the slate. Scanned it. Forty-one names, and at the bottom, added in a different hand, newer ink, flagged twice:

*Winterbourne, V. — status unknown — priority.*

"Find him," she said, before she could decide whether the order was for capture or rescue, and hated herself for not knowing. "Quietly. Before anyone else does."

* * *

They did not get Wren away. That was the simple truth of the afternoon, and Vesper carried it like a stone.

They had made it four streets — fox ahead, Wren between them, Vesper's hand clamped around hers — when the girl stopped humming and started walking. Not running. Walking, with the calm terrible purpose of sleepwalkers, straight through a market barrow, straight through Vesper's grip when he grabbed for her, and when he caught her wrist her arm was strong as a winch cable and her head turned and the tide-water eyes regarded him with something that was almost pity.

"*Not this one,*" said the god wearing her. "*This one sings tomorrow. But the small voice belongs to us now, and the choir gathers, and you—*" the voice paused, tasting him, and Vesper felt himself known the way a butcher knows a carcass— "*you we have wanted for a very long time, Vesper Winterbourne.*"

Then she blinked, sagged, and burst into tears, and he scooped her up and ran.

He ran because there was nothing else. He ran with a child on his hip and a fox streaming behind him and the whole city tilting eastward around them, people and animals alike drawn toward the cathedral like water finding a drain, and everywhere he looked he saw other walkers — a lamplighter with his ladder abandoned mid-street, an old woman still holding a half-kneaded loaf, a boy of perhaps fifteen whose lips moved silently around words in a language seven years dead. Forty-one, someone had said. He wondered how they were counting. He wondered if he was on the list.

He wondered, in the small cold room at the center of himself where he kept the truths he couldn't afford, whether hiding had ever been anything but a countdown, and whether he had always known it.

The fox caught up at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, breath ragged, ears flat. "The chapel," it gasped. "The drowned chapel. If the Door is truly there, if it opens while the gods are gathered and distracted—"

"Tonight?"

"Soon. Tonight, tomorrow. But Vesper—" the fox planted itself in his path, and its eyes were ancient and afraid, "—the pull will only grow. By nightfall you will not be able to choose not to walk. None of us will. Whatever we do, we do it *now*."

Behind them, across the rooftops, the great cracked bell swung and tolled and swung, keeping time for a congregation that had never been dismissed, and in the cathedral's golden dark, forty voices and more were learning, note by note, an old song — and Vesper Winterbourne, standing in a stinking lane with a weeping child in his arms, felt the first true tug of the summons take hold behind his sternum, gentle as a hand on his collar, patient as a god.

He turned west, toward the Fenmarch, and began to walk — fast, before the walking became someone else's idea.

He did not see the two Inquisitors peel away from the crowd's edge to follow him. He did not see the slate, or the name, or the word *priority*.

But the fox did. And the fox said nothing, and quickened its pace, and prayed — in the old tongue, to no one who was listening — that it would be enough.
