## 15. Taken

They came for him at the charm stall on a Tuesday, which Vesper would later consider the most insulting part of it. Tuesdays were slow. He had spent seven years learning the rhythms of the Gray Market the way a sailor learns tides—when to open early, when to pack up, when a customer's lingering questions meant curiosity and when they meant a badge—and he had never once been arrested on a Tuesday. It felt like a failure of craft.

The fox had warned him that morning. *They're moving in threes,* it had said through the window of his rented room, tail flicking with the particular irritation of an immortal creature forced to discuss logistics. *Inquisitors travel in threes when they mean to take someone alive. In twos when they mean to make an example. You should have left at dawn.*

*I was making breakfast,* Vesper had said.

*You were burning porridge.*

*That's how I make it.*

Now, standing behind his little table of luck-knots and river-stone pendants while three gray-cloaked figures cut through the market crowd like knives through bread, Vesper had time to reflect that perhaps the fox had a point about dawn. The sparrows scattered from the awning above him in a single startled breath, and their last words before flight were not words at all but one long, keening warning: *run run run run*—

He didn't run. Running confirmed things. Instead he smiled at the woman currently haggling over a fertility charm shaped like a fat clay rabbit and said, "For you, mistress, two coppers, and I'll throw in the blessing free."

"Blessings are illegal," said the tallest of the three cloaks, arriving at the table with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never once been told no by a crowd.

"Then it's a good thing I'm only selling rabbits," Vesper said. "Very legal animal, the rabbit."

The cloak pulled back its hood. Not Sable. A man with a jaw like a drawn bowstring and eyes the color of dishwater, and Vesper felt something in his chest unknot and then immediately retie itself tighter, because if it wasn't Sable, then Sable hadn't come herself, and that meant either she didn't know where he was—which was impossible—or she knew exactly where he was and had chosen to send other people, which was worse in ways he didn't have time to inventory.

"Vesper Winterbourne," the man said, producing a folded paper sealed with the black wax of the Office of Inquiry. "By order of the Grand Inquisitor, you are detained for questioning regarding the matter of the summoned speakers."

"I'm a charm seller," Vesper said. "I sell knots. Look at this knot. Does this knot look like it could hold a god?"

"It looks like a knot," the man agreed, and then, with genuine regret, as though he'd have preferred Vesper be difficult: "Come along quietly, sir. There's a bonus in it for us if there's no trouble."

"There's a bonus," Vesper repeated.

"Half a crown."

"Well." Vesper set down the clay rabbit, gave the haggling woman an apologetic shrug—she took the opportunity to simply steal the thing, which he supposed was fair—and stepped out from behind his table. "For half a crown, I'll walk so quietly you'll forget you caught me."

The fox, he noticed, was nowhere in sight. Clever animal. Or cowardly one. He hadn't yet decided which, and suspected he'd have years to work on it, assuming he had years, which the current evidence suggested he did not.

* * *

The ride to the cathedral took longer than Vesper expected, because the city had changed overnight.

Corvane had always been a city of noise—the fish-cries at the docks, the bell-towers arguing about the hour, the eternal low grumble of the Gray Market negotiating—but now beneath all of it there was a second sound, a hum, deep and patient, like a hymn being sung through the floor of the world. Vesper heard it through the bars of the prisoner cart, and worse, he understood it. It was the same melody the rats had recited to him weeks ago in the dark, the dead language rising out of the sewers, except now it wasn't coming from below. It was coming from everywhere. From the stones. From the sky. From the sparrows, who sat silent on the wires and would not meet his eye.

And in the streets, people were walking. Not the ordinary walking of errands and appointments, but a slow, drifting procession, hundreds of them, faces tilted up toward the cathedral spire with expressions Vesper recognized because he'd seen them on Merrin's face: wonder hollowed out into obedience. Some of them wore brands on their wrists, fresh and shining. Most of them didn't. They just walked.

"They're not summoned," Vesper said to his escort, who was watching the crowds with the tight expression of a man whose worldview was being renegotiated without his consent. "Those people. They're not speakers. They're just... going."

"People do foolish things," the man said. "Religious ones especially."

"That's not religion. That's—" Vesper stopped, because the word he wanted was *dinner*, and saying it aloud seemed unwise. The gods weren't gathering worshippers. They were gathering ingredients.

The fox had explained it once, in its dry, archaic way, perched on his windowsill like a small rust-colored accusation: *A god waking is a god hungry. Seven years of silence starved them. Now they feed—not on prayer, prayer is nothing, prayer is the smell of food through a window—they feed on voices. On speakers. Every voice they take makes them more awake, and every hour they are more awake, they want more voices. You see the arithmetic.*

Vesper saw the arithmetic. He was very much afraid he was the largest number in it.

* * *

The cathedral of Corvane had been built to be empty. That was the joke nobody said aloud anymore: a nation that outlawed its gods had built them the largest house in the city, all vaulted stone and cold blue windows, and then locked the door for seven years. Now the doors stood open, and the emptiness was gone, and Vesper understood, walking up the nave between his escorts, why the old maps called this place the Choir's Throat.

It was full. Not crowded—full, the way a cup is full, the way a lung is full. People knelt in the aisles in perfect rows. Candles burned in colors Vesper had never seen wax take: green-gold, tide-blue, a red so deep it looked like the candle was bleeding upward. And at the far end, where the altar should have been, Merrin stood.

She had been a laundress's neighbor, a mender of socks, a woman who laughed too loud at her own jokes. She still stood in Merrin's body, hands folded, head bowed, but her eyes were full of slow-moving water, tide-water, ocean-water, water that remembered drowning continents, and when she lifted her face to look at Vesper across the whole length of the nave, the humming hymn stopped.

Every head in the cathedral turned to him. In perfect unison. Like a field of flowers following the sun.

"Oh," Vesper said faintly. "That's not ominous at all."

"You came," Merrin said, and the voice was not Merrin's. It was Merrin's voice played underneath another one, the way a bell contains both the strike and the ringing. It was a kind voice. That was the worst of it. It sounded like someone's grandmother, someone's oldest friend, someone who had known you since you were small and had only ever wanted good things for you. "We hoped you would come. We asked, and you came."

"I was carried," Vesper said. "There's a difference. Also there was a half-crown involved."

"Bring him," the god said gently, and the congregation rose as one body, and Vesper had the distinct sensation of being a mouse that has just realized the barn cat has been watching it for some time with what can only be described as affection.

* * *

They seated him—not chained, which frightened him more than chains would have—in a chair before the altar, and the god in Merrin descended the steps slowly, barefoot on stone, and knelt in front of him so that they were eye to eye. Up close, the tide in her eyes moved with real currents. He could hear gulls in it, distantly. He could hear ships.

"Vesper Winterbourne," the god said, tasting the name. "Speaker of beasts. The last one who hid well enough to survive us. Do you know what we are?"

"A problem," Vesper said. "With bells."

A smile crossed Merrin's face, and it was almost entirely Merrin's own, and that broke his heart a little. "Humor. Yes. We remember humor. We had a speaker once, four hundred years ago, a jester, who made even the Storm-Father laugh until the rivers flooded. We ate his voice last night, from the memory. It still tastes of jokes." The tide turned. "We are the Choir, Vesper Winterbourne. We are the ones your country was built inside of. You have lived your whole life in our cupped hands and called it a nation."

"The fox says otherwise."

"The fox," the god said, and something moved through the tide-water, something old and hurt, "is a coward and a traitor and the best of us, and we will deal with it after. But first. First, you."

It reached out—Merrin's hand, chapped from laundry work, gentle as falling snow—and touched his chest, over his heart, and Vesper felt the touch go through him like light through glass.

And the cathedral showed him things.

* * *

This is what temptation looks like when a god does it properly: it doesn't wear glory. Glory frightens honest people. It wears *relief*.

Vesper stood in a version of Corvane that had never outlawed anything. He stood behind his charm stall, but the charms were real—luck-knots that actually bent luck, river-stones that actually remembered rain—and the customers came in their hundreds and none of them feared him, and the sparrows on his awning spoke to him in full sentences and the whole market leaned in to listen, delighted, because a speaker of beasts was not a criminal here but a wonder. A treasure. A man the city was proud of.

He saw himself walking openly down the Gray Market with the fox at his heel, and no one reaching for a badge. He saw himself standing in this cathedral, but not kneeling—standing, at the front, conducting, because the Choir needed a voice and had chosen his, and every beast in the country raised its throat to sing when he lifted his hand. He saw Sable in the crowd—Sable, not gray-cloaked, not armed, just a woman with a pressed flower in her pocket, looking at him the way she used to look at him across a kitchen table when the bread was rising and neither of them had anywhere to be.

*Never hide again,* said the god, from everywhere, from inside his own ribs. *That is what we offer. Not power. Power is such a small word. We offer you the end of flinching. You have spent your whole life making yourself small, Vesper Winterbourne. Small voice, small stall, small life. We know. We watched. We were asleep, but the land watched for us, and the land kept notes. You taught yourself to be unremarkable so thoroughly that you nearly succeeded—you are the only speaker in seven years we could not find until we stopped looking. Imagine, instead, being found. Being known. Being *loud*.*

"I'm a charm seller," Vesper whispered, but his voice shook, because the vision smelled like his mother's kitchen, and his mother had been dead eleven years, and gods had no business knowing that.

*You would never sell another lie,* the god murmured. *No more luck-knots that are only string. No more smiling at customers while the sparrows tell you secrets you must pretend not to hear. No more measuring your days against a woman you cannot speak to. Say yes, and we will wake the world so gently it will thank us. Say yes, and you will never again be a man practicing smallness. You will be the Choir's new voice, and the Choir will carry you, and you will rest, truly rest, for the first time since you were a child who talked to sparrows and was punished for it.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Vesper would carry into whatever came next: it was a good offer. It was tailored to him like a coat cut from his own measurements. Every other temptation he'd ever imagined had been grand and obvious—a crown, a fortune, fire at his fingertips—and he'd always known he'd refuse those. This one was quiet. This one was *rest*. This one was permission to stop hiding, offered by the very thing he was hiding from, and it wore his own face in the vision, his own face but unafraid, and he looked at that unafraid man and felt such longing that his eyes stung.

"You've done this before," Vesper said hoarsely. "To the others. Merrin. The two who walked into the cathedral. You showed them this."

*We showed each of them their own heart's shape,* the god said, unashamed. *Merrin wished to matter. She matters now. She sings prophecies in three dead languages and kings send for her. Is that cruelty?*

"She isn't in there anymore."

*Merrin is right here.* The god spread Merrin's arms, and somewhere deep in the tide-water of those eyes, something small beat against the surface—once, twice—like a swimmer signaling. *Merrin is everywhere now. Merrin is vast. Ask her.*

"Merrin," Vesper said, and leaned forward, and kept his voice low and human and aimed past the god at the woman underneath, the way he'd learned to talk to animals through storms, through fear, through the noise of their own natures. "Merrin Oakes. You owe me two coppers from the winter market, and you swore on your mother's mending basket you'd pay, and your mother's mending basket is the one thing in this world you'd never swear falsely on."

The tide went still.

For one heartbeat—one—one single heartbeat—Merrin's face was only Merrin's, terrified and drowning and furiously, wonderfully stubborn, and her mouth said, in a voice scraped thin as wire:

"Don't take it. Whatever it shows you. Don't—"

And then the tide closed over her, smooth as poured glass, and the god smiled with her mouth and said, warmly, patiently, the way one speaks to a child who has grabbed a hot pan:

*You see? Even her resistance is ours. Everything in this country is ours, Vesper Winterbourne. The borders closed like a trap because the trap is us. There is no Door that leads out of a god. There is only the Door that leads deeper in.*

"Actually," said a voice from the back of the cathedral, dry, archaic, and utterly unimpressed, "there is one that leads out. I've been through it. The view's lovely this time of year."

Every head in the congregation turned. Vesper's heart performed a maneuver that should have required a permit.

On the great sill of the western window, silhouetted against storm-light, sat a red fox with its tail curled around its feet like a judge settling in for a verdict.

"You," said the god, and for the first time the warm grandmother-voice cracked, and underneath it Vesper heard something enormous and wounded and afraid. "*You* were supposed to stay hidden."

"I generally am," the fox agreed. "But my speaker has a habit of getting taken on Tuesdays, and I find I mind." It looked at Vesper, and its amber eyes held an apology and an order in equal measure. *Run,* it did not say aloud, but said anyway, in the old way, in the way that goes straight down the spine. *When I say. Be ready to run.*

And Vesper—who had spent seven years practicing smallness, who had built a whole self out of being overlooked, who had just been shown heaven by a thing wearing his own face—felt something in his chest stand up straight for the first time in years.

Because the god had made one mistake. It had shown him the vision with Sable in it. And the vision's Sable had been happy, but the real Sable had sent three strangers and a half-crown's worth of distance between them, and Vesper knew her, god or no god, better than anything in this cathedral did.

She hadn't come because she couldn't watch. Which meant she was still in there. Still fighting. Still his.

"No," Vesper said, and stood up out of the chair, and the whole cathedral drew breath. "Thank you. Genuinely—it was a lovely offer, the kitchen was a nice touch, very cruel, well done—but I'm afraid I already have a choir. It's mostly rats and one extremely rude fox, and the acoustics are terrible, but they've never once eaten anybody."

The god rose with him, tide-water churning, and around the nave a thousand throats opened to sing one note, one vast crushing chord of command—

"Now," said the fox.

—and the western window exploded inward in a storm of wings, every sparrow and pigeon and starling in Corvane arriving at once, and Vesper ran.
