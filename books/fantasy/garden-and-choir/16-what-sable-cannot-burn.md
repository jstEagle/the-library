## 16. What Sable Cannot Burn

The order came at dawn, written in the Provost's own hand, which meant it could not be refused and could not be forgiven. Sable Halloway read it three times by candlelight in her office above the Inquisition barracks, and each time the words arranged themselves differently, the way a nightmare rearranges itself when you try to look directly at it.

*By order of the Provisional Council of Corvane: the speaker known as Vesper Winterbourne is to be executed publicly at midday tomorrow, his death to serve as warning to all who traffic with the waking gods.*

*Signed,* she thought bitterly, *a man who knelt to a tide-god yesterday afternoon and wept while he did it.*

The Council had spent a week worshipping the things they had outlawed, and now they wanted blood to prove they hadn't enjoyed it. She understood the arithmetic. Fear was a currency, and Corvane's mint had been running hot since the first answered prayer. A public execution would buy the city another season of quiet.

It would also kill the only person she had ever—

She set the paper down. Her hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the joke everyone told about Inquisitor Halloway, that her hands never shook even over the bodies. Nobody asked what it cost to keep them still.

Vesper sat in the holding cells beneath the cathedral, two floors down and forty feet of stone away, and she had not gone to see him since the Inquisitors brought him in. That had been deliberate. She knew herself well enough to know that if she looked at him through the bars, her face would do something unforgivable.

So instead she did paperwork. There was always paperwork. The Inquisition generated confessions the way a wet log generates smoke, and someone had to file them.

At noon, a junior Inquisitor named Pell knocked, held up a writ, and said, "Ma'am, the Provost wants the prisoner moved to the upper cell tonight. Better light for the— for the ceremony."

"Better light," Sable repeated.

"For the crowd, ma'am."

"The crowd will be outdoors, Pell."

"Yes, ma'am." He shifted. He was twenty-three and earnest and had joined the Inquisition because he genuinely believed in protecting people from dangerous magic, which made him either the last honest man in Corvane or the most deluded. "Only — some of us were wondering. The prisoner. Winterbourne. Is it true what they're saying? That he saved the child Wren from the raid on Tanner's Row?"

Sable looked at him until he found something fascinating about the floor.

"The prisoner will be moved," she said, "when I say he'll be moved. Dismissed."

He went. She sat a long while with the dawn order in front of her, and then she did something she had been practicing for seven years without knowing it: she lied beautifully, this time to herself, and told herself she was only going downstairs to confirm the transfer arrangements.

* * *

The crypts beneath the cathedral were older than the cathedral, older than the city's name, older — if you believed the fox stories, and lately Sable believed almost anything — than the country itself. The Inquisition used them for storage now: confiscated charms in lead-lined crates, censers, the great brass horns they blew at executions to drown out any last words that might sound like spellwork.

Vesper was in the third cell, sitting on the stone bench with his back against the wall, and he looked, infuriatingly, like a man waiting for a tardy friend rather than a man awaiting execution.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm not—" She stopped. "I wasn't aware I was expected."

"You weren't. But you've been pacing outside that door for six minutes. The rats told me." He smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked on one side, the one that had gotten him through every hard year by making the hardness look like a joke he was in on. "They also tell me you haven't eaten since yesterday. Which is hypocritical of you, given how many times you arrested pie-sellers."

"They weren't arrested for selling pies. They were arrested for selling pies with luck-knots baked into the crust."

"And were the knots lucky?"

"One baker claimed so. He was also claiming it on the way to the stocks, so I have my doubts."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of him — and then it died between them, because there was a writ in her pocket with his death written on it in the Provost's hand, and laughter has a way of curdling when it touches paper like that.

Silence filled the cell. It was an old silence, worn smooth by use, the silence of two people who had shared a bed and a decade and a war and had learned every shape of quiet that existed between them.

"I'm sorry about the cuffs," Sable said finally.

"You didn't put them on me."

"My people did. On my orders."

"On the Council's orders," he corrected gently. "On the god's orders, probably, if Merrin's prophecies are to be believed. You've got a long chain of command, Sable. Somewhere at the top of it is a thing with tide-water eyes."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," he agreed. "But it's a map. I deal in maps now. Ask my fox."

"How is the fox?"

"Insufferable. Archaic. Fond of rhetorical questions." He flexed his cuffed hands. "He says hello, incidentally. He says you owe him an apology for the incident with the net in chapter nine."

"There was no chapter nine."

"He's very insistent about the numbering."

And there it was again, the terrible ease of him, the way seven years of law and fear and separate beds fell away the moment he opened his mouth, as though the Purge had been a long market day and they'd simply lost sight of each other in the crowd. Sable pressed her palm flat against the cold doorframe and made herself be an Inquisitor for one more minute.

"I came to tell you the transfer is scheduled," she said. "Upper cell, tonight. Execution at midday tomorrow. Public. The Provost wants the horns blown during the—" She stopped. "During everything."

Vesper nodded slowly. He looked at his hands. When he spoke again, the humor was gone, folded away like a charm wrapped in cloth.

"All right," he said. "Thank you for telling me yourself. Most Inquisitors would send Pell."

"Pell would cry."

"Pell would cry," he agreed softly. "You won't."

"No."

"Because you already cried, years ago, and decided once was enough."

Sable did not answer. The torch in the corridor guttered. Down here, under forty feet of stone and seven years of law, the truth sat between them like a third prisoner.

"I filed false reports," she said.

It came out flat, faster than she'd planned, the way a splinter comes out — all at once or not at all. Vesper's head came up.

"After the Purge. Every quarter, for seven years. The Inquisition requires a census of suspected speakers. Names, locations, evidence." She heard her own voice go procedural, hiding inside the language of her office the way he hid inside his smallness. "Your name has been on my list every quarter. And every quarter I wrote *deceased* beside it. Died of fever, winter of the first year. It was tidy. Nobody checks the dead."

Vesper stared at her.

"Sable—"

"Forty-one reports. I kept copies." She pulled the little oilcloth packet from inside her coat — she had carried it so long the string had worn grooves in her fingers — and held it through the bars. "In case anyone ever audited. In case I ever needed to prove what I'd done. Or in case I needed to burn them and prove I hadn't."

He took the packet. His hands, cuffed together, fumbled it, and he clutched it against his chest like something rescued from a fire.

"Seven years," he said. "Every time an Inquisitor walked into the Gray Market, I thought — I built my whole life around not being seen by you. I changed my route. I stopped singing. I taught myself to be small, Sable, I made myself *nothing*, and the whole time—"

"The whole time I was signing your death certificate quarterly and filing it under *fever*." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "We are the two most careful cowards this country has ever produced."

"Don't say coward."

"It's accurate."

"It isn't." He stood, came close to the bars, close enough that she could see the exhaustion underneath the wit, the bruised lids, the way his jaw was set the way it set before he did something reckless. "A coward hides because he's afraid. I hid because if they found me, they'd find *you*. Every beast-speaker they caught in the first year broke, Sable. Everyone talks eventually. And the first question they ask is always *who else knows you*. If I'd stayed near you, stayed visible, stayed loved — they'd have had you inside a month. So I made myself unlovable. Unfindable. I let you think I ran because I was selfish." His voice caught. "That was the hardest charm I ever sold."

The torch guttered again. Sable became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking after all, and that the joke everyone told about Inquisitor Halloway had just quietly died in a crypt.

"You absolute idiot," she whispered.

"Yes."

"You let me grieve you. For seven years. I mourned you, Vesper. I did the whole — I burned the letters. Well. Almost all of them." 

His eyes sharpened. "Which ones survived?"

"A flower," she admitted. "From the night market, the year before the Purge. You bought it off a woman who swore it never wilted, and I told you that was obviously a charm and illegal, and you said—"

"*Everything good is slightly illegal,*" they said together, and then both stopped, because finishing each other's sentences after seven years of silence is its own kind of confession.

Sable reached into her coat again, into the inner pocket, the one over her heart where Inquisitors were supposed to keep their warrants, and drew out the flower. Pressed flat. Browned at the edges but stubbornly, illegally, un-wilted at the center. She had checked it every morning for seven years the way other people check a wound.

Vesper made a sound that was not quite a word.

"I should have burned it," Sable said. "When the orders came down, I sat at my desk with a candle and I told myself: this is what duty looks like. This is the cost. You burn the flower, you sign the order, you blow the horns, and Corvane gets its quiet season." She turned the flower in her fingers. "And then I thought about what you just said. About hiding so they couldn't get to me through you. And I realized I've been doing the same thing in reverse. I stayed in the Inquisition — I *rose* in the Inquisition — because as long as I was inside it, I could bend it. Falsify a report here. Lose a file there. Misplace a witness. I told myself it was penance. It wasn't. It was the same trick. We both built our lives around a lie whose entire purpose was keeping the other one alive."

"So what do we do now?" Vesper asked quietly. "Tomorrow at midday they blow the horns."

Sable looked at the lock on the cell door. Then at the ring of keys on her belt. Then at the flower.

Here was the thing she had understood at her desk at dawn, staring at the Provost's handwriting: everyone kept telling her she had to choose. Duty or love. Office or heart. The Council, the gods, the fox, the whole rotten architecture of this closing country insisted the choice was binary, that a person was a scale and must tip one way or the other.

But she had spent seven years falsifying reports, and falsification teaches you something profound: *the terms of a document can be rewritten.*

"I don't choose," she said aloud.

Vesper blinked. "That's usually how choosing works."

"Not this time. I refuse the choice. I am not going to stand at an execution and pick duty over you, and I am not going to run into the Fenmarch and pick you over every soul in this city who needs the Choir put back to sleep. Both of those futures are traps, and I've spent my career learning to spot traps." She pulled the keys from her belt. "So instead: I'm going to open this door, and you're going to finish whatever the fox is making you finish, and I am going to walk back upstairs and report that the prisoner escaped through a tunnel I have just invented, and neither of us will ever speak of which of us chose what, because the answer is *neither*. We choose the outcome. Not the terms."

For a moment Vesper just looked at her, and she watched something move across his face that she recognized from years ago, from mornings in a rented room above a bakery: the expression of a man realizing he has been loved more competently than he dared hope.

"You've changed," he said.

"I've always been like this. You just never saw me work."

The lock turned with a sound like a small, illegal bell. The door swung open. Vesper stepped out into the corridor, rubbed his wrists, and then — instead of running, instead of doing any of the sensible fugitive things — he took the pressed flower from her hand with excruciating gentleness, leaned in, and kissed her forehead the way he used to when she came home from night shifts smelling of smoke and other people's fear.

"For the record," he murmured, "the fever that killed me was very serious. Nearly fatal. You saved my life."

"Somebody had to. You were clearly unfit to manage it yourself."

"Terrible bedside manner, Inquisitor Halloway."

"Go," she said, laughing and crying in a manner entirely unbefitting her office. "Before I arrest you for resisting nothing."

He went four steps and stopped. Turned back. The torchlight caught the fox-shaped shadow at the end of the corridor, waiting, patient as a god.

"Sable. The song — the counter-hymn. If it works, I'll have to give something up. Something that anchors me here." He hesitated. "If I come out of it not knowing you—"

"Then I'll introduce myself," she said. "I'm told I make a strong first impression. I once arrested a pie-seller."

He smiled the crooked smile, and the shadow at the end of the hall stirred, and somewhere far above them the cathedral bells began to ring — not the hour, not the service, but the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, the sound Corvane made when the world broke.

Sable's blood went cold. The bells meant the vessels were moving. All of them. Toward the cathedral doors.

"Vesper," she said. "Whatever the fox taught you — I think you're going to need it sooner than midnight."

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, came a sound like the sea deciding to sing.
