## 17. The Song Beneath the City

The fox woke him an hour before dawn by standing on his chest and breathing into his face.

"You snore," it said.

"I don't."

"You do. It sounds like a cart with a bad axle going downhill forever. Get up. Tonight we sing, and you should practice not being terrible at it first."

Vesper sat up in the abandoned tannery loft where they'd spent what he suspected would be his last night of sleep — a thought he was trying very hard not to have, because thoughts like that had a way of becoming true if you fed them. Wren was still curled in the corner under a pile of sacking, one arm flung over the badger cub that had adopted her somewhere in the undercity and refused to be reasoned with. The child slept the way children sleep: completely, shamelessly, as though the world had never once tried to eat her.

Vesper envied her with his whole heart.

"Say it again," he said to the fox. "The first line. Slowly."

The fox's ears flattened — its version of a sigh. It had made him repeat the counter-hymn forty times yesterday, and thirty-eight times before that the day prior, and each time Vesper got it wrong in some small way that the fox described with escalating creativity. *You sang it like a man apologizing. You sang it like a man selling something. You sang it like a man who has never once in his life believed he deserved to be heard.*

That last one had stung, mostly because it was accurate.

"The first line," the fox said now, "is not words. Stop waiting for words. Words are the cage the gods built around music so they could own it. The first line is—" it paused, searching, "—it is the sound of snow deciding to fall. Do you have that?"

"No."

"Good. Honesty. That's a start." It paced to the window, where the sky was still the color of wet slate. "You will have it when it matters. That's how songs work. That's how *I* worked, once." It said this lightly, the way it always said the enormous things, and then hopped down and went to nose at Wren's sacking until she stirred.

"Is it time?" she mumbled.

"It's past time," said the fox. "The city is already singing. Badly. But singing."

And Vesper heard it then — or rather, he felt it, low beneath the floorboards, beneath the cobbles, beneath the clay and the bones of old Corvane. A hum. Not the gods' hymn, not anymore; the hollowed vessels in the cathedral had been screaming their masters' song for two days now, and every speaker in the city had felt the pull of it like a hook behind the sternum. This was something else. This was rats in the walls and pigeons on the eaves and moles under the gardens and dogs chained in yards, all of them humming, all of them carrying the melody the fox had taught them piece by piece through three desperate nights, passing it along the way water passes through roots.

Every beast in Corvane, learning one song.

It should have sounded chaotic. Instead it sounded like weather. Like something vast and patient deciding, all together, to rain.

* * *

They went down through the city as the bells rang false.

Vesper had learned, these past days, to read Corvane by its animals, and the animals told him a story he didn't want to hear. The gulls wheeled inland from the harbor in long reluctant lines — *the water tastes of iron*, they complained, *the water tastes of the deep places waking*. The cats had all gone up onto the roofs and would not come down. In Tanner's Row a horse stood trembling outside a burned stable while its owner wept and pulled uselessly at its halter, and Vesper stopped, because he couldn't not stop, and put his hand on the animal's neck.

*Not afraid,* the mare said, in the flat gray way horses say everything. *Listening. They are so loud now. The loud ones under the hill.*

"I know," Vesper murmured. "We're going to quiet them."

*You?* And there it was, the thing he'd been dreading since the fox first explained the plan: the doubt. Not cruelty — horses were never cruel — just the honest arithmetic of a creature weighing him against what was coming. *You are small. You smell of fear and charm-knots.*

"He does," the fox agreed cheerfully. "But so did I, once, and look at me now."

The mare looked at the fox for a long moment. Horses remember things older than cities; Vesper had always known that, the way you know a wall is load-bearing without knowing why. Whatever she saw in the little red body made her go still, and then she lowered her great head and breathed once, slowly, across both of them — a blessing, or a farewell, or possibly just a horse being a horse — and let her owner lead her away.

"What did she see?" Vesper asked quietly.

"A colleague," said the fox, and walked on, and did not elaborate, and Vesper had learned which battles not to fight.

Wren trotted at his side with the badger cub riding in her arms like a loaf of bread. She hadn't spoken much since the raid — since the Inquisitor's net had come down over Coppersmith Lane and Vesper had pulled her out of it by the collar while the world shouted — but she held his sleeve now with two fingers, the way she'd done since, a small anchor he pretended not to notice because noticing might make her stop.

"Merrin's still there," she said suddenly. "In the cathedral. I hear her at night. Through the mice." Her jaw set in a way that was far too old for her face. "She's still in there. Underneath. She told the mice to tell you — she remembers your name."

Vesper's throat tightened. Merrin Osgood, who had answered the summons because kindness made her think the gods must mean well. Who had looked at him with tide-water eyes and called him out of a crowd of hundreds. *There,* her borrowed mouth had said. *There is the voice. There is the last verse.* He had run from that. He'd been running from it ever since, in the way that a man runs from a thing only until he understands that the thing will simply wait for him at the end of every road.

"She remembers her name too," Vesper said. "Underneath. That's what we're counting on."

"That's a lot of underneath," said Wren.

"Yes," said the fox. "It is. Which is why we're not walking to the cathedral."

They weren't. The fox's route lay the other way entirely — down, and east, toward the Fenmarch and the drowned chapel where the Door of Unmaking waited under black water. Because the song had to be sung *at* the Door; that was the whole terrible architecture of it. The gods had woken through the Door's seam, leaking back into the world like light under a badly shut lid, and only at the seam could they be folded shut again. Sing anywhere else and you were whistling at a storm.

Sing at the Door, and you were the lid.

* * *

The Fenmarch received them the way it received everything: without enthusiasm.

The marsh country east of Corvane had never been good land, and seven years of godless silence had made it worse — drowned meadows, dead alders standing in black water like an audience that had given up on the performance and stayed anyway out of politeness. The chapel rose ahead of them out of the reeds as the sun went down, its bell tower tilted like a drunk leaning on a friend, its lower half swallowed by the flood that had taken the village of Fenlow and everyone's memory of Fenlow along with it.

The beasts were already there.

Vesper stopped at the edge of the last dry hummock and stared. He had known — the fox had told him, the plan had required it — but knowing and seeing were different countries with a hostile border between them. They came out of the reeds and the water and the dying light: herons standing in ranks like gray clerks. Rats in their dozens, sitting oddly dignified on the tussocks. Foxes from the warrens beyond the wall, a whole family of them, watching with yellow eyes. A heron. Two herons. An otter dragging itself up the mud with the expression of someone arriving late to a meeting they'd organized. Crows coming down out of the darkening sky in ones and twos and settling on the chapel roof until it glittered black. And overhead, high and silent, the bats beginning to gather as the light failed, because the night shift was clocking in.

They had all learned the song. Three nights of the fox running the rooftops and riverbanks like a fevered conductor, teaching eight bars here, four there, stitching a melody through a thousand small minds — and now they sat in the drowning dusk, waiting, humming it low, the whole marsh thrumming like a struck bell that hadn't yet decided to ring.

"They came," Wren whispered. Her fingers had found Vesper's sleeve again and closed properly this time.

"Of course they came," said the fox. But its voice had gone strange — softer, older, wearing its archaic Common like a coat it had taken out of storage. "They always come. That was the first covenant, before yours, before the Choir, before names. Beasts keep the songs. Men only borrow them." It looked up at Vesper, and in the last light its eyes were not a fox's eyes at all. "Are you ready?"

"No," Vesper said honestly.

"Wonderful. Neither was I, the first time. Ready is a story people tell afterward." The fox stepped forward onto the flooded threshold, water lapping at its paws, and turned. "Listen one last time. When we reach the Door, the gods will know. They will scream through every vessel they hold, and they will scream through the beasts they can still bend, and they will offer you everything you've ever wanted in voices you love. You will want to stop singing. You will want it more than you have ever wanted anything." A pause. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That it will hurt."

"I'm telling you it will *beg*. Gods do not hurt, Vesper. Gods *bargain*." The fox's tail swept once through the black water. "And the worst of it — the part I need you to carry down into that water with you — is that their bargain will be fair. That's what makes it terrible. Everything they offer will be real."

Vesper looked out over the marsh — at the herons and the rats and the crows and the small brave congregation of the voiceless, all of them gathered to help a charm-seller do the one thing he'd spent his whole life avoiding, which was matter — and found, to his mild surprise, that he wasn't afraid anymore. Or rather: he was afraid, thoroughly and completely, but the fear had finally stopped being about him. It had become a load-bearing fear. You could build on that kind.

"Then let's go make them beg politely," he said.

The fox made a sound that in any other creature would have been a laugh.

* * *

The water took him to the waist, then the chest. Wren stayed on the threshold with the badger — the fox had been absolute about that, and Wren had been absolute right back, and they'd settled on the compromise the way all compromises are settled, which is that the adult lied slightly less than the child noticed. But her hand caught his at the last moment, and she pressed something into his palm: a luck-knot. Badly tied, crooked, made from a bootlace.

"You gave me one," she said. "In the lane. Before the nets. I kept it." Her chin came up. "So. Luck."

He almost laughed, and almost cried, and managed — barely — neither. "Keep singing with them," he said. "All of you. However much you've got."

"We've got all of it," said Wren, with the terrifying certainty of nine years old, and let him go.

The chapel's drowned nave opened around him like a held breath. Moonlight came down through the broken roof in pale columns, and the water below was not water — not entirely. Beneath the surface, where the altar had been, there was a seam of light. Thin as a knife-cut in cloth. Patient as a wound that has decided to wait.

And the Door knew he was there.

The hymn rose — not from the cathedral, not from the city, but from everywhere at once, from under the world, vast and glad and hungry, the sound of things that had been asleep for seven years stretching their voices and finding the world still full of throats. Vesper felt it hook into him, felt his own lungs try to turn traitor and join it, and he did the thing the fox had taught him, the only thing, the small hard thing:

He listened instead.

Beneath the gods' great glad noise, the marsh was humming. The rats on their tussocks. The herons in their ranks. The crows on the tilted tower, the bats wheeling above, the otter and the foxes and the family of moles who had tunneled all the way from Corvane's gardens because someone had to and they were the ones who dug. Eight bars here. Four there. A melody stitched through a thousand small minds, carried down through water and mud and root, converging — on him.

Vesper Winterbourne, who had spent twenty years teaching himself to be unremarkable, stood chest-deep in a drowned church and began, very quietly, to sing.

His voice cracked on the first note. Of course it did. He was a charm-seller, not a chorister; his gift had always been for listening, not for making noise. But the beasts caught the crack and carried it, smoothed it, passed it outward through the reeds and the dark, and it came back to him from a hundred directions at once made enormous and true — the sound of snow deciding to fall, which he finally, finally understood, because it was happening in his chest.

The Door's seam of light shuddered.

And the gods screamed.

It came through the water and the stone and the marrow of him — fury wearing the shape of music, every vessel in Corvane opening its mouth at once, Merrin's tide-water voice and a hundred others crying out in harmonies no throat was built for: *stop, stop, thief, thief, the song is ours, the voices are ours, the world was ours before you learned to name it—*

Vesper sang louder.

Or rather — he sang quieter, which was harder, holding his small human thread steady inside the storm while the beasts bore the melody around him like porters bearing a beam. The seam of light bucked and flared. Shapes moved in it, vast and half-formed, pressing against the Door from the other side like faces against a window: the old gods, the fallen Choir, beautiful and ruined and *awake*, and one of them bent close, closer, until the light itself seemed to take a face—

A face he knew.

"Sable," it said, in Sable's voice, from Sable's mouth a mile away in the cathedral, carried here through whatever roads gods use. "Vesper. Look at me. Look at what I'm doing — I'm kneeling to them, I'm buying you time, I'm burning my whole life for you, and you would end them? You would end *this*?" The voice broke, beautifully, exactly the way it used to break in the years before the Purge, and it was a lie, it had to be a lie, and it was also — that was the horror of it — also true. "Come to me instead. Walk out of the water. We'll find another door. We'll find *any* door—"

The fox appeared beside him, treading water, fur slicked dark, eyes blazing.

"It lies with true things," the fox said sharply. "Sing."

"I know." His voice shook. He sang anyway.

The seam split wider. Light poured up through the black water like sunrise from below, and the hymn of the waking gods swelled to fill the world, and Vesper felt his grip on the counter-melody begin — just begin — to slip, his breath shortening, his arms heavy, the cold climbing his ribs like a patient tenant—

And then the marsh answered.

Not the beasts. Behind the beasts. A second voice, deeper than the rats and older than the herons, rising out of the Fenmarch itself, out of the drowned village and the dead alders and the mud that remembered being a country — a low, vast, patient harmony that Vesper had no name for, except that the fox beside him had gone utterly still, and utterly wet-eyed, and whispered one word in a language dead for seven years:

"*Vellichor.*"

The erased country, singing through its own grave.

The Door's seam of light trembled — narrowed — held —

And from the direction of Corvane, faint but unmistakable, cutting through the gods' great scream like a needle through wool, came a new sound. Small. Human. Nine years old and absolutely certain.

Wren was singing the counter-hymn in words.

Words the fox had never taught her.
