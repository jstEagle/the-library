## 18. Choir of the Hollow

The water in the drowned chapel was warm.

That was the first thing Vesper noticed, wading down through the flooded nave with the fox swimming beside him like a small red lantern, and it seemed wrong that it should be warm. Everything else about this place was cold — the stone pillars furred with black weed, the saints' faces worn smooth as river pebbles, the light seeping up from below in a color he had no name for except *door-colored*. But the water was warm as bathwater, warm as a hand held over a candle, and when he understood why, his chest tightened so hard he nearly swallowed the Fenmarch.

The water was warm because the Door was singing.

He could feel it now through his boots, through his ribs, through the small bones behind his ears: a hymn rising out of the seam of light at the chapel's flooded heart, and every note of it was an answered prayer, and every answered prayer was a hook, and every hook had a speaker on the end of it. Somewhere above him, Merrin was walking. Somewhere above him, Wren —

*Wren is here,* said the fox, treading water. *They brought her. They brought all of them.*

"I know." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "I can hear them thinking."

That was the other thing no one had warned him about, though perhaps there hadn't been time. The vessels didn't just speak for the gods anymore. As he sank deeper toward the Door, their thoughts bled through the water like ink through cloth, and they were not god-thoughts at all. They were human ones, worn thin. Merrin wondering if her sister had fed the cat. A ferryman named Aldous counting the strokes of his own oar and being unable to stop. And Wren — Wren, nine years old, thinking in a loop, *I want to go home, I want to go home, I want to go home,* over and over, the way a child worries a loose tooth.

*Vesper.* The fox's voice was very quiet. *When we reach the seam, I will teach you the counter-hymn. It is only seven notes. Seven notes and the truth underneath them.*

"And what's the truth underneath them?"

The fox swam a slow circle around him, and its eyes caught the door-light and held it. *That they are tired. That is the whole secret, Vesper. Gods are not defeated. They are put to bed. You cannot fight a thing that old. You can only sing to it the way you would sing to a frightened animal — and mean it.*

"Meaning it," Vesper said. "That's the hard part."

*For you?* The fox made a sound that might have been laughter, if foxes laughed and gods grieved in the same breath. *You have spent your whole life meaning things quietly. This is only the largest quiet thing you will ever do.*

He stopped arguing, mostly because his feet found the chapel floor and the water fell away from his shoulders, and because ahead of him the Door had opened its eye.

* * *

It was not a door the way doors are doors. It was a seam, exactly as the map-that-wasn't had promised: a line of light splitting the air above the flooded altar, tall as a cathedral window and thin as a knife, and through it he could see — or almost see — a country that did not exist. Green hills going gold at the edges. A river with no name because names had been taken from it. Apple trees in rows, heavy with fruit, tended by no one and waiting anyway.

Vellichor. The country erased from maps, still growing apples inside the wound of its own unmaking.

And standing before it, arranged in a half-moon like a congregation, were the hollowed ones.

There were more than he'd thought. Eleven, twelve — speakers gathered from every corner of Corvane and the fen-country beyond, standing in the knee-deep water with their arms at their sides, their eyes full of tide. Merrin stood at their center, and her mouth was moving, and what came out of it was not one voice but many, braided: the low bell-tone of the god beneath the cathedral, the wet whisper of something from the river, a sound like wind through a keyhole that might have been the oldest of them all.

*THE DOOR IS OPEN,* they said together, and the water shivered. *THE SINGER HAS COME TO THE SONG.*

"I haven't agreed to anything," Vesper said. It came out steadier than he felt, which was a small miracle he intended to be grateful for later, if there was a later.

*YOU WILL,* said the tide-eyed woman at Merrin's left, gently, the way you'd tell a child about winter coming. *ALL VOICES RETURN TO THE CHOIR. IT IS NOT CRUELTY. IT IS ONLY WHAT VOICES ARE FOR.*

"Well," said Vesper, "that's where you're wrong, actually."

And then, because he had learned long ago that the best lies were told quickly and the best truths slowly, he began to walk forward through the water, and the fox paced him on his right like a second shadow, and somewhere above — muffled by stone and flood — he heard boots. Many boots. Inquisitor boots, with Sable Halloway's stride somewhere among them, and he closed his eyes for half a heartbeat and let himself feel that too, because the fox had said *mean it*, and he was beginning to understand what the song would cost, and he wanted — selfishly, once, before the end — to hold everything he was about to lose.

*Now,* said the fox. *Listen. Sing after me.*

Seven notes.

They were nothing. That was the terrible beauty of them — a falling phrase any mother in any village might hum over a cradle, four notes down, three notes up, ending on a tone that hung in the air like breath on a window. Any lullaby anywhere. The fox sang it once in beast-tongue, once in the dead language of Vellichor, and then said: *Again. Slower. And underneath it, mean this: rest. You have carried enough. Put it down.*

Vesper opened his mouth and sang.

* * *

His voice was not beautiful. He'd sold luck-knots for eleven years; nobody had paid him to sing since he was a boy humming to calm a spooked cart-horse. But the first note left him and went out across the water and touched the nearest vessel — a fisherman Vesper didn't know — and the man's tide-filled eyes flickered, and his mouth, which had been reciting prophecy, stumbled.

"— the door," the fisherman said, in his own cracked voice, "the door is — no. No, I don't — my daughter, my daughter's waiting at the —"

*AGAIN,* screamed the gods through eleven mouths, and the water stood up in ridges, and the seam of light flared white. *SING WITH US OR BE UNMADE, LITTLE SPEAKER.*

"No, thank you," Vesper said, and sang the seven notes again.

Something in the chapel changed pitch.

It was hard to describe, even afterward, even in the years when he still had years to describe things in. It was as if the whole flooded nave had been holding one enormous chord — the gods' hymn, the summons, the pull — and someone had finally found the note that resolved it. The pressure eased. The water settled. And in the half-moon of vessels, one by one, the tide began to drain out of their eyes.

Merrin blinked. Looked down at the water around her knees. Looked up at Vesper with an expression of such naked, ordinary confusion that he nearly laughed with relief.

"Vesper Winterbourne," she said — her voice, only her voice, hoarse from disuse. "Why am I — where —" Then her face crumpled, because she remembered, all of it at once, what she had been used for, and Vesper reached her in three strides and took her hands.

"You're here," he said. "You're you. That's the whole battle. Stay with me."

"The song," she whispered. "I can hear it under yours. What *is* that?"

"A lullaby," said Vesper. "Apparently the oldest kind of magic there is."

And then Wren screamed.

She was at the far edge of the half-moon, smallest of them all, and the gods — feeling their congregation slip — had poured themselves into her like floodwater into a cellar. Her eyes were entirely silver now, no white left at all, and her voice came out layered, ancient, furious:

*THIS ONE IS OURS. THIS ONE PRAYED AND WAS ANSWERED. YOU CANNOT TAKE BACK AN ANSWER, SPEAKER.*

"I'm not taking anything back," Vesper said, wading toward her, heart hammering. "I'm asking you to put it down. She's nine. Whatever she prayed for, she prayed it the way children pray — she didn't know what she was asking. You knew. That's the difference between you and her, and it means the debt is yours, not hers."

*WE ARE STARVING,* the god in Wren howled, and beneath the fury Vesper heard it — the truth underneath, the thing the fox had promised. Hunger. Loneliness. Seven years of silence in a country that had outlawed remembering them. *SEVEN YEARS WE LAY IN THE DARK WHILE THEY BURNED OUR NAMES. AND NOW THERE IS WARMTH AGAIN, AND VOICES, AND YOU WOULD HAVE US GO BACK TO THE DARK.*

"Yes," Vesper said gently. "Because the dark isn't empty. We're all in it with you."

And he sang.

* * *

The seven notes again, slower, and this time he did not sing alone.

Merrin joined first — her voice rough and true, finding the phrase as if she'd known it all her life, which perhaps she had; perhaps it lived in the same deep place the gods had been sleeping in. Then the fisherman. Then Aldous the ferryman, who wept as he sang and kept singing anyway. One by one the hollowed ones remembered themselves and added their voices, and here was the thing Vesper understood only as it happened: the gods had never needed obedience. They had needed harmony. Every vessel that chose to sing was a voice returned willingly, and willing voices were the one food the waking could not refuse.

Even Wren fought it. Even Wren, silver-eyed and shaking, her small fists clenched in the water — and then, from somewhere inside the possession, her own voice broke through like a reed through ice, thin and off-key and absolutely certain, joining the seventh note a half-beat late the way children always do, and the god inside her made a sound that was not rage at all.

It was relief.

*Oh,* said every mouth at once, softly, the fury gone out of it. *Oh. Is that — is that how it goes. We had forgotten how it goes.*

The fox was crying. Vesper saw it without turning his head — great silent drops falling from the small red face into the flood — and the fox sang too, in a voice that was not a fox's voice at all but something older, something with bells in it, and Vesper understood in one blinding flash that the last god who refused to wake had been teaching him its own cradle-song all along. The one sung over it, ages ago, by hands that were dust before Corvane had walls.

The seam of light began to close.

Not violently. Gently, the way a hand closes a book. Through the narrowing gap Vesper saw the apple trees of Vellichor let go of their fruit all at once, a soft gold rain onto grass that no map would ever name, and the hills folded down like a sleeper pulling up blankets, and the unnamed river ran on, unnamed, content.

The gods folded back into the land the same way. Vesper felt them go — felt them pass through him like warmth leaving a bath, each one pausing, it seemed, to press something against the inside of his ribs in farewell. The bell-god from beneath the cathedral. The river-thing. The wind-through-the-keyhole, oldest and smallest. They sank down through stone and soil and fen-water, back into the roots of things, and the land received them the way soil receives seed: without comment, without gratitude, with only the promise of spring.

The last to go was the one wearing the fox.

No — Veper looked sideways. The fox still stood beside him, soaked and trembling and unmistakably present. But something *else* went — a second fox-shaped warmth, a ghost of it, sliding out of the small body and down into the earth, and the real fox sagged against Vesper's leg with a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh.

"It's done," the fox whispered. "Oh, it's done. They're asleep. Truly asleep — not silenced. Sung. There's a difference, Vesper. There was always a difference, and no one in this cursed country ever learned it."

Around the chapel, the hollowed ones were sitting down in the water, laughing, holding each other, checking their own hands as if surprised to find them attached. Wren had thrown both arms around Merrin's neck and was saying *I want to go home* again, but differently now — the way children say it when home is a place they expect to arrive at.

And Vesper stood in the warm water before the closing seam, and felt the triumph come up in him like sunrise, and felt, tangled inextricably with it, the grief.

Because the song was not finished.

He had known since the crypts. He had known since the fox taught him the seven notes and could not meet his eyes afterward. The hymn that woke the gods had been sung *through* people — through speakers, through vessels, through anyone with the gift — and a lullaby sung only once only works until morning. To finish it, to make it permanent, the last verse had to be carried by a voice the gods already knew. A speaker's voice. His voice. And the price of carrying it was written into the music itself, plain as print: *what anchors you to the country, give up.*

All of it.

*Vesper,* the fox said quietly. *The last verse. It's time.*

Above them, faint through the flooding stone, boots on stairs — Sable's Inquisitors, arriving at the end of everything, too late to stop it and exactly in time to witness it. He thought of her pressed flower. He thought of eleven years of luck-knots. He thought of sparrows gossiping on rooftops, of rats reciting dead languages, of a woman whose face he had measured his days against and would soon, if the song demanded what he believed it demanded, be unable to picture at all.

"Merrin," he called. "Take Wren home. Both of you. Don't look back at the water."

"What about you?" Merrin asked.

Vesper smiled. It was, he suspected, the last smile he would ever wear knowingly.

"I'm going to finish the song," he said. "And then I'm afraid I'm going to forget why I'm crying."

He turned back to the seam — one hand-width of light remaining, glowing over the water like a horizon — drew the deepest breath of his life, and opened his mouth for the final verse.
