## 19. The Price of the Last Verse

The Door had told him the price the way a physician tells you which limb must come off: gently, precisely, and with the terrible courtesy of someone who has done this before.

*The song cannot end while the singer holds anything back,* it had said — not in words, exactly, but in that seam-of-light way it had, meaning arriving whole inside his chest like a swallowed bell. *Give what anchors you. Give all of it.*

Vesper had stood at the water's edge of the drowned chapel, Wren's small hand gripping two of his fingers, Merrin swaying behind them with tide-water still rolling slow in her eyes, and he had thought: *anchors. Plural. That's fine. I'll give the bad ones.* The years of flinching at boots on stairs. The charm-shop ledger of small cowardices. The memory of the first time he'd lied to an Inquisitor's face and how good he'd been at it.

He was, he realized now, standing in the cold shallows with the hymn humming in his ribs, still trying to negotiate.

"You're doing the thing," said the fox.

They sat on the chapel's last dry step, the one that stuck out of the floodwater like a drowned man's knee. The fox's fur was singed along one flank from the cathedral, and its tail curled around its paws with the studied dignity of a creature pretending not to be exhausted. Gods, apparently, could be tired. Nobody warned you about that.

"What thing," Vesper said.

"The bargaining thing. You do it with customers. *Three coppers for the luck-knot, but for you, because I like your hat, two.*" The fox's ears tilted. "You're doing it to yourself."

"I don't have a hat anymore."

"You lost the hat defending a child from an Inquisitor raid. It was a good death for the hat." The fox looked out over the black water, where the Door waited — a vertical seam of pale gold light beneath the surface, wide as a church door, patient as one. "Vesper. Tell me what you're keeping."

And there it was. The question he'd been circling since the crypts, since Sable's hands shook as she turned the key, since she'd said *go, go now, don't look back* and he had looked back anyway, because he was who he was.

"The song needs everything," he said slowly. "Doesn't it. Not just the hiding. Not just the fear."

"The gods folded themselves into this land to sleep," the fox said. "Every stone of Vellichor holds a note of them. To sing them under again, the song has to be *whole* — no held-back verses, no singer standing outside the music. You have to pour yourself into it until there's nothing left standing on the shore." Its voice went quiet in the way it did when it was being gentle, which was worse than when it was being cruel. "All of it. Every year. Every charm. Every name."

Wren was asleep against Merrin's side, worn out from singing the third verse with a voice that had cracked in the middle and kept going anyway. Brave, stupid, magnificent child. Vesper watched her breathe and felt the shape of the price settle over him like snowfall.

"Sable," he said. Just the name. It came out steady, which surprised him.

"Yes."

"She won't remember me either. That's the cruelty of it, isn't it? I lose her, and she loses losing me. She'll just be a woman with a pressed flower she can't explain."

The fox said nothing, which was answer enough.

* * *

He made his list on the back of a charm-wrapping paper, because he was a shopkeeper down to the marrow and a man ought to take inventory before he sold the shop.

*One:* Mother's kitchen, the smell of bread and rosemary, her hands shaping dough while a wren on the sill told him the weather. Gone before the Purge, but he'd carried her twenty years; carrying counted.

*Two:* The first sparrow who ever spoke to him, perched on a fence post outside Corvane, saying *you hear us, you hear us,* like a door opening.

*Three:* Every market morning. The gray light on the stalls. Old Pem's cough. The particular squeak of the third wheel on his cart. Luck-knots and hare's-foot charms and the little tin birds that didn't do anything except be beautiful, which he'd always claimed was the most harmless magic of all.

*Four:* The fox. Seven days of impossible conversation, of archaic Common and dry commentary, of a god wearing feathers of rust-red fur. He would forget the fox too. He suspected the fox knew this and had volunteered anyway, and he decided not to say so out loud, because if he did he might not be able to finish the list.

*Five.*

His pen stopped.

Five was Sable Halloway. Five was a rooftop in the spring before the Purge, both of them nineteen and certain the world would make room for them. Five was her laugh, which she rationed like wartime sugar. Five was the pressed flower — a sprig of lavender, because she'd once said his gift smelled like clean linen and open windows, and he'd never recovered from it. Five was every cold procedure across a market stall, every beautiful lie they'd told each other, every mercy of the question not asked. Five was her turning the crypt key with shaking hands and saying *I reported false findings for six years, you absolute fool, did you think you were good at hiding?*

He hadn't been good at hiding. He'd been loved well. There was a difference, and only one of them had ever saved him.

"You're crying," Wren said. She was awake after all, watching him with those untutored eyes that saw maps where other people saw walls.

"No I'm not."

"You are. It's all right. My gran cried at weddings and funerals same as each other. Said it was the same water either way."

Vesper laughed, wet and short. "Your gran sounds wise."

"She was a liar and a thief and the best person I ever knew." Wren scrambled over and put her small hand flat on the paper, right over the word he hadn't written yet. "You don't have to write her down. Some things you keep even when you give them away. Gran said that too."

"Wren—"

"Merrin says the vessels remember, after. When the gods fold back in, whatever they borrowed gets returned." She said it with the fierce certainty of a child who has decided a thing is true and will defend it with her teeth. "So maybe not gone. Maybe just... lent."

"That's not how it works, love."

"You don't know how it works. Nobody knows how it works. That's why it's magic." She took the pen out of his hand with the authority of a much larger person and drew a lopsided flower next to his list. Lavender, if you squinted. "There. Now it's written."

* * *

The waking gods were coming. He could feel them through the soles of his feet — the drowned chapel shivering, the water in the nave trembling into rings, Merrin's mouth opening without her permission to pour out prophecy in a voice like a landslide given diction:

*HE COMES TO THE DOOR. THE LAST VOICE. STOP HIS SONG. STOP HIS SONG. STOP—*

Merrin clamped her own hands over her own mouth, eyes wild above them, and nodded at him. *Go. Now. While I can still want you to.*

Vesper waded into the water. It came up his shins, his knees, cold as confession. The Door's light lay across the flooded nave ahead of him, gold through black water, and the counter-hymn the fox had taught him rose in his chest on its own, the way a bird rises, the way grief rises, the way all the true things rise whether you invite them or not.

Behind him, the beasts of Corvane had gathered — he could hear them through the broken windows, the sparrows and the sewer rats and the pigeons and the moles and old Pem's cart-horse, hundreds of throats carrying the melody he'd taught them in fragments over three desperate days, passing the notes along like buckets in a fire-line. They couldn't sing the words. They didn't need to. A melody carried by every animal in a city is its own kind of choir.

The fox walked into the water beside him, up to its elbows, undignified and unbothered.

"You could stop me," Vesper said. "You could tell me to run. You're a god. Command me."

"If I command you, it isn't a gift, it's a theft." The fox's amber eyes caught the Door's light. "Besides. I've watched you refuse power for seven years, Vesper Winterbourne. It's the finest thing I've ever seen a mortal do. I wouldn't dream of interrupting the finale."

"And after?"

"After," the fox said, and for the first time since he'd known it, its voice cracked, "after, I will sit on a windowsill somewhere, and a man will offer me a scrap of cheese for no reason he can name, and I will tell him a story about a country that isn't on any map. And some of it will take root. Seeds do that. Even in poor soil." It touched its nose to his wrist, brief as a heartbeat. "Sing, speaker. I'll hold the melody when yours runs out."

Vesper filled his lungs with the smell of floodwater and old stone and lavender he was imagining, and began.

* * *

The first verse was easy. The first verse was the city — Gray Market mornings, the wheel-squeak of his cart, Pem's cough, the sparrows' gossip, the rats reciting dead words in the dark. He sang the years of hiding out of himself verse by verse, and each one left the way a tooth leaves: a moment of awful looseness, then a strange clean space. He sang the flinching and the lying and the practiced smallness, and felt lighter, and hated how light felt.

The second verse was harder. The second verse was the beasts — every conversation, every confidence, the sparrow saying *you hear us*, Wren's hand in his, Merrin's tide-water eyes softening back toward herself. He sang them out and the water around him shivered with returned voices, animals falling suddenly silent all across the city as their language went home to the land, and he understood that he was not only paying a price, he was closing a door behind every word.

The third verse, Wren had sung at the cathedral. This was the fourth, and the fourth was Sable.

He sang the rooftop. He sang the lavender. He sang the cold procedure and the warm history underneath it, the false reports filed for six years, the crypt key turning, her voice saying *go* and her eyes saying something no Inquisitor should ever have been able to say. He sang her laugh, rationed like sugar. He sang the flower pressed between pages she probably told herself was evidence.

And here is the thing nobody tells you about sacrificial clarity: it is clear. Standing in the dark water with his memories pouring out of him like light through a seam, Vesper understood — completely, calmly, for the first time in his life — what he was buying. Not escape. Not safety. He was buying a world where a laundress's son could be sick and simply get better, where a child's gift was a garden and not a death warrant, where love and duty and power could all finally lie down and sleep. He was buying Sable's freedom from a choice that had been eating her alive for seven years. He was buying it with the only coin he had that the gods couldn't counterfeit: himself.

Any merchant worth his stall knows: you don't haggle over the thing you actually came for.

The last of the verse rose in him, and with it the last of him rose to meet it, and he had one moment — one — where he stood on the shore of his own life and looked back at all of it, the whole small remarkable country of being Vesper Winterbourne, and he found, to his enormous surprise, that he wasn't afraid.

*I won't know why I'm crying,* he thought. *But I'll be crying. That counts. Wren's gran would say that counts.*

He sang the final note.

It was not loud. It was the note a mother hums over a cradle, the note a market makes at dawn before the shouting starts, the note under all other notes. The Door drank it. The water lit gold from beneath, then silver, then nothing at all, and across Corvane the hollowed vessels gasped and woke and wept and remembered their own names, and the gods — the vast, hungry, lonely gods — folded down into the stones and soil and roots of the land like great tired birds settling into a hedge, and slept.

The borders dissolved. Somewhere far away, maps redrew themselves in the dark.

And in the flooded chapel, a man stood waist-deep in black water, weeping, with no idea why.

* * *

He heard crying and thought, distantly: someone's hurt.

Then he realized the sound was coming from his own throat, and stopped, confused, and started again, because his body seemed to know something he didn't. His chest ached in the specific way of a room after the furniture's been carried out — every wall echoing with the shape of things no longer there.

"Hello?" he said.

His voice sounded wrong to him. Younger. Unaccompanied. For seven years — no, he didn't know it had been seven years, he didn't know anything, that was the trouble — for as long as he could remember, which was approximately eleven minutes, his voice had been half of a conversation with the whole living world. Now it was just a voice.

A small girl was picking her way along the chapel step toward him, and a woman with kind, exhausted eyes was steadying her, and neither of them looked like strangers, exactly, but he could not have said why.

"You did it," the girl said. She was crying too, and grinning through it, which struck him as the correct way to do things. "You great stupid hero, you *did* it."

"Do I know you?"

The girl's grin faltered, just slightly, and then firmed up into something stubborn. "Not yet," she said. "My name's Wren. I'm going to be your friend. That's not a prediction, it's a decision."

The woman — Merrin, though the name meant nothing to him yet — helped him out of the water, and someone put a dry coat around his shoulders, and in his pocket he found, inexplicably, a handful of small charms: a luck-knot tied in a pattern his fingers recognized and his mind did not, a tin bird that did nothing except be beautiful.

On the chapel step sat a red fox, watching him with an expression of unbearable tenderness, as if it were memorizing him.

"Well met," Vesper said, because it seemed polite.

"Oh, we've met," said the fox. "But don't worry. I remember enough for both of us — and I've got stories. Would you like to hear one? It's about a market, and a sparrow, and a woman with a pressed flower."

Something in his chest turned over, the way soil turns in spring.

"Yes," said Vesper, who did not know why he was already crying again. "I think I would."
