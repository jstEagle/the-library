## 20. A Man Without a Country or a Name

The first thing he knew was the smell of bread.

That seemed important somehow—that it should be bread and not smoke or blood or incense—and he lay very still on the cold cobbles of the Gray Market and let the smell tell him what kind of world he had woken into. A world with ovens in it. A world where someone, somewhere, was having a better morning than he was.

He opened his eyes.

Above him: awnings, gray-gold in early light, strung between stalls that were not yet open. A pigeon on the awning's ridge, regarding him with an expression he could only describe as *devastated*. Not hungry-devastated, the way pigeons usually looked at you. Something worse. Something like grief wearing feathers.

"Morning," he said, because his voice worked, and it seemed polite to confirm it.

The pigeon made a sound like a small hinge and did not leave.

He sat up. His head contained a fog where other things should have been—not pain exactly, but the absence of furniture. He knew what a market was. He knew what bread was, what morning was, what his own hands were. Beyond that, the shelves stood empty, dusted, waiting for goods that had been sold off long ago.

His pockets, at least, had not been emptied.

In the left one: six copper coins, worn smooth. In the right: a knot of braided cord, red and white and green, tied in a pattern his fingers recognized before his mind did. His thumb found the turns of it the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. *Luck-knot,* said some voiceless part of him. *Third braid doubled for safe roads, fifth for safe returns.* He had no memory of learning this. His hands simply knew, the way they knew how to blink.

There were four more knots in the pocket. Each one different. Each one, when he held it up to the light, beautiful in a small, deliberate way—the way things are beautiful when someone made them slowly, for no reason except that making them was the point.

"Well," he said to the pigeon. "Either I'm a craftsman or a thief."

The pigeon cooed, and the coo had a shape to it, and—this was the strange part—he understood the shape. It meant something like *you were kinder than you needed to be* and also something like *please don't go yet.*

"You know me?" he asked.

The pigeon puffed its chest and looked away, which he gathered was a yes, and also that the knowing was painful, and also that pigeons were constitutionally incapable of discussing their feelings before breakfast.

* * *

By midmorning he had learned three things about himself.

One: his name was probably Vesper, because when a fruit-seller's boy tripped over him and shouted "Sorry, mister!" and a woman nearby said "Careful, love, that's Vesper's spot you're standing in," everyone within earshot nodded as if this explained everything. *Vesper's spot.* There was a rectangle of cobbles near the fountain, swept clean, bordered by a folded blanket, where he had apparently conducted some manner of business for years. The blanket was his. The little folding stool was his. The tin cup with the dented rim was his.

Two: he sold charms. This became clear when a fishwife pressed a coin into his hand without asking for anything, saying only, "For the road-knot you made Tam last spring. He swears the ferry didn't sink." He stared at her. She stared back, then frowned. "You feeling all right, Vesper? You look like a man reading a letter he doesn't remember writing."

"I'm fine," he said, which was the first lie he told in his new life, and it came out smooth as water, which worried him more than anything else so far.

Three: the animals of Corvane loved him, and this was a problem.

Not in any way anyone else could see. To the market, he was just a man whom sparrows didn't fear, which earned him a reputation for gentleness and nothing more. But he could *hear* them. All day, from every eave and gutter and windowsill, came a low tide of speech—not words, exactly, but meanings, the way warmth comes off a stone. And every single meaning, from every creature, was some variation of the same unbearable thing:

*You're still here.*

*You stayed.*

*We thought we'd lost you.*

A sparrow landed on his folding stool and looked at him with eyes like wet beads and said, in the way sparrows say things, *You don't remember the rooftops. You don't remember the silence coming. That's all right. We remember enough for both of us.*

"What silence?" he asked, quietly, under the noise of the market.

The sparrow tilted its head. *See?* it said, tenderly, horribly. *Nothing. Never mind. Eat something.*

He ate something. The bread was good. He cried a little while eating it, for no reason he could name, and the sparrow pretended not to notice, and he was grateful to it forever for that.

* * *

In the afternoon, a fox sat down across from his stall and did not leave.

This drew attention. Foxes in the Gray Market drew attention the way fires did. But the fox sat with such composure—with the particular dignity of a creature attending a funeral it had organized itself—that the market gradually decided it must be somebody's dog, badly designed, and went back to its haggling.

"You don't remember me," the fox said.

Its mouth moved. Its voice was archaic and precise, like a book that had learned to breathe. Around them, nobody reacted, which meant either that the fox was speaking into his head alone or that Corvane had seen stranger things this year. Given the mood of the city—the shrines draped in black cloth, the Inquisitors walking in pairs, the way people flinched at church bells—he suspected the latter.

"No," he admitted. "Should I?"

"Yes," said the fox. "No." Its tail curled around its feet. "Both answers are true, which is the sort of thing I'd have explained to you once, and you'd have made a joke about it, and I'd have pretended not to enjoy the joke."

He studied the fox. Red coat, winter-pale at the throat. Eyes the color of deep water over old stones. There was something in those eyes that made his chest ache in a place that had no name—which, he was beginning to understand, was the general condition of his chest.

"Did I know you well?" he asked.

The fox was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, a cart wheel squeaked its one note.

"You knew me better than anyone alive," it said finally. "And then you chose to forget, and it was the bravest thing I ever watched anyone do, and I will never forgive it and I will never stop being proud of it. Both answers are true."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is," said the fox, with feeling. "Welcome to my entire existence."

It stood, stretched elaborately, and looked at him with that devastated tenderness again—the same look the pigeon had, the same look the sparrows wore, the whole animal kingdom united in a single expression of loving him and missing him simultaneously.

"I'm going to come back tomorrow," the fox said. "And the day after. I'm going to tell you stories. You won't believe most of them. Some of them will take root anyway. That's how it works now. That's how we do it."

"Do what?"

The fox began to walk away, then paused, and over its shoulder said, in a voice gone soft as ash:

"Garden."

Then it was gone between the stalls, and he sat with the word in his empty hands like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

* * *

He dreamed that night—in the narrow room above a chandler's shop that he apparently rented, that smelled of tallow and old rope—he dreamed of a garden.

Not a grand one. A crooked one, tucked behind a wall, full of beans and marigolds and one stubborn apple tree. Hands in the dream—his hands, he supposed, though dreams keep their own books—turned soil and tied knots of twine around tomato stems and hummed something. The humming was the heart of the dream. A melody in seven phrases, rising and settling, rising and settling, like breath, like tide, like a lullaby sung by someone who needed the sleeper to sleep more than they needed anything else in the world.

He woke with the melody whole in his mind and tears on his face and absolutely no idea why.

He tried to write it down and couldn't—it slipped through notation like water through fingers—but his body kept it. He caught himself humming it while washing. While counting coins. While watching the moon come up over Corvane's crooked skyline, sitting on his windowsill like a man keeping watch over a city he couldn't remember defending.

Below, in the street, a cat sat looking up at him. Just sitting. Just looking. With that tenderness again, that unbearable, patient tenderness, as if the whole animal world had agreed to take shifts.

"All right," he said aloud, to the cat, to the city, to whatever had happened to him. "I don't know who I was. But whoever I was, everybody seems to have liked him, so I suppose I'll try to be worth the trouble."

The cat blinked slowly, which among cats is a benediction, and padded away.

* * *

She watched him from the far side of the market, three mornings running.

Sable Halloway had gotten very good, over the years, at watching without being seen to watch—a professional skill, once; now a private penance. She stood at the spice stall pretending to compare cinnamon prices she had no intention of paying, and she watched Vesper Winterbourne sell luck-knots to people whose names he didn't know, in a spot he didn't remember earning, with hands that remembered everything he had given away.

He laughed differently now. That was the worst of it. Not worse than the forgetting itself—nothing was worse than that, she had decided, after many sleepless nights of competitive grief—but differently. The old laugh had always had a guard on it, a quick glance around first, humor as camouflage. This laugh was unguarded. Open. The laugh of a man with nothing to hide because there was nothing left hidden *in*.

She should have been glad. She told herself she was glad. She had rehearsed gladness on the walk over, the way he had once rehearsed indifference in front of her, and she suspected she was about as convincing.

On the third morning, a child bought a road-knot from him—paid with a button, which Vesper accepted with the gravity of a banker—and Sable saw him tie the knot wrong. Third braid undoubled. Fifth doubled instead. A harmless error, invisible to anyone who didn't know, and she felt her own hands twitch with the urge to cross the market and show him, *no, like this, double the third, you always doubled the third, you said the third was for coming home—*

She didn't cross the market.

That was the bargain she had made with herself, in the crypts, in the ashes, in the long quiet after: *he paid everything so that we could rest. The least I can pay is to stay away.* Love, she had decided, was sometimes just the discipline of not being known. She was, she reflected bitterly, extremely disciplined.

But she bought cinnamon. Overpaid outrageously for it. And as she turned to go, she let herself look at him one last time—laughing at something a sparrow had done, sunlight catching the dented tin cup, the whole small economy of his remade life running gently on—and she thought:

*Grow something, Vesper. Grow something where nothing was supposed to grow. I'll hear about it. I always hear about everything. It's my one remaining skill.*

Then she walked away, and did not look back, because looking back was a door, and she had promised every god asleep under the earth that she would not open it.

Behind her, unaware, the man without a country or a name pocketed a button, straightened his blanket, and began, without knowing why, to hum.
