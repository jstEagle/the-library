## 22. Garden and Choir

The garden had been a ruin when Vesper found it, which was to say it had been a ruin the way most things in Corvane were ruins: quietly, and with good posture. A collapsed wall along the south side, a dead fig tree that refused, year after year, to admit it was dead. Someone had once grown herbs here—rows of stone-edged beds gone to moss—and someone before that had carved a lintel over the doorway that read, in letters worn nearly smooth: *TEND WHAT GROWS.*

Vesper had taken the place for almost nothing. The landlord, a wheezing man named Cobb who owned half the Gray Market's back lots, had looked at him with something like pity and said, "You sure? Nothing grows back there but memories."

"Memories are cheap," Vesper had said, and hadn't known why the words sat so strangely in his mouth afterward.

That had been in the spring. Now it was spring again, a full turning of the year since he'd woken in the Gray Market with no name he recognized and a pocket full of luck-knots tied in patterns his hands remembered better than he did.

He knelt among the bean rows in the early light, thinning seedlings with a fish knife, and hummed.

He hummed without thinking about it, the way he did everything now. The melody had no words he knew, though sometimes he felt words hovering just behind it, like moths against a window at night—the shape of them there, the sound of them not quite arriving. It was a descending line, mostly. Patient. It sounded, if he was honest with himself, like something meant to put someone to sleep. He'd never examined this too closely. There were a great many things he'd learned not to examine too closely, the way a man learns not to press on a bruise.

The sparrows came first, as they always did. They arrived in a brown rustle along the broken wall and arranged themselves on the fig tree's stubborn branches, and when he reached the third phrase of the melody they went silent—not frightened-silent, but listening-silent, the way a congregation goes quiet. Then came the pigeons, two fat gray ones from the market eaves who had opinions about everything and shared them freely, though of course Vesper couldn't understand a word. And then, as the sun cleared the wall, the rest: a one-eyed cat from the tannery lane, three rats with clean whiskers and civilized manners, a hedgehog who came every morning and said nothing at all, just sat in the mint like a small prickly monk.

They gathered for the humming. That was the strange thing. That was the thing the whole strange year had been built around.

Because here was what Vesper Winterbourne—who was not called Vesper Winterbourne anymore, who was called Ves by the market folk and *that charm fellow* by everyone else—here is what he knew about himself:

He could talk to animals.

Not guess at them. Not read their moods the way a good horseman does. *Talk.* The sparrow perched nearest him, a ragged little female he'd named Button because she was small and held things together, cocked her head and said, "Again. From the top. You rushed the middle part."

"You say that every morning," Vesper said.

"And you rush it every morning." She hopped down onto the bean row and fixed him with one bright eye. "A melody like that deserves patience. It's not a tune you hurry. It's a tune you *mean*."

"It's just something I do," Vesper said, which was what he always said, and which was true in the way that all his explanations were true: technically, uselessly. "I don't even know where I learned it."

Button looked at him for a long moment. The sparrows did that sometimes—all of them did, the whole menagerie of them—they looked at him with an expression he had no better word for than *tenderness*, and it unsettled him more than hostility ever would have. Hostility he could work with. Tenderness implied a debt he couldn't see.

"You'll remember someday," Button said. "Or you won't. Either way, the beans need thinning."

She was right about the beans. She was usually right about everything except the matter of worms, which she insisted were a delicacy out of what Vesper suspected was pure spite toward the pigeons.

He worked through the morning. The garden had come back faster than any garden had a right to—the fig tree had leafed out green in its second summer, to Cobb's open disbelief, and the herb beds produced basil and clary sage and a mint so vigorous it had to be disciplined weekly. Vesper sold charms still, luck-knots and blessing-braids from a stall two streets over, but the garden was where he lived, in the way that mattered. He'd built a lean-to against the standing wall. He cooked on a small iron stove. At night the beasts arranged themselves around the ember-light like a congregation that had never heard of churches, and he hummed them to sleep, and none of them—not one—had ever once tried to explain why they came.

He'd asked, early on. He'd asked Button directly, in those first bewildered weeks: *Why do you gather here? Why me?*

And Button had said, gently, in the way of someone stepping around a hole in the floor: "Because it's quiet here. Because you're kind. Because some doors are better left closed until they open on their own."

He'd thought about that answer for a long time. He'd decided it was a sparrow answer—small, practical, slightly evasive—and he'd let it go. This was another skill he seemed to have arrived with, fully formed: knowing which questions would cost more than the answers were worth.

* * *

The fox came on Thursdays.

Vesper didn't know why Thursdays. The fox didn't explain itself, and Vesper had stopped asking questions that got answered in riddles. The fox simply appeared at the garden gate at dusk—one red flash of it, sitting upright with its tail curled around its feet like a gentleman at a lecture—and it would stay for an hour or two, watching Vesper work or eat or hum, and then it would leave.

It spoke Common. Perfect, archaic, faintly formal Common, the way nobody spoke anymore, and it was the only creature in Corvane that did.

"You've expanded the mint," the fox observed on this particular Thursday, materializing at the gate as if it had always been there. "Aggressive choice."

"The mint made choices of its own," Vesper said. "I'm just negotiating terms." He set down his trowel. "You're late."

"I am never late. Time is late for me." The fox trotted into the garden and settled on the flat stone by the fig tree, its spot, the one all the other animals tacitly ceded. It watched him with amber eyes that were older than they had any right to be. "You rushed the middle part again this morning."

"So I'm told."

"There are perhaps four creatures in this city who know that melody properly," the fox said. "You wrote it. Though you don't remember writing it, and three of the four are asleep, and the fourth is a hedgehog who has never told anyone anything."

Vesper's hands went still in the dirt. This was an old dance between them, this fox and he—a year of Thursdays, a year of remarks dropped like stones into deep water, ripples spreading outward toward meanings Vesper could feel but never quite reach. The fox knew things. The fox knew *him*, somehow, knew him with the terrible familiarity of an old friend, and every time Vesper pressed, the fox gave him exactly enough truth to keep him walking and not one step more.

"Why do you keep coming here?" Vesper asked. It was the Thursday question. He asked some version of it every week.

The fox considered him. "Because someone has to remember," it said. "Remembering is work, Vesper. Someone has to do it."

"I've told you—I don't go by Vesper."

"No," the fox agreed. "You don't."

The name had come to him the way all his fragments had: unbidden, unexplained. He'd woken in the Gray Market a year ago with nothing—no past, no family, no history anyone could verify—and within a month the animals were calling him Vesper, and it had fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. He'd never learned where it came from. When he asked the fox, the fox only looked at him with those old amber eyes and said, "It suits you," which was not an answer, and was somehow worse than no answer at all.

There were other fragments. A woman's laugh, low and dry, that surfaced sometimes near sleep. The smell of candle smoke and ink. A feeling he could only describe as *standing at a door*, though he'd never been able to say what door, or whether he'd been entering or leaving. And grief—that was the strangest fragment of all. A grief with no object. Some mornings he woke with wet eyes and a weight on his chest like a hand, mourning something enormous that had left no shape behind.

Button had a theory. Button's theory was that he'd loved someone and lost her, and that the loss had taken the memory with it, and that this was why the animals looked at him the way they did—with tenderness, and a carefulness, like people moving furniture in a house where someone was sleeping.

Vesper had decided not to have theories. Theories required a past to theorize from.

"You're doing it again," the fox said now.

"Doing what?"

"Standing at the edge of the question. Looking down into it. Then walking away." The fox's ears tilted forward. "One day you won't walk away."

"One day you'll give me a straight answer, and we'll both be surprised."

The fox laughed—it was a strange sound, a barking huff that managed to convey genuine amusement—and for a moment its eyes were not old but ancient, and sad, and fond. "Yes," it said softly. "Perhaps we will."

* * *

She came at the end of the day, in the hour when the light goes long and gold and forgiving.

Vesper was at the gate, tying up the morning's last bundle of sage for the apothecary, when he heard footsteps on the lane outside—unhurried, deliberate, the steps of someone who had walked past this gate many times and finally stopped. He looked up.

A woman stood in the lane. Tall, dark-haired going silver at the temples, dressed in plain traveling clothes that fit her like a uniform fits a soldier—precisely, and with the memory of something else underneath. She carried herself very straight, the way people do when straightness is a discipline rather than a nature. Her face was composed. Her eyes were not. Her eyes were doing something complicated that Vesper had no name for, something like a person looking at a house they grew up in, long after it burned.

"Good evening," she said. Her voice was steady. It had clearly practiced being steady for a very long time.

"Evening." Vesper set down the sage. "Are you lost? The market's back that way, if you're looking for it."

"No." She glanced past him, at the garden—at the fig tree, the mint, the flat stone where the fox sat. Something moved across her face when she saw the fox, something too quick to catch. "I was told there was a garden here. Where the animals gather."

Word traveled; he knew that. Half the market talked about the charm-fellow and his congregation of beasts. Still, something in how she said it—in how she stood there, at his gate, at golden hour, like a pilgrim at a shrine she wasn't sure she was allowed to enter—made the hair rise gently on his arms.

"There is," he said. "Though I couldn't tell you why they come. They don't explain themselves."

"No," she said, and something in her voice cracked, just barely, like ice over water. "They wouldn't."

The sparrows had gone quiet on the wall. All of them, every bird, watching the woman with an attention that felt less like curiosity than like witness. The one-eyed cat had risen from its sunning spot and sat at the edge of the garden, tail wrapped, solemn as a magistrate. Even the hedgehog had emerged from the mint, which Vesper had never once seen it do for a visitor.

The woman noticed too. He saw her notice—the way her breath caught, the way her hand rose halfway to her mouth and then deliberately lowered. As if she'd expected this. As if she'd been told, by someone, exactly what would happen when she came.

"Do I know you?" Vesper asked.

It was the obvious question. It was the question any man would ask, standing before a stranger who looked at him like that—as if he were a letter she'd written and never sent. But asking it hurt somewhere, in some place below memory, the way pressing on the bruise hurt, and he heard his own voice come out gentler than he intended.

The woman was quiet for a moment. When she answered, each word was placed like a stone across a stream. "No," she said. "You don't."

But her eyes said otherwise. Her eyes said *yes*, and *always*, and *you have no idea what you gave*, and Vesper stood in his own gateway with his heart doing something slow and enormous in his chest, grieving something he could not name, for a woman he had never met, in a garden where nothing was supposed to grow.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Sable," she said. Just that. No surname offered, and something in the omission told him the surname belonged to a life she had, in some sense, ended in order to stand here.

"Sable." He tried it. It fit strangely and perfectly, like the name Vesper had fit, like all the pieces of himself he kept finding in other people's mouths. "Well, Sable. It'll be dark soon, and I've soup on the stove, and the mint has gotten entirely out of hand if you're the type who holds grudges against plants." He hesitated. Everything in him hesitated, some ancient caution rising up—but beneath the caution, deeper than it, older than it, something else was speaking, something that had waited a full year behind his eyes. "Stay," he said. "For supper. For—for as long as you like. I don't know why I'm asking, but I find I mind very much what the answer is."

Behind him, on the flat stone, the fox made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob, and said, quietly, in its archaic and patient voice: "Some doors open on their own."

Sable Halloway looked at Vesper Winterbourne—at the man who had forgotten her so that she could be free to be remembered—and she smiled, and it was the smile from the fragment, the low dry laugh from the edge of sleep, the thing he had been mourning without a name for a year.

"Yes," she said. "I think I will."

And in the garden where nothing was supposed to grow, the fig tree—dead these seven years and longer, dead since the gods went silent—put out, in the gold light, unnoticed by either of them, one new leaf.
