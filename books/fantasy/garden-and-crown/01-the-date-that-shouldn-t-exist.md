## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The rain came down the mountain in ropes, and Lior Underhill climbed through it the way her mother had taught her: never straight up, always sideways, letting the stone take half your weight. The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads at all to anyone who hadn't been born to them — they were a rumor of ledges, a suggestion of switchbacks stitched into the cliff's black shoulder, invisible unless you knew where the mountain kept its seams. Lior knew. She had been reading those seams since before she could read letters.

The contraband rode against her spine in an oilcloth roll: forty goose-feather quills, each one tipped with a nib cut from raven bone, each one worth more than a farmer's winter. The Crown had outlawed them eleven years ago, along with every other instrument that wrote too well. A good quill made a scribe dangerous. A scribe who could copy a charter, a will, a writ — that was a scribe who might write something the king hadn't approved first.

Lior didn't much care about politics. She cared about the weight on her back and the drop on her left and the fact that the light was dying faster than the map said it should.

*Faster than the map says,* she thought, and then corrected herself: *faster than I say.* She had drawn this route herself, three seasons ago, in the careful shorthand the Underhills used for their own roads. Sunset at the Hollow Stair by the second bell past noon. She was still two miles from shelter when the sky went the color of wet slate and gave up pretending otherwise.

That was the first wrongness of the night, though she wouldn't understand it until later: the mountain keeping its own time, running ahead of hers, as if the day were eager to be over.

* * *

The waystation crouched in the fold of the valley like something trying not to be seen, which was precisely why it worked. Smugglers had built the Thornfell stations a hundred years ago and more — low stone shelters sunk into hillsides, roofed with living turf, doorless so that no broken hinge would ever announce a visitor. This one had been abandoned longer than Lior had been alive. Moss had taken the walls. Something small had died in the corner recently enough that she wrinkled her nose and stepped around it.

She got a fire going anyway, small and shielded, because wet wool kills slower than cold but just as surely, and because the quills needed dry air if they were to fetch their price. While the peat smoked and hissed, she peeled off her boots, wrung out her stockings, and told herself the ache in her chest was only the climb.

It wasn't only the climb. It had been three weeks since Old Maren had last stood up on his own, three weeks of coughing that sounded like water finding its way through a ruined wall, and the guildhall in Lowmark felt emptier every time she left it. Six Underhills once. Then four. Then two. Now one and a half, and the half was fading.

*After this run,* she promised herself, *I'll hire a physicker from the coast. A real one. We can afford it now.*

She believed herself the way you believe a rope bridge will hold: fully, and while refusing to look down.

She ate hard bread and dried plums, fed the fire, and then, because there was nothing else to do until the rain slackened and boredom is its own kind of hunger, she went looking through the station's old stores. Smugglers leave things behind. It's a superstition almost as old as the roads themselves — you leave a little something in every shelter you use, so the shelter remembers you kindly. Bootlaces. Candle stubs. Once, memorably, a tax collector's signet ring, which Maren had sold for enough to buy the guild a new mule.

The cache niche behind the third stone held a tin cup, a child's carved horse with one leg snapped, and a book.

Lior took it out with both hands, the way you take something that might be hot.

Books were trouble. Books were worse than quills; books were quills that had already done their work. But this one was bound in cracked grey leather gone soft as skin, and it had no lock, no seal, no crown-stamp, and it weighed almost nothing, which meant it wasn't a ledger and probably wasn't worth stealing. Which meant, her instincts said, that it had been left rather than lost.

She opened it beside the fire.

The paper inside was the color of old teeth, foxed at the edges, brittle when she tested a corner between finger and thumb. A century old at least. Maybe older. The handwriting shifted from page to page — different hands, different generations, some entries cramped and hurried, others formal as a court record. Marginalia in three alphabets, one of which she didn't recognize at all. Someone had pressed a sprig of rowan between two pages long ago; it crumbled when she touched it, and she felt oddly guilty, like she'd disturbed a grave.

It was a logbook. Waystation records. Deliveries, mostly — *two bolts grey wool, south road, paid in salt* — the dull heartbeat of a smuggling trade that had run these mountains before the Underhills, before the king, maybe before the war everyone still pretended had ended.

She was turning pages quickly now, skimming, enjoying the strange intimacy of strangers' secrets, when the entries stopped being about wool.

*The bearer comes in the year the river forgets its name.*

*Thirteen turns. Do not let it sleep past the thirteenth turn.*

*We are not guardians. We are jailers who have forgotten what we locked up. God forgive the ones who come after us.*

Lior sat back. The fire popped. Rain needled the turf roof above her with a sound like slow applause.

Superstition, she told herself. Mountain people wrote superstition the way other people wrote prayers — constantly, and about everything. Every clan in Thornfell had its doom-book, its ghost-story, its grandmother's warning. This was somebody's grandmother having a very bad century.

But she turned the pages anyway. Because the hand that had written those lines was steady and deliberate, and because whoever it was had underlined *thirteenth turn* twice, and because the next entry began:

*To the one who finds this in the year of the drowned moon:*

Her breath went shallow without her permission.

*— know that we did not choose you, and neither did the seed choose you, and this is the whole cruelty of it. The choosing is done by the date. By the date alone.*

There it was again. *Date.* Her eyes moved down the page before she decided to let them, the way a foot moves onto ice.

*If you read this on the night written below, then it is you. There is no arguing with it. We tried, in our generation, to argue with it, and the ink would not dry until we wrote the truth.*

Below that, ruled off carefully in a box, as if the writer had wanted it impossible to mistake:

*The ninth night of the Frostwane Moon, in the year the river forgets its name.*

Lior laughed. One short syllable, thrown at the fire like a stone. Because the Frostwane Moon was *this* moon, and tonight was its ninth night — she knew that the way she knew her own hands, because the ninth of Frostwane was her mother's remembering-day, and Lior had burned a sprig of juniper for her at dawn before taking the quill road. And the year the river forgot its name —

Two summers ago, the Ashwater had flooded so violent and so long that when it finally shrank back into its bed, the old ford had moved half a mile downstream and the ferry villages had argued for a season about which stretch of water was even the Ashwater anymore. The river had literally forgotten where it lived. Everyone in the valley joked about it. *The year the river forgot its name.* It was a joke. It was a joke that happened to be true, and someone a hundred years dead had written it in a box.

Her mouth had gone dry. She took a swallow from the tin cup, found it held only rainwater, drank it anyway.

*Fine,* she said aloud, to the empty station, because saying things aloud makes them smaller. *Fine. Coincidence. Dates repeat. Rivers flood. Books lie.*

Then she turned the page, because of course she did, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had never once in her life walked past a locked thing without touching the lock.

The next entry was shorter. Newer-looking, though the paper was the same ancient rag-stock, yellowed and soft. The same steady hand, but pressed harder now, the letters dug into the page like claw-marks:

*Her name is Lior.*

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a long sighing sound, the mountain breathing in its sleep.

*Underhill,* said the next line. *Last of her line, though she does not yet know she is the last. She carries quills on the ninth night of Frostwane and shelters where we sheltered, because the roads remember, even when the people forget.*

Lior did not move. She was not sure she was breathing. The cold had gotten into the station somehow, or into her, a cold that had nothing to do with the weather, rising up through the floor stones the way water rises through chalk.

*The ink will be fresh when you read this,* the final line said. *Do not be afraid of that. Be afraid of what it means: the book is not finished writing.*

She looked down at the page.

The ink was wet.

Not glistening, not dramatic — just faintly dark, faintly raised, the way a line looks ten seconds after the pen has lifted. She touched it with her smallest finger, the gesture absurdly gentle, the way you touch a sleeping animal you hope will stay asleep.

A fine black thread came away on her fingertip. It smeared, slightly, when she rubbed it.

Century-old paper. Fresh ink. Her own name, spelled correctly, in a book that had been hidden behind a stone before her grandmother's grandmother drew her first breath.

Outside, the rain stopped all at once, the way it does high on Thornfell — not fading but simply ceasing, as if the sky had closed a door. In the ringing silence Lior became aware of a second silence underneath it, deeper, coming from everywhere and nowhere, and it took her a long moment to understand what she was hearing, or not hearing.

No wind. No owls. No dripping eaves.

The mountain was holding its breath.

And far below the waystation, far below the roots of the pines, in the dark under the world, something that had been sleeping for a hundred years rolled over — and Lior felt it through the soles of her bare feet, faint as a heartbeat, patient as a debt.

She looked at the book. The book seemed, in the firelight, to look back.

"Who," she whispered, "is the seed?"

The answer was already drying on the next page.
