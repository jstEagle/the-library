## 2. What the Guild Buried

The rain had followed Lior down from the high roads all the way to Thornfell's underbelly, and by the time she reached the guildhall door it was falling in gray sheets that turned the cobbles to a river of drowned moonlight. She stood beneath the leaning eave of the old tannery across the lane, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like something stolen — which, in a sense, it was, though she'd found it fair in an abandoned waystation no one had claimed in forty years.

*The Crownless Bearer.* Her name. Tonight's date. Ink that gleamed wet on paper gone brown as autumn leaf.

She could have thrown it into the canal. She thought about it, standing there, watching the water swallow the lamplight in gulps. She thought about how easy it would be to pretend she'd never opened the cover, never read those lines in a hand that seemed to press hard enough to tear the page, as if the writer had been afraid of being interrupted.

But the book had been waiting for her. That was the part she couldn't shake. Not hidden — *waiting*, laid on a table dustless around its edges, as if everything else in that room had decayed around the one thing that mattered.

Lior Underhill had been raised on three commandments: move quiet, trust nothing, and never carry what you can't put down. She crossed the lane anyway.

* * *

Old Maren was dying the way a fire dies — not all at once but in stages, each one smaller than the last, until what remained was mostly memory and heat. The guildhall's back room smelled of willow bark and tallow and the sweet-rot smell of a body quietly closing its accounts. He lay propped on pallets that had once, Lior knew, served as bunks for smugglers sleeping off a bad crossing, and his hands — hands that had taught hers to pick a lock blindfolded at seven — lay open on the blanket like two shed tools.

"You're soaked," he said without opening his eyes. "And you came through the front door. Both of those are wrong."

"Front door was faster." She hung her dripping cloak on the hook by the stove, an act so domestic it felt obscene with the book burning a hole through the oilcloth against her ribs. "You're supposed to be sleeping."

"I'm supposed to be dead. I'm renegotiating." His eyes opened then, pale as river ice, and found the bundle in her arms before she'd decided whether to show him. Something moved across his face — not surprise. Recognition. And beneath it, something worse: relief, or grief, or both wearing the same coat.

"Where," Maren said slowly, "did you find that?"

"Waystation on the Kell Road. The one we stopped using after the Harrow winter." She set the book on the blanket between them, not yet unwrapping it. "Maren. There's a date in it. Tonight's date. And there's a name."

His breath went thin and whistling. For a long moment the only sounds were the rain hammering the shutters and the stove ticking as it cooled, and Lior understood, with the particular dread of someone watching a lock begin to turn, that whatever came next had been arranged long before she was born.

"Open it," he said. "To the marked page."

She peeled back the oilcloth. The cover was black leather gone green at the edges, stamped with a sigil she knew better than her own heartbeat — the Underhill mark, the hill with the hollow underneath, pressed deep enough that some early owner had wanted it *found*. The pages inside were foxed and fragile, a century old at least, and yet when she turned to the ribbon-marked leaf the ink there sat black and sharp as if set down that morning.

*"On the eve of the thirteenth moon's turning,"* she read aloud, because Maren's eyes told her to, *"the Crownless Bearer shall come into her burden. Lior, last of Underhill. Let her carry the seed through thirteen turns, and let no crown claim it, and let no flame unmake it. What the kingdom buried, the mountain remembers."*

The rain filled the silence after.

"That's my name," Lior said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew she was frightened; fear made her flat, made her precise, made her sound like Maren on his worst days. "In a book older than you. Older than your mother. The ink is fresh, Maren. The ink is *fresh* and the paper is a hundred years dead, and someone wrote me into this thing before I existed, and I want you to tell me that's a trick. A guild test. One last lesson for the last apprentice."

Maren laughed. It cost him; the laugh broke apart halfway through and became coughing, and she held the cup to his lips while he recovered, and neither of them spoke until his breathing steadied.

"No trick," he said at last. "Gods, child, I prayed it would be a trick. I prayed every year on this night, from the time I was younger than you. Every year I checked the book, and every year the page stayed blank past my own name — did you see mine? Third line. *Maren, keeper, who will fail.* Blank below that for fifty years. And now—" He gestured at the page with a trembling finger. "Now it's full, and I'm too weak to do anything but hand it to you."

"Hand *what* to me?" But even as she said it, Lior felt the floor of her understanding give way the way the old rope bridge used to sway over the gorge. "What is this? What has the guild been doing?"

"Guarding." The word came out of him like a stone pulled from a wound. "Four generations of Underhills, guarding a thing most of us never saw and none of us understood. My grandmother kept it before me. Her brother before her. We were smugglers on the surface, girl — quills and salt and uncut garnet, all the small dishonesties that keep a valley fed when the king's taxes don't. But underneath that, underneath everything, we were *keepers*. The guild wasn't founded to move contraband. The contraband was the cover. The guild was founded to make sure one particular cargo never moved at all."

Lior's mouth had gone dry. "The seed."

Something flickered in Maren's eyes — not surprise this time either, but the sharp attention of a teacher whose student has finally done the reading. "You've seen it? Touched it?"

"No. The book mentions it. *Let her carry the seed.*" She looked down at the page, at her own name sitting in century-old ink like a hook set in her flesh. "I don't know what it is. Maren, I don't even know what I'm—"

"Under the floor." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and the effort drained the color from his face so completely that Lior moved to push him back down, but he caught her wrist with shocking strength. "Third flagstone from the hearth. The loose one. There's a reason it's always been loose, Lior. There's a reason you were told to leave it alone and you never once obeyed."

Heat crawled up her neck. "How did you know I—"

"Because you're an Underhill. Because leaving things alone was never in our blood, only in our rules." He released her wrist. "Go on, then. You were always going to. Better it happens while I still breathe to explain it."

* * *

The third flagstone from the hearth had been loose for as long as Lior could remember, tilting underfoot with a hollow knock that every apprentice learned to step around and every apprentice secretly stepped on. She knelt beside it now, working her fingers into the gap, and it came up easier than it ever had — as if the stone itself had decided the hiding was over.

Beneath it: a cavity lined with lead sheeting gone dull as pewter, and in the cavity, resting on a bed of wool rotted nearly to lace, a shape the size of a fist.

It was iron, or looked like iron — dark metal veined with something finer, silver-bright, like frost caught mid-spread across a windowpane. It was shaped like a seed, teardrop-curved, seamless, with no hinge and no seam and no keyhole, closed the way an egg is closed. When Lior lifted it, it was warm.

Not stove-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warmth with a pulse under it, faint and slow, patient as roots.

"It doesn't rust," Maren said behind her. "Four generations in the dirt and it doesn't rust, doesn't cool, doesn't change. My grandmother swore it hummed once, the night the old king died. Nobody believed her. I believe her now."

Lior turned it in her hands. The frost-veins caught the firelight and seemed to hold it a half-second too long. "What's inside it?"

"The old royal line." Maren had dragged himself to the doorway; he leaned there, gray-faced, watching her hold their family's whole buried purpose in one palm. "Not a person. Worse than a person, maybe. Or better. The story my grandmother told: when the last true queen fell, her gardeners — her *witnesses*, she called them — took everything the crown had tried to bury. Every execution without trial. Every village struck from the maps. Every famine engineered and every ledger burned to hide it. They pressed it all into a seed grown from the queen's own orchard, sealed it in iron, and gave it to people who knew how to keep things moving and unseen." His mouth twisted. "They gave it to thieves, Lior. Because thieves know how to hide a thing, and outlaws know why a thing must be hidden, and neither ever wants a throne."

"And now it wants me."

"It doesn't want anything. It *waits*. That's all it's ever done." He slid down the doorframe until he was sitting, too weak to stand through what came next. "Thirteen turns of the moon, the book says. Carry it thirteen turns and something wakes. I don't know what. My grandmother didn't either — only that the keeping ends, one way or another, on the thirteenth turn. Either the seed opens and the truth comes out, or someone takes it and makes it a weapon, or someone destroys it and buries the dead a second time." He met her eyes. "Every path costs. That's the inheritance, girl. That's what we buried under our floor and called a legacy."

Lior looked down at the seed. Its warmth pulsed against her palm, slow, unhurried, utterly certain, and she hated it in that moment with a clean and total hatred — hated the generations who'd passed this weight down like a debt, hated the nameless hand that had written her into a dead book, hated the way the thing fit her grip as though her hand had been measured for it.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why the last apprentice? Why not you?"

"Because the book says *last*, and I am dying, and you are not." Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd ever seen him do. "And because whoever comes for it next won't be family. Word's already moving in the low country, Lior. Riders in the king's colors asking after waystation fires. A man named Ash, they say. Inquisitor. He has the same book you do."

The seed pulsed once against her palm, like a second heart deciding to start.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and somewhere down the valley — too far to hear, close enough to feel — Lior imagined she could hear hooves.
