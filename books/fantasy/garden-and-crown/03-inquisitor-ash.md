## 3. Inquisitor Ash

The valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet pine when Inquisitor Ash rode down into it, and he thought, as he always thought on arriving at some forgotten fold of the king's country: *here is a place that has learned to lie to itself.*

He came with twelve riders in the grey livery of the Crown's Inquiry, their cloaks beaded with the drizzle that had followed them three days down from the high passes. Behind them, on a mule, traveled an iron brazier, a coiled length of oiled cord, and a locked case of instruments whose purposes the muleteer had never been told and had wisely never asked about. Ahead of them, at the bottom of the switchbacks, lay Thornfell: a scatter of slate roofs, a market square gone muddy with autumn, and above it all — if one knew where to look, and Ash always knew where to look — the blind windows of the old smuggler roads stitched through the mountainside like seams in a coat.

He did not announce himself. He never announced himself. Announcement was for men who needed permission.

The first waystation burned before noon.

It stood a mile east of the village, a low stone shelter built into the shoulder of the fell, its hearth still warm when Ash's riders kicked open its door. Inside they found the leavings of recent passage: a straw pallet pressed into the shape of a small body, a clay cup with tea scum dried at its rim, and — this was what made Ash crouch and go very still — quill shavings. Feathers trimmed for fletching, scattered like snow across the flagstones. Contraband quills. The Underhill trade.

"Someone slept here last night," said Sergeant Vael behind him, a big man with a voice like gravel poured slow.

"Someone slept here *this morning*," Ash corrected. He touched the cup. Cold, but not long cold. He looked at the shape in the straw — narrow shoulders, slight frame — and filed it away without yet knowing why. "A runner. Small. Careful." He lifted one white feather shaving between two fingers and held it to the grey light. "Careful people leave nothing. This one left everything, which means she was hurrying."

"You say she."

"The straw says she. The stride says she." He rose, brushing ash from his knee. "Burn it."

Vael did not hesitate; that was why Ash kept him. They stacked the pallet against the lintel, fed the oiled cord into the hearth, and by the time they were back on the road the waystation's single window glowed like a baleful eye and black smoke stood up straight in the windless air, a pillar visible, Ash calculated, from every farmstead in the eastern half of the valley.

Let them see it. Let them understand that the king's eye had opened over Thornfell, and that it did not blink.

* * *

They took the village inn at dusk, quietly, without breaking anything, because Ash preferred his terror served neat rather than diluted with noise. He sat the innkeeper down in his own best chair, set the iron brazier glowing at the man's elbow, and asked him questions in the gentle voice he saved for exactly this purpose — the voice of a man discussing weather, or grain prices, or the health of the innkeeper's wife, who was listening from the kitchen doorway with a wooden spoon gripped like a knife.

"A book," Ash said. "Old. Leather gone dark with handling. Bound in three sections, the middle one water-stained. It will have passed through this valley within the last generation, perhaps the last year. Perhaps," and here he let the pause stretch until the fire ticked in the brazier, "perhaps it passed through last night."

"I don't—" The innkeeper's throat worked. "We're honest folk here, lordship. We keep our heads down. Whatever the Underhills—"

"Ah." Ash smiled, and watched the man flinch as though something had bitten him. "So you *do* know the name."

The silence that followed had weight. Ash let it sit. Around him his riders had arranged themselves with practiced idleness — two by the door, one turning the innkeeper's good knife over on the table, one feeding the fire — and the arrangement did what arrangements do: it reminded everyone present that the room belonged to someone else now.

"The Underhills are finished," Ash said at last, conversationally. "You know this. Their guildhall stands empty, their master is dying, their roads run with no one but ghosts and one last apprentice who has lately become very interesting to the Crown. I am not here to punish Thornfell for tolerating them. I am here for a book, and for the person carrying what the book describes. Help me find her, and I will burn nothing else in your valley. Withhold help—" he glanced at the brazier, mildly, the way a man glances at a clock, "—and I will be forced to conclude that Thornfell has chosen a side."

"We haven't seen anyone," the innkeeper whispered.

"No," Ash agreed pleasantly. "Not yet. But you will. She'll come through here — runners always come through the villages eventually, for food, for dry boots, for the illusion of being ordinary. When she does, send word to my camp above the mill. And in exchange..." He slid a small purse across the table, heavy enough to make its own argument. "Thornfell keeps its roofs."

The innkeeper stared at the purse as if it might hatch. His wife, in the doorway, had gone the color of tallow. Neither of them would meet the other's eyes, and Ash read that fluently: *they know something, or they suspect, and each is afraid the other will give it away.* Good. Fear was a lever, but fear *between* people was a crowbar.

He left the purse anyway. Bribes bought more when they were taken under threat than when they were earned in comfort.

* * *

That night, alone in his tent above the mill, Ash unrolled the copy.

It was not the original — the original had been lost, or hidden, or eaten by the decades, depending on which of his predecessors' reports one believed — but a transcription made eighty years ago by an inquisitor named Corvane, in a hand so cramped it looked like the writing was trying to escape the page. Ash had read it perhaps two hundred times. He could recite passages the way other men recited prayers, and in truth that was what they had become: the only liturgy he kept.

*When the moon makes her thirteenth turn upon the appointed night, the Crownless Bearer shall carry the seed through the dark of the world, and what sleeps beneath the mountain shall wake to judge the crown.*

And beneath, in Corvane's own ink, added like a man scrawling a warning to posterity:

*The date given is precise. The name given is precise. I have checked both against the parish rolls and found no match — no child born bearing it, none christened, none recorded. Either the prophecy names a person not yet born, or it names a person the records have been made to forget. I pray it is the former. I have seen what the crown does to make records forget.*

Ash ran his thumb along that line. Eighty years ago Corvane had prayed it was the former. Ash did not pray, but if he had, he would have prayed the opposite. A person not yet born was a problem for the future. A person the records had been made to forget was a problem *now*, walking around with legs and a name and, as of some days ago, according to his informants out of the high country, a certain iron object wrapped in oilcloth against her heart.

*Crownless Bearer.* He turned the phrase over. Names in prophecies were rarely kind. *Bearer* meant burden-bearer; *crownless* meant either lacking a crown or refusing one, and history suggested that the second sort were far more dangerous than the first. Kings could be bought, flattered, frightened, replaced. But a woman who wanted no throne — a woman who could not be bought with the only currency the Crown minted — that was a flaw in the architecture of order itself.

And order, Ash believed, was the only thing standing between the living and the dark. He had grown up in the border wars; he had seen what the valleys looked like when law withdrew, the ditches full and the churches full of smoke. Men like Vessik — dead these sixty years, thank all mercies — had flourished in that dark. If a seed, a book, a girl, whatever mixture of rumor and miracle this was, threatened to crack the crown that held back that darkness, then it was Ash's work to stop it. Not because he loved the king. The king was a mediocre man in a heavy chair. But because the alternative to imperfect order was perfect ruin, and Ash had buried enough of the ruined.

Still. There was the matter of the straw.

He lay back on his cot and let the image come again: the small pressed hollow, the hurried cup of tea, the feathers scattered like a confession. Somewhere up those black slopes, a young woman was running through roads her family had cut into the mountain generations ago, carrying something the oldest text in the kingdom had promised she would carry, on the night the text had promised she would carry it. She had not chosen this. That was the part that pricked at him, the splinter under the nail of his certainty. He had interrogated fanatics and found them almost restful — a fanatic's mind was a closed room, easy to search. But this one, by every report, was just a smuggler. An apprentice. Someone's dying master's last hand on the wheel.

She would believe she had a choice. That made her dangerous. That also, Ash admitted in the privacy of his own skull, made her worth meeting.

Outside, the rain had stopped and the moon stood clear over the fells — waxing, he noted, counting without meaning to. First turn past new. Twelve to go.

A horse sounded on the track below: one of his riders returned early from the village, dismounting hard. Ash pushed open the tent flap. It was the young one, Pell, breath steaming, face lit with the ugly excitement of a man bringing news he thinks will please his master.

"She's been seen," Pell gasped. "Mill road, an hour past, heading for the high passes. Small, dark-haired, moving like she knows every stone. The innkeeper's boy swears it's the Underhill girl herself."

Ash stood a moment in the cold, looking up at where the passes went black against the stars, and felt the familiar settling in his chest, the hunter's calm, the click of a lock finding its hasp.

"Then we've stopped chasing a name," he said, "and started chasing a person. Wake the others. We ride at first light — and Pell." He caught the boy's sleeve as he turned. "No fires tonight. No burning. From here on we move quiet. I want to come up behind her, not ahead of her. A cornered animal tells you nothing. A hunted one, though—" he smiled into the dark, "—a hunted one leads you to everything she loves."

Above the valley, thin as a paring, the moon began its second turn.
