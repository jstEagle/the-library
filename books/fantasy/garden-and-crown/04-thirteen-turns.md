## 4. Thirteen Turns

The seed rode against Lior's sternum like a second heart.

She had sewn it into a leather sling beneath her coat, the way smugglers carried the most dangerous cargo — not hidden where no one would look, but hidden where only she could feel it. Every step up the goat-track above Thornfell, she felt its weight, small as an apple and dense as sin. Iron, Tam had called it. But iron did not warm. Iron did not *pulse*.

Three days since the guildhall. Three days since Master Corvin had died with her hand in his and the book's last secret in his mouth: *Thirteen turns of the moon, girl. Count them. Never stop counting.*

She counted now. The moon hung over the eastern ridge, three nights past new — a thin paring of silver, like a nail-clip from something larger. First turn done, then. Or was the counting from the night she'd found the book? From the night the seed left the floor? The prophecy had not been generous with its arithmetic, and Lior had spent two days learning why that mattered more than anything else in the world.

The waystation at Crag's Hollow had been abandoned since before her mother's time, but its hearth still drew, and its shutters still held, and no one who wasn't running would climb this high for a view of nothing. She let herself in through the broken back wall, checked the old sightlines out of habit — the switchback below, the tree line, the grey scar of the king's road winding through the valley like a vein someone had forgotten to close — and then built a fire small enough to hide in her cupped hands if she had to smother it fast.

Tea first. Then the book.

* * *

The Thornfell Prophecies were bound in hide she preferred not to think about, and the pages had gone the color of old teeth. A century old, Corvin had said. Older. And yet when she turned to the page she had marked — the one with her name, her *name*, inked before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath — the letters still gleamed wetly when the firelight caught them, as though the scribe had stepped away only moments ago to warm his hands.

*Crownless Bearer,* the page read. *Seed-bearer. She who carries what must not be planted and must not be kept.*

Lior read it again. Then again, mouthing the words, because there was a trick to prophecy she'd learned from Tam's ledgers: the trap was never in what was written. It was in what was *not*.

*Must not be planted.* Fine. She hadn't planted it. She didn't even know how one planted a thing like this — did you dig? Did you just set it down and apologize?

*Must not be kept.* There it was. The door with no handle. Because keeping it was the entire task. Thirteen turns, Corvin had said. Carry it thirteen turns and something wakes. Surrender it early and —

She flipped forward, hunting through the cramped script, past diagrams of moons and mountains, past a drawing of a tree that made her skin crawl for reasons she couldn't name, until she found the passage she'd been avoiding since Crag's Hollow's chimney stopped smoking.

*And should the Bearer yield the seed to any hand — crown or wolf or grave — before the count is done, the buried years rise unbound, and the war that slept shall wake wearing new flesh.*

The war that slept. Everyone in the valley knew the shape of that sentence without knowing they knew it. The Old War, the teachers called it, back when there were teachers. The war that ended without ending — the king's grandfather had won it on paper, and paper was where it stayed won. Whole villages had burned. Whole *names* had been struck from the rolls. And somewhere beneath the ice of the high passes, they said, the war's worst captain had been buried alive rather than killed, because killing him hadn't taken.

Vessik. Even the name was a superstition mothers used to frighten children off the glaciers.

Lior sat back and looked at the fire and made herself say it plainly, the way you'd price contraband.

If she kept the seed, something woke at the end of thirteen turns. If she gave it up — to Ash and his inquisitors, to the king, to anyone — the war woke anyway, sooner, and angrier. There was no path in the book where nothing happened. The prophecy was not a warning. It was a *bill*, and every road led through payment, and the only choice she'd been given was which debt came due and when.

"I didn't ask for this," she told the fire.

The fire, being a fire, said nothing. But the seed against her chest pulsed once, slow and deliberate, like something turning over in its sleep, and she got the distinct impression it disagreed.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments. In the gaps between them, she worked.

Tam had taught her cipher-work on rainy afternoons in the guildhall cellar — *a smuggler who can't read a ledger can be robbed by one* — and the prophecy's margins were full of marginalia, older hands annotating older readings. Her predecessors. Other Underhills, other bearers, generations of them, each leaving notes like climbers driving pitons into a cliff face for those who came after.

*Counted from the dark moon following the finding,* wrote one hand, faded brown. *Not from the reading. Not from the taking.*

Lior's blood went cold and then colder. The finding. She had found the book four nights ago. The dark moon — the new moon, the moon that wasn't there — had been the night before that. Which meant the count had begun *before she knew it existed*. The clock had started without her consent, and she'd already spent five of her turns fumbling in the dark like a thief in a house she thought was empty.

Five turns gone. Eight remained.

She did the rest of the arithmetic by rote, because the numbers needed doing and her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Thirteen turns from that moonless night fell on — she counted knuckles, counted twice — the feast of Saint Aldery, midwinter's edge, when the whole valley would be drunk and loud and every road watched by men collecting feast-taxes. Of course. Of *course*. Prophecy had a smuggler's instinct for the worst possible timing.

Eight turns. Two hundred yards of vertical to the high passes. Ash somewhere behind her — she'd seen the smoke of burned waystations for two days now, a chain of little funeral pyres marching up the valley, each one closer than the last. And ahead, above the snowline, things the book hinted at with a reluctance that was itself a warning.

*The wolf under the ice dreams of the seed,* one margin note read. *Every turn of the moon it wakes a little more. By the thirteenth it will not need to dream.*

She banked the fire until it was an eye rather than a blaze, wrapped herself in her cloak, and lay staring at the rafters while the wind worried the shutters like a dog with a bone.

Against her chest, the seed pulsed. Pulse, pause. Pulse, pause. She matched her breathing to it without meaning to, and hated herself for the comfort it gave.

"You're not a heart," she whispered. "You're a ledger. Everything the crown ever buried, Corvin said. Every name struck from the rolls." She swallowed. "So whose heartbeat am I carrying?"

The pulse quickened, just slightly, and Lior closed her eyes and pretended very hard that she hadn't asked.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and mean, the kind of light that showed everything honestly and forgave nothing. Lior stood at the waystation's broken wall, chewing the last of her smoked eel, and looked north along the ridge line toward the passes.

That was when she saw the crows.

They rose off the slopes below in a ragged black sheet, hundreds of them, wheeling up from a point on the old king's road where no carrion should have been. Something had died down there. Something large, or several somethings, and the smoke rising after the crows was not the thin grey of a campfire but the fat black roll of a burning wagon.

A supply train. Or a patrol. Either way, someone was killing the king's men in the valley, and Ash would blame her for it whether she'd done it or not, and the roads below were about to fill with soldiers who had stopped asking questions and started collecting heads.

Lior looked at the smoke. Looked at the passes. Felt the seed pulse against her chest — faster now, eager, like a dog scenting something on the wind — and understood, with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, that whatever was waking under the ice had started walking.

Eight turns. She touched the leather sling once, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it still hurt.

Then she shouldered her pack, turned her back on the burning road, and started climbing.
