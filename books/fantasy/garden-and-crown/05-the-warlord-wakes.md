## 5. The Warlord Wakes

The ice had kept him the way a miser keeps coin: jealously, and with no intention of spending.

Vessik did not dream, exactly. What he had was older than dreaming — a slow turning in the dark, like a stone rolled by a river that no longer existed. For a hundred years he had been a rumor the mountain told itself. He had been cold down to the marrow of names. He had forgotten the taste of bread and remembered only the taste of orders, which was iron and ash.

Then something opened, far above him, and the dark grew a seam of light.

It came through the ice like a blade through silk — not warmth, not yet, but *attention*. Vessik had felt attention before: on battlefields, from kings who wanted things done quietly, from priests who wanted them done loudly. This was different. This was old. It fell through forty fathoms of glacier the way rain falls through a roof that has finally rotted, and it touched him, and it knew his name.

*Vessik.*

He had not heard his own name spoken aloud since the day they buried the war. His soldiers had chanted it once — *Vessik, Vessik, the mountain's own* — before the crown's fire took the valley and the ice took him. Now the name arrived without a mouth behind it, without breath, and he understood that whatever called him was not calling for a man. It was calling for what remained when a man is used up entirely.

He turned toward it. It took him a century to turn; it took him no time at all. Under the ice, those are the same thing.

The light widened. And Vessik, who had commanded ten thousand spears and betrayed three kings and wept exactly once, at a village whose name he had ordered struck from every map, felt something crack in his chest that was not ice.

*Come,* said the light. Or perhaps it said nothing, and he merely understood, the way one understands a wound. The seed had opened its eye. Somewhere above, in the world of roads and taxes and small frightened men, a thing the crown had buried was breathing again — and everything else the crown had buried was being asked to breathe with it.

Vessik breathed.

The glacier groaned. Not the ordinary complaint of moving ice, which he had listened to for a hundred years the way other men listen to crickets, but a deeper sound, structural, the sound of a promise being broken. A fissure ran past him like a blue vein. Through it he saw the first light not made of memory: grey, thin, honest dawn-light, sliding down into his tomb.

His hands moved. They were wrong hands now — the flesh long gone to the glacier's patience, the skin the color of deep lake water, veined with frost that glittered when he flexed his fingers. But they obeyed. That was all a soldier ever needed of a body, and Vessik had never asked more of anything than obedience.

He stood. The ice that had held him parted around his shoulders like a court parting for a king, or like a crowd parting for something they were afraid to touch. He put one hand against the fissure's edge and pushed, and forty fathoms of mountain's memory gave way with a sound like the end of an argument.

He climbed out into a world that had gone on without him, which he considered rude.

* * *

The valley below the glacier had been a battlefield once. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Here the crown's pikes had broken on his shield-wall; there, in the fold of the hill, he had burned the supply train and watched the smoke make a black flag against the sunset. The bones were still in the ground. He could feel them the way a blind man feels a wall — a pressure, a presence, ten thousand small attentions that were not quite dead enough.

They stirred when he passed.

Not rising — not yet, not as flesh. But the frost on the stones thickened where he walked, and the wind coming down the glacier carried a sound almost like breathing, almost like a camp at night, almost like ten thousand men murmuring *Vessik, Vessik* in their sleep. He stopped at the crest of the hill where his standard had flown and looked out over the valley, and the valley looked back at him with the eyes of everything buried in it.

"Good," he said. His voice came out ruined, a hinge unused for a century, but it carried. "You remember me. Good."

The wind answered with the smell of old smoke.

He went down into the living world carefully, because a warlord returning after a hundred years must first learn what kind of world he is returning *to*. He found a shepherd's hut, empty, its fire still warm — the shepherd had run at the sound of the glacier breaking, which showed sense. Vessik ate what there was: hard cheese, black bread, an apple gone soft. The apple nearly undid him. A hundred years without sweetness, and then this bruised, dying little fruit, and he sat on the shepherd's stool with juice running down his frost-blue chin and shook, just slightly, like a man remembering he had once been young.

So. The world still grew apples. The world still lit fires. There would still be men who wanted what they could not have, and women who had lost what could not be returned, and children grown cruel in the gaps left by dead parents. There would still be the disaffected. There were always the disaffected. A kingdom was simply a machine for manufacturing them.

And somewhere in that kingdom, a seed was waking, and it had called *him*.

He did not yet know what the seed wanted. That troubled him less than it should have. In his experience, powerful things rarely wanted anything good, and good was never what he had been for. He had been for winning. He had been for the mountain clans when the lowland crown decided the mountains belonged to it, and he had won and won and won until the crown did the one thing he had never planned for: it stopped fighting him and started erasing him. Burned the villages that fed him. Struck the names from the maps. Made his war so unwinnable that his own soldiers' grandchildren would grow up not knowing it had happened.

That was the crown's true weapon, Vessik thought, cracking the apple's last sweet flesh between teeth that ached with cold. Not pikes. Not fire. *Forgetting.* You cannot rally the dead if no one remembers there were dead.

But something up there remembered. Something had kept the ledger.

* * *

He found the first of them three days later, in a mining town the maps had also learned to ignore: Greyhollow, a scar of slate roofs under the mountain's shoulder, where the mine had killed forty men in a collapse the crown's assessor ruled an act of the mountain, no compensation owed, case closed, next.

The tavern was called the Broken Pick, and it was full at midday, which told Vessik everything about the mine.

He stood in the doorway letting the cold roll off him, and the room went quiet the way a room does when a wolf walks in — not all at once, but in a spreading ring of silence. Frost crawled off his shoulders and smoked in the hearth-warmth. His eyes, he knew, were the worst part: pale as glacier light, and old, and looking at each of them as if taking inventory.

"You lost forty men," he said. "Under the east shaft. Four years ago."

A woman near the fire stood up slowly. She had a miner's forearms and a widow's face, and she did not reach for the knife at her belt, which showed more courage than reaching would have. "Who told you that?"

"The mountain." He let that sit. Let them decide whether he was mad. Mad men were useful too. "The mountain keeps what falls into it. I was in the mountain a long time. I heard them."

"Liar," someone said from the back, but weakly, the way men say *liar* when they are afraid the word isn't true.

"I heard them counting," Vessik went on, and his ruined voice dropped into the register he had once used to hold ten thousand men steady before a charge. "Forty. Over and over. Forty, forty, because no one else would count them. No king. No assessor. No priest. Just the mountain, keeping your dead the way the crown would not."

The widow's face changed. It was the change he had built his whole first life on: the moment a grievance stops being private and becomes a banner. He had seen it on ten thousand faces. It never got old. It was, he thought with something like tenderness, the oldest crop in the mountains, and it grew best in soil the crown had salted.

"What are you?" she asked.

"A soldier," Vessik said. "Buried by the same crown that buried your husbands. Dug up by something that intends to settle accounts." He looked around the room, at the hollowed faces, the unclaimed griefs, the anger with nowhere to go that curdled daily into drink. "I am gathering the counted and the uncounted. Those who were erased. You may come, or you may stay, and either way the mountain will remember you now. That much I can promise. I am its witness as much as its prisoner."

He turned to go, because a hook sinks deeper if you let the fish swim with it.

"Wait." The widow's voice. "Settle accounts how?"

Vessik smiled, and frost cracked along his cheekbones, and the hearth-fire guttered though no door had opened.

"There is a seed," he said, "that remembers everything the crown buried. Every name struck from the maps. Every village burned. Every verdict bought." He let the silence stretch, let them lean in — and they leaned, God help them, they leaned. "And I intend to be holding it when it finishes waking. Whoever holds the record holds the truth. And whoever holds the truth—" his pale eyes swept the room, gathering them like grain, "—holds the future. Kings used to kill for less."

He walked out of the Broken Pick into the falling snow, and behind him he heard the benches scrape, and boots, and voices — first two, then six, then a dozen — following.

By the time the moon set over Greyhollow he had thirty of them, armed with picks and hatchets and one ancient spear some grandfather had hidden from the disarmament, and the mountain road north was filling with others drawn by rumor: charcoal-burners whose woods the crown had claimed, smugglers whose guilds had been taxed to death, a defrocked priest who wept when he saw Vessik's face and said he'd dreamed it, dreamed it a hundred times, a man of ice walking out of the white with a legion behind him.

Vessik let them believe what they needed to. He had always been generous with belief; it cost nothing and bought everything.

Only at night, alone at the head of his growing column, did he admit what the light under the ice had actually shown him — not a throne, not a banner, but a sliver of green in the dark, and within it, impossibly, a face. Young. Female. Smuggler's clothes, smuggler's wary eyes, and around her neck on a cord, a small sealed weight of iron that shone like a buried sun.

She was carrying it. She was running. And she was, the light had shown him clearly, going to refuse him.

He did not know how he knew that last part. He only knew it the way he knew the weight of a spear: completely.

Vessik walked north through the snow with his legion of the counted growing behind him, and for the first time in a hundred years, he was afraid — not of the crown, not of dying, but of a girl with a seed who might do the one thing no king had ever done with the truth.

Give it away.
