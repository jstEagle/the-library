## 6. Road of Thieves

The Greywater clans met at the drowned chapel of Saint Vellum, where the river had swallowed the nave a generation ago and left only the bell tower standing like a broken tooth above the water. Lior came to them the way her guildmaster had taught her to come to anyone with power and no honor: openly, unarmed, and carrying something they wanted more than her blood.

She waded the last hundred yards through thigh-deep water, the iron seed wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, its weight familiar now as her own heartbeat. The prophecy book she had left hidden in a hollow oak three miles back, because some things you did not show even to allies. *Especially* to allies.

They were waiting for her in the tower's flooded crypt, thirty-odd figures on the stone shelves where coffins had once lain, lanterns hooded so that only their hands and eyes showed. The Greywaters, the Marrowkin, the Tallow Street Rats — three clans who had spent forty years picking at the Underhills' routes like crows at a dying ox, and who had sung songs about it, crude ones, that Lior had heard since she was six years old.

"You're late," said a voice from the highest shelf.

"I'm careful," Lior said. "There's a difference."

A lantern unhooded. The woman holding it was old in the way river people got old — bent by cold water, face like driftwood — but her eyes were quick and mean and amused. Sera Quill, matriarch of the Greywaters, who had once spat on Lior's guildmaster's grave before the ground had even settled.

"Little Underhill," Sera said. "Come crawling to the carrion birds."

"I came to trade," Lior said. "Carrion don't trade. They wait. I'm offering you first pick."

That bought her silence, which was worth more than welcome. She climbed onto an empty coffin shelf — deliberately, letting them see she wasn't afraid of the dead or the living — and unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to show the seed's dull gleam.

Thirty pairs of eyes fixed on it. She felt their attention like heat.

"The king's inquisitors burned the Millbrook waystation two nights ago," she said. "And the Hollowford cache. And they took Tam Wren's boys alive." She let that sit. Everyone knew what happened to smugglers who went into Ash's questioning rooms; what came out was usually quieter, and never quite themselves. "Every route your clans use runs through territory he's about to close. You need what I have: the Underhill maps. Every blind path, every bribe ledger, every name on every gate guard from here to the coast. Forty years of my guild's work. I'll give it to whichever clan gives me safe passage east."

"And what do we get for not simply taking it?" asked a Marrowkin man with a voice like gravel in a sack.

Lior smiled, and was surprised at how easily it came. "Because the maps are keyed," she lied smoothly, "to my hand. Copy them wrong and they lead you into inquisitor camps. Because there are four other copies, held by friends who'll know if I don't reach the coast. And because—" she lifted the seed a fraction, "—this is worth more than all of it, and I'm not selling this. Which means whoever helps me is buying loyalty, not goods. Loyalty's rarer. It holds value longer."

Sera Quill laughed then, a dry rustle like reeds. "Oh, you're your master's whelp after all. He talked like that too, right up until the end." Her amusement cooled. "The Underhills are done, girl. Dying guild, dead routes, one apprentice carrying a pretty trinket the whole kingdom wants. Why should any of us spend blood on a corpse?"

"Because corpses don't pay debts," Lior said. "But I remember who does." She looked around the crypt, meeting eyes through the lantern haze. "When the famine came to Lowmarsh, eleven years ago, whose grain barges ran the closed river? Whose silver bought the medicine when the crown quarantined the town and let them rot? You did. All three clans. My guild kept your names off the king's lists, took the blame ourselves when the Harbormaster demanded someone hang. We gave you those years. I'm asking you to give me one road."

Silence again, but a different quality of it now. She watched old faces shift, memories moving behind them like fish under ice. The famine. Everyone over thirty in this valley remembered the famine.

It was a young Marrowkin woman who spoke first — sharp-faced, maybe Lior's own age, with a burn scar down one arm. "I was in Lowmarsh," she said quietly. "My brother ate bark that winter. Your guild's barges came upriver in fog, flying no colors, and nobody ever knew who to thank." She looked at Sera. "I say we hear her terms."

* * *

They argued until the river outside went grey with dawn. In the end it was settled the way smugglers settled everything: with shares. The Greywaters would guide her through the fens to the old drover roads; the Marrowkin would provision her and hold the false trail west; the Tallow Street Rats would pass word ahead through their web of beggars and tinkers. In exchange, Lior surrendered the Underhill ledgers — the real ones, though she kept the prophecy's location locked behind her teeth — and swore a thief's oath on salt and river water, which was the only oath any of them trusted.

What surprised her was not the bargain. It was what came after.

They fed her. That was the thing she hadn't expected — sat her down in the crypt among the coffin shelves and passed around hard bread and smoked eel and a bottle of plum brandy that had been hidden from three separate tax assessments, and suddenly she wasn't a bargaining counter anymore but a person at a table. The burn-scarred Marrowkin woman was called Hess. She sat beside Lior and refilled her cup without being asked.

"You look like you haven't slept in a week," Hess said.

"I haven't slept well in a month."

"Bad dreams?"

"Worse than dreams." Lior turned the cup in her fingers. "Do you know what it's like to find out your whole life was arranged by people who died before you could ask them why?"

Hess considered this with the seriousness she seemed to bring to everything. "My mother taught me letters in secret because teaching girls was against the mill owner's rules. She died when I was twelve. I've been angry at her and grateful to her ever since, sometimes in the same breath." She shrugged. "Maybe that's just what family is. A debt you didn't sign for."

"A burden," Lior said.

"A load," Hess corrected. "Burden's a load with no purpose. A load you carry somewhere can be a gift, if you ever get there." She tapped Lior's chest, lightly, right where the seed lay wrapped. "You getting there?"

"I don't know where *there* is yet."

"Then keep walking till you find out." Hess raised her cup. "To the Underhills. Dead guild, living whelp."

Around the crypt, cups rose — grudgingly at first, from the older ones, then more freely. *To the Underhills.* Lior drank and found her throat unexpectedly tight. For twenty years her guild had been a shrinking house of secrets, a dying man coughing in a dark room, and she had thought that was all belonging felt like: small, and frightened, and ending. This was different. This was loud and disreputable and smelled of eel smoke, and half these people had cursed her name a year ago, and still they raised their cups.

*This is what he wanted for me,* she thought, and grief and gratitude arrived together, twins she would apparently be carrying for the rest of her life.

* * *

They moved at dusk, the way all honest thieves move. The Greywater guides took her through the fens by paths that existed only at low water, wading and poling flat-bottomed skiffs through channels fringed with dead reeds, while the Marrowkin lit decoy fires ten miles west and let the valley believe the fugitive was hiding in the high caves. For three days it worked beautifully.

On the third night they made camp on a shingle bar where the river widened, and Lior slept for the first time without dreaming of ink, or fire, or her guildmaster's failing breath.

She woke to Hess shaking her shoulder, hard, in full darkness.

"Get up. Get up *now*."

Lamps were coming along the far bank. Not smuggler lamps — those moved low and shaded. These burned high and steady, in disciplined lines, the way soldiers carried light. And at the head of the column, unmistakable even across black water, rode a figure in a grey coat whose presence made the air itself seem to tidy itself, the way a room does when the magistrate walks in.

Ash.

"How?" Lior's mouth had gone dry. "The trail west—"

"It was good," Hess said grimly, hauling the skiff toward the current. "Too good. Someone fed him the real route." She pushed off from the shingle and the current took them, but Lior could already see it in her mind, assembling itself with terrible clarity: the council in the crypt, the argument, the faces. And one clan — the Tallow Street Rats, whose passage east she needed most, whose beggars' web reached everywhere — whose patriarch had asked one question too many about the seed, one question too gently.

"They paid you in safety," she whispered to the dark, to a man miles away now. "He always pays in safety."

Behind them, across the water, the inquisitor's lamps began to move — not chasing along the bank, but converging, unhurried, certain, the way a man moves when he already knows where the road must end.

Ahead lay the eastern hills, and the only pass through them, and the thirteen turns of the moon, of which nine were gone.

"Hold on," Hess said, and bent to the oar.
