## 7. The Seed Speaks

The pass had a name once, before the maps burned. The old smugglers called it the Shepherd's Tooth — a jag of granite where the trail narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for a mule's hooves and the wind came down off the icefields like something with an appetite. Lior climbed it in the last grey hour before dawn, when the stars were still sharp enough to cut, because the prophecy had said *high places* and she had learned not to argue with ink that dried faster than it should.

She had been walking for nine days. Nine turns of her own footsteps since the guildhall floor gave up its secret, since Master Corren's hand had gone cold around hers with the weight of the iron seed pressed between their palms. Nine days of sleeping in ditches with the thing wrapped in oilcloth against her sternum, warm as a sleeping bird, warmer every night.

Tonight it was hot enough to feel through three layers of wool.

"Almost there," she told it, and hated herself a little for talking to it, and kept doing it anyway. A person alone on a mountain talks to whatever will listen. Even a seed. Especially a seed that listened back — she was almost sure now that it did. When she dreamed, it dreamed too, and its dreams leaked at the edges like water through a cracked jar.

The ledge opened onto a shelf of bare stone no wider than a hearth, cupped beneath an overhang of blue ice older than any kingdom. Below her the valley fell away in folds of dark pine; above, the Tooth speared up into a sky going pale at the rim. She set down her pack, unwrapped the seed, and laid it on the stone.

It looked so small to have cost everything. A fist of black iron veined with silver, sealed with a sigil she'd never been able to read — until this morning, when she'd glanced at it over breakfast and found she could read it perfectly, the way you suddenly understand a language you've been dreaming in.

*Witness,* the sigil said. Just that. Just the one word, repeated in a ring, worn smooth as if a thousand thumbs had traced it.

The warmth pulsed under her palm. And then — gently, terribly, the way a door opens when someone on the other side has been leaning against it for a hundred years — the seed split.

A sliver. No more than a hairline crack along its seam, breathing out a light that wasn't light, a smell that wasn't smell: cold iron and woodsmoke and rain on fresh-turned earth and something sweet underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar. Lior had time to think *no, wait* — and then it took her.

* * *

She was standing in a square she had never seen, in a city whose name she somehow knew was *Vael Hallow*, though no map living held that name. Cobblestones. Rain. A scaffold built of green timber, still smelling of sap, and a crowd pressed shoulder to shoulder, and herself — not herself, but a woman whose eyes she wore the way you wear borrowed gloves — watching from the second row.

On the scaffold stood a girl of perhaps sixteen, wrists bound, chin up. The crier read the charges and the charges were nothing. *Sedition. Consorting with the old blood.* The girl's only crime, Lior understood with a certainty that lived in her teeth rather than her mind, was that her grandmother had been a princess of the line the new king had put down, and that she had a laugh exactly like her grandmother's laugh, and someone powerful had heard it across a market square and gone pale.

The drum rolled. The crowd was silent in the way crowds are silent when they are being watched by soldiers.

*I should look away,* thought the woman whose eyes Lior wore. *I should look away.*

She didn't. None of them did. That was the first thing the seed taught her: the crown did not only kill. It made witnesses, hundreds at a time, and then spent a century making them forget what they'd seen. But forgetting is a door, not a wall, and the seed had been built — grown, she corrected herself, *grown*, it was alive, it had always been alive — to hold every door open.

The axe fell. Lior felt it fall. Felt the crowd flinch as one body, felt the rain keep falling on the timber, indifferent, and somewhere behind her own ribs the seed drank the memory down and said, without words: *this one was buried deepest. This one they paid the criers to unwrite. Say her name.*

"I don't know her name," Lior whispered aloud, on the mountain, in the rainless cold.

But she did. It rose in her like water into a well: *Sera of Vael Hallow.* And saying it, she felt the memory settle — not fade, *settle*, the way silt settles — accepted, witnessed, given weight.

Then the next door opened.

* * *

Famine. A valley called Redmarch, though it was brown as old bone when she stood in it. She was a tax clerk this time, or riding with one, and the ledgers in the saddlebag recorded harvests that had never happened, yields invented so the grain quotas could be met and the surplus carted away to feed garrisons while the villages ate elm bark and boiled leather. She saw a mother divide a single turnip five ways. She saw a man walk into the river with his coat pockets full of stones, unhurried, almost polite about it. She saw the clerk write *deaths from fever* in a column that should have read *starvation*, because the clerk had children, and clerks who wrote the true word were sent to write it nowhere ever again.

Lior came back to herself gasping, on her knees on the frozen stone, sick into the snow. The seed sat in its little ring of melted frost, glowing faintly, patient as a confessor.

"That's not mercy," she rasped at it. "You can't just — pour all of that into someone."

But even as she said it she understood that it was mercy, of a kind so old and severe it had stopped resembling kindness. The seed was not punishing her. It was doing the only thing it knew how to do: refusing to be the only one who remembered. Every crime it carried had been carried alone for a century, sealed in iron, guarded by smugglers who died keeping a secret they were never allowed to understand. Now it had a bearer with a pulse and a conscience, and it was sharing the load the only way burdens get shared — by handing them over whole.

*Say their names,* it murmured. *Redmarch. Brannoc's Field. The drowned villages under Lake Sorrel, flooded for a reservoir to cool the foundries of a king who never saw them. Say them.*

She said them. All night, it seemed, though the sun barely moved past the Tooth's shadow. Door after door after door. Executions dressed as accidents. A plague quarantine that became a pyre. An entire town's records burned so a land claim would die with them, and the town itself soon after, scattered to the roads, its name scraped from boundary stones. Each time, the seed waited until she had spoken the truth aloud — wept it, screamed it, once laughed at the sheer obscene pettiness of a theft of three copper roofs — and each time, the memory settled into her like sediment into clear water, and she felt it change. Not lighter. *Held.* Carried now by two instead of one.

By the time the sun stood at noon she understood why the old war had nearly begun, and why it had never truly ended. It was not fought over thrones. Thrones were just the shape greed wears to a battle. It was fought over this: whether the dead get to be counted. Whether a kingdom built on unwritten names can call itself lawful. The loyalists hadn't been wrong to fear the old blood's return — but the old blood hadn't wanted crowns either, not really, not the ones who started it. They had wanted a witness. And the crown, which could survive rebellion, could not survive being *seen*, and so it had hunted the seeing thing to the edge of extinction and called the hunt peace.

Lior sat with her back against the ice, wrung out, hollowed, strangely clean. Her tears had frozen on her cheeks and cracked when she moved her face. The seed's glow had dimmed to embers. Through the sliver of its opening she could see — actually see, a green thread deep in the black iron — something growing. Waiting. Not a claimant. A record. A tree that hadn't been planted yet, holding a forest of truths in a single seed.

"You're not a weapon," she said softly. "You're a ledger that walks."

The warmth against her palm felt, unmistakably, like agreement.

* * *

She might have stayed there another day, learning it, letting it learn her. But the wind shifted, coming up the pass from the south, and it brought two things to her at once.

The first was smoke — thin, far below, wrong for the season. Waystation smoke. Someone was burning their way up the valley road, methodically, the way Ash burned things: not for warmth, not for spite, but to leave nothing behind him that could shelter a fugitive or carry a message.

The second was a sound threaded inside the wind, so faint she might have imagined it: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. And beneath them, rising off the icefields to the north like a note struck on a bell too large to see, a tremor in the seed itself — a shudder of recognition and dread, the way a dog goes stiff before the earthquake arrives.

Something else had felt the seed wake. Something old, entombed, and patient in a way no inquisitor had ever managed to be.

Lior wrapped the seed against her chest, shouldered her pack, and looked north, where the clouds above the high peaks had begun, very slowly, to move against the wind.

"Hold on," she told it. "Thirteen turns. We're at ten. Whatever's coming — it doesn't get you."

The seed pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, and far below in the smoking valley, the hoofbeats turned toward the mountain.
