## 8. Ash's Choice

The warrant was warm from his breast pocket, and that warmth had begun to feel like a lie.

Inquisitor Ash crouched at the edge of the high pass where the wind came off the icefields like a blade drawn slowly across the world. Below him, in the shallow bowl of stone the smugglers called the Shepherd's Cradle, a girl sat beside a small fire no larger than a cupped hand. She had built it in a hollow of rock so its light would not show against the sky — guild craft, that, the carefulness of people who had spent their whole lives being hunted — and she was turning something over in her palms. Even at this distance, even in this light, he could see it was not metal and not wood. It caught the firelight the way an eye catches it.

The seed.

Three weeks he had followed her. Three weeks of burned waystations and frightened men tied to chairs, of ledgers seized and cellars opened, of the same question asked until voices cracked: *Where is the girl with the book?* He had burned the waystation at Hollow Bridge on his second night in the valley, and stood watching the flames take the old timber while his clerks recorded the inventory of contraband quills and salt-spirit, and all the while the prophecy's words had turned in his head like a splinter working toward the heart:

*When the moon turns thirteen times, the Crownless Bearer shall stand where the mountain remembers.*

He had read the Thornfell Prophecies more carefully than anyone alive. That was his gift, the reason the king's seal hung at his throat instead of some other man's. Ash did not burn books; he *read* them, cover to cover, marginalia and misprint, until they confessed. And the prophecy had confessed everything except one thing — what the seed actually was. The text called it *the sealed remembrance*, *the buried witness*, *the root of reckoning*. His superiors had assumed a weapon. A claimant. Some relic of the old royal line that would legitimize a rebellion if it ever reached the right hands.

Ash had assumed the same. Assumption was the sin he had never been able to forgive in other men.

Now he watched the girl feed a stick to her tiny fire, and he thought: *she is twenty years old at most, and she has been running for three weeks on half-rations and fear, and she has not once tried to sell the thing in her hands.*

That was the detail that had kept him climbing. Smugglers sold things. It was the whole of their religion. Lior Underhill carried an object that any of the great houses would pay a duchy for, and she carried it like a woman carrying water through a drought — carefully, privately, as though spilling it would be a sin against someone she owed.

He rose, and let her hear him.

Not because stealth had failed him. Because he wanted to see her face when she understood there was nowhere left to run, and because — though he would not have admitted it under torture, which was ironic, given his profession — he was tired of learning about people through their terror.

"Master Underhill," he said, using the guild style deliberately, a courtesy and a threat in one. "You have a fire. I have a warrant. Let us not pretend either of us is surprised."

She came up off the ground fast, seed clutched to her chest, and put herself between him and it with her body angled wrong for fighting and right for flight. Her eyes went past him, counting — checking for clerks, for soldiers, for the quiet men with hooks. There were none. He had come up the pass alone, which she would understand meant either arrogance or something else.

"You're Ash," she said. "They said you'd be behind me by the third moon."

"They were generous. I was behind you by the first." He stopped ten paces away, close enough for conversation, far enough for dignity. "Give me the seed, Lior Underhill, and I will write in my report that I found the prophecy book abandoned in a burned waystation, its bearer dead of exposure. You will be free. This is the only kindness my office permits me, and I am offering it once."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take it from you, and I am not gentle, and you learn what the king's justice does with thieves." He said it flatly, without theater. Fear was a tool like any other, but he had never liked sharpening it more than necessary. "I have burned eleven buildings since I entered this valley. Do not make yourself the twelfth."

Something moved in her face then — not fear. Grief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. She looked down at the seed in her hands, and the wind pulled at her hair, and she said, quietly, almost to herself:

"They never told me what it was. Not really. My guildmaster died giving it to me and he didn't even know. They guarded it for a hundred years and none of them knew." She looked up. "Do you?"

"I know what the prophecies call it."

"That's not knowing." And before he could speak again — before he could decide whether this was a trick, whether the seed might be warded, whether touching it was how inquisitors died — she held it out to him across the space between them, into the wind, into the reach of his hands.

"Read it," she said. "You're the one who reads things. Read it, and then do whatever your oath tells you."

Later — much later, in the years when he told this story only to the tree — Ash would try to explain why he took it. The honest answer was that no one had ever offered him the truth before. People offered him confessions, denials, screams, silence. She offered him the object itself, unguarded, and dared him to look.

He took it.

It was warm. Warmer than the warrant against his chest, warmer than blood, and lighter than it should have been, as if it were mostly hollow, as if it were mostly *waiting*. The surface was iron-grey shot through with veins of green so fine they seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at them. And the moment his skin closed around it—

The pass was gone.

He stood in a village square under a white sky, and the square was full of people kneeling, and he knew — the way one knows things in dreams — that this was Errowfield, and that Errowfield had never existed, that every map and ledger and parish record naming it had been burned by order of the crown forty years before he was born. He knew it because the memory knew it, and the memory was not his own. He was inside it the way a man is inside weather.

There was a proclamation being read. Tax arrears, the reader said. Rebellion, the reader said. The village had withheld grain in the famine year, and the grain had been taken anyway, and now the village itself would be *taken*, in the administrative sense: struck from the rolls, its name forbidden, its people dispersed to mines and road gangs where names did not matter. An old woman was screaming a name — not her own — and soldiers were chaining her wrists, and a boy of perhaps six was holding her skirt, and—

Ash knew the boy's face.

He had seen it every morning of his childhood. It hung above the hearth in his grandmother's house in the low country, a cheap tintype gone brown with age, of a young man in a miner's coat with a crooked smile. His grandfather. Dead, the family story went, in a tunnel collapse before Ash was born. No grave. No records. *The collapse,* his mother always said, *took the whole register with it, that's why we don't know the date.*

The register hadn't collapsed. The register had been *burned*, by men wearing the seal that now hung at Ash's own throat, and his grandfather had knelt in this square beside this screaming woman — his grandmother's mother, the old woman was — and the six-year-old boy was his father, and Ash was standing inside the memory of the day his family was erased, and the proclamation was still being read, and the reader's voice was the voice of the law, and the law was him.

He came back to himself on his knees in the snow of the pass, the seed pressed to his forehead, weeping without sound, the way he had been trained to interrogate and never to break. Lior Underhill crouched near him, not touching him, saying nothing, giving him the privacy of his ruin the way one gives a wounded animal distance.

"How many," he managed. His voice came out sanded raw. "How many of those are in there."

"All of them," she said. "Every one the crown buried. Executions. Famines they caused and blamed on weather. Villages. Names. It doesn't punish anyone, Inquisitor. It just *remembers*. That's all it does. That's all it's for." She swallowed. "I saw yours. Or — I saw what you just saw. It shows you what you need. I think that's how it works."

"The warrant," he heard himself say, "names you a traitor to the crown."

"It would."

"The crown erased my family. The crown wrote the law that made the erasing legal, and the law sent me to hunt the only witness it left in the world." He laughed — a terrible sound, one note, no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing a joke told at his own funeral. "I have served that law for nineteen years. I have burned buildings. I have—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have done what the warrant said."

Lior said nothing. She was very good, he was beginning to understand, at saying nothing — at letting silence do the work that other people did with knives.

Ash drew the warrant from his breast pocket. It was heavy vellum, sealed in the king's black wax, signed in the king's own hand, and it had been warm against his heart for three weeks, and the warmth had been a lie the entire time. He read it once more, top to bottom, the way he read everything — thoroughly, respectfully, letting it confess. What it confessed was simple: that it was paper, and the truth was not.

There was no flint in his kit. Inquisitors did not permit themselves fire-starters; fire was a tool of the office, issued and inventoried. So he used the seed.

He held the warrant to it — to the green veins, to the buried heat of a hundred years of remembered wrongs — and the vellum caught with a soft breath of flame that burned green at the edges, and the black seal bubbled and ran like a weeping eye, and the king's signature curled into ash that the wind took south, toward the capital, toward the throne that had signed it.

He held it until the fire reached his fingers, because it seemed important to feel that.

"There will be others behind me," he said, when the last of it was gone. "Vessik is out of the ice — my scouts felt the mountains shaking two nights ago. He wants the seed as a weapon. He'll offer you thrones, or he'll take it, and either way he'll spend it in blood. And the king's second inquiry is already mustering at Grey Ford." He looked at her, and for the first time in nineteen years, the look in his eyes was not the look of an inquisitor. It was only a man's look, tired and clear. "You have maybe nine days. Less, if the weather breaks."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the law was supposed to be justice's servant," Ash said, rising, brushing the snow from his knees, "and I have finally read the document closely enough to see the forgery." He paused at the edge of the cradle, where the wind came up cold from the valley below. "The Shepherd's Road is watched at the lower switchbacks. Take the ice shelf along the north face — it's dangerous, but Vessik's men won't climb it, and neither will the king's. The mountain's heart is a day beyond the glacier mouth. You'll know the place. The prophecy says the mountain remembers. Trust it to remember better than we do."

"You're coming with me?" she asked.

He shook his head. "Someone has to go down to Grey Ford and tell the second inquiry that the Crownless Bearer is dead. Someone whose reports they still believe." A ghost of something crossed his face that might, in another life, have become a smile. "Besides. I burned eleven buildings in this valley. I owe it some honest labor."

He turned and started down through the snow toward the world of oaths and offices, carrying nothing now in his breast pocket, the space where the warrant had been strangely cold, strangely light — the weight of a man newly emptied, walking toward the largest lie of his career and the first true thing he had ever done.

Behind him, in the cradle, the girl with the seed watched him go, and fed another stick to her small fire, and began, methodically, to pack for the north face.

And far below them both, beneath the glacier, something ancient turned in its sleep of ice, and dreamed of thrones, and began, patiently, to count the days remaining.
