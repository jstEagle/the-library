## 10. The Witness Tree

The tree had no business growing where it grew.

That was the first thing every pilgrim said, standing at the mouth of the mountain's heart with their lanterns guttering in the updraft, staring up into a canopy that should not exist a thousand feet beneath the sky. There was no sun down there. No rain. Only the slow breath of the deep stone and the pale glow of veins of luminous quartz threading the cavern walls like frozen lightning. And yet the Witness Tree rose from the scar in the rock where Lior had pressed the seed home, its trunk wide as a guildhall, its bark silver-grey and warm to the touch, its roots running out along the cavern floor in braided rivers — through stone, through old collapsed tunnels, up toward the surface, no one knew how far. Smugglers who knew every crack of the Thornfell range swore they'd found rootlets seeping through the walls of waystations three valleys distant, thin as spider silk, patient as anything the Underhills had ever taught her.

Lior had stopped trying to explain it. The seed had been a record, and records, she had learned, wanted only one thing: to be read.

* * *

A year had turned since the thirteenth moon. She kept count now the way she'd once kept count of runs and debts — chalked marks on a slate hung by the door of the keeper's cottage, which was not really a cottage but an old smuggler's cache hollowed into the cavern wall, fitted with a hearth that vented through fissures Vessik's army had cracked open in their dying. She'd swept it out herself. Hung a kettle over the fire. It was the first room she had ever chosen for herself rather than been hidden in, and some mornings she still woke expecting the ceiling to be the low soot-black beams of the guildhall, expecting Maren's cough from the next room, expecting the day to be about survival instead of tending.

The guildhall was gone. Ash had seen to that, or the fires he'd set before his defection had, and what remained had been claimed by the clans — the Vetch and the Corrigan and even the river people, who had scorned the Underhills for two generations and now argued cheerfully over who got the melted lockplates. Lior had let them argue. Maren would have found it funny. Maren would have found most of this funny: the last apprentice of the last smuggler guild, keeper of a tree that could not be stolen, guarding the one treasure in the mountains that grew more valuable the more it was given away.

She made the morning rounds while the kettle heated.

The roots came first. She walked them barefoot, as she did every day, because the tree liked skin against bark — or so she'd decided, and after a year of deciding things on the tree's behalf without complaint, she felt entitled to her superstitions. The roots were warm. They always were, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and when she laid her palm flat against the widest one she felt the pulse beneath, slow and vast, a heartbeat measured in seasons rather than seconds.

"Morning," she said. "Quiet night."

It had been. Mostly. A party of lowland clerks had come down the switchbacks at dusk, six of them with ink-stained fingers and the nervous look of men sent by superiors who would have preferred to send no one. They'd touched the leaves — everyone touched the leaves; you couldn't help it, they hung low and silver-veined and seemed to lean toward your hand — and each of them had gone white, then grey, then very quiet, and in the morning they had left without their arguments. One of them, a young woman with a stammer, had come back to the cottage door before departing and stood there working her hands together until Lior took pity.

"What does it show?" the clerk had asked. "When you touch it. What do you see?"

"Myself," Lior had said, which was true, and insufficient, and the only honest answer there was. The tree showed you the buried thing. For the clerks it had been the ledgers — the crown's quiet ledgers, the ones where villages became line items and famines became rounding errors. For others it was a face. A name. A field where something had happened that the histories said never happened. The tree did not shout. That was the thing no one expected, the thing that unmade them: it simply showed, the way a witness shows, calmly, without hatred, and left you alone with what you'd seen.

The young clerk had nodded then, stammered something about reform commissions, and Lior had smiled and told her the truth: "The tree doesn't need your commission. But we'll take it."

* * *

By midmorning the cavern filled, as it did every third day, when the road-caravans came through.

They called it the Grove Road now, the old smugglers' route that Vessik's dying army had blasted open in its final convulsion — irony being the one crop the mountains grew in abundance. What had been a warlord's wound was now the easiest passage between the high valleys and the lowlands ever cut in Thornfell's history, and the clans ran it openly, tolls posted in chalk, disputes settled under the branches where any lie spoken aloud would be answered sooner or later by a leaf.

Lior watched them come down the last stretch: a Vetch caravan with wool and iron, mules jingling; behind them a knot of villagers from Millbrook, the ones whose grandparents' village existed now only inside the tree — they came every month, and every month they touched the leaves and wept and laughed and argued with the dead, and every month they left lighter. Behind them, walking apart as always, a handful of grey cloaks.

Inquisitors. Former inquisitors. The order had fractured the winter after Ash burned his warrant on the high pass — some had followed him, some had declared him traitor, and the king, in the way of kings, had done nothing at all, because a warrantless truth is a difficult thing to arrest. The ones who came to the tree wore no badges now. They came the way penitents come anywhere: quietly, in ones and twos, touching the leaves with the particular dread of people who enforced the burying and know exactly which graves will open for them.

One of them, an older man named Corvane whom Lior had once seen burn a waystation full of people's whole lives, knelt at the great root for a long time. When he rose, his face was wrecked and clean, both at once, the way faces got under the tree.

"He'll want to see you," Corvane said. "Ash. He's coming up from the coast. Says he has something for the archive."

"We don't have an archive," Lior said. "We have a tree."

"That's what he said you'd say." Corvane almost smiled. "He said tell her: it's the ledgers. The originals. The ones from the vault under the palace."

Lior felt that in her chest, an old cold turning over. "How does a defected inquisitor get the crown's own ledgers out from under the palace?"

Corvane looked at her with the mild patience of a man delivering someone else's joke. "He said you'd ask that too. He said: badly, and on fire, and I'm bringing them anyway."

* * *

Afternoon, and she climbed.

There was a path now, worn into the great roots by a year of feet, spiraling up the trunk to the lowest boughs — the tree permitted stairs the way it permitted everything, slowly, by growing to meet them. Lior went up past the first leaves, silver and veined with light, each one a window if you had the courage or the grief to press your palm against it. She didn't touch them anymore, mostly. She had read her book. She knew what was in hers: her mother's face, erased from a village record; the guildmasters' quiet compacts with magistrates; Maren's deathbed confession, which the tree had shown her the first week, gently, like a hand on the shoulder — *we buried it to protect you, girl, and calling it protection was the last lie I told*.

At the top of the climb she sat in the fork of the lowest bough, as she did every afternoon, and looked out over the cavern floor.

This was the part she had never imagined, back when the prophecy was a fresh-inked horror in a dead man's waystation: that the ending would be so ordinary. No throne. No court. The mountain's heart held no coronation, only a market day — clans haggling, children racing the root-rivers, a former inquisitor teaching a Vetch boy to read off a scrap of salvaged warrant-paper because paper was paper. Justice had come to Thornfell, and it looked like witnesses. It looked like people who could no longer pretend not to know, standing in the same place, breathing the same warm root-scented air, figuring out — clumsily, loudly, generation by generation — what to do about it.

Vessik was still here too. She visited him sometimes, though visit wasn't the word. Deep in the mountain's heart, past the last root-river, the wood had taken him — not cruelly. The tree did not do cruelty; it did witness, and Vessik had been the crown's greatest crime given legs, a man resurrected out of a war the histories had also buried, and the tree had simply shown him everything he had been made from until there was nothing left of him that wanted to fight. Now he stood bound in living bole, eyes closed, bark to the chin, and when Lior pressed her hand to the wood over his heart she sometimes felt a pulse answering hers, slow, dreaming. The clans left offerings. Old soldiers came and told him the war was over. Whether he heard, whether anything of him remained to hear — the tree kept that leaf to itself.

"They're bringing the ledgers," she told him anyway, because someone should. "Ash is. The real ones. Your war's going to be read, Vessik. All of it. Every name."

The pulse beat on, slow as seasons. She chose to believe it quickened.

* * *

Evening came down the mountain in the usual way — lamplight kindling along the caravan camp, smell of stew and pine resin, the quartz-veins brightening as the surface darkened, so that the cavern's night was always lit from below, like the underside of the world holding a candle for it. Lior banked her hearth, chalked the day's mark on the slate — three hundred and sixty-five marks, a full turn of her new life — and stepped outside into the root-shadow with her tea.

The tree rustled. There was no wind down there. There never was. Its leaves moved the way pages move when someone, somewhere, is reading.

She stood a long time with the warmth of the great root against her bare feet, listening to the camp noise fade into evening songs, and thought about a girl running quills through the dark with a price on her guild's name and no room in the world to stand in. The room had found her. It had grown around her, ring by ring, the way the tree grew — not because she'd earned it, exactly, but because she'd stayed. Because she'd witnessed.

High overhead, far up in the black vault of the cavern, a single leaf detached itself and came down through the dark, turning, silver-veined, catching the quartz-light like a coin falling through deep water. Lior put out her hand and caught it without thinking, the way you catch anything the tree gives you.

It was blank. That was new. In a year, every leaf had borne its truth.

She turned it over. On the underside, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh as the night she'd found her own name in a century-old book, small letters were writing themselves even as she watched:

*A second seed has woken. It grows in the lowlands, in the king's own garden. Come and see.*

Lior stood very still, tea cooling in her hand, the blank leaf trembling against her palm like a small live thing.

"Well," she said, to the tree, to the mountain, to whatever patient gardener had written the first line of her next life. "I suppose I'd better pack."
