## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with selling illegal charms, Sebastian Villalobos had learned over twelve careful years, was that it made you an expert in small things. Small magics. Small lies. The small, precise weight of a copper coin that might be a warden's informer.

So when he stepped out of his shop onto Marrow Lane that evening and looked up to check the weather — a habit, like checking for watchers — he noticed immediately that something small was wrong with the sky.

Not wrong-big. Wrong-small. The way a room is wrong when one object has been moved two inches left of where it always sat.

He stood in the doorway with his key still in the lock and counted stars. He didn't know why counting was the instinct; it simply was. Four above the chimney pots, as always. The pale one low over the tannery roofs that sailors called the Lantern. The tight little cluster southeast that farmers called the Sisters and that Sebastian's mother had called something else, in a language she'd only spoken when she thought he was asleep.

Four. The Lantern. The Sisters.

He counted again.

Nothing.

Sebastian Villalobos had spent twelve years teaching himself not to flinch, and so he did not flinch now. He simply stood very still on his own doorstep, in the smell of frying onions and horse dung and river damp that was Corren at dusk, and felt the ordinary world go quiet around him the way a market goes quiet when a pickpocket is working the crowd.

Above him, where four stars should have been, there was nothing. Where the Lantern burned, nothing. Where the Sisters huddled — nothing. Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from your mouth: you keep touching the space because your tongue refuses the news.

Down the lane, life continued with the beautiful stupidity of the unwatched. Old Maretta was hauling her pie cart toward the evening square, arguing with her mule. Two dockhands were brawling lazily outside the Salt Cellar, all noise and no damage. A child ran past Sebastian's boots chasing a hoop, shrieking with joy about something, about everything, about nothing.

Nobody else had looked up yet.

*Give it a minute,* Sebastian thought, with the strange calm of a man watching a fire start in a neighbor's house. *Give it one minute, and then every soul on this street will look up at once, and then—*

And then what? That was the question his mind kept sliding off of, the way a cart wheel slides off a rutted road. What did it mean, a city without stars? He knew what the priests would say. He knew what the wardens would say, which was worse, because the wardens wouldn't say anything at all. They would just start knocking on doors.

His door.

His door with its cellar full of charms that no honest charm-seller owned, siphoned from a source he had never once let himself name aloud in twelve years.

"Evening, Seb!" called Tam Wicker from across the lane, wrestling a shutter into place. "Cold one coming. Feel it in my knees."

"Cold one," Sebastian agreed, and heard how normal his own voice sounded, and hated how good he'd gotten at that.

He went back inside. He locked the door behind him with hands that were perfectly steady, which frightened him more than shaking would have.

* * *

The shop was small and warm and deliberately forgettable: candles, salves, luck-knots woven from river grass, fever-charms of the kind any hedge-witch in Corren could make. And beneath the counter, beneath the floorboards, beneath the honest inventory — the other stock. The charms that worked too well. The knots that untied locks. The coins that turned a warden's eye. Little pieces of impossible, each one humming faintly with a warmth he had never been able to explain and had long ago stopped trying to.

He lit lamps. He checked the shutters. He did all the things a careful man does, and then, because there was nothing left to check, he went down into the cellar.

The gate was at the back wall, behind a rack of drying herbs: a low stone archway, older than the house, older than the street, bricked up sometime before Sebastian had ever seen it. It had come with the shop. It came with the deed, actually — some clerk a century dead had written *including the arch in the north cellar wall* in faded ink, as if it were a feature, like a well or a privy.

For twelve years it had hummed.

That was the only word for it. A hum below hearing, felt more than heard, in the teeth and the breastbone and the backs of the knees. On bad nights it kept him awake. On good nights it was company. He had never told anyone about it, because telling anyone about it would mean explaining why he'd bought this particular shop, in this particular lane, three streets from the old Vellish border — and that explanation led down roads Sebastian had sworn never to walk again.

Tonight, descending the stairs, he felt the hum before his foot touched the bottom step.

It was wrong.

Twelve years of the same low, patient note — and tonight it was climbing. Rising in pitch like a kettle, like a choir reaching for a note too high, like something straining against—

The bricks of the arch were sweating.

Not damp. *Sweating.* Beads of moisture standing on mortar that hadn't seen water since before the war, running in thin lines down the stones like the wall itself was weeping, or bleeding, or—

Sebastian put his palm flat against the brick.

The pain hit him through the floor of the world.

It wasn't heat. It wasn't cold. It was *removal* — the sensation of something enormous being subtracted from somewhere deep inside him, some load-bearing wall of his own chest giving way, and he cried out and staggered back and caught himself on the herb rack, scattering dried yarrow across the flagstones, and through the roar in his ears he heard — distantly, absurdly — old Maretta's mule screaming out on Marrow Lane, and dogs, dozens of dogs, every dog in the quarter howling at once.

Then the sound.

Every story about the fall of Vell mentioned the sound. Sebastian had always assumed the survivors exaggerated. Nobody exaggerates tonight.

It began as glass — a high, thin ringing, as if every window in Corren had been struck by a hammer at once — but it came from everywhere and nowhere, from under the ground, from inside the walls, from inside his own skull. It climbed and climbed until it passed beyond sound entirely and became pressure, became light behind the eyes, became the feeling of standing too close to a struck bell.

And then the wards broke.

He couldn't see them. No one alive could see them; they'd been dormant for twelve years, since the night Vell fell, since the Crown's wardens had walked the border and sung the country out of existence. But Sebastian felt them break the way you feel a bone snap in a limb you can't see — a sickening give, a wrongness where structure used to be, a network of ancient tension going slack all at once along a line only he seemed to occupy.

Because the line ran through his cellar.

Through his gate.

The last anchor, said a voice in his memory that sounded like his mother, speaking Vellish in the dark, *the last anchor holds the door, mijo, the last anchor holds—*

"Hush," Sebastian said aloud, to a dead woman, to a bricked-up arch, to himself. "Hush, hush."

But the arch was glowing now. Faintly. A seam of gold light in the mortar joints, thin as wire, tracing the outline of a door that hadn't opened in twelve years — that everyone said was just an arch, just old stonework, just a quirk of the property —

— and through the glow, faint as breath on winter air, he heard singing.

Vellish. A lullaby. His mother's lullaby, the one about the garden and the ember, the one about keeping the fire small so the frost can't find it.

"No," Sebastian said. "No, no, no."

The light in the mortar pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat finding its rhythm after a long faint.

And then, from directly behind him, from the middle of his own cellar where no one stood, came a sound like a body hitting flagstones.

* * *

Upstairs, out on Marrow Lane, the whole city had finally looked up.

They stood in the streets with their dinners burning and their children lifted onto shoulders, pointing at the empty sky, and the empty sky gave them nothing back. Somewhere a temple bell began to ring — not the hour, not an alarm, just ringing, the way people ring bells when they don't know what else to do.

In the cellar of a charm-shop on Marrow Lane, Sebastian Villalobos knelt beside a girl who had fallen out of nowhere. She was perhaps nineteen, dressed in clothes cut in a style he hadn't seen since he was a boy — Vellish wool, Vellish buttons, a coat with the collar stitched in the old pattern that meant *border-born*. There was blood on her temple and frost on her eyelashes, though the cellar was warm, though the cellar had never been cold, though frost had no business existing anywhere within forty miles of Corren on a autumn evening.

Her lips were moving. He leaned close.

She was counting stars. In Vellish. Over and over, in a voice worn down to almost nothing:

"...four, and the Lantern, and the Sisters... four, and the Lantern, and the Sisters..."

"Ilsa," she whispered then, opening her eyes — grey eyes, frost-grey, looking straight through him at the glowing arch behind him. "You're the keeper. Thank the embers. You're the keeper, and I'm too late."

Behind him, the gate sang on, sweet and terrible, and above the city of Corren the sky stayed black and starless, and somewhere far away — though Sebastian would swear until his dying day that he heard it through six feet of earth and stone — a bell began to toll in a country that no longer existed.

"Too late for what?" Sebastian asked.

Ilsa's eyes found his. Frost crept from her lashes down her cheek like tears freezing mid-fall.

"The winter," she said. "Keeper — the winter is already here."
