## 2. Charms for a Lawless City

The shop opened at half past eight, the way it always did, because lawlessness kept better hours than honesty.

Sebastian Villalobos counted his stock by feel before he lit the lamps: three dozen luck-knots braided from gray wool, a tray of fever-charms pressed into wax like coins, six small bells that rang only in the ear of someone being lied to. He arranged them with the practiced carelessness of a man who wanted everything to look found rather than made. Found things could be explained away. Made things had makers.

By law — Crown Statute 114, *On Unlicensed Thaumaturgy*, which he could recite the way other men recited prayers — every item on his shelves was contraband. None of it was powerful. That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of his survival: he sold magic so small it insulted the wardens to confiscate it. A knot that turned one bad day into a tolerable one. A bell worth less than the fine for ringing it.

He had learned, twelve years ago, in a country that no longer existed, that enormous magic was what got people erased. Small magic just got you fined.

He lit the lamps. The shop woke up around him — dust motes turning gold above the counter, the smell of beeswax and river damp and the particular cold that lived in his cellar and never quite left, even in high summer. He did not go down to the cellar. He went down to the cellar once a week, on Warden's Rest days, when the market quarter emptied, and he stood at the bottom of the stairs for exactly as long as it took to confirm that the gate was still quiet, and then he came back up and did not think about it for another seven days.

That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

* * *

Twelve years ago there had been stars over Vell.

He remembered them the way you remember a face from childhood — imperfectly, and mostly by their effect on everything else. The night sky over the capital had been so thick with light that the streets never fully darkened, and children played outside until midnight without lanterns, and the old men in the wine-shops claimed you could read a treaty by starlight if the treaty were important enough.

He had been seventeen when the stars went out over Vell for the first time. Not all at once, the way stories told it later — first over the border towns, then the river provinces, then everywhere, a darkness spreading inward like ink through water. His mother had stood in the doorway of their house in Corren — they had already moved, already fled, two years before the end — and looked up at Corren's own intact sky and said, in the dialect she only used when she thought he wasn't listening:

*"It's eating their light."*

He hadn't understood. He still didn't, not really. What he understood was the practical arithmetic that followed: no more letters from his aunt in Vell. No more Vellish goods in the markets, then no more Vellish names in the census rolls, then no more Vell in the atlases — the maps redrawn between one printing and the next, the borders simply closed over the country like water closing over a stone. Twelve years, and now if you said *Vell* in a Corren tavern, people assumed you meant a family name. There was a tax clerk named Vell. Nobody found this strange.

His mother had burned her papers before the cough took her. All of them, in the kitchen stove, feeding them in page by page, watching each one curl. "You were born here," she'd told him, in proper Corren accent, her best accent, the one she'd spent ten years sanding herself into. "You were born here, and your father died here, and there is nothing else. Say it until it's true."

He had said it until it was true. Mostly. On quiet nights, standing at the bottom of the cellar stairs, listening to something behind the gate hum like a struck bell that would never finish its note — mostly true.

* * *

The morning's first customer was a fishwife who wanted a charm against her husband's temper, and Sebastian sold her a knot and lied gently about what it did. It didn't calm tempers. It warmed the hands. But warm hands made people patient, he had found, and patience did most of what temper-charms promised anyway, and he slept better selling warmth than selling lies about the shape of a soul.

The second customer bought a lie-bell for her daughter's betrothal negotiations. The third wanted nothing and stayed forty minutes, which was its own kind of purchase; half his trade was letting lonely people stand in a warm room among small bright things.

It was nearly noon, the shop full of ordinary noise, when the door opened and let in a man wearing the gray-and-silver of the Crown Wardens.

Sebastian's hands did not stop moving. That was the first discipline, learned early: hands never stop moving. He finished tying off a knot, set it down, looked up with the mild, slightly harried expression of any shopkeeper interrupted.

"Master Villalobos." The warden consulted a slate. Young, Sebastian noted. New to the quarter, or new entirely — the older wardens knew him by sight and had stopped bothering. "Routine inspection."

"Of course." He gestured at the shelves as if offering a tour of a bakery. "Luck-knots, fever-wax, truth-bells. All registered under my vendor's license, category four, decorative thaumaturgy. You'll want to see the license."

"I'll want to see the back rooms."

There it was. Sebastian felt the old cold move up from the cellar, up through the floorboards, into his knees. He kept his face pleasant and reached under the counter for the license, and while his head was down he did the arithmetic he hated doing: what was visible, what was deniable, what was fatal.

The workshop was fine. Dyes, wool, wax, a heating pan — craft, not sorcery, provided you didn't look too long at the way the dyes never spoiled. The storeroom was fine. The cellar —

The cellar was not fine. The cellar had never been fine. The cellar contained an iron-bound gate older than the building around it, humming with the residue of a nation, and twelve years of careful anonymity rested on the single fact that no warden had ever come down those stairs.

"Back rooms," the young warden repeated, when Sebastian took a moment too long.

"You'll forgive me, warden, but I've had thieves." He said it easily, wearily, the way a man complains about weather. "I don't allow anyone past the counter without a written order. It's nothing personal. If your captain will send the requisition—"

"This is a written order." The warden held up the slate. On it, in neat Crown script: *Inspection authority, Quarter 9, by order of the Office of Border Integrity.*

Border Integrity. Sebastian had not heard that office's name spoken aloud in eleven years. They had been dissolved after the Vell settlement — publicly dissolved, their records sealed, their work declared complete. An office that no longer existed had just signed an inspection order for his shop.

Someone, somewhere, was asking questions again.

"The Office of—" He laughed, and the laugh came out almost right. "Warden, that office was disbanded before I took this lease. Your slate's either old stock or somebody's idea of a joke. Check with your captain before you go waving defunct seals at honest tradesmen. If I'm wrong, fine me. If you're wrong—" he smiled, thin and friendly, "—well. I'd hate for a young man's record to start with a false seizure."

It was a gamble built out of bureaucracy, and bureaucracy was the one country where Sebastian had never been a refugee. The warden hesitated. Looked at the seal. Looked at Sebastian — at the shopkeeper's apron, the ink-stained fingers, the shelf of charms too petty to bother with — and visibly decided that whatever the Office of Border Integrity was, it was probably not hidden behind a luck-knot stall in the fishmarket quarter.

"I'll verify the authorization," the warden said stiffly.

"Do. And give my regards to Captain Ovesse. Tell him the fever-wax he bought for his daughter in spring is holding well."

The warden left. The bell over the door gave its small dishonest chime. Sebastian stood very still behind his counter, smiling at the middle distance, while inside him everything screamed.

Then he flipped the sign to *closed*, walked calmly down into the cellar for the first time in four days instead of waiting for Warden's Rest, and put his hand flat against the gate.

The hum was louder than it had been last week. Not much. A hair's breadth. But he had spent twelve years learning this gate the way a sailor learns a creaking hull, and he knew the difference between settling and waking.

Behind the iron, faint as breath, something shifted its weight.

* * *

That evening he allowed himself the ritual. One glass of plum brandy, poured into the good cup, drunk alone at the counter with the lamps low. He called it paying himself. Twelve years of fear earned a wage like anything else.

He thought about running. He thought about it the way you think about an old lover — often, fondly, without any real intention. He had money saved in three places under two names. He had a route south memorized since his second year in Corren. He could be gone in a night, and somewhere warm he could sell knots to fishermen until he died of comfort.

But the gate would still be here. That was the thing nobody who wrote the statutes understood: you could flee a place, but you couldn't flee a keeping. His mother had tried to make him born-here-only, and she had nearly succeeded, and then some hand he'd never traced had left him this lease, this shop, this cellar, this post — because someone had to hold the door, and the someone they chose was the son of the woman who ran.

He rinsed the cup. Set it upside down to dry. Locked the till, banked the lamps, and was reaching for the door bolt when he heard the sound from below.

Not the hum. The hum he knew the way he knew his own pulse.

This was different. This was the sound of a lock giving way somewhere far under the world — a deep, mineral groan, like ice on a lake deciding to break — and with it came a cold that climbed the cellar stairs and crossed the shop floor and touched the back of his neck like a hand.

And above the market quarter of Corren, though he would not see it until he stepped outside and tilted his head back and felt his heart stop —

the stars began to go out.
