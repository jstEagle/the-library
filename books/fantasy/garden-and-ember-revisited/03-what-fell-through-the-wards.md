## 3. What Fell Through the Wards

Sebastian did not sleep. That was the first thing. The second thing was that he tried, and the trying was its own kind of insult — lying in his narrow bed above the shop with his eyes shut and his hands folded on his chest like a man at his own wake, listening to the house settle around him while nothing inside him settled at all.

The stars had come back an hour before dawn. He knew because he'd watched for them, kneeling on the roof tiles in his nightshirt like a madman, counting them as they flickered into place one by one — as if they'd only stepped out for a moment and were embarrassed about it. Forty-one. Forty-two. By full dark's end there were hundreds, then thousands, the ordinary indifferent sky restored, and somehow that was worse than the emptiness had been. An empty sky was a wound. A refilled sky was a lie everyone had agreed to tell.

His bones still hummed.

He'd felt it when the wards broke — a deep ringing in the marrow, in the teeth, in the long bones of his forearms, as if someone had struck him with a hammer made of sound. It had lasted maybe four seconds. Then it faded to this: a low vibration, persistent, like a tuning fork buried under his skin. And underneath the vibration, fainter but unmistakable, a direction. Not north or south. *Down.* Toward the cellar.

He got up. Dressed. Went down the stairs past the shopfront with its shelves of small illegal comforts — luck-knots and fever-charms, quiet-keys and truth-bells, all of them humming now too, all of them singing slightly off-key, like instruments left out in the rain — and stood at the cellar door with his hand flat against the wood.

The wood was warm.

Cellars are not warm in Corren in autumn. Cellars are damp and cold and smell of stone and old onions, and Sebastian's cellar had been exactly that for twelve years, except for the one corner where the gate stood, which had always been cool in a way that wasn't temperature — a coolness like the memory of water. Now the whole stairwell breathed heat up at him, dry and mineral, like the air over a hearthstone hours after the fire has gone.

He should have run. He understood that later, looking back, cataloguing all the moments where a sensible man would have turned around. But a sensible man would never have spent twelve years selling charms siphoned from a sealed gate in the first place, so perhaps the question of sense had been settled long ago.

He opened the door and went down.

* * *

The cellar looked wrong in the way that dreams look wrong — everything in its place but the light coming from nowhere. His barrels of brine and his crates of bottled wort sat where they always sat. His workbench with its pliers and wire and jars of ground bone stood untouched. But the air itself glowed faintly, a gray-gold shimmer like dust motes caught in sunbeam, except there was no sun down here, no lamp lit, and the shimmer came from everywhere and nowhere at once.

And the gate —

The gate was open.

Not wide. Not the iron-bound oak swinging on screaming hinges. Just a crack. A hand's width of darkness between door and frame, and through that crack came air that smelled of woodsmoke and snow and something green and growing underneath, something alive, and the warmth poured out of it in a slow tide, pooling across the flagstones, lapping at his boots.

Twelve years he had tended this gate. Twelve years of oiling hinges that never needed oiling, of checking wards that never stirred, of telling himself it was a cellar door behind a shop in a city that had swallowed him gratefully and asked no questions. Twelve years of pretending the hum in his bones on certain nights was imagination.

Now it stood cracked open like a mouth about to speak, and everything he had pretended rose up in him at once and demanded to be acknowledged.

"Hello?" he said. Stupid. Absurd. But the word fell into the warm air and the warm air took it, and from somewhere beyond the crack — far away, or very close, distance meant nothing through a threshold — came an answer.

A scrape. A drag. The sound of a body moving badly over stone.

Then she came through.

She didn't walk through so much as fall through, catching herself on the doorframe with one hand, and even in the strange glow he could see the hand was black with blood to the wrist. She was young — nineteen, twenty, no older — thin as a winter branch, wearing a coat that had been good once and had been mended so many times it was more patch than cloth. Her hair was dark, cut short and unevenly, the way you cut it yourself with a knife. Her face was gray. Her lips were gray. There was frost in her eyelashes, actual frost, melting as he watched, running down her cheeks like tears that weren't tears.

She took two steps into his cellar and collapsed.

He caught her — barely, she weighed nothing, she weighed less than nothing, she weighed what a bundle of sticks weighs — and lowered her to the floor between the brine barrels, her head lolling against his knee. Up close he could see the damage: a gash along her scalp crusted half-shut, a burn along her jaw in the shape of fingers, three of them, pressed hard enough to blister. Frostbite whitening the tips of her ears. And under all of it a shivering so profound it seemed to come from the center of the earth, her whole body rattling like a shutter in a storm.

"No," she said. "No, no — I made it — I *made* it—"

Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw, but the words stopped him cold. Not the words themselves. The shape of them. The vowels. The soft collapse of consonants at the ends of words, the rising lilt that turned every sentence halfway to a question.

Vellish. She was speaking Vellish.

Not the Vellish of the ballads, the courtly tongue that scholars quoted in footnotes. This was border-Vellish, market-Vellish, the dialect of fishwives and drovers, the language his mother had sung over his crib before she died, before his father carried him across the mountains and told him never, ever to speak it again. A dead language. Erased. Twelve years gone from every map and every mouth, scrubbed so thoroughly that even Sebastian, who had been born to it, had to dig down through layers of Correnish to find it again.

She was speaking it fluently. Naturally. As if it had never died. As if somewhere it had lived on, whole and breathing, all this time.

"Where—" he started, in Correnish, then stopped, and heard himself answer in the other tongue, the buried one, rusty and stumbling: "Where are you hurt?"

Her eyes found his face. They were pale eyes, almost colorless, and they were fever-bright and utterly focused, and what was in them was not confusion, not fear, not the wild gratitude of the rescued. What was in them was recognition. Terrible, specific recognition, like a woman finding the one face she'd crossed a country of ice to find.

"You," she said. "You're him."

"I'm nobody. You're bleeding. Let me—"

"*Keeper,*" she said.

The word went through him like the ward-break had gone through him, straight to the marrow. He didn't know why. He had never heard it before, not applied to himself, not in any context that mattered. But his bones rang with it anyway, the same note, the same frequency, and the gate behind them both sighed — actually sighed, a long exhalation of warm smoky air — as if it agreed.

"I don't know what that means," he said.

"You do." She tried to sit up and failed, her head dropping back against his knee. "You know. You've always known. You just — you told yourself stories instead." Her colorless eyes moved past him, to the gate, to the crack of warm darkness, and something in her face broke open — grief and relief tangled together so tightly they were indistinguishable. "It's still there. Oh, thank the hearth, it's still *there*, we thought — after the stars went out, we thought maybe it had died too, we thought—"

"The stars," Sebastian said. "What happened to the stars? What happened tonight? What came through those wards?"

She looked at him then with an expression he would spend a long time trying to name afterward. Pity, maybe. Or the particular patience of someone who has traveled very far to deliver a message and cannot understand why the recipient keeps refusing to open the letter.

"You did," she said. "Tonight. All of it. The wards broke because the keeper stopped keeping, and the keeper stopped keeping because nobody ever told him what he was, and nobody told him because—" She coughed, wet and deep, and pressed a bloody hand to her ribs. "Because of what they did. Because of the erasure. You don't know the word erasure yet, do you? You think Vell burned. Everyone outside thinks Vell burned."

"Vell *did* burn. I saw the smoke. My father carried me out of—"

"Your father lied to you." No cruelty in it. Just flatness, the flatness of fact. "They all lied. They had to. It was easier than the truth." Her eyes were closing; she fought them, dragged them open again. "Listen. Listen to me, keeper, because I'm going to pass out soon and when I wake up I won't be able to say it twice. The gate is open. It will not stay open. It is dying, and everything behind it dies with it, and there are people — there are *children* — there is a whole—"

She swayed. He grabbed her shoulders.

"A whole what? A whole what?"

But her head had dropped. Her breath came shallow and whistling. In the gray-gold light of his impossible cellar, the girl who spoke a dead language lay bleeding on his floor, and the last thing she said before unconsciousness took her was not in Vellish at all but in careful, accented, learned-by-heart Correnish, as if she'd rehearsed it across all those miles of ice:

"Water. And don't let the door shut."

* * *

He worked. Work was the only prayer he knew.

He stitched the scalp gash with the fine curved needle he used for leather charm-casings, his hands steadier than he had any right to expect. He salved the finger-shaped burn with marigold ointment and wrapped it. He packed her frostbitten ears in warm cloth and forced water and honeyed wine between her lips, drop by drop, and each time she swallowed he felt an absurd flare of triumph, as if he'd personally argued death to a standstill.

All the while the gate breathed warmth at his back, and all the while he refused to look at it.

When she was bandaged and blanketed and propped on the pallet he kept for the nights he worked too late to climb the stairs, he finally allowed himself to turn and face the thing he'd been not-looking-at.

The crack had narrowed. A hand's width had become two fingers' width. The warm air still seeped through, but slower now, thinner, like a fire settling into ash. And he understood — with the same marrow-deep certainty that had told him *down* earlier — that the girl was right. It was closing. Whatever had broken loose tonight had broken it open, and whatever force it fought against was winning, slowly, the way winter wins.

He could close it. He could slam the oak door, set the wards, bolt it, brick it over, pour the cellar full of mortar and pretend for another twelve years. The sensible thing. The safe thing. Every instinct that had kept him alive and anonymous since he was nine years old stood in him shouting it.

Instead he found himself wedging the door with the iron poker from the hearth upstairs, jamming it into the gap like a splint holding a broken bone, and standing there with his palm flat against the warm oak, feeling the hum rise through his arm and settle into his chest like a bird coming home to roost.

"Keeper," he said aloud, testing the word in the empty cellar. It sounded ridiculous. It sounded true.

Behind him, the girl stirred and murmured something in her sleep — a single word, repeated, urgent even through unconsciousness:

"*Ilsa.* Ilsa. Ilsa."

Her own name, he realized. Telling herself to herself, so she wouldn't lose it on the road. So she wouldn't arrive at the end of everything and forget who had made the journey.

He looked at her — nineteen years old, frost-scarred, blood-crusted, born speaking a language the world had murdered — and felt the reluctant weight settle onto his shoulders with the familiarity of an old coat. He had spent twelve years learning how not to be responsible for anything larger than a shelf of charms. He had gotten very good at it. He had built a whole life on the foundation of *not my problem, not my war, not my country.*

And now his country — or somebody's country — was bleeding out on his cellar floor, and the door it had crawled through was dying behind it, and his bones sang a note he'd been hearing all his life without knowing it was a summons.

Outside, dawn came gray over Corren. Somewhere a market bell rang the hour. Somewhere in the city, he had no doubt, people were noticing the returned stars and deciding, collectively and comfortably, that it had been an atmospheric oddity, a trick of weather, nothing worth troubling the wardens about.

The wardens. The word arrived in his mind unbidden and unwelcome, trailing cold air behind it. If the wards had shattered audibly enough to break the sky, then the Crown had felt it too. And the Crown employed men whose entire profession was feeling for exactly this kind of breakage, and following it home.

He looked at the poker wedged in the door. At the girl. At the narrowing gap of warm dark that smelled of woodsmoke and snow and green things growing.

"Don't let the door shut," he said quietly. "Right."

He went upstairs to open the shop, because whatever else was true, hiding began with looking ordinary — and because he needed to buy himself time to figure out what a man does on the first morning of the rest of a life he never agreed to.

He was reaching for the shutters when he heard, from the street below, the measured, unhurried click of boot heels. Two sets. Patrol rhythm. Wardens' rhythm.

Stopping in front of his door.
