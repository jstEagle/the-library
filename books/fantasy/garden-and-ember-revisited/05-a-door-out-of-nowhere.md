## 5. A Door Out of Nowhere

Sebastian woke to the smell of burning rosemary and the sound of someone moving through his shop.

He came up the cellar stairs with a candle in one hand and a poker in the other, and stopped on the last step because the shop was full of light that had no source. Every charm on his shelves was glowing — the luck-knots, the fever-beads, the little tin houses he sold to homesick dockworkers for two coppers apiece. Twelve years of small magic, all of it lit at once like a street of windows at festival time.

Ilsa stood in the middle of it, wrapped in his spare blanket, feeding sprigs of rosemary into the brazier. She'd found his tea things. She'd made tea. There was something obscene about how domestic it looked.

"You should be resting," he said.

"I rested." She didn't turn around. "Your gate is louder tonight."

"It's not *my* gate."

"Keeper," she said, with the mild patience of someone correcting a child who has insisted the moon is made of cheese, "it is absolutely your gate."

He set down the poker. Picked it up again. Put it back by the door where it belonged, because some part of him needed one ordinary thing to be in its ordinary place before he could have this conversation.

"Sit down," he said. "Drink your tea. And you're going to tell me what's behind my cellar wall, and you're going to use small words, and you're not going to call me keeper again until I've decided whether I believe you."

She sat. She drank her tea. And she told him.

* * *

"Vell didn't fall," she said. "Vell was folded."

The words landed wrong, like a stair missing from a staircase. He actually swayed where he stood.

"Folded how?"

"The way you fold a letter." She held up her hands, palms together, and pressed them flat. "Twelve years ago there was a country east of the Salt Marches. It had eleven cities and four hundred villages and a river called the Amme that ran brown in spring. I know this because I was born there. Nine years *after* it stopped existing."

"That's not possible."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. That's rather the point of it."

She talked, and he listened, and the glowing charms threw her shadow huge against the rafters, so that she seemed to grow as she spoke, or the room seemed to shrink.

The erasure, she said, hadn't been an invasion. Armies were expensive and slow and left witnesses. What the Crown had done instead was older and cleaner: they had taken the borders of Vell and turned them inward. Folded the whole country into itself, sealed the seam, and then gone to work on memory. Not just the memories of Vellish people — those were easy, they were inside the fold. The harder work was everyone outside. Maps redrew themselves. Letters went blank mid-sentence. Wives forgot husbands, merchants forgot debts, and the border towns on the Corren side forgot they had ever traded with anyone across the Salt Marches at all. The Crown's wardens carried instruments that could cut a name out of ten thousand minds in an afternoon, and Davorin Hale — though she didn't say that name yet; that came later, in a frozen square — had been the one holding them.

"What happened to the people?" Sebastian asked. His voice sounded far away to himself.

"They're still there." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the sun is still up*. "All of them. The ones who weren't smuggled out before the seam closed. Eleven cities' worth. They can't leave, because the only way out is the original threshold — the door the country was folded through — and it's been shut for twelve years."

"And the stars?"

Something moved across her face then, the first crack in her composure all night. She looked at the ceiling as if she could see through it to the sky.

"When they folded Vell, they folded its sky too. We have our own sky in there. It used to be a copy of yours — same constellations, same slow wheel. But a copied sky is a borrowed thing. Three years ago our stars started going out. One by one. Like lamps nobody's tending." Her hands tightened around the cup. "Last night, when your sky went dark — that wasn't your stars dying, keeper. That was the seam tearing. Our sky is bleeding through into yours. Or yours into ours. Nobody in there knows which, and the cold that comes with it—" She stopped. Started again. "It's winter in Vell now. A real one. The kind that kills."

"How many people are in there?"

"Fewer every week."

* * *

He made himself do the ordinary things while the extraordinary thing settled over him like snowfall. He banked the brazier. He watered the plant on the sill that he watered every morning without thinking, a little rosemary bush he'd bought at market six years ago from a woman whose face he couldn't now remember at all, and something about that made his stomach drop through the floorboards.

"I sold charms," he said slowly. "For twelve years. Little ones. Luck, mostly. Fever-breaking. Safe passage over ice." He looked at his own hands. "Where did I get them?"

"You tell me."

"I don't—"

"You do," she said gently. "You told me last night, half-asleep, when I asked why you were chosen. You said the gate hums. You said you've heard it since you were a boy, and that when you were young and stupid and broke you learned how to dip a ladle into the humming and pour out something small and warm." She set down the cup. "That's Vellish craft, keeper. You didn't invent it. You remembered it. Somewhere under whatever they did to your memory, there's a boy who grew up on the other side of that wall."

"I would know that. Wouldn't I? If I were—" He gestured vaguely at everything. "If I were from a *country*."

"Would you?" Ilsa tilted her head. "What's your earliest memory?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was a kitchen, a warm one, a woman singing in a language he had always assumed was nonsense he'd invented as a child — a private melody, a baby-language, the kind children make up. He had sung it to himself his whole life, quietly, in cellars and boarding rooms, embarrassed even alone.

"Sing it," Ilsa said.

"I don't—"

"Sing it."

So he sang it. Four bars of nothing, a lullaby-shaped scrap of sound he'd never spoken aloud to another living soul.

Ilsa's eyes filled. She didn't cry, exactly; she just stood at the edge of it, jaw tight, hands flat on the table, and said, in careful Vellish, "*Sleep, the river watches, sleep, the ember keeps.*"

The nonsense resolved. It had been language the entire time. It had been language the entire time and he had been carrying it around like a stone in his shoe, singing his homeland to himself in the dark without knowing he had one.

"Sit down," Ilsa said, and this time he sat.

* * *

"Now," she said, "the part you're really not going to like."

"There's a part I'll like less than *I'm a citizen of nowhere*?"

"There's a part where you learn what your shop is standing on."

She got up and went to the cellar door and waited, and he understood she wanted him to lead, so he led, down the stairs he had climbed ten thousand times, past the shelving, past the barrels, to the back wall where the old iron-bound gate sat in its frame of black stone.

Except it wasn't sitting anymore.

The wards he'd felt shatter two nights ago had been the outer casing, Ilsa explained — the Crown's sealwork, laid over the true threshold to keep it shut and keep it forgotten. With the casing broken, the thing underneath was showing through. The iron bands had gone translucent, like ice over a lamp. Through them he could see — he made himself look — a road. Frost-rimed cobbles running away into a darkness that was not the darkness of his cellar but the darkness of a starless country, and along that road, impossibly far off, a scatter of lights. Windows. A city keeping its lamps lit out of habit, or hope, or spite.

"People are walking toward it," Ilsa said quietly. "They've been walking toward it since the wards broke. They felt the seam tear too. Everyone in Vell knows the door out is opening somewhere, and they're coming, and keeper—" she turned to face him, and in the gate's pale light she looked very young and very old at once, "—they're coming to a door that opens into a charm-shop cellar in a city that erased them. When it opens all the way, thousands of people are going to walk out of it into Corren with no papers, no names the registries will accept, no legal existence at all. And if it doesn't open—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He looked at the frost creeping in delicate ferns across his own flagstones, spreading outward from the gate like a stain, and thought about the cold snap the whole quarter had complained about for two days, the ice on the canal out of season, the breath-fog in a shop that had never needed a fire before noon.

"It's leaking," he said. "The cold is leaking through."

"The erasure is collapsing inward. The fold is coming undone, and it's coming undone from the middle, and everything touched by Vell is going to freeze before it's done. Your shop first. Then the street. Then the quarter." She hugged the blanket tighter. "There are maybe nine thousand people alive in Vell. The long winter will take all of them within a month unless the door out opens."

"So open it." He heard his own voice go sharp. "You clearly know more about this door than I do. Open it. Why does any of this need me?"

"Because it doesn't answer to me." For the first time she looked uncomfortable. "The threshold was bound to a keeper when it was sealed. Blood of the negotiator, the old stories say — the man who signed the surrender, whose line was charged with minding the seam. It wakes for that blood. It ignores everyone else. I've been scratching at it for three days and it treats me like weather." She put her hand flat against the iron, and the light beneath didn't flicker. "But watch."

She nodded at him. He didn't understand. She nodded again.

So Sebastian Villalobos, charm-seller, unlicensed, unremarkable, owner of one rosemary plant and four hundred small lies, stepped forward and laid his hand on the gate.

The humming he had heard all his life — the faint, ignorable drone he'd assumed was the building settling, or his own blood, or a trick of the ears — surged up through his palm like a struck bell. The frost on the flagstones bloomed outward in a perfect ring. And deep beneath the iron, beneath the stone, beneath twelve years of silence, something vast and patient and buried turned its attention toward him the way a sleeping animal turns toward the hand that feeds it.

*Oh*, he thought, with a vertigo so complete it was almost peaceful. *Oh, you've been here the whole time.*

His shop. His shelves. His careful, hidden, hard-won little life — the anonymity purchased one secret at a time, the routine built like a dam against the past. All of it had been built directly on top of the largest secret in the world. He hadn't been hiding *from* anything. He had been load-bearing. Every quiet year he'd spent above this cellar, minding his business, selling his charms, watering his rosemary — he had been, without knowing it, the only thing standing between a buried nation and the dark.

He took his hand off the gate. The humming faded to its old familiar murmur, and somehow that was worse.

"I need to sit down," he said.

"You already are."

"I need to sit down somewhere else."

* * *

Upstairs, the charms had stopped glowing. Ordinary lamplight again. Ordinary shadows. He sat at his own counter among his own wares and stared at his own hands as if they might explain themselves.

"My parents," he said. "In Corren. The couple who raised me. Were they real?"

"As real as anything. The Crown placed you. Best foster-family they could find — a child raised loved forgets faster than a child raised afraid." Ilsa paused. "I'm sorry. That's not kindness, it's just what the records say. I read the warden protocols. I was looking for my own family's names in them." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied. "They weren't there. Mine stayed inside."

"Nine thousand people," Sebastian said. "And the door only opens for me."

"For your blood. Which means yes." She came around the counter and stood in front of him, and she was nineteen years old and carrying a frozen country on her back, and she said the next part like someone setting down something heavy on his table and stepping back. "I didn't come here to guilt you, keeper. I came here because the stories said the keeper's line lived in Corren and I've been searching this city for a year. But I won't pretend it's a small ask. Opening that door ends your life as it is. Everything you built. Everything you hid. You'd be the most hunted man in the empire by morning — the Crown will want the seam resealed, and there are others, others who survived outside, who want it thrown wide, and neither of them will care what *you* want."

"Others," he said. "What others?"

"Exiles. Survivors of the smuggling networks. They kept Vellish craft alive out here the way you did — without knowing why, most of them. They call themselves the Ember Court." Something complicated crossed her face — loyalty and doubt sharing a chair. "They sent me to find you."

"And if I say no? If I want my small life back?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Outside, somewhere down the frozen street, a church bell rang the hour, and the note hung in the air longer than bells should.

"Then the cold reaches the canal by tomorrow night," she said, "and the harbor by the weekend, and nine thousand people die behind a wall in a charm-seller's cellar while the city that killed them complains about the price of coal." She picked up her teacup, drained it, and set it down with a small precise click. "But you're not going to say no. You sang the song. You put your hand on the door. You already know."

And the terrible thing, the thing that made his chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with fear, was that she was right. He had known since the moment the humming had answered him. You didn't spend twelve years learning to feel for other people's fevers and fortunes and heartbreaks, two coppers at a time, and then stand in a warm shop above a freezing country and choose the shop.

"Who do I talk to," Sebastian said, "about this Ember Court?"

Ilsa smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight.

"Put on your coat, keeper," she said. "We're going underground."
