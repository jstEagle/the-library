## 6. The Ember Court

Ilsa had walked the way down a hundred times, and it never got easier.

The entrance was behind the tannery on Sallow Row, through a door that smelled of lime and old blood, down stairs that had been cut by hands that expected to be caught. The steps were narrow. The walls sweated. Every forty paces there was a niche where a watcher could stand with a knife and wait, and Ilsa knew each one by name — not the niches' names, but the watchers'. Brannick in the second. The twins in the fourth. Old Sefa at the bottom, who had been a gate-sergeant in Vell before Vell was a rumor, and who still stood watch out of habit because she did not know how to do anything else.

"Who's this?" Sefa said tonight, stepping out of her niche with her hand already inside her coat.

"The keeper," Ilsa said.

Sefa looked at Sebastian Villalobos the way you look at weather. He was pale from the walk and the cold and the girl bleeding on his floor two nights ago, and he kept glancing up at the ceiling as if the weight of Corren above them were a thing he might have to hold.

"You're sure?" Sefa said.

"No," Ilsa said honestly. "But Maghret will want to see him anyway."

"That's what worries me."

Sebastian cleared his throat. "I'd like to say, before we go any further, that I don't actually know why I'm here."

"You're here," Ilsa said, "because your cellar is the last door to my country, and my country is freezing."

She pushed past Sefa and took him down the last flight, into the warm dark.

* * *

The Ember Court met in what had been a cistern once, back when this part of Corren was a different city with a different name. It was a long vaulted room, brick ribs overhead like the inside of a whale, and the floor was dry now and swept clean, and along both walls burned lamps of rendered fat that threw more heat than light. At the far end, on a dais of stacked crates, sat the Court itself: nine chairs, seven filled, and above them hung the banner they had sewn from whatever cloth they'd carried out twelve years ago — a garden lily crossed with an ember, stitched in thread that had faded almost to nothing.

The room held maybe two hundred people. Ilsa knew all their faces. She had been born down here, or somewhere very like it; her mother had gone into labor during a move between safehouses and delivered her on a kitchen table in a house whose owners had been erased so thoroughly that even the house had forgotten itself. She was nineteen years old and she had never seen the sky over Vell. She had never seen Vell at all. Everything she knew of her country fit inside rooms like this one, and she had spent her whole life being told it was enough, and her whole life knowing it wasn't.

Tonight the room was fuller than usual. Word had moved fast: the wards were broken, the keeper was found, the door was real.

She felt Sebastian stop beside her when he saw them all. Two hundred people turning to look at one man has a weight to it, like water finding a hole.

"They're staring," he murmured.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because for twelve years," Ilsa said, "you've been the most important person in Vell, and nobody knew your face."

* * *

Maghret came down from the dais to meet him, which Ilsa had seen her do exactly twice before, and both times it had been theater.

She was fifty or so, broad-shouldered, gray going on white at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome in a hard way and had decided to keep being handsome rather than soften. She wore a coat that had clearly been fine once — Vellish wool, garden-green, mended at the cuffs with thread that didn't match — and she wore it the way other people wore crowns. When she walked, the crowd parted without being asked to.

"Sebastian Villalobos," she said, and his name in her mouth sounded Vellish, sounded like something out of a song. "Charm-seller. Gate-keeper. Son of Corren." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Or so we're told."

"I'm just a shopkeeper," Sebastian said.

"Yes," Maghret said warmly. "That's exactly what a keeper would say."

There was laughter in the room — not much, but some, and it broke something loose. Ilsa watched Sebastian's shoulders come down half an inch. That was the thing about Maghret that Ilsa had understood since she was small and had never managed to say aloud: Maghret made you feel found. Not caught — found. Like something lost that had finally been looked for properly.

"You'll want to sit," Maghret said. "You'll want wine, probably. You've had a bad week. Stars went out, wards shattered, a bleeding girl fell through your floor." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "We know all of it. We've known about your cellar for six years, Sebastian. We've had people in your market. We buy your charms."

Sebastian blinked. "You — what?"

"The warming charms." Maghret turned to the crowd, lifting her voice so it carried to the back bricks. "Every winter, when the frost gets into the bones of our oldest ones, we send someone up to Corren with coin, and they come back with little brass charms that hold warmth like a coal holds fire. Real work. Clean work. Better than anything our own makers can do anymore, because our makers are dying, because our country is dying, because—" and here her voice did something Ilsa had heard maybe three times, a crack deliberately placed, a controlled fracture, "—because the world was allowed to forget us."

The room made a sound. Not a cheer. Something lower and older than cheering.

"And every one of those charms," Maghret said, coming back around to Sebastian, softer now, just for him, just for the front rows, "came off your gate. You've been feeding us for years, friend. You just didn't know it. So don't stand there telling me you're just a shopkeeper. You're the hearth we've been warming our hands at since before Ilsa was born."

Ilsa watched it land. She watched Sebastian look at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger — hands that had soldered those little brass charms, hundreds of them, thousands, sold cheap at the market because he liked selling things cheap, because he told himself it was charity, small magic for poor people. And all that time—

"We should talk," Maghret said. "Not here. The Court can wait. Come."

* * *

They talked in a side chamber that had been a sluice-room once, brick and dripping, lit by one lamp. Maghret poured wine from a jug with a chipped lip and gave the first cup to Sebastian, which among Vellish exiles meant something specific: *you are under my protection until you leave this room.*

Ilsa stood by the door. Nobody asked her to sit. She was the one who'd found him; she was furniture tonight, and she hated it, and she stayed anyway.

"Do you know what happened twelve years ago?" Maghret asked.

"Some," Sebastian said carefully. "Vell fell. There was a war, or — everyone says there was a war."

"There was no war." Maghret said it flatly, without heat, which was somehow worse than heat. "There was a surrender, and then there was an erasure, and the Crown called the erasure a mercy. They took our borders and folded them inward. They took our names out of the registries. They took the stars off the sky over us — do you understand that? Not metaphorically. The wardens wrote Vell out of the heavens. And the country didn't die, because countries are stubborn. It went *inward*. Behind the last anchor. Behind your gate." She leaned forward. "Twelve thousand souls, give or take, alive in the dark, waiting for the door to open."

"Twelve thousand," Sebastian repeated.

"Fewer every winter. The long winter is starting again — you felt it, the frost off your own cellar step. Without stars, without a border open to the world's weather, we freeze. Slowly. Politely. The Crown doesn't even have to kill us. They just have to keep forgetting us, and we die of it." Maghret set down her cup. "And you, Sebastian. You sit ten feet above us with your hand on the lock."

"I didn't know."

"I believe you." She said it easily, generously. "It changes nothing, but I believe you. Here's what I think you don't understand yet. The gate chose a keeper. Gates don't choose randomly. Somewhere in your blood or your history or your bad luck, there is a reason you ended up tending that door, and I promise you the reason is better than rent being cheap." She smiled again. "We can find out together. Or you can go home, sell your little charms, and lie awake every night for the rest of your life listening to twelve thousand people freeze underneath your floorboards. Both are available. I'm not a tyrant."

"You're describing my options pretty tyrannically."

"Good," Maghret said, delighted. "He argues. Ilsa, he argues. Do you see why I wanted him brought whole?"

Ilsa said nothing. She was watching Sebastian's face, and what she saw there frightened her more than the warden patrols ever had. It wasn't fear. It was hunger. Twelve years of careful anonymity, of being nobody, of belonging purchased one secret at a time — and here was a room full of people who knew what he was and wanted him anyway. Wanted him *for* what he was. She recognized the expression because she'd worn it her whole life in mirrors.

"It's not that simple," she said, before she could stop herself.

Both of them looked at her.

"The gate," Ilsa said. "Opening it fully. My mother used to say the door out isn't a door, it's a wound. You don't rip a wound open because you're cold."

"Your mother," Maghret said gently, "is four years dead, and died of the cold, in a country that used to grow oranges." She turned back to Sebastian, and her voice went quiet, and quiet was Maghret's most dangerous register. "Ilsa is young. Ilsa was born in the dark and doesn't remember the sun on Vellish stone. You do your own arithmetic, keeper. But know this: the Court has voted. The gate opens. The only question left is whether it opens with you or without you."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled by twelve feet of brick and earth, a cart rattled over cobblestones, and Sebastian flinched at the sound like a man remembering the ordinary world existed.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "what happens to me?"

"What happens to you?" Maghret spread her hands, and for one unguarded moment she looked tired, genuinely tired, a woman who had been keeping two hundred people alive on fat-lamps and stubbornness for twelve years. "You come home, Sebastian. That's all any of us ever wanted. To come home."

* * *

They let him go before dawn. Of course they did — Maghret wanted him willing, and willing men need to walk free awhile first — but Ilsa walked him up the tannery stairs herself, past Sefa's niche, out into air that tasted like snow that hadn't fallen yet.

At the top of Sallow Row he stopped and put his hands on his knees and breathed like a man surfacing.

"They knew about me," he said. "Six years. They've been watching me for six years and they just — let me live my life."

"You were more useful undisturbed."

"And now?"

"Now the wards are broken," Ilsa said, "and useful has changed meaning."

He straightened. In the gray pre-dawn his face was hard to read, but his voice wasn't. "That woman in there. Maghret. She'd burn Corren down to get her country back, wouldn't she."

"She'd burn herself down first," Ilsa said. "That's what makes her dangerous."

"And you? What do you want?"

It was the first time anyone had asked her that straight, and it caught her wrong-footed. What did she want? She wanted the stars. She wanted to see the country her mother sang about. She wanted to stop being a rumor's daughter. And she wanted, suddenly and badly, for this tired charm-seller to say yes, because if he said yes then everything she was would finally have a shape.

"I want the door open," she said. "Carefully. But open."

"Carefully," Sebastian repeated. He looked east, toward the market, toward his shop, toward the cellar where a girl had bled on his floor and a gate hummed under the flagstones like a sleeping animal. "There's a warden looking for me. Davorin Hale. He confiscated a charm with my mark on it. When he finds me, none of this—" he gestured at the tannery, the hidden stairs, the whole buried nation beneath the city— "none of it stays hidden."

"Then we move before he does," Ilsa said.

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

"It's the only answer there is." She pulled her coat tighter. The cold was coming off the ground now, off the stones, off the direction of his cellar, and she could feel it the way only someone born inside an erasure could feel it: the long winter reaching for them through the dark. "Come back tomorrow night. Maghret will want your answer. And Sebastian—" she hesitated, then gave him the only honest thing she had, "—two hundred people down there are going to spend today hoping. I've watched them hope before. It doesn't go well for anyone when it breaks."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he nodded once, turned, and walked into the waking city, a shopkeeper again, a nobody again, carrying twelve thousand lives home under his arm like bread.

Ilsa stood on Sallow Row until he was gone, and the frost crept up around her boots, and below her feet, faint as a heartbeat, the gate hummed.
