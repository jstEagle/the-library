## 7. Twelve Years of Small Magic

The Ember Court kept its candles lit at all hours, which Sebastian decided was either a comfort or an accusation, depending on how long you looked at them.

They had given him a room beneath the tannery — a real bed, a basin, a chair that didn't wobble. After two days in the frozen streets with Ilsa's hand clamped around his wrist like a manacle made of warmth, the luxury felt suspicious. Nothing in Corren was free. He'd built twelve years on knowing that.

The interrogation began on the third morning, and it began with tea.

Maghret poured it herself. She was smaller than she seemed in the great hall, he thought — a compact woman gone grey at the temples, with hands that moved like she was still doing some kind of work even when she wasn't. She set the cup in front of him without asking how he took it. Everyone down here assumed things about him. It was starting to feel less like courtesy and more like ownership.

"You slept," she said. Not a question. An observation, filed away.

"Eventually."

"Good. The mind does its best work after sleep." She sat across from him, folded her hands, and did not touch her own tea. "I'm going to ask you questions, Sebastian Villalobos. Some will seem small. None of them are. You may refuse any of them, and I'll note the refusal and move on. I won't threaten you. Threats make liars of honest people."

"That's a very reasonable speech."

"I've given it before." Something crossed her face — quick, buried, gone. "To people who mattered more than you'll ever know."

He drank the tea because his hands wanted something to do. It was bitter, cut with something resinous. Vellish, probably. Everything down here was Vellish, worn smooth by twelve years of being carried instead of lived in.

"Tell me about your shop," Maghret said.

"It's a shop. I sell charms."

"Which charms?"

"The ones people buy."

She smiled. It was a good smile, patient as frost forming. "Let me try again. A woman comes to you because her child can't sleep. What do you sell her?"

"A lull-charm. Birch wood, three knots, a thread of blue if I have it." He heard himself reciting it and stopped. "Why does this matter?"

"Because I want to know where the magic came from." She leaned forward slightly. "Every charm in this city has a source, Sebastian. Warden-blessed stock from the Crown foundries. Smuggled hedge-work out of the Marches. Or—" she tapped the table once, lightly, "—something older. Something that hums."

The word went through him like a splinter finding its depth.

* * *

He had never told anyone about the humming.

It had started the first winter, back when the cellar gate was just a door-shaped absence behind stacked crates, sealed with wax and superstition and a landlord's disinterest. He'd been inventorying stock at midnight, alone, when he felt it — not a sound exactly, more a pressure in the teeth, a note held so low and steady that his body registered it before his ears could refuse it.

Like a hearth fire burning in a house with no chimney smoke. Like something alive, keeping itself warm in the dark.

He should have reported it. Every warden's pamphlet said so: *anomalous resonance, report to your district office.* But reporting meant wardens, and wardens meant questions, and questions meant the name Villalobos entered a ledger somewhere and started gathering interest. So he'd stood in his cellar for one long hour, listening to a country breathe through a wall, and then he'd gone upstairs and told himself it was nothing.

It was never nothing. On bad nights — nights when the city felt like a coat that didn't fit — he'd come down and sit on the cold flags and let the humming hold him up. He'd thought of it as his. His secret, his comfort, his private hearth. He'd been sitting next to a dying thing and calling its last warmth *mine.*

"Sebastian," Maghret said gently. "You've gone somewhere. Come back."

"I've heard it," he said. "The gate. For years. Since my first winter here."

Maghret closed her eyes briefly, the way a person does when a prayer is answered and they hate what they prayed for. When she opened them, they were wet at the rims and absolutely steady.

"Twelve years," she said. "You've been hearing Vell breathe for twelve years."

"I didn't know what it was. Not at first."

"And later?"

Later. That was the wound under the other wounds. He looked into his tea. "Later I knew enough to be careful."

"Careful how?"

"I tuned my work to it." The words came out of him slowly, each one stepping off a ledge. "A charm is mostly structure, you understand. Wood, thread, salt, intent. The intent is the expensive part — it takes years to learn to pour yourself into an object without spilling. But if there's already power in the room..." He gestured vaguely, helplessly. "I wasn't creating. I was conducting. Drawing a little current off the hum and letting it do the heavy lifting. My charms worked better than they had any right to. Better than hedge-stock. People noticed. People came back."

"How much better?"

"Enough that I ate regularly. Enough that I rented the whole building instead of a room above it. Enough that—" He stopped.

"Say it," Maghret said. "All of it. We don't flinch down here. We can't afford to."

"Enough that I got a reputation." His voice had gone flat, which was how he knew it was bad. "Old Mother Quist, the midwife, sends everyone to me. Burned children. Fever cases. Women bleeding out on birthing sheets. I never turned anyone away — I want that understood. Whatever else is true, I never once turned anyone away."

"I believe you."

"But I charged them." Now it came faster, the confession finding its own downhill slope. "Not always money. Sometimes labor, sometimes silence, sometimes just their names in my book so I'd have leverage if the wardens ever came asking. But I took payment. From frightened people. For magic I didn't make, siphoned from a country I didn't save, while it froze in the dark behind my own cellar wall." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what the worst part is? I *liked* myself. Twelve years of small kindnesses, all of them paid for out of someone else's dying. I built a whole good man out of stolen heat."

The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft.

Maghret waited until the silence had done its full work before she spoke again.

"Do you know what we called people like you, in Vell?" she asked.

"Collaborators."

"Hearthsitters." Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "In the border towns, when a family lost its men to the wars, a neighbor would come sit by their fire. Not to warm herself — well, yes, to warm herself, no shame in that — but so the fire would be witnessed. So the house wouldn't go dark unnoticed. A hearthsitter was a parasite and a mercy both. Nobody ever resolved which." She pushed her untouched tea toward him. "You were our hearthsitter, Sebastian. The only one we had."

"That's generous."

"It's accurate. Generosity would be pretending you're innocent, and I don't deal in that." She rose and walked to the room's single window slit, high and narrow, showing a slice of frozen street. "Here is what matters now. The gate isn't humming anymore. It's *straining*. What you drew off it for twelve years — a thimble at a time — the erasure is drawing too, and it draws like the sea. Your little thefts were drops. The Crown's great theft was the ocean. And yet." She turned. "And yet you heard it. You sat with it. You never once tried to open it, or seal it, or sell it to the wardens, who would have paid a fortune for a live anchor. Do you understand how rare that is? Twelve years, and every instinct you had was to keep it company."

"I kept it company because it was convenient."

"You kept it company because you're your father's son."

The words landed before he understood them. "What did you say?"

Maghret studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide against answering. She gathered the teacups instead, moving with the brisk economy of a woman who had run a court, a resistance, and probably several funerals on the same pair of hands.

"One more question," she said. "Then you can rest. The woman with the sleeping child — the first one, the lull-charm with the birch wood. Did it work?"

"Yes."

"Did she ever come back?"

"Three times. Then her sister. Then her street." He remembered them: the queue of small desperate faces, the way he'd learned to read grief the way other merchants read weather. "There were hundreds, over the years. Maybe thousands."

"Thousands of people in Corren," Maghret said quietly, "have been warmed by Vell without knowing it. They've slept better, healed faster, grieved gentler — all on heat from a country they were told never existed." She set the cups by the door. "When the time comes — and it's coming soon — remember that. Remember that this city has already been drinking from Vell's cup for twelve years and calling the taste its own. That won't make your choice easier. But it will make it honest."

She paused at the door, hand on the latch.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the Court voted last night. While you slept. Unanimous." 

"On what?"

"On you." Now she looked back at him, and her eyes were kind in the way that a drawn blade is clean. "You're to be taken to the gate at moonrise. All of us. Ilsa will show you what you actually guard — not a door, Sebastian. A nation's only lung. And then you're going to tell us whether you'll help us open it."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," Maghret agreed. "But you drank the tea, and you answered the questions, and you sat with us in the dark for three days. Hearthsitters always do." She opened the door onto the corridor's candlelight. "Moonrise, keeper. Wear something warm. The cellar is colder than it used to be — we think it knows you're coming."

* * *

He dressed in the dark and did not look at the mirror, because he had spent twelve years not looking at mirrors and old habits were the load-bearing walls of a life.

Down in the corridor, the candles had been snuffed one by one behind him, as if the Court itself were closing its eyes. Ahead, Ilsa waited at the foot of the stairs, wrapped in a coat too big for her, her breath fogging in air that shouldn't have been cold indoors. Behind her, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the floorboards like water through stone, came the sound he had told no one about for twelve years.

Except it wasn't the same sound anymore.

The hum had changed key. It was thinner now, higher, frayed at the edges — a hearth running out of wood, a singer running out of breath. And underneath it, so faint he might have invented it, something that was almost a voice, almost a word, almost — he pressed his palm flat against the wall and felt the vibration stutter, stumble, and begin again —

Almost his name.
