## 9. What the Erasure Buried

The archive beneath the Ember Court smelled of tallow and cold stone, and it was smaller than a lie that big should have needed.

Ilsa had expected something grander — shelves climbing into darkness, a cathedral of paper. What she got instead was three rooms carved out of a wine cellar under a chandler's shop on Corren's south side, where the candles above masked the smell of old ink below. The Court had spent twelve years building this collection in secret: ledgers smuggled out in flour sacks, letters copied by hand by people who could not remember why they were copying them, maps of a country that officially did not exist, drawn from memory by cartographers who wept while they worked because they couldn't say what they were crying about.

She'd been given the task because she was good at reading. That was all. Maghret had put her hand flat on the first box and said, *You were born inside. The records will open for you faster than for us.* Ilsa wasn't sure what she meant — some quality of the erasure clinging to her like smoke to wool — but she'd sat down among the boxes and started reading, and the records did seem to come apart in her hands more easily than they should have. Wax seals softened at her touch. Coded columns resolved into plain Vellish as though the language recognized her.

For six days she read the ordinary history of a dying country. Grain tallies. Garrison rosters. A schoolteacher's diary from a town called Hesselmark, complaining about the price of lamp oil and the arrogance of capital officials. She read until the candle burned low each night, and each night she climbed back up into Corren feeling like she'd been swimming in someone else's bathwater — warm, close, faintly wrong.

On the seventh night she found the surrender.

* * *

It was filed wrong. That was the first thing she noticed. A folder marked *HARVEST DISPUTES, YEAR OF THE ASH FROST* — and inside, between two pages of grain-price arguments, a document with the seal of the Chancellery of Vell itself, the ember-and-garden sigil pressed deep into wax gone amber with age.

She almost skipped it. Her eyes were tired; the grain disputes blurred together; she wanted sleep and the thin barley soup the Court cooked in the cellar kitchen. But the handwriting changed mid-page, from a clerk's neat hand to something hurried, pressed hard enough to tear the paper in two places, and Ilsa had spent nineteen years learning to notice when adults stopped pretending.

*By order of the Chancellor and the Council of Nine, acting under emergency provision, the following terms are accepted without reservation.*

Then a list. She read it three times before her mind would let the words mean what they said.

The Crown of Corren demanded territory. Not tribute, not hostages, not the usual humiliations of a losing war. Territory — specifically, the border towns. Hesselmark. Osterlin. The Weir villages along the Senn. Eleven named settlements, their populations to be transferred to Crown authority, their lands annexed, their names struck from all registers of Vell.

And in exchange, the Crown would permit the remainder of Vell — the capital, the river cities, the heartland — to continue existing.

Not as a free country. As a protectorate, disarmed, its wards maintained by joint commission. But existing. On maps. In memory. In law.

There was a second page, and this one was worse, because it wasn't terms at all. It was a memorandum, unsigned but written in the same desperate hand, addressed to whoever came after:

*Understand what we have done. The war is lost. The Crown's wardens hold every approach and our own wards are failing from within — the anchor-lines were never built to last past a generation and the keepers are dead or scattered. We can save the center or we can save everything, and we cannot save everything. The border towns will be given over, and the Crown's erasure-craft will be turned upon them so that no trace remains to complicate the treaty. They will not remember being ours. In time, if the Protectorate holds, the erasure may be lifted and they may be returned to us.*

*If it does not hold, then let the record at least show that we chose. Let it show that we knew what we fed to the fire, and whose children we fed, and that we signed their names ourselves so that ours might be spared.*

*May whatever comes after judge us gently. We do not deserve it.*

Ilsa sat very still in the cold room and listened to her own heartbeat, which sounded suddenly loud, like someone knocking.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She went back through the boxes with a fury that made her hands shake, pulling anything dated near the Ash Frost year, and the picture assembled itself with horrible patience, like frost forming on glass.

The war had lasted nine years. Everyone in the Court knew that much — it was the one story they'd carried out whole, the story Maghret told new arrivals by firelight: *Vell fought the Crown to a standstill for nine years, and then the Crown did the unthinkable, and erased us.* It was a good story. It had heroes in it. It had an enemy you could hate cleanly.

But the garrison rosters told a different story. By the eighth year, Vell's armies weren't holding a standstill; they were dissolving. Desertion rates tripled. Supply convoys recorded losses not to enemy action but to rot and theft. One general wrote, in a letter that never reached its recipient because the courier defected, that he could field perhaps four thousand men against the Crown's twenty, and that his soldiers were boiling their boot leather.

And the wards — the wards everyone believed the Crown had shattered in a final act of sorcery — the wards had been failing for years. There was a commission report, stamped confidential, assessing the anchor-lines that held Vell's borders in the world. Most of the anchors were dead or dying. The report recommended, in dry bureaucratic language, that the country begin planning for the possibility of *localized boundary collapse within a decade.*

The border towns sat on those anchors. Of course they did. Borders always ran through the poor places; nobody built the wall through the garden district.

So when the end came, the arithmetic was simple and obscene. The Crown wanted the towns anyway. The towns' anchors were dying anyway. Give them over, let the Crown's erasure-craft do the work Vell's neglect had already begun, and buy the capital another generation behind a thinner, quieter border.

They hadn't even held out long enough to be conquered honestly.

Ilsa found one more document near dawn, and this was the one that broke something in her. It was a requisition order, mundane as bread, from the Office of Civil Transfer. It listed the households of Hesselmark — names, ages, occupations — under a column headed *PERSONS TO BE FORGOTTEN,* and beside each name, in a clerk's efficient shorthand, a disposition: *resettle, resettle, resettle, monitor, monitor, resettle.*

Her finger stopped on a name halfway down the page. *Villalobos, T. Keeper, third class. Disposition: extract.*

She stared at it. The name meant nothing. Keepers were a vanished profession; half the Court's oldest members claimed to remember what a keeper was and none of them agreed. She copied the name into her notebook anyway, because that was the discipline Maghret had drilled into her: *write down what you don't understand, girl. The erasure eats understanding first.*

She copied it, and moved on, and did not know yet that she had just picked up a thread that would unravel everything.

* * *

Maghret came down at midmorning, when the soup was heating and the other readers were shuffling up toward their bunks. Ilsa heard her boots on the stairs — Maghret walked like the floor owed her money — and had maybe ten seconds to decide whether to hide the documents.

She decided against it. That decision cost her more than she understood yet, but in the moment it felt simple: you didn't hide the truth from the woman who'd kept three hundred exiles alive under the nose of a nation that denied they existed. You brought her the truth like an offering, and you trusted her to carry it.

"Read this," Ilsa said, and pushed the surrender across the table.

Maghret read standing, fast, the way she did everything. Ilsa watched her face and saw nothing move in it for a long time — Maghret's face was a locked door, had been since the day Ilsa met her — and then, at the memorandum, at the line about feeding children to the fire, something did move. Not horror. Ilsa had braced for horror, or grief, or the wound of betrayal opening in the woman who'd lost everything to the erasure.

What moved in Maghret's face was calculation.

"They surrendered," Maghret said quietly. "The bastards surrendered."

"That's not—" Ilsa heard her own voice crack. "That's not the point. The point is they gave away the border towns. Our towns. They handed us to the Crown to save themselves, and the Crown erased us, and everyone in this Court has spent twelve years hating the wrong—"

"The point," Maghret said, "is that the treaty exists."

Ilsa stopped.

"A signed instrument," Maghret went on, tapping the page with one blunt finger, "under the seal of the Chancellery of Vell, acknowledging the Protectorate, committing the Crown to maintain the wards, to preserve the country in law. Do you know what that means, Ilsa? It means Vell was never legally destroyed. It means there is a paper trail from here to the throne room. It means that when we open the gate and bring Vell back, we won't be asking the world for a favor. We'll be enforcing a contract." Her eyes had gone bright, the way they did in council when a plan came together. "The Crown's own lawyers will choke on it. They can't call us rebels. We're creditors."

"You're not listening." Ilsa stood up too, and the chair scraped back loud in the little room. "The rulers of Vell sold us. Hesselmark, Osterlin, the Weir villages — they drew up lists of our grandparents and labeled them *persons to be forgotten*, and they signed it, and then they got twelve extra years that they still lost anyway. Everything. They traded us and still ended up erased. And now you want to use their signature as a weapon?"

"I want to use *anything* as a weapon," Maghret said. "That's what wanting your country back means. It isn't clean. It was never going to be clean." She gathered the documents into a stack, squaring the edges with a care that felt obscene, handling the evidence of the betrayal like something precious. "You're young. You were born inside, after. You think the homeland is a mother. It isn't. It's a body, and bodies do ugly things to stay alive, and you love them anyway or you don't."

"Somebody's grandmother boiled her boots for nine years," Ilsa said, "believing her government would never abandon her. And it had already signed the paper. It had signed the paper *before the war ended.*"

"Yes," Maghret said. "And she died forgotten, and the only thing that can un-forget her is the gate, and the gate needs a keeper, and you found me one." She tucked the stack under her arm. "Grieve later. We have work."

She went up the stairs. Ilsa stood alone in the archive among the boxes, in the smell of tallow and cold stone, and realized her hands were fists.

* * *

She didn't tell anyone else. Not yet. That was the part that shamed her most, later — not the discovery, but the silence afterward. She told herself she was protecting the Court from despair, that the truth would keep, that Maghret was right and the work came first. She told herself she'd find the rest of the record first, the full accounting, so she could present it whole instead of as a wound.

But underneath the telling-herself, something simpler was happening. The Ember Court was the only home she had ever known. She had been born inside the erasure, inside the long winter, inside the story where Vell was innocent and stolen and worth any price to restore. If the homeland wasn't innocent, then the story wasn't true, and if the story wasn't true, then she didn't know who she was loyal to, or why, or what she'd been raised to want.

So she did what the archivists taught her to do with a document that frightened her. She kept reading.

Three nights later, cross-referencing the transfer lists against the Court's own membership rolls — the secret registry of every exile, every descendant, every name the Court had painstakingly reassembled — she found the pattern that made her blood go cold.

The border towns hadn't just been erased. Their people had been *moved*. The Crown's erasure-craft hadn't dissolved eleven settlements into nothing; it had folded them somewhere, displaced them, walked thousands of men and women out of the world and left them walking. And the Court's registry — compiled from dreams, from half-memories, from strangers in Corren who woke weeping without knowing why — kept turning up names from Hesselmark and Osterlin and the Weir villages. Not descendants. The people themselves. Still alive. Still here, somewhere in the city, paperless and memoryless, invisible in plain sight.

The erased hadn't been destroyed. They'd been hidden. And somebody in the Crown had known — somebody had built the erasure with a door in it, a way out, a threshold—

Ilsa wrote it all down in her notebook, her hand moving fast, and at the bottom of the page she wrote the question that mattered more than all the rest:

*Where does the door out open?*

She looked at the requisition list again. At the name she'd copied the first night, the keeper, third class, extracted. *Villalobos, T.*

And beneath it, in the clerk's hand, a note she'd missed before, small as an apology:

*Line continues. See attached.*

There was no attachment. There was only the empty space where one had been torn away, ragged at the edge, recently — the paper pale where the missing sheet had shielded it from the dark.

Someone in the Court had gotten there first.
