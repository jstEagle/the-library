## 10. The Long Winter Begins

The frost came up through the floorboards like something rising from a drowned ship.

Sebastian noticed it first in the cellar, where it had no business being. It was the third week of Harvestmonth and the nights were cool but not cold — the kind of autumn that let you keep the shop shutters open past dusk. Yet there it was: a lace of white climbing the stone around the gate's frame, precise as etching, beautiful as anything he'd ever sold. He crouched and touched it and pulled his fingers back fast. Not cold. *Hungry.* The frost drank warmth out of his skin the way his charms drank from the gate.

Behind him, on the stairs, Ilsa said, "It's started."

"Started what?" He didn't turn around. "Give me the word for it. You're the one who speaks the language."

"*Vintergang*," she said. "The walking winter. When the erasure starts to eat itself." She came down the steps slowly, her bare feet leaving prints that filled with frost before she reached the bottom. "My grandmother told stories about it. I thought they were stories."

"Your grandmother was born inside a country that doesn't exist. Her stories are load-bearing now."

He hadn't meant it to come out bitter. But everything he'd said lately came out with an edge, because every hour since the stars went out, the gate had been humming louder — not the low contented drone he'd lived beside for twelve years, but a rising note, a kettle-note, the sound of something holding pressure it was never built to hold. He could feel it in his teeth when he bit bread. In his hips when he climbed stairs. In the scar tissue of a life he'd built one careful secret at a time.

Ilsa knelt beside him at the gate. Up close, the frost on its frame wasn't random. It grew in lines, in *patterns* — spirals and interlocking knots that Sebastian recognized with a lurch, because he'd been carving them into charms for a decade without knowing what they meant. Vellish knotwork. The gate was writing itself into the world's skin.

"It's leaking," Ilsa said. "The door out is holding, but this—" she touched the cellar gate, the little iron-banded thing he'd tended like a garden trellis, "—this was only ever a window. Windows break."

"How long?"

"I don't know." She looked at him, and for once the girl who called him *keeper* looked young. Nineteen, maybe twenty, and carrying a country on her back. "Days. Maybe less."

* * *

By morning the frost had reached the street.

Not spread — *reached*, the way news reaches, all at once and everywhere. Sebastian stood in his doorway with a cup of tea going cold in his hand and watched white creep along the gutters of Tallow Row against the grain of the slope, uphill, against reason. The cobblestones wore it like fur. The iron lamp-posts bloomed with it, feathers of ice growing off metal in warm air, and where it touched the wooden shutters of Madame Ostrey's hat shop next door, the wood grayed and went brittle as old bone.

Madame Ostrey came out, touched her own shutter, and frowned. "Sebastian. Is your cellar damp? This is damp damage, surely."

"Probably," he said. "Old buildings."

She nodded and went back inside, satisfied, and Sebastian watched the frost take the corner of her sign — the little painted hat curling white at its brim — and understood that Corren was going to keep explaining this to itself right up until it couldn't. Cities were good at that. A city would rather believe in damp than in magic; he'd built twelve years of anonymity on exactly that faith.

By noon the wardens' bells were ringing the cold-call — the signal for citizens to report burst pipes and shelter the poor — and by evening the frost had crossed the canal.

He took stock like a merchant, because taking stock was the only prayer he knew. His stock of coal: nine days. His stock of food: more, if he was honest about rationing. His stock of lies: thinning. Every charm in his front case had gone strange overnight — the warming-stones hot enough to blister, the calm-knots buzzing like trapped flies, the little luck-brassers weeping condensation. Whatever fed them was overfeeding now, gorging them, the way a body gorges a fever.

"You should close the shop," Ilsa said from the stairwell. She still wouldn't stand in the front room if she could help it. Too many windows. "If the wardens trace the cold—"

"The wardens can't trace cold. Cold is everywhere."

"Cold that grows in *patterns* isn't." She held out her hand, palm up, and he saw the frost had found her too — a fine white rime along her knuckles, delicate as sugar-work, that didn't melt when she came near the stove. "Everything touched by Vell freezes first. Then everything near it. Then everything." She closed her fist. "I'm touched, Sebastian. So are you. We just haven't noticed yet."

He wanted to argue. He was very good at arguing with her; it was almost domestic, the way they did it over breakfast. But his hip ached in time with the gate's hum, and his breath hung in the warm room like it belonged to winter, and somewhere under Tallow Row a country that had been murdered quietly was coming apart loudly, and arguments needed a world with rules.

"We go to the Ember Court tonight," he said. "Maghret will know more."

"She'll want something too."

"Everyone wants something. That's what makes them reliable."

* * *

The way to the Court took them through streets already changing.

Corren's poor quarter froze first, because the poor quarter always got everything first — plague, tax collectors, weather. Sebastian walked with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and counted the wrongnesses. Frost on the south faces of buildings only. Ice on the canal that formed in rings, like tree rings, like it was recording years. A baker's oven that wouldn't light no matter what was burned in it, the baker standing in his doorway telling anyone who'd listen that the flour had gone bad, the air had gone bad, something had gone bad.

And the refugees.

They'd started appearing two days ago, the watch said — people turning up in alleys and church porches and the mouths of cellars, dazed, half-frozen, with no papers and no names they could hold onto. The watch called them the Nowheres. There were forty in the cold-shelters already, maybe more. The law said you arrested people without papers, but the law also said you sheltered the freezing, and the watch sergeants of the poor quarter had solved the contradiction the way sergeants always do, by looking hard in the other direction.

Sebastian saw three of them huddled under the eaves of the tannery, and stopped without meaning to.

An old man. A woman of middle years. A boy of maybe ten, asleep against the woman's shoulder. They sat in a row with the terrible patience of people who have been waiting somewhere a long time, and their clothes were wrong — cut in a style Sebastian hadn't seen since he was a child, wool dyed madder-red and woad-blue in patterns that made his chest hurt to look at. Vellish border-weave. The kind his mother used to buy at the autumn fair in—

In.

He stood there while the memory knocked and knocked and refused to come through the door.

The woman looked up at him. Her eyes were pale, washed-out, the eyes of someone whose name has been taken out and the hole papered over. "Beg pardon," she said, in accented Correnish, "but have you seen our town? We were walking home and the road went strange."

"What town?" Sebastian asked gently.

She frowned, reaching. He watched her reach. It was like watching someone grasp at smoke. "It had a church," she said finally, uncertain. "Or a mill. Something with a wheel." The boy stirred against her. "We've been walking a long time. Everything's white now."

"Frost," Sebastian said. "There's frost on everything."

"No," the old man said, and his voice was quiet and certain and awful. "White *before*. White behind us. We walked out of the white." He looked up, and for a moment the washed-out eyes focused, sharp as needles. "You're Vellish," he said. "Underneath. I can smell it on you. Boy, you're Vellish underneath, and you don't even know it."

Sebastian's mouth was dry. "I'm from Corren."

"Nobody's from anywhere anymore," the old man said, satisfied, and looked away, and the conversation was over the way doors close.

Ilsa touched his elbow. "Border towns," she whispered. "The ones the Crown took during the surrender. They weren't destroyed, Sebastian. They were *folded in* — pushed into the erasure with the rest of us. And now the erasure is collapsing, and it's spitting them out." Her hand tightened. "They're coming out with holes where their memories were. The erasure ate twelve years of them on the way through."

"How many are there?"

"In the border towns? Before?" She did the arithmetic she must have done a hundred times, the arithmetic of a country. "Eleven thousand. Give or take the dead."

Eleven thousand people walking out of nowhere into a city that had agreed, twelve years ago, that nowhere existed. Sebastian looked down Tallow Row, at the frost climbing the walls, at the watchmen building barricades of nothing against weather, at the cold-shelters filling with the paperless and the nameless, and felt the deadline land on him like a weight.

Because here was the thing he understood, standing in the street, that he hadn't understood in the cellar: this wasn't a leak. It wasn't an accident. The erasure had been a wound stitched shut with a lie, and the lie had rotted, and the wound was opening — and when it opened all the way, it wouldn't be eleven thousand confused people staggering into alleys. It would be the whole country, all at once, every soul Vell had ever held, arriving in Corren in a single unmanageable instant. Or it would be nothing. Collapse inward, all of it, everyone, gone the way the stars had gone — not killed, just *unremembered*, unwritten, until even the frost forgot what it was growing on.

Mercy, it turned out, had a deadline. Nobody had told him that when he was learning to be kind in small installments.

* * *

The Ember Court received them in the old cistern hall beneath the soap-works, and Maghret received them standing, which she never did for anyone, which meant she already knew.

"The border towns are surfacing," Sebastian said, before she could perform whatever greeting she'd prepared. "Forty in the shelters. More in the alleys. They remember nothing and they're freezing faster than the locals, and the watch doesn't know whether to arrest them or bury them. And the frost is spreading in Vellish knotwork up my street. So. Tell me how long."

Maghret studied him for a moment — that long, weighing look she had, the look of a woman pricing goods. She was fifty-ish and handsome the way a drawn blade is handsome, and she wore a coat trimmed with fox fur that, Sebastian now realized, had probably been a flag once.

"Three weeks," she said. "Perhaps four. The Court's readers put it at the new moon after next, when the tide of the erasure turns fully inward." She gestured, and the hall's lamps guttered, and he saw that the cistern walls were frosted too — white knotwork climbing the ancient brick, the same script as his cellar, the same handwriting. "Everything Vell touched is becoming Vell again, because Vell has nowhere else to be. Your street. My hall. The bones of everyone born inside. And the girl most of all."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "I'm not a weather report."

"You're a *barometer*," Maghret said, not unkindly, which was worse. "Born inside the erasure, made of it. When it goes, dear one, you go with it — unless we make it into something else first." She turned back to Sebastian, and now the blade came out of the velvet entirely. "Which brings us to why I let you walk into my hall looking like a man who's already decided something. You want to save them. The border towns. Eleven thousand souls wandering in with holes in their heads. So does the Crown, in its way — it wants them registered, taxed, scattered, dissolved. And so do I, in mine. I want them *home*."

"There is no home," Sebastian said. "That's the whole problem."

"There is a *door*," Maghret said. "And a keeper standing in front of it, asking me for timetables." She stepped closer. The frost-light made her eyes silver. "Three weeks, Sebastian Villalobos. Three weeks to open the gate wide and bring a nation through before the nation stops existing. Or three weeks to seal it and bury eleven thousand people alive in the dark with their holes where their homes were. Those are your choices. Choose quickly. Winter doesn't wait on consciences."

He opened his mouth to say *those aren't the only choices* — some instinct, some third-door instinct he didn't yet have words for — and that was when the horns began.

Not the watch bells. Horns. Deep, braided, three-note calls rising from the streets above, echoing down through the soap-works grates, and Ilsa went rigid beside him because she knew them — everyone born inside the erasure knew them — the call-signals of Vell's own militia, the ones that shouldn't exist anymore, sounding from the frozen streets of Corren.

"That's not us," Maghret said sharply, to the hall, to her people, to herself. "We didn't call that."

No, Sebastian thought, already moving toward the stairs, already knowing. Not the Court. Not the watch.

Out on Tallow Row, in the snow that was falling upward past his shuttered windows, the border towns had found each other in the dark — the old man, the woman, the boy, hundreds more, walking out of nowhere in their madder-red and woad-blue — and they were forming ranks in the drifts outside his shop, around his cellar door, around the gate they could not remember but their feet clearly could.

And at the head of them, planted in the ice like a standard, stood a banner Sebastian had never seen and recognized instantly: the garden-and-ember, the old arms of Vell, risen from a country that officially had never existed at all.

The horn sounded again. Third note hanging. Waiting.

For him.
