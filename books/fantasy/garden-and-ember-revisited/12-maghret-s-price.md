## 12. Maghret's Price

Ilsa knew something was wrong before she reached the Ember Court, because the candles were burning the wrong color.

The Court kept its hall beneath a tannery in the Salt Ward, down a stair that smelled of lime and old hides, and for as long as she had known them they burned tallow — cheap, smoky, honest. Tonight the sconces along the passage flared blue-white, cold as starlight, and Ilsa stopped on the third step with her hand flat against the wall because she recognized the light. It was Vellish. It was the light of hearth-charms and border-wards, of the country behind the gate, and it meant someone had opened the Court's reserves. Someone had decided the time for hiding was over.

She had left an hour ago to walk Sebastian back to his shop through the frozen streets. An hour. In that hour the whole world under Corren had changed its mind.

The sentry at the bottom of the stair was a boy called Tam, fifteen years old, born inside the erasure like her, and he wouldn't meet her eyes. That was the second wrong thing. Tam always met her eyes; Maghret said eyes were the first country you lost and the last you defended.

"Where is she?" Ilsa asked.

"The long hall." He hesitated. "Ilsa — she's been asking for you."

* * *

The long hall had been a curing room once, and its stone walls still held the ghost-smell of salt. The Ember Court had hung it with everything they'd saved: a scrap of the Vellish flag gone gray, a carved lintel from a farmhouse door, forty years of names written on paper that wasn't supposed to exist. Ilsa had grown up beneath those names. She had learned to read by tracing them.

Maghret stood at the far end with her back to the hall, hands clasped behind her, watching the largest object they owned — a slab of true Vellish slate, pried from a schoolhouse wall during the exodus, on which someone had chalked the old alphabet. She was a big woman, broad as a door, with iron-gray hair cut short and a voice that could carry across a frozen square without effort. Twelve years ago she had carried three hundred people out of a collapsing border town on her back, more or less literally, and she had never once set them down.

"You went to see him again," Maghret said, without turning. "The keeper."

"I walked him home. His street is watched. I thought—"

"You thought." She turned then, and Ilsa saw the others: the whole Court assembled along the walls, thirty faces, and every one of them looking anywhere but at her. On the table in the center lay objects laid out with terrible neatness. A charm of Sebastian's make — one of the little brass-and-thread warmth tokens he sold in his shop like a fool selling pieces of his own heart. A scrap of paper with a warden's seal pressed into black wax. And a length of cord, knotted in the signal-code the Court used on the streets above.

Someone had followed her. Someone had counted her visits.

"We found this in the ash-bin outside the tannery," Maghret said, touching the charm without picking it up. "His maker's mark. And this—" the paper, the seal, "—was taken off a warden's courier we intercepted tonight. Davorin Hale is in Corren. And this knot-code was copied from our own book, which means either our book has been stolen or one of us has been walking messages to the enemy." Her voice stayed level, which was how Ilsa knew how bad it was. Maghret only went quiet when she was deciding what to destroy. "So. Tell me about the bargain, Ilsa. Tell me what the keeper and the butcher of Vell offered each other while you stood witness."

"It's not a bargain like that." Ilsa made herself stand straight, made her voice come out steady. "Hale doesn't want the gate. He wants the truth. He wants testimony — the border towns, the surrender, all of it — written down and witnessed before the gate is sealed forever. He's going to give it to the Crown. To the archives. To history."

Silence. Somewhere overhead, muffled through stone and frost, a cart bell rang.

"To history," Maghret repeated softly. "And who keeps history, child? The Crown that erased us? The same Crown whose warden wrote our name out of the maps with his own hand? You'd hand our dead to the men who killed them and call it justice?"

"He confessed. In the frozen square, in front of the border towns themselves—"

"I know what he did in the square. I have people everywhere, Ilsa; did you think I didn't?" For the first time something moved behind her eyes, something almost like pain, quickly buried. "I know he knelt in the snow and said *I did this* and some soft-hearted souls wept and forgave him. Forgiveness is free. It costs the forgiver nothing and the forgiven less. What I want to know is why my sworn sister was standing next to him when he did it."

Because I believed him. Because I read the archive and I know what our own rulers did and I can't un-know it. Because forgiveness isn't free, Maghret, it's the most expensive thing there is and somebody has to pay it—

What she said was: "Because it's true. All of it. The surrender, the traded towns, Hale's orders. If we open the gate and pour Vell back into the world without the truth, we're just another lie with a flag."

"Then we'll be a lie with a flag and a country," Maghret said. "A living lie beats a dead truth, Ilsa. Ask anyone freezing behind that gate."

* * *

They put her in the small room — not locked, never locked, because the Court didn't lock doors, they just filled them with people who loved you until leaving felt like betrayal. Old Sefa brought her tea. Tam sat in the corner and picked at his bootlaces and finally said, "You're not a traitor, are you?" and Ilsa said, "No," and he nodded like that settled it, and she hated how much she wanted it to be settled.

Through the wall she heard the Court arguing. Not shouting — Maghret didn't permit shouting — but the low grinding murmur of a family deciding something enormous. She caught fragments. *The influx.* *Born inside.* *Unmade.* She caught Sefa's voice cracking on a word she couldn't hear, and Maghret's voice answering, calm as deep water, and the murmur going silent after.

When they called her back, the hall had changed. The blue-white hearth-light burned brighter. The Court stood in a half-circle around the slate, and Maghret stood before them holding the oldest thing they owned: a key of black iron, longer than a forearm, wrapped in wire that hummed faintly when she shifted her grip. The key to the original threshold. They had carried it out of Vell twelve years ago at the cost of nine lives, and Ilsa had seen it exactly once before, at her swearing-in, when Maghret had held it over her head and said *this is heavier than it looks.*

It was heavier now. She could see it in Maghret's wrists.

"Sit," Maghret said, and Ilsa sat, alone in the middle of the floor like a defendant, because that was what she was.

"You found the archive records," Maghret said. "The surrender. The traded towns. I'm not going to insult you by denying it. Yes — our government sold two border provinces to the Crown to buy the capital twelve more years. Yes, the people in those provinces were erased alongside the rest of us rather than let them testify. I've known since before you were born." She let that land. "And I led the exodus anyway. I carried those people anyway. Do you understand why?"

"Because they were ours."

"Because a country is not its government," Maghert said, and her voice finally cracked, just once, like ice underfoot. "A country is a grandmother teaching a song. It's bread shaped a particular way. It's the way we say *come in from the cold* — do you remember how we say it? No. Of course you don't, you were four. But your bones remember, Ilsa, and so do mine, and that is what I am trying to save. Not the ministers who signed. Not the map. The bones."

"And the price?" Ilsa's mouth was dry. "Tell me the price, because I heard them talking through the wall. The influx. When the gate opens fully — when Vell comes back into the world — everyone born inside the erasure gets—"

"—remade," Maghret said simply. "Yes. The erasure runs both directions, child. We built a wall between the world and Vell, and the mortar was forgetting, and if you tear the wall down the forgetting tears down with it. Everyone born in the shadow — you, Tam, sixty others of ours, thousands behind the gate — will be unmade and remade. New memories. Maybe new names. Maybe nothing at all." She crouched, bringing her face level with Ilsa's, and her eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "I have spent twelve years refusing to spend lives. I have rationed our magic like bread. I have buried eleven of our own and paid for every grave. So believe me when I tell you I did not choose this price lightly. But I am choosing it. Because sixty children of the shadow weighed against an entire nation waking — because you would die *free*, Ilsa, you would die *Vellish*, instead of fading year by year into a girl who speaks a dialect nobody remembers—"

"That's not your choice to make!"

"No," Maghret agreed gently. "It's yours. That's why I'm telling you first, before the others, before the plan. Because you're the one who found the threshold's true nature. Because you're the one the gate answered when we tested it. And because—" and here her voice dropped to the register she used for funerals, for oaths, for the naming of the dead, "—because I love you, daughter-of-my-exile, and I am asking you to walk into that gatehouse knowing what it costs, and to say yes anyway. For the bones. For the song. For everyone who ever taught you *come in from the cold*."

The hall was utterly silent. Thirty people who loved her, watching. The blue-white light made them all look like ghosts already.

And Ilsa understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was how fanaticism worked. Not with cruelty. With love. With tea brought to your locked room and a boy picking at his bootlaces and a woman calling you *daughter* while she measured out your death like flour. The Court had spent twelve years being a family because families were the only country they could carry, and now the family needed a sacrifice and had dressed it in tenderness, and every face around the walls was saying *we would never ask this of anyone we didn't love completely.*

"I need to think," Ilsa said.

"Of course." Maghret straightened, and the warmth in her face folded away like a map being closed. "Take until dawn. But Ilsa — the keeper and the warden move tomorrow night. Whatever you decide, decide it before they take the choice away from you."

* * *

They let her walk the perimeter alone, which was its own kind of message: we trust you, come back, please come back. The frost had climbed the tannery stairs and silvered the street door. Above, through a grate, she could see the sky where stars should be — the same starless dark that had fallen the night the wards broke, the dark that had started all of this.

Tam found her at the grate. He didn't speak for a long time. Then: "If you go with them — the keeper and the warden — will you come back after? After the gate opens?"

"I don't know what happens after," Ilsa said honestly.

"If I get remade," Tam said, in the flat careful voice of someone reciting something rehearsed, "will I still be me? Will I remember the Court? Will I remember—" he stopped, ears going red, and Ilsa understood he'd been about to say *you*.

"I don't know," she said again, and put her hand on his head like Maghret did, and hated herself for borrowing the gesture.

He looked up. "Maghret says the ones who fade don't get to choose anything. At least this way we choose."

"That's what she says."

"Don't you believe her?"

Ilsa looked at the starless sky through the grate, at the city that had forgotten Vell so thoroughly it had forgotten it had forgotten, at the boy beside her who existed only because a country had been hidden and hidden well.

"I believe she loves us," she said. "That's the worst part."

* * *

Dawn came gray and colder. Ilsa stood in the long hall before the slate with the old alphabet, and she took the piece of chalk that hung on its string beside it — the last chalk from the last schoolhouse — and beneath forty years of names she wrote three words in careful Vellish letters:

*I refuse the price.*

Not *I refuse the gate*. Not *I refuse Vell*. Just the price. Just the arithmetic that said sixty children were worth spending. She wrote it where the whole Court would see it, because Maghret had taught her that a refusal spoken in private was a doubt, and a refusal written in public was a country.

Then she went up the stairs, past the sentry post, past Tam's stricken face, out into the frozen streets of Corren, toward the shop of a charm-seller who didn't yet know that the Ember Court was coming for his gate — and that their key was already humming.

Behind her, faint through stone and frost, she thought she heard Maghret's voice giving orders, calm as deep water, and the sound of the blue-white hearth-lights being fed.
