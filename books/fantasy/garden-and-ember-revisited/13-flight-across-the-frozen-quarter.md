## 13. Flight Across the Frozen Quarter

They ran through streets that had stopped being Corren.

Sebastian had lived in this city for twelve years. He knew its slums the way a man knows his own hands — which alleys bent, which stairwells stank of tallow, which doors opened for coin and which only for blood. But frost had rewritten all of it. The frozen quarter glittered under no moon, lit instead by the pale seep of Vellish magic bleeding up through the cobbles, and every familiar corner had become a stranger's face.

"Left," Ilsa said, tugging his sleeve.

He went left. Behind them, somewhere back near the Ember Court's tunnels, something heavy fell — a door, or a man, he didn't want to know which. Maghret's people moved quietly when they wanted to and not at all when they didn't, and tonight they did not want to.

Davorin ran beside him, breathing hard, his warden's coat traded two hours ago for a fishmonger's oilskin that made him look like exactly what he was: a powerful man dressed as a poor one. He carried no lantern. Wardens never needed one; their eyes had been trained on dark things so long that ordinary darkness had given up trying to blind them.

"How far?" Sebastian gasped.

"The tannery district," Ilsa said. "There are people there who'll take us in."

"People," Davorin said, "who know you?"

Ilsa glanced back at him, and in the strange light Sebastian saw her expression clearly for the first time since the Court's tunnels: she looked like someone walking toward an answer she'd spent her whole life avoiding.

"They know what I am," she said. "That's different."

* * *

The frost thickened as they went east. It wasn't weather-frost. Sebastian had grown up enough around magic to know the difference by now, though knowing it made it worse. Weather came from above. This came from below, from the cellar gate he'd tended without understanding, spreading outward through the city's bones like ink through wet paper. Doorways wore collars of ice. Wells had frozen from the bottom up. A fountain in a square they crossed stood mid-pour, water hanging in the air like a held breath.

And there were people in the streets. That was the thing that kept snagging his attention even as they ran — people standing in doorways, huddled under awnings, watching the three of them pass with eyes that were not afraid. In a city where fear was the default currency, these people watched with something closer to recognition.

A woman stood outside a shuttered bakery, wrapped in a shawl worked with a pattern Sebastian knew. He'd sold charms to a dozen women wearing that pattern over the years and never once asked where it came from. Now, running past, he saw it whole: interlocking gardens and flames, garden and ember, stitched in red thread gone grey with age.

Vellish. It had been Vellish all along.

"Don't stop," Ilsa said, but her voice cracked on the second word, because she'd seen it too.

* * *

The tannery district smelled of salt and old hides and, now, of cold. The buildings leaned together like conspirators. Ilsa led them down a passage barely wider than her shoulders, knocked twice on a low door, paused, then knocked three more times — a rhythm, Sebastian realized, that matched no code any Corren smuggler used. It was older than codes. It was a song. Four notes of some lullaby he almost remembered from a life he couldn't recall living.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, took in Ilsa, then Sebastian, then lingered on Davorin with open hostility.

"He's with us," Ilsa said.

"He's a warden."

"He's a confession," Ilsa said. "Let us in, Bren. The Court is behind us and the cold is ahead of us and I need to sit down before my legs figure out they're allowed to give out."

The chain rattled off. The door swung wide.

Inside was warmth of the stubborn, rationed kind — a single brazier, a kettle, a family of five arranged around it like planets around a dying sun. An old man mending nets by touch. Two children asleep against each other under one coat. A young mother who rose when they entered and put herself between the strangers and the children without appearing to move at all.

"Bren Osta," the man with the eye said, closing the door and dropping the chain. "Border town of Harrow's End, if you're keeping records." His voice was flat, rehearsed, the voice of a man who had said it so many times to people who didn't believe him that belief had become irrelevant. "Which nobody's keeping. So it doesn't matter."

"I believe you," Davorin said quietly.

Bren turned on him. Up close, Sebastian could see the hatred had structure to it, load-bearing hatred, the kind a man builds a life inside. "You believe me. That's generous. Twelve years I've believed myself, warden. Believing was the easy part. Nobody else would do it. Not the registries. Not the parishes. Not the courts. We walked out of the erasure with nothing — no papers, no names anyone recognized, no memories that held still. You know what the Crown does with people who can't prove they exist?"

"I wrote those procedures," Davorin said.

The room went very still. Even the kettle seemed to hold its steam.

"You what?" Bren said.

"He helped erase Vell," Ilsa said. "He built the wards. He's here to testify. That's why Maghret wants him dead and wants Sebastian dead and probably wants me dead by morning."

"And you brought him into my house."

"I brought the truth into your house," Ilsa said. "It travels badly. It needs shelter same as anything."

Bren looked at her for a long moment. Then at Davorin. Then, last and longest, at Sebastian — and something in his face shifted, some door opening onto a room Sebastian couldn't see.

"You," Bren said slowly. "You're the keeper."

"I don't—"

"The gate hums for you. Our people feel it. Every soul that walked out of the erasure feels it, faint, like a hearth in a house you were told burned down." Bren's voice had changed, flattened into awe or grief or both. "Twelve years we've felt you tending it. Selling your little charms off its edge. We thought—" He stopped. Started again. "We thought maybe you knew. We thought maybe you'd chosen not to come."

Sebastian felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where shame keeps its accounts.

"I didn't know," he said. "I swear it. I didn't know what it was until four days ago."

"No," Bren said. "No, I believe that too. That's the worst part. None of you ever knows. That's how it works, isn't it? You don't erase a country with soldiers. You erase it with forgetting, and then everyone gets to be innocent forever." He sat down heavily on a stool, suddenly old. "Sit. Drink. The girl's right — truth travels badly, and yours looks half-frozen."

* * *

They drank nettle tea that tasted of smoke and patience. The children slept on. Outside, the frost ticked against the shutters like something counting.

And the family talked.

Not all at once — it came in pieces, the way floodwater comes through a broken dam, first a trickle and then everything. The old man mending nets was Aldric Vey, of the town of Saltmere, and he could describe its harbor so precisely that Sebastian could smell it: tar and brine and the particular sweetness of the reed-beds in late summer. The young mother was Tam, from Colder's Ford, and her daughter had been born eleven years ago in a Corren cellar, and the child had never once seen the river her mother named her for. Bren himself had walked out of the erasure with forty neighbors and no memory of the road they'd taken, arriving in Corren's outskirts like rain that had always been falling.

"We're not refugees," Tam said. She said it gently, without heat, the way you correct a child. "Refugees come from somewhere. We come from nowhere. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole problem."

"The border towns," Ilsa whispered. She had her hands pressed flat against her knees, and Sebastian realized she was shaking. "Chapter nine of the archive. The surrender terms. They traded the border towns to the Crown to save the capital, and the Crown erased them first because erased towns can't object." She looked up at Sebastian. "They weren't destroyed. They were *moved*. Pushed through the wards into the space between, and then the space between was folded shut, and the ones who survived the folding walked out here with holes where their histories used to be. They've been here the whole time. All twelve years. Invisible."

"Invisible," Bren agreed. "Best kind of neighbor. Pay no taxes, draw no notice, die quiet." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "Your city has been full of us, keeper. Serving your beer. Sweeping your streets. Standing in your doorways while you walked past feeling sorry for us and never once asking why we had no answers."

Sebastian thought of every charm he'd ever sold to a customer whose papers were wrong. Thought of how he'd prided himself on not asking questions. Thought of the woman in the ember-and-garden shawl, and the dozens like her, and the way he'd filed them all under *poor* and moved on.

He had walked through a hidden country every day of his hidden life, and neither of them had seen the other.

"I'm sorry," he said, and heard how small it was, and hated it, and said it anyway.

Tam studied him across the brazier. Her face was young and tired and utterly unsentimental.

"Sorry's a start," she said. "Keep going after it. Most people stop at sorry and call it a destination."

* * *

Near dawn — if dawn could be said to exist under a starless sky — the knocking came.

Three soft raps. Then the pause. Then, instead of the lullaby's four notes, two.

Bren was on his feet before the sound finished. He blew out the brazier with one motion and the room dropped into darkness, and in the darkness Sebastian heard Davorin's whisper, close to his ear:

"That's not their knock."

"It's the Court," Ilsa breathed. "Two notes means *found you*. Maghret's runners use our own songs against us. She learned them from the archive." Her hand found Sebastian's wrist in the black. "Back door. Through the drying yards. Don't run until you're clear of the building — they listen for running."

They moved through the dark house by touch, Bren pressing a bundle into Sebastian's arms as they passed — bread, a flask, a child's knitted glove that Sebastian understood was meant as luck. At the back door Tam caught Ilsa's arm.

"There's a house on Tanner's Row," she said fast, low. "Blue door, third from the salt store. Ask for Maret Low. Tell her Saltmere remembers. She'll take you to the others — the ones gathering. If your warden means to testify, he'd better do it while there's anyone left to hear it. The cold's moving faster now. You've seen it."

Through the gap in the shutters, Sebastian looked out at the street. Frost climbed the opposite wall in patterns too deliberate to be natural — spirals, and within the spirals, if he squinted, letters. Old Vellish script, the writing he'd seen on Ilsa's charms, blooming across the brickwork in ice.

It wasn't random. The frost was writing. And though he couldn't read it, he understood it the way you understand a voice calling your name from another room.

*Come home,* the ice said. *Come home, keeper.*

Maghret hadn't sent the knock at all, he realized. Or she'd sent it, and this too. Both. Pressure from every direction, herding them toward the gate.

"Go," Bren whispered, and unchained the door.

They slipped out into the frozen dark, three shadows among many, and behind them the ice finished its sentence on the wall, letter by glacial letter, patient as winter:

*The door opens either way.*
