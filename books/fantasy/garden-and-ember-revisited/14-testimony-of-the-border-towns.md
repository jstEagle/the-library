## 14. Testimony of the Border Towns

Davorin Hale had spent thirty years learning how to make people talk, and in all that time he had never once been on the other side of it.

The frozen square had no name anymore. The maps called it Ashen Cross, a market junction in the drowned quarter where the frost ran thickest and the lamplighters had stopped bothering with their rounds. Now there were braziers — a dozen of them, dragged from cellars and workshops, burning anything that would burn — and around them stood perhaps three hundred people who did not officially exist.

They had walked out of nowhere over four days. Old men in coats cut to a fashion Corren hadn't seen in twelve years. Women carrying children wrapped in blankets dyed the deep ember-red of Vell. A whole street's worth of faces that matched no registry, no census, no warden's list. The Crown would call them illegal. The paperwork hadn't caught up yet. Paperwork never did.

Davorin stood at the edge of the square with his warrant case under his arm like a shield he'd forgotten how to use, and watched them arrange themselves.

They arranged themselves the way Vellish people arranged themselves for testimony: oldest first, in a half-circle facing inward, so that the speaker faced the fire and the fire faced the speaker and everyone else could see both truths at once. He knew the custom. He had helped write the manual on it, back when the Crown still bothered to study the country it intended to erase. *Know what you are burying*, his first commander had said. *A grave dug blind is a grave dug twice.*

He had thought of himself as a gravedigger ever since. It was more honest than architect.

Sebastian Villalobos stood near the largest brazier, arms crossed against the cold, looking like a man who had been arguing with himself for two days and losing. Ilsa was beside him, her jaw set, her hands buried in a coat that had belonged to someone from one of the border towns — someone who had pressed it on her without asking her name. Maghret's people were absent. That absence had a shape Davorin recognized. It was the shape of an army regrouping.

An old woman took the half-circle's center. She was small and straight-backed, with frost in the grey braid coiled at her neck, and when she spoke she did not raise her voice. She didn't need to. The cold carried sound strangely well; every word arrived crisp as struck flint.

"My name is Sera Voss," she said. "I was born in Keltern Bridge, which was a town on the Vellish side of the border, and which is now nothing, because we were told it was nothing." She paused. "I am seventy-one years old. I have been erased for twelve of them."

Davorin's breath came out white and slow. *Keltern Bridge.* He knew the name. Of course he knew the name. He had written it down himself, in the third week of the operation, on a list of settlements whose removal would be — the phrase was his own, he remembered composing it in his neat warden's hand — *strategically survivable.*

"Tell us how it happened," said a young man near the fire. Not hostile. Just hungry for the shape of it. They all were. Twelve years inside a country that wasn't, and none of them had ever been allowed to know why.

Sera Voss looked into the fire for a long moment.

"On the last night," she said, "the wardens came through our town in wagons with no markings. They told us there was plague. They told us we were being relocated for our health. My husband believed them because my husband believed everything printed on official paper." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "They marched us east along the river road until we reached a stone marker, and then they turned us south, and then the stars went out, and then we were walking in a country that had no outside. We walked toward Corren for twelve years. Some nights we could see its lights through the frost like a city behind glass. We could never reach it. The erasure kept us turning back, like water finding its level."

She lifted her chin.

"We did not die in a war. We did not die of plague. We were traded. Our towns were given to the Crown as payment, and the Crown erased the receipt along with us." She let that sit in the cold air. "I want it written down. I want it spoken in front of witnesses. I want the words to exist somewhere the frost cannot reach them."

"They exist," Sebastian said quietly. "We're writing everything down."

Davorin's hand moved before his mind agreed to it. He stepped forward into the half-circle, and three hundred heads turned, and he felt the particular silence of a crowd deciding whether he was a guest or a target.

"I wrote that list," he said.

His voice sounded strange to him. Flat. Unarmored. He had rehearsed nothing, which was unlike him; he had always rehearsed everything, every raid, every interrogation, every lie. But this was not a thing that could be made better by craft.

"Sera Voss. Keltern Bridge. The relocation order came from the Office of Border Integrity, and the settlement triage list was drafted by Senior Warden Davorin Hale. That's me. I decided your town was strategically survivable. I chose which towns to feed into the erasure and which to leave standing, and I chose based on grain output and road access and how much noise each removal would make." He heard his own voice do the thing it did in reports — the tidy bureaucratic cadence — and hated it, and kept going anyway. "I was twenty-nine years old. I believed the Crown's arithmetic. I have spent twelve years knowing better and doing nothing, and doing nothing is also a choice, and I made it every morning for twelve years."

The silence held. Somewhere a brazier popped and spat.

"You're the one," someone said from the back. Not a question.

"I'm the one."

An old man rose from the half-circle's edge. He moved slowly, favoring his left leg, and when he reached Davorin he stopped close enough that Davorin could see the frost-scars on his cheeks — the lacy white burn patterns the long winter left on anyone it touched too long.

"My daughter was nine," the old man said. "When the wagons came. She asked me if they'd have books where we were going." His voice did not shake. That was the worst part. It was steady as a level. "You want absolution. You stand here in front of us because confessing is lighter than carrying it."

"Yes," Davorin said. "Both things are true. I want to be lighter. And I want you to have the truth. I can't give you the first without giving you the second, and I won't pretend otherwise."

The old man studied him for a long time. Then he spat, carefully, into the snow between them, and turned, and walked back to his place in the half-circle. It was not forgiveness. It was not condemnation either. It was simply accurate, and Davorin found he could bear accuracy better than he'd expected.

* * *

They testified until dawn.

Not all of them — there wasn't time, and the cold took its toll — but enough. A ferryman who had poled the last families across the border river while the wards came down like a curtain. A schoolteacher who had kept teaching for six years inside the erasure, using a single primer copied out by hand until the letters wore off the page. Twin sisters who had been born inside, who had never seen a star, and who listened to the others' descriptions of night skies with the polite skepticism of people being told about dragons.

Sebastian sat with the ledger open on his knees, writing in his careful charm-seller's script, and Ilsa translated the old dialect phrases that surfaced now and then like stones in a stream. Davorin gave them what he had: names, dates, order numbers, the architecture of the machine he had built. Every fact he surrendered was a fact the Crown could hang him with. He surrendered them anyway, and with each one he felt something in his chest loosen that he had mistaken for a structural feature of himself rather than a knot.

Near morning, Sera Voss came to him while the others slept in shifts around the braziers.

"You'll be arrested," she said. It wasn't a warning. It was weather forecasting.

"After tonight? Certainly."

"And you came anyway."

"The truth doesn't work if only the victims hold it," Davorin said. "That's just grievance. It has to be held by the ones who caused it too, or it stays a wound instead of becoming a record."

The old woman considered him for a moment, frost glittering in her braid.

"My husband believed everything printed on official paper," she said. "I decided, after, that I would believe nothing printed on any paper. You are asking me to believe a man." She almost smiled. "It is a harder thing to ask. But I find I am considering it."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"Good. Hope less. Do more." She turned away, then stopped. "Warden. The girl — Ilsa. She was born inside, like the twins. Whatever door you're all whispering about, whatever choice is coming—" her eyes were suddenly very sharp, very old, "—remember that some of us have lived our whole lives in the dark and built lives there anyway. Don't you dare decide for us that darkness is unlivable. We are the experts. Ask us."

"I will," Davorin said, and meant it, and understood that meaning it might be the only clean thing left in him.

* * *

The Crown arrived an hour after dawn.

Davorin heard them before he saw them — boots on frozen cobbles, the disciplined crunch of a column that did not need to hurry because it had never once been resisted. Two dozen wardens in winter grey, halberds rimed with frost, and at their head a captain he knew slightly: Captain Ondrey, a career officer with a reputation for following orders the way rivers follow downhill.

The square woke all at once. Three hundred paperless people stood up out of the snow, and the sound of it — the collective scrape and breath of the erased rising to face the state — was the loudest thing Davorin had heard in twelve years.

Ondrey advanced to the center of the square and unrolled a writ.

"By order of the Crown, this assembly is unlawful. All persons present will submit to registration and—"

"They already have," Davorin said.

He walked forward, alone, into the space between the column and the half-circle, and set his warrant case down in the snow, and opened it. Inside were the documents he had spent the night assembling: the triage lists in his own handwriting. The relocation orders bearing his countersignature. The settlement ledger, complete, cross-referenced, witnessed by a novelist's careful script and a girl born inside the dark.

"Every person here is registered," he said. "In this book. With their name, their town, and the date their town was removed — by me, on behalf of the Crown, twelve years ago. If the Crown wants them registered, here is the registry. If the Crown wants the author of their situation—" he closed the case, "—here he is. I surrender myself. Take me. Leave the record."

Ondrey looked at the case. Then at Davorin. Then, unavoidably, at the three hundred silent faces behind him, and Davorin watched a career officer arrive at the understanding that arrests were easy and records were not.

"Hale," Ondrey said slowly, "you're asking me to arrest a senior warden on the strength of his own confession."

"I'm ordering you to. Consider it my last order as your senior."

The frost crackled. Somewhere in the crowd, Sera Voss stood very straight, watching.

And then the light changed.

It came from the east, from the direction of the market quarter, from the cellar where a gate hummed under a floor — a pale, wrong, auroral shimmer climbing the frozen air, and beneath it a sound like a great door being tested in its frame. The refugees knew the sound instantly. Davorin saw it hit them in a wave: the twins clutching each other, the schoolteacher going white, Sera Voss's hand flying to her mouth.

"It's opening," Ilsa breathed, at the edge of the square. "Someone's forcing it. She's actually—"

Davorin knew who *she* was. He had known since the shimmer began. Maghret had run out of patience, and patience had been the only thing holding her back.

He picked up his warrant case, and turned to Captain Ondrey, and said the truest sentence of his life:

"Captain, I withdraw my surrender. There's a door about to come off its hinges, and I'm the only man in this city who knows exactly what happens if it does. Arrest me later — or better, follow me, and arrest whoever's on the other side of it."

Behind him, three hundred erased people began, quietly, to move toward the light.
