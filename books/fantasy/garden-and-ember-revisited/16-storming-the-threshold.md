## 16. Storming the Threshold

Ilsa heard the gatehouse fall before she saw it — a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, stone grinding against stone somewhere beneath the frozen streets of Corren.

She was in the Ember Court's archive when it happened, sitting with the surrender ledgers spread across her knees, and for one long moment she thought the sound was her own heart breaking loose of its moorings. Then the lamps guttered blue, every charm in the room waking at once, and she knew. Maghret had moved.

*The night after the testimony,* Maghret had promised, smiling that smile that made you feel chosen and owned in the same breath. *While they're still weeping in their frozen square.*

Davorin Hale had confessed in a public square two nights ago. Ilsa had stood at the back of the crowd and watched an old man kneel in the snow and name his crimes aloud, and she had watched some people forgive him and watched others turn away, and she had felt something shift in herself that she still didn't have words for. She'd gone to Maghret afterward, meaning to argue for patience, and found the Court already arming.

Now the archive door banged open and Brenn stood there, breath steaming, frost on his lashes. "They want you topside. Maghret's orders."

"Which top?"

"The cellar." His face did something complicated. "Ilsa — the keeper's cellar. We're through."

---

The tunnels under Corren had been Vellish once, before the erasure, and walking them now was like walking through a body that remembered being alive. The walls sweated warmth on one side and frost on the other, as if the country behind the gate and the city above it were arguing through the stone. Ilsa ran with thirty of the Court at her back, boots hammering, and tried not to count how many of them were children of the erasure like her — born inside a country that officially did not exist, who had never seen a star.

That was the thing Maghret always said. *You've never seen a star.* As if the sky owed them something. As if the debt could be collected by force.

The keeper's cellar was already full when she arrived. Court soldiers lined the walls, torchlight jumping off their breath. The old gate — Sebastian Villalobos's quiet little cellar door, the anchor, the last threshold — stood open wider than Ilsa had ever seen it, wide enough to show a slice of what lay beyond: snowfields under a starless black sky, the distant smudge of Vell's capital burning its hearth-fires against the cold. Cold poured out of it into the cellar like water finding its level. Frost climbed the wine racks. Frost feathered across the ceiling beams.

And in the middle of it all, Maghret.

She stood with her back to the gate, arms folded, watching four of her strongest work at the doorframe with pry-bars and chisels and charms that made Ilsa's teeth ache. They weren't trying to open the door. It was open. They were trying to take the door *out* — rip the whole threshold free of its frame, tear the anchor loose from the wall of the world, so that nothing could ever close it again.

"You're late," Maghret said, without turning. "Good. You should see this."

"Maghret." Ilsa's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Where is he? Where's the keeper?"

"Gone to fetch something. He'll be back." She smiled over her shoulder. "He always comes back. That's what keepers do."

"And Davorin Hale?"

"Being dealt with." A flicker of something hard crossed her face. "The traitor's bargain dies with him. When the door comes out, it won't matter what the Crown knows or doesn't know. There will be no Crown jurisdiction inside Vell. There will only be Vell."

The pry-bar team struck together. The whole cellar rang like a bell, and beyond the gate, far off across the snowfields, Ilsa saw lights flicker in the capital — hearth-fires guttering, steadying, guttering again. Every blow they landed here landed there too. She understood that suddenly, viscerally, the way you understand a thing with your skin: the door wasn't a door. It was a rib. They were breaking the country's chest to widen its mouth.

"Stop," she said.

Maghret turned fully around then, and the cellar went quiet the way rooms go quiet when a knife comes out, even unseen.

"Say that again?"

"Stop." Ilsa stepped forward, past the torchline, into the cold spilling from the gate. Her breath tore white. "You're killing it. Don't you feel it? Every hit — the fires are dying over there. You're not opening a door, you're—"

"I'm freeing my country." Maghret's voice stayed gentle, which was worse than shouting. Gentle was what she used when she thought someone needed managing. "Twelve years, Ilsa. Twelve years of hiding under another nation's streets, eating their scraps, breathing their air, being *no one*. And now the wards are down and the door stands open and you want me to wait — for what? For the keeper's permission? For the warden's paperwork?" She laughed softly. "For truth? Truth is a story the strong tell afterward. I was born in the dark, little ember. I am done waiting for dawn to be *granted*."

"The border towns walked out," Ilsa said. "You saw them. Thousands of them, in the frozen quarter, with no memories and no papers. If you rip the threshold loose while the influx is still unstable—"

"Then Vell pours into the world all at once, and the world has to swallow." Maghret shrugged. "Some choke. Nations have been built on worse."

"Some *choke*." Ilsa heard her own voice crack. "People born inside the erasure, Maghret. People like me. The influx doesn't distinguish between the ones who left and the ones who were never let out. You know the archive readings as well as I do. Everyone born in the shadow of the gate is tied to it. Rip it out and—"

"And you'll be free or you'll be finished, and either way it will be *quick*, which is more than the erasure gave anyone else." Maghret came closer, and up close her eyes had the flat shine of ice over deep water. "You've been reading the enemy's books, Ilsa. Sitting with the warden's confessions. Letting the keeper's guilt get into you." She reached out and touched Ilsa's cheek, almost tenderly. "I found you starving in a storm drain when you were nine years old. I fed you. I taught you the old tongue your own parents were erased out of remembering. Everything you are, the Court gave you."

And there it was. The whole architecture of her life, laid bare in three sentences. Ilsa had known it was true for days — since the square, since the ledgers, since she'd read the names of the border towns traded away by Vell's own rulers and felt the ground tilt under everything she believed. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud, as a claim of ownership, were different animals entirely.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "You gave me everything. And now you're asking me to help you kill everyone you gave it to."

The pry-team struck again. This time the crack that answered them came from everywhere at once — from the walls, from the floor, from Ilsa's own bones — and beyond the gate, one of the distant hearth-fires went out and did not come back.

---

She moved before she decided to move.

Later — in the months after, in the registry offices with their endless queues of the paperless — she would try to reconstruct those few seconds and find that memory had taken them apart and hidden the pieces. What remained was this: the pry-bar rising. Maghret's hand lifting in the signal. The oldest soldier, Vetch, setting his shoulder against the doorframe for the final strike.

And Ilsa throwing herself into the gap between the bar and the wood.

The impact took her across the collarbone. She heard it go — a wet green-stick sound, felt the whole left side of her light up white — and then she was on the flagstones with frost biting through her coat and thirty faces staring down at her, and the pry-bar hanging slack in Vetch's hands because no one had ever trained for this, for one of their own putting her body in the way.

"Get her out of the line," Maghret said. Her voice hadn't changed at all. That was the horror of it. "She's confused. She'll thank us when the stars come back."

"She won't." Ilsa rolled onto her knees, cradling the arm, and looked up at the woman who had raised her. Blood was in her mouth; she couldn't tell if she'd bitten her tongue or if it was just the cold. "Because I've read the ledgers, Maghret. I've read what our rulers traded away to save the capital. You know what's funny? You hate the Crown for erasing us. But you're doing exactly what they did. Sacrificing the border towns. Sacrificing anyone inconvenient. Trading lives for the *idea* of a nation." She got her feet under her, swaying. "Vell isn't worth that. Nothing is worth that. Not even home."

Something moved behind Maghret's eyes — hurt, maybe, real hurt, buried fast — and then it was gone, and what looked out at Ilsa was simply a commander assessing a problem.

"Take her weapons," Maghret said. "Put her in the north passage. When the door is out, she can walk into Vell with the rest of us, or she can stay here and freeze with the keepers and the wardens and everyone else who loved their chains." She turned back to the gate. "Resume."

They bound Ilsa's arm against her chest with a strip of banner-cloth — someone's kindness, or someone's habit; the Court took care of its wounded even when the wound was treason — and marched her toward the north passage. And Ilsa let them. She went quietly, head down, counting doors.

There were three ways out of the keeper's cellar. The stairs up into the shop, which Maghret held. The south tunnel to the Court, which Maghret held. And the north passage, narrow and half-collapsed, running along beside the gate itself — the passage where the threshold's power bled thinnest, where the frost grew teeth, where nobody liked to go.

They put her in the north passage because it was the closest. They put her there because it was worthless.

It was the most valuable ground in Corren.

---

The cold in the north passage was a living thing. It came off the gate in slow tides, and where it touched the stone it grew structures — ridges, arches, filigrees of ice that looked almost written, almost language. Ilsa pressed her back against the wall and breathed through the pain in her collarbone and watched the passage mouth, forty paces away, where a single bored sentry paced.

She had perhaps minutes. Maybe less. Somewhere above, Sebastian Villalobos was coming back — Maghret had said so, *he always comes back* — and when he came back, he would find his cellar full of soldiers prying his door out of the world. Unless someone told him. Unless someone bought him time.

Her arm screamed when she moved it. She made herself move it anyway, working the banner-cloth loose with her teeth and her good hand, and as the binding slipped she felt the break settle wrong and nearly blacked out. She bit down on the sound. Nine years old, starving in a storm drain, and Maghret had taught her the first lesson of the Court: *pain is information, not instruction.* Well. Here was information.

The sentry passed. Turned. Passed again.

On his third turn she stepped out of the ice-shadow where she'd been standing and put her good hand flat against the gate's frame.

The world went through her like a bell being rung.

Every charm she'd ever carried, every word of Vellish she'd ever learned, every winter she'd ever endured — all of it lit at once, and through it she felt the threshold itself, vast and patient and *wounded*, feeling the pry-marks in its frame the way a person feels broken ribs. It knew her. Of course it did. She'd been born inside its shadow; she was one of its children whether she liked it or not. And it was dying, or its country was, and it had been waiting twelve years for someone to ask it a question instead of give it an order.

*I can't hold them forever,* she told it. *But I can make them slow.*

The gate poured cold into her, and she poured it back out.

Frost detonated down the north passage — not spreading, *growing*, walls of it rising between the sentry and the gatehouse, sealing the passage mouth in a lattice of ice thick as a castle wall. The sentry hammered on it. Somewhere beyond, shouting. Boots. Maghret's voice, sharp and furious, ordering chisels brought forward, ordering fire-charms, ordering *Ilsa found, Ilsa found, bring me Ilsa.*

They'd have to cut through their own ice to reach the gate. Fire-charms against Vellish frost, in a passage soaked in threshold-power — it would take time. It would take longer than they wanted. It would take exactly as long as she could make it.

Ilsa slid down the gatepost to sit on the frozen floor, cradling her arm, and laughed until it hurt, which did not take long.

All her life she had wanted one thing: to belong somewhere so completely that belonging could never be taken away. Maghret had offered it wrapped in certainty — *we are right, we are owed, we will take what is ours* — and Ilsa had drunk that certainty for ten years like it was warm milk. Now she sat bleeding and freezing in a collapsing tunnel, exiled from the only family she'd ever had, guarding a stranger's door with one working arm, and she had never in her life been more certain of anything.

Not certain of the outcome. That was the difference, she realized. Maghret's certainty had been about being *right*. This was different. This was just — choosing. Choosing uncertain truth over certain belonging, with no guarantee on the far side except that she could live with herself there.

The ice wall boomed. Cracks spidered through it, glowing orange where fire-charms ate at the far side.

"Hold," she whispered to the gate, to the passage, to her own stubborn failing body. "Just hold."

Boots on the stairs above. Many boots — but moving fast, unevenly, not a formation. And cutting through the noise, a voice she knew, hoarse with cold and fear, shouting her name down into the dark.

Sebastian Villalobos had come back.

Ilsa hauled herself upright against the gate, set her shoulder — the good one — against the frame, and prepared to explain, very quickly, why the door out of nowhere was currently guarded by a girl with a broken collarbone and no plan at all.

Above her, the first fire-charm breached the ice.
