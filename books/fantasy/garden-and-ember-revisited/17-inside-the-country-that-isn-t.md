## 17. Inside the Country That Isn't

The gate did not open so much as admit him.

Sebastian had expected a door, a seam, some violence of light. Instead the cellar wall simply stopped being a wall, and he was walking through cold that felt like recognition — like stepping into a room he'd dreamed a thousand times and never once described correctly.

Then grass. Real grass, silvered with frost, sloping down toward a river valley where a city lay under a sky the color of old pewter.

Vell.

He stood very still and let it hurt him.

He had imagined this moment for twelve years without admitting he was imagining it — on slow market mornings, in the gap between customers, in the half-second before sleep took him. He had pictured ruins, maybe. A wasteland. Something punished and empty that would let him feel righteous about having run.

It was none of those things.

It was beautiful.

The city of Vellemark spread along the river in terraces of pale stone, roofed in slate gone green with moss, its towers crowned with weathervanes that had nothing left to tell them — no wind up here, or none that mattered. Orchard rows climbed the hillsides in disciplined lines, bare now but pruned with care, every tree tended. Lamplight glowed in windows even though the sky offered no night and no day to measure it against. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, orderly columns. Someone was still sweeping a street down there. Sebastian could see the small figure of it, moving back and forth, back and forth, keeping a country clean that the world had agreed to forget.

*They kept it,* he thought, and his throat closed. *All this time, they kept it.*

Ilsa came through behind him and stopped at his shoulder. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. He could feel her watching him see it.

"You said it was dying," he managed.

"It is." Her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant she was holding something down. "Dying things can be beautiful. That's the trick of it. That's why nobody wants to look."

He looked anyway.

The frost was the tell. Once he saw it, he couldn't unsee it. The frost wasn't sitting on the grass the way frost should — melting at the edges, breathing with the ground. It was *in* the grass, threaded through the blades like veins, and where the orchard rows climbed highest, the trees stood gray and glassy, leaves frozen mid-fall around them, hanging in the air like a held breath. The river ran, but slowly. Too slowly. Sebastian watched a leaf drift downstream at the pace of a funeral.

And above it all, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not haze. An absence with texture to it, a ceiling of smooth dark, as if someone had painted over the sky and the paint was starting to peel at the edges of the world.

"Twelve years," Ilsa said. "No sun that rises. No seasons. The days don't move. We count time by lamplight shifts." She hugged her arms against a cold that seemed to come from inside her. "You get used to it. That's the worst part. You get used to anything."

* * *

They walked down into Vellemark, and the people came out to look at him.

That was the part he hadn't prepared for. Not the beauty, not the wrongness of the frozen orchards — the *people*. They emerged from doorways and alleys and gathered at street corners with the quiet, hungry attention of the starving, and they looked at him the way you look at a letter that's been twelve years in coming.

An old woman touched his sleeve as he passed. Just his sleeve, two fingers, briefly, and then she withdrew her hand and stepped back into the crowd as if she'd taken communion.

"They know what you are," Ilsa said quietly.

"I'm a charm-seller from Corren. I sell luck knots to sailors and fever-charms to mothers who can't afford a physician."

"You're the keeper. You held the anchor open for twelve years. Every charm you ever siphoned from that gate — every little warmth you sold — that was Vell leaking out into the world. People here could feel it. Faint. Like hearing music through a wall." She looked away. "Some of them hated you for it. Taking from a dying country to decorate your life. Most of them just... hoped. Hoped the leak meant the keeper would come someday."

"And I never came."

"You never came. You didn't even know." She said it quickly, and he understood she was defending him, and he understood also that she shouldn't have to.

A man near the fountain — a big man, a stevedore's build, hands like shovels — spat when Sebastian passed. Nobody scolded him. Nobody echoed him either. The spit landed in the frost and froze there, a small dark jewel, and the crowd absorbed Sebastian again as though nothing had happened, which was somehow worse.

*You profited,* said the voice in his head that sounded like Maghret. *Twelve years of small magic, and every coin of it drawn from these people's dying hearth.*

He had told himself the charms were kindnesses. Fever-charms for children. Luck for the desperate. He had priced them low. He had given away more than he sold. And every one of them had been a cup dipped into a country that was freezing to death, carried out through a gate he didn't know he guarded, poured out for strangers who would never know the cost.

Kindnesses. Complicity wearing kindness's coat.

"Where are we going?" he asked, because he needed to be walking toward something instead of drowning in place.

"The archive," Ilsa said. "There are people you need to meet first. Then the records. Then—" She stopped. "Maghret will want to see you before anything else. But I wanted you to see the city first. Before she makes her case. I wanted you to see it with your own eyes, not her words in your ears."

"Why?"

"Because her words are true too," Ilsa said. "And it's easier to resist a lie than a truth that's starving."

* * *

They fed him in a hall off the temple square — a long room with a hearth at each end, both burning low, rationed down to embers. The bread was dark and dense and real. The stew was mostly root vegetables, and it was hot, and it was the best thing Sebastian had ever eaten, and he hated himself for thinking so.

Around him, the people of Vell ate in silence broken by small talk, the way people do everywhere. A girl argued with her brother about whose turn it was to fetch water. Two old men played a board game with pieces carved from chair legs. A mother sang under her breath to a baby — an actual baby, born here, inside the erasure, who had never seen a star.

Sebastian set down his spoon.

"How many?" he asked Ilsa.

"Born inside? Like me?" She counted with her eyes, table by table. "Here, four. In the whole country, thirty-one. The rest remember the other side. Remember Corren. Remember the surrender."

"The surrender," he repeated, and the word sat strangely in his mouth. Everyone here used it casually, the way you'd say *the flood* or *the fire*. As if it were weather. As if it hadn't been a choice.

"You've heard," Ilsa said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a country this size; his conversation with Davorin, the alliance, the plan to reach the threshold — Maghret would have known within hours.

"That Vell wasn't conquered. It traded its border towns to the Crown to save the capital. That my—" His voice snagged. "That the negotiator who signed it was my father."

"Yes."

"And they still want me to open the door."

Ilsa turned her spoon in her bowl, around and around. "Wanting and forgiving aren't the same animal. Some of them want you because you're the keeper. Some want you because you're the treaty made flesh, and opening the door with your own hands would be a kind of justice. And some—" she glanced at the hall, at the quiet eaters, at the low fires— "some just want to go home, and you're the only door there is. Motives mix. People are allowed to be complicated. Countries too."

"And which are you?"

She looked at him then, fully, and he saw how tired she was — nineteen years old and carrying a country's hope like a yoke. "I was born here," she said. "I've never seen a star. When I found out what Vell did — traded towns, sacrificed thousands to save itself — I wanted to hate it. I tried." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, young and old at once. "But it's hard to hate a place that feeds you. Harder still when the feeding gets thinner every year."

* * *

Afterward, an old man walked him up through the terraces toward the palace district — not the Ember Court's hidden halls below Corren, but the actual seat of Vell, still standing, still swept, still flying a banner whose colors had faded to ghosts of themselves. His name was Aldric, and he had been a warden of Vell before the erasure, and he walked with the stiff dignity of a man who has decided his body will hold until the work is done and not one step further.

"You'll want to see the surrender chamber," Aldric said. "Before anyone fills your ears. See it empty."

The chamber was exactly that: a round room with a round table, tall windows looking out on the frozen orchards, and dust sheets over the chairs. Twelve years of dust, undisturbed except by their footsteps. On the table lay a document under glass, its wax seal cracked, its signatures ranked down the page like a row of executed men.

Sebastian read the names without meaning to. Crown signatories. Vellish signatories. And there, third from the bottom, in a careful clerk's hand: *Tomas Villalobos, Envoy Plenipotentiary.*

His father's name. His own surname. Written into the death of a country in ink that had dried before Sebastian learned to write.

"He signed it in this room," Aldric said. "I stood guard outside. I heard him afterward, in the corridor. He was sick. Actually physically sick, against the wall, like a boy who'd drunk too much. And then he straightened his coat and walked out to face the crowds and tell them it was the only way." Aldric's voice was dry as the dust sheets. "I have spent twelve years deciding whether that makes him a monster or a martyr. I have concluded it makes him a man, which is worse, because men are what we have to work with."

"He raised me in Corren," Sebastian said. "He never spoke of Vell. Not once. He grew tomatoes. He went to church. He died when I was twenty-two, and I buried him, and I grieved him, and I never knew—" His voice failed. He pressed his palm flat against the glass over the document, over his father's name, as if he could warm it. "Was it true? Was it the only way?"

"No," Aldric said simply. "It was *a* way. There were others. Costlier ones. Ways that required courage instead of arithmetic." He looked out the tall windows at the gray orchards. "Your father chose the way that saved the capital and damned the borders, and then the Crown erased us all anyway, because mercy invited is mercy exploited. That is the lesson of Vell, keeper. Never mistake it for wisdom."

* * *

They came for him at dusk — or what passed for dusk, the lamplight shift, the hour when a country without a sky pretends to have an evening.

Not soldiers. Vell had no army anymore. Just people: the stevedore who had spat, and the old woman who had touched his sleeve, and dozens more, filling the surrender chamber's doorway, silent, waiting. Word had moved through the city like frost through soil.

Maghret stood among them, and even here, in her own country at last, she wore her authority like a blade worn openly. She did not speak first. She let the silence do it.

Sebastian faced them. All of them. The saved and the sacrificed, the born-here and the fled-here, the ones who remembered Corren and the ones who had only ever heard of it as a rumor called *the world*.

"I'm not going to make speeches," he said. His voice sounded small in the round room. "I've read the surrender. I know what my father did. I know what your own rulers did, trading your towns like coin. I know this country isn't innocent." He swallowed. "I also know it's dying. And I know you've waited twelve years for a keeper, and the keeper was selling luck knots three streets from your border the whole time."

The stevedore shifted. The old woman bowed her head.

"So ask me," Sebastian said. "Whatever you want to ask me. Ask me now, while I'm still honest. Because tomorrow Maghret will have her case, and Davorin will have his testimony, and everyone will start telling me what justice looks like, and I won't be able to hear myself anymore."

For a long moment, nobody moved.

Then the old woman stepped forward, out of the crowd, and stood before him, and looked up into his face with eyes gone pale as the frost on the orchards.

"My town was Brenholt," she said. "On the border. Your father's signature sent it to the Crown, and the Crown's magic sent it nowhere. My husband is in that nowhere. My daughters. My grandsons." Her voice did not shake. That was the most terrible thing about it. "I don't want your guilt, keeper. Guilt is cheap and we are past buying. I want to know one thing, and I want you to answer it the way you answered everything tonight — honestly, before you learn to be clever."

"Ask," Sebastian said.

She gripped his wrist, and her hand was cold as the country around them, and her pale eyes held his.

"When you open the door — *if* you open the door — will you save the people, or will you save the place? Because they are not the same thing anymore. And whatever you choose, someone in this room will bury someone because of it."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere beyond the walls, the frost crept another inch up the glass of the world.

Sebastian opened his mouth to answer — and found, for the first time in his life, that honesty had run out.
