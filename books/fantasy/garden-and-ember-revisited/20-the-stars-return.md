## 20. The Stars Return

**Sebastian**

The last of them came through at dusk.

Sebastian stood at the cellar gate and counted them out the way a man counts his own heartbeats — not because he needed to know the number, but because stopping would mean something had ended. An old woman in a coat stitched from three other coats. A boy carrying a birdcage with no bird in it. A pair of twins who walked through hand in hand and did not let go on the Corren side, as if letting go might undo them. Behind each of them the door stood open onto Vell: starless sky, white fields, the black teeth of mountains he had never seen but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face from a dream you've been having all your life.

Ilsa stood beside him at the threshold, her breath smoking in air that was two temperatures at once — Corren's thawing frost on one cheek, Vell's deep winter on the other.

"How many left?" Sebastian asked.

"However many are left," she said. "There's no list. There was never a list." She said it without bitterness now. That was new. Two days ago it would have been a knife; tonight it was just weather.

They had stopped trying to count somewhere past four thousand. The Ember Court's cellars had emptied into the streets above, and the streets had emptied into the market square, and the square had become something no one in Corren had ever seen: a crowd of people with no papers, no names the registries would accept, standing in the falling slush and waiting to be told they existed.

Maghret had crossed at midday. She had walked through the gate upright, chin high, past Sebastian without a word, past Ilsa without a word, and had not looked back at the country she'd wanted to resurrect. Sebastian kept expecting to feel triumphant about that and kept not feeling it. She had lost. Everyone had lost. The only prize anyone was going to take home was being alive, which is a prize you only appreciate when you've watched someone refuse it.

"Keeper." One of the last men paused at the threshold, an old fellow with a woodcutter's shoulders and eyes gone pale from twelve years of snowlight. "You'll close it behind us?"

"I'll seal it," Sebastian said. "From this side."

"Good." The old man nodded slowly. "It should be shut by someone who chose to stay out."

He stepped through, and then there was no one left on the Vell side but the country itself.

Sebastian looked back once. He had promised himself he wouldn't, and then he did. Vell lay under its lightless sky like a body under a sheet: the terraced farms gone gray, the capital's towers furred with ice, the garden districts — *garden*, the word had been in the country's name for a reason — buried under drifts that had been falling since the night the stars went out. Somewhere in there were empty houses with warm hearths nobody would sit at again. Somewhere in there was his father's signature, carved into a treaty table, in a room that was about to stop existing.

"Ilsa," he said. "It's time."

She took his hand. Her fingers were cold and certain.

"Together," she said. "You're the keeper. But I was born inside. It should be both of us or neither."

So they closed it together — Sebastian laying his palms flat against the old wood, Ilsa's hands over his, and speaking the words Davorin had taught him, the architect's own words turned inside out, unbuilding instead of building. The gate did not slam. It exhaled. The winter air of Vell poured out around them in one long silver ribbon, and then the wood went simple under his hands — just oak, just iron, just a door in a cellar wall that led to coal dust and cobwebs and nothing else.

The silence afterward was enormous.

And then, above Corren, the sky caught fire.

* * *

**Davorin**

Davorin Hale was sitting in the frozen square with the refugees when it happened, which is where he had decided to be when they came for him.

He had spent the day doing the thing he dreaded more than any cell: listening. The border townspeople told their stories in the flat voices of people who had rehearsed grief until it wore smooth. The town of Harrow's Bend, surrendered first. Then the mill villages along the Sere. Then, in the final negotiation, everything north of the river — eleven thousand people handed to the Crown's erasure wardens like a line item, traded for a capital that stayed safe for exactly twelve years before the whole arrangement rotted through anyway.

His orders had moved them. His wards had swallowed them. His design had made eleven thousand lives into a rumor, and then no rumor at all.

An old woman named Petra had finished her testimony an hour ago and had not forgiven him. She had said, "I don't curse you. Cursing is a kind of remembering, and I'm tired of remembering you," and had turned away, and Davorin had understood that this was worse than any sentence the Crown could pass, and had deserved it.

Then the sky broke open.

It began at the horizon, over the eastern hills — a thin seam of gold, like a crack in a kiln. Then another seam to the north. Then the whole dome of the dark peeled back, and the stars came through.

Not gradually. All at once, thousands of them, as if someone had lifted a lid off the world. The square gasped — a single collective sound, eleven hundred people inhaling together — and then the noise that followed was not cheering, not at first. It was weeping. Grown men and women who had not seen a star in twelve years dropped to their knees in the slush and howled at the sky like the newly sighted. A child asked her mother what the bright ones were called, and the mother said, "Everything, love, they're all of them, they're every one," and could not say anything else.

Davorin did not kneel. He stood in the middle of it with his face tilted up, taking the light on his skin like a sentence being read aloud, and he felt the thing he had carried for twelve years shift in his chest — not lift, not release, nothing so clean as that — but settle. Find its true weight. The guilt did not leave him. It simply stopped pretending to be anything other than what it was, and there was a terrible comfort in that, the way there is comfort in a wound finally being named.

The frost was breaking too. He could hear it — a vast ticking, cracking, groaning sound as the unnatural cold that had gripped Corren for weeks let go all at once. Ice slid from rooftops in glittering sheets. The canals boomed and shivered. Somewhere a bell tower began ringing, and then another, unsanctioned and joyful, the sort of ringing that got a sexton fined.

Petra appeared at his elbow. She looked up at the stars, then at him.

"You gave them back," she said.

"No," Davorin said. "A charm-seller gave them back. I'm the reason they were taken."

"Yes," she agreed, without heat. "Both things are true. That's the worst part, isn't it?"

It was. He said so.

She studied him for a long moment. Around them the square was filling with lanterns as people climbed out of cellars and doorways to see the impossible sky. "My husband was a warden's clerk, before," she said. "He always said the law is just memory with teeth. So." She nodded toward the Crown garrison at the end of the square, its windows lit, its officers surely already scrambling. "Go give them your memory. Let them decide about the teeth."

Davorin straightened his coat. Twelve years of command sat oddly on his shoulders now, like a uniform borrowed from a dead man.

"Will you testify?" he asked. "All of you? What happened needs to be written down by more than my hand."

"We'll testify," Petra said. "Every one of us. You built the erasure, warden. We'll build the record. Stone by stone, if the Crown makes us."

He walked toward the garrison alone. The stars came down the street with him, reflected in every puddle, every window, every blade of the melting frost, so that it seemed the whole city had been seeded with small fires. At the garrison door he stopped, took off his warden's sigil — the silver wheel that had meant authority for twenty years — and held it out to the startled sergeant on duty.

"Davorin Hale," he said. "Former First Warden of the Border Erasure. I'm surrendering myself, and I'm bringing a confession the size of a country. Wake whoever has the keys to the deep archive."

* * *

**Ilsa**

Ilsa had never seen stars before.

She had heard about them her whole life — the way children in Vell heard about everything that had been taken: in fragments, in elders' voices, in the shape of a hand cupped overhead. *They were like salt thrown across black cloth. They were like holes punched in the roof of the world to let the light of somewhere else leak through.* She had never believed the descriptions matched anything real. Nothing real could be worth the ache in the old people's voices when they spoke of it.

Standing in the market square of Corren with four thousand people and a sky full of fire, she understood that the descriptions had all failed. There was no image for it. There was only looking up and feeling your whole body say *oh* — the oh of a door opening, the oh of a name you didn't know you'd lost suddenly spoken aloud.

Beside her, Sebastian was crying without any attempt to hide it. She found she liked that. Twelve years of his careful anonymity, his shuttered shop, his charms sold in whispers — and here he stood in the open square, soaked in starlight, weeping in front of everyone, keeper of a sealed door and a jarred ember and nothing to hide behind anymore.

"You're staring at me," he said, catching her look.

"I'm deciding whether to forgive you," Ilsa said.

"For what?"

"For keeping it shut." She tipped her head back. Above them the constellations the elders had named — the Plough, the Hearth, the Long Road — wheeled into view one after another, and the crowd below cried out at each one like a congregation. "I spent my whole life wanting the door open. You closed it, and the sky came back, and I'm standing here trying to be angry and I can't find the anger anywhere. It's very rude of you."

Sebastian laughed — a wet, cracked sound. "I can apologize, if it helps."

"Don't you dare." She bumped her shoulder against his. Below them, Maghret's remaining loyalists stood apart from the crowd, silent, watching the stars with expressions Ilsa couldn't read and decided not to try. Maghret herself was gone — crossed over, or fled into the city, no one knew. That was a debt the future would collect. Not tonight. Tonight even grief was off duty.

An hour later, when the first wild rumors of Davorin's surrender reached the square, Ilsa went still.

"He walked in," said the fishwife who brought the news, breathless. "Gave up his sigil. Said he's got a confession as big as a country and they should wake the archivists. They've taken him down to the deep cells."

Ilsa looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked at Ilsa.

"The testimony," she said. "Eleven hundred witnesses in this square, and he's handing them the pen."

"Somebody had to," Sebastian said quietly. "Better him than nobody."

Ilsa thought of the archive beneath the Ember Court — the records she'd pulled, the surrender documents, the proof that Vell had been sold by its own rulers. She thought of Maghret saying *the truth is whatever keeps us alive*, and of how wrong that had been, and of how the truth, it turned out, was heavy enough to stand on if enough people held it up together.

Tomorrow the work would start. Registries, hearings, names — thousands of names, spoken aloud into rooms full of clerks, until a country that officially never existed was written back into existence one line at a time. Not as a place. As a people. As paper, and testimony, and a garden growing over a sealed door.

But tonight — tonight the sky was full, and the frost was running off the eaves in silver ropes, and somewhere in the deep cells a guilty man was telling the truth to people paid to write it down.

Ilsa stood in the square until dawn, among her people, under her stars, learning their names.

* * *

**Sebastian**

Near morning, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the bells had finally been ordered to stop, Sebastian walked home through streets that steamed.

The thaw was violent. Snow slid from awnings in wet avalanches. The gutters ran loud. In the market quarter, shopkeepers were already opening early, drawn out by the light and the noise, staring upward between tasks as if the stars might be repossessed if unattended. Someone had chalked on the cobbles outside the baker's: *THEY CAME BACK AND SO DID WE*, and half the city had already walked through it, smearing the letters, improving them.

In his cellar, the gate was just a gate. Sebastian put his palm against the oak one last time. Cold wood. Iron hinges. No hum. For twelve years he had felt it singing under his skin, faint and constant, a note he'd mistaken for his own loneliness. Now the silence where the song had been was not empty. It was full — full of four thousand people sleeping in Corren's churches and warehouses and spare rooms, full of a girl upstairs asleep in his kitchen chair because she'd refused to go farther than one floor from the door, full of an ember in a jar on his shelf that glowed a little brighter whenever he looked at it, as if it knew.

He climbed the stairs, put the kettle on, and opened the shop's front shutters to the first honest sunrise Corren had seen in weeks.

On his doorstep, someone had left a package. Brown paper, tied in string, no mark. Inside: a single sheet of Crown stationery, official seal, and a message in a clerk's careful hand.

*To the Keeper of the Gate, address unknown.*

*The Crown acknowledges receipt of one (1) surrendered First Warden, one (1) confession, and approximately eleven hundred (1,100) witnesses presently encamped in the Market Square, all claiming citizenship of a nation that does not appear in any registry, map, treaty, or census held by this office.*

*The Office of Records requires a name. Eleven thousand names. The witnesses state these names exist and that you know where they are written.*

*You will attend the Hall of Records at the ninth bell.*

*Bring the archive.*

Sebastian read it twice. Then he looked up — at the stars fading politely into the dawn, at the steam rising off the roofs, at the city waking around a mystery it had slept through for twelve years — and he laughed, alone in his doorway, until his ribs hurt.

They wanted the names. Of course they did. The whole country reduced, one last time, to paperwork.

He folded the letter, set the kettle whistling, and went upstairs to wake Ilsa. They had a registry to build, and for the first time in his life, Sebastian Villalobos intended to sign his own name first.
