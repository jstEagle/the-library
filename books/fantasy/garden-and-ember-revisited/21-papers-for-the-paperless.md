## 21. Papers for the Paperless

The Registry of Persons occupied a former granary on Tallow Street, and it smelled like every bureaucracy Ilsa had ever heard described: dust, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that had been waiting too long for something to happen. She had been there since before dawn, as she was every morning now, arranging the day's testimony ledgers into stacks by district. Fourteen months since the stars came back. Nine months since the Crown, under pressure it could no longer deny, established the Office for the Registration of Displaced Persons — a title so carefully empty that Ilsa had laughed out loud when she first read it.

*Displaced.* From where? That was the question the whole office existed to avoid answering.

She set the last stack down and flexed her fingers. They ached already. They would ache worse by evening. Every name that crossed her desk had to be written three times: once in the Crown's ledger, once in the registry's copy, and once in her own book — the Vellish book, bound in grey leather, that she kept in the drawer beneath the inkwell. The clerks thought it was sentiment. Let them. It was the only ledger in Corren that recorded people as citizens of somewhere.

The first petitioner arrived before the doors officially opened, because they always did. An old man with a cartwright's shoulders and a hat worn soft as cloth, holding his number ticket like a talisman.

"Number forty-one," he said. "I was told to come back today."

"You were told to come back Tuesday," Ilsa said, but she was already pulling his file. Hessel Brandt. Border town of Kessing, which had walked out of the erasure fourteen months ago along with eleven thousand other people who had no papers, no birth records, no tax history, no existence. He'd been coming back every week since spring.

"Tuesday is also today, if you say it is," Hessel said, sitting down across from her without waiting for permission. He'd learned the office's rhythms well enough to know that confidence moved files faster than correctness.

Ilsa opened the folder. Inside were the documents she'd spent weeks assembling: sworn statements from three neighbors in the frozen quarter, a work record from the cooperage where he'd taken a job, a letter from the parish priest attesting he'd attended mass — badly, the priest had noted, but attended. It was a biography built entirely of other people's signatures. That was how it worked now. A person who didn't exist on paper could only be vouched into existence by people who did.

"The clerk in District Four rejected your file again," she said. "He says the neighbors' statements are insufficient because the neighbors are also unregistered."

Hessel's face did something complicated. "So we can't testify for each other."

"So you can't testify for each other," she confirmed. "Which means the only way through is a registered sponsor. Which is why—" she turned the folder around and tapped the newest page, "—I found you one. Master Aldwin at the cooperage. He's registered, he's respectable, and he's willing to swear you've worked for him since autumn. Sign here, and here, and initial there."

Hessel signed. His hand shook slightly on the third initial, and Ilsa pretended not to notice, the way she pretended not to notice the shaking hands of nearly everyone who sat in that chair. Fear of paperwork was its own species of fear. In Vell, a signature had just been a signature. Here, it was a claim staked against a country that insisted you were nobody.

"Congratulations," she said when he finished. "As of this morning, you exist."

"I existed in Kessing for sixty years," Hessel said, standing, tucking his ticket away. "Nobody needed to congratulate me."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "They didn't." She watched him go, straight-backed, refusing to look grateful, and she respected him enormously for it.

* * *

By midmorning the queue stretched out the granary doors and down Tallow Street, and Ilsa worked the counter while two Crown clerks worked the back offices, processing what she sent them. This was the arrangement: she interviewed, gathered testimony, verified names against the lists the border-town elders had reconstructed from memory, and then passed the files inward. The clerks stamped. The clerks were very good at stamping. It was everything before the stamping that required a person who understood what had actually happened — which was why the office had quietly stopped pretending she wasn't essential, and started paying her a wage that suggested she might be.

A young woman came to the counter holding an infant and a sheaf of papers that had clearly been rejected multiple times; the corners were soft with handling.

"They say my son can't be registered," she said, "because he was born inside."

Inside. Everyone in the queue knew what the word meant now, though the official forms still said *within the affected region*. Born inside. Born behind the gate, in the country that had never legally existed, during the twelve years when Vell had been a sealed room full of starving people keeping their own calendar. There were thousands of them. Children who had learned to walk in a place the maps denied, whose births had been recorded in parish books written in a dialect the Crown's archivists classified as extinct.

Ilsa knew exactly what those children were, because she was one. Born inside the erasure. The first person to walk out of it alive.

"His name?" she asked.

"Tomas. Tomas Veyra."

"And your name?"

"Marta. Marta Veyra. I have my own file pending — number eight hundred and six. It's been pending since winter."

Ilsa pulled the pending file. Of course it was pending. Marta's case had hit the same wall as hundreds of others: she had no record of residence in Corren before the stars returned, no entry document, no explanation for where she had come from, because the true explanation — *she walked out of a country your government erased and pretended not to see* — was not a field on any form.

"Here's what we're going to do," Ilsa said. She took a fresh sheet. "Your son was born inside, yes. But under the terms of the Amnesty Provision — Section Eleven, which I helped draft, so I know exactly what it says — any child born within the affected region after the date of severance is eligible for registration as a dependent of a sponsoring household. So we don't register him as born inside. We register him as born to you, and we register you first, today, under emergency family unification. You become real, and then he becomes real, attached to you like a shadow."

"That's not how it should work," Marta said slowly. "He shouldn't have to be a shadow."

"No," Ilsa said. "He shouldn't." Her pen hovered over the form. "But shadows can be made solid later, one amendment at a time, and dead paperwork helps no one. I've spent nine months learning that lesson. Take the shadow. Grow the rest."

Marta looked at her for a long moment — the look of someone deciding whether to trust a stranger with the shape of her whole life — and then nodded once.

It took until past noon. It took a runner to the District Four office, a verbal argument with a supervisor that Ilsa won by citing regulations the supervisor hadn't known existed, and a final signature from a clerk named Pellworthy who had started the year hostile to the whole enterprise and had lately begun bringing Ilsa tea without being asked. When it was done, Marta Veyra held two registration certificates, and her son Tomas officially existed in the Kingdom of Corren, population adjusted upward by one.

"He'll never know what this cost," Marta said at the door.

"He'll know it was paid," Ilsa said. "That's enough."

* * *

In the afternoon lull, Pellworthy brought the tea and the news, in that order.

"There's a delegation arriving tomorrow," he said. "From the capital. From the Ministry of Interior itself."

Ilsa's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "What kind of delegation?"

"The kind that inspects things before they close them." Pellworthy lowered his voice, though the counter was empty. "My cousin works in the Minister's correspondence office. The Interior has been reviewing the registry's costs. Nine months of operation, forty thousand registrations processed, and the Ministry's position is that the emergency is over."

"The emergency isn't over. There are still eleven thousand unprocessed files in the eastern districts alone."

"My cousin says the phrase being used in the Minister's office is 'sunset the provisional structures.'" Pellworthy shrugged apologetically. "Sunset. As in, let it go dark."

Ilsa set the cup down carefully, because if she set it down carelessly she would throw it. Forty thousand people registered — and how many thousand more still waiting, still shadowless, still walking through Corren invisible? And the answer from the capital was sunset, a lovely word, a word for evenings and endings, deployed against people who had already lost their country once and were now in danger of losing the proof they'd ever existed.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"If the delegation recommends closure — closure could be voted by month's end."

Month's end. Three weeks. Ilsa looked out at the queue still winding down Tallow Street in the cold afternoon light: old men with soft hats, mothers with infants, teenagers clutching testimony letters written by sponsors who barely knew them but had signed anyway, because someone had to. Forty thousand names written three times each. And a drawer beneath her inkwell with a grey leather book that held all of them again, in Vellish, in her own hand.

She thought about Maghret, who had wanted to force the door open regardless of the cost, who had believed belonging was worth anything. Ilsa had chosen truth over tribe in a freezing gatehouse, and she had never regretted it, not once, not even in the worst weeks. But she understood Maghret better now than she had then. She understood the hunger underneath it. Belonging was not a luxury. Watched from the wrong side of a counter, it looked exactly like survival.

"Pellworthy," she said. "If the Ministry closes this office, what happens to the unprocessed files?"

"They'd revert to the district offices. Standard channels." He didn't have to explain what standard channels meant for people with no papers and no sponsors. Standard channels meant years. Standard channels meant some of them dying in the wait, unnamed and unregistered, erased a second time — this time not by magic, but by indifference, which was slower and required no wards at all.

"Then we have three weeks," Ilsa said, "to make closing us more expensive than keeping us."

Pellworthy blinked. "How does one do that?"

"We process everyone we can, faster than we've ever processed anyone. And we build a record the Ministry can't sunset — a public one. Testimony. Names. Faces. Forty thousand citizens of a country that never existed, standing in front of the one office that saw them." She was already pulling fresh paper toward her, the weariness in her bones transmuting, as it always did, into motion. "Get me the eastern district counts. And find out when the delegation's coach arrives. I want to meet it at the door."

* * *

She worked until the lamplighters came, and after. The queue emptied. The clerks went home. Ilsa sat alone in the great dusty granary with her ledgers, adding the day's names to the grey book: Hessel Brandt of Kessing. Marta Veyra. Tomas Veyra, aged four months, born inside, registered as a shadow, destined to be made solid.

When she reached the end of the page she paused, pen lifted, and wrote one more line — not a registration, just a note to herself, in the dialect no archivist could classify:

*Nine months to build. Three weeks to lose it. Not while I'm counting.*

She closed the book, blew out the lamp, and stood in the dark listening to the building settle around forty thousand lives in triplicate. Tomorrow a coach would come from the capital carrying men with the word *sunset* in their mouths. Let them come. She had learned from the best — from a charm-seller who carried a country's memory in his chest, from an ex-warden who traded his freedom for the truth — that nothing truly ends unless you agree to stop writing it down.

And she was not done writing.

Outside, frost was forming on the windows of Tallow Street — ordinary frost, honest frost, the kind that came from weather and not from grief. Winter was only winter now. Ilsa buttoned her coat, locked the granary door behind her, and began composing, in her head, the speech she would give the Ministry delegation at dawn.

It began, she decided, with a name. Theirs always would.
