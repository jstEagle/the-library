## 22. Garden and Ember

The shop reopened on a Tuesday, because Sebastian had always believed that beginnings should be unremarkable.

He'd taken the boards off the windows himself, prying each nail loose with the same flat iron he'd once used to lever open crates of smuggled charm-glass. The sign above the door — *VILLALOBOS: CHARMS MENDED, WARDENS ANSWERED* — was the same sign, repainted, though he'd crossed out the second line and written beneath it in smaller letters: *and questions asked honestly*. It wasn't much of a joke. Half his customers didn't get it. The other half got it too well and stood in the doorway a moment longer than they needed to, looking at him like a man who had come back from somewhere.

Which, in a way, he had.

The city outside had changed and hadn't changed. Corren still smelled of coal smoke and river mud and frying onions at the corner of Tanner's Row. The market still shouted itself awake at dawn. But there were more people now — thousands more, walking with the particular carefulness of those who had learned not to trust solid ground. They wore Crown registry tags on their collars, stamped PENDING, and they bought bread and lamp oil and small charms like anyone else, and when they passed Sebastian's shop some of them slowed, just slightly, the way you slow passing a grave or a shrine.

He didn't mind. He'd gotten used to being looked at like that.

Inside, the shop was the shop. Shelves of mended luck-charms. Jars of salted light for dark hallways. A drawer of honest buttons that would never lie about coming undone. He had stopped selling anything siphoned from the gate — there was no gate to siphon from anymore, which was its own strange grief, like losing a limb you'd never known you were leaning on. His magic now came from the ordinary places: hearth-ash, rainwater collected at midnight, the patience of tending things. Slower work. Better work, maybe. Certainly quieter.

And behind the shop, where the cellar stairs went down to a door that no longer opened, there was a garden.

* * *

He had built it in the first thaw, over the course of six weeks and one argument with a mason who kept asking why anyone would put soil over a perfectly good cellar.

"Because I want to," Sebastian said, the fourth time.

"But it's a *cellar*," the mason said, as if this settled something.

It had been a cellar for twelve years, and before that — well. Before that it had been the last anchor of Vell, the threshold through which an entire country had walked out into the cold light of Corren while Sebastian stood on the outside and pulled the door shut behind them. Forty-one days of crossing, the wardens' tallies said. Forty-one days of refugees emerging starved and frostbitten and blinking, into a city that had spent twelve years pretending their home had never existed.

Sebastian had been the last one out. Someone had to seal it from the outside, and it turned out that someone was always going to be him.

So: a garden. Six feet of good loam over the old gatehouse stair, planted with things that didn't care what had been underneath them. Beans. Squash. Marigolds, because Ilsa had once said — in one of her rare unguarded moments, back when she was still learning how to be nineteen in a world that kept trying to make her a symbol — that marigolds grew wild along the Vellish border roads, gold as struck matches, and that her mother used to say they bloomed brightest where something had burned.

He thought about that every time he watered them.

The garden did well. That was the thing nobody warned you about grief: the world doesn't pause for it. The beans came up fat and indifferent. The squash ran riot over the edge of the plot and had to be beaten back with a stick. By midsummer the whole yard was green and loud with bees, and beneath it all, under two yards of packed earth and stone, the sealed door sat in the dark like a heart that had finally agreed to stop beating.

Some nights he lay awake and listened for it anyway.

* * *

The ember lived on the mantel above his hearth, in a jar.

Not a jar, exactly — a lantern-glass, the kind used for storm-lamps, fitted with a brass lid drilled full of holes so the fire inside could breathe. Davorin Hale had made the lid, in the weeks before he surrendered himself to the Crown's judgment. He'd done it without being asked, arriving at the shop one grey morning with the glass wrapped in oilcloth and his hands shaking in a way Sebastian had never seen them shake, even during the flight across the frozen quarter, even during Maghret's siege of the gatehouse.

"It's from the third hearth," Davorin had said. "The longhouse in the border camp, where the townspeople kept a fire going through the whole winter. They fed it for twelve years inside the erasure. Never let it die." He'd set the glass down carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening. "They wanted you to have it. They said—" and here his voice had failed him, briefly, the architect of an erasure undone by a handful of words from people he had erased— "they said keepers keep."

Then he'd walked out of the shop and up the hill to the warden's garrison, and given himself up, and Sebastian had not seen him since. There were rumors. That the tribunal had sat for eleven weeks. That Davorin had confessed everything in a voice like reading a ledger, name by name, town by town, order by order. That some of the border-town families had attended every session, silent, watching. That when the sentence came down — twenty years, commuted to service, assigned to the very registry offices processing the paperless — one old woman from the town of Harrow's Edge had stood up and said, loudly enough for the whole chamber:

"Good. Let him spend the rest of his life writing down names. He owes us that many."

Sebastian liked to imagine him at a desk somewhere, ink-stained, meticulous, doing penance one record at a time. He wrote to him sometimes. Davorin never wrote back. But the letters never came returned, either, and once — only once — a package had arrived containing a tin of Corren black tea and a note in a warden's cramped hand: *The registry reached the H-surnames today. There are more of them than the maps ever admitted. — D.*

Sebastian kept the note in the drawer with the honest buttons.

As for the ember itself: it burned low and steady in its jar, the color of late coals, needing almost nothing. Once a day he lifted the lid and fed it a sliver of dried apple wood, and once a week he let it taste a drop of honey-wine, because the Vellish had always fed their hearth-fires sweet on the seventh day and it seemed wrong to break the habit now, when the habit was nearly all that was left.

It never grew. It never died. It just burned, patient as a held breath, and when Sebastian sat alone in the evenings with his tea going cold, he sometimes caught himself talking to it. Not prayers, exactly. News, mostly. The price of beans. The weather. Which customers had paid and which had promised.

"You'd laugh at my bookkeeping," he told it one night, and the ember flickered in a way that was probably draft and probably wasn't, and he decided not to decide.

* * *

Ilsa came on Thursdays.

She'd been coming since spring — irregularly at first, then regularly, the way people settle into rituals they'd deny were rituals. She never knocked properly; she rapped twice and let herself in, calling "Keeper!" in that Vellish accent that twelve years of erasure hadn't managed to sand off her tongue, and Sebastian would call back "Not your keeper, I've told you," and she'd say "You'll have to sue me," and that was the whole liturgy, performed weekly, neither of them ever varying it.

She was twenty now. She'd grown into her sharpness, softened it into something more like wit. She worked three days a week at the registry — Ilsa's idea originally, then Ilsa's crusade, then officially Ilsa's department, because the girl born inside the erasure had turned out to be the only person in Corren with both the standing and the stubbornness to fight the paperwork wars. She'd testified before the Assembly. She'd shamed two ministers in public session. She'd gotten the word *Vell* back into the official gazetteer, after a decade in which printing it had been legally actionable, and she'd done it by standing up with the old maps — the true ones, the pre-erasure ones that Davorin had hidden and then handed over — and reading aloud, slowly, the names of forty-one towns until the chamber went quiet enough to hear the truth land.

"They can argue with me," she'd told Sebastian afterward, flushed and fierce and holding the maps like weapons. "They can't argue with forty-one names."

On Thursdays she arrived, argued with him about something trivial, drank his tea, inspected the garden with the critical eye of someone whose homeland had been agrarian, and left. And in all those months, across all those Thursdays, neither of them had spoken of the door.

Not once. Not the sealing. Not the forty-one days of crossing. Not the choice itself — the third path, the terrible clarity, the moment when Sebastian had stood at the original threshold with the power to resurrect a nation or end it cleanly and had instead flung the door wide and held it open until every living soul walked out, and then closed it forever from the outside, ending Vell as a place while saving Vell as a people.

Maghret had called it betrayal, in the last hour, spitting blood and defiance from the wreckage of her seized gatehouse. *A country is worth any price,* she'd said. *You sold ours for a clean conscience.* She and what remained of the Ember Court had scattered after that — north, rumor said, into the hill country, fewer than thirty now, keeping the old songs alive in defiance of everything, including arithmetic. Ilsa never mentioned her. Sebastian never asked. Some wounds you leave covered so they can close.

But the silence about the door wasn't avoidance. Or not only avoidance. It was closer to a shared understanding, the kind that forms between people who were present at the same earthquake: *we both know what happened; we both know words won't survive the weight; we will carry it without describing it.* They spoke of everything else instead — the registry, the weather, whether squash could be trained or only defeated — and the door sat under the garden between them, unmentioned, unforgotten, tended in the only language left for it.

Which was dirt, and water, and time.

* * *

One Thursday in late summer, Ilsa arrived early, carrying a basket, and refused to explain the basket.

"Sit," she said, pointing at his own chair, in his own shop, with the particular authority of someone who had spent a year telling ministers where to stand. "And don't watch me. You're terrible at not watching."

"I'm excellent at not watching."

"You're watching right now."

He sat. She unpacked the basket on his counter with her back half-turned, and he heard paper, and string, and the clink of glass, and when she finally stepped aside there was a small cake on a plate — lopsided, over-browned on one edge, dusted with sugar that had mostly migrated to the plate — and beside it a jar of honey with a handwritten label in careful Vellish script.

He read the label. Then he read it again.

*From the hives at New Harrow's Edge. First harvest. The bees remember the old flowers. So do we.*

New Harrow's Edge was what they called the settlement now — one of seven, scattered through Corren's outer quarters and the river flats, where the border-town families had rebuilt in miniature. Not Vell. They'd been very clear about that, in the months after the crossing. Not a restoration, not a government-in-exile, not Maghret's dream dragged up from under the earth. Just neighborhoods. Streets with the old names. A longhouse with a hearth that had never gone out, feeding its daughter-fire to every new stove on the street. And hives, apparently. Bees that had crossed through the door with the last of the refugees, descended from stock that had foraged the hills of a country that officially never existed, and that now, absurdly, gloriously, made honey in Crown territory from flowers growing over ground that remembered being otherwise.

"The whole street contributed," Ilsa said, suddenly finding the counter fascinating. "For the shop reopening. It's late, I know, you reopened in spring, but the honey wasn't ready, and I told them better late than—" She stopped. Started again, in the brisk voice she used for committees. "There's a girl there, Petra, nine years old, born inside, same as me. She's never seen a map of Vell that isn't in the archive. Last month she asked me what the stars looked like there." Ilsa's voice did something complicated. "I couldn't remember. Isn't that — twelve years, and I couldn't remember whether our stars were different, or whether that was just a story mothers told. So I told her they were the same stars everywhere, and that the night they went out wasn't the sky's fault. And she said okay, and went back to her bees." A breath. "Anyway. Honey."

Sebastian cut the cake. It was dense and a little burnt and absolutely perfect, and they ate it standing at the counter like thieves, and neither of them said anything about doors, or countries, or the price of any of it, because the cake was saying it, and the honey was saying it, and the label in careful Vellish script was saying it better than either of them ever could.

*We remember. We are here. We are making something sweet.*

* * *

Afterward, they took tea out to the garden, because the evening was warm and the light was doing that thing it did now — that long amber spill across the rooftops that everyone in Corren still noticed, still remarked on, twelve months after the stars came back. People hadn't stopped looking up. Sebastian suspected they never would. There was a whole city's worth of necks that had learned, in one terrible starless winter, exactly how much sky meant, and the sky had come back like forgiveness nobody earned, and forgiveness like that gets watched.

The beans had gone over. The marigolds were at their loudest, gold as struck matches, blazing along the borders of the plot exactly where something had burned. Ilsa walked the rows with her hands behind her back, inspecting, and stopped at the center — the deepest part, directly over the sealed stair — where Sebastian had planted nothing at all, just a flat stone, worn smooth, with no inscription.

She looked at the stone for a long time.

"People ask me," she said quietly, "at the registry. The ones from inside. They ask if it's true that the keeper sealed it from the outside. If it's true he stayed out here with us instead." Her thumb moved over the stone's edge, back and forth, a small unconscious motion. "I tell them yes. I tell them he holds the door in his memory so we don't have to hold it in ours. That's what you're for now, I think. Not the gate. The remembering. Somebody has to carry it whole, so the rest of us can carry pieces."

"That's a lot of weight to assign to a charm-seller," Sebastian said.

"You were never a charm-seller."

"No," he agreed. "I suppose I was a keeper the whole time. I just had the wrong inventory."

Ilsa laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound of someone twenty years old and alive in a country that had tried very hard to prevent her — and then she crouched and pressed her palm flat against the smooth stone, the way you'd press a hand to a sleeping animal, or a grave, or a door. Sebastian watched her do it and understood that this was their ritual, the real one, the one under all the others: not speaking of the door, but touching the place where it slept. Acknowledgment without excavation. Memory kept alive without letting it rule.

She stood. Brushed off her knees. Became brisk again, committee-brisk, announcing that she had to go because the registry had a hearing tomorrow and forty-one families waiting on surnames beginning with K, and that he should eat more vegetables from his own garden instead of buying them like a man of means, and that the cake tin needed returning by Thursday or there would be consequences.

At the garden gate she paused. The amber light caught her face, and for a moment she looked, again, like the bleeding girl who had collapsed in his cellar a lifetime ago, calling him *keeper* in a dead language.

"There's a petition," she said. "Going to the Assembly next month. To build a memorial. A real one — not a statue, nobody wants a statue. A garden. Public. Over the site of the old border, wherever the wards actually stood." She was studying the marigolds with tremendous focus. "They'll want a dedication. Words. From someone who was there. From the keeper."

"Ilsa—"

"Not asking you to decide tonight." She swung the empty basket onto her hip. "Just — think about what you'd want it to say. Whatever you choose." And then, softer, in Vellish, four words he hadn't heard since the crossing, the old farewell of the border towns, the one that meant *may your hearth stay lit*: 

"*Ember and garden, Sebastian Villalobos.*"

Then she was gone, down the alley, footsteps fading into the market noise, and Sebastian stood alone among the marigolds with the cake tin under his arm and the first star — the first star, always the first, the one everyone still looked for — pricking through the amber overhead.

He went inside. He fed the ember its sliver of apple wood, and it took the light and held it, low and steady, patient as a promise. He thought about what a memorial should say. He thought about forty-one names, and bees that remembered old flowers, and a door sleeping under six feet of green.

Tomorrow he would write to Davorin. Tomorrow there would be a hearing, and squashes to beat back, and customers wanting charms mended. Tonight there was just the jar, and the garden, and the long amber dusk, and the quiet, unhurried work of keeping —

which, it turned out, had never been about the door at all.

He trimmed the wick of the lamp, and the ember burned on, and outside the stars came out one by one over Corren, over the seven settlements, over the graves and the gardens and the living, like a country counting itself home.
