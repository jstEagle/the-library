## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The night market in Grayhollow smelled of wet coal and fried onions, and August Dunmore loved it the way you love a scar — because it was yours, and because it hurt to look at directly.

She'd set up her stall in the crook between a chandler's cart and a woman who sold caged wrens for luck, which was a joke if you thought about it too long. Luck in Grayhollow was a currency like any other, and most people were short on it. Her table was draped in gray linen gone soft with age, and across its surface she'd arranged her inventory with a jeweler's care: small glass bottles on waxed cord, each one stoppered and sealed, each one holding something no honest market would sell.

Memories.

Not metaphors. Not "keepsakes" or "sentiments," whatever the euphemisms printed on the wardens' posters said. Actual memories, drawn out of living heads and bottled by thieves like her, glowing faintly under their stoppers like fireflies that had forgotten how to fly.

Magic was outlawed in Grayhollow. Had been for twelve years, ever since the Breaking Night, though nobody could tell you exactly what broke or why, only that afterward the Ash Court rose and the wardens came and the pyres started smoking in Cinders Square. The penalty for working magic was fire. The penalty for *selling* it was fire plus confiscation of everything you owned down to your boots. August had seen two memory-thieves burn in her time. She'd stood in the crowd both times, anonymous and sweating, and told herself the same lie both times: *that will never be me.*

It was a good lie. It kept her hands steady.

"Charm-seller." The voice came from the shadow of the chandler's cart, an old man in a fishmonger's apron, wringing his cap. He'd been circling her stall for twenty minutes, the way they all did, pretending to browse the wrens. "They say you have... the real thing."

August didn't smile. Smiling invited conversation, and conversation invited witnesses. She just turned her head slightly, letting the lantern catch the bottles, and watched him follow the light like a moth with debts.

"They say right," she said. "What are you grieving?"

He flinched. They always flinched. Nobody came to her stall for happy memories; happiness you could make yourself, badly, from food and drink and other people's company. What people bought in the dark of the night market was loss. A dead wife's laugh. A mother's kitchen. The last afternoon before a son marched off to a war nobody remembered starting.

"My wife," the old man said. "Marigold. Eleven years gone this winter. I can't—" His throat worked. "I can't see her anymore. It's all gone soft at the edges. Like looking through fogged glass."

August nodded once, businesslike, and reached beneath the table to the locked case where she kept the stock she didn't display. She knew what he needed before he finished asking. She always did. Grief had maybe six shapes, and she'd catalogued them all.

The bottle she set before him held a small warm gold light, the color of late afternoon through kitchen windows. Inside it, folded impossibly small, was a memory of a woman singing while she kneaded bread, flour on her forearms, turning to scold someone off-camera with a wooden spoon raised in mock fury.

"That's not her," the old man whispered.

"It's someone's wife," August said. "Singing in a kitchen. That's what you're buying. Don't confuse yourself about the rest."

Because here was the trade's dirty heart, the thing August never said aloud: she didn't harvest these herself, mostly. She bought them from extractors — the ones who did the actual theft, slipping into sleeping houses with their silver needles and their numbing salts, drawing years out of strangers' heads like pus from a wound. The extractors sold to fences, the fences sold to charm-sellers, and the charm-sellers sold to the grieving, and everyone in the chain told themselves they were the innocent link.

The old man pressed coins into her palm — too many, she noted, and slid two back without comment — and lifted the bottle with both hands, the way you'd lift a bird with a broken wing.

"How do I—"

"Uncork it. Hold it close. Breathe in when it fogs." She paused, then added, because some part of her that hadn't yet died of cynicism stirred briefly: "It'll feel borrowed. That fades. Mostly."

He shuffled away into the crowd clutching it, and August felt nothing, or told herself she felt nothing, which amounted to the same thing on a Tuesday.

* * *

Business stayed steady until the bells rang midnight. A widow bought the smell of her father's workshop. A young man with burned hands paid triple for a memory of swimming — *swimming*, of all things, clean cold river water, something Grayhollow's ash-choked river would never offer again. A nervous clerk wanted a memory of being brave, and August sold him one and didn't bother feeling guilty about it, because bravery bottled is bravery diluted, and he'd figure that out or he wouldn't.

She was packing the last of the unsold stock into her case when the trouble arrived.

He came out of the crowd like weather — a big man, mid-forties, dressed in the decent wool of a man who worked with his hands but got paid for thinking. Dockmaster, maybe, or a guild factor. He had the careful walk of someone slightly drunk and the fixed stare of someone about to become much more so.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. Not a question.

"I'm a seller of keepsakes. Sentimental items. Perfectly legal."

"Show me something." He dropped a purse on the table. It clinked heavy. Too heavy. August's instincts, honed over nine years in the trade, sat up and growled.

"What kind of something?"

"Home." He stared past her, at the lantern flame. "I want home. Before the fire."

Ah. Fire-grief. One of the six shapes, and the worst one, because fire-grief came with guilt attached, and guilt made buyers erratic.

She reached into her case and drew out a bottle she'd taken in trade from Odile's lot just three days past — a memory of a stone farmhouse, apple trees, a dog barking at geese. Warm stuff. Golden-hour stuff. The extractor's tag had said *rural, childhood, strong resonance*.

The man uncorked it right there at the stall, which was against every rule she had, and breathed in the fog before she could stop him.

And then he went still. Completely still, in a way living people aren't, and August's hand crept toward the knife in her boot.

"No," he said softly. "No, no, no."

"Sir—"

"This is mine." His voice cracked open. "This is my house. That's my dog. Her name was Juniper. That's — I *remember* this. I remember this and I lost it, I lost it twelve years ago, when—" He looked up at her, and his eyes were terrible. "Where did you get this?"

"Sir, I buy from licensed dealers in sentimental—"

"*Where did you get it?*" He slammed his palm on the table. Bottles jumped. A wren screamed in its cage. Heads turned along the market row, and heads turning meant wardens, and wardens meant fire.

August did the arithmetic she'd done a hundred times: the man was bigger than her, drunker than her, and standing between her and the main lane. Behind her, the alley mouth yawned dark between the chandler's cart and a wall stained with old ash — the city's permanent memorial to itself.

"I don't know what you think—"

"Twelve years ago they came to our farm." He wasn't talking to her anymore. He was talking to the bottle, to the ghost inside it. "Wardens. Ash Court men. They took things out of our heads, whole years of them, and they said it was for the covenant, they said we wouldn't miss it, and then the house burned and my wife—" His fist closed around the bottle. "My wife is in here. Part of her. And you *sold* it."

Somewhere behind the market, a whistle blew. Short, sharp, answered by another. Wardens.

The man lunged across the table. August was already moving — she'd been moving since the second whistle — ducking under his grab, sweeping the lantern off the table into his face. He roared and staggered, and she vaulted the stall's end, snatched her case by its handle, and ran.

* * *

Grayhollow's alleys at night were a maze designed by a paranoid god, and August knew every turn of it. She ran with the case hugged to her chest, boots silent on cobblestones slick with coal-dust drizzle, and behind her the whistles multiplied and the shouting started.

*"Thief! Stop, thief!"*

Always *thief*. Never *witch*. The Ash Court had learned that accusing people of magic emptied streets; accusing them of theft filled them with helpful citizens. So she ran the gauntlet of faces leaning out of doorways, pointing, calling directions to her pursuers, and hated every single one of them with a clean professional hatred.

Left at the tannery. Through the gap in the wall where the mortar had crumbled — sideways, case first, exhaling. Down the stair cut into the aqueduct's spine, three steps at a time, one hand on wet stone. The whistles fell behind, tangled in the maze, and she allowed herself one breath of victory before the city reminded her it was never done with her.

A warden stepped out of the archway ahead.

Just one, young, his ash-gray cloak still carrying the stiffness of new wool, his truncheon half-drawn and his face full of the particular panic of a man who'd expected an easy shift. They stared at each other for one full second — long enough for August to see the poster pasted on the wall beside him, ink still sharp:

**MAGIC IS THEFT. THOSE WHO TRADE IN IT STEAL FROM EVERY CITIZEN. REPORT ALL SUSPICION. THE ASH COURT PROTECTS YOUR REMEMBERING.**

*Your remembering.* As if the city had any left.

"Evening," August said, and threw the chandler's candle she'd snatched off a sill straight into his face, and was past him and gone before the wax stopped burning.

* * *

Her workshop was three rooms above a shuttered dyehouse in Tanners' Row, reached by a staircase that groaned like a betrayed witness. She bolted the door, wedged a chair under the knob, and stood in the dark listening to her own heartbeat slow from gallop to trot.

The case sat on the table where she'd dropped it. She lit one candle — small, shielded, the flame barely taller than a thumbnail — and checked her stock with shaking fingers. Most of it intact. Two bottles cracked. The farmhouse memory gone, still clenched presumably in the fist of a dockmaster who now knew exactly what she was and what she'd sold him.

Word would spread. By morning, Odile would hear. By tomorrow night, either the price on her head would change or the extractors would stop selling to her, and both possibilities ended the same way: hungry, or burning.

She should leave the city. She'd known that for years, honestly, the way you know a tooth is bad. But leaving required money, and money required the trade, and the trade required Grayhollow, and so the circle went, round and round, a wheel she'd been running inside so long she'd forgotten it was a wheel.

August sat down at her workbench and did what she always did after a bad night: she took out the one bottle she never sold.

It was plain glass, unremarkable, stoppered with ordinary cork. No glow. No fog. If you shook it, nothing shifted inside — or nothing visible. She'd bought it eight years ago from a dying extractor who'd gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said, *"You'll want this back someday, girl. It's the only thing anyone ever stole from you that matters."*

She'd never opened it. She didn't know what was inside. That was the whole horror of it, the thing she circled at 3 a.m. the way her tongue circled a broken tooth: somewhere in her past there was a hole, twelve years wide, and she had no memory of losing anything because — presumably — the memory of losing it had been taken too. She knew the dates the way you know your own birthday. Twelve years ago. The Breaking Night. Everyone in Grayhollow had gaps from that season, little missing seams the official story papered over as *confusion during the unrest.* But everyone else's gaps were days or weeks. Hers was a childhood.

She had parents once. She assumed. There was a scar on her chin she couldn't account for and a lullaby she hummed sometimes whose words were gone, just the tune remaining, like a house with the doors bricked over.

And here was the thought that found her every night, patient as a debt collector: *you sell stolen memories, August. You've handled thousands. You've never once opened them to check. What if some of them are yours?*

What if the widow's father's workshop was her father's workshop? What if the swimmer's river ran through a town she'd loved? What if she'd already sold her own mother's laugh to a stranger for four coppers, and it was fogging the air in some rented room across the city right now, making someone else feel a love that belonged to her?

She set the plain bottle on the bench and looked at it, and it looked back, the way it always did, saying nothing.

Outside, the bells rang three. Somewhere below, on the street, footsteps stopped — deliberate, unhurried, right outside the dyehouse door. Then silence. Then, after a long moment, the sound of a body sliding down wood, and a wet, labored breathing.

August blew out the candle.

Knife in hand, she eased the chair from under the knob and opened the door onto the landing, where a man lay crumpled against her threshold, dark blood pooling black under him, one hand pressed to his stomach and the other stretched out toward her — holding, sealed in dripping red wax, a rolled map.

His lips moved. She knelt close to hear.

"The garden remembers," he whispered. And then, with the last of his breath pushing the words into her ear like a key into a lock: "Find what they took. You're the only one who — it has to be *you*—"

His hand fell. The map rolled against her knee.

Below, in the street, whistles began to sing.
