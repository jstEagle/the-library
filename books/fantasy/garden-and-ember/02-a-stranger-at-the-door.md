## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at the hour when even the ash lamps gave up pretending.

August was sealing the last of the night's charms — a widow's memory of her husband laughing, bottled in blue glass — when she heard it. Not a knock, really. More like something heavy leaning against her door and then forgetting how to stand.

She went still. In Grayhollow, you learned to go still. Sound traveled wrong in the Warrens; a footstep on the stair could be a customer, could be a warden, could be nothing at all. She counted to ten. The sound came again — a slow scrape, like a body sliding down wood.

"Closed," she said. Her voice came out flat and professional, the voice she used for drunks and desperate men. "Come back at dusk."

The door handle turned anyway. The lock held. It had better hold; she'd paid Odile's cousin three silver marks for that lock, and he'd sworn it would stop anything short of a battering ram or a determined woman.

"I know what you sell," said a voice through the wood. Male. Wet-sounding, like words dragged through water. "I know what you are."

August set down the charm. Her hand drifted under the counter, to the knife she kept there not for cutting wax. "Then you know I'm closed to people who say things like that out loud in a hallway where anyone can hear."

A pause. Then a laugh — short, cracked, half cough. "Anyone who hears is already dead." Another slide of weight against the door. "Please. I don't have anywhere else."

Everyone in Grayhollow said that. Everyone in Grayhollow was lying about something, usually the same something: they'd touched magic, or wanted to, or sold it, and now the city's fear had its teeth in them. August had built a life on being the one who didn't open doors. Doors opened onto gallows. Doors opened onto pyres.

But the voice was wet in a way that wasn't metaphor, and when she pressed her eye to the gap beside the hinge, she saw him: a man folded against her doorframe like laundry left out in rain, one hand pressed flat to his side, and between his fingers, black in the lamplight, blood coming through his coat in a slow tide.

She should have let him die on the landing. That was the smart play. That was the play that kept her alive twelve years in a city that burned people like her.

She opened the door.

Later — much later, in the years after everything — she would try to remember why. She never found a reason good enough. Only the feeling, which she couldn't explain then and couldn't explain after: that looking at his face was like hearing her own name spoken in a room where no one knew it.

* * *

He weighed more than he looked. She got him inside by hauling him under the arms while he tried to help and mostly made it worse, and together they collapsed behind the counter, into the narrow space where she slept on a cot among shelves of other people's pasts. Hundreds of little bottles glinted around them in the lamplight — griefs and first loves and childhood summers, each one sealed with wax, each one technically evidence of a hanging crime.

"Press here," she said, taking his hand and putting it back against his wound. "Hard. Don't be brave about it."

He was young, she saw now. Younger than her, maybe twenty-five, with dark hair stuck to his forehead and a face that — she kept catching herself looking at it. A face like a word on the tip of her tongue. There was a scar through his left eyebrow, thin and old, and every time her eyes found it her chest did something strange and painful, like a bruise being pressed.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

"No," he said. And then, softer, almost wondering: "Not anymore."

That should have frightened her more than the blood did. Instead she knelt and cut his coat open with the counter knife, because some part of her had apparently decided this man's survival was her problem now, and the part of her that made decisions had gone somewhere else entirely.

The wound was a slash across his ribs, deep but not — she thought, pressing cloth against it — immediately fatal. Someone had known what they were doing. This wasn't a mugging. Muggings didn't leave you alive long enough to bleed slowly.

"Wardens?" she asked.

"Hollow Men," he said, and laughed again, worse than before. "Wardens chase thieves. These were something else. They wanted what I carried, and when they couldn't take it—" He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were gray-green and steady in a way that made her want to look away. "They'll come here. I led them in circles, but they'll come. I'm sorry. I need you to take it."

"Take what? I don't take charity cases. I especially don't take dying men carrying things people kill for—"

"The garden remembers," he said.

August stopped talking.

She couldn't have said why those four words landed in her like a stone dropped down a well — she waited for the splash and felt none, just the falling, going deeper than any well had a right to go. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her missing twelve years lived as a smooth blank wall, something knocked. Once. Like a hand from the other side.

"What did you say?"

"You'll understand." He fumbled at his coat, at an inner pocket, and she helped him because her hands were moving before her mind agreed to it. He drew out a roll of oilskin, small, wrapped tight, sealed with a blob of dark red wax. Into the wax, pressed while it cooled, someone had stamped the shape of a flower. Five petals. She didn't know its name, and knowing that she didn't know hurt, somehow, like the scar above his eye hurt.

He pressed it into her hands and closed her fingers over it with both of his. His hands were cold.

"Three threads," he whispered. "Salt. Bone. Ember. Follow them, and at the end—" A cough took him, bending him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lips and his eyes had gone glassy-bright. "At the end, you'll find what was taken from you. You'll find what you were."

"You don't know me," August said. Her voice sounded far away. "You don't know anything about me."

"I knew your mother."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that hadn't been there a moment ago. Around them, the bottles on their shelves caught the light and threw it back in tiny trembling points, hundreds of captured memories watching, and August stood in the middle of them holding a dead man's secret and feeling, for the first time since she was a child too young to know what she'd lost, like the floor of her life had opened onto a staircase going down.

"My mother," she repeated, "died when I was small."

"Yes," he said gently. "And before that?"

Before that was the wall. Before that was nothing — a childhood that began abruptly at age nine with cold and hunger and a name scrawled on a workhouse ledger, AUGUST DUNMORE, NO KIN, NO HISTORY, and a gap where a person should have been. Twelve years of nothing, and she'd built a whole self on top of the nothing, the way weeds build a meadow over a cellar.

"How do you know about—" she started.

Boots on the stairs.

Three pairs, maybe four, heavy, unhurried, the tread of men who owned the night and knew it. The stranger's eyes went wide and then, terribly, calm. He grabbed her wrist.

"They can't have it," he said. "Promise me. Whatever they offer, whatever they threaten — the map goes to the salt, and the salt goes to the bone, and the bone goes to the ember. Promise."

"I promise," August said, because there was no time to do anything else, and because some traitor part of her meant it.

He nodded once, satisfied, and pushed himself up off the cot with the last of whatever was keeping him upright. "Back door," he said. "There's always a back door. Go. Now."

"And you?"

"I'm done running." He smiled at her — and God, that smile, it cracked something in her, it was familiar the way the scar was familiar, the way the flower in the wax was familiar, a familiarity with no address, no origin, just weight. "It was worth it," he said. "Finding you. Tell them whatever you like. They'll believe a frightened shopgirl. That's what we're for, isn't it? Believing what's easy."

The boots reached the landing. Fists hit the door hard enough to shake dust from the rafters.

"Open! City wardens!"

August shoved the oilskin roll down the front of her shirt, against her skin, where it lay warm and terrible as a second heartbeat. She looked at the stranger one last time. He was standing straight now, swaying slightly, blood dripping off his fingertips onto her floorboards, and he mouthed two silent words at her.

She read them. *Garden remembers.*

Then she pulled the bolt and opened the door, and became, for the first time in her life, a liar.

* * *

There were four of them. She catalogued them the way you catalogue anything that might kill you: two big ones by the door, one wiry one already drifting toward her shelves with the professional interest of a man who'd found contraband before, and one in front — a sergeant, gray at the temples, with a warrant badge polished bright and eyes that had seen every lie Grayhollow could produce and believed none of them.

"August Dunmore," the sergeant read from a paper. "Charm-seller. Unlicensed trade in stolen remembrance. We've been meaning to visit you for some time."

"How flattering," August said. Her heart was slamming so hard she was sure they could hear it, sure it was shaking the bottles on the shelves. She made her voice bored instead. Terrified people got searched. Bored people got underestimated. "Is there a reason you're kicking my door in at the hour when honest criminals are asleep?"

"A man ran this way. Bleeding. Tall, dark hair, wounded in the side." The sergeant's gaze moved past her, into the room, and August stepped sideways — casually, she hoped — to keep herself between him and the cot.

"This is Grayhollow, Sergeant. Half the city is bleeding and the other half did it."

"Search the premises."

The wiry one moved. August watched him work — gloved hands, quick, checking under the counter, behind the shelves, lifting each bottle to the light with a connoisseur's frown. Every bottle he lifted was a death sentence if he understood what he held. Most wardens didn't. Most wardens were farm boys given badges and told magic was the enemy, and they saw trinkets where a trained eye would see theft.

This one was not a farm boy. He paused over a green bottle near the top shelf — a child's memory of flying, one of her most expensive pieces — and looked at it a long moment.

"That's river glass," August said. "Folk charm. Keeps moths out of wool."

"Mm," the warden said, and put it in his pocket.

Behind her, the sergeant said, quietly: "Where is he?"

"Gone," August said. "Out the window, down the drainpipe, before you lot started playing drums on my door. If he was ever here at all. People see shapes in the fog and run to the wardens, and the wardens run to me, and I'm trying to sleep."

The sergeant looked at her. She looked back. It was the longest three seconds of her life.

She had lied before — everyone in her trade lied, small lies, lies of packaging and provenance, *this charm belonged to a sailor, madam, he sailed right past the edge of the world.* But she had never lied to authority with blood drying on her floorboards and a dying man standing six feet behind her, and she discovered that lying, real lying, felt exactly like working magic: a gathering in the chest, a shaping of will, a moment where the world either bent or broke you.

The world bent.

"Window," the sergeant said, and crossed to it. It was shut. Locked, even — she'd locked it herself at dusk, out of habit, and now the lock sat there gleaming like a witness she'd forgotten to bribe. The sergeant examined it. Examined the sill. Below, three stories down, the alley ran dark.

"Drainpipe's rotted through," one of the big ones offered from the doorway. "Wouldn't hold a cat."

"Then he went down the stairs past us," the sergeant said, "or he's still in this building." He turned, and his eyes found August with the patience of a man who could wait out a siege. "Or she's lying."

"Arrest me, then," August said. "I'll enjoy the trial. 'Charm-seller accused of hiding a bleeding man she's never seen.' Half the jury will be customers. The other half will want to be."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face — not belief, never belief, but arithmetic. Four wardens, one suspect, a warrant that named a smuggler and not a murderer, and a city full of paperwork waiting at the end of the night. She watched him do the sums. She hated him a little for making her watch.

On the cot behind her, very softly, the stranger made a sound.

It was nothing. A breath, a wet rattle, the noise a dying man makes without meaning to. But in the silence it was a bell, and the wiry warden's head came up like a hound's.

"What was that?"

"Cats," August said.

"Cats."

"This is Grayhollow, Sergeant. Everything down here is cats." She met his eyes and poured every ounce of boredom she had ever felt into her face, and underneath the boredom, where no one could see, she was screaming. "Are we finished? Because I have stock to inventory and you have a bleeding man to not find."

The sergeant held her gaze a moment longer. Then, to her eternal surprise, he nodded — a short, sharp motion, a debt noted rather than forgiven.

"We'll finish another night, Mistress Dunmore. Count on it." He turned for the door, then paused. "The man we're hunting stole something from the Ash Court. Do you know what the Ash Court is?"

"No," said August, who until tonight had only known it as a story mothers told children, a fairy tale grown teeth: *be good, or the Hollow Men will come.*

"Few do. That's how it likes it." He settled his cap. "If a dying man brings you a gift, Mistress Dunmore — burn it. Whatever it is. Burn it and sleep well. That's the only advice that keeps people like you breathing."

Then they were gone, boots fading down the stair, the street door banging, and August stood alone in her workshop with her heart going like a hammer and a dead man's warmth still rising off the cot behind her.

* * *

He lasted eleven minutes.

She spent them kneeling beside him with cloth and water and the useless fury of someone who has finally found a question worth asking and is running out of person to ask it of. He faded in and out. Near the end he surfaced one last time, caught her sleeve, and pulled her close enough that she could smell blood and, beneath it, something green — crushed leaves, growing things, a smell that had no business in the Warrens of Grayhollow and that made her eyes sting without permission.

"Sefa," he whispered. "Find Sefa. She sings to the roots. She'll know your face." His grip weakened. "She knew mine. Before."

"Before what?" August demanded. "Before what? Who am I?"

His eyes found hers, and in them she saw it again — that recognition running the wrong direction, him looking at her like she was the memory. His lips moved. No sound came. Then his hand slid off her sleeve, and the rattling breath slowed, and slowed, and the workshop went very quiet, and the hundreds of bottled memories on their shelves seemed to lean in around the silence like mourners at a graveside.

August sat back on her heels.

She should move him. Wardens came back; wardens always came back; a body in a charm-seller's rooms was a confession written in the plainest hand. She should panic, plan, run. Instead she sat in the blood-cool air and looked at his face — at the scar through the eyebrow, at the gray-green eyes half open on her ceiling — and felt the strangest grief of her life, grief with no root, mourning a man she'd known for a quarter hour as though she'd known him all her life.

As though she'd known him *before.*

Slowly, she drew the oilskin roll from her shirt. The wax seal caught the lamplight, five-petaled flower dark against dark red. Three threads, he'd said. Salt, bone, ember. Words that meant nothing and hummed like struck wire.

She didn't break the seal. Some instinct older than sense told her not to — not here, not with wardens circling and a corpse cooling at her feet. She wrapped it away again and stood, and her knees nearly buckled, and she caught herself on the shelf, and a dozen bottles chimed softly like small bells.

Outside, dawn was still hours off. Somewhere across the city, men were reporting that the courier had vanished, and a woman named Wren Halloway — though August didn't know the name yet, wouldn't for chapters and miles — was learning that the thing she'd killed for had slipped through her fingers into the hands of a shopgirl with a hole where her childhood should be.

August looked down at the dead man who knew her mother.

"All right," she said quietly, to him, to the map, to the wall inside her own skull. "The garden remembers. Let's find out what it remembers about me."

She fetched her coat, her knife, and a sack of charms worth more than her life, and she left the body where it lay — because the first rule of Grayhollow was *don't be found*, and she had already broken every other rule she had.

Behind her, on the workbench, the lamp guttered and went out.
