## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The stranger had no name that August could find, and no name meant no trail, and no trail meant the wardens would be working blind — which was the only reason she was still breathing.

She'd spent the night after his death doing what she did best: nothing. She'd sat in her workshop with the shutters barred and the lamp turned low, the wax-sealed map lying on her bench like a thing with a pulse. She hadn't touched it. Touching it felt like an admission. As long as it stayed where it was, unopened, unexamined, it was just paper. The moment she looked, it became evidence, and evidence in Grayhollow got people burned.

But morning came the way it always did, gray through the shutter slats, and with it came the arithmetic she couldn't avoid. A man had bled out on her floor. Wardens had come asking questions. She had lied to them — lied to *wardens*, which was not something a person did twice and lived to brag about. Sooner or later they'd come back, and next time they wouldn't ask.

So she opened the map.

It was good work, she'd give it that. Not ink on vellum but something finer — lines burned into the parchment itself, as if drawn with a wire pulled hot from a fire. Three threads radiated from a central point she didn't recognize: one running west toward the coast, one south into country she'd never traveled, one simply labeled in a script she couldn't read, characters that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them. At the center, where the threads met, someone had pressed a seal into the wax so old it had gone amber, and inside the seal was the impression of a flower she'd never seen before but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a word from a language you dreamed in once.

A garden. Rows of it. Stone walls warm with sun.

August put the map down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw sparks.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You sell other people's pasts for coin. Of course your head is full of rooms you've never stood in.*

That was the professional explanation, and it was almost good enough. Almost.

* * *

By midmorning she was out in the city, because staying home was how people got found. Grayhollow in daylight was all smoke and commerce — fishwives arguing over the canal stalls, ash from the foundries sifting down like dirty snow, warden patrols moving through the crowds in their slate-gray coats with the ember-and-bar sigil stitched at the shoulder. Magic outlawed for twelve years now, and the city had learned to perform its innocence. Every charm-shop fronted as an apothecary. Every hedge-witch sold "remedies." And every memory-thief in Grayhollow knew the patrol rotations better than the wardens did.

August kept to the market crowd, hood up, and let herself think about the stranger's face.

That was the part she couldn't shake. Not the blood, not the dying, not even the words — *the garden remembers* — though those had a way of replaying themselves at bad moments, like now, crossing the Tanners' Bridge with the canal stinking below her. It was the face. She'd seen maybe four hundred faces across her bench in six years of selling charms, and she could count on one hand the ones that had made her feel *known*. His had. Not familiarity exactly. Something worse. Recognition without content — the shape of knowing with none of the substance, like reaching for a stair that isn't there.

She'd seen a face like that once before. In a mirror, some mornings, when she caught herself off guard.

The lodging house on Candlewick Row was exactly the kind of place a man with no name would die in: three stories of sagging timber over a chandler's shop, rented by the week, forgotten by everyone. August circled it twice before going in, checking sightlines the way Brannoc — no, not Brannoc, she didn't know any Brannoc, why had that name surfaced? — the way anyone careful would check them. No gray coats on the row. Just a knot of neighbors at the corner being neighborly, which in Grayhollow meant comparing notes on who'd been taken last.

The door to the third-floor room hung open.

Not broken — *open*, swung wide, deliberate, the way a stage curtain is open. August's skin went cold. Ransacked rooms were bad. Invited rooms were worse. Someone wanted the room seen.

She went up anyway. Stupid. She went up anyway.

Inside, the ransacking had been thorough and joyless. Mattress gutted, floorboards pried at the seams, the little iron stove dismantled piece by piece. Whoever had done it had been looking for something small and flat and had not found it, because it was currently burning a hole against her ribs where she'd sewn it into her coat lining two nights ago. They'd left everything else scattered like an insult. On the desk, someone had arranged what remained of the stranger's papers into a neat fan — and burned them there, right on the wood, leaving a black bloom of char and a smell that turned her stomach.

And beneath the burn, half-hidden by its own ash, a photograph.

Photographs were rare in Grayhollow. Expensive, foreign, faintly suspect — the wardens didn't like images that remembered too well. This one had been through fire. Its edges were curled and blackened, its surface blistered, but the center had survived, and the center showed a garden.

Stone walls. Warm light. Two figures standing among rows of green.

One was a woman, older, silver-haired, her arm around the shoulders of —

August stopped breathing.

The second figure was a girl. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Dark-haired, thin-faced, standing with the particular awkwardness of someone photographed against her will. And the face — blistered, faded, half-eaten by fire — was her own.

Not similar. Not a resemblance. *Her own.* The face she shaved around in the mornings, the face she saw in shop windows when she passed, younger by twelve years and wearing an expression August had never once worn in the life she remembered: trust. Open, unguarded, looking-up-at-someone trust.

Her hand was shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

Twelve years ago she'd woken in a charity ward with no name she could prove, no family anyone claimed, and a gap behind her eyes so clean it frightened the physicians. Twelve years of a self assembled from scraps — a first name that fit like borrowed clothes, a trade learned fast because memory-work came to her disturbingly easily, a life built sideways in the cracks of a city that burned people like her. She'd told herself the story of that gap a thousand ways. Fever. Accident. The kind of theft nobody investigates because the victim has nothing left to steal.

She had never once let herself believe someone had chosen it.

But here was the proof, charred and curling in her palm: a woman with her arm around a girl with her face, in a garden that existed nowhere in Grayhollow, and someone had burned this room to make sure August Dunmore never saw it.

*Whoever I was,* she thought, and the thought arrived with a strange calm, like water finding its level, *someone killed a man to keep it buried.*

She took the photograph. Of course she took the photograph. She was a thief; taking things was the one honest skill she had.

* * *

Odile Vash kept her counting house above a dye-works in Sable Lane, where the air was permanently violet and everything smelled of chemicals and money. August had been selling to her for five years, and in five years Odile had never once offered her a chair, a drink, or a kindness. It was why August trusted her completely.

Today Odile took one look at her face and locked the door behind her.

"Sit," she said, which was as close to alarm as she ever came.

"I'll stand."

"You look like something the tide brought in." Odile settled behind her desk, a small sharp woman with dyed-black hair and fingers stained permanent blue from decades of handling charm-glass. "Talk."

"A man died in my workshop."

"I heard. Wardens are saying a courier, name unknown, killed by persons unknown." Odile's eyes were flat and quick. "They're also saying the wardens canvassed your street twice yesterday, and that a certain charm-seller gave testimony that didn't quite hang together. You want to know who tells me things? Don't. You want to know what it costs me? Also don't." She leaned forward. "What did he give you?"

"Nothing."

"August." Odile said her name the way a surgeon says *tumor*. "I've fenced stolen memories for thirty years. I know what guilt looks like walking through my door. What did he give you?"

August weighed it. Lying to Odile was possible but expensive; Odile always collected the difference later, with interest. "A map."

"Of what?"

"I don't know. Old roads. Something sealed."

Something moved behind Odile's expression — brief, quickly buried, but August had spent years reading buyers' faces for the flicker that said *this charm is worth more than I'll admit*. She knew that flicker. Odile had just had it.

"You know something."

"I know that you should burn it." Odile's voice dropped, and for the first time in five years, it sounded genuinely afraid. "Tonight. In your stove, with the flue open, and then scatter the ashes in the canal. Whatever he was carrying, whatever he was, men have already torn apart his lodging looking for it, and those men don't stop at lodging houses. Burn the map, August. Take the photograph—" August's hand went to her coat, and Odile's mouth went thin. "So you've already got it. Then hear me clearly. There was a woman came through here eight months back asking after charms from before the Proscription. Paid absurd rates. Wanted anything touching the old covenant, the garden-realm, the Ember Road. I sold her what I had and forgot her face on purpose, which is a skill, and I'm telling you for free: she paid like someone spending someone else's money, and she asked about *you*."

The room went very quiet. Below them, the dye-works thumped and steamed.

"About me."

"By description. Dark hair, scar through the left eyebrow, works the night markets." Odile spread her blue-stained hands. "I told her nothing, because I tell everyone nothing, but August — someone is collecting the pieces of whatever you were, and they're paying gold for them, and the only reason to collect pieces is to control the whole. Burn the map. Change your face if you can afford it. And stop asking questions, because the last man who asked them is on a slab in the warden-house."

"The last man who asked them," August said slowly, "died pressing that map into my hands. Which means he thought I was the answer, not the question."

"Then he was a romantic, and romantics die first." Odile unlocked the door, conversation over. But as August reached it, the fence spoke once more, quieter, without her armor of contempt:

"For what it's worth — the woman who came asking. When I refused her, she smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a living face. Like she'd already lost whatever she was hunting." A pause. "Like she was hunting herself."

* * *

August walked home the long way, through streets she normally avoided, doubling back twice, and the whole time the photograph lay against her ribs like a second heart.

Burn the map. That was the sane play. That was the play that kept her alive, kept her shop, kept the small gray life she'd mortared together out of stolen goods and caution. Odile was right. Odile was always right, that was the whole horror of Odile.

But the map had three roads on it, and one of them ran west toward the coast, and somewhere at the end of it was a drowned chapel or a dead god or a name — *her* name, the real one, the one that fit — and twelve years ago someone had decided August Dunmore was easier to use empty than full.

She climbed to her workshop, barred the door, and laid the map on the bench beside the photograph. Garden and girl. Roads and seals. A dead man's last act, aimed at her like an arrow loosed twelve years too early.

In the stove, the coals were still live. All she had to do was feed it paper.

Instead, August Dunmore did the one thing she'd sworn she never would.

She started packing.
