## 5. Memories Not Mine

Brannoc made me sleep in the chapel's vestry, on a cot that smelled of brine and old incense, and I dreamed of nothing at all — which for me was normal, and which I hated more than usual when I woke.

Dawn came gray through the drowned chapel's broken windows. The tide had retreated overnight, leaving the nave glistening and exposed, puddles catching light in the cracks of the flagstones like spilled coins. Brannoc was already up. Of course he was. Men who guard stones out of guilt don't sleep late.

He had the salt keystone out of its hiding place again, sitting in his broad palms like something he was afraid to look at directly. It was smaller than I'd expected — a fist-sized crystal, clouded white, shot through with veins that might have been mineral or might have been something older. Salt crusted its base where it had rested against the chapel floor for years.

"You're staring," he said without turning around.

"I'm deciding whether you're going to try to keep it."

"I am." He set it down on the altar — a slab of black stone furred with dried kelp — and stepped back from it like a man stepping back from a cliff edge. "And then I'm not. That's how it works. You'll see."

"Enlighten me."

He wiped his hands on his coat, and I realized with some surprise that the gesture was nerves. A disgraced warden who'd faced down Hollow Men and execution lists, nervous about a rock.

"The keystones aren't objects," he said. "Not really. They're — anchors. Knots tied in something bigger. The covenant ran through them, the way a rope runs through pulleys. And rope remembers the shape of every knot it's ever held." He looked at me. "You're covenant-blood. When you touch it, it will remember at you."

"Remember *at* me?"

"That's the best word I have. I'm not a scholar. I was a warden, and a bad one, and now I'm a hermit with a salt problem." He gestured at the stone. "Touch it or don't. But if you do, hold on to yourself. People come back from it changed. Not always worse. Just changed."

I should mention here that I've spent my whole adult life handling other people's memories. Bottled grief, bottled joy, bottled first kisses and last words. I know what borrowed past feels like against the skin — a warmth, a pressure, a whisper of someone else's weather. I thought I knew what to expect.

I put my hand on the salt keystone.

The world went white, then green.

---

I was standing in a garden.

Not standing — being. There was no *me* in it, no edges, no body I recognized. I was a point of attention drifting through rows of flowers that glowed faintly from within, petals lit like paper lanterns. The air tasted sweet the way water tastes after a long thirst. Above me, a sky the color of a bruise healing — violet shading into gold — hung over trees whose leaves chimed softly when the wind moved them.

A woman was singing.

Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, threading through the garden the way the paths did. I couldn't make out the words but I understood them anyway, the way you understand things in dreams: she was singing a list. A long, patient list of names. Every name she sang, somewhere in the garden a flower opened.

*I know her,* I thought, and the thought had no voice, and still the singing faltered — just a beat, just a hitch — as if the woman had heard me.

Then fire.

It came from the west, rolling across the garden like a wave, and it wasn't ordinary fire. It burned cold. Where it passed, the glowing flowers went gray, the chiming leaves fell silent, and the sky's gold curdled to ash-color. The woman's song broke off mid-name. I felt her feel it — a tearing, a severing, a rope cut under tension so that both ends whip free —

— and through the smoke, a figure walked toward the heart of the garden. Tall. Calm. Carrying a lantern that gave no light.

The figure turned its head and looked directly at me. At the point-of-attention that was me. And it said, in a voice like a door closing:

"*You were here.*"

---

I came back to myself on the chapel floor with Brannoc's hand fisted in my collar and my own scream still dying in my throat.

"Breathe," he was saying. "Breathe, girl. You're back. You're back."

I was not entirely convinced. The chapel looked wrong — too gray, too dim, the puddles dull as dirty glass. My skin remembered a garden that had never existed and grieved for it like an amputated limb.

"What," I said, and my voice cracked. I swallowed and tried again. "What was that."

Brannoc lowered me onto a pew and crouched in front of me, studying my face with uncomfortable intensity. "Tell me."

"A garden. Flowers that glowed. A woman singing names, and — and the flowers opening when she sang them. Then fire. Cold fire. Someone walking through it with a lantern." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "And it said I was there. How could it say I was there? I've never seen any of it."

"Describe the woman."

"I didn't see her. I heard her."

"Singing. What kind of song?"

"A list. Names. Like a — like a census set to music." I lowered my hands. "Why does that matter?"

"Because that's the seal's song." He said it flatly, but his knuckles were white where they gripped his knees. "The living seal sings the covenant. Holds the boundary by holding every name on both sides of it. Garden-side and city-side. That's not a story I heard. That's what the wardens' old training manuals said, back before they burned the manuals." He paused. "You heard the seal's song, August. From inside it."

"I told you. I've never—"

"No," he agreed. "You haven't. Not with your mind." He tapped his temple. "But the keystone doesn't talk to your mind. It talks to what the covenant left in you. And apparently what it left is considerable."

I stood up too fast and had to catch the pew. The chapel tilted, steadied. "That's not possible. Whatever was done to me took everything. Twelve years, gone, clean as a shaved head. If there were pieces left rattling around in me, I'd have found them. I make my living finding pieces in other people. I'd know."

"Would you?" Brannoc rose too, unhurried, and nodded at the salt keystone on the altar. "You touched that stone for maybe ten seconds. In ten seconds it showed you the Breaking Night from the inside. You want to know why? Because the covenant didn't store itself in your head, girl. Heads are easy to rob. It stored itself in the knots — the keystones, the road, the gates. And in the seal herself. You're not carrying memories *of* the covenant." His eyes held mine. "You're carrying the covenant. There's a difference, and the difference is that nobody can take it out of you without killing you. So instead they took your name and your years and locked the door. But the house is still standing."

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "That's insane."

"Probably."

"People don't *contain covenants.*"

"No," he said. "One person does. Or did. Or was supposed to." He picked up the keystone, wrapped it in oilcloth with the tenderness of a man bandaging a wound, and held it out to me. "Take it."

I didn't move. "You guarded this for twelve years. Out of guilt. Your words. And now you're handing it to a stranger because she had a bad dream?"

"Because you didn't have a bad dream." He pushed it closer. "Because the stone showed you the Breaking Night, and there are maybe four people alive who've seen it, and three of them are lying about what happened. Take it. It wants to go with you. It's been pulling toward the Ember Road since you walked in yesterday — you'd know that if you'd been paying attention to anything besides how much you distrust me."

He had me there. I had noticed it — a faint tug at the base of my skull whenever I faced the chapel's seaward wall, like a fish on a line. I'd blamed the tide.

I took the keystone. It was warm through the oilcloth, warm the way a hand is warm, and the pull toward the east settled into something almost companionable.

"So what happens now?" I asked. "I walk the Ember Road to the second stone, touch it, get another — vision? Another piece?"

"Another piece," Brannoc said. "And each one will cost you. The first one's cheap. It's only showing you what you already lost." He shouldered his pack — he'd packed in the night, I realized; he'd decided before I woke. "The later ones show you what you *are*. That's harder."

"And the guardian of the second stone? The map says the road runs through the border villages. Who's out there?"

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, not quite a wince. "Someone who knew you," he said. "Before."

My hand tightened on the keystone until the oilcloth creased. "Everyone keeps saying that. Everyone knows me. Everyone knew me. Twelve years ago I was somebody, and everybody in this rotten conspiracy of a country remembers her except me." I heard my own voice climbing and forced it down. "Who. Give me a name."

"I'll give you better than a name." He walked to the chapel doors and pushed one open onto the gray morning, the tide flats shining beyond, the Ember Road's first gate a dark seam in the cliffs two miles east. "I'll give you a direction. East, along the road, half a day. Look for a garden that shouldn't be growing where it grows. And when you meet the woman who keeps it—" he glanced back at me, and for the first time since I'd met him, the disgraced warden looked almost gentle, "—let her sing before you ask her anything. Trust me on that."

"Why?"

"Because if you ask first, you won't be able to hear the answer over your own questions." He stepped out onto the flats. "Come on, seal-bearer. The road's been waiting twelve years. It's rude to keep it waiting longer."

I followed him out into the salt wind, the keystone warm against my chest, and behind us the drowned chapel settled back into its silence — but I carried the garden with me now. The glowing rows. The patient song. The cold fire coming over the hills.

Somewhere inside me, a woman was still singing a list of names, and one of them, I was suddenly certain, was mine.

I just couldn't hear it yet.

* * *

We made the cliffs by midmorning. The Ember Road revealed itself slowly — at first just a seam of darker stone in the rock, then a paved way, wide enough for four abreast, running straight as a ruled line toward the horizon. The paving stones weren't granite. They were something smoother, veined faintly gold, and warm underfoot despite the sea wind. Magic in the mortar. Enough magic, laid openly into the ground, to hang every mason who built it.

"They never tore it up," I said. "Twelve years of outlawry and they left the road."

"Tearing it up would have meant touching it," Brannoc said. "Wardens won't walk it. Ash Court won't either. It remembers who walks it, and nobody official wants to be remembered." He glanced sideways at me. "It's already remembering you. Feel that?"

I did. With every step, the faint tug at the base of my skull strengthened, and beneath it something else — a low, subterranean hum, like a tuning fork struck far away. The keystone against my chest hummed back at it, harmonizing.

"It knows me," I said quietly.

"Everything on this road knows you." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "You were raised on it. Sefa used to say you learned to walk between Virelle and the coast, back and forth, back and forth, carrying messages no child should carry."

I stopped walking. "Sefa."

Brannoc kept going three more steps before he registered my stillness and turned. His face did something complicated.

"You said the name," I said. "Just now. You said it like it slipped."

"Did I."

"Don't. Don't do that thing where you pretend you didn't say the thing you said. Who is Sefa?"

He looked east down the long gold-veined road, then back at me, and whatever calculation he'd been making all morning, he finished it. "The woman who sings to roots," he said. "The keeper of the second keystone. And the woman whose voice you heard in the garden last night — or close enough that you'll spend the first hour thinking it's her." He started walking again. "She was your nurse. Before."

The word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for the splash — for some echo, some fragment, *anything* — and got nothing. Just the well's dark depth and my own breath bouncing off it.

Twelve years. A nurse. A garden. A song. All of it sitting on the other side of a door someone had welded shut, and everyone I met seemed to have a key except me.

"Well," I said, when I trusted my voice. "Then we'd better hurry. I've got a lot of questions and only one lifetime to be furious in."

Brannoc huffed — it might have been a laugh. "There she is," he murmured, mostly to himself. "Wren said you'd be all business. Wren was wrong about you in most ways, but she was wrong about that most of all."

I stopped dead for the second time in a mile.

"Wren," I repeated. "Who's Wren?"

The wind came up the cliffs and pushed at our backs, and the road hummed, and Brannoc's face went carefully, deliberately blank — the face of a man stepping back from a cliff edge.

"Ask Sefa," he said. "Some names are heavier than others. Hers most of all."
