## 6. The Ember Road

The road began where the map said it would, which was the first lie August caught it telling.

There was no road. There was a sheep track that wandered up out of Saltmere's drowned parish, climbed through gorse and fog, and then — at a bend where a lightning-split hawthorn leaned like a drunk against its own shadow — became something else. The ground under her boots changed texture. Not stone, not packed earth. Something older, smoother, faintly warm through her soles, as if she were walking on the back of something sleeping.

"The Ember Road," Brannoc said behind her. He'd been quiet for an hour, which for him was a kind of prayer.

"You've seen it before."

"Once. When I was green." He came up beside her, warden's coat gone, dressed now in a fisherman's oilskin that made him look like what he was: a man with no country. "The wardens burned every mile of it they could find. Took thirty years. It grows back faster than they can burn it."

"That should terrify you."

"It does." He said it simply, and kept walking.

August looked down at the road again. Between the cracks of whatever it was made of, tiny veins of color ran — ember-orange, garden-green — pulsing slow as a heartbeat. She crouched and touched one. Warmth crawled up her finger, into her wrist, and stopped there, waiting, like a dog at a door it wasn't allowed through.

*Twelve years ago,* she thought. *Someone took everything I was, and my hands still know things.*

She stood up too fast. Brannoc watched her with those gray warden's eyes that missed nothing and offered less.

"Feel something," he said. Not a question.

"I feel like we're being watched." That was true too, but it wasn't what she felt. What she felt was recognition — the road knew her stride, adjusted to it, the way an old horse picks up its rider's rhythm without being asked.

They walked until the fog thickened into dusk and the road carried them between two standing stones carved with script that hurt to read directly. August's eyes slid off it. Brannoc's didn't; he stopped and stared, lips moving.

"Covenant-script," he said. "I only ever saw it in evidence lockets. They burned the last of it when I was a boy." He looked at her sideways. "You're not even trying to read it."

"No."

"And you can't tell me why."

"I can't," August agreed, "tell you anything, because I don't know. Ask me how to pick a lock blindfolded. Ask me how to spot a forged grief in a buyer's face. I'm useful right up until the moment this road starts expecting me to be somebody else."

Brannoc was quiet a moment. Then: "My mother used to say the Ember Road doesn't take you anywhere. It takes you back." He started walking again. "Come on. We'll make the ridge before full dark or not at all."

They didn't make the ridge.

* * *

Brannoc smelled them first — cold iron and wet ash, the smell of a fire put out by someone who hated that it had ever burned. He caught August's sleeve and pulled her off the road into the gorse without a word, and she went, because twelve years of thieving had taught her that when a man who used to hunt people for a living goes still, you go still with him.

They came up the road from the south. Three of them.

Hollow Men.

August had heard the stories the way you hear stories about plague — distant, deniable, someone else's problem. Men who had given the Ash Court everything a man could give: name, memory, mercy, reflection. What walked now wore warden-gray gone the color of old teeth, and their faces were smooth where faces should have had weather. No scars. No lines. Nothing written on them at all. They moved in step without looking at each other, three bodies sharing one intention, and the ember-veins in the road dimmed as they passed, like candle flames leaning away.

"They're tracking us," Brannoc breathed. His hand found the knife at his belt — a real knife, honest steel, useless. "How. The map's wax-sealed, it doesn't—"

Then August understood, all at once, the way you understand a trap you're already standing in.

"It's not the map," she whispered. "It's me."

The Hollow Men stopped. All three. In unison, heads turning toward the gorse like weathervanes finding wind.

"There," the middle one said. Its voice was the worst thing yet — not empty, but *averaged*, a voice with all the individuality sanded out of it. "The seal walks. The Court requires the seal."

*Seal.* The word went through her like a fishhook. She didn't know why. She wouldn't know why for chapters yet. But her body knew, and her body did something her mind hadn't authorized: it stood up out of the gorse, into the open, onto the road, and faced them.

"August—" Brannoc hissed.

"Hush." Her own voice came out strange. Steady. Older than she was.

The Hollow Men advanced. Not fast. They never needed to be fast; that was the whole philosophy of them. Everything the Ash Court did was patient, because patience wins when your enemy has to sleep and eat and doubt themselves, and you are nothing but the Court's hand extended.

"Give back what was taken," the middle one said. "The seal returns to the Court. The Breaking is completed. You will be unmade gently."

And here was the thing August would examine later, turning it over in the dark like a charm bottle held to lamplight: some part of her wanted to say yes. Some hollowed-out room inside her heard *unmade gently* and felt relief, the way a tired thief feels relief hearing the cell door click. Whatever Wren Halloway had scooped out of her twelve years ago had left a shape behind, and the shape recognized the spoon.

"No," August said.

Her hands moved.

She didn't decide to move them. They rose — left palm flat, right hand closing around nothing, around air, around *something* — and the ember-veins in the road flared white-hot, and the standing stones behind her sang one low note like a struck bell made of summer, and the air itself folded.

Not fire. Not light. Something simpler and far more frightening: the road remembered what roads were for, and the Hollow Men were suddenly forty yards further away, stumbling, their perfect synchronization broken for the first time in years, three separate men staggering in three separate directions because the space between them and her had been *re-stitched*.

August's knees buckled. Brannoc caught her arm.

"What was that," he said. "What *was* that. That's covenant-work. That's high covenant-work, that's — nobody living can do that—"

"I don't know!" It tore out of her louder than she meant, ragged, and the fog swallowed it. "I don't know, I didn't think it, my hands *knew* it, do you understand? My hands knew and I didn't!"

The Hollow Men had recovered. Of course they had. They were already coming on again, unhurried, inevitable, ash-smell rolling ahead of them.

"Run," Brannoc said.

"I can't — whatever I did, I can't do it twice, I can feel it, there's nothing *left*—"

"Then run first and philosophize later."

They ran. The road helped — she could feel it helping, tilting its grade, softening its stones under her boots and hardening them under the Hollow Men's, and that frightened her worse than the pursuers, because the road was choosing her, and things that chose you could also be chosen *by* you, and she had spent twelve years making sure nothing in Grayhollow had a claim on her.

Gorse tore at her sleeves. Fog closed behind them. And still the ash-smell gained, patient, patient, the Court's hand extended across miles.

* * *

Brannoc counted his breaths because counting was the last discipline he owned.

Four years he'd worn the gray. Four years of serving a Court that called itself salvation, before the night he'd seen what happened to a village that asked one question too loudly, and walked out of the wardens' barracks with nothing but the coat on his back and a debt to a dead courier named Merrin who had gotten him over the border when every road had eyes. He owed Merrin. He owed Merrin's map. And now he owed a memory-thief who worked magic that should have died with the covenant, and who lied the way other people breathed, and who had just stood in front of three Hollow Men like a woman answering a knock she'd been expecting all her life.

He didn't trust her. Trust was a luxury item and he'd been broke a long time. But he believed her terror, and belief was enough to run on.

They came off the road at a place where it dipped through a hollow full of standing water and dead reeds, and Brannoc made the call he'd been dreading: off the road entirely. The Ember Road was faster, but it was also a line the Hollow Men could follow, and following was the one thing they did perfectly.

"We cut east," he said. "Through the fens. There's a village — Sefa's village, the herb-women, they hide from the Court. If anyone can put distance between us and those things—"

"How do you know about a hidden village?"

"Because I signed the order that burned its neighbor." He said it flat, no ceremony, the way you'd report the weather. "I've been paying it back since. Come on."

Behind them, at the edge of hearing, the averaged voice carried across the fen water, unhurried, terrible in its mildness:

"The seal cannot run forever. The Court remembers everything. The Court will remember you."

August stumbled. Brannoc caught her elbow and got a look at her face in the dying light, and what he saw there wasn't fear of the Hollow Men.

It was longing.

"Don't," he said quietly. "Whatever they're offering — and they're always offering — don't listen to it. That's how they get people. Not the taking. The offering."

"I know how they get people," August said. "I'm a thief. It's how *I* get people." She pulled her arm free and kept walking. "That's what frightens me. I recognize the technique."

They crossed the fens in the dark, on paths Brannoc half-remembered and the land half-forgave, and twice August stopped without warning, head cocked, listening to something neither of them could hear, and both times she changed their route afterward, and both times, come morning, they found Hollow Men sign along the path they hadn't taken — bootprints in peat that held no water, ash pressed into mud in the shape of kneeling men.

"She's tracking me," August said the second time, staring at the prints. "Not chasing. Tracking. Like I'm — like I'm a keystone that grew legs."

Brannoc didn't answer. He was thinking about the execution list in the Saltmere chapel archive, the one dated twelve years back, with a child's name on it that matched the woman beside him. He was thinking about how the Ash Court executed a child on paper and somehow, impossibly, hadn't managed it in fact. He was thinking that Wren Halloway did not leave loose ends, and that August Dunmore was the loosest end in the world.

"Keep walking," he said.

* * *

Dawn found them at the fen's edge, where the dead reeds gave way — abruptly, impossibly — to living ones. Green ones. Then flowers August had no names for, nodding in a breeze that didn't reach the fen behind them. The air changed taste. Sweeter. Older.

August stopped at the boundary like a swimmer feeling the shelf drop away beneath her.

"I know this place," she said.

"You've never been here."

"I know this *place*." She pressed her fist against her sternum, hard, as if she could knock the knowledge loose from wherever it was lodged. "The flowers. There's a well with a wooden cover and a blue door on a house with a crooked roof, and someone — someone sings to the roots, someone sings and the garden *grows*, I can almost hear it, it's right *there*—"

Her voice cracked. Brannoc watched her stand in the flowering border of a hidden village, shaking, hungry for a memory she couldn't reach, and understood two things at once.

The first was that the Hollow Men hadn't been tracking a keystone.

They'd been tracking a homesickness.

The second was that somewhere past that blue door was a person who could explain it — and that explanations, in his experience, cost more than the questions did.

"August," he said. "Whoever lives here — when they open that door, they're going to know your face. And I need you to decide, right now, whether you want to know theirs."

Down the lane, behind the crooked roof, someone had begun to sing.

August went very still. The song drifted over the garden wall — low, wordless, a melody with no beginning she could find — and August Dunmore, who had built herself out of cynicism and stolen goods and twelve years of careful forgetting, put her hand over her mouth and wept without knowing why.

"Yes," she said, when she could. "God help me. Yes."

They walked toward the singing together, and behind them, far back in the gray fen, something knelt in the mud and did not follow — yet — because it had been ordered to wait, and the Court always remembered its orders, and the Court had remembered something else besides:

that the seal was going home.
