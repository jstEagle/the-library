## 7. The Woman Who Sings to Roots

Sefa heard them coming before she saw them — two heartbeats on the road, one steady as a plow horse, the other stumbling like water over stones.

She set down her trowel and listened with more than her ears. The garden told her things. It had always told her things, even before Virelle went gray at its edges, even before the singing became a chore instead of a joy. The beans in their rows leaned toward the strangers' footsteps. The old pear tree dropped three ripe fruit into her basket without being asked, which it only did for family.

*Family,* Sefa thought, and her hands began to shake.

She had felt this coming for days. A pull behind her sternum, like a hook set in her chest and reeled slowly across the miles. She had sung to the roots that morning longer than usual, and the roots had sung back a name she hadn't spoken aloud in twelve years — not because she'd forgotten it, but because saying it made the losing of it fresh again.

She wiped her hands on her apron and walked to the gate at the edge of the hidden garden, past the false wall of bramble that turned away anyone who came with harm in them, and waited.

They came up the cart track through the birches as the light went long and gold. The big man first — she knew him by his walk before his face resolved, shoulders like a doorframe, moving careful, like a man who'd learned the world punished carelessness. Brannoc. Older than he'd been, grayer, but the same guilt carried in the set of his mouth. He had been a young warden once, the kind who wept after executions when he thought no one saw. She had seen. She had never forgotten that either.

And behind him —

Behind him was a girl.

Not a girl. A woman, mid-twenties, ash-dark hair cut short and practical, a thief's coat over a thief's frame, eyes doing what thieves' eyes did: cataloguing exits, counting valuables, measuring Sefa for threat. But under all of that, under the wariness worn smooth as river stone, was a face Sefa had last seen small and screaming in firelight, being carried away from everything she loved.

The pear tree shivered though there was no wind.

"Hello, Brannoc," Sefa said, because it was easier to start with him. "You look terrible."

"Sefa." His voice cracked on the second syllable. He cleared his throat. "You're — I thought you were dead. After the Breaking Night, we were told—"

"You were told a great many things by people whose job was telling you things." She looked past him. "Are you going to introduce us? Or has she forgotten how names work?"

The woman's jaw tightened. "August," she said. "August Dunmore."

The name hit Sefa like cold water. Not the right name. A made name, a survival name, stitched together from nothing and worn so long it had grown into the skin. She could hear the seams in it. Twelve years of wearing a false thing until the false thing became true.

"That's a fine name," Sefa said gently. "It isn't yours."

* * *

They sat at the scarred table under the pear tree, and Sefa fed them first, because grief and revelation both go down easier with bread and honey and strong tea, and because she needed the minutes to steady herself. Brannoc ate like a man who'd been rationing fear. August ate like a thief — fast, watchful, one hand near her knife — but she ate everything, and when Sefa refilled her cup without asking, something flickered across the girl's face that might have been confusion. As if kindness required decoding.

"The salt stone," Sefa said. "You carry it."

August's hand went to her coat. "How do you know that?"

"I can feel it from here. It's been humming for two days, like a struck bell that won't finish ringing." Sefa folded her hands. "It hums louder the closer its bearer comes. And it has only ever hummed for one person. I just didn't think I'd live to hear it again."

Brannoc set down his cup. "You know her."

"I sang her to sleep for six years." Sefa looked at August and let herself see it — really see it, the child inside the thief, the ward inside the stranger. "You were born in Virelle. Do you understand me? Not Grayhollow. Not anywhere on this side of the veil. You were born in the garden itself, in the house beneath the mother-tree, and your first word was a word of the old tongue, and every root and branch in that realm knew your footsteps before they knew mine."

August had gone very still. Thieves went still when cornered; Sefa recognized the posture and hated it. "That's not possible. I grew up in Grayhollow. Foundling row. There are records."

"There are records of a girl delivered to foundling row twelve years ago, half-burned, remembering nothing." Sefa kept her voice soft. "Records made by the Ash Court. Ask yourself who benefits from those records, child."

"Call me August."

"I will call you whatever you ask," Sefa said, "and grieve the other name in private."

The silence stretched. Brannoc shifted. Somewhere in the garden a thrush started up its evening song, and Sefa noticed — noticed with an ache — that the girl's head turned toward it before she could stop herself, tracking the sound like a compass finding north.

"Why?" August said finally. "Why would someone dump a child across the veil and burn her memory out? What was I to them?"

"Not to them." Sefa stood, because sitting felt wrong for what came next, and walked to the low stone wall where the oldest rose grew, the one that had crossed over with her in a clay pot the night everything ended. "To them you were nothing. An obstacle. A loose thread. But to the covenant—" She touched a bloom. "Do you know what the covenant is? Truly? Not the stories. The mechanism."

"Wren Halloway's people say it was slavery," Brannoc said quietly. "A seal that gets eaten alive so two worlds can trade memories like grain."

"And Wren Halloway's people say the Ash Court brought freedom." Sefa turned. "Both things are true, which is why the lie works so well. Yes. The covenant demanded a living seal. Someone who held the boundary between realms in their own body, giving memory continuously so neither side forgot the other. It is a terrible burden. It consumed the woman who bore it before you." She watched August's face. "Your mother."

The cup in August's hand stopped halfway to her mouth.

"She didn't die quickly or cleanly. The covenant took her a piece at a time — a summer here, a face there, whole years poured out of her into the boundary until there wasn't enough left to hold a self together. I sat with her at the end. She couldn't remember my name, but she remembered the song. She asked me to keep singing it." Sefa's voice caught; she let it. "I have kept singing it. For twelve years. To roots, mostly. They're better listeners than most."

"And then me," August said. Her voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "They made a child do it. They made a *child*—"

"They chose you before you were born. Seal-blood runs in lines; it doesn't skip. Your mother agreed to bear you knowing what you'd be, because the alternative was the boundary failing and both realms starving each other of memory — gardens going gray, cities going hollow, everyone forgetting everyone they loved within a generation." Sefa came back to the table. "But here is what Wren will never tell you, because Wren either doesn't know it or can't bear it: a seal must consent. Even a child. Especially a child. The covenant cannot take what is not given. That is its oldest law, older than keystones, older than the Ember Road."

August's eyes had gone bright and hard. "Then what happened on the Breaking Night?"

"You happened." Sefa sat down across from her. "You were nine years old. The rites of succession were beginning — the slow teaching, the songs, the preparation. And Wren Halloway came to Virelle with fire and Hollow Men, and she wanted to seize the seal intact, to control the boundary rather than serve it. Power hoarded instead of given. But the seal wouldn't open for her, because you —" Sefa reached across the table and took the girl's hand, and August let her, frozen, "— you hid yourself. Nine years old, and you understood what was happening, and you hid the seal inside your own memory, buried it so deep that when Wren burned your recollection out of you, she burned away the hiding place too. She got a vessel with no seal in it. She's spent twelve years thinking the covenant simply died."

The thrush had gone quiet. The garden seemed to lean in.

"So the Breaking Night," August said slowly, "wasn't just theft."

"It was a failure. Hers. Because of you." Sefa smiled, and it hurt. "You've been protecting the covenant your whole life without knowing it existed. Every year the boundary thins and the realms forget a little more, but it hasn't collapsed, because somewhere in you, in whatever bottle or charm or scrap of stolen childhood holds it, the seal is still shut, still yours, still waiting for you to decide."

* * *

Later, when Brannoc had gone to check the perimeter out of warden habit and the dishes were done, Sefa walked August through the garden.

It was not a grand place. Half an acre behind a ruined mill, walled in hawthorn and bramble, terraced down toward a stream. But it was alive in a way nothing in Grayhollow was allowed to be — herbs and vegetables and old roses, a stand of apple trees that predated the Breaking Night, bees working the lavender in the last light. And threaded through all of it, faint as a pulse, the smell of Virelle. The other side of the veil, leaking through a crack Sefa had spent twelve years keeping open with song.

"This shouldn't exist," August said. "Magic's outlawed. Gardens like this get burned. I've seen the notices."

"The Ash Court burns what it can find." Sefa snapped a pea pod off the vine and handed it over. "So I make sure it can't find this. Sing to the roots, and the roots hide you. Bramble turns away hostile feet. The stream buries scent. It's not power the way Wren means power. It's cooperation. That's the whole secret of the old craft, child — nobody commands a garden. You ask."

August ate the peas raw, like a child, and Sefa pretended not to notice the way her shoulders came down half an inch.

"You said the third keystone's guardian hates my kind," August said. "What did you mean, my kind?"

"Thieves." Sefa said it plainly. "Tam Vess. He lives up the coast past the salt marshes, in a house full of locks. Twelve years ago a memory-thief took the best day of his life out of his head and sold it, and Tam has spent every year since becoming an expert on locks, wards, and hatred. He guards the ember keystone now, though I doubt he knows exactly what it is — he knows it's precious and he knows the world is full of people who take precious things." She met August's eyes. "He will know what you are the moment he sees your hands. Thief's hands. He'll hate you before you speak."

"Then I'll handle it the way I handle everything." August's mouth twisted. "Trade. Everyone has a price."

"No." Sefa took her by both shoulders, and felt the girl flinch and then, remarkably, hold still. "Listen to me. That is the Grayhollow answer, and it will fail, because Tam's price is the one thing you can't buy — he wants back what was taken, and it's gone, sold in bottles to grieving strangers. If you go to him offering coin or cunning, he will destroy the keystone rather than let a thief have it. I've watched men like him. Grief with nowhere to go becomes a kind of armor, and armor doesn't bargain."

"What then?"

"Truth. Only truth. Go to him empty-handed and tell him what was taken from *you*, and let him see whether a thief can bleed like an honest woman." Sefa smoothed the collar of the girl's coat, an old habit, a mother's habit, and hated herself a little for how natural it still felt. "It may not work. But it's the only door that isn't locked."

August was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a smaller voice: "The flash. At the salt chapel. A garden, and a woman singing, and fire. That was you. That was here — or somewhere like here."

"That was Virelle. The night before the Breaking Night. I sang the succession songs to you under the mother-tree, and you fell asleep in my lap, and I thought — I truly believed — we had time." Sefa's throat tightened. "The keystone showed you that?"

"A piece of it. Like watching through someone else's window." August hesitated. "I didn't feel anything. That's the worst part. I should have felt something, seeing — seeing *myself*, and there was just glass between me and it."

"Oh, child." Sefa drew her in before she could think better of it, and August stiffened like a cat being held, and then, after a long moment, didn't pull away. "You felt plenty. You just felt it twelve years ago, and it's been locked in a bottle ever since. The feeling isn't gone. It's *waiting*. Everything they took from you is waiting, and when you get it back — and you will — it won't come gently. Grief delayed is grief with interest."

"I'm not looking forward to the bill."

"No one ever is."

They stood in the garden while the light failed, and Sefa sang — quietly, without announcing it, the way she'd sung a thousand evenings, the old succession song with its nonsense syllables that weren't nonsense at all but the boundary's own language. And she felt it happen: the girl in her arms going loose by degrees, breath slowing, some guard lowering that had probably been raised at age five and never lowered since. The pear tree dropped another fruit, unasked. The bees went home. Somewhere beyond the bramble, Brannoc stood watch and pretended not to listen.

When the song ended, August stepped back and scrubbed at her face with the heel of her hand, embarrassed.

"Don't apologize," Sefa said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You had the face for it." She smiled. "Sleep in the mill loft. There's straw and an old blanket that smells of apples. Tomorrow you'll want an early start — Tam Vess keeps banker's hours and a crossbow by the door."

August nodded, then paused at the edge of the terrace. In the dark she was just a silhouette, a thief's shape, but her voice came out clear.

"Why help me? You don't know me. The woman you knew was a child. I'm a stranger who steals memories for coin."

Sefa considered the question honestly, because the girl deserved honesty more than comfort.

"Because I sang you to sleep for six years and I have missed you every day since," she said. "Because the garden knew your footsteps before you reached the gate. And because someone has to tell you the truth before you reach Tam Vess, and truth-telling is the only magic I have left that the Ash Court hasn't outlawed."

The silhouette was silent a moment. Then: "Goodnight, Sefa."

"Goodnight, little seal."

A sharp breath in the dark — but no correction came, and the footsteps went on up to the loft, lighter than they'd come in.

* * *

Sefa did not sleep. She sat by the cold hearth with the rose from Virelle in a pot on her knees and let herself remember, which was its own discipline, its own nightly penance.

Twelve years ago she had run through burning orchards with a child's shoe in her fist — the only thing left where the girl had been standing — and crossed the veil with the bone keystone strapped to her back and the taste of smoke in her mouth, and had sworn she would wait. However long. Whatever it cost. She would keep the garden, keep the song, keep the crack in the world open, and someday the seal would come home and decide what it wanted to become.

She had imagined that day a thousand ways. In none of them did the seal arrive with a thief's calluses and a false name and eyes that measured every room for exits.

In all of them, it arrived.

She stroked the rose's leaves and felt the far-off gray of Virelle pressing against the boundary like a face against glass, and felt, beneath the garden's evening peace, the ember keystone's distant heat — two days' travel north, guarded by a man with locks on his heart — and felt, closer than any of it, the sleeping weight in the loft above her, humming like a struck bell.

Tomorrow the girl would walk into Tam Vess's parlor with empty hands and a story no honest man would believe.

Sefa closed her eyes and sang softly to the roots, asking them for one more miracle, and the roots answered the way roots always did: slowly, patiently, and with the unmistakable sense that they already knew how it would end.

Above her, in the loft, the seal-bearer slept without dreaming — or slept dreaming of fire — and the garden kept its vigil, and the last light of Virelle leaked through the crack in the world and lay across the floor like a path pointing north.
