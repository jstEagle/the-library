## 8. The Second Keystone: Bone

Sefa's garden smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

August stood at its edge while the old woman knelt in the dark soil, her hands moving through the green with the unhurried certainty of someone who had been talking to roots for longer than August had been alive. The border village sat behind them, small and smoke-scented, its people careful not to look too long at the two strangers who had arrived on the Ember Road at dusk. Here, past the last warden checkpoint, magic wasn't outlawed so much as it was *kept* — tucked into cellars and hedgerows, whispered to seedlings, hidden the way Grayhollow hid its dead.

"You keep staring at my hands," Sefa said without turning.

"They're shaking."

"Old hands shake. It's the cold they're remembering." She rose, brushing earth from her knees, and looked at August with those eyes that had sung a woman's song inside a stolen memory. "You slept badly."

"I didn't sleep."

"No," Sefa agreed. "You didn't." She wiped her palms on her apron and studied August the way she might study a plant gone yellow at the edges — not with pity, but with diagnosis. "Brannoc told me what happened on the road. The Hollow Men. The way you worked the gate-spell without knowing it."

"He shouldn't have—"

"He should have, and he did, because he's a frightened honest man and you are neither yet." Sefa smiled, and there was no cruelty in it, only the patience of long grief. "Come inside. There's something I've been carrying for twelve years, and my back is done carrying it."

* * *

The cottage was one room, mostly garden — pots on every surface, cuttings rooting in jars along the windowsill, a whole wall of shelves where other houses kept books or dishes. Sefa lit a single lamp and set it on the table between them, and then she went to the hearth, knelt, and pried up a flagstone with her fingernails.

The thing beneath it was wrapped in oilcloth, bound in twine, and buried under a layer of dried lavender that had gone gray with age.

"I was the covenant's singer," Sefa said, untying the knots with fingers that were suddenly steady. "Did I tell you that? In Virelle, before. My voice kept the boundary soft. A covenant is like a graft, child — two living things joined so they feed each other. Left alone, the join scars over and strangles. Someone has to sing to it every day."

"And now?"

"Now the graft is dying. Both of it. The garden first, then the city after." She lifted the bundle free and set it on the table, and even through the oilcloth August felt it — a pull behind her sternum, faint but insistent, like hearing her name called from another room. "I ran with this the night everything broke. I've kept it under my floor ever since, and every year it gets heavier, and every year I tell myself next spring I'll find someone worth giving it to."

She pushed it across the table. "Open it."

August's hands wouldn't move.

"Child." Sefa's voice gentled. "You crossed the Ember Road. You faced the Hollow Men. You touched the salt stone and came out the other side still yourself, more or less. Whatever's in here has been waiting for you since before you could walk. Open it."

August unwrapped the oilcloth.

The bone keystone was smaller than the salt one — a piece of pale femur, polished by centuries of handling, carved with lines so fine they might have been veins or script or both. It should have felt dead. Bone is dead; that's the whole point of it. But it was warm, and it pulsed against her palm with a rhythm she recognized before she understood why: it beat the way her own heart beat when she stood in doorways at night and couldn't remember why she was waiting.

"It knows you," Sefa said quietly.

"I don't want it to know me."

"That's never been one of the options."

August closed her hand around it.

* * *

The memory came like drowning in reverse.

One moment she was sitting at Sefa's table with a lamp burning low; the next she was twelve years old and standing in the great hall of the covenant-house in Virelle, and everything was firelight and singing and the smell of crushed herbs, and she knew — the way you know things in dreams — that this was hers, this hall, this life, this name she had—

The name slid away from her even as she reached for it. But everything else stayed.

She was small. She was hiding behind a pillar, which was also familiar, because she had always been a girl who watched from edges. Below her, in the circle of light, the adults of the covenant were arguing, and their argument had a shape she'd heard all her life the way you hear weather coming: the humans wanted more from the garden than the garden wanted to give, and the garden's wardens wanted the humans held to terms, and everyone was tired, and tired people say cruel things.

And at the center of it, calm as still water, stood Wren Halloway.

Not the Wren of Grayhollow rumor — not the ash-voiced founder of the Ash Court whose name parents used to frighten children. This Wren was younger, sharp-faced, brilliant-eyed, wearing the gray robes of a covenant arbiter, and she was kneeling to speak to August's mother.

August's mother. The realization landed in the middle of the memory like a dropped glass. A tall woman with August's own dark hair, wearing the seal-mark at her throat — the same mark, August realized with a lurch, that lived somewhere under her own skin, a birthmark she'd never been able to explain.

"—the terms are killing her," Wren was saying, and her voice was warm, reasonable, the voice of a friend. "You give and give, and the council takes and takes. I can end it. I can end all of it. No more seal, no more sacrifice. Freedom for both realms."

"The seal isn't a cage, Wren." Her mother's voice was gentle and exhausted. "It's a door. Doors need a hinge."

"Doors," Wren said softly, "can be locked from either side."

Then the memory tore open.

Fire — not hearth-fire, not candle-fire, but the white heat of a covenant breaking, the sound of a boundary tearing like wet cloth. August watched herself, small and terrified, running through the burning halls while adults screamed in two languages. She watched Wren Halloway stand at the heart of the covenant-house with her arms raised and her face *rapturous*, drinking in the raw power that poured off the shattered bond like light off an opened furnace — hoarding it, funneling it, binding it into herself and her chosen, the first Hollow Men, their eyes going flat and gray as they filled.

She watched her mother fall. Not dramatically, not screaming — just folding, the way a door folds when its hinge is pulled out, the seal-light guttering out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

And she watched herself — twelve years old, smoke-stained, holding the one thing she'd been told to protect if everything else failed — make a choice. She saw her own small hands hide the seal, saw her own small self turn and run toward the human side of the road, saw Wren's face when she realized the child hadn't burned with the rest:

Saw Wren Halloway look at a fleeing girl across a burning hall and say, in a voice of terrible tenderness: *"There you are."*

The memory ended.

August came back to herself on the floor of Sefa's cottage, cheek against cold stone, the bone keystone still clenched in her fist hard enough to ache. Brannoc was crouched over her, saying her name — the false one, the bought one, *August, August* — and Sefa was pressing water to her lips, and outside the window the village dogs had started barking at nothing.

"How long?" she rasped.

"An hour," Brannoc said. "You screamed twice. Then you went quiet, and quiet was worse."

August sat up. Her body felt borrowed and new. Every detail of the Breaking Night sat in her head now with the terrible clarity of a thing finally returned to its owner — the fire, the argument, the hinge, the falling, the *there you are*.

"Wren Halloway," she said.

"What about her?"

"She was my mother's friend." August's voice came out flat and strange, stripped of everything except fact. "Her arbiter. Her confidante. And she broke the covenant from inside it, and she took the power for herself, and she's been feeding on it for twelve years while the garden dies and the city forgets and my mother—" Her jaw locked. She made herself finish. "My mother died being the door, and Wren pulled out the hinge and called it freedom."

Brannoc exchanged a look with Sefa. "August. What are you going to do?"

What was she going to do. For twelve years she had run — from wardens, from customers, from the shape of her own missing childhood. Running was the only trade she'd ever mastered. Steal, sell, vanish. Survive.

But survival was just running in slow motion, and she was done with it.

"She thinks I'm a loose end," August said. She opened her hand and looked at the bone keystone, pulsing gently against her palm like a second heart. "She's spent twelve years thinking the seal died with my mother. She doesn't know I'm the fourth keystone. She doesn't know I'm coming."

"Coming for what?" Sefa asked, though her eyes said she already knew.

"For her." August stood, and her legs held, and the keystone's warmth spread up her arm like a vow. "For the third keystone. For every memory she burned and every person she hollowed. She made a weapon out of forgetting." She looked at the two of them — the disgraced warden and the old singer, the only family the last twelve hours had given her. "So I'm going to make a weapon out of remembering."

Outside, far off toward the coast, thunder rolled — or something that sounded like thunder, if thunder could come from underground, from beneath the drowned chapel where a salt keystone waited in the dark.

Brannoc went pale. "That's the chapel," he said. "That's the direction of the chapel."

August was already reaching for her coat.
