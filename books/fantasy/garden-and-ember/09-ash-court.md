## 9. Ash Court

Wren Halloway kept no clocks in the Ash Court. Time was a courtesy the old covenant had paid her, and she had stopped accepting its gifts twelve years ago.

But she felt it anyway — felt it the way a scarred hand feels weather coming. A pressure behind the sternum, faint as a struck bell heard through three walls of stone. Somewhere in Grayhollow, or beyond it, something that had been still for twelve years had begun to move.

She set down her pen mid-sentence and listened with the part of herself she never showed anyone: the part that had once been a gardener's apprentice in Virelle, kneeling in loam, learning the names of roots.

The bell rang again. Closer to meaning this time. Not one keystone stirring but two — no. She counted carefully, the way you count heartbeats when you suspect they're failing. One stone moving along the coast road. And something else, something older and quieter, moving *with* it. A resonance she knew better than her own name.

The seal was walking.

"Vess," she said to the empty room.

Her secretary appeared in the doorway within four seconds, which meant he'd been waiting outside it, which meant he always was. Tam Vess had a talent for being where she needed him before she needed him there. It made him useful. It also made him the only person in the Court who frightened her slightly, because a man who anticipated your orders might someday anticipate your thoughts, and Wren Halloway had built an empire on the principle that no one should hold anything of hers she hadn't given freely.

"The salt stone," she said. "It's moving."

Something crossed his face — quick, swallowed, gone. Grief, maybe. Or hunger. With Vess it was hard to tell the difference; he'd been hollowed so long he wore his emptiness like tailoring.

"I'll wake the Hollow Men," he said.

"No." She rose from the desk and went to the window. Below, Grayhollow lay under its permanent shroud of ash-fall, the city she had saved, the city that lit candles to her in secret and spat at her name in public. Both were fine. Both were honest. "Send two. Quietly. I want to know who carries it before I want it back."

"And if it's who we think?"

Wren watched the ash drift past the glass like gray snow falling upward, which is what it did here, over the Court, where the old magic pooled and curdled. Twelve years of ash. Twelve years since she had stood in a garden full of singing light and put a knife through the heart of heaven.

"Then," she said, "we finish what we started."

* * *

People believed the Hollow Men were monsters. Wren encouraged this. Monsters could be feared from a distance; men had to be reasoned with, and reasoning was how cities died.

They had been men once — wardens mostly, the ones who'd stood with her on the Breaking Night and afterward couldn't bear the silence where the garden's song used to be. So she'd given them a way not to hear it. A small surgery, done with consent, done with care: she took the memory of the singing and left them the purpose. They walked through Grayhollow in their gray hoods and their gray masks, and children hid from them, and mothers told stories about them, and every story was wrong except the truest one, which no one told: that they were volunteers.

That was the thing about the covenant nobody wanted to remember. Everything it gave, it took twice. The garden sang to you, and you loved it, and loving it, you obeyed it, and obeying it, you forgot you had ever been anything but its instrument. Harmony, they'd called it. Communion. Wren had another word for it.

Slavery dressed in flowers.

She walked the long gallery of the Court that evening, past the windows where the ash fell forever, and let herself do the thing she did on nights like this, when the old bell-tone stirred in her chest: she argued with the dead.

*You saved us,* the dead said, meaning the garden, meaning the seal, meaning the woman whose face Wren still saw sometimes in firelight. *You were our hands in the human world. You tended us. We gave you peace.*

"You gave me a leash," Wren answered aloud, because the gallery was hers and no one was there to hear a founder talk to ghosts. "You gave everyone a leash. You just made it feel like a lullaby."

That was the whole of it. That was the argument she'd been making for twelve years to anyone with ears, and almost no one had ears, because memory was the first thing people surrendered and the last thing they missed. The covenant had run on given memory — freely surrendered, the old texts said, as if *freely* meant anything when the alternative was watching your grandmother's face go strange and far away while the garden drank her childhood out of her like nectar. People gave. That was the horror. They lined up to give. They called it devotion.

And at the center of it all, holding the boundary between realms like a living hinge, there had been a seal. A person made into a door. Born to it, raised to it, consumed by it — slowly, generation after generation, each seal worn thin by the constant giving until nothing remained but function. Wren had watched the last seal fade. Had held her hand at the end, that gray, translucent hand, and heard her say, with the garden's voice already half in place of her own: *It doesn't hurt. That's the worst part. You stop minding.*

Wren had minded enough for both of them.

So: the Breaking Night. The knife through the covenant's heart. The keystones scattered to guardians she trusted — trusted then, anyway — and the seal's memories torn out and hidden where no one would ever think to look, because the seal had been a child, and children grow up, and grown women with missing years make excellent weapons if you aim them carefully.

Except she hadn't aimed this one. She had simply buried her deep enough and hoped the ground held.

Now the ground was shaking.

* * *

The report came at midnight. Two Hollow Men, kneeling in the map room, their masked faces tilted up toward her like question marks cut from slate.

"A charm-seller," the taller one said. "August Dunmore. Works the night market. Sells bottled memory — stolen goods, most of it. The courier Halvard reached her before he died. She has the map."

"Halvard." Wren closed her eyes briefly. Of course. Faithful, doomed Halvard, who had carried messages between the guardians for twelve years and finally carried one too far. "Did she open it?"

"She fled before we could take her. The fence Odile says—"

"I know what Odile says. Odile says whatever keeps Odile breathing." Wren turned to the great table at the room's center, where a map of the old Ember Road lay under glass, its three branches inked in fading gold: salt to the drowned chapel, bone to the border villages, ember to — well. To the ember. "Dunmore."

The name sat strangely in her mouth. She had chosen it herself, twelve years ago, handing the child to a smuggler with instructions and a purse: *Give her a life. Give her nothing else.* August, for the month the gardens bloomed hardest. Dunmore, for the fortitude she'd need. A name was a kind of mercy, Wren had thought then. A self you could build from scratch, unburdened by what you'd been born to carry.

She had not thought of it as cruelty. She still didn't. That was the difference between her and everyone who would ever judge her: she had never once flinched from the arithmetic. One girl's childhood against two worlds' freedom. Every guardian who'd ever guarded a keystone had made the same trade and called it duty.

"Dunmore has the salt stone," the second Hollow Man said. "Or will. Brannoc guards it still."

"Brannoc." Wren almost smiled. The disgraced warden, the guilt-bearer, the man who'd knelt in the ashes of the chapel and sworn to keep watch over a rock until the world ended or he did. Sentimental fool. Useful fool, once. "He'll give it to her. He'll probably apologize while he does."

"Shall we take them?"

She considered the map. Three roads. Two stones accounted for — salt with Brannoc, bone with the singer in the border village, old Sefa, who sang to roots and had sung to seals and had earned her long exile honestly. And the third stone, the ember, in the keeping of a man who hated magic more than any warden alive, because magic had eaten his life out of him and left him standing.

Three stones. And a fourth that no map showed, because the fourth was flesh.

"No," Wren said again. "Let her gather them."

Both Hollow Men looked up. Even masked, she could feel their confusion — twelve years of obedience and they still expected her to crush threats early. That was the old covenant thinking, and she'd burned it out of them slower than she'd liked. Strike early. Strike always. Hold everything.

"We are not the garden," she told them. "We don't take. If Dunmore comes to the stones, let her come. Let her touch them. Let her *remember*." The word came out harder than she intended, and she let it. "A seal who remembers is a seal who can choose. And a seal who chooses—"

She stopped. Looked at the map. At the gold thread running from the market square of Grayhollow out along the Ember Road to the last gate, the gate she herself had sealed shut with her own two hands and a grief she permitted herself exactly once a year.

"—a seal who chooses can be reasoned with," she finished. "Bring me the singer first. Sefa talks too much, and everything she says is true, and truth is the one weapon I won't leave lying around."

* * *

Alone again, Wren did what she did on the worst nights. She unlocked the drawer.

Inside lay a photograph, charred at one corner — the twin of the one she'd left in Halvard's lodging, the one that would lead the girl straight to her, because Wren had planned it that way and then hated herself for planning it. In the photograph, a woman stood in a garden that no longer existed anywhere but here, laughing at someone off-frame, her sleeves rolled, dirt on her hands, alive in the way only the truly unburdened are alive.

Maren. The seal before the seal. The woman Wren had loved, in the plain unglamorous way you love a colleague who becomes a horizon — everything measured against her, everything short of her.

The covenant had taken Maren the way it took all of them: gradually, then completely. Wren had watched the laughter drain out of that face year by year, replaced by the garden's serene, terrible contentment, until the day Maren looked at her with eyes gone green as pond water and said, *I don't remember my mother's name, and it doesn't hurt, and isn't that wonderful?*

It wasn't wonderful. It was the most obscene thing Wren had ever heard, and she had heard the covenant's founding hymns.

So yes. She had burned the garden. She had shattered the covenant into three stones and a hiding child, and she had spent twelve years building a city where no door between realms could open, where no voice could sing you out of yourself, where every man and woman owned their own mind or died defending it. They called her tyrant. They called her butcher. They burned her effigy in the winter festivals, and she attended in disguise some years, just to watch, because their hatred was the freest thing in Grayhollow and she had made it so.

Half-believe me, she thought at the city, at the girl, at the dead. Half-believe me, and then come tell me which half.

The pressure behind her sternum pulsed again — stronger now, unmistakable. Two stones in motion. A seal waking to her own shape. Somewhere out on the dark road, August Dunmore was touching things that would unmake the careful lie of her life, and every hour brought her closer to the choice Wren had killed heaven to make unnecessary.

Restore the covenant, and dissolve into a door. Destroy the stones, and free everyone — at the cost of letting both realms forget each other into gray extinction. Those were the options. Wren had lived inside them for twelve years like a cell with one wall missing, and she knew which option she'd chosen, and she knew what it had cost, and she would pay it again.

But she remembered something else too, something she rarely let surface: Maren, in the last lucid month, gripping her wrist with the last strength that was wholly her own, saying, *If you burn it — find another way. Promise me. There's always another way. The garden lied about that too.*

Wren had promised. Then she had burned it anyway, because there hadn't been time, and promises made to the dying are the easiest ones to break.

She locked the drawer. She blew out the lamp.

"Come find me, August," she said to the ash-dark window, to the city, to the child she had orphaned on purpose. "Come find me, and we'll see which of us the truth unmakes first."

Out along the Ember Road, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the third keystone began to move.
