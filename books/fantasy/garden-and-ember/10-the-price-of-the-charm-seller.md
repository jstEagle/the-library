## 10. The Price of the Charm-Seller

They took her at the mouth of Tanner's Row, where the cobblestones went slick with dye-water and the lamplight turned everything the color of old bruises.

August had felt the trap before she saw it — the particular emptiness of a street that should have had beggars in it and didn't, the shuttered windows with eyes behind the slats. Twelve years of working Grayhollow's gray edges had taught her to read a street like a charm: shake it, listen for the rattle, know when something inside was wrong.

She'd known. She'd walked into it anyway, because the only other road out of Tanner's Row was the one the Hollow Men watched, and between wardens and Hollow Men she'd have chosen the wardens every time.

Almost every time.

"Charm-seller." The warden in front was young, square-jawed, his badge polished bright enough to catch the lamp. Behind him, two more fanned out with the bored efficiency of men who'd done this a hundred times. "August Dunmore. You'll come quiet or you won't come at all."

"You've got the wrong thief," she said. "I'm just a girl selling nostalgia to sad people."

The young warden smiled without warmth. "We pulled your stall apart an hour ago. Found three charms with covenant-workings in them. That's a burning offense, Dunmore." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "But Captain Vess says you might be worth more talking than burning."

*Vess.* The name landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there. Tam Vess — no. There were a hundred Vesses in Grayhollow. It meant nothing.

It meant everything, and she knew it did, and she let them put the iron cuffs on anyway because the alternative was running and she'd already spent the last of her running on the Ember Road.

* * *

The holding cells beneath the Warden's Hall smelled of wet stone and old smoke — the smoke of burnings past, soaked so deep into the mortar that no amount of rain could wash it out. They sat her on a bench in a cell with no door, just a barred gate, and left her alone long enough to be afraid properly.

That was technique. She knew it because she used it herself, on buyers who haggled. Let them sit with the silence until they'd rather pay anything than keep sitting in it.

The difference was that her buyers walked out with a bottle. She didn't know what she'd walk out with.

Footsteps. Not the flat boots of patrol wardens — something softer, deliberate. A woman rounded the corner of the corridor carrying a lantern, and August's stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

Odile.

Her fence stood on the other side of the bars looking exactly as she always looked: silver-streaked hair pinned with surgical precision, a coat worth more than August's whole stall, rings on six fingers and none of them cheap. Her face held the mild, patient expression of a woman waiting for tea to steep.

"You," August said.

"Me." Odile lifted the lantern, light washing across the cell. "You look terrible, dear."

"You sold me out." It wasn't a question. It couldn't be. Odile didn't visit holding cells out of charity; charity wasn't in her inventory.

"I facilitated an exchange," Odile said. "There's a difference, though I understand why you'd miss it from where you're sitting." She crouched, bringing her face level with August's, and for a moment the mask thinned and August saw something underneath it — not guilt, exactly. Accounting. "Three weeks ago, a man came to me asking after a memory-thief who dealt in covenant-work. Described your charms down to the wax you seal them with. Offered a great deal of money for a name and an address."

"And you gave him mine."

"I gave him *a* name and *an* address," Odile said. "Yours, yes. August — I sell information. That is what I do. It's what I've always done, and you've always known it, because you've sold information through me too. Do you remember the cooper on Candle Lane? The one whose wife was poisoning him? Who do you think told his wife where he hid his ledger?"

"That's different."

"It is not different at all. It is precisely the same, and the only thing that changed is which end of the trade you're lying on." Odile straightened, brushing nothing off her coat. "You want to be angry at me, fine. Be angry. But be accurate about it. I never promised you loyalty. Loyalty is a currency I don't stock — too volatile. Everything else, I deal in honestly."

August gripped the bars. The iron was cold enough to ache. "Who was the man?"

"A courier. Thin, coughing, dying — I could hear it in his chest. He paid in old coin, real silver, pre-Breaking mint." Odile's eyes flickered, the first genuine interest she'd shown. "He asked about you by description, not name. Which means someone sent him. Someone who knew your face but not what you call yourself now."

The stranger. The bleeding stranger on her workshop floor, the map pressed into her hands, *the garden remembers*. Someone had sent him to her. And someone — Odile — had made sure he could find her.

"You set the whole thing in motion," August said slowly. "You pointed him at my door."

"I pointed a dying man at a locked door and sold him the key. What he did once he was inside was his business." Odile tilted her head. "And then you went and got yourself caught by wardens, which was nobody's business but your own. Honestly, August. I expected better tradecraft."

"Why are you here?"

"Because Captain Vess wants to talk to you, and I wanted to see your face before he does, so I can price what happens next." Odile paused. "And because — and I'll deny saying this — you were useful to me, and I dislike wasting inventory."

"*Useful.*"

"Don't repeat everything I say back at me in that tone, it's tedious." Odile turned to go, then stopped, her back to the cell. "One piece of advice, free of charge, which should tell you how serious it is. Vess is going to offer you a trade. Whatever he offers — think very hard about whether you're the kind of person who takes it. Because I've watched you for six years, girl, and I genuinely don't know anymore. Neither, I suspect, do you."

She left. The lantern-light shrank down the corridor and the dark came back in behind it, and August sat on the bench with her cuffs and her fear and the terrible accuracy of Odile's last words ringing in her skull.

*Neither, I suspect, do you.*

* * *

Captain Tam Vess came at dawn, or what passed for dawn in a city that lived under ash-cloud — a graying of the dark, nothing more. He dismissed the guards at the corridor's mouth and stood outside her cell a long moment before speaking, studying her the way she studied a doubtful charm.

He was maybe fifty, heavyset, with a face built for scowling and a scar splitting one eyebrow. His badge was old-style, worn dull, the kind wardens stopped wearing when the Ash Court reorganized the force twelve years ago. He hadn't updated. That said something about him. August hadn't decided yet whether it said anything good.

"August Dunmore," he said.

"Captain Vess." She kept her voice level. Inside, the name rang like a struck bell. *Vess.* The man whose memory she'd sold — no. Couldn't be. Coincidence. Grayhollow was full of Vesses.

"Do you know why you're still alive?" he asked.

"My winning personality."

"Because three days ago, one of my informants reported two travelers on the Ember Road. One of them worked magic on the open road — raw, unlicensed, powerful. And one of them," Vess leaned closer to the bars, "was described as a woman with ash-brown hair and a scar on her left palm, traveling with a disgraced warden named Brannoc Hale."

The floor seemed to tilt. August kept her face still through sheer professional habit. "Don't know him."

"Brannoc Hale guarded the drowned chapel at Saltmere for eleven years. Until last month, when he abandoned his post and vanished." Vess's voice stayed flat, procedural, but something moved underneath it — grief, maybe, or its fossil. "Hale was the best of us, once. Then he started guarding stones in the dark like a madman, and wouldn't say why, and wouldn't come home." He straightened. "You were seen with him. Which means either you know where he is, or you know what he was guarding. Either way, you're the most valuable prisoner I've taken in a decade."

"And if I'm neither?"

"Then you're a charm-seller with covenant-work in her stock, and you burn on Friday with the other heretics." He said it without relish, which somehow made it worse. This was a man who hated his own job. "So. Here's your trade."

August waited.

"The Ash Court has been hunting Brannoc Hale for twelve years. Not the wardens — the Court. Their enforcers, their Hollow Men. They want him badly enough that they've burned three informants who claimed to know his location and got it wrong." Vess's jaw tightened. "I want to reach him first. I want to bring him in alive, before they do, because if the Court takes him he'll die slow, and he doesn't deserve slow. Give me his location. Give me the chapel, the road, whatever you have. And you walk out of here. Charges dropped. My word on it."

"And your word is worth what, exactly?"

"More than hers," Vess said, nodding toward the corridor where Odile had stood. "Yes, I know she visited you. She visits all my valuable prisoners. It's how she stays rich." He paused. "You have until I finish this cigarette. Then I go back to paperwork and you go back to Friday."

He lit it. The match flared, throwing his scarred face into sharp relief, and August sat with the offer turning over in her hands like a bottle she was deciding whether to buy.

Because here was the thing. She *had* the location. Better than that — she had the salt keystone's resonance humming in her blood, she had the map's second thread, she had Brannoc's trust, given reluctantly, earned barely, sealed with a dead man's debt. Brannoc, who had looked at her execution list and handed her the stone anyway. Brannoc, who owed the courier his life and was paying the debt forward to her.

And here was the other thing, the thing crawling up her throat: she wanted to give Vess the location. Not for freedom — she could probably talk her way out, she'd talked her way out of worse. She wanted to give it because giving it was *easy*, because it solved everything at once, because survival was the only law she'd trusted for twelve years and the law said: *trade what you have for what keeps you breathing.*

The stranger had died pressing a map into her hands. Sefa had surrendered a stone she'd guarded half her life. Brannoc had forgiven her before she'd even finished betraying him — no, not yet. Not yet. That was still ahead of her, a door she hadn't walked through.

But she could feel herself walking toward it.

"Captain," she said, and heard her own voice go soft and reasonable, the voice she used on grieving buyers, the voice that opened purses. "If I tell you where Brannoc is, the Hollow Men will get there before you do. Your informants aren't the only ones watching the roads. You'll be leading them to him with your torch lit."

Vess's eyes narrowed. "You're stalling."

"I'm negotiating. There's a difference — ask my fence." She rose, cuffs clinking, and came close to the bars. Close enough to lower her voice. "You want Hale alive. Fine. So do I — he's the only reason I'm not dead on the Ember Road. So here's my counter-offer. I don't give you a location. I give you a *meeting*. I tell you where he'll be, when, and I'm there, and we settle this face to face instead of you dragging him in chains to whatever passes for mercy in this building."

"And why would I trust that?"

"Because if I wanted to sell him, I'd have sold him already, and you know it. You've been in this game longer than I've been alive, Captain. You know the difference between a seller and a broker." She held his gaze. "I'm offering to be your broker. One meeting. If it goes wrong, arrest us both."

Vess smoked in silence. Ash grew on the cigarette's tip.

"The border village," he said finally. "The one off the Ember Road. That's where he's headed, isn't it. That's where the second stone is."

August said nothing, which was answer enough, and hated herself for how much answer it was.

"There's a garden there," Vess said quietly. "Old woman tends it. Sefa." Something crossed his face — quick, buried, gone. "I knew a woman who sang like her once. Before." He crushed the cigarette under his boot. "Fine. One meeting. You'll be escorted — my people, my terms. Try to run and the deal's ash."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

He unlocked the gate himself. The cuffs came off and her wrists ached with the sudden freedom of them, and August walked out of the Warden's Hall into the gray morning feeling exactly as imprisoned as she had inside — only now the bars were ones she'd forged herself, link by link, out of other people's trust.

* * *

Odile was waiting outside, naturally, seated in a hired carriage with the window down, as if she'd simply been passing.

"Broker," she said, as August climbed in uninvited. "How novel. Six years I've watched you sell other people's pasts, and today you finally sold something of your own."

"I didn't sell him. I bought time."

"You sold a location dressed as a meeting, and you told yourself a story about the difference while you did it." Odile passed her a flask — real tea, hot, sweetened. August drank it because her hands were shaking and she refused to let them show. "Congratulations, dear. You're officially one of us."

"One of *you*?"

"Traders. People-dealers. The honest vermin of Grayhollow." Odile's tone wasn't cruel. That was the worst part — it was almost kind, the way a surgeon is kind. "I told you to think hard about what kind of person you are. Did you?"

August stared out the window at the waking city — the market stalls unfolding, the ash-sifters sweeping their eternal gray harvest, children running messages between doors. All of it built on the same trade she'd just made. Everyone selling everyone, everyone surviving.

"Yes," she said finally. "And I didn't like the answer. So I'm going to spend the rest of this becoming someone who would have answered differently."

"Mm." Odile signaled the driver. "For what it's worth — and it's worth little — the courier who came to me three weeks ago. Before he paid me, he asked one question. Not where to find you. He asked whether you seemed happy." She looked at August directly for the first time since the cell. "I told him I'd never checked. He said that was the saddest thing anyone had ever sold him."

The carriage rolled. August held the warm flask against her sternum like a charm against a curse, and thought about Brannoc at the drowned chapel, waiting, trusting, not knowing yet that his location had already changed hands twice.

She had perhaps four days before Vess's escort marched her back toward Saltmere. Four days to decide what she'd do when she stood in front of the man she'd traded.

Or — a colder thought, arriving uninvited — four days to decide what she'd do if the Hollow Men reached him first, and the only person who could warn him was the woman who'd already proven his trust was a currency she'd spend.

The carriage turned toward Odile's shop, and August watched Grayhollow slide past, gray and grinding and full of people trading people, and understood, finally, why the courier had died with the words he'd chosen.

*The garden remembers.*

She just hoped it remembered something kinder than what she was becoming.
