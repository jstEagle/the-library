## 12. The Third Guardian

August found the name the way she found most things in Grayhollow: by paying for it.

Three coppers to a lamplighter who'd seen a tall man with river-gray eyes buying candles every seventh day. Two more to a fishwife who knew where he lived — a room above the rope-walk in Saltrow, where the air smelled of tar and old hemp and the sea got into everything. And one silver, reluctantly, to a boy who ran messages for anyone who didn't ask why.

*Tam Vess.*

She'd said the name aloud once, on the walk over, just to feel its weight. It had meant nothing to her then. A name attached to a face she couldn't forget: the man in the night market, three weeks ago, whose hands had shaken around a charm that held his own stolen past. The man she'd run from through ash-lit alleys while wardens' boots rang off the cobbles behind her.

The rope-walk was quiet at midmorning. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings below. August climbed the outside stairs slowly, each step announcing itself, and stood at the door with her knuckles raised.

*He hates memory-thieves,* Odile had told her, when August had gone asking — against every instinct, because Odile's advice was usually worth exactly what it cost. *Hates them more than the wardens do. The wardens burn you out of law. He'd burn you out of grief. Whatever you're planning, girl, plan better.*

She knocked anyway.

No answer. She knocked again, harder, and heard something shift inside — not footsteps. Something heavier. A chair dragged across floorboards.

"I know you're there," she said to the door. "I'm not a warden. I'm not from the Ash Court. I need to talk to you about a memory."

Silence, long enough that she almost turned away. Then the bolt scraped back, and the door opened six inches, and Tam Vess looked at her through the gap with eyes like water over stone.

"You," he said.

"Me," August agreed. "Can I come in before you decide what to do about it?"

He didn't move. Up close, without the market lanterns painting him gold, she could see what twelve years of hollowness did to a face. It wasn't age. It was the absence under the skin, the look of a house where someone had carried the furniture out room by room until only the walls remembered living.

"I could call the wardens," he said.

"You could. They'd take us both." She kept her voice level. "Or you could hear me out, and then decide. I've been called many things, Mr. Vess, but I've never been called a coward, and I don't intend to start today."

Something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, nothing like warmth. Curiosity, maybe, or the animal patience of a man who had spent twelve years waiting for something to happen to him and had learned to let it arrive.

He opened the door.

* * *

Tam watched the thief sit down at his table as if she owned it, and hated how steady her hands were.

He'd thought about this moment for three weeks. Lying awake in the dark with the shape of the loss pressing on him like a hand over his mouth, imagining what he'd say if he ever found her again. He'd imagined shouting. He'd imagined worse. In the worst imaginings there was a length of rope and the old gallows tree on Warden's Hill, and he was not a gentle man in those imaginings, though he had never once been a violent one.

Now she was here, small and sharp-eyed, wearing a coat too big for her and an expression like a locked drawer, and all he could think was: *she's younger than me.* Whoever she'd been before the trade took her, she hadn't been at it long enough to grow the calluses she wore so well.

"You sold my memory," he said. "Twelve years ago. You'd have been a child."

"I was working for someone else then." Her jaw tightened, just slightly. "That's not an excuse. It's a fact."

"Whose?"

"A woman named Wren Halloway."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Tam sat very still. He knew the name — everyone in Grayhollow knew it, though few said it above a whisper. The founder. The Burner. The woman who had made magic a death sentence and memory a commodity, and built an empire on the difference.

"She stole it first," Tam said. "My memory. From my own head. And then she—" His voice caught on the old rust of it. "Do you know what it was? What she took?"

"No."

"My daughter." The word came out flat, worn smooth from use, a coin rubbed featureless. "Her name was Liss. She died when she was nine. Fever, the winter the harbor froze. My wife went two years after — grief, they said, though I think it was simpler than that. And the only thing I had left of either of them was one afternoon. One afternoon on the riverbank, Liss teaching me to skip stones, laughing every time hers sank. Twelve years of holding that. And then I woke up one morning and it was *gone*, and there was a hole in me shaped like a riverbank, and no one in this city would even say the word magic out loud to help me find it."

August said nothing. Tam was grateful for that. He'd had people try to comfort him, over the years, and their comfort had always felt like salt in the hollow.

"Then three weeks ago," he went on, "a charm-seller in the night market is selling bottled memories to grieving fools, and I buy one because I buy one every month, because hope is a habit I can't break. And I uncork it, and it's *mine*. The riverbank. The stones. Her laugh." His hands had closed into fists without his permission; he made himself open them. "And the seller runs. Like a thief. Because that's what she is."

"Yes," August said quietly. "I ran."

"And now you're here. Why?" He leaned forward. "Why does a memory-thief walk into the house of a man who has imagined her hanging for twenty-one days straight? What could you possibly want from me that's worth the risk?"

She reached into her coat.

He flinched — he'd never been a violent man but he'd learned to expect violence from her kind — but what came out wasn't a knife. It was a stone. Black, shot through with veins of dull red, no bigger than a fist, and the moment it touched the table the whole room changed. The candle flames bent toward it. The tar-smell of the rope-walk gave way, for half a breath, to green growing things and warm earth.

Tam's heart slammed once, hard, and went still.

"That's not possible," he whispered.

"It's the ember keystone," August said. "One of three. There's a covenant, Mr. Vess. An old one, between this city and another realm, and it was broken twelve years ago — the same night your memory was taken. I've spent the last month learning that. I've nearly died twice getting these stones. And the third keystone is guarded by someone the map calls *the keeper of the given thing*." She met his eyes. "It's you. It's been you the whole time. You're the third guardian, and you don't even know it."

* * *

August watched him understand it in stages, the way frost climbs a window.

First disbelief — his mouth opening, closing. Then the arithmetic: *twelve years, the same night, the hole shaped like a riverbank.* Then something worse, something she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a man realizing the story of his suffering had a chapter he'd never read.

"The guardian of the bone keystone is a woman named Sefa," August said. "She sang to gardens in Virelle before the Breaking Night. The guardian of the salt keystone was a warden named Brannoc, who took the post out of guilt." She paused. Brannoc's name still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. "They were chosen because they'd lost something to the covenant and stayed standing anyway. That's what a guardian is. Someone who remembers what was broken well enough to keep the pieces."

"I don't remember anything worth keeping," Tam said bitterly. "That's the whole—"

"You remember the *loss*." She leaned forward. "That's the piece, Mr. Vess. The keystone doesn't need your happiness. It needs your grief. It needs the exact shape of what was taken, because that shape is the proof the covenant existed at all. Your hollow isn't damage. It's a record."

He stared at her. Outside, a gull screamed and fell silent, as if listening.

"If that's true," he said slowly, "then why did Halloway leave me alive? Why leave a guardian breathing?"

"Because killing you wouldn't have served her. A dead guardian is a closed door. But a hollowed one—" August spread her hands. "A hollowed one is a lock with the key thrown away. She didn't need to kill the guardians. She needed you all to forget you were guarding anything. And it worked. For twelve years it worked."

Tam stood up. Walked to the window. Stood with his back to her, looking out at the gray water, and August let the silence stretch because she understood — she understood better than he could know — that a man sometimes needs to be a stranger in his own life before he can agree to reenter it.

"There's a condition," he said finally. "Before I give you anything. Before I go anywhere with you."

"Name it."

He turned. And August saw, with a lurch, that the hollowness in his face had shifted — not filled, but aimed. Pointed at her like a drawn bow.

"You carry memories," he said. "Bottles of them. Other people's pasts, and maybe your own — I saw your face in the market, girl, when I held that charm. You looked at your own childhood like a stranger's photograph. So here's my price." He crossed the room, slow, deliberate, and stopped an arm's length from her. "You don't pay me in coins. You don't pay me in promises. You pay me the only honest currency a thief has ever had."

"No," August said, before he finished saying it.

"*A memory.* Yours. Freely given, freely taken — the way the old covenant worked, if half of what you've told me is true. You choose which one I take. I won't choose for you; I'm not Halloway. But you'll sit in that chair, and you'll open your mind to me, and you'll let me reach in and take what we both agree on. And when it's done, it's done. No taking it back. No charm to buy it home again." His voice didn't shake at all. "You want me to trust you with the last piece of a broken world? Then show me what trusting costs. Show me you can pay it."

* * *

The room was very quiet.

August looked at the ember keystone on the table, at the black stone with its buried fire, and thought about Sefa's garden — the hidden green place behind the border village, the old woman's hands in the soil, the smell of rosemary and rain, the first time since her stolen childhood that August had felt the ground hold her instead of trying to swallow her. She thought about Brannoc dying in the sacked chapel, forgiving her with his last breath, calling her *seal-bearer* as if the words were a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there.

She thought about the empty shelves inside herself, and how careful she'd always been never to count them.

"If I refuse," she said, "you keep the keystone."

"I keep the keystone," Tam agreed. "And you go back to selling ghosts in bottles until the wardens catch up with you. And the covenant stays broken, and whatever's left of the garden-realm finishes dying, and Halloway wins by default because no one was willing to pay." He shrugged, and the shrug was terrible in its gentleness. "I waited twelve years, girl. I can wait twelve more. Can the garden?"

August laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "You've been talking to Odile."

"I've been talking to everyone. It's amazing what people tell a hollowed man. They think nothing reaches us." His eyes didn't soften. "Choose."

She sat in the chair. It was absurd, how ordinary the chair was — pine, scarred, one leg shimmed with folded paper. She gripped the arms and made herself breathe the way she breathed before opening a customer's mind for extraction: slow, deep, finding the floor beneath the fear.

"I choose," she said, "the village. Sefa's village. The garden, the meals, the nights by the stove. All of it."

Tam went still. "That's recent. That's—"

"That's the only thing I have that's mine." She heard her own voice go rough and hated it. "Everything else was stolen or bought or bartered. That month is the one thing I chose to keep. So it's the one thing worth giving." She lifted her chin. "Take it. All of it. Not a sliver — I know how thieves work, and I won't be left with a scab of it, some ache with no story attached. If you take it, take it whole, and I'll never know what I paid. That's the deal. That's the honest version."

For the first time, something moved across Tam Vess's face that wasn't grief. It might have been respect. It might have been horror. With a hollowed man, she suspected, the two lived close together.

"You understand," he said quietly, "that afterward you won't know why this hurts."

"I'm counting on it," August said. "Hurts are the only records I've got left."

He pulled the second chair around to face her. Sat. Reached out, and she nodded once, and his fingers came to rest against her temples — light as a charm-maker's, and just as practiced, because of course he'd learned; twelve years of studying the trade that ruined him, learning its grammar so he could hate it precisely.

"Wait," he said. "One thing. Before I do it. Tell me your name. Not the one you sell under. Your real one. So that somewhere, in whatever's left of me after this, someone remembers that the woman who paid was called something."

And August opened her mouth to answer —

— and found nothing there.

Not hesitation. Not refusal. *Nothing.* She reached for her own name the way she reached for her keys, her knives, her mother's face, and her hand came back empty. Somewhere in the last month — the chapel, the road, the garden-flash of singing and fire — something had already begun collecting its fee, and she had been walking around inside the debt without feeling the weight of it.

"I don't know," she whispered.

Tam's hands stilled against her temples. "What do you mean, you don't—"

"I mean I reach for it and there's a hole." Her heart was suddenly going very fast. "There's a hole where my name used to be, and I never noticed, because I've been using 'August' so long it felt like mine. But it isn't. It's a coat I put on. And underneath—" She stopped. Breathed. Made herself finish. "Underneath there's nothing. The covenant already took it. Or Wren did. Or I gave it away and don't remember giving."

The gulls had gone silent outside. Even the tar-smell seemed to hold its breath.

"So take the village," August said, and her voice came out steadier than anything inside her deserved. "Because apparently names aren't mine to lose anymore. Take the one thing I actually chose. And then, Mr. Vess — guardian of the given thing — you're coming with me to Virelle, because I have a feeling the fourth keystone is going to need witnesses."

Tam Vess looked at her for a long moment — at the thief sitting in his chair with her fists white-knuckled on the arms, offering up her one true month like a coin she'd bitten to test.

"Liss would have liked you," he said, which was not forgiveness, and was not nothing.

His fingers pressed her temples. The candle flames bowed. And August Dunmore — or whoever she had been before the name — closed her eyes, and opened the door, and let him in.
