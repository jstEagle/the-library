## 13. What August Gave Away

The memory left her the way a tooth leaves a gum: with pressure, then a strange lightness, then the tongue going back to find the hole and finding nothing.

Tam Vess held the charm up to the lamplight, turning it slow, watching the ember-glow inside it swirl like smoke in a bottle. His hands shook. Twelve years of shaking, she supposed, didn't stop just because the thing you'd been shaking for was finally in your grip.

"That's it," he said. Not a question. He'd know it anywhere. She understood that now in a way she hadn't in the market all those weeks ago, when he'd looked at her across a stall and she'd seen a mark instead of a man.

"It's yours," August said. "It was always yours."

He closed his fist around it. For a long moment he just stood there in the doorway of his house — a narrow place above a shuttered dyeworks, smelling of tallow and old rain — breathing like a man who'd been underwater for a decade and had only now thought to surface.

Then he set the ember keystone on the table between them.

It was smaller than the salt stone, smaller than the bone. A knuckle of dark glass with a coal buried at its heart, and when August's eyes touched it, something behind her ribs answered — a pull, low and patient, like a hook set in meat.

"Take it," Tam said. "I don't want it in my house another night."

"You could have sold it. Wren Halloway would pay—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final. "Don't say her name in here."

* * *

They sat across from each other because neither of them seemed able to leave. Tam poured tea that had gone bitter in the pot and drank his without tasting it. August wrapped both hands around her cup and tried to take inventory of herself.

That was the trade. That was what she'd offered, kneeling on his floor with the three keystones between them like cards laid face-up: *take a memory from me. Any one. Freely given.* She'd said it before she fully understood what she was saying, which, she was learning, was how the covenant worked. It didn't want payment extracted. It wanted surrender.

Tam had chosen without hesitation. "The village," he'd said. "The one where you found the bone stone. The singer's village. I saw it when I touched your mind — I saw you happy there. Take that one out."

She'd almost argued. Almost said *take anything else, take Grayhollow, take Brannoc dying in the chapel, take the worst thing I own.* But she'd looked at his face — twelve years hollowed out, twelve years of reaching for a past that some thief like her had bottled and sold to strangers — and she'd understood that this was the point. It had to cost. If she'd gotten to choose what it cost, it wouldn't have been a covenant. It would've been a purchase.

So she'd opened herself the way Brannoc had taught her, the way the salt stone had shown her, and let him reach in.

And now Sefa's garden was gone.

Not gone the way stolen things are gone — not locked away behind someone else's teeth. Gone the way a room is gone after fire. She knew the facts of it. She knew she had traveled to a border village and met an old woman who sang to roots, and that the woman had fed her stew and told her about the Breaking Night, and that August had slept there feeling — 

Feeling what?

That was the hole. She knew the word *belonging*. She knew she had felt it, because she'd written it in her own head like a label on a bottle: *this is what it is, remember this.* And now she held the label and the bottle was empty. She knew the shape of the feeling the way you know the shape of a country from a map. She could not feel it. When she reached for Sefa's kitchen — the steam, the dog by the stove, the old woman's cracked voice rising through the floorboards into August's room — she got facts. Inventory. A list where a life used to be.

"I can see why you wanted it back," she said quietly.

Tam looked up. "What?"

"The memory. What they took from you." She turned her cup. "You don't know what you're missing. You just know you're missing. That's worse than grief. Grief has a shape."

Something moved in his face — not softness exactly. Recognition, maybe. The terrible fellowship of people who've been robbed by the same kind of hands.

"You gave it up easy," he said. "For a thief."

"Not easy." She thought about how to say it honestly. "But I'm starting to think everything worth doing costs exactly this much. You just don't find out until you've already started paying."

* * *

The ember keystone rode against her sternum in a sling of leather, and all through the small hours it pulled.

She sat in Tam's kitchen while he slept — actually slept, for the first time in years, she guessed, with his memory warm in his fist like a child's toy — and she studied the stone by candlelight and made herself understand what she was holding.

Three keystones. Salt, bone, ember. Each one guarded by someone who had paid to guard it. Brannoc, guarding out of guilt, giving twelve years of exile. Sefa, guarding out of love, giving a hidden garden and a lifetime of watching the border for signs of ruin. Tam, guarding out of hate, giving — what? She'd assumed hatred was cheap fuel. Now she wondered what Tam had surrendered to keep that stone safe. Everyone who touched the covenant paid in the same coin. She just hadn't known how to read the ledger until she'd made an entry of her own.

Because that was the lesson, wasn't it. That was what the trade had shown her, plain as a price chalked on a stall door.

The covenant ran on given memory. Freely surrendered. Not harvested, not stolen, not taken from children in the night — *given*. The old seal hadn't been a battery. She'd been a volunteer. Every day of her life, she must have chosen, again and again, to pour what she was into the boundary between worlds so that both worlds would remember each other. And Wren Halloway had looked at that endless willing sacrifice and seen only waste. Seen power being spent instead of hoarded. Had cut the seal open and poured the contents into her own keeping.

August turned the ember stone in her fingers and felt it turn something in her.

Restoration meant her unmaking. She'd known that since the garden spirit showed her the fourth keystone wearing her own face. But she'd held onto a loophole, some smuggler's hope tucked in her coat: *maybe there's a way to do it and keep the profits. Maybe I can be the seal and still be me.*

There wasn't. There was no version of this where she kept everything. The covenant didn't run on everything anyone had. It ran on what was given — and what she would give was the whole of herself, continuously, forever, until there was no August Dunmore left to give anything. Just a function wearing her body. Just a seal, singing to two realms, remembering everything except whose throat the song came from.

Unless.

She set the stone down. Her hands were steady. That surprised her.

Unless the covenant could learn a new arithmetic. Unless it didn't need one person carrying everything if it could carry a little weight in ten thousand hands. She thought of the market square, of the wardens, of Odile's tired face, of every buyer who'd ever stood at her stall with coins sweating in their palm because they needed a dead wife's laugh more than they needed rent money. People *wanted* their memories. They'd fight for them, bleed for them, sell their beds to buy them back. Nobody had ever asked them to give one freely. Nobody had ever told them the walls between the worlds were held up by memory at all.

Maybe that was the third path. Maybe that was what she'd been walking toward since a dying man bled out on her workshop floor.

But first she had to get out of Grayhollow with three keystones and a plan half-formed, and the Ash Court already knew she was coming.

* * *

Dawn found her at the door. Tam was awake, washed, dressed — standing straighter than any man had a right to after one night with his own past in his hand. He looked different. Not younger. Anchored.

"I remember my daughter's name," he said, apropos of nothing, as she gathered her coat. "Maren. She died of lung-rot when she was nine. I've been saying *the girl*, *my girl*, for twelve years, like she was anybody's." His voice caught and he pushed through it. "Maren. God. Maren."

August stopped with her hand on the latch.

"I hated you," Tam said. "In the market. I'd have burned you myself if the wardens hadn't come. I want you to know I still think memory-thieves should burn." He met her eyes. "But you gave me back my girl, and you gave me your village to do it, and I don't know what to do with a debt like that. So I'll tell you what I know instead."

"Wren?"

"Wren." He nodded toward the window, toward the gray city waking beyond the glass. "The Hollow Men don't patrol. Everyone thinks they do, but they don't. They *wait*. They gather where the covenant gathers strength. My keystone hummed last night for the first time in twelve years — hummed loud enough to wake me before you ever knocked. They'll have heard it too. They'll come here, and they'll find me holding my daughter's name, and I'll tell them whatever I please." A thin smile. "But they'll follow you, August Dunmore. You're carrying three stones and a grudge. You're the loudest thing in this city."

"Then I should leave while it's still dark."

"It's never dark in Grayhollow," Tam said. "That's the whole problem with this place. Nobody remembers what dark is for." He pressed something into her hand — a twist of paper, heavy, smelling of tallow and iron. "Warden whistle-oil. Smear it on any door and it'll read as warded for a day. Buy yourself an hour somewhere you'll need it."

She took it. At the door she paused.

"Tam. The memory I sold you — the one that started all this. In the market. Do you remember what was in it? Before you knew it was yours?"

He was quiet a moment. "A garden," he said. "A woman singing. Fire coming over a wall." His jaw tightened. "I thought it was beautiful. That's why I bought it. Some part of me recognized it even then, and I couldn't stand that something that beautiful belonged to a stranger."

August walked down into the waking streets with the ember stone pulling at her chest and the hole in her head where a village used to be, and she understood, finally, completely, what the road ahead cost.

She'd felt belonging once. She'd given it away to buy a stranger's daughter back. And the covenant was waiting at the end of the Ember Road to ask her for everything else — and the only question left, the one she carried through the ash-lit morning like a lit fuse, was whether she could teach a whole city to pay willingly what one woman had always paid alone.

Behind her, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she heard the ember keystone begin — very softly — to sing.
