## 16. Wren's Argument

Wren Halloway had not set foot in Virelle in twelve years, and the garden-realm remembered her the way a body remembers a knife.

The hedges recoiled as she walked the Ember Road's last mile. Vines pulled their thorns in when she passed, then flung them out again behind her like curses whispered at a departing back. The roses — her mother had grown roses here once, before the covenant took her too, before Wren understood what *took* meant — the roses turned their faces away.

Good. Let the garden hate her. Hatred was honest. The covenant's love had never been.

She found August Dunmore standing at the edge of the orchard where the spirit kept its court, three keystones cradled against her chest like a stolen infant. Salt, bone, ember. They glowed faintly even in daylight, and around them the gray rot of the dying realm had pulled back in a rough circle, grass green for six feet in every direction. The girl was a hearth and didn't know it yet.

Wren stopped ten paces off and let her hood fall back.

"You look like your mother," she said.

August spun, keystones clutched tighter, and Wren watched her take inventory the way she'd taught her to years ago — exits first, then weapons, then faces. The girl's eyes went wide with something worse than fear. Recognition. That was the cruelty of it; Wren had made sure the child would remember her face even if she remembered nothing else. Some lessons were carved deeper than memory.

"You," August said. "You're real."

"I'm sorry about that." Wren spread her hands, empty. No Hollow Men. She'd left them at the gate, all forty of them, and had walked in alone because this was not a battle she intended to fight with soldiers. "I imagine you've built quite a picture of me. The monster of the Breaking Night. The woman who burned the garden."

"Didn't you?"

"Yes." No flinch. Lying to this girl would be wasted breath; she'd been raised on truth before Wren unmade her, and some part of her still knew how to taste a falsehood. "And I'd do it again. But not for the reasons you think. May I show you something? It won't hurt. I've had enough of things that hurt children."

August's jaw tightened. "Everything you say is a knife with a ribbon on it."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in twelve years." Wren knelt slowly, deliberately, and pressed her palm flat to the grass. "Watch."

She let the memory rise — not a charm, not a bottle, just the raw thing, offered open the way the old garden-magic allowed between two people standing on living ground. She felt August feel it: the pull, the involuntary lean, the way the girl's breath caught as the past poured across ten feet of dying grass.

* * *

The memory was of a woman named Lark Dunmore, and it was the last one Wren had of her alive.

Lark stood in this same orchard, twenty-two years ago, with her sleeves rolled and her forearms green to the elbow. She was laughing at something Wren had said — Wren couldn't remember what, which was its own small grief — and then she wasn't laughing, because the covenant had come for its tithe.

Wren showed it whole. No editing. That was the point.

Lark going still as the garden's roots rose gently around her ankles, tender as hands. Lark smiling through it, because she was seal-born and had been taught since birth that this was holy. Lark saying, *tell my daughter I chose this,* and meaning it, and the horror of it being that she truly did mean it. Then the giving — memories first, name second, self last — and the thing that remained afterward, standing in the orchard wearing Lark's face with nothing behind it, singing to the roses in a voice that knew no words anymore. The seal renewed. The boundary strong. Both realms fed.

And the garden blooming, lush and obscene, on what it had eaten.

Wren released the memory and sat back on her heels. Her own eyes were wet. She let them be. Sentiment, weaponized honestly, was still sentiment.

"She volunteered," Wren said quietly. "That's the part no one tells you. The covenant doesn't take. It *asks*, in a voice you can't refuse, and it has spent centuries breeding people who can't conceive of refusing. Your mother died believing she was a bride. She was a meal."

August hadn't moved. The keystones pulsed against her chest, faster now, agitated, and the green circle around her feet had widened by a full yard. Even her grief fed the thing.

"You're lying," August said, but her voice cracked on it.

"I have lied to wardens, to the Ash Court, to myself, for twelve years. I am not lying to you. Ask the spirit. Ask the stones. Ask the garden — it remembers her better than I do." Wren rose, knees cracking, an old woman's body she resented more each year. "Her name was Lark. Yours is August. And there was a third name, once, that neither of us can say anymore, because the covenant ate it when you were six years old and it will eat yours too if you do what the spirit is asking."

* * *

They walked while they talked, because August wouldn't sit across from her, and Wren understood that too. Side by side was easier. She'd learned that in the early days of the Ash Court, when former covenant-wardens came to her tribunal expecting chains and got instead a chair level with hers and questions instead of sentences. Half of them had wept. People could bear almost anything except being understood by the person they'd decided to hate.

"The spirit told you," Wren said. "I felt it from Grayhollow, the moment it showed you. The whole realm shuddered. So let's not pretend you don't know what restoration costs. You dissolve. Not die — worse. You become the seal. A function. Your mother's mother before her, eleven generations of women who walked into the roots smiling. The covenant keeps the *shape* of you and pours out everything else, and both realms get another century of borrowed time until it needs feeding again."

"And your answer is to burn it all." August's voice had gone flat, warden-flat, and Wren recognized the armor because she'd invented it. "Let both realms forget each other. Let Virelle go gray. Let Grayhollow keep choking its magic out of people like me — out of thieves, out of children—"

"There is no version of this where no one bleeds." Wren stopped walking and turned to face her fully. "I looked for one. Twelve years, August. I tore apart every archive, every sealed vault under the Ash Court, every fragment of pre-covenant text the wardens ever confiscated. There is no third door. There is the covenant, which eats a person every hundred years and calls it harmony. Or there is the ending, which hurts, and grieves, and sets everyone free."

"The city isn't ready—"

"The city will *never* be ready. That's what readiness is for." Wren laughed, short and bitter. "You think I wanted this? I was a warden of the old order. I sang the tithe-songs. I held mothers' hands while the roots took their daughters, and I told them it was beautiful, because believing it was beautiful was the only way I could keep doing it. Then your mother—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Then Lark. Then the Breaking Night. I didn't burn the covenant because I hated the garden. I burned it because I loved someone it ate, and I swore on her ashes that no one else would ever be asked to smile while it happened."

The wind moved through the orchard, carrying a smell like overripe fruit and old incense. Somewhere deep in the realm, the spirit was listening. Wren felt it the way you feel a spider on your neck.

"So here is my offer," she said. "No war. No Hollow Men at your back — I gave the order to hold at the gate, and they'll hold, because unlike the old wardens, mine follow me out of choice. You give me the three keystones. We destroy them together, here, on dying ground, where the failure will be visible and honest. The boundary closes forever. Virelle finishes fading — decades, maybe a generation, but it fades free. And Grayhollow—" she held the girl's gaze, "—Grayhollow gets to learn to live without stealing. Magic outlawed or legal, it doesn't matter, if the well it drinks from runs dry. No more charms cut from grieving strangers. No more children marked seal-born at birth and raised as livestock. No more you."

August was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, and Wren heard the trap assembling inside the care.

"And the memories already taken? The ones in bottles all over Grayhollow? The twelve years sitting in my head that you stole?" A pause. "Did you steal them, or did the Breaking Night?"

Ah. There it was.

"Both," Wren said. "I broke the seal. The stolen years spilled loose, and my Hollow Men gathered what they could — not to use. To *keep*. Because a seal's memories are load-bearing, girl. Scattered, they're harmless. Bottled in one place, they're a key. I've kept yours locked in my vault for twelve years rather than risk them finding their way back into you." She smiled without warmth. "Yes. I have your childhood in a box. And yes, destroying the keystones means destroying any hope of ever getting it back. I won't pretend otherwise. I'm asking you to choose a future over a past. It's a monstrous thing to ask. I know, because someone asked your mother the opposite, and she said yes, and it killed her."

* * *

August turned away and walked to the orchard's heart, where the oldest tree stood — the First Tree, the covenant's anchor, its bark gray and peeling now like sunburnt skin. Wren followed at a distance, letting her have the space. You couldn't rush a person toward a decision. You could only make sure that when they reached it, they reached it knowing everything.

"I saw her," August said finally, not turning around. "In the salt stone. A garden. A woman singing. I thought it was Sefa." Her hand rose to the stones at her chest, unconsciously, the way you touch a wound. "It was her, wasn't it. My mother. The covenant keeps echoes. Everything it eats, it keeps a little of, humming in the roots."

"It does."

"Then if we destroy it — destroy the keystones, break the boundary — those echoes die too. Whatever's left of her. Whatever's left of the eleven before her." Now August turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "You're asking me to kill my mother twice."

"I'm asking you to let her rest." Wren kept her voice level. This was the hinge, and she knew it, and she would not flinch from it. "There is a version of her in those roots, August, and it is not sleeping and it is not saved. It is *used*. Every bloom in this realm grows out of what's left of her. Every year the covenant sings through her mouth. Is that mercy? Keeping the echo running so you have somewhere to visit? I burned incense at her memorial for six years before I understood I was praying to a cage." She stepped closer. "Freedom sometimes looks like loss. That's why most people never choose it."

"And if I restore the covenant instead? Reforge it, find some new seal, spare the echoes, save the realms — and just make sure it's *me* who pays, so no one else has to?" August's chin lifted, and for one vertiginous second Wren saw Lark standing there, sleeve-rolled and stubborn, twenty-two years old and walking toward the roots. "Wouldn't that be kinder than burning everything?"

"No," Wren said. "It would be the same altar with a newer victim. And in a hundred years, some warden like me stands in an orchard watching some other girl walk into the ground, and nothing — *nothing* — has changed except the name on the stone." She let the anger show, finally, all twelve years of it. "Kindness that requires a sacrifice isn't kindness. It's bookkeeping."

The silence stretched. Above them the First Tree creaked, shedding a strip of gray bark, and beneath it Wren saw the faint pulse of the roots — the covenant's heartbeat, slow and patient, waiting. It had waited twelve years. It could wait an hour more. It had all the patience of a thing that had never once been hungry and never once been full.

August looked down at the keystones in her arms. Salt, bone, ember. Three doors out of four.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "if I hand them over — what happens to the fourth keystone? To me? If the covenant dies, does the seal die with it? Do I get to just be a person?"

Wren hesitated.

It was a small hesitation, half a breath, but August Dunmore had been raised in this garden before Wren unmade her, and she caught it the way a fisherman feels the line go taut.

"What aren't you telling me?" August said.

"The archives were incomplete," Wren said carefully. "On one point only. Everything else I've told you is true — I'd swear it on her grave, and I don't swear lightly. But the texts disagree about what happens to a living seal when the covenant it anchors is destroyed. Some say freedom. Some say..." She exhaled. "Some say the seal *is* the covenant, in the end. The last knot of it. And cutting the rope doesn't always spare the knot."

August stared at her. The keystones pulsed. Somewhere beneath their feet, patient as winter, the roots began very quietly to sing.
