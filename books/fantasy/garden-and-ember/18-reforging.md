## 18. Reforging

The bell above the market gate rang at dawn, and Maren Oduya counted the strokes out of habit. Nine strokes for nine o'clock — no, wrong. The sun was barely up. She leaned out her window over the tanner's row and watched a woman walk into the empty square carrying three stones in a satchel and nothing else.

Maren had seen August Dunmore before. Everyone in Grayhollow had, if they'd ever needed to forget something or remember it. She'd bought a charm once, after her husband died — not to keep his memory but to blunt its edge, so she could sleep. It hadn't worked. Nothing worked. That was the whole rotten bargain of this city: you paid someone to steal your grief and you woke up the next morning with the same hole in you and less money besides.

But this woman walking into the square wasn't selling anything. She set the satchel down on the fountain's rim like she owned the ground under it, and Maren felt something in her teeth ache — an old feeling, like the hum before lightning.

"Get inside," Maren called down to nobody, to everybody. "Get your children inside."

Nobody moved. Nobody ever did, when the thing worth watching was also the thing that would kill you.

* * *

Tobias Fenk was sweeping ash off his doorstep when he saw the memory-thief raise her hands over the fountain, and his first thought was *wardens*, because that was what twelve years of living in Grayhollow taught you. You saw magic, you called the wardens, you kept your eyes on the cobbles while they burned whoever it was. His father had done it. His grandfather too. It was how you stayed alive in a city that had decided, one night long ago, that remembering was a crime.

Except when Tobias opened his mouth to shout for the patrol, no sound came out. Because the thief — Dunmore, the charm-seller, the one whose face was on half the warden posters in the low quarter — looked up at him across the square, and her eyes were the color of banked coals, and she said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"You're allowed to watch."

And gods help him, Tobias stood there with his broom in his hand and watched.

She drew something from the satchel. A stone, pale as sea-foam, and when her fingers closed around it the fountain water went still as glass. Then a second stone, bone-white, and the stillness spread outward like frost across a window. Then a third, dark red, veined with light, and Tobias felt it in his chest — a pull, gentle and enormous, like a hand reaching into him through the ribs.

Not taking. Asking.

"No," he whispered. "No, no, don't—"

The city remembered.

* * *

It came to Odile Vass first as a smell. Beeswax and river water and the particular green scent of growing things, which no one in Grayhollow had smelled in twelve years because nothing grew here anymore except debt and fear. She was standing at her stall in the covered arcade, counting coins she didn't need, when the smell hit her and her knees buckled against the counter.

A garden. She was in a garden. Not dreaming — *remembering*. She could feel dirt under fingernails she hadn't bitten in years. She could hear a woman singing to rows of pale flowers, a song in no language Odile knew and understood anyway, the way you understand your mother's voice through a wall.

She had been *there*. Before. When? How?

The memory unfolded like a letter she'd written to herself and forgotten to open. Virelle. The garden-realm. The covenant — the word arrived whole, heavy, true, and with it everything the Ash Court had burned out of every book and every head: the two worlds bound together, human memory feeding the garden, the garden's abundance feeding back, a seal who held the door between them, a child raised to be that door—

Odile gripped the counter and wept without knowing why, and around her the whole arcade was doing the same. The fruit-seller on her knees. The moneylender clutching his ledger like a life vest. An old man laughing and sobbing at once, saying a name over and over — *Sefa, Sefa* — as if calling someone home across a great distance.

Twelve years of forgetting, coming back all at once, and it hurt worse than any knife, and not one person in that arcade ran.

* * *

August felt them arrive — the memories, the city's stolen inheritance pouring back through the keystones and into ten thousand minds at once — and she staggered, because it was like holding a river in her arms. The salt stone sang against her palm. The bone stone thrummed. The ember stone burned cold, the way real fire burns cold if you've stood close enough to lose yourself in it.

She had planned to speak. She'd rehearsed it on the Ember Road, words for the crowd, words about cost and choice. But the crowd was already past needing words. They were on their knees in the ash-gray square, holding their own heads, gasping out names of people and places the Ash Court had scraped from the world. A woman was screaming *Virelle* at the sky like a prayer. A boy of maybe ten was staring at his own hands and saying, wonderingly, "I had a garden. I had a garden," though he couldn't have been more than a toddler when it was taken.

So August said only what mattered.

"This is yours!" Her voice cracked across the square, and the stones amplified it, carried it down every street. "They didn't burn magic out of you — they stole it! Every year since the Breaking Night, they've taken your memories to feed themselves and left you gray! I'm not here to sell you anything. I'm here to give it back!"

Silence, except for weeping. Then somewhere a voice — Tobias Fenk's, though she didn't know it — shouted back: "The wardens will come!"

"They're already coming," August said. "I know. Stay anyway."

Because she'd heard them. Boots, many, moving fast up the processional way. And beneath the boots, a sound worse than boots: the dry whisper of Hollow Men, that sound like pages turning in a book no one should read.

She set the three stones in a triangle around the fountain's rim and stepped back into its center, and she put her hands at her sides like a woman standing for judgment, because that was exactly what she was doing. If the covenant needed a seal, let it have a witness instead. Let the whole city see what was done in its name. Secrecy was Wren Halloway's weapon; it had always been. So August stood in the open, in the light, and refused to be secret even now, even with death coming up the street.

"Whatever happens," she told them, "don't look away. That's all I ask. Don't look away."

* * *

Wren Halloway had ruled Grayhollow for twelve years by understanding one thing about people: they would trade almost anything for safety, and afterward they would call the trade wisdom. She had built the Ash Court on that trade. She had built it on the night she took a child's memories and shattered a covenant that had been eating humanity alive for a thousand years, dressed up as partnership, dressed up as harmony, while the garden-realm drank the city's pasts like wine.

She had never regretted it. Regret was a luxury of people who hadn't read the old ledgers, who didn't know what the previous seal had become by the end — a woman hollowed into a doorway, smiling, empty, *grateful*, until there was nothing left to bury.

So when the resonance hit her — three keystones waking in the market square like struck bells — Wren did not panic. She put on her ash-gray coat, took up her iron-tipped staff, and went to finish what she'd started twelve years ago.

"Hollow Men," she said, and the six of them unfolded from the shadows of her hall like paper figures, faceless, patient, made from the memories of the condemned — she'd never told anyone that, either. Every Hollow Man wore what remained of a burned thief. It seemed fitting. It seemed just.

"The girl is in the square," she said. "Publicly. In daylight." Something twisted in her chest that she refused to name — not admiration, never that. Foolishness, she decided. The girl was foolish, spending her one advantage on spectacle.

Or she was doing exactly what Wren would have done at twenty-five, before the ledgers, before the truth.

Wren pushed the thought down where she kept all the others and walked out into the morning.

* * *

The crowd saw the wardens first — forty of them in ash-gray, fanning across the square's mouths — and the crowd did not run, which surprised everyone including, visibly, the wardens. Then the Hollow Men came through the ranks like a wind through wheat, and the crowd *still* did not run, and August understood that something had already changed. Fear kept people alone. Memory, even agonizing memory, gathered them.

"Wren!" August called, as the Ash Court's founder crossed the square, unhurried, terrible. "Come to see what you buried?"

"I came to bury it deeper." Wren stopped ten paces off, and up close her face was older than the posters made it, lined around the eyes like cracked glaze. "You've given them back the hunger, girl. Do you know what the covenant took every year? Whole decades. Whole *people*. I've read the ledgers from Virelle's high days. Villages that gave so much memory they forgot how to speak. Seals worn down to doorways. Your mother—"

"Don't." The word came out of August like a blade leaving a wound. "You don't get to say her name."

"She gave everything," Wren said, relentless, "and the covenant thanked her by consuming her. And you want to rebuild it. With *their* memories this time." She gestured at the kneeling, weeping crowd with something like contempt. "Look at them. Already paying. Already giving. It never changes, August. There is always a seal, and the seal is always someone, and someone is always spent."

"Then we'll spend no one," August said. "Not me. Not them. I found a third way on the Ember Road, and I'm standing in it right now."

"There is no third way. There is the door or there is the dark."

"Then watch me build one."

Wren sighed, and it was almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened August more than any threat. "Hollow Men," she said quietly. "Take the stones. Take her. Leave the crowd. They'll thank me later — they always do."

The Hollow Men moved.

What happened next, the survivors of that morning would tell a hundred different ways, and none of them wrong. The salt stone flared and the fountain erupted upward, a wall of gray water that caught two Hollow Men mid-stride and held them, thrashing, their paper faces dissolving. The bone stone answered it, and the cobbles themselves rose — not violently, but the way a body rises to defend itself, cobblestones tilting into a barricade between the wardens and the crowd. And the ember stone —

The ember stone sang.

Every person in the square who had received a returned memory felt it answer, felt their own recovered past rise up like heat, and the Hollow Men faltered, because a Hollow Man is made of stolen memory, and the square was suddenly full to overflowing with memory freely held, and stolen things drown in that kind of flood.

One of them — the tallest, nearest August — shuddered, and its blank face split, and beneath the blankness was a man's face, terrified, human, mouthing a name. Then it collapsed into ash.

Wren's staff came up. Light gathered at its iron tip, ugly and absolute.

August stood her ground in the center of the stones, arms spread, heart hammering, and began the reforging — not the old covenant, not the hungry one, but the new thing she'd promised herself on the road: a bond built on witnesses, on thousands of minds choosing to hold what was theirs. She reached for the crowd's attention the way the old seal must once have reached for memory, and the crowd, astonishingly, gave it. Ten thousand pairs of eyes fixed on her. Ten thousand wills, leaning in.

The keystones rose from the fountain's rim and began to turn around her, slow as planets.

"No," Wren said, and for the first time in twelve years her voice shook. "No — you don't know what you'll wake—"

The light at her staff's tip flared white.

The turning stones screamed — a sound like a choir heard underwater — and August felt the fourth keystone wake inside her chest, felt herself begin to open like a door, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever came next, she would not survive unchanged.

Behind Wren, at the square's far edge, the air itself split along a seam of green.

Something from Virelle was coming through.
