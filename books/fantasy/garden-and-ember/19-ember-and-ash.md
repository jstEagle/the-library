## 19. Ember and Ash

The bell in the market square had not rung in twelve years. It rang now.

Mara Tull heard it from her stall where she sold eels by the canal, and her hands went still on the knife because the last time that bell rang she was nine years old and her mother was singing in a garden that did not exist anymore — except it did exist, she could *see* it, sudden as a slap, green light through leaves, her mother's voice threading between the rows like water.

"By the ash," said the man buying eels, and he was crying without knowing why.

Mara looked up. In the center of the square, on the old fountain steps where wardens made their proclamations, stood a woman with three stones floating around her head like moons. Salt-white, bone-pale, ember-red. The woman's coat was scorched. Her face was familiar the way a word is familiar when you've forgotten its meaning.

She raised her arms, and the world remembered.

* * *

The first thing Tam Vess felt was salt.

Not the sea-salt of Grayhollow's gray harbor but garden salt, chapel salt, the taste of a drowned place where he had once knelt beside a woman with August Dunmore's face and sworn an oath he could not now remember making. The memory came back to him whole, unasked for, flooding up through his boots like water through a cracked hull: *the covenant, the seal-child, the night the sky over Virelle tore like paper.*

He had spent twelve years hollowed out and calling it peace. Now the hollowness filled, and filling hurt worse than empty ever had.

Around him the square was coming apart into weeping. A fishwife dropped to her knees. Two warden recruits clutched each other. An old man beat his fists against the fountain and shouted a name, over and over, a name no one in Grayhollow had spoken aloud since the Burning Laws — and every person who heard it felt it land somewhere under the ribs.

Magic was not returning to the city. Memory was. And memory, it turned out, had been the magic all along.

* * *

Wren Halloway stood at the mouth of Copperlane with forty Hollow Men behind her and watched her city dissolve into grief.

"Loose," she said.

The Hollow Men moved. They were not men anymore, mostly — they were what remained after Wren's people had stripped them for parts, memory by memory, until only obedience was left. They carried iron hooks and ash-lanterns, and they crossed the square in silence, and August Dunmore turned to meet them with three keystones orbiting her skull like a crown she hadn't asked to wear.

What happened next, Mara Tull would tell her grandchildren wrong for the rest of her life, because there was no right way to tell it.

August did not fight. She *opened*. The salt keystone flared white and the first Hollow Man to reach the steps stopped mid-swing, hooked arm frozen, as something poured into him — his own past, the one Wren's people had taken, the wife whose face he'd been unable to hold onto, the trade he'd loved before the trade he'd been given. He sat down on the cobbles like a puppet with cut strings and began, very quietly, to sob.

The second one got closer. The bone keystone answered him the same way. Then the ember stone caught, and the resonance spread through the square like fire through dry grass, and everywhere the Hollow Men advanced they were met not with blades but with themselves.

* * *

Odile Vasse had sold information about August Dunmore for enough coin to retire on, and she stood now at her window above the pawnshop watching the consequences bloom across the square, and she found she could not look away.

Because she remembered too.

She remembered her sister. Twelve years ago the wardens had come for anyone who kept a garden charm, and Odile had given them her sister's name to keep her own shop, and then the laws had burned the memories out of the city so thoroughly that Odile had woken the next morning unable to say why her back room held two of everything. Two chairs. Two cups. A second bed, cold, unslept in, year after year, while she told herself she was simply a woman who liked spare furniture.

Now she knew the second cup's weight in her hand. She knew her sister's laugh, low and crooked. She knew exactly what she had done.

Odile left the window. She took down the iron poker by the hearth, which was the closest thing to a weapon she owned, and she went out into the street toward the square, an old fence with an old grief, because there are debts that only memory can collect and only action can pay.

* * *

In the square, the battle had become something no one had a name for yet.

The Hollow Men who still fought fought badly, staggering under returned lives. Citizens who should have fled instead pressed forward, hungry for it, holding out their hands as if the memories might be caught like rain. The keystones sang — actually sang, a chord below hearing that Mara Tull felt in her teeth — and with every citizen who stopped running and started remembering, the chord deepened, widened, took on voices.

August stood at the center of it with blood on her lip from a hook's backswing and her arms spread wide, and she was not conducting it. She had understood, somewhere in the last terrible minute, that she couldn't. This was not a working. It was a *communion*, and communions cannot be cast, only witnessed into being.

A Hollow Man broke free of the resonance and lunged for her throat —

— and stopped, because Brannoc's face surfaced in him. Brannoc, dead in a sacked chapel, forgiving a thief with his last breath. The memory wasn't the Hollow Man's own; it came off August like heat off a stove, everything she carried, everything she'd paid, broadcasting through the stones whether she willed it or not. The Hollow Man touched his own mask, found the seam of it, and pulled.

Underneath was a boy. Sixteen, maybe. Eyes like a startled animal.

"I had a mother," he said, to no one, to everyone.

"I know," August said. "So did I."

* * *

Wren Halloway walked into the square alone.

Her Hollow Men were kneeling or weeping or gone over entirely, and her city was remembering in waves, and she had prepared for this day for twelve years and had imagined every version of it except this one: not a war, but a *homecoming* she was losing.

"Dunmore." Her voice carried the way it always had, the voice that had once taught a young apprentice to bottle grief without spilling any into herself. "You think you're restoring something. You're rehanging a cage and calling it a garden. I have seen what the covenant does to its seal. I have *held* what's left of one."

"You mean my mother." August's voice didn't shake. "I know. I've seen her too."

"She burned out in nine years," Wren said. "Nine years of feeding herself to your precious boundary, and when she was done there wasn't enough of her left to bury. I was her friend. I was the one who scraped what remained off the covenant-stone. So don't stand there on stolen magic and tell me I'm the monster for breaking the machine."

And here was the thing that Wren Halloway had not planned for, the flaw in twelve years of perfect control:

Saying it cost her.

Because to say it she had to reach for the memory, and the memory lived in her like a splinter she'd driven too deep to remove, and the square itself — resonant, singing, wide open — took her reaching and *answered*. The keystone-light bent toward her like a compass needle. The ember stone pulsed once, twice, and gave her back the rest of it: not just the scrapings, but the nine years before. Lira Halloway laughing in a garden. Lira teaching Wren the first charm either of them ever worked, a bottle of sunlight, worthless and perfect. Lira asking her, near the end, hollow-eyed and translucent, *Wren, am I still in here? Am I still anywhere?*

And Wren, who had answered *yes, always* — and had lied, and had known she lied, and had built an empire on the lie because the alternative was to admit the covenant had eaten her best friend while she stood by and called it harmony —

That memory arrived in the middle of the market square in front of four hundred witnesses, and Wren Halloway, founder of the Ash Court, breaker of covenants, killer of kings of nothing, put her hand over her mouth.

It was not a weapon that felled her. It was a Tuesday afternoon, thirty years gone, and sunlight in a bottle.

* * *

Mara Tull saw the Ash Queen stagger and thought, absurdly, of eels — how they go still right before they strike, how stillness is never surrender in anything that has teeth.

But the woman on the fountain steps didn't strike. August Dunmore descended the steps slowly, keystones dimming around her, and stopped an arm's length from Wren Halloway, and the whole square held its breath hard enough to hurt.

"You're right," August said.

Wren's eyes came up, red-rimmed and furious and lost.

"The covenant ate her. It would eat me. It's *designed* to eat whoever holds the seal alone." August's voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm not here to restore it. I came back to Grayhollow to end the argument you and I have both been having for twelve years — the one where somebody has to be sacrificed so everybody else can sleep. You chose my mother. The covenant chose me. I'm choosing neither."

"You can't choose neither," Wren whispered. "There is no third door."

"There is," August said. "It's just one nobody's ever been willing to pay for honestly. Not one seal. Not one arsonist. All of us — a little, freely, together." She looked out over the square, at the fishwife and the fence with the poker and the boy with the pulled-off mask and the old man still saying his dead wife's name like a prayer he'd finally been allowed to finish. "They're already paying it. Look at them. They've been paying since the bell rang."

Wren followed her gaze. Four hundred faces, wet and open and unafraid.

Her hand was still over her mouth. When she lowered it, her voice came out small, stripped of thirty years of command, and it was the truest thing she had said since the Breaking Night:

"It shouldn't have been her."

"No," August said gently. "It shouldn't have been anyone."

The ember keystone rose from its orbit and hung in the air between them, red as a coal, waiting — and August realized, with a lurch, that the stones were not waiting for her.

They were waiting for Wren's answer.
