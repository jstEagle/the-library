## 20. The New Covenant

The square was screaming.

Not the screaming of terror—I knew that sound, had sold charms to people trying to forget it. This was different. This was a thousand voices remembering at once, and memory, it turns out, is loud.

I stood on the fountain's rim with three keystones in my arms and ash raining down like gray snow, and I let the city wail around me. The baker by the chandlery was on his knees, hands pressed to his mouth, whispering a name over and over—a daughter, maybe, or a mother, someone the Ash Court's twelve quiet years had scraped out of him. A warden had dropped his cudgel and was staring at his own gloved hands as if they belonged to a stranger, which, in a way, they did. An old woman near the well laughed and wept in the same breath, clutching a sprig of something green that hadn't grown in Grayhollow since before the ban.

The keystones hummed against my chest. Salt, bone, ember—three stones, three threads of a covenant Wren Halloway had cut with fire, all of them singing now because the city was listening.

And through the middle of it came Wren.

She walked the way she'd taught me to walk a market crowd: unhurried, certain, reading every face for the weakness she could use. The Hollow Men flanked her, gray shapes in gray coats, but even they had slowed. Even they were hearing things. I wondered what memories lived inside those hollowed bodies—whether the flood reaching them found anything at all to hold onto, or just poured through them like rain through an empty jug.

Wren stopped ten feet from the fountain. She looked at me the way she used to look at my first clumsy thefts: disappointment sharpened into something almost like pride.

"You've made a mess," she said.

"I learned from the best."

"August." My name in her mouth still fit like a key cut for a different lock. "Look at them. Look what remembering does. It breaks people open. Twelve years ago I watched the covenant do this to a whole city every single day—everyone bleeding their pasts into a garden so a *seal* could stay fed. You think you're saving them? You're feeding them back into the machine."

"The machine is me," I said. "That's the point. And I'm not doing it."

Something flickered across her face—the first honest thing I'd seen there all day. "Then put the stones down and walk away. Let both realms fade slow and painless. That's mercy, girl. That's the only mercy left."

"No."

"You'd rather watch everything die than give up your precious self?"

"I'd rather stop pretending those are the only two choices."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about standing in the wreckage of a battle with the fate of two worlds in your arms: your feet hurt. Your arms shake. The adrenaline drains out of you and leaves behind something cold and clear, like well water, and in that cold clarity you can finally see the shape of the trap you've been walking toward since a dying man bled out on your workshop floor.

Dissolve into the seal. Become function, boundary, hunger's vessel—the way my mother had, until there was nothing left of her but the covenant wearing her face. Or let the keystones shatter, let Virelle go gray and then go nothing, let Grayhollow forget magic ever grew here at all. Sacrifice or surrender. The knife or the dark.

Wren had built her whole revolution on that fork. So had the covenant itself. Both of them agreed on one thing: somebody had to pay alone.

Standing there in the ash-fall, holding three stones that wanted to be four, I understood that agreement was the lie. Not Wren's lie, exactly. Older. The covenant's own founding assumption, baked in before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath: that memory is a currency one person spends on behalf of everyone.

But I'd spent twelve years selling other people's pasts in bottles. I knew something about memory that neither a garden-spirit nor a revolutionary could know from the inside.

Memory given freely weighs nothing. Memory taken costs everything. And the covenant had been running on taken things for so long it had forgotten its own rule.

"Sefa told me," I said, and my voice carried farther than it should have—the keystones were amplifying me, or the city's new-opened ears were. "In her village. She said the old covenant began with a gift. One woman gave her memories to the boundary because she *chose* to, because she loved both worlds enough to hold the door between them. It wasn't a tax. It wasn't a tithe. It was a wedding vow."

"And when she burned out," Wren said, "they found a child to replace her. And when that child burned out, another. Generations of girls fed into a furnace and called it harmony. I read the records, August. I stood in Virelle's archive while it burned and I read every name."

"I know." I looked at her, really looked, and let myself see what the returned memories were already showing everyone else in that square: a young woman with ink-stained fingers kneeling in a garden, laughing; a mentor's hand on a student's shoulder; a night of fire and a body Wren had loved more than the cause. "I know what you lost. The city knows now too. They're seeing it right along with me."

"Don't." The word cracked. For one heartbeat Wren Halloway, founder of the Ash Court, breaker of covenants, killer of my childhood, looked less like a monument and more like a woman standing in the ruins of her own argument. "Don't you dare use it against me."

"I'm not using it. I'm *returning* it. That's the difference between us, Wren. Everything you took, you took to keep people safe from giving. But love isn't safe. It never was. The covenant didn't fail because it demanded memory—it failed because it demanded it from one throat until the throat tore."

The salt keystone pulsed under my palm. Brannoc's voice, dead a week now: *seal-bearer*. Sefa's voice, older still, singing to roots. Tam Vess's hollow eyes. Everyone who had paid for me to stand on this fountain rim.

"So here's my third path," I said. "Listen carefully, because I'm only saying it once, and after tonight the whole city will be able to tell you whether I lied."

* * *

I set the keystones on the fountain's edge, in a row, like bottles on my market shelf.

Salt. Bone. Ember. Three points of light in the gray afternoon, and above them, thin as smoke, the seam between worlds had already begun to show—a shimmer where the air remembered being a gate.

"The old covenant needed a seal," I said. "One living person, holding the door, paying forever. I refuse. Not because I'm afraid—" and here I had to laugh a little, because I was terrified, down in the marrow, in the place where my missing years lived— "but because I've seen what it makes. It made my mother into a doorway. It made Wren into an arsonist. It made me into a thief. One person carrying everything always, *always* ends up dropping it on someone else."

I turned to face the square. Hundreds of faces, tear-streaked, ash-streaked, wide open in a way Grayhollow faces never were. The baker. The old woman with her impossible green sprig. Wardens who an hour ago would have burned me at a stake without breaking stride.

"The new covenant asks something different," I said. "It asks everyone. A little. Freely. Every person in this city who wants magic to live—who wants the gardens back, the gates open, the road to Virelle real again—gives one memory. Just one. Chosen by you. Given by you. Not harvested, not stolen, not taxed. A gift, witnessed. Thousands of small flames instead of one person burning whole."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"The covenant runs on given memory," I said. "That was always its engine. Wren proved what happens when you take. Let's find out what happens when we offer."

A woman's voice from the crowd—Odile's, of course, my fence, my betrayer, my unlikely ally, pushing through the press of bodies with her chin up and her eyes wet and furious. "And what do you give, August Dunmore? Easy to spend other people's coin."

There it was. The question I'd been waiting for, from the one person sharp enough to ask it.

"Everything," I said. "I'll be the witness. The seal held the door alone; the witness holds it with everyone, and pays the fee that keeps the ledger honest. Whatever the covenant takes from me for brokering this—whatever it costs to stand between the gift and the giver—I give it now, in front of all of you, so no one can ever say the new bargain was struck in the dark."

"That's not a price," Odile said. "That's a blank check."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

* * *

The first memory came from the baker.

He climbed the fountain steps like a man walking to his own wedding, took off his flour-white cap, and pressed his hand flat against the salt keystone. "My wife's laugh," he said. "She died in the fever year. I want the garden to have it. I want something of hers growing somewhere green." Light ran up his arm like dawn through a window, and he smiled—actually smiled—as it left him, and I felt the keystone drink, and I felt the seam above us widen by a hair's breadth, and I heard, faint and far, birdsong from a realm that hadn't sung in twelve years.

Then the old woman gave the smell of bread from her mother's kitchen. Then a dockhand gave the memory of his brother teaching him to swim. Then a warden—Hale, the one who'd hunted me through the ash-lit alleys a lifetime ago, three weeks ago, whatever time was anymore—gave the memory of his father's hands showing him how to hold a hammer straight.

They came in ones, then twos, then a river of them. The line stretched across the square and down Copperlane and I don't know how far beyond, because word travels fast in a city that has spent twelve years starving. Each gift lit the keystones brighter. Each gift stitched another thread across the seam, and the shimmer became a veil became a window, and through it I could see Virelle—gray at the edges still, dying still, but *greening*, color crawling back along the garden-roads like sap rising.

I stood at the fountain and witnessed every gift. That was my office now. When each giver pressed their hand to stone, I spoke the words I invented on the spot because nobody had written them yet: *"Given freely. Witnessed truly. Carried kindly."* Four words, over and over, hundreds of times, until my voice was sand and the words had worn grooves in the air themselves.

And with every gift, I felt the covenant take its broker's fee from me. Small things at first. The color of the sky over my seventh summer. The taste of the first charm I ever stole. My mother's voice saying a word I could no longer hear. It was like being unpicked, stitch by stitch, by gentle hands. I stayed standing. I kept witnessing. I did not let my face show what it cost, because a witness who flinches teaches the givers to fear.

Wren watched from the edge of the square, and the Hollow Men watched with her, and slowly, one by one, the hollow men sat down on the cobblestones like children too tired to stand, because somewhere in the flood of the city's remembering, even they had caught something—a reflection of warmth off a thousand nearby hearts, if not the warmth itself.

* * *

Near dusk, Wren crossed the square.

The crowd parted for her the way water parts around a keel. She stopped at the fountain and looked at the river of givers, at the greening gate, at me—thinner now, I could feel it, lighter in some unaccountable way, as though the covenant were sanding me down grain by grain.

"It's working," she said. No triumph in it. No defeat either. Something worse: doubt.

"It's working."

"For now. Give it fifty years. Give it a hundred. Someone will decide the gifts should be required. Someone will build a bureau of memory, a tithe, a list. It rots, August. Every covenant rots into law, and law rots into force."

"Probably," I said. "And someone will remember this day. Thousands of someones. That's the difference. The old covenant kept its truth in one skull so it could be stolen in one night. This one keeps it in ten thousand. You can't burn what everyone carries."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The gate-light played across her face, and in it I saw the young woman from the returned memories, kneeling in the garden, ink on her fingers, before grief made her a philosopher and philosophy made her a butcher.

"My sister's name was Lark," she said suddenly. "I haven't been able to say it in twelve years. Saying it felt like handing the Ash Court a knife." Her jaw worked. "The covenant took my grief and I called the taking freedom. I built a religion on not feeling it." She looked at the ember keystone, glowing soft as a hearth. "You're going to need guards for that gate. People who understand what taking costs. Who better than the hollowed?"

"Wren—"

"Don't mistake this for absolution. I'll answer for the Breaking Night. I expect the city will want a trial, and I won't run from it." She pulled off her gray coat—the uniform of the Ash Court—and dropped it on the cobbles like a shed skin. Underneath she was just a woman, sixty-ish, tired, ink stains still ghosting her fingertips after all these years. "But I'll stand the first watch. Call it payment on account."

Behind her, the Hollow Men rose from the cobbles. Waiting for orders that would never come in the old language. Wren glanced back at them, and something in her face broke open—not the way stone breaks, the way soil does, when something decides to grow.

"Lark liked gardens," she said, to no one, to everyone, to the gate. "She sang off-key. I'd forgotten that. Gods help me, I'd *forgotten*."

Then she turned and walked toward the shimmering seam, and the Hollow Men followed her, and the crowd let them pass, because the city was learning, faster than I dared hope, that remembering a person whole means remembering their worst alongside their best.

* * *

By full dark, the line of givers still wound through Copperlane like a luminous river, and the gate stood open—a true gate now, an archway of living green and banked ember-light, stable, humming, *fed*. Not on one sacrificed life. On ten thousand small, chosen flames.

Sefa came through it at moonrise, leaning on a young gardener's arm, and when she saw the square—when she saw what we'd built—she started to sing. Off-key, probably. Nobody minded.

I slid down against the fountain, finally, and let myself shake.

Odile found me there. She sat down on the wet cobblestones beside me without asking, which was the closest thing to tenderness she'd offered anyone in the twelve years I'd known her.

"You did it," she said.

"We did it."

"Don't get sentimental, it doesn't suit you." But her shoulder pressed against mine, briefly, deliberately. Then she went still. "August. What did it take from you? The broker's fee. What's missing?"

I reached inward, the way I'd done ten thousand times in my life, checking my shelves, counting my bottles.

And I found the gap.

Not a memory, exactly. Deeper than memory. I searched for the word the gap wore, the handle I'd always used to lift myself, and my mind closed on—nothing. Smooth emptiness, shaped exactly like something precious.

"Odile," I said, and my voice sounded very far away. "What's my name?"

She frowned. "What?"

"My name. Say my name."

"Au—" She stopped. Her brow furrowed. I watched her reach for it and find the same smooth absence, watched the color drain out of her face as she understood. Around us, the givers were still murmuring my name in awed whispers—*August Dunmore, the seal-bearer, the charm-girl*—but even as they said it, I could feel the syllables going strange in their mouths, slippery, unmoored.

The covenant had named its price.

I sat in the square I had saved, surrounded by thousands who owed me everything, and listened to my own name dissolve out of the world like sugar in rain.
