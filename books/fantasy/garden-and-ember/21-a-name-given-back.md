## 21. A Name Given Back

The first thing I lost was the sound of my own name.

I didn't notice it right away. That's the cruel trick of it — you can't miss what you can't hold up to the light. For three days after the square, after Wren knelt in the ash and the keystones sang themselves quiet, people called me things. *Seal-bearer.* *The charm-seller.* *August* — because that was the name Grayhollow had given me when I crawled out of whatever life came before, and August was the only handle anyone had ever gripped.

But on the third night, standing behind my rebuilt counter, wrapping a memory of a summer storm for a woman whose husband had drowned, she said, "What do I call you? Properly, I mean. Everyone just says *the charm-seller*, but that's not a name."

And I opened my mouth to answer, and there was nothing there.

Not a blank, exactly. Blanks have edges. You can feel the shape of what's missing, like a tooth gone from your jaw — your tongue keeps finding the hole. This was worse. This was smooth. I reached for my name the way you reach for any fact about yourself — *I am left-handed, I hate eels, my mother's hands smelled of thyme* — and my hand closed on air so complete it didn't even feel like loss. It felt like the question had never made sense.

"I'm August," I said, and heard how thin it sounded. A season. A borrowed thing.

She smiled and paid me, and I stood alone in the shop with the lamps lit and understood what the covenant had taken as its fee.

Not my memories. Not my years. Not my blood or my breath or my future. My name — the one word in all the world that had been mine before anything else was mine, before Grayhollow, before the theft, before the garden. The old covenant had been forged on given memory, freely surrendered, and I had stood in the market square and offered the city everything I had left to give.

It turned out the last thing I had left was the oldest thing.

* * *

Magic is legal now. I still say it out loud sometimes, just to hear how strange it sits in the air, like a coat cut for someone else. The Ash Court's edicts were struck from the warden rolls by the new council — Brannoc's old second, a gray-eyed woman named Pell, presiding with the tired authority of someone who never wanted the chair. The burn-pits in Cinder Row have been filled in and planted over. That was Sefa's doing. She arrived in Grayhollow six weeks after the reforging with two carts of soil from Virelle and an expression that dared anyone to object, and now there are gardens where the pyres used to stand.

Tomatoes, of all things. The dead ground of Cinder Row grows tomatoes fat as fists, and children steal them, and nobody burns anybody.

You'd think the city would be different overnight. It isn't. Cities don't work like charms — you can't uncork them and pour the change out. But it's different in the way a healed bone is different: same shape, wrong in ways you can feel when it rains. People look at each other longer. They remember things they'd spent twelve years not-remembering, and some of those things are ugly — neighbors who informed on neighbors, wardens who fed the pits — and the remembering hurts, and they do it anyway. That was the covenant I forged. Not forgetting, not hoarding. Honest remembering, carried by everyone, owned by no one.

Some mornings I walk through the market and feel it humming under the cobbles, faint as a pulse through a wrist. Thousands of small surrenders, freely given, holding the door between the realms open. Virelle is greening at its edges again. Sefa says the roots have stopped whispering about dying.

And me?

I build a life. That sounds smaller than it is. Building a life when you've just learned you were born to be a seal — a function, a hinge, a living lock — is not a small act. Every morning I wake up in the room above my shop and choose to be a person instead of a purpose, and every morning the choice holds, because thousands of other people are holding it with me.

But the name.

I've tried everything. I went back to the ember keystone — what's left of it, a dull red stone no bigger than a plum that sits on my shelf and hums when it rains. Nothing. I asked Sefa, who knew me as a child in Virelle, who sang to me, who must have said it ten thousand times into my small ears. She sat across from me with her ancient hands folded and her eyes wet and said, "I can hear myself saying it. I can hear the shape of it. But when I try to bring the word itself up—" She shook her head. "It's like reaching into water for a reflection."

The covenant took it everywhere. Out of me, out of her, out of the records, out of the world. Somewhere in Grayhollow there may be a ledger, a birth-roll, a christening cloth with stitches spelling it out — but if such a thing exists, no one can read it anymore. The letters would be there. The meaning wouldn't. I know this because I found one: a scrap of embroidery in the ransacked lodging of the courier who died pressing a map into my hands, thread forming a word I could look at for hours without the slightest sense that it referred to me. Or to anyone. Just thread.

He knew my name. He died carrying it toward me, or away from me; I'll never untangle which. His face was familiar in a way I couldn't explain, back when he bled out on my workshop floor. I understand it better now. He was Virelle-born too. One of the garden's scattered children. Maybe he'd known me before the Breaking Night. Maybe he'd been the one who carried me out.

I keep his embroidered scrap in the drawer under my counter, next to the wax-sealed map that started everything. Some nights I take both out and lay them side by side and let myself grieve properly — not for the name, exactly, but for the person who had it. Someone was that child once. Someone answered to that word with a whole unguarded heart, before the world taught her to sell pieces of herself to strangers. I mourn her the way you mourn the dead: with love, and without expectation of return.

Then I put the drawer shut and go downstairs and open the shop, because the living have appointments.

* * *

Odile came by last week. That deserves recording, if only for the record's sake.

She looked older. Not frail — Odile will never be frail; she'll go out like a blade snapping — but worn down to something finer and more honest. The betrayal sat between us the whole visit, unspoken, the way it always does. She sold information about me to the wardens, weeks before the end. I sold Wren Halloway's location to save my own skin. We are two women who learned the same lesson in the same school and paid different tuitions, and neither of us has apologized, because we both know an apology would be a lie dressed in silk.

"You could leave," she said, turning a bottled memory over in her long fingers — one of hers, I realized. A grief-charm I'd made her years ago, back when magic was contraband and my customers were mostly people trying to survive their own heads. "Grayhollow owes you nothing. You could go through the gate, see Virelle whole, start over somewhere the ash hasn't reached."

"And do what?"

"Whatever you want." She set the charm down. "That's rather the point, isn't it. You bought everyone that."

I thought about it. Honestly, I did. There's a version of me — the seal-bearer, the function, the thing Wren wanted to destroy and the spirit wanted to restore — that would have walked through that gate and dissolved into the green without a backward glance, and it would have been called sacrifice, and it would have been easier than this. Easier than staying in the city that burned memory-thieves, selling honest charms at honest prices, being known by a name that fits like a coat cut for someone else.

"No," I said. "Virelle doesn't need me. That's the whole miracle of it. Neither does Grayhollow, anymore. Which means for the first time in my life, whatever I do next belongs to me."

Odile studied me for a long moment. Then she did something she has never done in all the years I've known her. She reached across the counter and touched my hand, briefly, like a bird landing.

"August," she said, "suits you better than you think. A month with nothing in it. Everything after it undecided." A pause, and the ghost of her old dry smile. "Whoever named you that saw further than either of us."

After she left I turned the thought over for a long time. A month with nothing in it. Maybe that's what a chosen name really is — not a replacement, but a permission slip. *Nothing is decided yet. Go on.*

* * *

Sefa teaches now, in the garden on Cinder Row, under the trellis where the pyres used to stand. Twice a week the children come — real children, ash-street children, the kind who would have been burned a year ago for so much as humming a hedge-song — and she teaches them the old covenant songs, the ones that aren't spells exactly, more like manners. How to greet a growing thing. How to ask before you take. How to give something back.

I sit in the back sometimes, ostensibly mending charms, actually listening. Last week a small girl with charcoal on her chin raised her hand and asked, "Miss Sefa, what was the garden like? Before?"

And Sefa looked at her for a moment, and then looked at me, and something passed between us that had weight to it.

"It was loud," Sefa said. "People think gardens are quiet, but they're the loudest places there are, if you listen right. Everything talking at once. Roots arguing with rain. Old trees telling the seedlings stories they've told a thousand times." She smiled. "And singing. There was always someone singing."

The children wanted to know who. Sefa said, "A friend of mine," and caught my eye again, and I bent lower over my mending so no one would see my face.

Because here is the thing I haven't told anyone, the thing I carry the way I once carried stolen memories in glass: I remember the song. Not the words — those went with everything else — but the melody, the way it moved, the particular warmth of it. It survived the theft the way a scar survives the wound. And lately, in the evenings, when the shop is shut and the lamp is low, I find myself humming it. I don't know where it goes. I don't know how it ends.

But I've started writing it down. Notes on scraps of paper, tucked into the drawer with the map and the embroidery. A melody with no name, being slowly remembered by a woman with none either.

Maybe that's the trade, in the end. The covenant took my name and gave me back everything else — the choice, the city, the garden, the grief, the girl with the charcoal chin learning to ask before she takes. I'd make that trade again. I'd make it a hundred times.

Still. Some nights I stand at the window and watch the tomato-plants of Cinder Row silvering under the moon, and I press my hand flat against the glass, and I say to the dark, the way you'd speak to someone across a wide room:

*I don't know what you were called. But I know you were loved before you were taken, and I know you chose, at the end, and I know the song.*

*Whoever you were — thank you. I'll carry her forward.*

Below, in the garden, something rustled — wind, probably, or a cat, or the old magic settling into its new bones. Probably nothing.

But I've learned to pay attention to probably-nothing. It's how every story I've ever been inside of began.
