## 22. Garden and Ember

The garden behind the shop grew in defiance of everything Grayhollow believed about itself.

August knelt in the dark soil with her hands buried to the wrist, coaxing a row of emberwort toward the light. It was stubborn stuff — it wanted heat, wanted ash, wanted the kind of ground most gardeners would call ruined. She'd learned that from Sefa, who had arrived in the city last spring with two trunks of seeds and an expression that dared anyone to object. The wardens who once would have burned her at the stake now tipped their hats and asked after her roses.

Magic was legal. That still caught in August's throat some mornings, like a bone she hadn't finished swallowing.

She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her apron. Behind her, through the open back door of the shop, she could hear the morning trade beginning: the bell over the door, Odile's voice — Odile had never left, though neither of them ever said why — the murmur of customers choosing their charms.

Honest charms, now. That was the difference. Every bottle in the shop held a memory freely given, signed for, paid for fairly. No more lifting grief off strangers in the night market. No more wondering whose past she was pouring into glass. When someone sold a memory here, they walked out lighter by choice, and when someone bought one, they knew exactly what they carried.

It was slower work than thieving. It was better work.

August stood, brushed the dirt from her knees, and went inside.

* * *

The shop smelled of wax and rosemary and the faint green scent of the garden drifting through the back door. Shelves lined every wall, hundreds of bottles catching the morning light, each one labeled in her own careful hand: *First snowfall, given willingly. A wedding dance, given willingly. The smell of bread in a dead mother's kitchen, given willingly.*

Given willingly. She wrote it on every label. It was almost a prayer.

Odile was behind the counter, arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of a memory of his late wife singing. August let them argue. Odile had a gift for making haggling feel like friendship, and the fishmonger would leave happy no matter what he paid.

August drifted along the shelves until she reached the one near the window, the one set slightly apart from all the others, where a single bottle sat alone.

It was small — smaller than the others, plain glass, plain cork, sealed with plain wax. And its label was blank.

Not smudged. Not torn. Blank, because there was nothing to write on it. She had tried, a dozen times in the first months, standing with her pen hovering over the paper. There was no name to give it. Whatever the covenant had taken as its fee, it had taken so completely that even she could not have told you what was missing. She only knew that something was. A shape of absence, like the negative space around a figure cut from paper.

She kept it anyway. She never opened it. She wasn't sure anything was inside — perhaps the bottle was empty, perhaps it held the name itself, folded up small and silent. But it was hers, whatever it was. The one thing the new covenant had charged her, and the one thing she refused to pretend she hadn't paid.

"August." Odile's voice cut through the shop. "Customer."

* * *

The customer was a child.

A girl, maybe eight years old, with ash-brown hair and a smudge of coal dust on one cheek, the way all Grayhollow children wore coal dust like a birthright. She stood in the doorway clutching a copper coin in both fists, staring at the shelves the way other children stared at sweetshops.

"I'm not supposed to be here," the girl announced. "My gran says charm-sellers are liars. But she also says you're the honest one, so I don't know what I'm supposed to think."

"Your gran sounds wise," August said, crouching to the girl's level. "What are you looking for?"

The girl's bravado faltered. Her fists tightened around the coin. "My da died in the winter. The collapse at the foundry. And my gran cries at night when she thinks I'm asleep, but I'm not asleep, I hear her. And I thought—" She stopped, jaw working. "I thought if I could buy her something happy. Something from before. So she'd remember being happy instead of just sad."

August felt the old ache, the one that never quite healed, settle behind her ribs. Twelve years ago — no. One year ago, and twelve years before that — she had been the person who profited from exactly this kind of grief. She had bottled other people's happiest hours and sold them back to the desperate at market prices.

"We don't sell memories here," she said gently. "We keep them. If your gran wants to give one away, she can bring it herself, and we'll pay her fairly for it. But we don't take memories from people who aren't standing right here, choosing."

The girl's face crumpled. "But I only have a copper."

"Then here's what a copper buys." August took the coin, then reached under the counter and brought out a small vial — not a memory-charm, just good Grayhollow glasswork — and filled it with a sprig of emberwort from the garden, a curl of red-orange bloom that seemed to hold fire inside it. "You tell your gran this is from the charm-seller behind the shop, and that gardens grow in Grayhollow now. That's worth more than any happy memory. Happy memories tell you the past was good. Gardens tell you the future might be."

The girl took the vial with the reverence of someone receiving a crown. Then, on her way to the door, she stopped. Turned. Stared.

At the blank-labeled bottle.

"What's that one?" she asked. "It doesn't say anything on it."

August followed her gaze. The little bottle sat in its place by the window, catching the light, holding its silence.

"That one isn't for sale," August said.

"But what's *in* it?"

And here was the question she had been asked a hundred times, by customers and neighbors and once, memorably, by a warden-captain who should have known better. She had answered it a hundred different ways. *Nothing. A mistake. Old stock.* Lies, all of them, and lies were the old currency, the outlawed currency, the currency the new covenant had been built to abolish.

She looked at the child waiting for an answer, and she told the truth.

"The most important memory I own," August said.

The girl frowned. "If it's important, why don't you write what it is?"

"Because I don't know what it is," August said. "I gave it away, a long time ago, to buy something that mattered more. And part of the price was forgetting what I'd paid. But I know it was important, because everyone who remembers me from before tells me I used to be someone else entirely. Someone with a different name." She smiled. "So I keep the bottle to remind me that I chose this. Whoever I was, she decided who I'd become. I'm just keeping her promise."

The girl considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "That's sad."

"It is," August agreed. "It's also mine. Both things can be true."

The girl nodded slowly, satisfied, and pushed the door open into the street. Halfway out she turned back.

"What do I call you? Gran says your name but I forgot it."

"August," she said. "Just August."

"It's a good name," the girl decided, and ran off down the lane, emberwort blazing in her fist like a captured spark.

* * *

That evening, Sefa came.

August heard her before she saw her — humming, always humming, the old garden-song that had survived a broken covenant, a stolen childhood, and the death of two worlds' forgetting. Sefa came around the side of the shop with a basket of cuttings and let herself into the garden without knocking, because Sefa had never once knocked anywhere in her life.

"You've let the north bed go wild," Sefa said, by way of greeting.

"It's not wild. It's *self-directed*."

Sefa snorted and settled onto the bench August had built for her, old bones folding down with a sigh. For a while they worked in comfortable silence, August turning soil, Sefa trimming and muttering encouragement to the plants, which leaned toward her the way sunflowers lean toward the sun. Some things didn't change. Some things shouldn't.

"Tam came by today," Sefa said eventually. "While you were out."

August's hands stilled in the dirt. Tam Vess, who had spent twelve years hollowed by the theft of his own past, who had taken from her the memory of Sefa's village as payment for the ember keystone — who had, in the year since, become something almost like a friend, visiting the shop on the first day of every month, buying nothing, saying little.

"He left something," Sefa said. She reached into her basket and drew out a small parcel wrapped in brown paper, tied with string. "He wouldn't tell me what it was. He said—" She paused, and her old voice went careful. "He said you'd understand."

August dried her hands and took the parcel. It was light. Lighter than it should have been, for its size.

She untied the string.

Inside, nested in straw, was a bottle. Plain glass, plain cork, sealed with plain wax.

Blank-labeled.

Her breath went out of her slowly. Beside the bottle lay a note in Tam's blunt, careful hand:

*You gave me a memory once, freely, and it cost you more than you let me see. I've spent a year trying to repay it. I couldn't. So instead I made you this. An empty bottle, from the same glassblower who makes yours. Put in it whatever you choose to keep. That's the whole trick of it, seal-bearer. That's the whole trick of anyone. We are not what was taken from us. We are what we choose to put in the glass.*

August sat down hard on the edge of the raised bed, the bottle in her lap, the evening light going gold and then amber and then the deep ember-red that gave the flower its name.

Sefa watched her, saying nothing, humming softly.

"I had a name," August said at last. "Before. Everyone says so. Brannoc read it off an execution list. Wren knew it. The covenant took it as its fee, and no one can recall it — not me, not them, not the archives. It's gone as thoroughly as if it never was." She turned the empty bottle in her hands. "Some days I think I should stop looking. Some days I think I've already become whoever I am, and the girl on the list is just... a stranger I keep house for."

"And other days?"

"Other days I stand in front of that shelf and I want to break the glass and pour myself into the gap and *remember*." She laughed, short and unsteady. "Is that mad?"

"No," Sefa said. "That's gardening. You plant what you have and you grieve what won't grow and you keep tending anyway." She looked at the empty bottle, then at August, and her eyes were bright and old and kind. "So. What will you put in it?"

August looked at the garden — her garden, grown from Sefa's cuttings, rooted in Grayhollow ash, blooming against every law the city had ever written. She looked at the shop with its honest charms and its willing labels. She thought of Brannoc forgiving her with his last breath. Of the square full of strangers remembering together. Of a child running down the lane with a spark of living fire in her fist, carrying it home to a grieving grandmother.

She thought of the blank bottle on the shelf, and the blank bottle in her lap, and the space between them where a name used to be.

"I'll start small," she said. "Today. Today I'll put in the smell of this soil, and Sefa humming, and the girl with the copper coin. Tomorrow, whatever tomorrow grows." She stood, and brushed off her knees, and smiled — not the market smile, not the thief's smile, but the real one, rare and crooked and hers. "Whoever I was, she saved two worlds and paid a name for it. The least I can do is make sure the name was worth spending."

Sefa laughed, warm as banked coals, and began to hum again.

And in the shop behind them, on its shelf by the window, the first blank bottle sat in the fading light beside the second — two small vessels of glass and silence, one holding the most important memory August owned, and one waiting, patient and open, for all the rest.
