## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Hollow Guild kept its ledgers in a cellar that smelled of tallow, dust, and older money, and Juniper Pemberton had spent six years learning to love that smell the way other people loved rain.

She sat at her desk in the third alcove from the stairs, where the lamplight fell at its most forgiving angle and the drafts from the door could not reach the pages she was auditing. Around her rose the shelves: hundreds of ledgers bound in grey calfskin, their spines stamped with years going back past anyone's living memory. Somewhere above her, carts rattled over the cobbles of Vane Street, and somewhere below her—two floors down, behind three locked doors—the vault held the actual impossible things, each one tagged and catalogued and sleeping in its little drawer like a wasp in winter.

The ledgers were her country. She knew its borders better than anyone.

*Entry 4,112,* she read, running one finger beneath the copperplate script. *One bottled echo, provenance: the Bellfounder's Row fire, year of the Long Frost scare. Contents: approximately eleven seconds of screaming, preserved in glass. Condition: stable, though it has begun to whisper when the moon is new. Storage fee: two crowns per annum. Notes: DO NOT SHAKE.*

Juniper made a small tick beside the entry with her pencil, then paused, because the note said the echo had been moved to Drawer 88 last spring, and the cross-reference card in the drawer index said Drawer 61. Someone had transposed the numbers. It would have been easy to let it go—it was the kind of error that took a clerk four seconds to notice and four months to fix—but errors in the Impossible Ledger were not small things. A misplaced echo was an echo nobody could find when they came to buy it back, and an echo nobody could find was a scream loose in the dark with no name attached.

She corrected the card, initialed the margin in her tiny, deliberate hand—*J.P., audited, this date*—and felt the small, clean satisfaction that was the daily bread of her life. The ledger balanced. Therefore the world, in one narrow corner, balanced too.

"Still down here, Pemberton?"

The voice came down the stairwell ahead of its owner, preceded by the particular clatter of Supervisor Dreech's boots—heels struck flat, toes dragged, a man who had never once hurried in his life and considered urgency a form of bad manners. Juniper slid the drawer index under a blank folio with the ease of long practice. Not because there was anything wrong on her desk. Because there was never anything wrong on her desk, and she intended to keep it that way until she died old and unremarkable and full of secrets.

"Just finishing the Impossible Ledger, sir," she called up. "Drawer 88 needed a correction."

Dreech descended into the lamplight. He was a narrow man built like a closed umbrella, with a face that seemed perpetually to be waiting for someone else to finish talking. He carried his own lamp, though the cellar was lit, because Dreech trusted no light he did not personally control. He stopped at the mouth of her alcove and looked at her desk the way he looked at everything: as if it might be stealing from him.

"Auditing the whole ledger in one evening?"

"The quarterly review is Friday."

"The quarterly review is Friday," he agreed, "for clerks who do a quarter's work in a quarter. You do yours in advance and then sit here inventing corrections." His eyes flicked to the open ledger, to her neat ticks, to the bottle-green ink she used for annotations because black ink smudged and green did not. "You know what I told Master Hesse about you?"

"No, sir."

"I told him you're the only clerk in this building who treats the impossible like it's arithmetic." He sniffed. "He didn't think it was a compliment. I meant it as one."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me. Check Drawer 61 while you're down here. If the echo's actually in 88, I want the physical inventory matching by morning." He turned to go, then paused, umbrella-shaped silhouette against the stairwell glow. "And Pemberton. There's a delivery coming tonight. Confiscated lot out of the Marches—a book, among other things. Old. It goes to Cataloguing, not to you, so don't get ideas. You've enough to balance without chasing curiosities."

"Yes, sir," said Juniper, who had absolutely already begun wondering about the book, because she was a person made almost entirely of disciplined curiosity, like a kettle built to hold steam.

Dreech clattered back up the stairs. Juniper waited until his footsteps faded into the general noise of the Guildhall above—doors, voices, the bell that rang whenever something in the vault changed temperature—and then she let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

This was the shape of her days, and she had built the shape herself, stone by careful stone. Up before dawn. Down to the cellar with the first bell. Work that was exact enough to be praised and dull enough to be ignored. Lunch alone in the archive stacks, where even the rats had learned manners. Home by darkfall along the river road, past the tanneries, up the crooked stair to her room, where she ate bread and cheese and talked—to no one, officially—to the mice in the wainscoting about their opinions on her landlady.

Because that was the secret, the one thing, the single thread holding her entire invisible life together over a drop: Juniper Pemberton spoke Beast.

Not studied it. Not picked up phrases, the way a merchant picks up dockside curses. Spoke it, native and fluent, the way she spoke Vanelow and High Court both. She had been doing it since before she had words for it—since she was four years old and had asked a cart-horse why he was crying and the horse had answered, *because my knees hurt and nobody asks.* Her mother had gone white as flour and taught her, in the same breath, two rules: *never let them hear you, and never stop being useful.* A hidden thing with no value to anyone stays hidden. A hidden thing that is useful gets found.

So Juniper had become useful in every direction except the dangerous one. Useful with ledgers. Useful with numbers. Useful, quiet, punctual, forgettable. Six years in the cellar of the Hollow Guild, and not one soul suspected that the best auditor in the building could hold a conversation with the vault rats—and did, most evenings, because the rats saw everything that moved through those three locked doors, and Juniper liked knowing where all the wasps slept.

It was a good life. She reminded herself of this often, the way one reminds oneself a corset fits. It was a good life, carefully laced, and if she sometimes stood at her window at night listening to the city dogs trade news across the rooftops—*cold coming early, cold coming wrong, the north packs have gone quiet*—and felt less like a woman and more like a key that had never been shown its lock, well. That was the price of safety, and she paid it gladly.

Mostly gladly.

* * *

Drawer 61 held, in fact, a jar of preserved moonlight and a deed to a field that existed only on Tuesdays. The echo was in 88, exactly where the ledger said, whispering to itself in its glass like a seashell full of grief. Juniper fixed the drawer index instead, noted the discrepancy in triplicate, and was carrying the paperwork toward the filing room when the vault bell rang.

Everyone in the cellar looked up. The vault bell rang perhaps twice a month; it meant something had entered or left the deep storage, and it meant paperwork, and paperwork meant clerks, and clerks meant Juniper, because Juniper was always nearest and always willing and always, always careful.

Two porters came up the vault stairs between two wardens, carrying a long crate between them on padded poles. The crate was iron-banded and sealed with grey wax, and even from across the room Juniper could feel it—not hear it, nothing so vulgar as hearing—feel it the way you feel a storm front through a window. A pressure. A wrongness in the air's weight.

"That's the Marches lot?" she asked the senior porter, keeping her voice at the bored register she used for work talk.

"Confiscated off a dead peddler," he said. "Wardens want it inventoried tonight. There's a book in it, they say. Old one." He shifted his grip and grimaced. "Cold, too. Whole crate's cold as a grave in July. You want to log the seals, clerk? Wardens don't wait."

The wardens did not wait. They never did; the crown's men had the patience of weather. Juniper fetched the intake ledger, checked the grey wax against the confiscation order, noted the crate's chill (a full twelve degrees below the cellar's temper, which she recorded with a small frown, because objects did not stay cold for a fortnight in transit unless they were either enchanted or lying), and signed the transfer into Cataloguing's custody.

As the porters bore the crate away toward the upper floors, the cold of it brushed past her like the hem of a passing coat.

And the rats, who had been sleeping soundly in the wall behind the filing shelves all evening, woke all at once and went silent.

Juniper stood very still with her pen in her hand.

Rats woke for cats, for poison, for footsteps. Rats did not wake for a book in a crate—unless the book was not only a book, unless the cold was not only cold, unless something in that iron-banded box had made the oldest, smallest, most cautious citizens of the Guildhall decide, unanimously and instantly, to be somewhere else.

*What is it?* she asked silently, uselessly, toward the wall. She could not ask aloud; Dreech was somewhere on the stairs, and the wardens were still in the building, and six years of discipline sat on her tongue like a bit and bridle. But the silence from the wainscot was answer enough. Whatever had come up out of the vault tonight, the beasts wanted no part of it.

That frightened her more than any noise would have. In six years, the vault rats had never once refused to tell her something.

* * *

She finished the Impossible Ledger by the second bell. Entry 4,113: one debt of gratitude, purchased from a dying knight, resold twice, currently held by a baker in Lowmarsh who was reportedly finding it burdensome. Entry 4,114: seven years of a man's life, paid to the Guild as surety against a loan, stored in a brass locket, accruing interest. Juniper verified the locket's seal, noted that the years inside had gone from gold-colored to grey (they did that, the longer they sat; everyone pretended not to know what it meant), and closed the ledger with the soft, final sound of covers meeting.

A perfect evening's work. Balanced, corrected, initialed. Tomorrow the quarterly review would find nothing to find, and Dreech would grunt, and Master Hesse would nod at her across the counting floor the way he nodded at furniture he approved of, and her careful invisible life would continue another quarter, another year, another decade, safe as arithmetic.

She blew out her lamp.

Then she sat in the dark a moment, because the filing room was on her way out and the intake ledger for tonight's delivery was still open on its stand, and Juniper Pemberton had never in her life walked past an open ledger without reading it. It was not curiosity, she told herself. It was thoroughness. It was practically a professional obligation.

The entry was brief. *Confiscated lot, the Marches. One crate, contents assorted: tinware, dried herbs, false relics (3), and one book, hand-copied, age estimated 100+ years. Book withheld from general sale pending appraisal. Destination: Cataloguing, restricted shelf.*

*Restricted shelf.* Books went to the restricted shelf for two reasons: because they were valuable, or because they were trouble. Juniper had handled books from that shelf exactly once, during a flood two winters ago, and remembered the titles still—*a genealogy of a family that had never existed*, and *a cookbook whose recipes cooked the cook*. Restricted books were the Guild's way of saying *we do not know what this is, so we will keep it where we can watch it.*

Age estimated one hundred years. Cold as a grave. Enough to wake every rat in the walls.

She copied the entry number into her memory—she did not write such things down; writing things down was how careful people became careless people—and hung up her apron, and climbed the cellar stairs into the noise and lamplight of the Guildhall proper.

Above, the Hall was closing for the night. Clerks snuffed lamps. Porters stacked the day's crates. Through the tall windows, the sky over Vane Street had gone the color of a bruise, and the first stars were out, and the wind coming under the doors had an edge to it that did not belong to the season. Harvest month. The wind should have smelled of apples and smoke. It smelled instead of iron and far-off snow, and the city dogs had been saying for a week that winter was coming early, and Juniper had been telling herself they were exaggerating, because dogs loved drama the way clerks loved ink.

At the top of the stairs, she paused. Below her, the cellar lay dark and balanced and safe. Ahead of her, through the archway, she could see the Cataloguing room, where a single lamp still burned and where, on the restricted shelf, behind a door with a lock older than the Guild itself, a hundred-year-old book sat cooling the air around it like a stone pulled from a river of winter.

Tomorrow, Dreech had said. Don't get ideas, he'd said.

But tomorrow the quarterly review began, and after the quarterly review came the annual inventory, and after that the autumn auctions, and the book would sink into the restricted shelf the way things sank into the vault—tagged, catalogued, forgotten, sleeping like a wasp in winter. Tonight it was newly arrived. Tonight the seals were fresh and the wardens were gone and the Cataloguing clerk had stepped out to his supper, leaving his lamp burning and his keys, in flagrant violation of Guild policy, hanging on the hook by the door because the man had the memory of a moth.

Juniper stood at the top of the stairs for the length of ten heartbeats, weighing her careful invisible life in one hand and a cold iron-banded crate in the other.

Then she crossed the hall, took the keys off the hook, and let herself into Cataloguing—just to look, just to log the shelf location properly, just for thoroughness—and somewhere behind her ribs, soft as a page turning, the first line of a story she did not yet know she was in quietly wrote itself down:

*Entry 4,115. One book, confiscated. One clerk, curious. Balance: about to change.*

The restricted shelf was colder than the rest of the room. And on its spine, in faded letters a century old, the book bore a sigil she knew better than her own name.

The sigil of the Hollow Guild.
