## 2. A Date in an Old Book

The Confiscations Room smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of the river — that green rot-smell that crept up through the floorboards whenever the weather turned damp. Juniper Pemberton had worked in it for six years, and she had long ago stopped noticing the smell the way other people did. To her it was simply the smell of work: the smell of things the Guild had taken from people who should not have had them.

She sat at her desk beneath the one high window, a single candle burning at her elbow even though the evening light was still strong enough to read by. That was not superstition. It was policy. *A ledger-keeper works by her own light,* Hesse had told her when she was new, *because the Guild's business does not wait on the sun.* She had thought it a grand thing to say at seventeen. At twenty-three she understood it was just another way of saying the hours were terrible.

The stack before her was tonight's harvest: eleven items seized from the rooms of a hedge-witch in Tanner's Row who had made the mistake of selling a luck-charm that actually worked. The Guild did not care about charms, as a rule. Charms were small change, folk-magic, the kind of thing that kept the common people happy and harmless. But a charm that *worked* drew attention, and attention drawn to magic in Hollow City always, eventually, drew attention to the Guild itself — and the Guild could not abide being noticed by anyone it had not chosen to notice.

So: eleven items. Juniper squared the first of them before her and opened the intake ledger to a fresh page.

*Item one,* she wrote, in the small, precise hand that six years of practice had worn into her like a groove worn into a stair. *Copper amulet, verdigris at clasp. Effect: minor fortune-bending, radius approx. one room. Provenance: Tanner's Row, workshop above the chandlery. Condition: fair.*

She turned the amulet over twice, felt the little tick of its working against her palm like a beetle's heartbeat, and set it in the tray marked *Low Vault.*

*Item two.* A jar of preserved moths whose wing-patterns shifted to match whatever they rested upon. *Effect: concealment, passive. Note: specimens appear distressed; recommend transfer to Vivarium within the week.* The moths had all arranged themselves into the pattern of the ledger's ruled lines, which Juniper chose to find funny rather than sad. It helped, some nights, to find things funny.

*Item three.* A love-knot woven from two different women's hair, still warm. She did not write down that it was warm. Some things went into the ledger; some things went into the private catalogue she kept behind her eyes, the one no supervisor would ever audit, because it existed nowhere but in her.

It was while she was writing the disposition line for item three — *Hold for claimant inquiry; if unclaimed after ninety days, burn* — that she heard the footsteps in the corridor outside, and her whole body changed shape without her permission.

She knew those footsteps. Everyone in the Guildhall knew them. Master Corvane, Second Auditor, walked the way a man walks when he is listening to the building breathe. He paused outside the Confiscations Room door — she heard the pause, the half-second where his boots' rhythm broke — and then moved on, and Juniper let out a breath she had been holding since the second step.

He didn't know. He couldn't know. Six years, and no one knew.

That was the arithmetic she lived by, the sum she checked every morning the way other people checked their purse-strings: *no one knows.* Not Corvane with his listening walk. Not the other keepers, who thought her quiet because she was dull rather than because she spent her silences listening to the mice in the walls complain about the cats. Not even Hesse, who trusted her with the strange entries precisely because he believed her giftless — believed that a woman with no talent of her own would be too careful, too hungry for the Guild's regard, ever to let anything slip.

The mice in the wainscot said otherwise. The mice said, in their small dry voices, *the quiet one hears us, the quiet one hears us,* and Juniper had learned to smile at nothing when they said it, so that if anyone glanced up they'd think she was pleased with her figures.

She was careful. She was so careful it had become the architecture of her life, load-bearing and invisible, like the keystone of an arch you never see because everything else rests on it. She spoke to no beast inside the Guildhall. She spoke to no beast within earshot of any window. On the roof — the roof was different, the roof was hers, forty feet of slate and lead flashing where the pigeons came to gossip and the city's wolves sang from the far chimneys on cold nights — on the roof she let herself be what she was, briefly, the way a drowning man lets himself breathe.

But that was later. Now there were eight more items, and a candle to conserve, and a supervisor who expected the Tanner's Row intake catalogued by morning bell.

* * *

Item seven was the book.

She almost passed over it. It arrived looking like nothing — a slim volume bound in cracked grey leather, water-stained along the spine, its boards warped from some long-ago soaking. Hedge-witch stock, she assumed. Psalms, maybe, or a herbal, or one of those self-printed dream-books that sold for two coppers at market fairs and were worth exactly that.

She opened it to check the collation — pages present, pages missing, damage noted — and stopped.

The paper was wrong. Not hedge-witch paper, not cheap rag stock gone soft with damp. This was vellum. Fine vellum, the kind that cost more per sheet than the hedge-witch had probably earned in a year, and it had been *bound into* a cheap grey skin deliberately, the way a noblewoman might wear a servant's cloak over her silk to walk through a bad part of town.

Juniper looked at the door. Looked at the candle. Then she did something she almost never did during working hours: she locked it.

The bolt slid home with a sound like a held breath released.

The text inside was handwritten, brown-black ink gone the color of dried blood, in a hand that looped and slanted with an impatience barely held in check. Not a psalm. Not a herbal. The opening page bore a title in letters larger than the rest:

*THE ACCOUNT OF THE LONG WINTER, AS WITNESSED AND SET DOWN BY ONE WHO WILL NOT LIVE TO SEE IT END.*

Below that, smaller: *Being a true prophecy, though prophecy is never true the way men want it to be.*

"Well," Juniper said softly, to the empty room. "Aren't you dramatic."

The book did not answer. Books rarely did, in her experience, though she had once catalogued a mirror that hummed, and she kept her expectations accordingly flexible.

She began, professionally, to catalogue. *Item seven: bound manuscript, vellum leaves (approx. 60) sewn into concealing cover, grey calf, water-damaged. Text: prophetic verse and commentary, single hand, undated.* Her pen scratched. The candle leaned. Somewhere below, the Guildhall settled into its evening noises — doors, distant voices, the clank of the vault gate — and above, on the roof, a pigeon muttered in its sleep about hawks.

She read as she catalogued, because reading was faster than not reading, and because the voice of the book had gotten its hooks into her despite herself. It was angry, this dead scribe. Angry and frightened and very precise. The verses came in numbered sections, each headed with a date in the old reckoning, and each date was annotated in the margin in the same impatient hand — corrections, glosses, occasional furious underlining.

*In the hundredth year of the count, the frost comes early,* read section twelve. *Not the winter that follows autumn, but the winter that ends all following. The Long Winter, which has no spring but the one bought in blood.*

And beside it, in the margin, in ink slightly fresher than the main text: *Checked against grain ledgers. Holds.*

Juniper frowned. Someone had annotated this book across decades — she could see it now, the layers of marginalia, ink fading and darkening in strata like river mud. A century of readers, or one reader across a century, arguing with a prophecy in the margins the way scholars argue with scripture.

She turned pages. Section thirteen. Fourteen. Frost imagery, crown imagery — *a king crowned in cold, who smiles with borrowed warmth* — the usual apocalyptic furniture. Prophecies were all the same, really. She'd catalogued dozens. They promised ruin, named no names, gave no dates that mattered, and gathered dust in the Low Vault until some collector paid an embarrassing sum for them.

Then she reached section fifteen, and the floor dropped out of the ordinary evening.

The date at the head of the section was written in numerals. Not the old reckoning, not regnal years — plain numerals, the merchant's shorthand, the kind used in ledgers. And Juniper Pemberton, who had written ten thousand dates in ledgers, who knew dates the way musicians know notes, read it once and felt the room tilt.

It was today's date.

Not approximately. Not in the loose way prophecies are dated, *in the days of such-and-such*, open enough to fit any century. Numerals. Day, month, year. Tonight. The night she was sitting in the Confiscations Room with a bolted door and a guttering candle, cataloguing a smuggled book.

Her first thought, absurdly, was that she had misread it. Her second was that the scribe had miswritten it. Her third — arriving cold, sliding down her spine like meltwater — was that neither of those explanations explained the circle.

Because the date was circled. Circled hard, the pen pressed deep enough to emboss the vellum, and beside the circle, in that same layered margin-hand, someone had drawn a sigil.

Juniper knew the sigil. She drew it forty times a day. It was stamped on every intake ledger, branded into every vault door, stitched into the collar of her coat: the interlocked ring and key of the Hollow Guild.

She sat very still. The candle flame stood straight up, unnaturally still, as if it too were waiting.

"Someone's joke," she said aloud, because saying it aloud made it smaller. "Some clerk a hundred years ago with a sense of humor."

But clerks did not buy vellum to make jokes. Clerks did not annotate prophecies in strata of ink across decades. And clerks certainly did not — her eyes dropped back to the margin, and her stomach followed them down — clerks did not write, in a fine, controlled hand directly beneath the circled date, four words that had nothing to do with kings or winters:

*The keeper will find it.*

Juniper looked at the word *keeper*. She looked at the guild sigil beside it. She looked at the bolted door, at the eleven confiscated items, at the tray marked *Low Vault*, at her own neat handwriting drying on the intake ledger — *item seven: bound manuscript* — and she understood, with the particular vertigo of a woman standing on a floor that has quietly become a trapdoor, that she was not cataloguing this book.

This book was cataloguing *her*.

* * *

She did the sensible thing. She did what six years of training demanded: she finished the entry, closed the ledger, and reached for the tray tag to mark item seven for the Low Vault.

Her hand stopped above the tag.

The Low Vault was where impossible things went to be forgotten. She had filed bottled echoes there, and debts paid in years, and a letter that rearranged itself whenever anyone tried to read it aloud. Once something entered the Low Vault, retrieving it required three signatures and a reason, and *a prophecy circled my name* was not a reason; it was a confession. Corvane would have it off her desk by morning bell. By noon it would be a number in a catalogue. By winter it would be dust.

By winter.

The phrase snagged in her mind like a fishhook. *The Long Winter, which has no spring but the one bought in blood.* Section fifteen. She hadn't even read the verse yet — only the heading, only the date, only the circle. Her hands, moving before her caution could stop them, opened the book again.

Section fifteen ran nine lines. She read them three times, and each time they got worse.

*On the night the frost comes early, the hollow king will sit his throne and smile, and the smiling is the sign. Beneath the crown there is a man, and beneath the man there is nothing, and the nothing is hungry. Let the ledger-keeper mark this well: what the crown eats, it keeps. What it cannot eat, it fears. And it cannot eat the beasts' tongue, nor the hand that keeps accounts of impossible things.*

The margin beside the last line held a single annotation, in the oldest ink, the stratum nearest the vellum itself — the scribe's own hand, not a later reader's:

*I have written this for whoever needs it. I am sorry it must be someone.*

Juniper became aware, distantly, that she was shivering. The Confiscations Room was always cold — north wall, stone foundations, the river's breath through the floorboards — but this was a different cold, an interior one, the cold of a certainty arriving ahead of its evidence.

Tonight. *Tonight.* The frost coming early. Harvest month, and the evenings already carrying a bite that felt less like autumn than like a promise being kept by something patient.

She thought of the wolves. She had not been to the roof in four nights — inventory season, audits, no time — and the wolves sang on cold nights, and wolves knew weather the way ledgers knew numbers. Wolves would know if the winter was coming early. Wolves would know if it was coming *wrong*, if wrongness was a thing winter could do.

They were her truth-tellers. They had been since she was nine years old and a limping street-dog had told her where to find her mother's lost brooch, and she had answered him — *thank you* — out loud, in words, and the dog had looked at her the way no dog had ever looked at her, and her life had split into the part before and the part after, and the part after had been a secret ever since.

No one knew. No one could know. Beast-speech was not merely forbidden; it was *unthinkable*, the one gift the Guild's charter named before all others, the gift that had gotten the last speaker of it burned in the square before Juniper's grandmother was born. If Corvane knew. If *anyone* knew —

But the book knew. Somehow, impossibly, a dead scribe a century gone had known, had written *the beasts' tongue* into a prophecy and circled a date and left a message like a hand reaching down through a hundred years of dust to close around her wrist.

*The keeper will find it.*

"I found it," she whispered to the book. "Now what?"

The candle guttered, steadied. Down in the walls, the mice had gone silent — all of them at once, everywhere, the way animals go silent before an earthquake or a storm. Even the pigeon on the roof had stopped muttering.

Juniper looked at the window. Beyond the glass, the evening had deepened while she read, blue going to black over the rooftops, and the air beyond the pane had a brightness to it, a hard clean edge, that had no business existing in harvest month.

Frost, she thought. That's frost-light. That's the light the air gets when—

She stood up so fast her chair skidded. She caught the book, caught the ledger, caught herself, and stood in the middle of the locked room holding a century-old prophecy against her chest like a shield, listening to the silence of the mice.

There was one place in this building where she could ask the sky a question and get an honest answer. One place, forty feet up, where the slate was hers and the wind carried wolf-song from the northern chimneys on clear nights.

Juniper snuffed the candle, tucked the book inside her coat — a violation of about nine separate guild regulations, each of which she catalogued and dismissed in the space of a heartbeat — and unlocked the door.

The corridor was empty. The Guildhall murmured around her, doors and distant voices, ordinary evening sounds. Everything was exactly as it had been an hour ago.

Except that somewhere above her, past the attic and the slates, the first snow of the Long Winter was deciding whether to fall — and the only creatures in Hollow City who might tell her why were waiting on the roof, and they did not yet know she was coming.

She took the stairs two at a time.
