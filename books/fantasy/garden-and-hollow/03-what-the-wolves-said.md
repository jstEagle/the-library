## 3. What the Wolves Said

The bell over the guild's counting room rang the ninth hour, and Juniper Pemberton did what she always did when the day finally let go of her: she climbed.

Not home. Home was a rented room above a chandler's shop on Tallow Lane, where the smell of rendered fat seeped into everything she owned and the landlady asked too many friendly questions. No—when the ledgers were balanced and the lamps were trimmed and Supervisor Cray had given his nightly little speech about *accuracy is devotion*, Juniper took the back stair behind the map room, the one that creaked in a rhythm she'd memorized, and she went up to the roof of the Hollow Guildhall like a sinner climbing to confession.

The roof was slate and moss and forty years of pigeons' grievances. It overlooked the Guild Quarter on one side and, beyond the tanners' smoke, the gray sprawl of Coldharbor where the city gave up pretending to be pretty. From up here the whole city looked like a ledger itself—streets ruled straight as columns, rooftops entered in neat rows, the river a line of ink someone had drawn and then regretted.

Juniper pulled her coat tight, checked the roofline out of habit, and whistled.

Not a tune. Not anything a person would recognize as a whistle at all. It was three notes with a shape to them, a rising fall and a long low hold, and if you had ears that were not human ears—if your ears were built for wind and distance and the small desperate sounds of prey—you would have heard it for what it was: *Juniper. Roof. Come.*

She waited, arms folded on the parapet, breath fogging in air that should not have been cold enough to fog it. Harvest month. The apples were still on the trees in the temple orchards. And yet her breath hung white in the lamplight, and the slate under her palms held a chill that felt less like evening and more like intention.

They came out of the dark the way they always did, which was to say all at once, as though the shadows had simply decided to grow legs.

Three wolves on the tanner's warehouse across the lane. Then two more flowing along the base of the guild wall, keeping to the deep seams of shadow the way other people kept to paving stones. The last one came straight down the middle of Copperlane Street, unhurried, tail level, ignoring the late carter who froze mid-step and then convinced himself he'd seen a large dog. People were very good at convincing themselves. It was one of the things Juniper liked least about them.

*You called,* said the big male, the one the others deferred to without ever seeming to. His voice in her head was like gravel turning under a heavy cart—slow, patient, worn smooth by repetition. The city called him Old Grey, though no wolf had named him; names were a human vanity and he tolerated his only because Juniper needed something to write in her head.

*I called,* Juniper answered.

Speaking with beasts was not words, exactly. That was the first thing anyone with the gift learned, if they were unlucky enough to have it and lucky enough to survive learning it: there was no language, not really. There was meaning, passed mind to mind like a coal passed hand to hand, and it arrived already shaped by the giver. Wolf-meaning came scented and layered, tangled with position and season and hunger. Juniper had spent eleven years teaching herself to wrap her own meanings tightly enough that they didn't unravel into scent and instinct on the way out. She still wasn't sure she succeeded. When she spoke to wolves she sometimes caught herself thinking in weather.

*You are troubled,* Old Grey said. It wasn't a question. Meaning doesn't lie, which was precisely why she came here. Up on this roof she could not perform calm. Her worry arrived as worry, smelling of copper and cold stone, and there was no ledger neat enough to hide it.

*There's a book,* she began, and stopped, because how did you hand someone a coal that size? *A book that says tonight begins a winter that lasts a hundred years.*

Silence, but not empty silence. The five wolves below the wall sat down in a row, which was their version of leaning in. On the warehouse roof, a young female—Fern-toes, because Juniper refused to think of any living creature as *the second-year female*—tilted her head so far sideways it looked painful.

*We know,* said Old Grey.

The word landed in Juniper's chest like a dropped coin. *You know.*

*The north packs sent word before the frost left the high passes.* He rose, shook once, and crossed the warehouse roof to its edge, close enough now that she could see the frost in his ruff—not frost that had landed on him, she realized, but frost that had come *from* him, breath-crystals that refused to melt. *Word travels on four legs faster than any bird. The high country went quiet in the way it goes quiet before a hard season. Prey moving wrong. Rivers icing from the banks inward instead of the middle out. And the deer—*

He paused, and the pause had a texture to it, a reluctance she'd never heard from him before. Old Grey was old the way mountains were old; nothing surprised him and almost nothing grieved him. Grief was a luxury of short-lived things.

*Tell me,* Juniper said.

*The deer came down to the valley two months early. All of them. Every herd at once, walking south in lines so straight the hunters thought men had moved them.* His meaning shivered. *Deer do not walk in lines, Juniper-of-the-ledgers. Deer scatter. Deer are fear wearing legs. These walked like soldiers. Like they were being* counted.

Juniper's arms prickled under her coat. She thought of the prophecy book upstairs in the cataloguing room, tonight's date circled in faded iron-gall ink a hundred years ago, the guild's sigil pressed beside it like a wax seal on a letter that had taken a century to arrive. *Counted,* she repeated. *By what?*

*That,* said Old Grey, *is why we came.*

* * *

They told it to her in pieces, the way wolves told everything—in overlapping fragments that Juniper had to assemble, and the assembling itself told her things. Fern-toes spoke first, young and quick, her meanings bright-edged with fear she wasn't bothering to hide:

*Rats. The rats left the granaries. Whole colonies, moving east toward the river, carrying their pups in their mouths in broad daylight. Rats do not leave grain, Juniper. Rats would rather starve beside grain than leave it.*

Then the lean female with the torn ear—Juniper thought of her as Stitch—who ranged the docks:

*The harbor ice. I put my paw on it last night. Salt water, harvest month, and it bore my weight. The fishermen saw me do it and one of them wept. Not from fear. He said his grandfather told stories of a winter when the sea froze to the horizon and ships became houses and half the city ate the other half.*

And the last one, the young male who was barely more than a yearling and whom Juniper privately called Ember for the color that flashed in his eyes when lamplight caught them—Ember brought the piece that made her blood go strange in her veins:

*I followed the smell. The wrongness has a smell. Under the frost-smell there is something else, something old, like the inside of a mountain, like stone that has never seen sun. It comes off the palace hill at night. It flows downhill through the streets like water finding its level, and where it pools, the street dogs will not sleep. They stand in the doorways of the poor-houses and howl at nothing until the watch drives them off.*

*The palace,* Juniper said slowly. *The king's hill.*

*The crown's hill,* Old Grey corrected, and the correction had teeth in it.

Juniper looked at him. Below, the city went about its evening—a knife-grinder calling his last customers, a mother hauling a child indoors, lamplighters working their poles along Copperlane with the mechanical serenity of men who had done ten thousand nights and expected ten thousand more. None of them knew. None of them could know. The city's truth-tellers were standing on a rooftop talking to a clerk.

*What do you mean, the crown's hill?*

Old Grey was silent long enough that Fern-toes glanced at him, and even the yearling went still. When he finally spoke, his meaning came slow and deliberate, each part placed like a paw on uncertain ice.

*My grandmother's grandmother was alive the last time the cold came down from the palace hill. She was born in the year the Long Winter was supposed to begin—the year your book speaks of, if books can be believed—and she lived forty winters after, and she told the story until her jaws gave out. A young king was crowned that spring. A crown no one had seen before, forged in secret, paid for with a treasury that emptied overnight. And within a season the winters began to lengthen. Not much. A week here. A fortnight there. Enough that the granaries thinned and the trade roads closed and the packs in the north grew hungry and mean.* His eyes caught the lamplight, flat and gold. *Enough that people noticed, and then enough that people stopped noticing, because noticing changed nothing, and forgetting is easier than grieving.*

*But the winter broke,* Juniper said. *It's in every history. The Long Winter never came. The century turned and the springs kept—*

*The winter did not break.* Old Grey's voice dropped into something she had no better word for than *old*. *The winter was* fed.

The word sat between them. Down in the street, the knife-grinder laughed at something, a bright human sound that seemed to belong to a different world.

*Fed,* Juniper repeated. *Fed what? Fed how?*

*That my grandmother's grandmother did not know. She knew only that the hard seasons came and went in a pattern too regular for weather, and that the pattern centered on the hill, and that the wolves of that generation made a law: the hill is not ours. The hill is not anyone's. Whatever sits on the hill, we do not hunt near it, we do not den near it, we do not answer any call that comes from it, no matter whose voice it wears.* He stepped closer to the roof's edge, and the frost in his ruff glittered. *Your guild trades in impossible things, Juniper-of-the-ledgers. We have smelled what goes up that hill in sealed wagons. Cold does not get carried in wagons unless someone is feeding something.*

Juniper thought of the ledgers. Of course she thought of the ledgers—it was the only way she knew how to think. Entries she'd audited and approved without a second look. *Palace requisitions, seasonal, category unlisted.* Shipments logged as *salt*, as *quicksilver*, as *stone, dressed*. One entry, years ago, that had bothered her for a week before she'd buried it under work: a payment received from the palace treasury for goods described only as *winter, per measure*. She'd assumed a transcription error. Errors happened. Errors were comfortable. An error meant the world was merely sloppy rather than arranged.

*You're telling me the guild has been shipping cold to the palace.*

*I am telling you what we have smelled,* Old Grey said. *You keep the numbers. You tell me what the numbers say.*

* * *

She told them about the book. It came out of her in a rush once she started, the way water finds a crack—all of it, the cataloguing shift, the confiscated prophecy, the date circled a century ago, the guild sigil stamped beside it like a signature on a contract. The wolves listened the way wolves listened, totally, without interrupting, their attention a physical pressure against her mind like a hand laid flat on her sternum.

When she finished, Fern-toes said, *Circled a hundred years ago. Before you were born. Before your mother was born.* Her young voice was hushed. *Before my grandmother's grandmother was born. The book knew your night before there was a you to have one.*

*Books don't know things,* Juniper said, and heard how thin it sounded even inside her own skull.

*No,* Old Grey agreed mildly. *Writers do.*

Below them, the city bells rang the tenth hour, and with the bells came a sound that stopped Juniper's breath: howling. Distant, ragged, from the direction of the river and the poor-house districts—the street dogs, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, singing at the dark in voices cracked with something that was not moonlight and not loneliness. The lamplighters paused. A window banged shut somewhere. The sound rolled across the rooftops and broke apart against the hills.

The wolves on the wall below had risen, every hackle up, facing the sound.

*They're not howling at nothing,* Stitch said quietly. *Listen. It has a shape.*

Juniper listened. She was not a dog; the shape was mostly lost on her. But underneath the raw animal sound she caught something rhythmic, something almost structured, and the structure of it raised the hair on her arms, because it wasn't random and it wasn't a warning and it wasn't grief.

It sounded like counting.

*They hear it too,* Ember said. His yearling bravado had burned away entirely; what was left was just a young animal trying not to shake. *The thing on the hill. It's louder every night. My ears ring when I face the palace now. Like standing near a struck bell that never finishes ringing.*

*Then we stop circling it,* Juniper said, and was surprised by the hardness in her own voice. She was a clerk. Her weapons were ink and patience and an excellent memory. But something had been building in her since she'd opened that book and seen tonight's date waiting for her like a trap sprung a century in advance, and it came out now with her spine straightening. *Something is being fed on that hill. Something is counting deer and freezing harbors and keeping a hundred-year appointment, and everyone who could look into it is either blind or bought. So I'll look. I keep the ledgers. If cold has been moving through my guild in wagons, there are entries. Entries have hands behind them. Hands have names.*

The wolves regarded her. In the beast-way, regard was not subtle; she felt their assessment move over her like weather, weighing her the way they'd weigh a stranger appearing at the edge of territory.

*You are one small warm thing,* Old Grey said at last, without cruelty. It was simply arithmetic, and arithmetic was a language she spoke fluently. *The thing on the hill is old and cold and it counts kingdoms the way you count coins.*

*Then it and I already have something in common,* Juniper said. *We both keep accounts.*

For a moment—she would remember this moment later, though she couldn't have known she'd need to—one of the wolves made a sound that was not quite a bark and not quite speech, and the others looked at him, and she understood with a jolt that he was laughing. It was Ember, the yearling.

*She'll do,* he said. *The north packs sent us to find whether the soft southern city had any teeth left. I'm beginning to think they do. They're just filed away in a ledger somewhere.*

*One more thing,* Old Grey said, and the humor drained out of the air between them as quickly as it had come. *The law of my grandmother's grandmother stands. Whatever calls from the hill, we do not answer. But you—you are not of the hill. If you call, we come. Remember that you have that. Whatever waits for you in that book of yours, whatever the hundred years have planned—* his meaning brushed hers, gentle as a muzzle pushed under a hand— *you do not stand alone on your roof.*

Juniper's throat tightened. This was the trouble with truth-tellers: they told true things, and some of those things got past all her careful walls and lodged somewhere soft.

*Thank you,* she managed.

*Go down,* Old Grey said. *Go back to your numbers. And Juniper-of-the-ledgers—check the ones from the palace twice.*

* * *

She watched them go, five shadows folding themselves back into the dark of Copperlane, unhurried, invisible, gone. The city swallowed them without a ripple. Somewhere a drunk sang. The knife-grinder had packed up. The world resumed pretending.

Juniper stayed on the parapet a while longer, fingers white on cold stone, doing what she did best: ordering things. *Winter fed, not broken. Palace hill. Sealed wagons. A crown forged in secret. Deer counted. Dogs counting back.* Each fact went into its column. The columns did not balance. The columns were not supposed to balance; that was how she knew they were true. Falsehoods always balanced beautifully—that was practically their defining feature.

Her breath hung white in the lamplight. Harvest month, and the slate beneath her hands was rimed with frost that had not fallen from any sky.

She was cold, and afraid, and certain—down in the place beneath the ledgers, beneath the careful invisible life she'd built one unremarkable entry at a time—that the ordinary evening she'd been protecting for eleven years had ended tonight, on this roof, with the wolves.

She was halfway down the back stair, one hand on the rail, when she heard footsteps in the corridor below. Heavy ones. Deliberate ones. And then Guildmaster Hesse's voice, pitched low for the sleeping guildhall, saying words that stopped her foot on the stair:

"Miss Pemberton. I know you're there. And I know exactly what you've been doing on my roof."
