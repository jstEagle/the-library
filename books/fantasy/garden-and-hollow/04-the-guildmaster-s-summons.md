## 4. The Guildmaster's Summons

The lamp in Guildmaster Hesse's office had been burning since the second bell after midnight, and the oil was going the way of everything else in the Hollow Guild—thin, stretched, asked to do more than it should.

Corvus Hesse sat with three ledgers open before him and a fourth on his lap, and he was not reading any of them. He was listening.

Below his floor, through the old bones of the guildhall, came the sound of Juniper Pemberton working late. He knew her sounds the way a sailor knows weather. The scratch of her pen—quick, even, unhurried. The soft thump of a ledger being squared against the desk edge. Once, faintly, a hum: some tuneless thing she made when she thought no one could hear, half a melody and half a habit. And then—the sound that mattered, the sound that had kept him awake three nights running now—a pause. A long one. The particular silence of a girl listening to something that was not there.

Or rather, that was there, and was not a person.

Hesse poured himself two fingers of plum brandy and did not drink it. He had learned, over forty years in the impossible trade, that courage was mostly a matter of timing, and timing was mostly a matter of waiting until the brandy was no longer needed.

He had known about the girl's gift for eleven years. He had known since she was fourteen, since the morning a delivery horse threw its shoe on Copperlane and screamed at her—not the scream of an animal, but the scream of a creature trying to say *something*, and she had said back, clear as church bells across a frozen yard, *I know, I know, it's your off foot, hold still*—and Hesse had been standing behind the gate with a bill of lading in his hand and his whole understanding of the world quietly rearranging itself around a fourteen-year-old clerk who talked to horses like they were overdue accounts.

He had done nothing. That was the first mercy he had ever paid her, though she would never know it. In the Hollow Guild, knowledge was currency, and currency unspent gathered interest. He had watched her instead. Watched her build herself into the ledgers the way a crab builds into sand—carefully, invisibly, every grain placed to look like every other grain. She arrived early. She left late. She never took credit, never made waves, never once gave the assessors a reason to look twice at her. She had made herself the most boring woman in the building, and Hesse, who had spent his life among people who trafficked in bottled echoes and debts paid in years, considered this the single most impressive act of magic he had ever witnessed.

Because it *was* magic. Beast-speech. The old gift, the deep gift, the one the Concord had burned people for before the Concord got respectable and started burning ledgers instead. There were maybe four living souls in Vanehollow who could do what she did, and two of them were wolves, and wolves didn't file paperwork.

And now the third thing had happened. The thing he'd been dreading and expecting in equal measure, the way you dread and expect a tooth to finally crack.

Tonight's date, circled in a book a century old.

* * *

The prophecy had come in at dusk with the confiscation carts, one of nine crates seized from the house of a dead collector in the Weft District whose heirs had gotten ambitious about taxes. Ordinary work. Catalogue, appraise, shelve or sell. Juniper had drawn the crate the way she drew everything—by being early, by being reliable, by making herself so seamlessly part of the machinery that assigning her the strange items felt like letting water find its level.

Hesse had read her intake slip within the hour, because he read all her slips, because he had spent eleven years reading them, looking for the day one of them trembled.

This one trembled.

*Item 4471: bound manuscript, untitled, provenance unknown, c. 100 yrs. Marginalia throughout in unknown hand. Note: internal dating references present. One date circled.*

One date circled. Tonight's date. And beside it, pressed into the margin in wax gone amber with age, the sigil of the Hollow Guild—an open ledger beneath a crescent moon, the mark that hung over his own door, stamped by a hand dead a hundred years onto a page that named tonight.

Hesse had sent for the book and sat with it for two hours while the lamp burned down. He was not a scholar. He was a tradesman, and proud of it; the guild dealt in impossible things precisely so that ordinary people wouldn't have to believe in them. But he knew what a century-old appointment looked like. He'd seen contracts like it. Somebody, a hundred years ago, had known the Hollow Guild would be holding this book on this night, and had wanted whoever held it to know they were expected.

Expected by whom? Expected *for* what?

There was only one person in the building he trusted to answer that question, and trusting her meant using the very secret that could destroy her, which was, he reflected grimly, the most guild-like thing he had ever done.

A knock. Soft, precise. "Guildmaster? You rang for the night tea."

"Leave it, Tamsin. And send me Clerk Pemberton."

A pause at the door. "At this hour, sir?"

"Especially at this hour." He softened it with the ghost of a smile he didn't feel. "Tell her to bring her reading glasses and her patience."

* * *

She came up the stairs the way she always did—quietly, as if the stairs might be sleeping—and knocked at his door with two knuckles, gentle as rain.

"Enter."

Juniper Pemberton closed the door behind her and stood with her hands folded, waiting. That was the thing about her that always caught him slightly sideways: everyone else in the guild came into his office already talking, already justifying, already spending. She simply arrived, complete, and waited to see what was wanted. It was a servant's posture worn like armor. He wondered sometimes whether she knew she wore it.

"You wanted me, Guildmaster."

"I wanted," Hesse said, "to ask you about item 4471."

Something crossed her face—fast, controlled, but he had spent eleven years learning to read the small weather of Juniper Pemberton, and he saw it: not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone who has been walking on ice and hears it begin to sing.

"The confiscated manuscript," she said evenly. "It's catalogued. Provenance unknown, marginalia in an unidentified hand, condition fair, binding original. I flagged the circled date per protocol."

"Per protocol." He turned the book around on his desk so it faced her. "Sit down, Juniper."

She sat. Back straight, hands in her lap. Waiting again.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Hesse said, "and then I'm going to ask you to do something that may be the death of both of us, and I want you to understand, before either of those things, that I have known what you are for eleven years."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the guildhall settled its old bones, and neither of them moved.

"The morning of the Copperlane shoe," he said. "Fourteenth of Thawmonth, eleven years ago. You told a lame horse about its off foot. You told it kindly. And I stood behind the gate and heard every word, and I have said nothing from that day to this."

Her face did something extraordinary. It went through fear—real fear, the animal kind, the kind that makes pupils widen and breath go shallow—and then it went through calculation, fast as a card sharp counting a deck, and then it landed somewhere he hadn't expected: relief. A terrible, exhausted relief, like a runner seeing the finish line and the cliff edge in the same view.

"How long," she said quietly, "have you been waiting to tell me that?"

"Eleven years."

"Why?"

"Because the moment I told you, you'd stop being careful." He leaned forward. "You are the most careful person I have ever employed, Juniper. You've built a life out of being overlooked, and it is a masterpiece, and I have protected it by leaving it alone. But tonight—" he tapped the manuscript—"tonight the overlooking stopped being possible. So we're past the point where silence serves either of us."

"What do you want from me?" No tremor in it. He admired that more than he could say.

"I want you to read this book properly. Not catalogue it. *Read* it. Every page, every margin, every smudge. And I want you to tell me why a hundred-year-old prophecy has my guild's seal pressed next to today's date, and what it expects us to do about it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll assign it to Assessor Crane, who will have it copied, translated, and reported to the Concord within the month, along with whatever else he finds interesting in this building." Hesse let that sit. He hated wielding Crane like a stick; the man was a decent enough assessor and a loyal informer, which in the guild amounted to the same virtue. "I don't want that. I want this handled quietly, by someone I trust, before anyone else learns there's anything to handle."

"You trust me." Her voice was flat, but underneath it ran something else, something wounded and sharp. "You've known my whole life could end on your word, for eleven years, and you trusted me to keep working under your roof."

"Yes."

"That's not trust, Guildmaster. That's leverage."

"It's both," Hesse said, because she deserved the truth even if the truth was ugly. "In this trade we call it collateral. I call it—" he searched, and found the honest word, and used it—"shameful. But necessary. Because there is a date in this book, Juniper, and the date is *today*, and I am an old man who deals in impossible things, and I know better than most what happens to people who wait for impossible things to explain themselves."

She looked down at the manuscript. Her fingers hovered over the cover without touching it, the way you hover over a door you can hear breathing behind.

"There's more," she said. Not a question. She'd seen something. Of course she'd seen something; she'd had the book in her hands all evening.

"Tell me."

"The marginalia isn't random. It's a voice. Someone wrote in this book for years—decades, maybe—commenting, correcting, arguing with the text." Her eyes lifted to his. "And Guildmaster, some of the annotations are fresher than others. The last ones aren't a hundred years old. They're not even fifty. Someone has been writing in this book recently. Possibly—" and here her voice did finally waver, just barely, the ice singing underfoot—"*possibly still is.*"

Hesse sat back. The brandy sat untouched, and he understood suddenly that he would not be needing it after all, because courage had arrived by another road entirely: hers.

"Then you'll want to start tonight," he said.

* * *

She worked in his office, at the long table under the good lamp, because he refused to let the book out of his sight and refused, equally, to let her out of his. He told himself it was prudence. He knew it was also that he could not bear, on this of all nights, to watch her walk away alone into whatever waited between those pages.

For two hours the only sounds were her pen, the lamp, and the turning of old paper. Hesse pretended to review quarter accounts. He watched her instead—watched her lips move faintly over the archaic script, watched her frown, watched her stop.

Stop the way she'd stopped earlier. Listening.

"Guildmaster." Her voice had changed. Lower. Careful. "The annotator. Sorrel, she signs herself. She says—" Juniper's hand flattened on the page, holding it still as if it might bolt. "She says she wrote this prophecy. All of it. And she says the circled date isn't a warning."

"No?"

"It's an instruction." Juniper looked up at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were very bright and very afraid and absolutely steady. "It says: *tell the keeper of the ledgers. Tell her the winter has learned to wear a king.*"

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

Hesse found that his hands, flat on the desk before him, had gone white at the knuckles. He was sixty-one years old. He had sold a bottled echo to a grieving widow and slept fine. He had appraised a debt of seventeen years and taken his commission and slept fine. He had spent his whole life believing that impossible things were safest inside ledgers, priced and shelved and owned.

And now an invisible hand in a hundred-year-old book had reached across a century and addressed his quietest clerk by name—or near enough—and spoken of winter wearing a king, and outside his window the first week of Harvest Month had turned cold enough that frost was forming on the glass, and he could not remember frost forming on this window in Harvest Month in forty years of sitting behind it.

"Juniper," he said slowly, "what else does she say?"

But the girl wasn't listening to him anymore. She was leaning toward the book, toward the margins, her head tilted at that angle—that listening angle he knew too well, the angle she bent toward horses and, apparently, toward ghosts—and her lips parted, and she whispered:

"She says she's been waiting a hundred years for someone who could hear her." A breath. "She says... she says she's sorry. About what comes next."

Frost crept across the windowpane in long silver feathers, and the lamp flame stood perfectly still, and Guildmaster Corvus Hesse, who had traded in impossible things for four decades without once believing in them, reached at last for the brandy—and found his hand would not obey him.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a wolf began to howl. Then a second. Then a dozen, a chorus rising from the dark edges of the city where wolves had no business gathering, singing something long and low and urgent into the freezing night.

Juniper Pemberton rose from her chair without being asked, without being dismissed, her eyes still fixed on the open page.

"Guildmaster," she said, "I think I need to speak with the wolves."
