## 5. Sorrel, Who Waited a Century

The prophecy book did not want to be read.

Juniper had catalogued four hundred and eleven confiscated volumes in her three years at the Hollow Guild, and she knew the way books resisted. Some sulked—pages sticking together like children refusing to share. Some lied, their indexes pointing to chapters that weren't there. One grimoire had screamed for a week until Hesse drowned it in salt water. But this one, *The Wintering of the Vale*, bound in gray leather gone soft as old skin, resisted the way a person resists: politely, persistently, and with an intelligence behind it.

She had been trying to open it properly for two hours.

It was past midnight in the Guildhall's cataloguing room, the hour Juniper liked best because no one else claimed it. A single lamp burned low on her desk. Rain ticked against the high windows. Around her, the shelves of the Restricted Annex rose into shadow, holding everything the guild had seized from smugglers, hoarders, and fools over two centuries: mirrors that showed tomorrow's weather, keys to doors that no longer existed, a jar of someone's last words still faintly muttering.

Her ledger lay open beside the book, her pen poised. Entry 412: provenance unknown; recovered from the estate of the late Magister Corvane; condition poor; contents—

And there she kept stopping, because she could not honestly write *contents* when she hadn't been able to finish reading them.

She turned back to the beginning. The paper was the color of old milk, dense as felt, and the ink had gone brown at the edges of every letter, as though each word were rusting in place. The hand was small and slanted, urgent without being sloppy—a working hand, a hand used to writing fast by bad light.

*In the year the frost came early,* it began, *I set down what I saw, so that those who come after will not be able to say they were not told.*

Juniper read on. The frost. The king who smiled wrong. The crown that ate. She had read these lines six times now, and each time the same cold walked up her spine and settled between her shoulder blades, because tonight's date sat circled on the page like a target drawn a hundred years before she was born, with the Hollow Guild's sigil stamped beside it in wax gone black as a closed eye.

Tonight. Which was nearly over. Which was passing, minute by minute, into nothing—or into whatever the century-old ink said it would become.

"Contents," she murmured, and wrote: *prophecy, apocalyptic; authorship uncertain.* Her pen hesitated. *Circled date corresponds to present night.*

There. It was done. Whatever happened before dawn, the guild would have its record, and Juniper Pemberton would have done her small, careful, invisible job. That was the whole architecture of her life: ledgers balanced, gift hidden, head down, heart locked. She reached to close the book.

The margin moved.

Not the page—the *margin*. In the narrow white space beside the line about the crown, something shifted, the way a shape shifts under a blanket. Juniper went very still, the way she went still around animals, which was most of her life's training: don't startle, don't stare, let the wild thing decide you're furniture.

The shape unfolded anyway.

It rose out of the paper the way steam rises off a winter street—gray, wavering, resolving—and then a girl was sitting on the edge of her desk, feet dangling, translucent as breath on glass. She looked perhaps nineteen. She wore a coat a century out of fashion, patched at both elbows, and her hair was the color of dried sorrel leaves, reddish-brown, chopped short as if she'd cut it herself with a knife and no patience. Her eyes were the pale green of ice seen from underneath.

She was looking at Juniper with an expression of profound irritation.

"Well," the ghost said. "Finally."

Juniper did not scream. Later she would be quietly proud of that. What she did was set down her pen, square the ledger, and say, in the flat tone she used for inventory discrepancies: "You are not listed in the annex manifest."

"No," the ghost agreed. "I'm listed in the *book*. Page forty-one, margin, left side, if you'd bothered to look instead of skimming like a customs officer." She jerked her chin at the prophecy. "A hundred years I've waited in that margin. Do you know what a hundred years is, in a margin? It's a very narrow place to wait. Like standing in a doorway your whole life."

"You're anchored," Juniper said. Her mind was moving fast now, cataloguing the way it always did when fear arrived—fear made her precise. "Bound to the volume. That's necromantic residue, third-order at least. The guild has protocols for—"

"The guild has protocols for *burning* things like me," the ghost said. "Yes. I've watched them do it. Don't change the subject." She leaned forward, and though her body was made of lamplight and nothing, her gaze had weight enough to press Juniper back in her chair. "You read my book. You found the date. You're sitting here at midnight with your little pen and your little ledger, and you haven't run screaming, and you haven't called for Hesse. So either you're braver than the last eleven people who opened this book, or you've got a secret of your own worth protecting."

The room got very quiet. The rain filled it.

"I don't know what you mean," Juniper said.

"Mm." The ghost hopped off the desk—which she did without a sound, landing like snowfall—and drifted along the shelf, trailing one finger through the spines without touching them. "You talk to animals."

Every muscle in Juniper's body locked at once.

"Don't bother denying it," the ghost said, not turning around. "I wrote a book about the end of the world. Do you think I spent a century in a margin watching the guild carry me around and learned *nothing*? I've seen you on the roof. Three nights ago. Talking to the wolves. And they talked back, and you laughed—" her voice caught strangely on the word, "—and then you cried a little, and then you went inside and locked your door and didn't sleep. I know what that is. I invented knowing what that is."

Slowly, deliberately, Juniper closed the ledger. "If you tell anyone—"

"Tell them?" The ghost spun, and for a moment her edges flared bright with something that might have been anger or grief or both wearing the same coat. "Girl. I wrote a prophecy naming tonight as the first night of the Long Winter, and every single person who could have stopped it dismissed me as a madwoman, a fraud, and finally a ghost, in that order. Telling people things is the one trade I have thoroughly retired from." She drifted closer. "I need you to *listen*. There's a difference. Listening is rarer."

Juniper looked at her for a long moment. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if it too were waiting.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Sorrel."

"Like the herb."

"Like the plant, yes, my mother was a hedge-witch and unoriginal. And yours is Pemberton, Juniper Pemberton, junior ledger-keeper, third rank, commendation for accuracy twice, reprimand once for unauthorized after-hours access which you served out polishing the annex banisters." Sorrel's mouth twitched. "I told you. A century in a margin. I've read your personnel file four times. It's the most interesting thing in this building."

Despite everything—the hour, the dead girl in her cataloguing room, the circled date burning through the back of her skull—Juniper felt the corner of her own mouth move. She smothered it. "You said eleven people opened this book before me."

"Twelve, counting you. Eleven before. Every one of them read the date, went pale, and either ran or reported it up the chain, where it was filed under *allegorical devotional text* and forgotten. Bureaucracy is the true immortality, Pemberton. Empires crumble; filing systems endure." Sorrel landed on the corner of the desk again, cross-legged now, hovering an inch above the wood. "But you—you started *cross-referencing*. Shipping manifests against weather records. I watched you do it Tuesday. You found the frost reports from the northern valleys. You noticed they've been arriving altered."

The bottom dropped quietly out of Juniper's stomach, the same sensation as chapter two of any disaster: not the fall itself, but the moment you realize the floor was never load-bearing.

"They have," she said. "Three entries rewritten in different ink. Someone is hiding how early the frost is coming."

"Yes," Sorrel said softly. "Someone is. And that someone works in this building, or signs this building's papers, and I have spent one hundred years being right about it with absolutely no one to be right *at*." She spread her hands. "So. Here we are. You can't report me without reporting yourself. I can't leave this book without unraveling. Neither of us can sleep. I propose an alliance."

"That's not an alliance," Juniper said. "That's mutual blackmail."

"It's *both*," Sorrel said cheerfully. "The best ones are. Ask any marriage."

*

They negotiated until the rain stopped.

Sorrel wanted, first and always, to be believed—not comforted, not pitied, *believed*, the way a debt wants paying. Juniper wanted, first and always, verification, because a ghost who appeared out of a forbidden book on the exact night of a doom-prophecy was precisely the sort of thing the guild trained you to treat as evidence of fraud. They circled each other for an hour, prickly as two cats introduced in a small room, and then, somewhere past two in the morning, they found the thing that held.

It was the wolves.

"I heard what you said to them," Sorrel said, quieter now. "On the roof. You asked if they would warn you when the frost crossed the river. The big one with the torn ear said the frost had already crossed. He said it smelled *wrong*—like winter but hungry, like winter that eats." Her translucent hands folded together. "That's exactly what I saw, a hundred years ago. Exactly. Word for word. Hungry winter. I thought I'd gone mad. For thirty years I hoped I'd gone mad. Then I watched the crown go onto his head, and I stopped hoping."

"The king," Juniper said. "Aldous."

"Aldous the Second. Young then. Twenty-two. He had a laugh like a dog getting up—all elbows and enthusiasm. His mother had just died and he wanted, more than anything, to be good at grieving in public." Sorrel's voice had gone distant, the voice of someone reading from a page only she could see. "They gave him the crown to save him from the cold. That was the story, anyway. A hard winter coming, a kingdom frightened, and a smith in the mountains who swore the crown he'd forged could hold winter at bay. Hold it *in*, rather. Like a jar holds smoke." She looked up. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The crown doesn't keep the winter out of the kingdom. It keeps the winter *in the king*. And the winter is patient, and the king was young, and a hundred years is a long time to hold your breath."

Juniper's pen had stopped moving some minutes ago. She realized she was gripping it hard enough to bend the nib.

"The prophecy says the Long Winter begins tonight," she said. "Your prophecy. Written by your hand."

"My hand," Sorrel agreed. "Which brings us to the difficult part, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because I've rehearsed this confession for a century and I only get the nerve once per decade." She took a breath she did not need. "Everything in that book is true except the ending. The ending I wrote myself. Not foretold—invented. There is no verse in the frozen god's liturgy that names a deliverer. I put one in. A ledger-keeper, with the beasts' tongue, who would find the book and believe it, because believing is the one thing the guild never teaches and the one thing the task requires." Her pale eyes fixed on Juniper. "I wrote you into existence, Pemberton. Or near enough. I wrote the shape of you and waited a hundred years for the world to pour someone into it."

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a dog began to bark—one sharp note, then another, then a third, and then every dog in the district was barking, a rising tide of sound rolling in from the north, from the river, from the direction of the palace.

Juniper stood up so fast her chair went over.

"The wolves," she breathed. "The frost's crossed the city wall."

"Or it was never outside it at all," Sorrel said, rising with her, streaming gray light like a banner. "Listen to me. Tomorrow—today—the guild receives a summons. Royal audit. It comes every seven years, it's a formality, everyone yawns through it. This time it won't be a formality. This time *he* will be there, wearing his face like a well-kept coat, and he will look at every clerk in that hall, and when he looks at you, Pemberton, you must remember one thing above all others—"

The dogs were silent now, all at once, which was worse.

"—don't blink first," Sorrel finished. "He counts blinks. It's how he finds the warm ones."

Juniper stared at her. "What happens to the warm ones?"

Sorrel smiled, and it was the saddest thing Juniper had ever seen a dead girl do.

"He sends for them," she said. "Alone. And they come back smiling wrong."
