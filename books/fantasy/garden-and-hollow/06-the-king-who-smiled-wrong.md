## 6. The King Who Smiled Wrong

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a warden whose boots squeaked against the guildhall's marble as though the floor itself were trying to get away from him.

"Royal audit," he said, and handed Guildmaster Hesse a letter sealed in blue wax — the crown's color, the color of deep ice, the color of nothing that grows. "The King requires the Hollow Guild's ledgers presented in person. The master, and one clerk of his choosing."

"One clerk," Hesse repeated, and I felt his eyes find me across the counting room the way a hand finds a knife in the dark.

I should have pretended not to notice. That was my whole art, after all — noticing without being noticed, hearing without being heard, existing in the margins of every room like a well-kept footnote. But Hesse had already decided, and when the warden had gone he crossed the floor to my desk and set the blue-sealed letter down beside my inkwell with a gentleness that frightened me more than any shout could have.

"You'll come with me," he said. "You keep the cleanest books in the Hollow. If anyone can make sense of what the crown is asking, it's you."

"What is the crown asking?" I asked.

Hesse looked at the letter for a long moment before answering, and in that moment I saw something cross his face that I had never seen there before. Not fear exactly. Something older than fear. The look of a man reading a bill that has finally come due.

"An accounting," he said. "Of everything."

* * *

We traveled to the palace in a guild carriage drawn by two gray horses who would not stop talking.

*I don't like this road,* said the near horse, whose name was Barley and who had once told me, at length, about a dream he'd had involving an enormous turnip. *The stones are wrong under my feet. They're cold from underneath. Cold goes up, Juniper-of-the-ledgers. Cold shouldn't go up.*

"Cold often doesn't behave," I murmured, low enough that only the horses could hear me over the wheels.

*She talks!* the far horse whispered, delighted. *Barley, she talks!*

*Hush, Clover, you'll get her hanged.* But Barley said it the way one says something pleasant, like *hush, the sun is out*, and I had to press my lips together to keep from laughing, because that was the terrible arithmetic of my life: the only honest conversations I had were with creatures who could get me killed for having them.

Beast-speech was treason. Everyone knew it the way everyone knew fire burned. Three hundred years ago, the last beast-speaker had been a hedge-witch who told a duke's horses to throw him into a river, and the crown had written the law so broadly that even listening to animals talk was technically a crime. I had been committing small treasons since I was six years old, when I'd realized that the stray cats of Mill Row weren't yowling randomly but complaining, specifically and eloquently, about the quality of fish scraps behind the tannery.

I had built my whole life on the principle that no one must ever know. Careful hands. Quiet mouth. Ledgers balanced to the half-penny. A woman made entirely of margins.

And now the crown had summoned me by name.

Not by name, I corrected myself, watching the palace grow out of the morning fog ahead of us — first the walls, then the towers, then the great glass roof of the Winter Court shining like a blade laid flat across the sky. By function. *One clerk of his choosing.* Hesse had chosen me. The king had only asked for a clerk.

Still. My hands would not stay warm, and we were not yet inside.

*The palace smells wrong,* Clover said suddenly, all her delight gone. Her ears flattened. *It smells like the inside of a snowdrift. Like the middle of winter, where nothing has ever lived.*

I looked at her. She looked back at me with one rolling brown eye, and what I saw in it made my stomach drop through the carriage floor and keep falling.

Clover was afraid of the palace. Horses were afraid of loud noises and narrow bridges and things that rustled in hedgerows. Horses were not afraid of buildings. Buildings did not smell like anything to a horse except stone and straw and the people inside them.

But Clover smelled winter in a palace in harvest month, and she was trembling so hard the carriage shook.

*Turn around,* she whispered. *Juniper-of-the-ledgers, turn the carriage around.*

I couldn't tell Hesse why. So I reached through the window gap and pressed my palm to her neck, feeling the sweat there, the galloping pulse beneath the skin, and I said the only thing I could say: *"Stay brave. I'll be quick."*

The gates opened for us like a mouth.

* * *

The Winter Court was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: perfectly, and with the understanding that its beauty existed to kill you.

Everything was white marble veined with silver. Everything was cold — not the honest cold of a cellar or a winter morning but a curated cold, a cold that had been arranged, the way flowers are arranged. Servants moved through it in furs, silent as snowfall. Chandeliers of cut glass hung from the ceiling and threw light in sharp little pieces, and none of it warmed anything. My breath fogged. So did Hesse's. Nobody else's seemed to.

"The court keeps a particular temperature," said our escort, a chamberlain with a voice like dry paper sliding over dry paper. "His Majesty prefers it."

"Prefers it," Hesse repeated, in the tone he used for entries he intended to investigate later.

The chamberlain smiled. It was a professional smile, applied to the face like paint. "You'll present your ledgers to His Majesty within the hour. Until then, you are guests of the Winter Court. Please do not wander. Please do not touch the ice garden. Please do not speak to the King unless spoken to."

"And if the King speaks to us?" I asked, before I could stop myself.

The chamberlain turned his head toward me slowly, the way doors close. He looked at me for perhaps three seconds, though it felt like a season. Then he smiled again, the same smile, re-applied.

"Then you answer," he said, and left us in a gallery full of portraits.

I hated him immediately and completely, which was a comfort. Hatred was simple. Hatred kept the fear in a box.

The portraits were of kings. Aldous the First through Aldous the Ninth, each one painted in the same pose, the same crown, the same distant expression — and then, at the end of the row, Aldous the Tenth, painted only eleven years ago, when he took the throne at nineteen. In the portrait he was young and ruddy and grinning like a boy who has just been handed the best toy in the world. There was a falcon on his wrist. There was mud on his boots; the painter had kept it, some act of royal whimsy, the king who would rather hunt than sit.

I remembered him. Everyone did. The Falcon Prince, they'd called him, the wildest heir in a century, who once rode out of the palace in midwinter and came back three days later with frostbite and a wolf story no one believed. He'd laughed in public. He'd danced with kitchen girls. He'd stood up at his own coronation feast and toasted *the beasts of field and forest, my oldest friends.*

That was eleven years ago.

Last month, the coin had changed. New minting, new profile, and the face on it had stopped me cold at my own desk: the same jaw, the same brow, and eyes that looked at nothing at all.

"Master Pemberton." Hesse touched my elbow. "They're ready."

* * *

The throne room had been emptied of everyone but us.

I had expected an audience — courtiers, guards, the machinery of majesty. Instead there was a single long table, a single chair at its far end, and the King of Vell sitting alone in a room built for a thousand, the way a spider sits alone at the center of a web it has deliberately cleared.

King Aldous the Tenth rose to greet us.

He was tall. He wore white and silver, and the crown — I had seen the crown a hundred times on coins and stamps and the seal of every official document I had ever filed, and still seeing it live was different, the way a drawing of fire is different from fire. It sat on his head like it belonged there more than his skull did. Old iron worked into a circlet of thorned branches, black metal drinking the chandelier-light instead of returning it. When he moved, the crown did not quite move with him. I want to write that sentence more plainly, because I have thought about it every day since: when he moved, the crown lagged. A half-breath behind. As if it were heavy. As if it were deciding.

"Guildmaster Hesse." The King's voice was warm. That was the first wrong thing. It was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it has burned down. "And the clerk. Come forward, clerk. Let me see you."

I came forward. I bowed the exact depth required of a guild clerk before her king — I had looked it up the night before, twice — and I kept my eyes lowered as protocol demanded, which meant I saw his boots, and his hands, and the hem of his white cloak, and I noticed that the floor around the throne was free of dust. Not swept. Free of dust. Dust does not settle in a room that cold. Nothing settles anywhere in a place where even the air has been told to hold still.

"You keep the ledgers," the King said. "All of them?"

"The impossible ones, Your Majesty."

A pause. Long enough that I risked looking up.

He was smiling at me. It was a courteous smile, well-made, symmetrical, the kind of smile that opens doors and ends wars. And it was the most frightening thing I had ever seen, because it arrived a moment too late. I asked my question, and there was a silence in which a man might have blinked, might have breathed, might have done any of the ten thousand small involuntary things that living bodies do — and then the smile came, fully formed, like a servant entering on cue.

"The impossible ones," the King repeated. "How wonderful. Tell me, clerk — what is the strangest thing you have ever recorded?"

I knew the answer instantly, because I had asked myself that question many times on slow evenings, the way other people count sheep. "A bottled echo, Majesty. Lot 114. Purchased from a widow in Grayharbor. It says her husband's name when the cork is drawn. She paid four years of her life for it, and told the assessor it was worth every one."

Something moved behind the King's eyes. I want to be precise about this, because precision is my trade. It was not emotion. Emotion moves across a face like weather. This moved *underneath*, the way something moves under ice — a shadow passing below the surface of a frozen pond, too large for the pond.

"A fair price," the King said. "For an echo. For a voice that persists." He tilted his head, and the crown tilted a half-beat later. "Do you believe things persist, clerk? After they're... spent?"

"I'm a bookkeeper, Majesty. I believe everything balances."

"Everything balances," he echoed softly. And for one instant — one — the smile dropped away entirely, like a mask slipping its cord, and what was underneath looked at me.

I have tried, in the days since, to describe those eyes, and I have failed, because the problem is not that they were cruel or dead or strange. The problem is that they were *hungry*. There was a wanting in them so vast and so patient that it made the room feel small, made my body feel like a ledger entry, made me understand in one cold flash that I was standing inside an appetite the size of a kingdom.

Then the smile returned, right on time, and the King clapped his hands together once — a sound like a branch breaking — and said, "Well! Let us look at these famous ledgers."

* * *

The audit lasted four hours, and it was the strangest accounting of my life, because the King of Vell personally examined every page.

A king does not do this. Kings receive summaries. Kings receive the shape of a thing, not the thing itself. But Aldous turned each folio with careful white fingers and read every line, and I watched him read, and I noticed the second wrong thing: he did not blink enough. A person blinks, on average, fifteen times a minute. I know this because I am the sort of person who counts things. The King blinked perhaps once a minute. Twice. His eyes stayed open and wet and fixed, and between blinks they did not glisten or shift or do any of the things living eyes do; they simply remained, like glass eyes in a hunting trophy, aimed at the ledger, aimed at Hesse, aimed at me.

And whenever he came to an entry involving cold — frost-wine, glacier salt, winter-iron, shipments marked only with the sigil for *perishable* — his finger would rest on the line a moment longer. Rest, and tap. Once. Twice. Like something tasting through a fingertip.

"Tell me about this," he said, tapping an entry from six years ago. Forty crates, origin redacted, destination: the palace cellars. Contents listed as *seasonal goods.*

"That predates my tenure on the impossible ledger, Majesty. The notation style is old. I'd have to consult the archive."

"Consult it," the King said pleasantly. "I do so love a thorough clerk."

There were thirty such entries. Sixty. I counted as he turned the pages, and I counted the way his hunger sharpened on each one, subtle as a dog going still before a flush of birds, and somewhere in the fourth hour a thought assembled itself in me with the quiet click of a lock turning:

*The King is auditing us. The King is checking whether we know what he's been buying.*

He wasn't examining our books. He was examining *us*.

At last he closed the final folio and laced his fingers atop it, and the crown settled on his brow with a sound I will hear until I die — a faint creak, like lake ice shifting in the dark, like something adjusting its grip.

"Your guild serves the crown admirably," he said. "Guildmaster. Clerk. You will both dine with me tonight."

"Majesty, we hadn't hoped—" Hesse began.

"No," the King agreed, smiling, blinking, once. "You hadn't."

* * *

We were given a guest chamber while the table was set, and the moment the door closed behind us Hesse rounded on me with his voice strangled to a whisper.

"Don't look at him."

"Sir?"

"In the throne room. When he speaks to you, you look at the ledger, at the table, at your own hands. Never at his eyes. Do you understand me?"

"Hesse, what is—"

"*Do you understand me?*" It was the first time in nine years I had heard him raise his voice, and it came out cracked down the middle, and beneath the crack was the thing I'd seen on his face at dawn: a man reading a bill come due. He gripped my shoulders. His hands were shaking. "Juniper. Whatever you think you saw in that room — whatever your *gift* told you — you bury it. You bury it so deep the grave grows grass. Swear it."

I looked at my master, my mentor, the man who had taught me double-entry and discretion, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as a single event.

He knew. He had always known about me — about the horses, the cats, the treason in my blood — and he had kept my secret for nine years, and he was spending that secret now, all of it, in terror, to keep me alive through dinner.

And he was more afraid of the King than he was of the law.

"I swear," I said.

He exhaled and let go of me and turned to the window, and I followed his gaze out over the palace grounds, where the famous ice garden glittered in the afternoon light — sculpted swans and roses and a fountain frozen mid-pour, beautiful, immaculate, dead. Beyond the wall, the city went about its harvest. Carts. Bells. Smoke from a hundred chimneys, rising straight up in the windless gold air of a season that was supposed to be autumn.

Nothing about the day was cold. Except here. Only here, where the cold had been *arranged*.

"Eleven years ago," I said quietly, "the King toasted the beasts. They say he could whistle up a falcon out of a clear sky. They say wolves came to the wall for him like dogs."

"People say many things," Hesse said, not turning around.

"He doesn't blink, Hesse."

My master was silent for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was so low I nearly lost it under the wind.

"Then mind your ledger, girl. And pray to whatever gods listen to clerks that whatever sits on that throne never asks you a question you can't answer honestly."

Below us, in the frozen garden, the ice fountain poured its silver nothing, forever.

And along the garden path, unhurried, pale as paper, the King walked alone among his sculptures — and stopped, and lifted his head, and looked directly up at our window from across the whole width of the courtyard, as if he had known exactly where we stood.

He smiled.

He did not blink at all.

"Dinner," said a voice behind us, soft as snowfall, "is served."
