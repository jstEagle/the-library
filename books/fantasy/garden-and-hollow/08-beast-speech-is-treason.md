## 8. Beast-Speech Is Treason

The horse's name was Marrowbone, and he was dying of loneliness.

Juniper knew this the way she knew most things the guild would rather she didn't: through the small, treasonous door in her mind that opened when an animal spoke and she answered. She had been walking the delivery lane behind the Guildhall with a stack of misfiled invoices under her arm when the dray cart pulled up, and the great gray gelding hanging between its shafts turned his head and said, *Cold coming early this year. Cold coming wrong.*

She should have kept walking.

That was the rule she had made for herself at eleven years old, when she first understood that what lived in her was not a gift but a sentence waiting to be pronounced. *Hear them if you must. Never answer where anyone can see.* For fourteen years she had kept it. Fourteen years of biting her tongue in stables, of letting dogs' questions die unanswered, of standing in market squares while pigeons shouted warnings about hawks and cats and boys with slingshots, her face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a woman who heard nothing at all.

But Marrowbone's voice had a crack in it. The crack of something breaking slowly, in the dark, where no one listened.

*Who feeds you?* she asked, before she could stop herself.

The horse's ears went forward like two hands reaching. His whole body changed — the dull sway of the overworked draft animal becoming, for one moment, attention. Hope. It was terrible to see, hope on an animal that big; it looked like watching a cathedral lean.

*The carter's boy. He forgets. The master says hay is dear.* Marrowbone shifted his weight off his near foreleg, and Juniper saw what she hadn't seen from a distance: the leg was swollen at the fetlock, hot and tight, the beginning of an infection that would travel upward and kill him by midwinter if no one did anything. *I am not lame,* the horse said quickly, as though she might report him for it. *I can still pull. I only rest it when I stand.*

*I know,* Juniper said. *I know you're not lame.*

And that was when Tobias Grell stepped out of the shadow of the cooper's shed and saw her mouth moving.

* * *

Later — much later, after everything that followed — Juniper would try to reconstruct those three seconds. Had her lips actually shaped the words? Beast-speech needed no voice; it happened somewhere behind the breastbone, in the place where meaning lived before language got hold of it. But she had a habit, an old and dangerous habit, of whispering aloud when she spoke with animals, as if the words needed air to be real. Her mother had caught her at it once, kneeling in the garden telling worms the news of the rain, and had gone white as salt and gripped her shoulders and said, *Never again where there are eyes, Juniper. Never again.* Her mother had known why. Her mother had never said why.

Three seconds. That was all Grell needed.

He was a junior clerk in Requisitions, a narrow man with narrow eyes and a widower's stoop, and everyone knew what he was. Every guild has its informers the way every body has its lymph: unglamorous, everywhere, necessary to the circulation of fear. Grell reported to the Second Chair, and the Second Chair reported upward, and the reports flowed like water finding cracks. He had gotten two apprentices dismissed for pilfering candle-ends and one journeyman flogged for falsifying weights, and he wore his usefulness the way other men wore medals.

He didn't say anything at first. That was the worst part. He simply stood there in the lane with his ledger-clutching hands folded, watching her watch him, while behind him Marrowbone sensed the change in the air and went still and stupid-eyed, performing the perfect ignorance of beasts.

"Good morning, Mistress Pemberton," Grell said at last.

"Master Grell." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Are you lost? Requisitions is the other side of the hall."

"I was admiring the horse." He glanced at Marrowbone with the flat appraisal of a man pricing meat. "Fine animal. Old, though. You can tell when they start talking to themselves."

The lane was empty. The delivery carts had gone; the cooper's boys were at their lunch; the nearest living witness besides the three of them was a gull on the ridgepole, screaming about fish. Nobody had heard anything. Nobody had seen anything except a clerk catching a ledger-keeper pausing beside a cart, perhaps murmuring to herself — women did murmur, women counted sums under their breath, women prayed. There was still a version of this where nothing happened.

Juniper reached for that version. "I was checking the manifest," she said. "The carter's seals don't match the invoice. I'll have to note it."

"Mm." Grell smiled. It was a careful smile, built like a bridge, designed to bear weight. "Of course. Seals." He took one step closer, and his voice dropped into the register men used for transactions they didn't want named. "You know, Mistress Pemberton, I've always admired your work in the ledgers. Such precision. Such discretion. A person could learn from you."

"What do you want, Master Grell?"

"Want?" He spread his hands, wounded. "I want nothing. I'm a servant of the guild, same as you. I only observe, and report what I observe, and let wiser heads decide what observations mean." He let that sit in the cold air between them. "Though I confess I observed something just now that I'm having trouble interpreting. A woman, talking to a horse. Mouth moving, eyes soft. Almost as if—" he tilted his head, savoring it, "—almost as if she expected an answer."

Somewhere above them the gull screamed again. Marrowbone blew out a long breath that steamed.

"You're mistaken," Juniper said.

"Probably." Grell nodded agreeably. "Probably I'm mistaken. Mistakes happen to careful men all the time. Of course, mistakes also get corrected, when a man is motivated to correct them. And motivation, Mistress Pemberton, is so often a question of . . . consideration."

There it was. Not even cleverly disguised. Grell wasn't subtle because he'd never needed to be; the machinery of denunciation ran so smoothly in Vane Hollow that subtlety was a luxury. He wanted payment, and he wanted her to know that the alternative was a word in the right ear — *beast-speech*, the old crime, the hanging crime, the one they still burned into the lintels of certain houses in the low country as a warning. *Here lived a speaker. Here lived a traitor to the human shape.*

Under the Edict of Quiet Tongues, beast-speech was classed with necromancy and unlicensed prophecy: commerce with the non-human world, which the Crown had declared, some two hundred years ago, to be inherently seditious. Beasts could not swear oaths. Beasts could not testify. Therefore anything a beast told a speaker was by definition conspiracy. Juniper had read the statute a dozen times, the way a hunted thing reads its own description, and had long ago stopped being frightened of the wording and started being frightened of how reasonable it sounded on the page.

"I don't bribe people, Master Grell," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You keep ledgers. Which is better. Because a ledger-keeper can make entries." His eyes flicked toward the invoices under her arm, and Juniper felt the floor of the morning tilt beneath her. "Requisitions has been waiting six weeks for the tallow allocation from the southern stores. Six weeks. My chair is asking me daily. And you, I understand, control the cross-index. A pity if the entry were . . . misplaced. A tragedy, really, if the requisition had to wait another month. Or two."

She understood then, fully, what he was asking, and it was worse than coin. Coin was clean; coin left no fingerprints in her own house. He wanted her to use the ledgers — her ledgers, the ones she had spent fourteen years keeping immaculate, the ones whose integrity was the single wall she had built between herself and the world's contempt — to move goods for him. To become, in however small a way, a corrupt instrument of the guild she served.

And the alternative was the rope, or the quiet room in the Warden's Tower where speakers went and did not come back out talking.

Marrowbone shifted his weight again and winced, and the small sound of it decided her more than any argument could have. Because here was the arithmetic she would carry for the rest of this story, the first entry in a column that would grow long and dark: *a horse's leg against a ledger's honor*. Weigh them. Weigh them honestly. See which side the scale tips.

"The southern tallow allocation," she said quietly, "was delayed because the southern stores filed under the old seal format. It's a clerical error, not a shortage. If someone corrected the format and resubmitted, the release would process within the week."

Grell's smile widened by a degree. "If only someone had the authority to correct such a format."

"If someone did," Juniper said, "it would be a favor. Between colleagues. Forgotten immediately."

"Forgetten," Grell echoed, tasting the word. "Immediately."

"And it would buy your silence. Permanently. On the matter of the horse."

"On the matter of the horse," he agreed, and then, because he was Grell, he added: "And any similar matters that may arise."

"There will be no similar matters."

"Of course not." He bowed slightly, a clerk's bow, precise and insulting. "Then we understand each other. I'll leave you to your manifests, Mistress Pemberton. Give my regards to the horse."

He walked away down the lane with the light, quick step of a man who has just been paid, and Juniper stood in the cold with the invoices pressed against her chest like a shield that had failed, and listened to Marrowbone ask, gently, *Was that a bad man?*

"Yes," she said.

*Will he hurt you?*

"Not today."

*The tall one with the kind hands used to say that too,* the horse said. *About the boy who forgot the hay.* He lowered his great head and rested his muzzle against her shoulder, briefly, the way horses comfort — a weight, a warmth, a trust so total it was obscene. *Thank you for answering me. Even if it costs. Most days, the answering is worth the cost.*

*Not most days,* Juniper said. *Today it wasn't.*

But Marrowbone had already turned back to his shafts, back to the simple horizon of hay and road and pain, and had not heard her, or had chosen not to. Animals, she thought, were honest in a way humans couldn't afford to be. They never pretended a kindness was free.

* * *

She made the entry that afternoon.

It took her twenty minutes to write four lines. She sat at her desk in the records room with the lamp turned high and the cross-index open before her, and she performed the corruption with the same care she brought to everything, because that was the cruelest joke of it: her precision was exactly what made her useful to men like Grell. A sloppy keeper's favor would be found in a season. Hers would never be found at all.

She corrected the southern stores' seal notation. She flagged the tallow allocation for expedited review. She initialed the correction in the margin — her initials, J.P., in her own iron-gall ink, in her own hand, evidence with her name on it — and then she blotted the page and closed the index and sat looking at the leather cover for a long time.

Fourteen years. Fourteen years of invisible, immaculate, careful life, and it had taken one swollen fetlock and one narrow-eyed clerk to put her initials on a lie.

"It's done," she said aloud to the empty room, and hated the sound of her own voice, and hated more the small practical part of her that was already noting, coolly, that the favor had cost her nothing but ink. That was the trap inside the trap. The first compromise never feels like a fall; it feels like a step. A reasonable step. A step taken for a good reason, with a lame horse as its justification, and surely she would never do it again, and surely next time the reason would be equally good, and the time after that—

The lamp guttered. Outside the records room's high window, the afternoon light had gone strange — thin, silvery, the color of light through ice. Juniper frowned at it. Harvest month. The light should have been honey-thick, slanting gold across the rooftops. Instead it lay on the floor pale and hard, and when she pressed her palm flat against the windowsill, the stone was cold. Not autumn-cold. Cold like a held breath. Cold like something waiting.

The wolves had been saying it for a week now, on the roof tiles at dusk, in voices that carried up to her like smoke: *Winter comes early. Winter comes wrong. The packs in the north have gone silent, and the frost walks like it has somewhere to be.*

She had half-listened, the way you half-listen to weather. Now, with Grell's initials drying in her margin and his silence purchased and her soul one entry lighter, the wolves' words came back to her with new weight, and she found she could not shake them.

She climbed to the roof that evening instead of going home.

* * *

The Guildhall roof was her secret second study: slate slopes and lead gutters and, at the very peak, a flat stretch of ridge tile warm from the day's sun where she could sit with her back against the chimney and watch the city. She had come here since her first year at the guild, fleeing the closeness of the records room, and the city's wolves had learned her scent the way they learned everything — patiently, completely.

They came at dusk, as they always did. Three of them tonight, ghost-gray against the grayer sky, flowing up the fire stairs and along the parapet with the unhurried confidence of creatures who owned the night shift of the city. The leader was an old female with a torn ear whom Juniper privately called Chancellor, because she had the manner of a woman who had chaired difficult meetings for decades.

*You smell of fear and ink,* Chancellor observed, settling onto the warm tiles a respectful arm's length away.

"Both are permanent conditions of my employment."

*One of them increased today.* Wolves did not ask questions so much as state facts and wait for you to contradict them. Juniper didn't bother.

"A man saw me speaking to a horse," she said. "I bought his silence with a ledger favor. It's the first time I've ever—" She stopped. Started again. "It won't be the last. That's what the young ones don't understand about the first compromise. It isn't an event. It's a door."

The youngest wolf, a gangly male with oversized paws, tipped his head. *What's behind the door?*

"More doors," Juniper said. "All the way down."

Chancellor chewed on this, or on a flea; with her it was hard to tell. Then she stood, stretched, and turned her amber gaze eastward, toward the palace hill, where the royal banners hung limp in a windless evening.

*We came to tell you the news, ledger-keeper, but you already smell of it. The frost crossed the river last night. In harvest month.* Her hackles lifted, slowly, like a sentence being written. *And it did not come down from the mountains the way frost comes. It came out of the ground. Upward. From underneath the palace.*

Juniper followed her gaze. The palace sat black against the last light, its hundred windows beginning to glitter — and she realized, with a slow crawl of cold up her own spine, that the windows were glittering on the eastern face only, the face she could see, and that the glitter was not lamplight.

It was ice. Frost was blooming on the inside of the glass.

*The old stories say,* Chancellor went on, in the tone wolves reserved for the oldest stories, *that when winter comes wrong, it is because something in the high house is hungry. Our grandmothers' grandmothers sang of it. A crown that eats. We thought it was a song for cubs.* She looked back at Juniper, and her torn ear twitched. *We do not think that anymore. Something is waking under the palace, ledger-keeper. And three nights ago, a stranger came south across the ice asking our packs questions no stranger should know to ask. A wolf who walks like a man. The packs sent him to the city. He arrives tomorrow.*

Juniper's heart did something complicated and inconvenient. "A wolf who walks like a man?"

*So the runners say.* Chancellor rose, shook herself, and paused at the parapet's edge, silhouetted against the frosted palace. *Be careful with your new door, ledger-keeper. Doors swing both ways. And whatever comes through yours tomorrow —* the old wolf's eyes glinted — *smell it carefully before you speak to it. Not everything that wears a friendly shape is friendly. And not everything that wears a cold one is cruel.*

Then the three of them poured off the roof into the darkening city, and Juniper sat alone on the cold tiles with her arms around her knees, watching frost climb the palace windows like a slow white fire, and understood that her careful, invisible life had ended sometime that morning in a delivery lane, between a dying horse's gratitude and a clerk's careful smile.

Tomorrow, a wolf who walked like a man was coming to find her.

She discovered she was not afraid.

That, more than anything, was what frightened her.
