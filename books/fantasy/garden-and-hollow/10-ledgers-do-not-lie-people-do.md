## 10. Ledgers Do Not Lie, People Do

The night after the frost came early to the northern quarter, Juniper Pemberton sat down at her desk in the counting room of the Hollow Guild and did what she always did when the world frightened her: she opened a ledger.

The counting room at midnight was her favorite place in the city, though she would never have admitted it aloud. It was a long stone chamber lit by a dozen brass lamps turned low, their flames reflected in row upon row of green glass shades that hooded the desks like sleeping eyes. The air smelled of tallow, iron gall ink, and the particular dry sweetness of old vellum — a smell Juniper had loved since she was nine years old and first allowed to sweep the shelves. Other children had dreamed of dragons. She had dreamed of a complete set.

Tonight the ledgers were not comforting her.

She had pulled the winter requisitions — the guild's outbound trade records for the cold months, forty years of them, stacked in six heavy volumes beside her elbow — because of something Ash had said on the roof of the archive two nights past, before the frost, before everything. *The cold isn't falling on this city,* he'd told her, his voice low, his amber eyes catching the lamplight like a wolf's even in his human shape. *It's being poured.*

She hadn't understood him then. She was beginning to.

Ledgers do not lie. That was the first law of the Hollow Guild, carved into the lintel above the counting room door in letters a century deep: *THE LEDGER REMEMBERS WHAT MEN FORGET.* Juniper had taken comfort in that law all her working life. Numbers were honest in a way people were not. A number could be wrong, but it could not deceive; error was innocent, and fraud left fingerprints, because every hand that wrote in these books was trained differently, pressed differently, thought differently. After eleven years of auditing, Juniper could read a falsified entry the way Ash read a trail of tracks — by what was missing, by what was too neat, by where the ground had been deliberately swept.

And someone had been sweeping the ground.

She found the first anomaly an hour past midnight, in the volume for thirty-one years ago. The outbound shipments for the month of Deepfrost ran down the page in the usual tidy columns: salted eels to Vellmark, candle-wax to the mining towns, a consignment of bottled echoes — secondhand ones, shopworn — to a collector in the coast cities. And then, wedged between the wax and the echoes, a single line:

*To the Palace of Aldous: 40 measures, winter-grade. Received under seal. No further particulars.*

Forty measures of *what*? Every other entry in the book named its goods. Grain was grain, iron was iron, memories were memories, and impossible things were described with the loving precision the guild demanded — a bottled echo was catalogued by its pitch, its age, the voice it repeated. But this line said only *winter-grade*, as if winter were a commodity that came in grades, and no further particulars, which in guild language meant *stop reading here*.

Juniper turned back a page. Turned forward. Found the same line again the next month. *To the Palace of Aldous: 60 measures, winter-grade.* Then eighty. Then, in the deepest weeks of that winter, one hundred and twenty.

Her quill hovered over her own audit sheet and did not move.

There was a proper procedure for anomalies. She knew it better than anyone in the building; she had helped write the current edition of the audit manual. An unexplained entry was flagged in red, cross-referenced, and reported to the Guildmaster within three days. That was the rule. That was the clean, well-lit road.

Instead she sat very still and listened to the counting room breathe — the tick of cooling lamp-brass, the distant shuffle of the night clerk two rooms away, the wind testing the shutters. Then she reached for the next volume, and the next, and began to hunt.

They were there in every year. Not every month — that would have been too visible, a pattern any junior auditor would catch. They appeared twice or three times a winter, always in the coldest weeks, always growing a little larger, always written in the same careful hand across four decades. That was the thing that raised the hair on her arms. Hands changed. Clerks died, retired, were promoted. Forty years of entries in one unchanging script meant either a clerk who did not age —

— or a hand that copied itself, generation to generation, the way a forgery teaches its own children.

Juniper had grown up on guild stories the way other children grew up on saints' lives. She knew the tale of Old Maren who had audited a duke into ruin with nothing but a sharpened quill and patience. She knew the story of the founding, when the first guildmaster had traded a year of his own life to buy the guild its charter, and the charter had held ever since. She had believed, with the simple fierce faith of a girl who had nowhere else to belong, that the Hollow Guild was the honest thing in a dishonest city — that it trafficked in impossible things but never in lies, because the ledger remembered what men forgot.

She turned to the most recent volume, the current winter, and found the newest line still wet enough to smudge:

*To the Palace of Aldous: 200 measures, winter-grade. Received under seal. No further particulars.*

Two hundred measures. Whatever a measure of winter was, the palace was taking five times what it had taken thirty years ago, and taking it now, in Harvest Month, before the first true frost. Before the frost that had already come early and wrong, the frost the wolves had sung about from the rooftops until her bones ached with listening.

Ash's words came back to her. *It's being poured.*

She looked at the entry for a long time. Then, because she was Juniper Pemberton, and because the ledger-keeper's discipline was the only spine she had, she did the thing the manual told her never to do alone: she went looking for the source.

* * *

The shipping manifests lived in the sub-cellar, three floors down, behind a door that required two keys and a spoken word. Juniper had both keys — senior auditors did — and the word she had earned at great cost and never used except in emergencies, because the door remembered voices the way the ledger remembered numbers, and she did not trust anything in this building to forget her.

The sub-cellar was cold in a way that had nothing to do with season. Rows of manifest chests stretched back into the dark, each chest warded against damp and theft and, rumor said, against ghosts, though Sorrel — who had been haunting Juniper's margins for a week now, prickly and brilliant and dead a hundred years — claimed wards against ghosts were mostly superstition and flattery. Sorrel had wanted to come along tonight. Juniper had left the prophecy book locked in her desk drawer anyway, wrapped in wool, because a talking ghost was one secret too many to carry into the dark with her.

The chest marked WINTER REQUISITION — OUTBOUND was heavier than it should have been, and older than it should have been. Its lock was guild-standard. Its contents were not.

She knelt on the flagstones with a manifest across her knees and read by the light of a single shuttered lantern, and the picture assembled itself piece by piece, the way a beast reads a kill site — not from any one bone, but from the shape of what's missing.

The manifests matched the ledgers. That was the first shock; she had expected them not to. Forty years of shipments to the palace, itemized at last: *winter-grade cold, bound measures, certified stable.* There were handling instructions. There were safety protocols. There was a schedule of carriage routes that avoided every main road and every checkpoint, running instead through the old quarry tunnels beneath the Hollow district — the tunnels that officially had been sealed since before Juniper was born.

And there was a supplier column. Every shipment, every year, forty years of them, listed the same source:

*Hollow Guild, internal stock. Requisition approved under Master's seal.*

Not sold to the palace. Supplied. Drawn from the guild's own stores, against the guild's own books, authorized by the man whose office Juniper had walked past ten thousand times without fear.

Guildmaster Hesse had signed her first employment contract. He had stood at her mother's funeral with rain running off his hat brim and said, *She was a good woman, and you are your mother's daughter, and this house knows how to keep what is good.* He had taught her the difference between an error and a lie. He had once spent an entire evening showing her, entry by entry, how the guild had refused a fortune in tainted memory-stock during the plague winters, because some things must not be traded even when everyone is buying.

*Some things must not be traded.* His voice, in her memory, was perfectly clear. So was the signature on forty years of manifests.

Juniper closed the chest and knelt in the cold dark and felt something in her chest go quiet and hard, the way a stream goes quiet just before it freezes over.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not avoid. The palace was receiving two hundred measures of winter-grade cold per month, up from forty. The cold in the city was arriving early and arriving wrong, poured rather than fallen, exactly as the wolves had said. The guild was the supplier. Therefore the guild was not merely complicit in whatever was happening — the guild was the pipeline. The honest house, the house that kept what was good, had been feeding winter to the crown for longer than she had been alive, and hiding it in plain sight inside the one place no thief would ever think to look: the truth.

That was the elegant horror of it, she realized, kneeling there with the lantern guttering. Ledgers do not lie. So they had never lied. They had simply written the truth in a language designed to be unread — *no further particulars* — and trusted that honesty itself would be the best disguise. The ledger remembered what men forgot. It had remembered everything. It had simply never been asked the right question.

Until now.

She repacked the chest with hands that did not shake, because she would not let them shake, and she copied three manifest numbers onto a scrap of paper and sewed it into her sleeve, and she climbed the three floors back up to the counting room with the lantern burning low and steady in her grip.

Which was when she saw that the lamp outside Guildmaster Hesse's study was lit.

At half past two in the morning. With the door open.

* * *

"Sit down, Juniper."

Hesse stood at his window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the city. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, silver-bearded, with the patient face of someone who had spent fifty years listening to other people's mistakes. His study smelled of pipe smoke and leather and, faintly, of something colder underneath — a smell she had noticed before and never been able to name, like the air off a frozen lake.

She sat. She did not put the manifest numbers on his desk. She kept her hands flat on her knees and waited, because in the guild, whoever spoke first paid a price, and she had learned that lesson at nineteen.

"You've been in the sub-cellar," Hesse said, still facing the window. "The ward-door logged your voice at one o'clock. The winter chest has been moved." He turned around. His eyes were kind, and tired, and absolutely without surprise, and that frightened her more than anger would have. "I want you to hear from me what you would otherwise spend a week digging out of my records, and I want you to hear it once."

"The palace," Juniper said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "You've been shipping cold to the palace for forty years."

"Winter," Hesse corrected gently. "We call it winter-grade because the palace calls it that. What it is, precisely, is none of our business, and that is the first thing you will accept if you want to remain in this house."

"None of our—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Master, we're the Hollow Guild. Our whole law is that we record. We don't look away. I was taught that by *you*."

"And it's true, and it costs us dearly every day of our lives, and I have never regretted teaching it to you." He came around the desk and sat opposite her, folding his big hands on the blotter, and for a moment he looked simply old. "But listen to me, child, because I'm going to say this only once, and I am saying it because I respect you and because I have watched you since you were nine years old sweeping shelves. Some trades are made so that worse trades don't happen. You don't need to know what's in those carriages. You need to know that if those carriages stop, something comes out of the north that makes the plague winters look like a mild autumn. The arrangement holds. It has held for forty years. Your job is to hold your part of it, which is to say: your job is to forget this conversation, close the anomaly file, and lose the key to that chest somewhere in the river."

"The wolves know," Juniper said.

Something flickered across Hesse's face — fast, quickly mastered, but she caught it, because she had spent her whole life learning to catch such flickers. Fear. Not of her. Of the sentence.

"The wolves," he repeated, "are animals."

"The wolves are the only ones in this city telling the truth about the weather." She leaned forward. "Master. The frost came to the northern quarter eight days early. The canal ice took a duck in Harvest Month. Whatever the palace is doing with two hundred measures a month, it's doing it *now*, and the arrangement you keep telling me holds is visibly not holding. If I can find the shipments in one night, others can. If the prophecy books are right —"

"Ah." Hesse exhaled through his nose. "So it's the prophecy. The confiscated book. The date circled a century ago." He shook his head slowly. "I wondered when that would reach you. Juniper, prophecies are inventory. Nothing more. People bring us impossible things; sometimes the impossible things are books; we catalogue them and we sell them and we do not build our lives on them. The Long Winter is a story the coast cities tell to raise the price of coal."

"Then show me the final page," Juniper said quietly.

The silence that followed lasted four full breaths. Outside, the wind worried the shutters. Somewhere below, the night clerk coughed in his sleep.

"There is no final page," Hesse said at last. "The book was incomplete when it was confiscated. Damaged. These things happen."

"It wasn't damaged." Juniper heard her own heartbeat. "It was torn. Carefully, along the binding seam, by someone who didn't want the ending read. And this guild doesn't destroy inventory, Master. It sells it. Which means somewhere in forty years of auction records, there is a lot entry for one page of prophecy, and I am going to find it."

Hesse looked at her for a long time. The lamplight made hollows of his eye sockets, and for just a moment — a trick of the flame, surely — he seemed to wear the same expression she had seen on King Aldous at the royal audit: courteous, cold, and blinking far too seldom.

"Juniper," he said softly. "I am going to give you the same advice I gave your mother, in this room, twenty years ago, when she asked me a question about the palace shipments. Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow, audit the candle accounts, which are a disgrace, and leave the winter accounts to me. Some doors in this house open onto cellars. You do not want to live in the cellar."

"What happened to my mother?"

"She listened," Hesse said. "She was promoted twice, and she was happy, and she died old, in her bed, surrounded by people who loved her. Listen to me, child. Being seen is not the same as being safe, and I see you more clearly than you know. Now go home."

Juniper rose. She bowed, because the form required it. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"For the record, Master," she said, "the ledger remembers what men forget. It's carved over your own counting room door. Whatever you're protecting in those carriages — the ledger already knows. It's only waiting for someone to ask it properly."

She heard him stand behind her. She heard him say, very quietly, almost to himself: "Then I pray to every god that froze, girl, that you never learn the right question."

She walked home through streets that smelled of coming snow, in Harvest Month, with the manifest numbers sewn into her sleeve and her heart beating like a trapped bird. Behind her, the guild lamps burned steady and honest over a house built on a lie.

And in her desk drawer, wrapped in wool, the prophecy book lay warm as a living thing — and on its torn last page, in margins only she could see, Sorrel was already writing: *I know where the page went. Child, I know who bought it. Come at moonrise, and bring the wolf.*
