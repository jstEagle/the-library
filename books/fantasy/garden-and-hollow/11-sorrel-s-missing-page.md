## 11. Sorrel's Missing Page

The wolves of the city had a saying: *a scent that fades was never meant to be followed.* Ash had never believed it. He had crossed four hundred miles of frozen ground on the strength of a fading scent, and it had led him to her.

Now, in the third hour past midnight, he crouched in the rafters of the Hollow Guild's archive and watched Juniper Pemberton argue with a dead woman's handwriting.

"You're certain," she said for the fourth time. Not to him. To Sorrel, who hung above the open prophecy book like smoke deciding to be a person — a girl-shaped shimmer with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of old tea. "You're *certain* there was more."

"I wrote it," Sorrel said, and her voice did the thing it always did when she spoke of the writing — went thin and strange, like wind through a keyhole. "Forty-one pages. I bound them myself with thread pulled from my own shroud, because I wanted no one to say a ghost's work came apart. Forty-one pages, ledger-keeper. You are holding forty."

Juniper turned the last leaf over as if the missing page might be hiding behind it. The paper made a sound like a held breath. Ash watched her shoulders from above — watched them set, the way they set whenever the world handed her something broken and expected her to file it.

"Then where is it?" she asked.

"That," said Sorrel, "is an excellent question, and I have had one hundred years to hate it."

* * *

Ash had been in the guild three nights now, sleeping by day in the disused cold-storage cellar beneath the counting room, where the frost never quite left the walls and his wolf-shape raised no eyebrows among the rats. He had learned the building the way he learned all buildings: by smell first, then by sound, then by the grammar of its doors. The Hollow Guild smelled of tallow, dust, old money, and — underneath, faint as a scar — cold. A specific cold. A cold he knew.

He had not yet told Juniper about the cold. He was saving it, the way you save the worst part of a hunt until the quarry is tired.

Tonight she had smuggled him up through the archive's back stair, past the night clerk's dozing desk, because Sorrel had finally agreed to speak while the book was open and the candles were few. Now Ash folded himself into the shadow of the rafters, human-shaped, barefoot, listening.

"Think," Juniper said to the ghost. She had her ledger out — of course she did; the girl kept accounts of everything, including, he suspected, the number of times she'd bitten her lip since sundown. "A hundred years ago. Who handled this book between your death and its confiscation?"

Sorrel drifted a slow circle around the candle flame. "I died before it was finished being dangerous. That was rather the point of me." Her shimmer tightened. "But books have lives after their authors. Mine passed through — let me remember, remembering is the one thing I'm still good at — the house of a chandler who thought it was a recipe book. Then a pawnbroker on Coil Street who knew better. Then—"

She stopped.

"Sorrel?"

"Then the Guild." The ghost's voice dropped to almost nothing. "It came through the Hollow Guild, child. Through your own counters. And it did not leave the way it entered."

Juniper set down her pen very carefully, the way people set down things when they want to throw them. "What does that mean?"

"It means forty pages went into the guild's custody and forty came out of it. Someone in your house tore away my ending and sold it." Sorrel's shape flickered, guttered, steadied. "Someone in your house has been sitting on the last word of the world for sixty years."

* * *

Later — much later, when the candles had burned down to their collars and Sorrel had thinned herself back into the margins to rest — Juniper sat on the archive floor with her back against the shelf and her knees drawn up, and Ash came down out of the rafters to sit beside her.

He liked the floor. Floors were honest. Rafters were for watching; floors were for being beside.

"The guild confiscated it in the spring confiscations," she said, without looking at him. She was talking to the dark, and letting him overhear, which was its own kind of intimacy and she probably didn't know it. "Year sixty-two. There'll be an intake record. Intake records list condition. Torn pages get noted. *Torn pages get priced.*"

"You think someone here sold it."

"I think someone here *catalogued* it." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Do you understand what that means, Ash? It means the answer to all of this — the winter, the crown, whatever I'm supposed to be — it's been in a drawer somewhere in this building, filed under some clerk's tidy little heading, for longer than I've been alive. While I was learning my letters, it was ten feet from me. While I was hiding what I am, it was *here*, waiting, and nobody—"

Her voice cracked. Not much. A hairline fracture, the kind you'd miss if you weren't a creature built entirely out of listening.

Ash waited. Wolves are good at waiting. Waiting is most of what a wolf is.

"I'm angry," she said finally, as though reporting it to a superior. "I don't know where to put it. There's nowhere in the ledgers for it."

"There's room on the roof," Ash said. "That's where I put mine, growing up. My mother said anger is just grief that hasn't found its name yet. Run until it does."

Juniper looked at him then — really looked, the candlelight doing something generous with her face — and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the silence between them was the good kind, the kind with weight and warmth, the kind he had crossed a country to find and hadn't dared to name even inside his own skull.

"We should sleep," she said at last, standing too quickly. "Intake records at dawn. If I pull them during working hours it won't look strange. If *you* pull them—" she gestured at all of him, "—it will look extremely strange."

"Understood," Ash said gravely. "I'll continue being furniture."

"You're terrible furniture. You breathe like a bellows and you shed."

He grinned, and she tried not to, and failed, and that small failure was worth more to him than he could ever put in any ledger.

* * *

Dawn in the archive was a gray, clerical light, the color of unpaid debts. Juniper moved through the intake stacks with the brisk competence of a woman whose whole life was a performance of ordinariness, and Ash — granted, after negotiation, the role of "guildhound, newly acquired, do not pet" — trotted at her heel and catalogued the building's smells.

The intake records lived in the long drawers under the east windows. Juniper found the year sixty-two volume and carried it to the reading table, and Ash flowed up onto the bench beside her, and she pretended not to notice that a dog weighed as much as a man.

"Here." Her finger ran down the columns. "Confiscation batch eleven. *One book, bound, forty leaves, author unknown, subject: divination, assessed value: negligible.* Negligible." She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "They assessed the end of the world at *negligible*."

"Keep reading."

"*Condition note:*" she read, and stopped.

"What does it say?"

Her voice came out flat. "*Condition note: final leaf removed prior to intake. Removed leaf retained separately by order of Master Corvane. See private holdings.*"

The reading room was very quiet. Somewhere below them, the counting room was waking up — the clink of weights, the cough of the junior clerks, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning. Above them, faintly, the air over the closed prophecy book stirred.

"*Corvane,*" Sorrel said, rising out of the binding like frost forming on glass. "That name. I know that name. He was guildmaster in my day too. No — his father. They keep the same name like a coat." Her tea-colored eyes had gone hard. "The private holdings. What are the private holdings?"

Juniper's jaw worked. "There aren't any," she said. "Officially. Every apprentice learns it the first week: *the guild holds nothing privately; all holdings are common to the charter.* It's the founding principle. It's why we're trusted with impossible things — because no one person can hoard them."

"And unofficially?" Ash asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended; the human throat still betrayed him sometimes.

"Unofficially," Juniper said slowly, "there's a rumor. Every guild has one. A sealed room. A vault that isn't on any floor plan. The story goes that each master hands down a key along with the chair, and that what's in the vault is stuff the guild decided the world was safer *not* trading." She stared at the entry. "We were all taught it was a myth. A ghost story for new clerks. But intake records don't lie, Ash. Ledgers don't lie. People lie, and then they write down the truth anyway, because paper is patient and clerks are thorough, and somebody sixty years ago was thorough enough to note exactly where my ending went."

She closed the volume with a snap that made a junior clerk two tables away jump.

"So we find the vault," Ash said.

"Oh, certainly. We'll just stroll into a sealed chamber that six generations of guildmasters have kept off every record, guarded by whatever oaths and wards men like that build around their secrets, and take what we need." She pushed her hair back from her face. In the gray light she looked, for a moment, very young and very tired and absolutely unbending. "And then we'll be dismissed, or arrested, or worse, because beast-speech is treason and consorting with ghosts is worse than treason, it's *embarrassing*, and the guild forgives anything except embarrassment."

"Then we find it carefully."

"Ash." She turned to face him fully, and lowered her voice to the register she used only for truths. "Do you know what's in that page? Sorrel wrote the ending. She wrote what unbinds the crown. Whatever it says — whoever it names — it's been locked in the dark for sixty years by the same institution that's been feeding the palace cold." She was trembling now, finely, like a wire. "Every day we spend being careful is a day closer to the date in that book. Tonight's date. *This week's* date. I can't be careful at that speed."

He reached across the table — slowly, giving her time to refuse — and laid his hand over hers. Her fingers were cold and ink-stained and they did not pull away.

"Then we won't be careful," he said. "We'll be fast, and quiet, and lucky. Those are the only three virtues my pack ever recognized anyway."

Above the table, Sorrel laughed — a dry, rustling sound, like pages turning in the wind. "Oh, I like the wolf," she said. "Ledger-keeper, keep the wolf."

* * *

They spent the day assembling the map of a room that officially did not exist.

Juniper worked it the way she worked everything: laterally, patiently, through the grain of the records. A vault needs air, so there would be a ventilation allowance in the building accounts. A vault needs light, so there would be lamp-oil requisitions that served no listed room. A vault needs a door, and doors, however hidden, must exist in the mason's original measurements — and the mason's original measurements, from the guildhall's construction two centuries ago, lay in the deep archive in a hand-bound folio that no one had opened since before Juniper was born.

Ash's contribution was simpler. He walked the building. All of it. On four feet, nose down, through cellars and crawlspaces and the gap behind the great hearth where the chimney drew strangely, mapping the guildhall the way his kind mapped forests — by what the stone remembered. Twice he felt it: a wrongness in the air, a hush, a cold that was not cellar-cold but *held* cold, the palace-cold, the crown-cold, seeping up through the foundations like water through a grave.

Twice he stood in the dark with his hackles lifted and his heart going like a drum, and twice he chose not to tell her yet. Not because he doubted her courage. Because he knew what fear did to a plan, and because — though he hated himself for it — he wanted one more night of her laughing at his shedding before the world got worse.

By evening they had it. The ventilation allowance pointed to the northwest corner of the undercroft. The lamp-oil pointed the same way. And the mason's folio showed, in faded ink, a staircase in that corner that appeared on no later plan — nine steps down, ending at a wall that was drawn slightly thicker than any other wall in the building.

"Nine steps," Juniper whispered, tracing it. "Nine steps down to a door that isn't there."

"And a lock," Ash said, "that six masters have kept keys to."

They stood together in the gathering dusk of the archive, the prophecy book closed between them, the ghost drowsing in its margins, and Ash watched Juniper Pemberton make the decision he already knew she'd make — saw it arrive in her the way spring arrives, not all at once but everywhere at once.

"Tomorrow," she said. "During the audit recess, when the counting room empties. Nine steps, one impossible door, and sixty years of secrets." She looked up at him, and there was fear in her face, and under the fear something brighter and harder that fear had no power over. "Whatever's down there, Ash — whoever bought my page, whatever they were afraid of — it ends tomorrow."

Far off across the city, beyond the rooftops, beyond the walls, the northern sky was doing something no harvest-month sky should do. Neither of them could see it from the archive. But Ash's blood could feel it, the way a river feels the sea: the winter coming south, early and wrong, and gaining.

Tomorrow, then. He only hoped the world would give them the whole of it.
