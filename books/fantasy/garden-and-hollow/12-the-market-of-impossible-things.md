## 12. The Market of Impossible Things

The invitation came the way all impossible things came to Juniper Pemberton: through the ledgers.

She found it on a Tuesday evening, tucked between an entry for *sixty feet of river, bottled* and a receipt for a debt paid in years—three of them, deducted from a cooper's life and credited to a widow's account. The paper was black, the ink silver, and it had no business being in her ledger at all. Someone had put it there deliberately.

*You are invited to witness the Exchange of Rare Goods,* it read. *Midnight. Beneath the Guildhall. Bring nothing you cannot lose.*

Juniper sat very still in her alcove, lamplight pooling around her, and read the card three times. Then she did what she always did with dangerous information: she filed it.

But filing was not forgetting, and by the time she climbed to her narrow room above the counting-house, the card was burning a hole through her thoughts like a coal wrapped in silk. *Beneath the Guildhall.* She had worked in the Hollow Guild for nine years. She knew its cellars—the wine stores, the salt vaults, the deep archive where confiscated goods slept in numbered crates. She had never once heard of anything beneath *those*. Which meant either the card was a lie, or the guild had a floor she didn't know about.

And if the guild had a hidden floor, then it might have a hidden market. And if it had a hidden market—

Then Sorrel's missing page might be there. Sold decades ago through guild channels, torn from the prophecy like a tongue from a mouth. Somewhere in the dark under her own feet, someone had bought the ending of the world, or at least the instructions for preventing it.

Juniper dressed in her plainest clothes—gray wool, no guild colors—and slid the black card into her bodice, over her heart, where it felt less like paper and more like a key.

* * *

Finding the way down took her until midnight exactly.

There was a door in the salt vault that wasn't a door—a section of wall whose stones were fitted too perfectly, without mortar, the kind of craftsmanship that existed only when someone wanted a seam invisible. Juniper had audited the vault inventory twice; she knew the wall's dimensions matched the cellar's outer measurement to within a hand's width. A gap. A swallowed room.

She pressed her palm flat against the coldest stone and whispered, in the old speech, the word for *open*—not because she thought the wall understood her, but because saying it steadied her hands. The stone did not move. But somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat through floorboards, she heard music.

So she went down instead—down the archive stair, past the numbered crates, past the crate marked with the sigil she'd seen circled beside tonight's date a century ago, and there, behind the crate, a hatch in the floor, iron-ringed, warm to the touch despite the cellar cold. Warm, as if something beneath were breathing heat upward. Or breathing at all.

She lifted the hatch. Stairlight rose to meet her: gold and green and the color of candleflames seen through wine.

Juniper Pemberton, keeper of ledgers, invisible woman, descended into the Market of Impossible Things.

* * *

It was not what she expected, which was chaos. It was worse. It was orderly.

The cavern beneath the Guildhall was vast and ribbed with ancient brick, and it had been furnished like a counting-house dreamed by a drunk architect: long tables draped in velvet, brass lamps on articulated arms, clerks with ink-stained fingers seated at little desks taking bids in perfect ledger-hand. There were perhaps two hundred people, and every one of them wore a mask—not festival masks but plain white ovals, featureless, so that the crowd looked like a field of eggs, of skulls, of blank pages waiting to be written on.

At the center of the cavern, on a dais, stood the auction block. And on the auction block, under a glass bell jar, floated a small grey cloud shaped like a scream.

"Lot forty-one," announced a voice from everywhere and nowhere, smooth as tallow. "One echo, bottled, provenance disputed. It is said to be the last word of a man who died owing money. The word is not repeatable. Bidding opens at forty crowns."

A clerk raised a paddle. Another. The echo screamed silently inside its jar, and Juniper felt her stomach turn, because she had entered that echo into the official ledger herself, years ago, catalogued as *recovered property, pending valuation*. She had never asked what happened to recovered property after valuation.

*Of course you didn't ask,* she told herself bitterly. *Asking is how people get noticed.*

She pulled her hood lower. She had no mask; she would have to rely on being unremarkable, which was the one art she had mastered. She moved along the edge of the cavern, past tables where the impossible was laid out like produce:

A jar of last summer, sealed with wax, the light inside it golden and thick as honey. A knife that cut debts instead of flesh—she could tell because the merchant demonstrating it sliced the air toward a debtor's contract and half the parchment crumbled to ash. A birdcage containing, not a bird, but a small wind that sang. Vials of courage, vials of patience, vials labeled simply *Tuesday*.

And at the far table, guarded by two masked figures who stood too still to be entirely alive, a single shallow dish of water. Floating on the water: a scrap of parchment, torn raggedly along one edge.

Juniper's heart stopped, stumbled, restarted.

She drifted closer, wearing her best expression of bored appraisal, the one she used when auditing shipments she already knew were fraudulent. The parchment was old, foxed at the corners, written in the same cramped brown ink as the prophecy upstairs. Even from six feet away she could see the first line:

*...and the unbinding shall be wrought by—*

The rest was turned face-down. Of course it was. The Market did not sell endings. It sold beginnings, and let you bleed for the rest.

"How much?" she asked the nearer guard.

The guard's mask tilted. Behind the blank oval, something breathed. "Not coin," it said, in a voice like frost forming. "The Market trades in weight. What will you give?"

"I have—" She stopped. She had eleven crowns sewn into her hem, a gift of beast-speech worth hanging, and a rented room with a chipped cup in it. "I have little."

"Everyone has little," the guard said. "Little is not the same as cheap." It gestured, one gray finger, toward the far end of the cavern, where a smaller archway opened onto a second room lit in blue. Above the arch, in letters of tarnished silver: *THE TELLER OF WEIGHTS.*

"The page's price is set," the guard said. "You may learn it inside. Whether you can pay is another matter."

* * *

The Teller of Weights was an old woman at a scale.

That was all—no grandeur, no horror, just a small round woman with kind eyes and a balance scale of black wood, seated at a table in the blue-lit room, with a single empty chair before her. But when Juniper looked closer at the scale, her skin crawled. In one pan lay a pebble-sized lump of darkness that was somehow heavier than the iron counterweight in the other pan, bending the whole apparatus toward itself like grief bends a spine.

"Sit, child," the Teller said. Her voice was warm. That was the worst part. "You want the torn page. Everyone wants something tonight. Sit, sit."

Juniper sat. The chair was warm too. Everything down here was warm, as though the Market fed on body heat, on the warmth people brought down and never carried back up.

"The price of the page," the Teller said, folding her hands, "is one memory of sufficient weight. Not any memory. Weight must match want. The page holds the end of a hundred-year prophecy—the end of winter itself, some say. Light things won't balance it. Your name won't do. Your childhood dog won't do." She smiled gently. "You'll need to give me something heavy."

"Heavy how?"

"Heavy the way stones are heavy. Heavy the way years are heavy." The Teller leaned forward. "Memories are the only currency we accept, child, because memories are the only thing people truly own. Coin can be stolen. Land can be taken. But what lives in your skull is yours alone—until you choose to spend it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk up those stairs, and the world ends this winter, and you keep your memory to warm yourself with while everything freezes." The Teller shrugged, motherly. "Or you pay, and save the world, and forget why it mattered. Every customer faces the same arithmetic. Most of them run. You haven't run yet. That interests me."

Juniper's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees.

She thought of Sorrel, flickering in the margin of the book, a century patient and a century afraid. She thought of the wolves on the roofline saying *wrong, wrong, early and wrong*. She thought of Ash—of gray eyes in a human face, of the way he'd looked at her across the frozen garden as if recognition were a wound and a gift at once. She thought of King Aldous smiling wrong, blinking too seldom, a hollow man wearing a kingdom like a coat.

And underneath all of it, rising like groundwater, she thought of her mother.

Her mother's voice. Low and roughened from years of mill-work, humming while she kneaded bread, singing off-key on purpose to make Juniper laugh. Saying *my little wren* in the dark when the fevers came. Saying, once, on the last morning, *you'll keep careful accounts of everything, won't you, love? So nothing gets lost.*

Nine years dead. And the only thing left of her that lived anywhere outside Juniper's own head was that voice—worn smooth by a thousand repetitions in memory, played back on bad nights, on good nights, on every night that needed getting through.

"There it is," the Teller said softly. "I can always see it when they find it. The heavy thing." She reached out and turned the scale's pans upright, empty, waiting. "Lay it on the pan, child. Just think of it. Hold it in your mind and lean toward the scale. The rest is my work."

"If I give it," Juniper heard herself say, "will I know I gave it? Will there be a hole?"

"A hole, yes. A shape of a hole. You'll know something was there the way you know a tooth was pulled—you'll keep reaching for it." The Teller's kind eyes grew, briefly, bottomless. "But you won't miss what you can't remember. That's the mercy of it. That's also the crime of it. Both are true. Everything down here is both true."

Juniper stood up. Sat back down. Stood again.

"You could walk away," the Teller said, not unkindly. "Most do. The world ends anyway; why should you be the one to bleed? Let some hero pay. There's always a hero."

"No," Juniper said quietly. "There isn't. I've read the ledgers. Heroes are just people whose sacrifices got recorded." She thought of her own neat columns, her invisible life, her pride in being careful and unseen. Nine years of keeping accounts for other people's impossible things. "Someone has to enter this into the record. It might as well be me."

She closed her eyes.

She built her mother's voice out of memory, plank by plank: the mill-grit rasp underneath the melody. The hum, rising and falling, the bread-song, the one about the miller's daughter and the moon. The fever-night whisper. *My little wren.* The last morning, clear as struck glass: *keep careful accounts, love. So nothing gets lost.*

*I'm sorry, Mama,* she thought, leaning toward the scale. *This is the account. This is what it cost. I'm keeping it as carefully as I can.*

The pan filled with weight she could not see. The black wood groaned. Across the scale, the counterweights rose, rose, rose—iron climbing as if ashamed—and the Teller made a small sound, almost a sob, almost a prayer.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, that's heavy. That's heavy enough to buy a kingdom's morning."

Something lifted out of Juniper.

There was no pain. That was the obscene part. It felt like exhaling after holding breath for nine years—like setting down a load she'd forgotten she carried. Warmth drained out through her sternum, through her palms, into the pan, and the scale balanced, and balanced, and then the Teller closed her hands over the emptiness in the pan like a woman catching a firefly, and it was done.

Juniper opened her eyes.

The blue room was the same. The Teller was the same. But somewhere inside her chest there was now a room with the door bricked over, and she could not have said what had been kept there. She only knew that her cheeks were wet, and she was grieving something enormous, and she did not know its name.

"What did I give you?" she whispered.

"That's not how it works, child." The Teller was wrapping the emptiness in black cloth with great tenderness, like a baker handling dough. "Ask me tomorrow and I couldn't tell you. Ask me tonight—" she paused, and for one moment her kindness cracked and showed something older and sadder beneath, "—tonight I'll say only this: whoever gave you that voice loved you very much. It rang all the way down here."

*My little wren.* The words surfaced and sank again, meaning nothing, meaning everything, a hook with no fish.

Juniper wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "The page," she said. Her voice sounded scraped. "Our trade is done. Give me the page."

* * *

The guard at the far table lifted the glass cloche from the dish of water and drew out the parchment scrap with tweezers, blotting it dry with reverence. He held it out to her.

"One moment," he said. "Market law. Before delivery: the seller speaks the warning."

Behind him, the Teller had followed, and now she stood with her black-wrapped bundle clutched to her breast, and she recited, in a voice gone formal and ancient:

"*What is bought in the Hollow cannot be returned. What is paid cannot be reclaimed. The buyer walks out owning less than she came with, and knowing less than she owed. May her accounts balance in the end.*"

"May they balance," murmured two hundred masked heads, all together, all at once, and Juniper's skin crawled from crown to heel.

She took the page.

It was lighter than it should have been, the way everything down here was wrong—warm stone, silent screams, grief folded into parcels. She turned it over with fingers that wanted to shake and did not quite manage it. Brown ink. Cramped, urgent, a century old. The torn edge fit, she knew without testing, the wound in the prophecy book upstairs.

She read.

*...and the unbinding shall be wrought by the ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue, who shall pour forth a memory of equal weight to a king's whole reign—for the crown was bought with a kingdom, and only a kingdom's price can unbuy it.*

Juniper read it twice. Three times. The words did not change. The words were worse each time.

*A memory equal to a king's whole reign.* She had just spent her mother's voice—one voice, one life, one small warm human love—and the ledger of the world called that insufficient. Whatever the rite required, it would cost more than this. It would cost more than she could imagine, and she had just learned precisely how much she could imagine losing.

"Well?" the guard asked. "Is the price fair?"

"It's never fair," Juniper said. "That's what makes it a price."

She tucked the page into her bodice, next to the space where the black invitation had been, next to the bricked-over room where her mother's voice used to live. Then she walked up the stairs, out of the warmth, into the cold cellar air, and stood a long time in the dark among the numbered crates, listening to her own heart ask a question she could not answer.

Above her, muffled through floors of stone, the city bells rang three in the morning. First snow was coming—she could smell it now, even underground, that mineral hush on the wind. Days, perhaps. Hours.

And somewhere in the palace, a king who did not blink enough was waiting for his winter.

Juniper pressed her hand flat against her sternum, over the hole with its perfect shape, and began, slowly, to climb toward the light.
