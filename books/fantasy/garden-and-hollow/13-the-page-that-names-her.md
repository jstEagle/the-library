## 13. The Page That Names Her

### JUNIPER

The page was heavier than paper had any right to be.

Juniper felt it through her coat, pressed against her ribs where she'd tucked it after the auction house burned down around their escape—a story she would tell later, or perhaps never. For now there was only the walk back through the sleeping city, Ash a grey shadow at her heel, and the weight of the thing against her heart like a second heartbeat.

She did not open it on the street. She did not open it in the alley behind the guildhall, or on the stairs, or in the corridor outside her room where the candle stubs guttered in their iron brackets. She waited until the door was locked, the shutters drawn, the wolf curled beneath her window like a promise, and only then—hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her skirts—did she draw the page out into the light.

It was old. Older than the book it had been torn from; the edges were browned to the color of tea leaves, and the ink had gone that particular rust-brown of iron gall left too long against damp air. Someone had handled it often before it was sold. The creases were soft with use, worn glossy at the folds, as if a hundred years ago someone had read these words over and over, looking for something.

*Looking for me,* she thought, and hated herself for the thought, and could not stop thinking it.

The script was Sorrel's. She knew it now—the sharp ascenders, the little hooks on the letter *g* like talons. She had spent three weeks reading the ghost's handwriting in margins and footnotes, and here it was again, whole, unbroken, filling the page top to bottom in that tight, urgent hand of someone writing against a deadline they would not live to meet.

She read it once quickly, the way you'd snatch your hand from a stove. Then she made herself read it slowly, ledger-keeper's discipline, word by word, because ledgers do not care whether you are afraid.

*...and when the crown has eaten its fill of the king,* the page said, *it will turn to the kingdom, and the kingdom will not know winter from grief. There is one binding that holds it: the rite of equal weight. A reign's worth of memory, freely given, poured into the cold.*

*The hand that pours must be a ledger-keeper's hand—one who has counted impossible things and not flinched. The tongue must be the beasts' tongue, for the cold cannot abide what speaks in voices older than crowns. And the heart must be whole enough to break entirely, for half a sacrifice buys nothing.*

*A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue. She will come when the snow comes wrong. I have written her into this page so that she will know, when she finds it, that she was never an accident.*

Juniper set the page down on her desk with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something venomous.

Then she sat very still for a long time.

The candle hissed. Somewhere below, the guildhall settled into its nighttime creaks and groans, the familiar music of the building she had lived inside for eleven years, whose every corridor she knew the way she knew her own hands. Except it wasn't familiar anymore, was it. It hadn't been since she found the altered entries, since Hesse's face went smooth and closed when she asked about shipments of *cold*. The guild she had built her invisible, careful life inside was a house with rot in its beams, and now this page wanted to make her the axe.

*A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue.*

There could not be two. There could not plausibly be one. She thought of all the clerks she'd worked beside—Maren who sneezed at dust, Oskar who cheated at cards, the night-auditors with their ink-stained cuffs—and none of them, not one, had ever paused mid-inventory because a rat wanted to complain about the cats. None of them heard the horses in the delivery yard gossiping about their drivers. None of them had spent their whole life hiding the one true thing about themselves behind columns of neat figures.

She had been written into a dead woman's book a century before she was born.

And the worst part—the part that made her throat close—was the line about knowing she was never an accident. Because she had spent eleven years being an accident. Being careful, being quiet, being nothing anyone had planned for. That invisibility had been her armor and her pride. This page took both away in four lines of brown ink. She was not hidden. She had never been hidden. She had been *expected*, the way a trap is expected, sitting patient in the dark with its jaws oiled and waiting.

A knock. Soft, deliberate—three taps, pause, two taps. Their signal.

She opened the door and Ash slipped in, and even mid-crisis some traitorous part of her noted how he filled the small room, how the candlelight moved on him like water. He wore his human shape tonight, all long limbs and watchful amber eyes, hair the color of wet slate falling past his jaw. He had dressed the shape in stolen clothes badly; the collar gaped; he was beautiful in the way weather is beautiful, indifferent to opinion.

"You haven't slept," he said.

"Neither have you."

"Wolves sleep with one ear open." He nodded at the desk. "Is that it?"

She picked up the page instead of answering, which was answer enough, and held it out to him. He took it with a strange reverence, the way he touched most old things—as if they might remember him.

He read. She watched him read. His face did the thing it always did, going stiller and stiller, the human expression draining off it until what remained was the wolf underneath, all attention and no performance.

When he finished, he didn't speak for a moment. Then: "She wrote you."

"She wrote *a* ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue."

"Juniper." He said her name the way he always did, carefully, like a word in a language he was still learning. "How many ledger-keepers do you know who talk to rats?"

"One." Her voice came out flat. "One, and she's standing in a locked room holding the page that says she's a weapon somebody forged a hundred years ago."

"That's not what it says."

"It says I was *planned*. It says a ghost I've known for three weeks sat down at a desk before my grandmother's grandmother was born and decided what I would be." The words came faster now, the fear finally finding its voice. "I have spent my entire life making sure no one decided anything about me. My gift was mine. My ledgers were mine. Even Hesse doesn't own me—he just thinks he does. And now—" She stopped, pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Now I find out I'm a sentence in someone else's book."

Ash set the page down. He crossed the small room in two steps, and then he did the thing he almost never did in this shape: he reached out and took her hands, pulling them gently from her face, and held them between his own. His palms were warm. That always surprised her. Everything about him looked like winter and everything about him ran hot, like a banked fire.

"Sorrel wrote a prophecy," he said quietly. "Prophecies are just predictions wearing better clothes. You're still the one who chooses."

"Do I get to choose *no*?"

He was silent. Honest silence—she could hear him deciding not to lie to her, could nearly see the false comforts lined up and rejected one by one. *Yes, of course. No one can make you.* All lies. They both knew it.

"No," he said at last. "Not really. If the snow comes wrong and the crown eats the king and the kingdom freezes, and you stood aside knowing you could have—" He shook his head. "You wouldn't stand aside. That's not a prophecy. That's just who you are. She saw who you'd be. She didn't make it."

"And if who I am isn't enough? It says the heart must be whole enough to break *entirely*, Ash. Entirely. What does that mean? What does it cost?" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I already paid it a memory of my mother's voice. What's the next installment?"

He didn't answer. He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—or could read too well—and something in it frightened her more than the page did. Something that looked like a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"Ash," she said. "What are you thinking?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "I'm thinking we should show this to Sorrel. Confirm it's genuine. Forged pages sell well at auctions—even ones that burn down."

It was deflection and they both knew it, but she let him have it, because her hands were still shaking and because his were warm around them, and because there would be time—there had to be time—to pull the truth out of him later.

Outside, far to the north, thunder rolled across a sky that should have been clear autumn. But the thunder was wrong. It had no rain in it. It sounded, if anything, like ice cracking.

### ASH

The packs had a saying: *the trail tells you what the prey will not.* Ash had learned it as a cub, nose in the frost, learning to read ground the way Juniper read ledgers. Every creature left a record. Every path was a confession.

Sorrel's page was a trail. And it told him things its writer had never intended.

He sat on the guild roof while Juniper slept—truly slept, finally, exhaustion dragging her under around the fourth hour—and let the night wind carry the city's smells to him. Woodsmoke. Horse. The river's mud-and-iron. And underneath it all, growing stronger every night, a smell that had no business in harvest month: deep cold, mineral and old, like the air off a glacier's tongue. Like the inside of the mountain dens where the eldest wolves went to die.

The crown. He could smell the crown from here, faint but unmistakable, seeping out of the palace district like smoke through floorboards. It was getting hungrier. He'd known hunger like that once, in a hard winter, watching a starving pack circle a dying elk—not malice, exactly. Just appetite so total it erased everything else, including the question of whether the thing being eaten had ever been a person.

King Aldous had been a person. Probably. Once.

Below him, through the shutter slats, he could see the candle in Juniper's window go out. He listened to her breathing slow. He had been doing this every night for two weeks—sitting sentinel over a woman who did not need a sentinel and had never asked for one—and he was honest enough with himself to admit it was not duty. Duty was what he'd told the elders. What it actually was had no name in any language he spoke, wolf or human, though the packs had certainly named it, in their contemptuous way, when they summoned him home.

*Come south to investigate,* they'd said. *Report what you find.* They had not said: *and if you find yourself lying down beside a human's door at night like a hound, we will cut you from the pack-lines so cleanly your name won't scar.*

He flexed his hands. In this shape they were clumsy things, blunt-toed, poor at smelling, worse at hearing—but they could hold things. They could hold a page. They could hold her hands. He kept choosing the shape anyway. He suspected that choice was also a confession.

The page lay on the roof beside him where he'd carried it up to read again without her watching. He smoothed it flat against the slate tiles and read it a third time, and this time he read it the way a wolf reads a trail—not the obvious marks, but what lay between them.

*A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue. She will come when the snow comes wrong.*

Fine. True. He believed it; Sorrel's ghost had the specific gravity of truth, the way a scent has a source.

But Ash had grown up in a culture where stories were survival tools, and survival tools got examined for flaws. And there was a flaw here, a gap in the trail, and it was shaped like the rest of the page. Sorrel had written pages and pages about the crown, the king, the rite, the unbinding. She had specified the instrument down to the tongue in its mouth. And nowhere—not in the recovered page, not in the book's surviving chapters, not in a single marginal scrawl—had she written what happened to the instrument afterward.

Rites of equal weight required payment. He understood payment. He understood debts that outlived the debtor. And a memory heavy enough to balance a king's entire reign—

His stomach turned over, slow and cold.

She hadn't asked yet. Juniper hadn't done the arithmetic. She was brilliant with numbers, with ledgers, with other people's accounts—but people were constitutionally incapable of auditing themselves, and she was looking at the rite the way she looked at any entry: as a problem to solve, not a price to pay. *The heart must be whole enough to break entirely.* She'd read that aloud and shuddered at the drama of it, and Ash had watched her miss the meaning entirely, and he had said nothing, because saying it would make it real, and because some cowardly animal part of him hoped the page meant something gentler than what it meant.

It didn't. He knew what it meant. He'd known since the auction house, when she'd traded her mother's voice like a coin and walked out with her spine straight and her eyes wet, and he'd understood then that she was a person who paid her debts in full, always, even when the debt was robbery.

If the rite needed a reign's worth of memory, she would give it. She would stand in front of the crown and pour herself out and not flinch, because she was written that way, because Sorrel had seen truly, because that was simply who she was.

And what she would pour out—what weighed the most, what was heaviest and newest and most ferociously hers—

He looked down at the dark window. At the sleeping woman inside it.

*No,* he thought. Not a prayer; he didn't pray. A decision, laid down like a scent-mark, like a border. Whatever the rite took, it would not take that. He would find another way or make one. He would stand between her and the arithmetic if he had to, and if the price came due anyway—if the cold demanded exactly what she chose to give—then he would spend whatever remained of himself making sure she never woke up hollow.

The packs would call it madness. Let them. He'd already chosen exile in his heart; this was only the second clause of the same contract.

Wind came off the north roofs and brought the glacier-smell with it, stronger tonight. He lifted his muzzle—it was easier to think clearly in the other shape—and tasted the air properly for the first time that night.

And went rigid.

Under the woodsmoke and river-mud and crown-cold, there was something new. Something moving through the city streets in the small hours, leaving a trail that smelled of iron filings and lamp-oil and a wrongness he'd smelled only once before: in the palace wardens' barracks, on the men who guarded the king's cold rooms.

Wardens. In the merchant quarter. At the third hour past midnight. Moving toward the guildhall.

He was through the window before the thought finished forming, human shape shedding off him like a coat, four paws silent on the stairs.

### JUNIPER

She woke to a wolf standing on her bed.

Not climbing onto it—standing on it, all four feet planted precisely on the quilt as if the bed were territory to be claimed, amber eyes level with hers, close enough that his breath stirred her hair. Every instinct she owned fired at once, and then recognition arrived a half-second behind, and her scream died as a strangled squeak.

"*Ash*—"

He nudged her shoulder, hard, insistent, then leapt down and paced to the door and back, the whole body speaking urgency in a grammar she was still learning. *Up. Now. Danger.*

"What time is it—" She fumbled for the candle, struck light, and froze.

Under the door, along the gap where the corridor floor showed, shadows were moving. Many shadows. Moving slowly, in step, the way soldiers move. And beneath the smell of tallow and old wood that always haunted the guildhall's night hours, she caught it: iron filings. Lamp-oil. Cold.

Wardens. In the guildhall. Past midnight.

Her mind, trained by eleven years of audits, did the terrible arithmetic instantly. Wardens did not visit the Hollow Guild at night for routine matters. Wardens visited at night for *her*—for the informer she'd bought with a ledger favor, the one whose silence had a price tag she'd foolishly assumed was fixed. Or for the altered entries. Or for all of it at once, every compromise coming due in a single collection.

Or—for the page.

She snatched it from the desk. Where? Not her coat—they'd search her room first, obviously, she was the beast-speaker, the anomaly, the clerk whose file Hesse kept in his own drawer. Under the floorboard with the loose nail? No time. The window, then—she wrenched the shutter open and cold air flooded in, harvest-month air that smelled faintly, horribly, of the far north.

"The roof," she whispered.

Ash was already at the sill. She climbed onto the desk, onto the sill, felt his teeth close gently on the back of her collar like a mother cat—undignified, infuriating, and the only reason she didn't fall—as he guided her up the drainpipe and onto the wet slate. Below, through the window they'd left open, she heard her door splinter inward. Boots. Voices, low and official.

They lay flat against the roof slope, side by side, breath fogging. Below them the guildhall's courtyard lanterns bloomed with torchlight, and figures moved in it—six, eight, wardens in their grey lacquered coats, and behind them, wrapped in a fur-trimmed cloak that did nothing to hide his stoop, Guildmaster Hesse, barefoot on his own cobblestones, hands raised in placation.

She couldn't hear the words. She didn't need to. She watched Hesse's posture—the bowed shoulders, the open palms—and read the entry the way she'd read ten thousand entries: *cooperation. Full disclosure. No resistance offered.*

"He sold me," she breathed. "He didn't even wait for them to ask."

Beside her, Ash made a sound low in his chest, not quite a growl. His eyes weren't on Hesse. They were tracking the wardens' formation, counting exits, doing wolf-arithmetic. Two at the gate. Three sweeping the east wing—her wing. The rest at the doors.

"They'll find the room empty," she whispered. "They'll know I ran. Ash, the page names me—if they take it to the crown—"

He looked at her then, and in the torchlight from below his eyes caught orange, and she saw in them the same thing she'd seen in Sorrel's ink: a plan older than the moment, waiting for her to catch up.

*Not the page,* his posture said. *You. Get you out. The page is bait we leave in the trap.*

"I'm not leaving it—"

His growl, barely audible, was pure disagreement.

And that was when the shouting started below—not at the guildhall, but beyond it, from the direction of the river. Torchlight bloomed there too, a second bloom, a third. Bells began, ragged and unsynchronized, church by church, the way bells ring when no one has ordered them to and everyone is frightened.

Juniper pushed up on her elbows and looked north, over the rooftops, toward the palace hill.

The sky above it was glowing. Not firelight—colder than firelight, a white-blue luminescence, auroral, wrong, pouring up from behind the palace walls like light through a cracked door. And down the hill, spilling through the streets, she could see it coming: frost. Visible even at this distance, a glittering tide crawling across the cobbles, climbing the walls, sheathing the bell-towers in white. The Long Winter, arriving early, arriving *fast*, exactly as a century-old page had promised.

The wardens below had stopped searching. They stood in the courtyard staring north like everyone else, and one of them dropped his torch, and the torch went out in the sudden frost with a hiss she could hear from the roof.

Snow began to fall. In harvest month. Fat, dry flakes, each one catching the auroral glow, falling straight down through windless air, as if the sky itself had been commanded.

Juniper clutched the page to her chest and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the date circled a hundred years ago had never been a warning.

It had been a schedule.

"We have days," she said. "Maybe hours." She looked at Ash, and Ash looked at her, and somewhere below Hesse's voice rose thin and pleading over the bells—begging the wardens, or begging the sky, she couldn't tell.

Ash stood, snow gathering on his shoulders, and pointed his muzzle north, toward the palace, toward the cold-light, toward the thing wearing a king.

Then he looked back at her, and waited. It was, she realized, the first time in her life anyone had ever waited for her decision instead of deciding for her—not the guild, not Hesse, not even a ghost with a pen.

Chosen by prophecy. But chosen by *him*, freely, right now, in the snow.

"North," she said.
