## 14. What the Crown Wants

*Cold today. Cold yesterday. Was there ever a day that was not cold?*

The king wakes in his own bed and does not know the ceiling.

It is a fine ceiling—carved oak, painted with stars by a dead craftsman whose name Aldous once knew, whose name he is almost sure he knew—and he lies beneath it in a body that feels borrowed, counting the stars to see if they are still the same stars as last night. Seven hundred and forty. Or was it seven hundred and twelve? There used to be a constellation shaped like a heron over the western arch. He looks for the heron.

The heron is gone.

*You never liked the heron,* says something at the base of his skull, in a voice like frost forming on glass, patient and crystalline and utterly without breath. *I took it.*

He does not remember agreeing to that. He does not remember much of anything before this morning, and this morning is already going grey at the edges, softening like snow left too long in a warm hand. He sits up. His hands are steady. That is the first wrong thing—he remembers, distantly, the way you remember a story someone told you, that his hands used to shake before audiences. Now they are perfectly still, always, like things carved from ice.

"Good morning, Your Majesty," says the chamberlain, who is not really a chamberlain but a warden wearing a chamberlain's face, one of the crown's own, its fingers too many-jointed inside their gloves. "You slept well."

"I dreamed," Aldous begins, and stops, because he cannot remember what he dreamed, only that somewhere in it a man was screaming, and the screaming had stopped only when—

When what?

*When I let him stop,* says the voice at the base of his skull, kindly. *Eat your breakfast.*

* * *

There is a man inside him.

Aldous knows this the way a house knows, in its bones, that there is water in the walls. He feels the other one sometimes—a pressure behind his eyes, a fist beating on the far side of a door, fainter every year, fainter every month, and some mornings Aldous cannot tell whether the fist is real or whether it is only his own heart, knocking to be let out of a chest that no longer belongs to it.

His name was Aldous too. That is the joke of it, if there is a joke. The crown did not bring a stranger; it wore what was already there. It slid into the shape of a young king the way water slides into a footprint, filling every hollow of him, learning his smile from the inside until the smile fit so well that no one—not the court, not the priests, not the queen who died confused and grieving for a husband who still sat beside her—no one could say where the man ended and the winter began.

Aldous—the thing that walks and signs decrees and dines on soup it cannot taste—remembers being made. Not born. Made. He remembers the foundry under the mountain, though those memories are not his; they belong to the crown, and the crown lends them to him the way a master lends a servant his coat. He remembers the god. He remembers the fingerbone, white as the heart of a glacier, singing when they quenched it, a sound like a thousand doors closing. He remembers the smiths weeping without knowing why, and the old sorcerer saying, *it will hold back the winter*, and the young king—*their* king, the true one, the one with the shaking hands—lifting it toward his own head with such hope on his face that even now, even wearing him, even having eaten most of him, Aldous can feel that hope like a coal swallowed whole, still burning somewhere down in the dark of what remains.

*It will hold back the winter.*

It holds everything now. The winter was only the beginning. The crown learned quickly that cold is not merely a temperature. Cold is an appetite. And memory—memory is the warmest thing there is.

* * *

In the third hour after dawn, the king holds audience.

He is good at this. He has had a century of practice, and the crown is very good at wearing people, and between them they have perfected a king so plausible that history will never question him. He listens to petitions about grain tariffs and bridge tolls. He nods at the correct moments. He smiles—there, the court relaxes, the smile is right, the smile is *him*—and when the ambassador from the southern coast presents a gift of candied figs, Aldous hears himself say, warmly, "My favorite," and watches the man glow with pleasure at having pleased his king.

Aldous has never tasted a fig. He is not certain he has ever tasted anything. But the memory of liking figs belonged to the true king, and the crown keeps such things the way a miser keeps coins: not to spend, only to count.

Beneath the smile, behind the door, the fist beats.

*They think you are me,* the fist says, or seems to say, or perhaps Aldous only imagines it says because imagining it is the last freedom he owns. *They bow to you and they are bowing to my grave.*

"There, there," murmurs the crown, in the privacy of the king's own skull, as the audience ends and the courtiers file out. "Jealousy is so small of you."

"You ate him," Aldous says. He does not know he is going to speak until the words are already out, low, under his breath, addressed to no one visible. In the emptying hall a page glances up, frowns, decides the king was clearing his throat. "You are eating him still. I can feel him going. Like snow going into a river."

"And yet here you are," the crown says, reasonable as arithmetic, "complaining, in his voice, with his mouth. Tell me, majesty—if I have eaten him, what are you?"

And that is the question, isn't it. That is the question he wakes with every morning and goes to sleep failing to answer every night. Because the crown needs a king the way a glove needs a hand, and it has kept him—kept *something*—all these years, some residue, some rind of the man, enough to move the lips and sign the name and sit the throne with a living weight upon it. Whether that residue is the true Aldous diminished, or a copy the crown maintains for its own convenience, or simply the part of the original that agreed—agreed, all those years ago, in the foundry dark, when the crown whispered *let me carry your burdens* and a tired young king, orphaned by war, said *yes*—whether any of these is true, Aldous cannot know. The crown keeps his memories the way a jailer keeps keys.

Some nights he finds one lying around unattended. A memory out of its cell. Last week it was a garden: a woman laughing, dirt on her hands, planting something pale and sweet-smelling, and the true king kneeling beside her in the loam, happy, so simply happy, and Aldous had stood in the middle of his own bedchamber at midnight holding that memory like a candle, weeping tears he did not know he could still make, until the crown noticed the leak and sealed it, and the garden went out, and he stood in the dark not knowing why his face was wet.

"What am I?" he asks the empty hall now.

"Mine," says the crown.

* * *

The hunger comes at noon.

It always comes at noon, when the sun is highest and furthest from the throne, and the crown grows restless in its silver circlet of hair, and Aldous feels it turn its attention outward the way a wolf turns its head toward a treeline. Today it finds the city. Today it finds the city *warm*.

He stands at the window of the high gallery, hands folded behind his back in the posture the portraitists love, and looks down over Vane's Crossing—no, that is not the city's name, the city's name is—*the city's name is*—

The name slips. For three full seconds the capital of the kingdom has no name, just a shape below him, rooftops and smoke, and then the crown supplies it, generously, the way it supplies everything, and Aldous hates it a little more for the kindness.

Below, the city moves. Carts, crowds, a market roaring with autumn noise, children running under the aqueduct with sticks for swords. Warm. All of it warm. Ten thousand hearths, ten thousand little fires, and every fire a wound in the crown's side, every laugh a small blasphemy.

*I want it,* the crown says, and there is nothing reasonable in its voice now, nothing arithmetical. This is the other voice, the deep one, the one from the foundry, the one that sang through the fingerbone when the god's cold entered the metal. *I want all of it. Every hearth. Every name. I want the summer out of the ground. I want the green gone gray. I want them to forget the word for warm.*

"You promised," Aldous says. His reflection in the windowglass says it with him, lips moving together, and he cannot tell which of them is speaking—the man or the mirror. "At the forging. You said you would hold the winter *back*. You were made to be a wall."

"I was made from a god," the crown says, "who was killed by men who wanted his cold for themselves. What did you imagine walls dream of, majesty? Walls dream of falling. I have been falling for a hundred years, and the fall is called *reign*."

The hunger rises in him then, and this is the part Aldous cannot describe to anyone, because there is no one to describe it to and no words for it anyway: the hunger is not near him, it is *in* him, it uses his body the way a rider uses a horse. His mouth floods with a taste like biting iron in January. His eyes ache. Below him the market crowd shimmers, and for one vertiginous moment he sees them as the crown sees them—not people but *provisions*, ten thousand small warm memories walking around unguarded, names and mothers and first loves and griefs, all of it loose in the open air, all of it *unclaimed*, and the crown's hunger pours through him like floodwater through a broken door, and Aldous understands, with the pity that is the cruelest thing left in him, that the crown is starving.

It has been starving for a century. It eats a king's memories and stays hungry. It eats the queen's face from his mind and stays hungry. It sends its wardens out into the night to drink warmth from sleepers, and the sleepers wake duller, and the crown is fed, and the crown is starving. It is a fingerbone of a murdered god, and gods do not grow less; their hungers do not grow less either. Nothing fills it. Nothing ever will. It will eat the whole world and lie in the silence afterward, at the bottom of its own endless cold, still hungry, forever, and some part of Aldous—the rind, the residue, whatever he is—pities it, pities it the way you pity a fire trapped in a room with nothing left to burn.

And another part of him, the part that is only terror, wants to run from his own skull and cannot.

"Breathe," the crown tells him, noticing his hands trembling against the sill—his hands, trembling, *his*, the first honest tremor in years. "You'll split something. You're the only vessel I have."

"I'm not," Aldous whispers. "There's a girl."

The hunger stops. The whole gallery goes still, the way air goes still before snow.

* * *

He does not know how he knows about the girl. That is the terrible grace of it: the true king knows things, sometimes, and leaks them upward through the rind, and Aldous receives them like messages passed through a wall—scraps, whispers, a name half-heard. *Ledger-keeper.* Beasts' tongue. A book with a torn page. These fragments have been drifting up for weeks now, and the crown has been tasting them, puzzled, finding them bitter, because prophecy is the one food it cannot digest.

"A girl," the crown repeats.

"The ledger-keeper." Aldous's mouth is moving on its own now. He watches his own reflection speak and cannot stop it and does not entirely want to. "With the beasts' tongue. She's coming. He's—*we're*—sending the wolves. No. The wolves are coming anyway. She'll stand in the throne room and she'll—" The vision gutters. He grasps at it. "She'll trade. That's what she does. She trades. Everything has a price with her, she keeps the books, she keeps the *books*—"

"Stop," says the crown, softly.

He stops. Of course he stops. His jaw locks, his throat closes, and the vision goes out like a pinched wick, and he is left standing at the window with the taste of iron fading and the city unnamed again below him, and the crown thinking its slow, cold thoughts.

But it heard. That is the thing. It heard, and crowns do not forget—they only take things away from other people.

"She sounds useful," the crown says at last, musing, and Aldous feels the hunger turn, feel it pivot from the warm city below to something smaller and further off, a single point of heat on a distant road, and the tenderness in the crown's attention is worse than its rage has ever been. "A keeper of ledgers. A trader of precious things. Do you know what I would give, majesty, for a memory worth a king's reign? Do you know what I would pay?"

"Don't," Aldous says. It comes out cracked, human, the truest sound he has made in decades. "Whatever you are thinking. Leave her out of it. Take me instead. Take what's left of me, all of it, finish the meal—I'll walk into the deepest vault you have and hold still while you eat—but leave the girl alone."

The crown is quiet for a long moment. When it speaks again, its voice has gone gentle, which is how Aldous knows the matter is decided.

"You mistake our relationship," it says. "You think you protect things from me. But majesty—you are not my shield. You are my mouth. Everything you have ever begged me for, I have taken. Everything you have ever loved, I have eaten *from your hand*." A pause, delicate as frost climbing a pane. "And now you have shown me a girl who can unmake me, and asked me, wept at me, to spare her. Which means, of course, that she matters. Which means, of course—"

"No," Aldous says.

"—that I am *hungry*," the crown finishes, "and she smells like the last warm thing in the world."

* * *

That evening the king dines alone, as he always does, at a table long enough for forty, lit by candles that do not flicker because the air around them is too cold to move. The soup is brought. The soup is taken away untouched, as it always is; the crown does not permit waste of attention on food. A warden in a minister's robes reads reports aloud—grain stores, border levies, the guild's quarterly accounts—and the king nods at intervals, and none of it touches him, and none of him touches any of it.

Then the minister reaches the last page, and pauses.

"One further item, Majesty. The Hollow Guild reports an irregularity in its ledgers. An audit is underway. A junior clerk discovered... discrepancies. Shipments mislabeled. The Guildmaster believes the matter internal, but given the nature of the goods—" the minister's tongue hesitates on the word, as tongues always do, because the word is *cold*, and even the crown's own servants flinch from naming it plainly— "given the nature of the goods, the crown may wish to send observers."

"Send observers," the king says.

The minister bows and withdraws, and the great hall settles into its candlelit silence, and Aldous sits at the head of the long table with his hands folded, and inside him two things scream.

One is the true king, fainter than ever now, hammering on his door, and what he screams is not *save me*—he gave up on save me decades ago—it is her name, or the shape of her name, *the ledger-keeper, the ledger-keeper*, over and over, like a man shouting a warning down a well.

The other is the crown, silent, savoring, turning the evening's news over and over like a jewel held to the light. *Discrepancies. Discrepancies.* Somewhere in the city below, in a guildhall full of ink and paper, someone has been reading numbers that were meant to stay unread. Someone has found the trail of shipments leading, year by year, decade by decade, up the hill to the palace—to the crown's own table, its own larder, its own slow century-long feast.

Someone is asking questions.

The crown does not ask questions. The crown *answers* them, in its own way, in the currency it keeps: cold, and forgetting, and the long white quiet at the end of every ledger line.

*Rise,* it tells the king, and the king rises, because he always rises, because his legs are its legs and his will went into the soup he never tasted. *We will walk tonight. There are preparations to make. Winter is coming early this year, majesty—earlier than anyone suspects—and I find I have an appetite.*

And as the false king walks the long cold corridors of his palace, past guards who salute him and portraits of ancestors who would weep to see what sits their throne, the true king screams beneath, and the crown hums above, and somewhere across the sleeping city a ledger-keeper blows out her candle unaware that the oldest hunger in the world has just learned her name.
