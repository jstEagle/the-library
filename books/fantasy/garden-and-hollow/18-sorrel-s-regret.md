## 18. Sorrel's Regret

A ghost does not sleep. This is the first thing I would tell you about being dead, if you asked, and it is the thing I have hated longest.

For a hundred years I have sat inside this book like a moth inside amber, watching the pages yellow around me, watching hands turn me over and put me down and forget me. I have felt every cataloguer's thumb. I have heard every bored apprentice sigh at my prophecies and shelve me between a treatise on drainage rights and a cookbook confiscated from a poisoner. A century of dust settling on my name.

And then she came. The ledger-keeper with ink on her fingers and a secret folded up small behind her ribs, and when her eyes passed over my words, I felt them land — truly land — for the first time since I died.

Juniper Pemberton reads the way I used to write: carefully, hungrily, as if the truth were a small animal that might startle if approached too fast.

She is in the guild archive now, three shelves away, pretending to inventory a crate of bottled echoes while actually reading me for the fourth time tonight. She thinks I don't know. She thinks the book is only a book between her visits.

I am going to tell her tonight. What I did. What I made her.

Gods forgive me — if there are any left who aren't wearing crowns — I am going to make her into a weapon and then ask her to forgive me for the forging.

* * *

You should understand what a prophecy actually is, because no one understands, not even the people who write them.

It is not sight. Sight is cheap; any hedge-witch with a copper mirror can see tomorrow's weather. Prophecy is architecture. It is standing in the ruins of something that hasn't fallen yet and writing down the shape of the fall so precisely that the falling cannot help but follow your lines. A true prophecy doesn't predict. It *insists.*

I was good at insisting. That was my gift, before the crown took everything else. Words with weight in them. Sentences that bent the world toward themselves like iron filings toward a lodestone.

And a hundred years ago, I watched a young king put on a crown forged from a frozen god's fingerbone, and I understood — too late, always too late — what we had done.

We had wanted winter kept out. That was all. The Long Winter of my grandmother's time had eaten half the kingdom, and the court mages came to me, the royal scrivener, the woman whose words stuck, and they said: *Sorrel, help us write a binding strong enough to hold back the cold.* And I wrote it. I wrote it into gold and fingerbone and oath, and I stood in the coronation hall and spoke the binding aloud while the new king knelt, and I felt my own sentences close around his head like a jaw.

The crown was meant to be a wall. Walls do not care what they keep in.

I watched him change over seven years. Watched the warmth go out of him by degrees, the way heat leaves a house room by room. Watched him stop blinking. Watched him smile at his queen with a mouth that had forgotten what smiles were for, and underneath the smile — I could see it, because my words had built the cage and I alone could see through its bars — underneath, the true Aldous screaming.

So I did the only thing left to a scrivener whose king was already eaten. I wrote an ending.

* * *

They caught me at it, near the end. The crown's wardens. They burned my body in the palace yard and they thought fire would finish me, but you cannot burn a sentence once it has been spoken into the world with enough weight. They burned me and I fell backward into the one object I had soaked with more of myself than any other: the book. My book. The prophecy I had spent my last living year writing in secret, page by page, hidden in plain bindings.

I have been here ever since. Anchored. Waiting. Watching the crown eat king after king — for that is the thing no one guessed, the thing I only learned after death, from inside the book where the cold could not reach me: the crown does not die with its wearer. It waits. It hollows Aldous and wears him until he crumbles, and then it smiles at the heir and says *kneel,* and the kingdom, wanting warmth, wanting safety, kneels.

A hundred years. Four kings. All of them Aldous now, in the way that matters. All of them smiling wrong.

And all that time, the ending I wrote sat waiting in my final pages like a trap sprung shut around a future I would never see. *A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue shall unbind the winter-crown.* I chose those words the way a surgeon chooses a blade. Not kindly. Not fairly. Chosen because they were the only shape of person the binding could not digest.

Here is the terrible arithmetic of it, the sum I have carried for a century: the crown eats memory. Warmth, love, the animal heat of a life remembered — that is its food. So the unbinding must be paid in kind. Whatever soul comes against the crown must bring memories heavy enough to feed it until it chokes. A king's reign weighs a certain amount. The price is fixed. I could not lower it. I could only choose who would pay.

I looked a hundred years down the dark road and I picked a stranger out of the future and I priced her heart.

* * *

"Your margins are smug," Juniper says.

I nearly come out of the binding. She is standing directly in front of the open book, arms crossed, looking at the space just above page forty-one where I have been pacing — or whatever it is ghosts do that resembles pacing — and there is a small, dangerous tilt to her mouth.

"You can see me properly tonight," I say.

"The candle smoke keeps bending toward you." She pulls a stool over and sits, uninvited, the way she does everything: as if permission were a formality other people require. "Also, you've been muttering. Ghosts mutter. Nobody warned me ghosts mutter."

"I was rehearsing."

"I know." Her eyes flick to mine, and they are sharper than any auditor's. "You've been rehearsing for four nights. You think I haven't noticed a ghost working up to a confession? I keep ledgers, Sorrel. I notice balances that don't add."

I laugh. It comes out of me rusty, a hinge unused for a hundred years, and the sound bends the candle flame flat.

"Then you know this will not be pleasant accounting," I say.

"Few honest entries are." She folds her hands. Waits. And I understand suddenly why the wolves trust her, why the prophecy chose her shape and no other: she does not flinch from a number, however ugly. She writes it down.

So I tell her.

I tell her about the binding written into gold and bone. About the young king kneeling, warm-handed, laughing at some joke of his bride's even as the words closed over him. About the seven years of watching him cool. About the wardens and the fire and the long amber century inside these pages. I tell her how the crown passes from skull to skull, patient as frost, and how every king since has been Aldous-shaped and empty, and how the true kings scream beneath, unheard, unrecorded, unwritten — because I never found words heavy enough to free them.

"And then," I say, and my voice does something I don't let it do often, which is shake, "I wrote you."

The archive is very quiet. Somewhere above us, water drips through the roof-gutter, counting seconds the way water does.

"The final page," I say. "The torn one. *A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue.* I didn't see you, Juniper. I want that understood, because you deserve to be angry at the truth and not a flattering version of it. I couldn't see. Dead women don't get visions. I *engineered* you. I went looking down the road of the future for a mind the crown couldn't eat — someone who lives in numbers, who hoards truth in columns, who speaks to beasts because beasts don't lie — and I wrote a door in the shape of that person and I nailed it shut with a date. Tonight's date. Circled. A hundred years ago."

Her face does not move. That frightens me more than tears would.

"Why the beasts' tongue?" she asks finally. Quiet. Auditor-quiet. The voice she uses on entries that have been falsified.

"Because the crown eats memory, and memory is a human country. Beasts remember differently — in scent, in season, in the body. A person bound to the beasts carries a second ledger the crown cannot read. I needed someone the parasite would starve beside. Someone it couldn't fully digest." I spread my hands, translucent, useless. "And because I needed someone who would pay the price without being lied into it. Liars feed it forever. Only a keeper of true accounts could hand it exactly what it was owed and nothing more."

"And the price." Still that flat, level voice. "You knew the price. When you wrote it."

"Yes."

"A memory of equal weight to a king's whole reign." Her hands have gone white where they're folded. "You knew whoever walked through your door would have to give up—" She stops. Starts again, colder, precise as a blade laid flat on a table. "You knew they'd have to give up the heaviest thing in their ledger. And you wrote the door anyway, and you dated it, and you waited a hundred years for a girl to open it."

"Yes," I say. "That is what I did."

* * *

She stands. Walks to the window. The city glitters below, warm-windowed, oblivious, and beyond the walls the frost line creeps south through fields that should be golden with harvest. First snow due any day now, and it will be early, and it will be wrong, and it will be the crown stopping its pretense at last.

I wait. A ghost is good at waiting. It is nearly all I am.

"You could have warned them," she says to the glass. "A hundred years. You had a hundred years of kings. You could have written warnings into other books, other hands. You could have—"

"I did." The words come out of me like teeth. "Do you know what the crown does to prophecy, Juniper? It buys it. Your own guild sold shipments of cold to the palace for a decade and altered the ledgers to hide it — you found that yourself. Now imagine a century of that. Every copy of my book that surfaced was bought and burned by the crown's agents within a year. Every scribe I tried to warn died poor and discredited, their warnings filed under madness. The crown is not merely a parasite. It is an *editor.* It cuts anything that threatens the story it is telling." I drift closer, and the candlelight passes through me and pools on the floor. "This book survived because it looked worthless. Because I hid the weapon in a margin, in a date, in dullness. Because I made myself small enough to be shelved between drainage rights and poisoned cookbooks and forgotten."

"So you gambled," she says. "On a girl not yet born."

"I gambled on *arithmetic.*" My voice cracks across the archive. "One unbound soul against a hundred years of eaten kings. One heart against a kingdom. Do you think I don't know what that makes me? I have had a hundred years to know exactly what that makes me. I took a woman I would never meet and I weighed her love in my hands and I decided it was currency. I decided her grief was a fair exchange rate for everyone else's survival. And then I waited, and the years went past like snow, and every time a child was born with ink-stained fingers somewhere in this city, I wondered — is that her? Is that the one I'm spending?"

She turns from the window. Her eyes are wet, but her voice isn't, and somehow that is worse.

"The worst part," she says, "isn't that you used me. The worst part is that you're right. I've read the page. I've read all of it. There's no other door. Hesse is complicit, the guild is complicit, the packs are exiled and scattered, Ash is—" she stops on his name the way one stops on a bruise, and I mark it, and grieve it, because I know what she is planning to spend and he is in the coin, "—Ash is risking exile and worse, and the snow is coming early, and there is no other door. You built the only door. And you built it out of me."

"Yes."

"And now you want forgiveness."

"I want—" I begin, and find I have no polished sentence for it, I who was the finest scrivener of my age. A hundred years of rehearsal and every speech I drafted in the amber dark falls out of me now like ash. "I want you to know that I am sorry in the only way a ghost can be sorry, which is uselessly. I want you to know that if there had been another way, I'd have written it. I want you to hate me cleanly, if hating helps carry the weight, because the weight is coming either way and no one should carry it alone. And I want—" my voice fails, restarts, "—when it's done, when the crown is ash and the true king is free or dead and free, I want you to live somewhere warm and keep ledgers of ordinary things. Bottled echoes. Debts paid in years. Small impossible things. I want the rest of your ledger to be light. That is my apology. It isn't enough. Nothing I have is enough. But it's what I have, and I'm asking."

The silence stretches.

Then Juniper Pemberton, ledger-keeper of the Hollow Guild, chosen instrument of a dead woman's desperate architecture, does something I did not engineer and could not have predicted in a hundred years of watching.

She takes out her own little pocket ledger — the personal one, the one she thinks no one knows about — and uncaps her pen, and writes.

"What are you doing?" I whisper.

"Making an entry." Her pen scratches. "'Received from S., one confession, freely given. Assessed value: insufficient. Accepted anyway.'" She caps the pen and looks up at me, and her eyes are fierce and wet and absolutely steady. "Forgiveness isn't yours to grant conditions on, Sorrel. But hear me: I'm not your weapon. I'm not your plan. I'm walking through your door because it's the right door, and I'll hate you on my own schedule, and forgive you on my own schedule too. Understood?"

"Understood," I say, and mean it, and have never meant anything more.

* * *

She is almost to the archive door when I call her back. Because there is one more entry, and I have carried it a century, and she deserves it before she walks into the palace.

"Sorrel?" She pauses, hand on the door.

"The crown's origin," I say. "You'll need it, and the guild's records won't hold it — the crown edited those decades ago. But I was there. I saw what the forge-masters brought to the binding, and I wrote it once, in cipher, in a place the crown's editors never thought to look." I let myself smile, thin and old. "Not in a book. In stone. In the old royal garden, on the sundial's base, beneath the moss — a scrivener learns to hide her truest lines where no one reads anymore. Go to the garden, Juniper. Read the sundial. And take your wolf. You'll want his nose for finding things buried in frozen ground."

Her breath catches — at *your wolf,* at how much I know, at how long I've watched — but she only nods once, ledger-keeper to the bone, filing it.

"One more thing," she says at the door, not turning around. "The true king. Aldous. Beneath the crown. Is he still in there? After a hundred years — is there anything left to save?"

And I, who have lied with words all my life and sworn off it in death, give her the true account:

"He is still screaming," I say. "And he still begs — I've heard him through the binding, all these years — he begs whoever comes to kill the crown to let him die with it. He would rather be unmade than worn one more winter." I pause. "But that is his choice to offer and yours to refuse. Kings get their deaths. Ledger-keepers get to decide what mercy costs."

The door closes behind her. The candle steadies. I settle back into my amber, into my dust, into the last hours of a century's waiting — and far to the north, through walls and wards and the book's old paper skin, I feel the frost line move again.

Faster now.

The crown knows its door has opened. And it is very hungry.
