## 19. The Palace Library

*Juniper*

The palace had a hundred doors, and the guild's ledgers had taught her which ones were never counted.

"Servants' postern," she whispered, crouched beside Ash in the shadow of the east wall. "Entry forty-one in the annual audit. 'One door, iron-bound, unused.' Unused means unwatched. Unwatched means unlocked. Nobody guards a thing they've stopped believing in."

Ash's breath fogged the dark beside her. Even in his human shape he smelled of pine and cold stone and something wilder underneath, something that made her pulse trip whenever the wind shifted. He had insisted on coming tonight. She had not argued as hard as she should have.

"And if it *is* watched?" he asked.

"Then we run, and I add a new entry to my ledger: *one wolf, lost to stupidity.*"

His mouth curved. In the moonlight his face was all sharp planes and patient hunger, and she had to look away before looking became something else. There was no time for that. There would be no time for anything, if Sorrel was right about what the crown was and what it intended.

The prophecy book lay hidden in her rooms at the guildhall, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, but its ghost walked with them still. *Find where it began,* Sorrel had said, on their last night together, her voice like wind through a keyhole. *A crown does not dream of winter on its own. Something taught it. Find the teaching.*

The postern door was exactly where the ledger promised, and exactly as neglected — hinges furred with rust, the iron binding flaking like old scabs. Juniper worked a thin blade of steel into the gap, the same tool she used to open ledger seals, and felt the lock surrender with a click that sounded obscenely loud.

"You pick locks the way you read," Ash murmured. "Fast, and without permission."

"I read *with* permission."

"You read ledgers you weren't assigned."

"That's different." She slipped through the door into a corridor that smelled of tallow and cold ash. "That's diligence."

Behind them, the door swung shut. Ahead, the palace of King Aldous stretched upward into darkness, all its thousand candles burning steady and cold, as if even the flames were afraid to flicker here.

They moved the way she imagined water moved through a ship's hull: quietly, finding every seam. Ash went first when the corridors were open, his senses reading danger in ways hers could not; she went first when there were locks or papers or the small bureaucratic architecture of a building, because a palace, she was learning, was only a ledger written in stone. Every room an entry. Every servant a figure carried forward. Every secret a line item someone had tried to hide.

Twice they froze against walls as wardens passed — the crown's men, in grey livery stitched with a white circle, the sigil Sorrel had called *the mouth of winter*. Their footsteps made no echo. Juniper noticed that. She noticed everything now, cataloguing horrors the way she once catalogued bottled echoes: *one warden, silent tread; two wardens, eyes reflecting light at the wrong angle.*

"They're wrong," Ash breathed against her ear as the last one passed. "Not men wearing something. Something wearing men."

"I know." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Keep walking."

The library occupied the palace's north tower, three floors of it, according to the inventory she'd memorized from the guild's records of the royal estate. *Item: one library, contents various, last full appraisal ninety-one years past.* Ninety-one years. The appraisal predated Aldous's reign by decades, which meant no guild assessor had set foot inside since before the crown had settled onto a young king's head like a hand closing.

Nobody had inventoried the truth in almost a century.

The tower door was locked with a mechanism older and stranger than the postern's — a dial of black iron marked with runes she didn't recognize, except that her skin did recognize them, prickling from wrist to shoulder like the air before lightning.

"Ash." She held up her hand without touching it. "Feel this."

He reached past her, stopped a finger's breadth from the metal. His hackles would have risen, if he'd had hackles. In his human shape it showed only in his eyes, going flat and gold and ancient.

"Wolf-craft," he said quietly. "Old wolf-craft. My grandmother's grandmother might have known these marks. The packs say the north used to have gods." His jaw tightened. "Before the frost took them."

"The frost didn't take them." Juniper stared at the dial, at the runes worn shallow by a century of turning hands. "I think the crown did."

She tried the sequence carved faintly into the doorframe's shadow — a maintenance code, because even miracles required upkeep, and upkeep always left records. The dial resisted, then turned, then opened with a sound like a long-held breath finally released.

Cold poured out of the library. Not draft-cold, not stone-cold. A cold with intention in it, rolling down the stairs like something descending to meet them.

Juniper stood in it and made herself breathe.

"Well," she said. "At least we know we're in the right place."

* * *

*Ash*

The human world built its dens tall and its memories shallow. Ash had thought he understood that. Then he climbed the library stairs behind Juniper and understood nothing at all.

Shelves rose out of the dark like cliffs. Books stood on them in their thousands, spines cracked, gilt gone dull, and over everything lay a rime of frost so fine it looked like the books had been dusted with ground glass. His breath plumed. His ears — the true ones, beneath the human illusion — strained forward, hunting for the sound of the cold, and found it: a hum below hearing, vast and slow, like a heartbeat that beat once per year.

"This place is *alive,*" he said.

"It's a library."

"It's a *den.*" He moved closer to her, close enough that his arm brushed hers, and did not pretend the touch was accidental. Everything in him wanted to put her behind him, between his teeth if necessary. Nothing in him could explain that to her. "Something sleeps here. Or something is kept here and pretends to sleep."

Juniper had already produced her little book — she carried a notebook everywhere, wrote in a script so small it looked like insect tracks — and was sketching the shelves by candlelight she'd shielded with her palm.

"If something's kept here, it will be catalogued," she said. "Everything is catalogued. That's the one law of the world, Ash. Kings, gods, wolves — everything ends up in a ledger eventually." She glanced up at him, and the ghost of a smile crossed her face. "You'll end up in mine."

"I'm honored."

"Don't be. My ledgers are how people get found."

They worked the shelves in opposite directions, meeting at the tower's heart, where a reading table stood beneath a dome of dark glass. And there, laid out under the glass like a saint's relic — which, Ash would learn, was precisely what it was — sat a single object on a cushion of rotted velvet.

A fingerbone. As long as Ash's forearm. Pale as the ice on a winter lake, veined with lines of silver that caught the candlelight and seemed, when he wasn't looking directly at it, to *move.*

He stopped breathing.

Juniper did not stop. Juniper leaned in. Of course she did. She was already reaching for her notebook, already reading the plaque beneath the glass, her lips moving over words a century old.

"'Relic of the Frozen God,'" she read aloud, "'recovered from the northern ice, Year of Thaws 212. Presented to Crown Prince Aldous upon his majority.'" She paused. "Aldous. This was *his.* Before the crown. Someone gave a boy a god's finger for a birthday present."

"Not a present." Ash's voice came out rougher than he meant. "A warning. Look at the case. Look at the *seals.*"

She looked. So did he. Around the glass dome ran a ring of iron, and on the iron were the same runes as the tower door — wolf-runes, god-runes, old beyond old — and threaded through them, newer, cruder, were scratches in a different hand. Scratches that had been made *after* the case was sealed. Scratches that said, in the trade-tongue both of them could read:

*IT IS NOT DEAD. IT IS COLD. COLD DOES NOT DIE. IT WAITS FOR A WARM THING TO HOLD.*

Below that, in yet another hand, smaller, shakier:

*We were fools to bring it south. We were greater fools to think glass and iron would hold what the whole north could not.*

Juniper's pen had gone still. In all the time Ash had known her — weeks that felt like years, years that felt like minutes — he had never seen her pen go still.

"Sorrel said the crown was forged to hold winter back," she said slowly. "That was the story. The court story. The young king, the terrible winters, the smiths who made a miracle." Her finger traced the scratched warning without touching the glass. "But this says the crown wasn't forged to fight the frozen god. It was forged *from* him. They didn't build a wall against the cold. They built a *crown out of the cold itself* and put it on a warm young head."

"And the cold has been holding him ever since," Ash finished. "One fingerbone. One warm thing to hold." He thought of the king at his audit, courteous and glacial, blinking too seldom. He thought of the wardens with light at the wrong angle in their eyes. He thought of the packs in the north, and the stories the old wolves told when the aurora burned: *the god who froze rather than die, the god whose death took a thousand years, the god who learned patience in the ice.* "It isn't haunting the crown. It *is* the crown. Aldous hasn't been replaced by a parasite. He's been *inhabited* by a relic. By a god dying so slowly it learned to call its dying a reign."

Juniper straightened. When she turned to him, her face in the candlelight was terrible and beautiful, the face of a woman doing arithmetic with the end of the world.

"Then the Long Winter isn't a conquest," she said. "It's a *funeral.* The god is finishing its death, and it wants the whole world to lie down in the grave with it. And the crown—" her voice caught, steadied— "the crown is just the finger pointing."

Above them, in the dome of dark glass, something shifted. Not a reflection. Not a draught. A pressure, brief and vast, like the attention of something that had been pretending to sleep for a hundred years and had just heard its own name spoken.

The candles guttered. The frost on the shelves crept a visible inch toward the table.

Ash moved without deciding to — one motion, wolf-fast, putting himself between Juniper and the bone, a growl building in his human throat where it had no business being.

"We're leaving," he said. "Now."

"No." But her hand found his sleeve, gripped it. "No — wait. Look."

On the table beside the reliquary, where nothing had lain a moment before, rested a book. Slim. Black. Its cover frosted white, its pages breathing cold.

Juniper knew it. Ash saw her know it — saw recognition hit her like a blow, saw her go pale beneath her freckles.

"That's Sorrel's handwriting on the spine," she whispered. "That's the *prophecy.* The original. The book she's anchored to — it's been *here* the whole time. In the one room in the kingdom no auditor has entered in ninety-one years." Her laugh came out cracked. "Of course. Of course the guild confiscated a copy. The original never left the palace. It's been sitting next to the god, keeping it company, waiting—"

The book fell open on its own.

And in the frost on the table, letters began to form, written by no hand, in a script like cracks spreading through ice:

***YOU BROUGHT HER TO ME, LITTLE GHOST. HOW CONSIDERATE.***

The cold closed around them like a fist.
