## 20. The King in the Mirror

The fingerbone had been warm.

That was the thing Juniper could not stop thinking about as she and Ash climbed the servants' stair behind the palace library. Everything else about the relic had been wrong—wrong light, wrong cold, a wrongness that pressed against the eyes like deep water—but the bone itself, sealed in its case of black glass, had radiated a heat like a sleeping animal. A god's finger, frozen for a century inside a crown, and still it dreamed of warmth. Still it wanted.

"Two turns more," Ash murmured at her shoulder. He wore the shape of a palace guard now, borrowed from a man he'd left sleeping in a linen closet with his boots unlaced and his dreams rearranged into something unremarkable. In this shape he was broad and brown-bearded and utterly forgettable, which was, he'd told her once, the finest disguise there was: to look like everyone's assumption. "There will be a door with no handle on the outside."

"And how do you know that?"

"Because palaces are all the same," he said. "They are built by people who want doors they can close and never open again. Every great house has one. The wolves have dens like that too." A pause, and something older moved under his borrowed voice. "We do not use them anymore."

"Why not?"

"Because we learned what happens to the things inside them."

The stair ended at a landing where the plaster gave way to bare granite, old work, pre-palace work, stone laid before Aldous's grandfather's grandfather had worn any crown at all. The air changed here. Juniper felt it first in her teeth—a faint ache, like biting ice—and then in the ledger-keeper's part of her mind, the part that noticed imbalances, that knew when a column of numbers refused to sum. The palace above them was warm. Fires in every grate, braziers in every corridor, the crown's cold fed daily through the guild's hidden shipments of winter—and yet down here the warmth simply *stopped*, the way a river stops at a dam, pooling and pressing and going nowhere.

She thought of Hesse's face when she'd confronted him. *You don't understand what we're holding back,* he'd said. *You think we sell the cold. We're the dike, girl. God help us all when someone pulls one stone loose.* She had believed him, or wanted to. She was no longer sure those were different things.

"There." Ash stopped at a stretch of wall indistinguishable from any other, except that Juniper's breath did not fog near it, though the air was cold enough to fog. The wall drank the mist. She watched her own exhalation vanish an inch from the stone and felt her scalp tighten.

"No handle," she said.

"No handle."

She set her palm flat against the granite. It was not stone-cold; it was *hungry*-cold, the cold of a thing conserving itself, and beneath her hand she felt—not vibration exactly, but attention. The sensation of being counted. Of being entered into a ledger somewhere, weighed, found interesting.

"It knows I'm here," she whispered.

"Of course it does." Ash's borrowed face was grim. "It's been eating this city's memory for a hundred years. You're the first thing to walk down here carrying a prophecy in your head and wolf-warmth at your side. To a creature like that, you must smell like a feast and a fire all at once." He shifted, and for a moment his human shape flickered at the edges—she saw the grey of him underneath, the long muzzle, the patience of a hunting stance held for hours. "Say the word and we go back. There is no shame in it. Even the packs say so, and the packs say nothing kind."

"The packs say nothing at all about me," Juniper said. "You've told me that twice now, and both times you looked at the floor."

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and she let it go—there would be time, she promised herself, there would be *time*—and turned back to the doorless door.

Sorrel's voice came thin from the book in her satchel, strained, as if speaking through wool. *The rite needs a king who consents. Remember that. Whatever you find down there—the crown cannot hold a man who is truly willing to be free. That is its one flaw. That is why it keeps them asleep.*

"Asleep," Juniper murmured. "And if he won't wake?"

*Then you have come all this way to knock on a coffin,* said the ghost, and fell silent, and Juniper thought she heard—in the silence after, in the paper-hush of the book—something that might have been a century-old woman weeping, or might have been nothing at all.

She spoke the words Sorrel had taught her, the counter-sigil of the guild's oldest ledger-form, the one used to open accounts that had been deliberately closed: *What was entered may be read. What was read may be answered.*

The wall opened.

Not swung—*unzipped*, the granite parting along a seam that had always been there, revealing a corridor lit by no torch she could see, the light coming instead from the walls themselves, a pale glow like moonlight trapped in milk. Frost feathered across the threshold and crept toward her boots and stopped, abruptly, at a line drawn in salt and iron filings older than the kingdom's current dynasty.

"Ledger-work," Ash breathed. "Someone bound this place with accounting."

"Somebody in my guild," Juniper said quietly, "a hundred years ago, drew a line and said: thus far, and no further. And every master since has renewed it without asking why." She stepped over the line. Her heart was hammering, but her hands were steady, and she noted—with the strange double vision that had followed her since the night she'd read her own name in a dead woman's book—that steadiness frightened her more than fear would have.

* * *

The corridor ran straight for perhaps fifty paces and ended in a round chamber, domed, its ceiling lost in soft dark. And the chamber was full of mirrors.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. Tall ones and hand-mirrors and great clouded plates of silvered glass mounted floor to ceiling, some cracked, some fogged, some so old their surfaces had gone the color of tarnish—and every single one of them showed the same thing.

A king on a throne.

Juniper stopped dead. Ash's growl started low in his borrowed chest and built, and she put a hand on his arm without looking away, because she understood what she was seeing the way she understood a falsified ledger: not with her eyes first, but with the part of her that knew how lies were structured.

The mirrors were not showing the present. They were showing a *record*. Each one held a moment, frozen or looping, of a young man with sandy hair and a laugh-lines already starting around his eyes, seated on a plain wooden chair—no throne, a *chair*—while robed figures lowered a circlet of white metal onto his head. In some mirrors the moment was just before the crowning: the young man smiling up at someone off-frame, mouth open mid-joke. In others it was after: the smile still on his face, but the eyes beginning to change, frost climbing the irises like ivy climbs a wall, slow and patient and total.

"He was crowned in this room," Juniper said. "Or one like it. These aren't mirrors, Ash. They're receipts."

"A receipt of what?"

"Of a purchase." Her voice came out flat, because the alternative was for it to shake. "Look at the frames. Look at the dates scratched under them." She moved along the wall, reading. Year one. Year three. Year nine. Each mirror a year of the reign, each image a little colder, a little stiffer, the young king's smile preserved like a flower pressed between pages until it stopped being a smile and became a *mask hung where a smile used to be*. By the mirrors marked in the forties, the figure on the chair had stopped blinking entirely. By the sixties, the robes had changed style four times while the face had not changed at all.

"He's been thirty years old for seventy years," she whispered.

And then she reached the last mirror—the largest, set alone in an alcove at the chamber's far side, its frame carved with the sigil of the Hollow Guild—and it was not showing the past.

It was showing her.

Juniper stood in the glass, satchel on her hip, lamplight caught in her hair, and behind her reflected shoulder, where Ash should have stood, there was only darkness and, deep in it, two points of gold-green light that might have been eyes. But none of that held her. What held her was what stood behind *her reflection*: a second figure, fainter, rendered in the mirror as if seen through a pane of dirty ice. A man. Sandy-haired. Thirty years old forever. His palms pressed flat against the inside of the glass.

His mouth was moving.

She couldn't hear him. The mirror gave back silence, and his lips shaped words over and over with the desperate fluency of a man who has been saying the same thing for decades to no one: *Can you hear me. Can you hear me. Please. Can you hear—*

"I can hear you," Juniper said aloud, and her voice cracked the chamber's hush like a stone through a window.

Every mirror shivered. The frost on their surfaces bloomed and retreated, bloomed and retreated, like the breathing of some vast sleeping thing. And the man behind the ice went still—utterly still, the stillness of a wild creature that has heard a sound it stopped hoping for—and then he pressed closer, and this time when his lips moved, the words came through, faint and warped, as if spoken underwater, as if spoken from the bottom of a well, as if spoken from inside a mountain:

"*No.*"

Juniper blinked. "*No?*"

"*Not—you.*" His voice was rust and dust and disuse. "*Whoever you are—whatever you came to do—no. Go back. Tell them the king is dead. Tell them whatever makes it easier. But do not—*" He broke off, coughing, and the cough shook the whole chamber; frost skittered down the mirrors like startled birds. "*Do not dig. Do not pry. It lets you see me because it wants you to try. Trying is how it feeds.*"

"I know what it is," Juniper said. She stepped closer to the glass, close enough to see the fine grain of the ice between them, close enough to see that his eyes—his real eyes, under the frost—were brown and human and exhausted past all measure. "I've read the origin. I've held the ledger of its feeding. I know it was made from a god's fingerbone to hold back a winter, and that it decided the best way to hold back winter was to become one. I know it wears you."

Something moved through his face—too fast to name, gone before she could catch it, but it left his jaw trembling.

"*Then you know more than the last one,*" he said. "*The last one was a priest. It kept him talking for nine days. On the tenth day I woke up wearing his voice, and I had killed forty people in the eastern quarter, and I remembered every one of their faces, and I could not make my hands stop smiling.*" He pressed his forehead to the ice. Up close, Juniper could see that his hair was full of frost, that frost grew *from* him now, delicate crystals budding along his temples like some terrible flowering. "*Please. I am begging a stranger. Walk away. Let it keep me. A hundred years of me has bought a hundred years of the kingdom, and that trade—*"

"That trade is a lie," Juniper said. "Your masters in the guild told themselves it was a dike. It isn't. It's a tap. The cold doesn't stay behind the wall—it leaks, it grows, it eats memory from the whole city, and the Long Winter your crown was made to prevent is coming *because of* the crown. I've seen the date. Tonight. Tomorrow. However you count it, it's here."

The king stared at her. And Juniper watched—watched with her ledger-keeper's eye, the eye that could not help but audit everything—watched hope arrive in a man's face like water arriving in a desert, too much, too fast, cracking the ground.

"*Tonight,*" he repeated. His voice had gone very small. "*Then there is no time. Then you cannot—listen to me. Listen. There is a rite. I have felt it in the thing's dreams, the one flaw, the one door it fears—I can feel it flinch even now, hearing you speak of it. But the price—*" He stopped. Started again. Stopped. When he spoke again, each word came out like a tooth pulled. "*The price is a memory of equal weight. A king's whole reign. Seventy years of a life. No single mind holds that much. So the rite will take everything adjacent. Everything tethered. Everything loved.*"

"I know," Juniper said quietly.

Ash made a sound behind her—her name, half-formed—and she raised one hand without turning, *wait*, and he waited, because he was Ash, and waiting was what he did, patiently, at windows and gates and the edges of rooms, and she would think about what that meant later. Later. Everything was later now.

"You don't understand," the king said. "*I know what it takes. I have had seventy years to study the thing that ate me. It will not take a stranger's memories. It will take yours—the ones that anchor you. The ones you would burn the world to keep. And I am telling you, as one soul to another, as the last honest thing in this palace: I refuse. I refuse the trade. I have refused it for a century, and that refusal is the only part of myself it has never managed to digest.*" He lifted his chin, and for one moment the frost fell away from his eyes entirely, and Juniper saw the young man from the earliest mirrors, the one laughing at someone off-frame before the circlet came down. "*Let me die in here. Let the winter come and take the city if it must—that is grief, and grief ends. But do not empty yourself to pull me out. I am not worth a stranger's love. No crown is worth a stranger's love.*"

The chamber was silent. The mirrors breathed their frost-breath. Somewhere very far above, muffled by stone, a bell began to ring the hour, and Juniper thought: *the city doesn't know. They're lighting lamps and arguing about grain prices and kissing their children goodnight, and the hour is coming down on them like an avalanche.*

"You misunderstand me," she said at last.

His brow furrowed—an expression so humanly puzzled that her chest ached with it.

"I'm not here out of love for you," Juniper said. "I don't know you. You're right—you're a stranger. Seventy years of you is a ledger entry I'll never fully read." She set her palm against the ice, over his palm, separated by a finger's width of frozen god. "But I keep accounts for a living, Your Majesty, and here is what mine says. A hundred years ago, a guild sold a crown to a frightened court, and every master since has signed off on the shipments, and every citizen since has paid the interest without knowing it. Their memories. Their winters. Their children's names, eaten one by one. That debt is not yours. But it is *ours*, and debts don't dissolve because payment would be painful. Someone pays. It has only ever been a question of who."

"*And you've decided it should be you.*"

"I've decided it should be *me*," she said, "because I'm the one standing here. Because the prophecy named a ledger-keeper, and I have spent my whole life learning that the only way to balance an impossible entry is to pay it yourself and never tell anyone what it cost." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was steady. "You asked me to let you die so that no one would spend their love on a crown. That's generous. It's also the same arithmetic the crown uses—everything priced, everything traded, nothing given. I'm done doing sums in its currency."

The king looked at her for a long moment. Frost crawled. Bells finished ringing.

"*You sound like her,*" he said finally, and his voice had gone strange. "*The ghost. Sorrel. She came to me once, in the first year, before I learned to stop dreaming. She said she'd made a mistake writing the ending, and that someday someone would come to correct it, and that I should trust them even if they frighten me.*" A pause. "*You frighten me. So I suppose I trust you.*"

"Then help me," Juniper said. "The rite needs a king who consents. Truly consents—not the hollow consent the crown can mimic, but the whole, unwilling-to-be-held consent of the man himself. That's the flaw. That's the door. Will you give it?"

He closed his eyes. Behind the ice, his shoulders squared—seventy years of prison squaring themselves for one last act—and when he opened his eyes again, the frost in them had begun, very slowly, to melt.

"*Yes,*" said Aldous, true king of the realm, for the first time in a century. "*Yes. But quickly. It's noticed you. Can't you feel it coming home to check its larder—*"

He got no further. Every mirror in the chamber shattered at once.

Glass burst outward in a silent bloom, a thousand fragments hanging in the air like a blizzard stopped mid-fall—and through the alcove wall, through the granite, through everything, the cold arrived. Not temperature. *Presence.* Juniper's breath locked in her chest. The lamp died. And in the dark, from everywhere, a voice like a ledger closing spoke her name—

"*Juniper Pemberton. Keeper. We should discuss terms.*"

Beside her, Ash dropped his human shape like a coat, and the last thing she saw before the dark took them both was the king behind the broken glass, mouthing, urgent, silent:

"*Run.*"
