## 21. Betrayal at the Guildhall

The informer's name was Tam Wicker, and Juniper had bought him for the price of one erased debt.

She thought of that often now—of the ledger line she had struck through with her own careful hand, the small forgery of mercy she'd committed in the dark of the counting room. *Debt of T. Wicker, three crowns and interest, forgiven per Guildmaster's order.* Hesse had never given such an order. She had invented it the way she invented all her invisible things: neatly, precisely, with a steady hand.

Three crowns. That was what a man's silence cost, when his conscience was already cracked.

She should have known that anything purchased so cheaply could be resold just as easily.

* * *

It began, as betrayals often do, with something almost kind.

Tam found her in the guildhall yard at dusk, where she was checking the seal on a crate of confiscated charms—the ordinary work of an ordinary evening, except that nothing had been ordinary since the snow fell in Harvest Month. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a face like a question no one wanted answered, and he hovered at the edge of her lamplight the way moths hover before they commit.

"Ledger-keeper Pemberton." He touched his cap. "A moment?"

"Make it brief, Wicker. The manifests won't audit themselves."

"That's just it." His voice dropped. "I've been thinking about our arrangement. About the favor you did me." He smiled, and there was something wrong in the shape of it, something rehearsed. "And I've been thinking a man in my position ought to be worth more than three crowns."

The cold went through her like a needle finding the gap between ribs. She kept her hands still on the crate. She had learned stillness from the wolves; fear, they taught, was a scent you gave off or withheld, and the withholding was a discipline like any other.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Tam agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. A woman who speaks to horses—that would be a strange thing to know." He let the words sit in the frozen air between them. "Strange enough to be worth a great deal to certain parties. Parties who pay better than the guild does."

"Wicker." She turned to face him fully, and put into her voice every ounce of the flat authority the ledgers had taught her. "Whatever you think you saw, you saw nothing. Beast-speech is a fable told by drunk drovers. If you carry a fable to the wardens, do you know what happens? They laugh at you. Then they ask why a guild clerk is wasting the crown's time. Then they look into your accounts. And I have read your accounts, Tam. All of them."

It was a bluff built on truth, which was the only kind that held. His smile flickered.

"You'd burn me down with you."

"I don't need to burn anyone. I only need to open a book." She lifted the lamp between them, and its light made his face look older and hungrier than she remembered. "Go home, Wicker. Sleep on it. In the morning you'll remember that three crowns of freedom is worth more than any price the palace pays—and that the palace doesn't forgive debts. It collects them."

He stood there a long moment, working his jaw, and then he touched his cap again and walked away into the blue dusk, and Juniper told herself she had won.

That was the last lie she would believe for a very long time.

* * *

She went up to the roof afterward, because the roof was where truth lived.

Ash was waiting, as he always waited—a grey shape at the parapet that became a grey-eyed man when the shadows were deep enough. The city below them glittered under early frost, beautiful and wrong, harvest lanterns burning above fields that should have been gold and were instead white as bone.

"Well?" he asked.

"He blinked first."

"For now." Ash's breath fogged. He had not stopped watching the streets since the snow came. "Juniper, the packs are saying the cold isn't weather anymore. It has a direction. It moves toward the palace at night like water finding a drain. Whatever the crown is doing—it's getting worse, and it's getting faster."

"Then we're out of time." She pulled Sorrel's torn page from inside her coat, though she knew every word of it by heart now. *A ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue shall unbind what was bound.* Her name, written a century before her birth, in a dead woman's hand. "Hesse wants my report tomorrow. And after what I found in the shipping entries—the 'cold' consignments—I'm going to give him a report he won't enjoy."

Ash was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful in the way it always was when he talked about the guild. "Do you trust him? Truly?"

"Hesse raised me. He taught me double-entry bookkeeping and how to hold a pen so my hand doesn't shake in front of merchants twice my age." She looked out over the frozen city. "He's the closest thing to a father I've ever had."

"That wasn't my question."

"I know it wasn't." She folded the page away. "But he's also the man who assigned me to this investigation knowing what I am, and never once used it against me. That has to count for something, Ash. In a world where everything else gets sold, that has to count."

Ash said nothing. Wolves, she had learned, did not argue with people who needed to believe things. They simply watched, and waited, and remembered.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, and it did not come through the usual channels.

No clerk with a slate. No bell. Just two men in the counting room doorway wearing the grey-and-silver of the crown's wardens, and between them, wringing his cap, Tam Wicker—who could not meet her eyes, which was the only honest thing he'd done all week.

"Juniper Pemberton," said the taller warden, in the tone of a man reading a bill of lading. "You are remanded to the custody of the Crown Office of Inquiry, by authority of the Regency Council, on charges of sedition, theft of state correspondence, and traffic in forbidden knowledge."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small weight, like coins being counted out onto a table.

"Theft of—" She stopped. Recalculated. "I want the Guildmaster present. Now. This is a guild matter, and by charter, internal inquiries are—"

"By charter," the warden agreed, "which is why we are here with the Guildmaster's full cooperation."

The floor did not drop out this time. She had learned, somewhere in the last weeks, to stand on falling floors. But something behind her sternum went very quiet and very cold, colder than the winter outside, and she understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only once, at the exact center of a trap snapping shut—that Hesse had known. Had perhaps always known. That the investigation she'd been handed was not trust but bait, and she was the fish, and the hook had gone in clean.

They walked her through the guildhall's main corridor, past clerks who looked up from their desks and quickly away. Past the great brass scales in the entry hall that weighed incoming goods. Past the wall where the guild's founding charter hung in its gilded frame, all those noble words about fair measure and honest trade.

At the end of the corridor, outside his office door, stood Guildmaster Hesse.

He was an old man built like a piece of furniture—broad, deliberate, made to bear weight. She had seen him face down tax collectors and smugglers and once, memorably, a dragon-broker with a forged pedigree and a live wyvern in a wagon. He had never looked small before. He looked small now, standing with his hands clasped behind his back, and his eyes—his eyes would not settle anywhere near hers.

"Hesse." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Tell me this is a mistake."

"Juniper." He said her name the way he said it across the audit table, gentle and final, the sound of a stamp coming down on a document. "I'm sorry."

"You're sorry." Something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. "I found the shipments, Hesse. Cold, crated and manifested as salt-glaze ware, going to the palace for thirty years. Someone altered the ledgers to hide it, and I traced the alterations back to this office—to *your* cipher. And instead of answering for it, you hand me to the wardens?"

"Keep your voice down." It was reflex, the old reflex, father and master both, and it made her laugh—a short, awful sound.

"Or what? You'll have them take me too?" She stepped closer, close enough to smell the lamp oil and old paper that clung to him, the smell of every safe evening she'd ever spent in the counting room. "Was any of it real? The assignment? Did you send me into the prophecy because you thought I could stop it—or because you needed someone expendable to hold the book when the crown came asking?"

For one moment—one flicker—something moved behind his eyes that looked like grief. Like a man watching a fire consume a building he had built with his own hands.

"It was real," he said quietly. "All of it. That's what makes it unforgivable." He turned to the wardens. "She'll go with you without trouble. See that she does."

"She's heard enough," the tall warden said, and took her arm.

* * *

They put her in a closed carriage, which was worse than chains. Chains would at least have admitted what was happening.

Through the shutter slats she watched the guildhall recede, its copper roof green with frost, its weathervane—a little brass scale, balanced forever—turning slowly in the wind. She had spent eleven years in that building. She had learned to write there, to count there, to hide there. Every careful invisible thing she had ever built, she had built inside those walls, and she had believed the walls loved her back.

Compromise by compromise, she thought, watching the streets slide past. That was how they got you. Not with one great betrayal but with a ladder of small ones, each rung reasonable, each rung necessary, until you looked down and saw how high you'd climbed and how far the fall.

She had erased Tam Wicker's debt. She had hidden her gift from the guild for eleven years. She had traded her mother's voice for a page. She had lied to Hesse about where she went at night, and lied to Ash about trusting Hesse, and lied to herself most fluently of all, in the neatest hand, in the fairest columns.

Every entry comes due. That was the first law of the ledger, and she had written it ten thousand times without once believing it would apply to her.

The carriage turned toward the palace district, and the air changed. She felt it before she saw it—the way the wolves had taught her to feel weather. A pressure. A *direction*. The cold outside the shutters was not the diffuse cold of winter but a gathered thing, a held breath, flowing past the carriage windows toward the palace spires like every river in the world running home to one sea.

The crown was hungry. And today it was being fed.

* * *

The Wardens' House stood in the shadow of the palace walls, a building she had passed a hundred times without seeing, the way one passes gravestones. Inside, it was warm—which was somehow the worst detail yet, warmth in a place like this, braziers crackling while the city froze. They took her coat. They took her satchel. They took Sorrel's page from inside her lining with gloved fingers, delicately, the way one handles evidence, and she watched a century of a dead woman's hope disappear into a warden's document case and said nothing, because there was nothing left to buy it back with.

In a small stone room with one table and two chairs, a woman was waiting.

She was not dressed as a warden. She wore plain grey wool and no insignia at all, and her hair was silver, and her eyes were pale as shallow ice, and when she smiled at Juniper it was with genuine, unhurried pleasure—the smile of a collector whose shipment has finally arrived.

"Ledger-keeper Pemberton." She gestured to the chair opposite. "Please. Sit. You've had a long morning, and I find that important conversations go better seated."

"I'll stand."

"As you like." The woman folded her hands. "My name is Vess. I serve the Crown Office of Inquiry, which serves the crown, which serves—" a small pause, delicate as a knife turning, "—the winter. I've read your file. It's remarkably thin for someone so accomplished. Eleven years of flawless service, no vices on record, no debts, no scandals. A woman who leaves no marks." Her pale eyes brightened. "Except, of course, the ones in the margins of old books. And the ones horses talk about."

Juniper kept her breathing even. Kept her hands loose at her sides. *Fear is a scent,* the wolves had taught her. *Withhold it.*

"If you have charges, read them. If you have questions, ask them. I've audited harder interrogators than you before breakfast."

"Oh, I'm sure." Vess unfolded a paper—she recognized the guild's letterhead, recognized the blocky clerk's hand. Tam Wicker's deposition, sworn and sealed. "A remarkable document. Our friend Wicker initially offered it to a palace steward for forty crowns. We paid twenty-five. We find that men who sell their first loyalty always discount the second." She set it on the table between them, face up, like a card. "But this isn't why you're here, Juniper. The sedition, the stolen correspondence—those are levers, nothing more. Levers are for moving furniture. You are not furniture."

"Then what am I?"

Vess leaned forward, and the brazier light moved in her eyes like something swimming under ice.

"You are the person the prophecy sent for," she said softly. "And so is His Majesty. Don't look surprised—the crown has known about Sorrel's book far longer than you have. We let you keep it. We let you find the page. Do you imagine a guild that has shipped cold to this palace for thirty years couldn't have searched one clerk's lodgings?" She spread her hands, gracious as a hostess. "We needed the unbinding rite recovered, dear girl. You did our work beautifully. And now that the last obstacle between the Long Winter and its completion is sitting in this room—"

The door opened behind her. Two wardens entered, and between them, wrapped in a cloak that swallowed her thin frame, they brought a second prisoner.

Grey-haired. Barefoot. Ghost-pale in a way that had nothing to do with imprisonment.

Sorrel lifted her head, and her eyes found Juniper's, and in them was a hundred years of guilt arriving all at once.

"They followed me," the ghost whispered. "Juniper—they followed *me*. I'm so sorry. I led them straight to you."

Vess smiled her unhurried smile, and the brazier crackled, and somewhere far above them the winter pressed its cold face against the palace walls like a child against a window.

"Now," she said. "Shall we discuss what you're willing to trade?"
