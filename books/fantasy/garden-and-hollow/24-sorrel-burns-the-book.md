## 24. Sorrel Burns the Book

A hundred years is a long time to be a page.

I had forgotten, mostly, what it was to have hands. I remembered them the way you remember a dream of flying—vividly and uselessly. What I had instead was paper: the vellum skin of the prophecy I had written in a cellar that smelled of tallow and fear, the ink I had ground from ash and my own dried blood because the crown's wardens were already at the door and there had been no time for anything holier.

For a century I had been the book. The book had been me. When apprentices thumbed its corners, I felt it like a stranger touching my face. When damp swelled the boards of the guild archive, I ached in my nonexistent joints. And when Juniper Pemberton first opened me—careful, skeptical, her finger tracing the circled date with the particular reverence ledger-keepers reserve for numbers—I felt something I had not felt since the night I died.

Hope. Which is, of course, only another word for terror wearing its good coat.

Now I stood—or did whatever it is ghosts do instead of standing—in the Cold Room beneath the palace, watching them take her apart.

* * *

The crown did not need chains. It needed only patience.

Juniper sat on a bench of black ice in a cell where the walls sweated frost, and the crown-entity wore the king's body the way a hand wears a glove two sizes too large. Aldous's face smiled down at her, courteous as ever, and his voice was warm as a hearth, which was the most obscene thing about it.

"You misunderstand," the thing said gently. "I am not asking you to betray anything. I am offering you a trade. Your memories for the kingdom's warmth. A fair price. You of all people understand fair prices."

"I understand ledgers," Juniper said. Her lips were blue. Her hands were bound not with rope but with rings of frost that tightened when she moved. "And this entry doesn't balance. Nothing balances against a kingdom."

"Everything balances," said the crown. "That is the whole of arithmetic, little keeper. Something is always taken. The only question is who signs for it."

She lifted her chin. Even half-frozen, even afraid—and she was afraid; I could see it in the flutter of her pulse at her throat, the small animal honesty of her body—she looked at the thing wearing her king and said, "No."

The word fell into the cold room like a coin into a well.

"No," she said again, clearer. "You can take everything I am and it will still not be enough, because I won't *give* it. There's a difference. Look it up."

Something flickered across Aldous's stolen face—not anger. Hunger recalibrating. And beneath it, so deep that only something that had watched him for a hundred years could see it: the true king, screaming silently behind his own eyes, mouthing words he had been mouthing for decades. *Run. Run. Let me die.*

I had heard that mouth say those words through riots and coronations and funerals. No one had ever listened. I intended to be the last thing that ever did.

But first: the girl.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being a ghost: you do not haunt places. You haunt purposes.

I had been dead for a century, but I had never been finished. That was the trap I'd built myself into—the ink, the blood, the desperate arithmetic of a dying woman who wanted one more chance to correct the world. I had written a prophecy the way you write a letter you know will arrive after you're gone, and then I had discovered that letters don't get to rest until they're read, and prophecies don't get to rest until they're *paid*.

My anchor was the book. While the book existed, I existed—scattered through every page, every margin note, every correction I'd scratched in over the decades when some fool tried to alter my work. I had watched four generations of guild-masters handle me like inventory. I had watched Hesse, young once, actually, before the ledgers taught him to weigh human lives in trade-weight—read my pages by candlelight and close me softly, like a man closing a door on a crying child.

And now I watched the crown's wardens carry Juniper's confiscated satchel into an evidence vault, and inside the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, was me.

They were going to burn me eventually. The crown burned prophecy; it was the one hygiene it kept. But not yet. Not tonight. Tonight they thought they had already won.

Tonight they were wrong, because tonight I finally understood the last line of my own book.

* * *

Let me tell you what I wrote, a hundred years ago, in a cellar, with wardens' boots on the stairs.

I wrote that the Long Winter would come, because I had seen it coming—I had seen the crown eat the young king bite by bite, memory by memory, and I knew that whatever wore him would eventually stop pretending and simply *take*. I could not stop it. I was one woman with a scribe's pen and three days before they found me.

So I did the only thing left to a person who cannot fight: I made a weapon and hid it in the future.

I wrote a ledger-keeper. I wrote beasts' tongues. I wrote an unbinding that required a price equal to a king's whole reign, because I knew the crown would never accept less, and I knew—this is the part I am not proud of, the part I have carried like a stone in my chest for a hundred years—that whoever came after me would need to be *willing*. Prophecy cannot compel willingness. It can only arrange the meeting.

I forged the ending. Sorrel's Regret, if you are keeping score, and I was always keeping score; it is the family disease of anyone who has ever held a pen over another person's life. I arranged a girl I would never meet like a gardener arranges a trellis, and then I died, and then I waited, and then she came, and she was better than anything I had planned—braver, angrier, kinder—and now she sat in a cell of ice saying *no* with her whole spine, and the crown was patient, and patience wins. Patience always wins. Unless someone cheats.

Ghosts are very good at cheating. We have nothing left to lose except our existence, and existence, I had learned, is mostly a habit.

* * *

The vault was three floors up and guarded by two wardens whose hearts beat slow and cold, slowed further by proximity to the satchel—they didn't like guarding it; the book frightened them, though they'd never have admitted it. Fear of books is the oldest and most sensible fear there is.

I poured myself along the draft under the door. Moving as a ghost is like moving as smoke thinks: you go where the world is thin. Stone is thick. Iron is thicker. But every room leaks a little air, and air remembers being outside.

The satchel lay on a shelf between a confiscated music box and a crate of unlicensed star-charts. I settled against the oilcloth and pushed.

Pushing is all a ghost has. A hundred years of pushing. Usually it accomplished nothing—a candle guttering, a lock clicking loose, a warden dreaming of his mother. Tonight I pushed everything I was into one intention, and the intention was simple:

*Burn.*

There was a lamp on the warden's desk. Oil lamp, brass, badly trimmed wick—it had been smoking all evening, and the wardens had been too bored to fix it. Boredom is a door. I went through it.

The flame stood up straight, then leaned, then leaned further, impossibly, toward the shelf, and the nearest warden frowned at it the way men frown at weather, and I gathered the last of my strength—which is to say, the last of my *self*—and threw it into that lean.

The flame jumped the gap like a fox taking a fence.

Oilcloth catches beautifully. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger, in some honest hand: oilcloth catches beautifully, and a hundred years of waiting ends in eleven seconds of fire.

* * *

Burning, when you are the book, is not pain exactly. It is *unbinding*—every page a knot coming loose, every word I ever wrote returning to the ash and blood it came from. I felt my prophecy open like a throat. I felt the circled date curl and blacken. I felt the final page—the forged ending, my great sin, my great gift—go up in a bloom of gold, and I laughed, or did whatever it is ghosts do instead of laughing, because of course. Of *course*. The answer had been in my own handwriting for a century and I had been too much a coward to read it aloud.

A prophecy that burns frees what it bound. Everyone knows that. Every hedge-witch knows it. It is the first law of written things: what is spoken can be unsaid, what is written can be burned, and what is *bound to the writing* goes with it.

I had anchored myself to the book to survive long enough to matter.

To free her, the book had to die.

To free her, *I* had to die.

Well. Second time's easier. That's what nobody tells you either.

* * *

The wardens shouted. One stamped at the flames; the other rang the alarm bell, which brought running boots and made everything worse and more flammable, because panic is kindling. The fire took the shelf, the star-charts (they burned green and lovely, and I hope their smuggler somewhere felt a moment of professional pride), the music box (which played four notes of a lullaby as it melted, and I am not ashamed to say I listened).

And I went with the fire. Down through the vault floor, along the thin places, following the cold—because the Cold Room was the coldest place in the palace, and cold calls to cold, and I had spent a century learning the grammar of the crown's hunger.

I arrived in the cell as ash arrives: everywhere at once, and light.

* * *

Juniper saw me. Of course she saw me; she had been seeing me for weeks, since the margins, since the first prickly conversation in which she'd called me a liar and I had called her a pawn and we had somehow, in the way of prickly things, become allies.

"Sorrel." Her breath fogged. Her frost-bound hands gripped the bench. Behind her, the crown-entity wearing Aldous turned, and for the first time in a hundred years, the thing looked uncertain. "Sorrel, the book—the wardens said—"

"Burning," I said. My voice was already thinner than it had ever been, a thread pulled through a needle's eye. "Isn't it wonderful."

"What did you do?"

"The only thing left in my ledger." I drifted closer, and I let myself take shape—one last indulgence, one last luxury: the shape of the woman I had been, gray-haired, ink-stained, ordinary. I wanted her to remember a person, not a presence. "Listen to me, girl. Listen, because I will only say this once, and then I am going to be extremely unavailable."

"Don't." Her eyes were wet and furious, which is the best combination eyes can be. "Whatever you're doing—don't. Find another way. There's always another way, you taught me that, you *wrote*—"

"I wrote a weapon," I said. "You are the weapon. And a weapon in a vault is just clutter. The rite needs a free hand, Juniper. It needs you out of this room and on that throne-room floor before the winter finishes falling, and the crown will never release you while my book exists—because as long as the book exists, the prophecy is *live*, and live prophecies frighten it. Burn the book, end the prophecy, and it thinks it's won. It relaxes. It gloats. Gloating things make mistakes."

"That's not worth your—"

"It is entirely worth my—" I stopped. Corrected myself, the way I had corrected a thousand marginal errors. "Juniper. I have been dead for a hundred years. Everything since has been an epilogue. Do you know what a gift that is? Most people don't get to choose their endings. They get whatever the world leaves them. I get to choose mine, and my ending is *you walking out of here*."

The crown-entity spoke, and its voice had lost its warmth, which meant it understood, which meant I had perhaps thirty seconds. "Ghost," it said. "Your anchor burns. You are spending yourself."

"Yes," I said happily. "It's marvelous. I recommend it."

"Ash is coming," I told her, fast now, the thread fraying. "The packs broke him out of exile an hour ago; the northern wind is full of teeth. He'll reach the ice gardens by the time you're free. Go to him. Don't argue, don't grieve, don't waste one second of your fury on me—spend it on the crown. And Juniper—"

"Don't," she whispered again, but softer now, the way people soften right before they break.

"When you perform the rite, it will ask a price equal to a king's reign. It will show you options. It will be very reasonable." I put everything I had left into my voice, all the hundred years, all the regret, all the love I had no right to feel for a woman I had invented on a bad night in a cellar. "Read the fine print. And whatever you choose—choose with your eyes open. That's all any of us can do. That's all a ledger is. Eyes, open, and the sum written down honestly."

The fire reached me then—not the vault fire, but the true one, the unbinding, my name coming off the last page. I felt myself go the way snow goes in spring: not destroyed, *released*.

"Sorrel!" She was on her feet, frost-rings cracking, reaching for a woman who was mostly light now.

"Thank you," I said. "For reading me. Nobody else ever read me properly. You were the best entry in my book."

And then, because a ghost should get one vanity, one last flourish, I spent my final shred of self not on farewell but on *work*: I tore loose the last binding I had woven into the prophecy a century ago, the contingency, the key hidden in the burning—the seal on the Cold Room's door, which answered to the book's destruction. The lock shattered. The door swung wide onto the frozen dark of the service stair.

"Run," I told her, with the last syllable I would ever own.

* * *

Afterward—if afterward is a word that applies to what I became—I was briefly everywhere.

I was in the smoke rising through the palace chimneys. I was in the alarm bells still ringing their stupid urgent song. I was, for one impossible moment, in the northern wind, where I felt the packs running, hundreds of hearts hammering in the dark, and one heart ahead of them all, gray-eyed and desperate, running south toward a girl who was, at that exact moment, stepping over a broken door into freedom.

He was singing. Wolves sing when they run for love; it is the oldest song there is, older than prophecy, older than crowns. I rode the wind beside him for the length of a heartbeat and I thought: *ah. So that's what she chose. Good. Good.*

Then the wind moved on, and I went with it, and I did not mind.

A hundred years is a long time to be a page. But it turns out an ending, chosen freely, given gladly, takes no time at all.

The last thing I was, was warm.

* * *

In the ice gardens below the palace, Juniper Pemberton ran barefoot through the first true snow of the Long Winter, and far behind her the evidence vault burned, and far ahead of her something gray ran out of the treeline with its whole heart in its throat—

and the winter, suddenly, inexplicably, hesitated.
