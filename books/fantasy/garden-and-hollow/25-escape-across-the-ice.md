## 25. Escape Across the Ice

**Juniper**

The palace quarter burned in colors that had no business existing.

Fire should have been orange. This was blue-white and silver, flames that gave off cold instead of heat—*coldfire*, the wardens called it, though no one had ever told her what it actually was—and it ate through the archive wing with a sound like a thousand pages sighing shut at once. Behind them, Sorrel's book was ash. Sorrel herself was nothing at all now, not even smoke, and Juniper had no time to grieve because the roof of the Records Hall chose that moment to come down in a sheet of burning ice.

She ran.

The streets of the palace quarter had gone strange with the Long Winter's first true night. Snow fell upward in places, caught in the crown's unraveling hunger, drifting toward the sky like the city itself was being exhaled. The cobbles were glazed black and slick, and every window they passed showed either darkness or faces—pale, watching, too frightened to open their doors to two fugitives streaked with soot and blood.

Ash ran ahead of her, half-shifted, his coat rippling between wolf-grey fur and the bare skin of his arms as he fought to keep a shape that could open doors and pull her over walls. His hand found hers without looking back. That was the thing about him she kept forgetting and relearning: he never had to look.

"Left," he said. "The gardens."

"The gardens are walled."

"The wall has a gate, and the gate has a lock, and locks are just small stubborn doors." He glanced back at her, and even now—even with the sky falling silver behind them and the wardens' horns sounding three streets over—there was something in his face that was almost a smile. "You taught me that. Ledgers have locks. You open them anyway."

"I pick ledger locks with *paperclips*, not while running from the crown's entire garrison."

"Then we'll improvise."

They went left.

Behind them the palace quarter groaned. She could hear the wardens now—not shouting, which would have been almost human, but calling to one another in that flat, resonant tone the crown put in their throats when it wore them. *Find the ledger-keeper. Find the wolf. The winter is owed.* The words carried strangely on the frozen air, arriving already inside her ears, and she hated how much they sounded like entries in a book. Debts. The crown thought in debts. So did she, once. She wondered if that was why it had wanted her alive rather than dead.

They cut down a servants' alley where the snow lay hip-deep and unbroken, and Ash pulled her through it by the hand, breaking trail, his shoulders broad enough to make a corridor of the drifts. Her lungs burned. Her legs were lead. Somewhere under her ribs, where Sorrel used to be—a presence like a candle held near her ear for weeks—there was only an ordinary silence, and grief kept trying to surface in it, and she kept pushing it down because drowning now would kill them both.

"There." Ash stopped so suddenly she walked into his back.

Ahead, the garden wall rose out of the snow, ten feet of old palace stone furred white with frost. And set into it, exactly where he'd said, a gate: iron bars, a lock the size of her fist, and around the lock a rime of ice so thick it looked poured rather than grown.

"Coldfire came through here," she breathed. "Look at it. It froze the *lock*."

"Can you open it?"

"I'm a ledger-keeper, not a locksmith."

"You're a Pemberton," he said. "I've seen you open things that were never meant to open."

She looked at him. He looked at her. Then he turned his back to the gate, cupped his hands, and laced his fingers, and she stepped onto them like it was the most natural thing in the world—as if they'd done this a hundred times before, in some other life neither of them had gotten to live—and hauled herself up the bars until she could reach the lock.

Her fingers were numb. The ice around the keyhole was smooth as glass and just as hard. She dug her thumbnail into the seam and worked it, chipping, scraping, breath fogging, and below her Ash stood braced against the gate and hummed—low, tuneless, a wolf's sound more than a man's—and she realized after a moment that he was humming the way you talk to a horse. To a frightened animal. To her.

"It hears you," she muttered. "Locks don't hear."

"Everything hears," he said. "That's the whole trouble with the world."

The ice cracked. A piece the size of a coin dropped away, then another, and then her hairpin—she still had her hairpin, absurdly, after everything—found the mechanism inside, and the lock turned with a click that sounded, in the terrible quiet, like a full stop at the end of a sentence.

The gate swung. They fell through it together into the dark.

* * *

**Ash**

The royal gardens had been beautiful once. He could smell what they had been under the frost: roses gone woody and patient, boxwood clipped into obedience, the mineral ghost of a fountain. Now everything stood under a foot of ice that had come down in a single night, and the roses were sculptures of themselves, and the fountain was a frozen white fist clutching at nothing.

It was, Ash thought, the safest place left in the palace quarter. No one fled *toward* the king's gardens on the night the Long Winter fell. Fear pointed people the other way. Fear was useful that way.

He got Juniper under the stone arch of the old orangery, where the roof still held and the wind couldn't find them, and only then did he let himself look at her properly.

She was bleeding. He'd known it since the guildhall—he'd smelled it, copper-bright under the smoke—but knowing and seeing were different animals. There was a gash above her eyebrow where a warden's gauntlet had grazed her, and her palms were torn from the gate, and she was shivering in a way that had stopped being about the cold and become about everything else.

"Sorrel," she said, before he could speak. "The book. I didn't— I didn't even get to say—"

"She knew," Ash said.

"You can't know that."

"I can. She waited a hundred years in the margins of a book for someone worth burning it for. You were worth it. She counted the cost the day she met you; I watched her do the arithmetic." He crouched, tore a strip from the hem of his ruined shirt, and took her hands. "Hold still. You're worse at being bandaged than you are at picking locks."

"That's not a high bar tonight."

"No," he agreed. "Tonight the bar is underground."

He wrapped her palms in slow, careful turns. Her hands were shaking. So were his, though he'd have bitten off his own tongue before saying so. Outside the orangery arch, the coldfire over the palace quarter lit the frozen garden silver and blue, and the light moved across her face in waves, and he thought: *if this is the last night, let me spend it looking at this.*

He had been exile for six days now. Six days since the packs had turned their backs on him in the northern snow and told him to choose, and he had chosen, and there was no undoing a choice like that. Every road home was closed. Every road forward led through a throne room guarded by things that used to be men. By any honest accounting—and he had learned accounting from her, learned to love the clean cruelty of a balanced column—the odds of either of them seeing morning were poor.

So.

"Ash," she said quietly. "You're staring."

"Yes."

"You're staring like you're memorizing."

He tied off the bandage. "Maybe I am."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she looked away, out at the burning sky. "Don't talk like that. Like we've already lost. We haven't lost. We have the rite. We have—" She stopped. He saw her decide not to finish the sentence, and filed that away, the way he filed away all the small doors she closed in front of him. There had been a lot of those doors lately. He hadn't asked why. Some part of him, the part that was still a wolf underneath everything, had already scented the answer, and had chosen not to raise its head.

"We have the rite," he said instead. "And tomorrow we walk into the throne room. And tonight—" He hesitated. In the pack, there were words for moments like this, whole ceremonies of posture and breath. In human shape, he had only ever been clumsy. "Tonight we're alive. I keep noticing it. I keep wanting to say it out loud and then finding reasons not to, and I'm tired of reasons."

Juniper looked back at him.

The coldfire-light moved across her face again. Under the soot and the blood and the exhaustion, she was watching him with that expression he had learned to read across a crowded guildhall and a frozen market and one impossible night on a rooftop: the expression of a woman auditing her own heart and finding the numbers refusing to balance.

"Ash," she said. "When this is over—"

"When this is over, you'll owe the crown a memory, and I'll owe the packs my life, and neither debt may be collectable. I know. I've done the sums." He laughed, low and helpless. "You've ruined me, Juniper Pemberton. I think in your columns now."

"Is that a complaint?"

"It's the opposite of a complaint. It's—" He stopped. Started again. The words had waited so long that they came out all at once, tumbling, unledgered, unbalanced, the most reckless entry he had ever made. "I love you. I loved you on the roof the first night, when you talked to the wolves like they were people and lied to yourself about why. I loved you in the auction house when you paid a memory of your mother's voice and walked out with your spine straight and your hands shaking. I loved you when you decided to save a king who begged you not to, because mercy isn't a thing you can argue someone out of. I will love you tomorrow in that throne room, whatever happens there. And if there's an after—if the world is kind enough to give us an after—I intend to love you in it, loudly, in front of everyone, until the packs regret their decision and the new king regrets his coronation. That's all. That's the whole entry. I just needed it written down somewhere, even if the only place is the air."

Silence. The fire over the palace roared silently, blue and vast. Somewhere beyond the garden wall, a warden's voice called the winter's owed debt to another warden, and neither of them flinched.

Juniper was crying. Not the way people cry in stories—she cried the way she did everything, precisely, furiously, wiping her eyes with the back of her bandaged hand and glaring at him as though the tears were a clerical error he had personally caused.

"You absolute fool," she said. "Do you know what I was going to say? When you interrupted me?"

"Tell me."

"I was going to say *when this is over, I have something to tell you*. Because I've been carrying it for weeks. Since the market. Maybe since the roof." She laughed, wet and broken and real. "And I kept not saying it because saying it felt like spending it, and I'm a ledger-keeper, and I hoard. But you're right. Tonight we're alive. So." She drew a breath that shook all the way down. "I love you. I love you, Ash, wolf and man and both and neither, and I am going to be so angry at you if you die tomorrow, because I finally said it out loud and you don't get to take it away from me by dying. Do you understand? That's the entry. Witnessed and sealed."

"Witnessed and sealed," he repeated, and his voice failed on the last word, and he didn't care.

He reached for her and she came, and they held each other under the arch of the dead orangery while the world outside burned cold, and it was not a gentle embrace—it was the grip of two people holding on against a current—and it was the gentlest thing that had happened to either of them in a year.

Above them, through the glass of the orangery roof, the snow had stopped falling upward. It drifted down now, ordinary and slow, settling on the frozen roses, on the iced fountain, on the roof over their heads, softening the edges of the whole ruined garden. As if the winter, having made its claim, was willing—for one hour—to let them keep this.

* * *

**Juniper**

Later—how much later she couldn't say—they sat against the orangery wall under his cloak, her head on his shoulder, watching the coldfire die down over the palace quarter to a sullen silver glow. The wardens' voices had moved off toward the river, hunting rumors of them instead of the truth. The garden ticked and settled around them, ice contracting in the deep cold, a sound like a great house thinking.

"We should sleep in watches," Ash murmured. His chin rested on her hair. She felt the words more than heard them.

"In a moment."

"That's what you said an hour ago."

"And I stand by it. Ledgers teach patience."

"Ledgers teach *evasion*," he said, but he didn't move, and she smiled into the dark of his shoulder.

She should have been afraid. Tomorrow they would cross the frozen gardens to the palace's postern door, and then the throne room, and then the rite, and the rite required a price she had already chosen in secret and told no one—not Sorrel, who was past telling, not Hesse, who was past trusting, and not the man whose heartbeat she was currently listening to. The price sat in her chest like a stone wrapped in silk. She had weighed it a dozen times in the dark and found, every time, that it balanced. A king's whole reign against a handful of months in a rooftop dark and a market stall and a frozen garden. It wasn't fair arithmetic. It was simply the only sum that came out right.

But tonight—tonight she let the stone lie quiet.

"Ash," she said. "Tell me something true. Something small. Something that isn't about tomorrow."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "When I was a cub, I fell through the ice on the Mirror Lake. My mother pulled me out by the scruff and didn't speak to me for three days—not from anger. She said fear needed somewhere to go, and if she spoke, hers would go into me." His arm tightened around her. "I understand her now. All week I've been afraid, and I haven't spoken of it, because I didn't want it going into you."

"It went into me anyway. I'm a ledger-keeper. I read silences for a living."

"I know," he said softly. "That's how I knew you'd already priced the rite. You've been carrying it like a debt you're ashamed of. And I'm not asking what it costs, Juniper. Whatever it is—you'll pay it, and it will be right, because it's yours to pay. But I want you to know that whatever you're hiding behind those little closed doors of yours—" his voice roughened, "—it doesn't change the entry. Witnessed and sealed. Nothing unseals it. Not the crown. Not the winter. Not anything you forget."

Her breath stopped.

For a moment the garden was very silent, and the stone in her chest turned over, and she almost—almost—told him. Told him everything: the page, the price, the memory she meant to pour into the crown like wine down a frozen throat. His name. His name above all.

Instead she lifted her head and kissed him, slow and thorough, in the blue light of a dying fire, and when she pulled back she said, "Nothing unseals it," and hated herself for how easily the words came, and loved him too much to ruin his last quiet night with the truth.

Beyond the wall, far off toward the river, a horn sounded—one long note, then silence, then two short ones. Not a hunting call. A summons. The wardens gathering at the palace, called home by the thing that wore their king.

Ash's head came up. Wolf-sharp, listening.

"Dawn soon," he said. "We should move while the streets are empty."

She rose with him, stiff and cold and steadier than she had any right to be. Together they stood at the broken garden gate and looked out at the palace rising against the grey east—its towers rimed white, its windows dark, and above the throne room, impossibly, faintly, a light like a held breath.

The crown knew they were coming. Of course it did. It had always known; it had written them into its books the way Sorrel had written them into hers.

"Whatever happens in there," Ash said quietly, taking her hand, "remember the garden. Remember tonight. Hold onto it."

"I will," said Juniper Pemberton, who intended, with the last act of her own free will, to remember nothing at all.
