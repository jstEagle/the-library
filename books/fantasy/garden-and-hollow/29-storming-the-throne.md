## 29. Storming the Throne

The city had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Ash ran the rooftops of the palace quarter with forty wolves at his back and snow in his teeth, and beneath him the streets lay white and empty—not sleeping-empty but *emptied*-empty, doors standing open, supper still on tables gone stiff with frost, a doll abandoned mid-fall in a doorway with its painted face turned up at the sky. The Long Winter did not take people first. It took their reasons to stay.

*Faster,* he sent down the pack-mind, that old thread of scent and instinct and wordless knowing. *The cold is ahead of us.*

He felt them answer—not words, exactly, but the pack's assent, forty hearts beating into one rhythm. The northern packs had come south across the ice in a single night, breaking exile like a bone, and every wolf who crossed the border had known it might be the last border they crossed. They came anyway. They came because he asked, and because the winter was wrong, and because—though none of them would ever say it aloud, for wolves do not shame each other with tenderness—they knew what she was to him.

Juniper Pemberton ran below him, on the ground, hood up, ledger-satchel banging against her hip. She could not run rooftops; her feet belonged to floors and staircases and the careful geometry of ledgers. But she ran anyway, breath smoking, and Ash kept pace along the cornices above her, matching her stride for stride, the way he had matched it through a hundred small nights when the only thing at stake was the warmth between them.

Now everything was at stake, and she still ran like a woman who had spent her life being careful, except that tonight she had chosen not to be.

That was the thing about courage, Ash thought, as the palace rose out of the white ahead of them—black stone furred with frost, windows glowing that wrong pale blue, like eyes open underwater. Courage was not a thing you had. It was a thing you did, over and over, each time more expensive than the last. He had watched Juniper choose it in a frozen garden with doom at the edge of every sentence. He had chosen it himself the night he broke exile, and he would choose it again at the palace gates, and again inside, and again, as many times as the night demanded, until either the crown broke or he did.

*There,* said Sif, his second, from the western wall—a grey bitch with frost-scars on her muzzle and no patience whatsoever for human architecture. *The gate is guarded.*

The gate was guarded. Of course the gate was guarded. The wardens stood in ranks before the palace's iron mouth, twenty of them, thirty, armored in black lacquer that did not reflect the light so much as swallow it, and where their faces should have been there was only smooth frost, featureless as the inside of a mask. They did not shift their weight. They did not breathe—at least, not in any rhythm Ash recognized. When they exhaled, the air around them crystallized and fell as glittering dust.

*Cold-wrought,* Sif said, with a growl that needed no translation. *Dead things walking.*

*Then we do not fight fair,* Ash said. *We fight like wolves.*

* * *

The plan was simple, because plans made in running snow must be simple or they die.

Sif would take the eastern wall with half the pack and make noise—real noise, the kind that raised hackles, the kind the wardens' hollow attention could not ignore. Grey Maren would circle south and find whatever servants' door the palace had forgotten it owned; there was always such a door, because palaces were built by humans and humans were built to leave ways out. And Ash would go through the front, straight up the middle, with Juniper, because the prophecy-ghost's last message had been specific about one thing: the rite required her presence, her voice, her memory given freely, and the crown would know her the moment she crossed its threshold. There was no sneaking past a thing that ate memory. It would taste her coming.

So they would not sneak. They would arrive.

Ash dropped from the wall beside her and took his other shape in the shadow of a burned-out chandler's shop—the shape he had worn so rarely this past year, the tall man with the winter-grey eyes who felt, always, faintly like wearing someone else's coat. Juniper looked up at him. Her face was wind-burned and set, and under all the resolve her eyes were doing something complicated, because she had told him what she meant to trade and he had not been able to stop her and neither of them had spoken of it since.

"You don't have to say anything," she said, before he could. "I can see you deciding whether to say something. Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything," Ash lied.

"You're a terrible liar for a wolf."

"I'm an excellent liar for a wolf. I'm just bad at lying to you." He pulled her hood straight, a small useless gesture, the kind of thing hands did when they wanted to hold on and couldn't afford to. "When we're inside. Whatever happens. You run for the throne room and you don't stop, not for me, not for any of them. Promise me the way you promise a ledger. In ink."

Her jaw tightened. "Ledgers don't lie," she said. "People do. I'll promise if you do."

"I promised already," Ash said. "The night I let the packs cast me out. Everything after that is just keeping it."

Somewhere to the east, Sif howled—and the sound went up like a blade, long and rising, and forty voices took it and threw it back doubled, trebled, a chorus of the north pouring itself over the palace walls. The wardens turned. All of them, in perfect unison, heads rotating with a sound like lake ice shifting, toward the noise.

"Now," Ash said, and they ran for the gate.

* * *

The first warden died badly, which was fitting, because it had been dead for some time already.

It came around the gatehouse at the sound of their footsteps—too fast, faster than armor should move, frost spraying from its joints like flour—and Ash met it low and hard, shoulder into its chest, and felt the cold go through him like a spike of nail-ice. His breath tore white. His fingers went numb to the wrist where he gripped the thing's lacquered gorget. Under the armor there was no flesh to hurt, only a kind of packed dark, a hunger shaped like a man, and it put one freezing hand against his face and began, methodically, to pull.

Not his strength. Not his heat. His *memories.* He felt them loosening—the smell of the den where he'd been whelped, the taste of his first kill, the sound of Juniper laughing in a garden—loosening like teeth, like things uprooted—

And then a ledger-satchel hit the warden across the head with a crack, and Juniper was there, wild-eyed, swinging the heavy book by its strap like a flail, and the contact broke something. The warden staggered. Its grip slipped. Ash tore free, gasping, his memories snapping back into place one by one like bones reset, and drove his knife up under the lacquered jaw into whatever passed for a spine.

The warden folded. Did not bleed. Just came apart into snow, quietly, almost politely, a drift collapsing in spring.

"Ledgers," Juniper panted, hauling the satchel back onto her shoulder, "are heavier than they look. I've never been grateful for that before."

"Remind me to thank Hesse for the binding," Ash said, and then the rest of the gate guard was on them and there was no more talking.

They fought up the great stairs two abreast, and it was nothing like the clean violence of stories. It was clumsy and close and freezing; it was slipping on iced marble; it was a warden's fist catching Juniper's shoulder and spinning her into a balustrade hard enough to crack the baluster, and Ash going for it with a sound that was not human and barely wolf, all fury and no strategy, and learning in the same heartbeat that fury alone would lose. Because the wardens did not tire. The wardens did not fear. Every one he put down cost him heat he could not spare, and the cold drank from him with every touch, and somewhere in the third gallery he realized the truth of the thing with a clarity that felt like falling:

The crown did not need to beat them. It only needed to make the walk long enough. Every step was a withdrawal from accounts that could not be refilled. The Long Winter was patient because patience was free and courage was not.

So he stopped spending courage like a rich man and started spending it like a ledger-keeper: precisely, deliberately, only where it bought ground. He called Sif's pack off the walls and into the halls, where numbers mattered and the wardens' unison became a weakness—forty wolves cannot be unisoned, forty wolves are chaos with teeth. He put Juniper behind him and taught her, in three shouted sentences, the trick of the satchel: the wardens reached for memory, and a book dense with a century of impossible transactions confused them like smoke. She learned it the way she learned everything, instantly and without complaint, and by the fourth landing she was fighting like someone who had done it all her life, which was a lie her body told with her permission.

At the fifth landing, a warden got past him.

He heard it happen more than saw it—the scrape of lacquer on marble behind him, the wrongness in the air—and turned in time to see its hand close on Juniper's arm, and see her *not scream*, and see her instead look directly into that blank frosted face and say, clear and furious, in the beast-tongue, a word he had never heard a human speak with such precision:

"*No.*"

The warden hesitated. That was the horror of it—it *hesitated*, the way a dog hesitates, the way a creature does when it hears something older than its orders. And in that hesitation Juniper drove her knife up through its throat, and it came apart around her like a snowman in a lit room, and she stood in the settling powder shaking, and looked at her own hand, and said, very quietly, mostly to herself:

"They were animals once. Some of them. The crown hollowed them the way it hollowed the king." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of soot. "I heard it. Under the frost. Something still in there, asking."

"Juniper—" Ash started.

"Don't," she said. "If you tell me to feel less, I'll start feeling less about everything, and I need all of it tonight. Come on. We're close. Can't you smell it?"

He could. Under the cold and the blood and the wolf-smell of the pack flooding the halls behind them, there was a scent from the heart of the palace, from the high seat of it: old ice, and godbone, and hunger. It smelled the way the prophecy book had smelled. It smelled like a page torn out and sold and carried a hundred years to this exact staircase.

* * *

The last corridor was the throne gallery, and the crown had saved its best for last.

The wardens here did not rush them. They stood in two silent ranks down the full length of the hall, fifty at least, and the temperature dropped so sharply that Juniper's next breath cracked audibly in her throat, and the frost on the walls began to glow, faintly, that drowned-blue glow, and Ash understood that this was not a defense. This was an audience. The thing in the crown wanted to watch her walk the length of its hall. Wanted to measure her. Wanted her to feel, with every step, exactly how much winter stood between her and the throne.

"Good," Juniper said, so softly that only he heard it. "Let it look."

She stepped forward, and Ash stepped with her, and the pack poured into the gallery behind them and stopped at the doors as if told—because they *had* been told, by the oldest law there is, that some thresholds a pack does not cross with you. Sif pressed her flank against his leg, once, briefly, the whole farewell of her kind, and fell back.

They walked.

Every step was a choice. That was the thing Ash kept coming back to, step after step after step, boots crunching on frost that thickened beneath them as they went, breath freezing in their lashes, the wardens' blank faces turning in slow unison to follow them like a field of sunflowers tracking a dying sun. Courage was not the fire he'd imagined as a young wolf listening to the old ones tell stories of heroes. Courage was arithmetic. Courage was choosing, again, with your legs burning and your lungs full of knives, to take the next step when the next step cost more than the last, and then choosing it again, and meaning it each time, because the moment you stopped meaning it was the moment you sat down in the snow and let the winter have you.

Halfway down the hall, the cold found the softest thing in Ash and squeezed.

It did not take his strength. It offered him something instead—an image, warm and detailed, blooming behind his eyes like a hearth-fire: a den in the northern forest, deep snow outside, deep peace inside, and himself grown old in it, grey-muzzled, surrounded by the pack, and the war a story told to pups, and no throne room, no rite, no price. All he had to do was turn around. Walk out. The winter would remember him kindly. The winter was generous, the winter whispered, the winter only wanted to keep things safe and still and *forever*—

"That's not peace," Juniper said beside him, not looking at him, still walking. "That's a ledger entry with the costs left out. Keep walking."

He laughed—a broken, freezing, grateful sound—and kept walking.

Because that was the other thing about her, the thing he had known since the first night on the guild roof when she'd argued politics with a wolf: she could read the true column in any book, even the book of her own terror, even the book written in frost on the inside of his skull. She walked like a woman auditing the apocalypse and finding errors in it, and the errors gave her strength, and her strength gave him his.

Twenty steps from the throne. Ten. The doors at the end of the gallery were taller than trees, black oak banded in silver gone grey, and they stood closed, and from behind them came a sound that stopped Ash's heart colder than any warden's touch.

A voice. Two voices, actually, in the same throat—one smooth and courteous and terribly patient, the voice of the king who smiled wrong, saying something over and over in a tone of perfect hospitality:

*"You've come so far. You must be tired. Sit. Stay. Stay forever. Everyone stays forever, here."*

And underneath it, thin and ragged and human, a second voice screaming—not words anymore, maybe hadn't been words for years, just the raw animal fact of a man buried alive inside his own smiling face, screaming the way the beasts screamed when the hunters came, screaming the way Ash had screamed the night the packs cast him out.

Juniper stopped. Put her hand flat against the door. Froze her palm to the wood and didn't seem to notice or care.

"Aldous," she said quietly, to the man under the monster. "I'm the ledger-keeper. I'm here about the debt."

The doors swung inward without being touched, exhaling a hundred years of cold, and beyond them the throne room opened up like the inside of a glacier, blue-lit and vast, and at the far end of it, on a throne furred white with frost, the King Who Smiled Wrong lifted his head.

His smile widened. He did not blink.

"Ah," said the crown, through the king's mouth, in a voice like a lake freezing over, "the little accountant. Come to settle accounts."
