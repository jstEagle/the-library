## 31. The Unbinding

The throne room had stopped pretending to be a room.

Frost climbed the pillars like ivy given permission to be honest. The great windows—stained glass depicting the first king kneeling before a wheat field—had gone white from the inside, so that the light falling through them was the color of old milk, and the heroes on the glass were only shadows now, kneeling to nothing. Juniper's breath came out in ribbons. Her boots crunched on snow that had fallen indoors, that had no business existing, that fell anyway because the thing on the throne had decided winter was not weather but law.

Ash's blood was on her sleeve. Not his—she had checked twice, hands shaking, pressing her palms against his ribs while the wolves held the doors behind them—but one of the wardens', flung across him when he tore its throat out, and it had soaked through her coat and dried stiff in the cold, and she could not stop touching it, as if touching it proved he was alive.

"Say it again," Ash said.

"You're not hurt."

"Not that." His eyes were gold in the milk-light, wolf-gold, and they did not leave her face. "The other thing."

She swallowed. The words were still true; they had been true since the frozen gardens, since the ice cracked under their feet and she had said *I love you* the way you say a name you've been carrying too long without saying. "I love you," she said. "I love you, and I'm going to spend it, and don't you dare argue."

"I'm not arguing." His voice was rough. "I'm memorizing."

Behind them, the doors boomed. Something enormous struck them from the corridor—the last of the warden-guard, or the cold itself wearing armor—and the hinges groaned, and the wolves' growl rolled through the hall like a low sustained note. They would hold. Ash had promised they would hold, and wolves kept their promises the way rivers kept their courses: absolutely, and at whatever cost.

Juniper walked toward the throne.

Each step was a ledger entry. She knew this because she was, at the bottom of everything she was, a keeper of accounts, and even now—even here—her mind insisted on recording. *One step. Cost: breath. One step. Cost: the memory of my mother's kitchen, already sold, already gone, so no cost there.* The floor was slick with frost and her boots found purchase where none should have been, as if the room were letting her come, as if the crown wanted an audience for what happened next.

King Aldous sat slumped in the throne like a man set down there by someone else and told to wait.

He was dying. She could see it now, up close, in a way she had not been able to see it at the royal audit all those weeks ago, when he had smiled wrong and blinked too seldom and she had thought *monster* and been afraid. He was dying the way a candle dies when the wax is gone but the wick keeps burning—thinly, stubbornly, on nothing. His skin was the gray-white of paper left too long in a window. Frost furred his beard. And his eyes—

His eyes were his own. That was the horror of it. Whatever the crown had hollowed, whatever it had eaten and worn and paraded around court for a hundred years, it had never managed to eat the part of Aldous that looked out from behind his face, and that part had been watching, trapped, for a century.

His lips moved. No sound came. Juniper leaned close, close enough to feel the cold radiating off him like heat off a forge, only inverted, a forge of un-heat, and heard the whisper:

"Don't—" A crack in the voice, like ice on a pond. "Don't save me. If it costs—let me go. Let me go and keep your—"

"I know," Juniper said gently. "You asked me once already. In the mirror. I remember."

"That wasn't—" His hand twitched against the armrest, fingers clicking with frost. "That wasn't me asking. That was me begging. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "And I'm refusing both."

Something moved in his ruined face—not a smile, nothing so simple. Recognition, maybe. Relief. The look of a man who has argued his case for a hundred years to an empty courtroom and finally sees the judge walk in.

Above his head, the crown began to laugh.

It was not sound, exactly. It was the sensation of sound, felt in the teeth and the breastbone and the backs of the eyes, a vibration like the moment before glass breaks. Snow lifted off the throne steps and hung in the air, suspended, turning slowly, each flake catching the milk-light. The temperature dropped so fast that the moisture in Juniper's next breath crystallized and stung her throat like swallowing needles.

*LEDGER-KEEPER.*

The voice filled the room the way water fills a hull. It came from everywhere—from the frost on the pillars, from the hanging snow, from the dead air itself.

*I KNOW YOUR LEDGER. I HAVE READ IT FOR MONTHS THROUGH MY WARDENS' EYES. YOU HAVE ALREADY PAID SO MUCH. YOUR MOTHER'S VOICE. YOUR SLEEP. YOUR YEARS OF CAREFUL INVISIBLE LIFE, SPENT LIKE A DRUNKARD IN THE MARKET OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS.*

Juniper stood at the foot of the dais and made herself look up at it. The crown sat on Aldous's head like a spider on an egg. Up close she could see what the palace library's records had only described: that it was not metal, not entirely. That beneath the gold there was a grain, a structure, pale and dense and ancient—a fingerbone, the last joint of a god of winter, hammered flat and circled and set with points of ice that had never melted, not in a hundred years, not in a thousand summers.

*I OFFERED YOU A KINGDOM,* the crown said. *YOU REFUSED. VERY WELL. THEN I WILL TAKE WHAT YOU CAME TO GIVE, AND I WILL TAKE IT ALL, AND I WILL STILL BE HERE WHEN YOUR CITY IS A FIELD OF ICE AND YOUR GRANDCHILDREN'S GRANDCHILDREN HAVE FORGOTTEN THE WORD FOR SUMMER.*

"No, you won't," Juniper said.

Her voice surprised her. It was steady. Somewhere between the Cold Room and these stairs, terror had burned down to something else—not courage exactly, courage was a word for people who hadn't done the accounting yet—but clarity. The clarity of a final column. Everything above it was spent. Only this remained.

"Sorrel left me instructions," she said. "You ate her book. You ate her ghost. But you couldn't eat what she wrote in the place you weren't looking—in the guild ledgers, in the margins, in the boring parts, because you are a thing of hunger and hunger never reads the fine print." She began to climb the dais steps. "The rite is simple. Simpler than anything the Guild ever traded. Equal weight for equal weight. A reign for a reign. You took a hundred years of a man's life—his coronation, his marriage, his wars, his griefs, every morning he woke up being king. To unmake that, I have to give you something that weighs the same."

*AND WHAT COULD YOU POSSIBLY—*

"Not quantity," Juniper said. "Weight. Sorrel was very clear. It isn't how much memory. It's how much it holds." She reached the top step. Aldous watched her with his dying eyes, and she saw—or imagined she saw, and chose to believe it—one tear freeze in the corner of one eye, a small bright stone. "A whole reign weighs less than some things. I've audited stranger entries than that."

She turned to Ash.

He had followed her to the base of the dais and no further, because she had asked him not to, because the rite required her hands free and her heart unguarded and she could not do either with him touching her. He stood in the milk-light with blood on his jaw and frost in his dark hair, and he was looking at her the way wolves look at the moon: helplessly, completely, knowing the distance cannot be crossed and loving it anyway.

"No," he said. Quietly. He had known—he must have known since the gardens, since she'd gone quiet in a particular way she went quiet when doing sums—and still he said it, because he was Ash, and he would say it with his last breath. "Juniper. Take mine instead. Take my memories of you. They weigh the same, they must, they weigh more—"

"They're not mine to give." She smiled, and her face cracked with the cold, and she smiled through the cracking. "That's the whole point of a price, love. It has to be yours. It has to *hurt you* to pay it, or it's counterfeit, and the crown knows counterfeit. It's been eating counterfeit kings for a century."

"Then find another way—"

"There isn't one. Sorrel looked for a hundred years. Hesse looked for fifty. There isn't one, and the snow is falling in Harvest Month, and the city is freezing by the hour, and I am the ledger-keeper with the beasts' tongue, and this—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, she didn't smooth it away, "—this is the entry."

The doors boomed again, harder. A hinge shrieked. Somewhere beyond, a wolf cried out in pain, and Ash's head snapped toward the sound, torn, and Juniper used that half-second to cross the last distance and take his face in her hands.

His fur was soft against her palms where it shadowed his jaw. His breath was warm—the only warm thing in the whole white world—and it fogged between them.

"Listen to me," she said. "In a minute, I'm not going to know you. I need you to hear this while I still can say it, because after, it'll be true but I won't know why, and that seems unbearable, so: I love you. I loved you on the roof with the city wolves. I loved you at the black auction when we stole back a page with a dead woman's handwriting on it. I loved you in the frozen gardens, and I loved you every ordinary hour between, and I am going to pour all of it into that crown until it drowns, and I want you to know—I need you to know—that I would make this trade a thousand times. In a thousand winters. It isn't a sacrifice if the alternative is a world without you in it, walking around, being warm."

"Juniper—"

"It's a bargain," she said. "And I drive a hard bargain. Everyone at the Guild knows it."

He laughed—a broken, wet sound, half sob—and pressed his forehead to hers, and for three heartbeats, four, five, they simply breathed together in the cold, two hidden things that had found each other, and she catalogued it, furiously, desperately, the way she catalogued everything: the exact warmth of his skin, the small scar through his eyebrow, the way his lashes frosted, the smell of pine and smoke and wolf that she had loved in secret long before she'd had a name to put to it.

*Keep it all,* she told herself. *Every entry. Because you can only give away what you truly hold.*

Then she kissed him—once, thoroughly, a signature on the last page—and stepped back, and turned to the throne.

* * *

The rite had no circle to draw, no words in any language. Sorrel's marginal note had been maddeningly plain: *Hold the memory in mind. Hold the crown. Mean it.*

Meaning it, Juniper thought, climbing the last steps to the dying king, would be the easy part.

She reached out and touched the crown.

Cold went up her arm like a scream. Not pain—worse, an *auditing*, a vast dispassionate attention turning over everything she was, page by page, looking for weaknesses, looking for the cheap memories, the ones she wouldn't miss, the ones she might be persuaded to settle for. She felt it paw through her childhood, her ledgers, her mother's kitchen (already sold, already a blank, and the blank itself a wound the crown probed with something like appetite). She felt it find Ash and pause over him, savoring, the way a miser savors a chest he believes is already his.

*YES,* the crown said, deep in her skull. *GIVE ME THE WOLF. GIVE ME THE LOVE. I WILL KEEP IT AS I KEPT THE KING. I WILL WEAR IT.*

"You misunderstand the transaction," Juniper said through chattering teeth. "You don't get to keep it. You get to *drown* in it."

She closed her eyes.

And she opened the ledger of her heart.

Not gradually. All at once, the way you break a dam, because Sorrel's note had warned her: *do it wholly or not at all; a partial payment will only feed it.* So she gave it everything in a single flood—the roof at dusk, the wolves' voices in her head like silver bells, and Ash climbing over the parapet in human shape, awkward and beautiful, saying her name like a question he already knew the answer to. The market of impossible things, his hand finding hers in the dark under the auctioneer's cage. The frozen gardens, snow in his hair, *say it again*, and her saying it, saying it, saying it. Every night at her window, the amber eyes. The way he slept curled with his back to a wall, always, even in human shape, even safe. The way he said *memorizing*. The way he looked at her across a room full of danger as though the danger were furniture.

She poured it out.

She felt it leave her the way heat leaves a house when the door stands open in January—not ripped, not stolen, *draining*, willingly, in a torrent, and with it went the color of the world. She had not known that love was a sense until she lost it. But she felt it go, and the world went grayer and quieter and smaller with every heartbeat, and somewhere far away a man was shouting her name in a voice breaking apart, and she noted, distantly, with the last of her ledger-keeper's precision: *I know that voice matters. I no longer know why.*

The crown screamed.

It was screaming in a hundred voices—the young king's voice, the wardens' voices, the voice of the god whose fingerbone it had been, all of them drowning together in a flood of warm memory, of spring, of a woman's laugh and a wolf's devotion, and the cold that had ruled the room for a century *recoiled*. The frost on the pillars blackened and ran. The hanging snowflakes fell straight down, all at once, like a curtain dropping. The milk-light in the windows flushed gold, then green, then the honest colors of the painted wheat field, and the first king on the glass seemed almost to bow.

Juniper held on.

Her hands were fused to the crown with ice and she pried them loose finger by finger, and each finger cost her something she could not name anymore, a weight leaving, a column closing, and she did not stop, because the accounting was not finished. Beneath the crown, King Aldous was changing. The gray was draining out of his skin. His body sagged forward, suddenly heavy, suddenly *old*—a hundred years arriving all at once, every one of them his own again, and his eyes cleared, and he looked at her, and he was only a man, an old, tired, freed man, and he mouthed:

*Thank you. Thank you. Tell my son the kingdom was never—*

He died mid-word. Gently. Like a lamp going out in a warm room. Free.

The crown came apart in her hands.

It did not shatter; it *unraveled*, the gold unwinding into threads, the threads into light, and beneath the gold the fingerbone showed for one instant—pale, dense, ancient, and then it too softened, and warmed, and became what it had been before a king's smiths had hammered it into a circle of hunger: bone becoming dust becoming nothing, a god's last remnant finally allowed to end. The points of unmelted ice melted. Of course they melted. They had only ever been waiting for permission.

The last thread of gold drifted past Juniper's cheek and dissolved.

Silence.

The throne room was just a room. Warmth was seeping back into it from somewhere—from the stones themselves, from the thawing glass, from the ordinary world reasserting its terms. Through the tall windows, past the painted wheat, the sky over the city was still boiling with storm-clouds, but the clouds were *breaking*, tearing open along seams of blue, and the snow that had swallowed the streets in hours was slackening, thinning, losing its conviction.

"Juniper."

The voice came from very far away. From the bottom of the stairs. A man's voice, rough and desperate, and she turned toward it politely, the way one turns toward a stranger addressing one in the street.

He was standing at the foot of the dais with blood on his jaw and tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face, and he was looking at her with an expression she recognized the way you recognize a word in a language you used to speak: *the shape of it is familiar; the meaning is gone.*

"You're safe," he said. His voice shook. "It's over. Do you—"

He stopped. She watched him see it—watched the hope go out of him in real time, a slow collapse, as she looked at him with the courteous blankness of a ledger-keeper regarding an unfamiliar entry. Behind him the great doors stood open, and wolves were flowing into the hall, huge and gray and steaming in the returning warmth, and every one of them stopped when they saw her face.

One of them whined. Low, questioning, terrible.

Juniper looked at the man, and at the wolves, and at the dead king smiling faintly in his thawing throne, and at the gold dust still settling through the golden light like the aftermath of fireworks, and she did what she had always done, the only thing she had ever been good at.

She reached for her ledger to record what had happened.

And found the pages where he lived torn clean out.

* * *

Later—minutes later, hours, she would never be sure—she stood at a window in the wrecked palace while healers moved around her and the city below rang its bells, all of them, every tower at once, the sound rolling over the rooftops like the world exhaling. Snowmelt ran in the gutters. Somewhere a child was shouting about the sun.

At the window, a wolf sat watching her through the glass. Big, dark, patient. Its amber eyes did not leave her face.

"Hello," Juniper said kindly, because she liked animals, she had always liked animals, everyone knew that about her.

The wolf made a sound she had never heard an animal make. It was almost a word. It was almost a name—*her* name—and then it lay down on the frozen balcony outside the window, put its head on its paws, and waited, the way you wait for spring.

Juniper pressed her hand flat against the cold glass, for no reason she could name, and felt her heart ache in a place that had no entry, no date, no explanation.

Only a hole, shaped exactly like a wolf.
