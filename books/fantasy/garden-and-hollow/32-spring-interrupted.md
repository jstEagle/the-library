## 32. Spring, Interrupted

The first thing Juniper felt was warmth on her face, and the second thing was wrongness, because she could not remember why warmth should feel like an apology.

She opened her eyes to a ceiling of pale plaster veined with water stains, and light coming through a window in long gold bars, and somewhere below her a city making noise — real noise, the ordinary racket of carts and vendors and a dog losing an argument with a cat. Not the silence of ice. Not the creak and groan of a world freezing from the inside out.

She sat up too fast and had to press a hand against the mattress until the room stopped tilting.

A bed. A strange bed. Her own room above the guildhall, she realized after a moment — her narrow iron-framed bed, her ledger shelves, her one good chair with the worn arm. But the window stood open to air that smelled of wet stone and green things, and the last time she had looked out that window—

The last time she had looked out that window.

She went to it. The gardens below were drowning. Snowmelt ran off every roof in silver ropes, pooled in the courtyards, turned the lanes to brown rivers that children were already sailing paper boats down. The palace hill gleamed white-gold where the frost was letting go of it in great sloughing sheets. On the horizon, the northern mountains had their winter caps back, but the low country was bare and black and steaming in the sun like something breathing again after holding its breath for a very long time.

Spring. Out-of-season spring, arriving all at once, as if the year had been robbed and was now being repaid with interest.

Juniper stood at the window a long while, waiting for joy.

It did not come. What came instead was a feeling she had no ledger-entry for: a hollowness behind her sternum, clean-edged, precise, like a space where something had been kept and was kept no longer. It did not hurt, exactly. That was the terrible part. It was the absence of hurt, the absence of anything, in a place that clearly ought to hold something enormous.

She pressed her palm flat against her breastbone, the way you'd press a hand against a door to confirm it was really shut.

"Hello?" she said softly, to herself, to whatever lived in that empty room inside her.

Nothing answered. And yet — and this she would remember later, turning it over like a coin she couldn't identify — some part of her flinched at the word *hello*, as if it were not the first word she'd said to that silence.

* * *

They told her she had been asleep for nine days.

Hesse told her, actually, standing in her doorway with his hat in his hands — Hesse, who never took his hat off indoors, looking suddenly old, his face slack with a grief he seemed to be carrying carefully, like a full cup.

"Nine days," she repeated. "And before that?"

"Before that," Hesse said, "you ended the Long Winter."

He said it plainly, without ceremony, and then he stood there as if he expected her to say something back, and when she didn't — when she only looked at him with what she could feel was a blank, polite incomprehension — something moved through his face that looked almost like fear.

"You don't—" He stopped. Started again. "What do you remember?"

She took inventory aloud, because inventory was the one language she trusted. "I remember the guild. The ledgers. The prophecy book. Sorrel." Saying the name made her chest ache in a way she couldn't source, a bruise pressing from the inside. "Sorrel burned the book. I remember fire, and snow, and going to the palace. I remember—" She frowned. "There's a gap. A large one. After the throne room."

"The rite," Hesse said quietly. "The unbinding. You performed it, Juniper. You paid the price."

"What price?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — watched the truth come up to his mouth and get swallowed back down, an old habit of his, a guildsman's habit, weighing what a thing costs before spending it.

"A memory," he said finally. "Of equal weight to a king's reign. That's what the rite required. Whatever you gave, you gave whole. The crown took it into itself and unraveled with it." His voice roughened. "You saved us all. The king died free — truly free, at the end. The wardens melted like candle wax. The packs—" He stopped there, and something flickered across his face, quick as a fish under ice. "The wolves broke the siege and vanished north the same hour the thaw began. No one knows why."

"No one knows why," Juniper repeated.

"It doesn't matter now," Hesse said, too quickly. "What matters is that you're alive, and the kingdom is thawing, and the guild owes you a debt we can never—"

"Stop." She held up a hand. Her voice came out flat and cold and she didn't know where the coldness came from either. "Don't owe me things, Hesse. Don't thank me. I can't—" Her hand drifted to her chest again, that hollow place, that clean-edged nothing. "Something's missing. Something big. I can feel the shape of it, the way you feel the shape of a tooth that's gone. Every time my tongue finds the gap."

Hesse's eyes were wet. "I know," he said. "I'm so sorry, child. I know exactly what you lost."

*Then tell me,* she wanted to scream. *You know. Say it.*

But he only put his hat back on, and bowed, and left her alone with the sound of melting snow running down the gutters like the whole city was crying and didn't know why.

* * *

The city spent the next week celebrating, and Juniper spent it walking through the celebrations like a ghost attending someone else's feast.

They had already given her a name in the streets — the Thaw-Bringer, the Ledger-Saint, the Girl Who Paid. Ballads were being composed badly in taverns; she heard three competing versions through her window on the second night, each one more wrong than the last. In the market square they'd raised a banner with her guild-sigil stitched beside a rising sun, and flower-sellers sold little paper crowns to burn in effigy of the old cold king, and children played a game called *unbinding* in which one child was wrapped in gray scarves and the others unwound them, shrieking, victorious.

Juniper bought a meat pie from a vendor who refused her coin — "Not from you, mistress, never from you" — and ate it standing in the square, watching the paper crowns burn, and felt absolutely nothing where everything should have been.

That was the shape of it, she was learning. Not sadness. Sadness would have been a mercy, because sadness has an object, a name, a weight you can carry or set down. This was grief with the object amputated — mourning that reached for its reason and closed on empty air, over and over, all day, every day. She would catch herself smiling at something and the smile would fall off her face mid-curve. She would turn to say something to someone who wasn't there. Twice she woke in the night reaching across the cold half of her bed, hand open, fingers curled to hold — what? She lay in the dark afterward staring at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger who knew something she didn't.

Her body remembered. That was the horror of it. Her body was a ledger someone had torn pages out of, and the stubs of the missing entries caught on everything.

* * *

On the fourth day she went back to work, because work was the only prayer she knew.

The guildhall was half-drowned — meltwater had gotten into the deep archive, and clerks moved through the lower stacks in rolled trousers hauling sodden ledgers into the light, laying them open on every flat surface like patients. The smell of wet vellum and ink filled the building. Juniper rolled up her sleeves and joined them without asking anyone's permission, and for a few hours the hollow place in her chest went quiet, because there is no room in a person for emptiness when both hands are full of someone else's ruined accounts.

It was in the archive, wringing out a ledger of grain futures from the Year of the False Drought, that she found the wolf.

Not a wolf. A drawing of one — pressed between the waterlogged pages like a flower, done in charcoal on a scrap of cheap paper. It was good. It was very good. Whoever had drawn it had known wolves, had known this wolf: the lean skull, the ears slightly asymmetric, one nicked at the edge, the eyes rendered with a care that bordered on devotion. On the back, in a hurried hand, a single line:

*He watches her window. Says he'll wait as long as it takes. — S.*

Juniper sat down slowly on a crate of ruined maps.

*He.* *Her.* She read the note four times. The paper trembled in her fingers and she could not have said why. S. — Sorrel. Sorrel, who had burned herself out of existence to free her, whose name still ached in Juniper's chest like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. Sorrel had written this. Sorrel had known this wolf, this *he*, this watcher-at-windows.

And the drawing — she touched the charcoal eyes, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal — the drawing made her want to weep, sudden and violent and completely unmotivated, tears standing in her eyes before she had decided anything at all.

"What is this?" she asked the empty archive. "Who is this?"

No one answered. Somewhere above her, the thaw ticked and dripped off the eaves, patient as a clock.

She put the drawing in her breast pocket, over the hollow place, and told herself it meant nothing.

She carried it everywhere for the rest of the day anyway.

* * *

The beasts found her on the sixth day.

She was on the guild roof at dusk — an old habit, though she couldn't have said what the habit was *for* anymore — when the pigeons came. They arrived in a loose gray spiral and settled along the roof ridge around her, closer than pigeons ever came to people, and the eldest of them, a ragged old cock with one clouded eye, tipped his head and spoke.

*"Thaw-bringer. Sister-of-ledgers. You are well?"*

"I'm well," she said automatically, and then stopped, because the words had come out in answer without passing through thought, and because the pigeon's speech had settled into her ear like a key into a lock she hadn't known was hers. Beast-speech. Her forbidden gift. It had survived whatever the crown had taken — of course it had; it was woven into her, not stored in her — and hearing it again after nine days of human noise was like stepping into warm water.

*"You are not well,"* the old pigeon said, with the blunt tactlessness of his kind. *"Your heart smells wrong. Like a den after the pups are gone."*

"I don't know what you mean," Juniper said.

The pigeons exchanged looks. There was a rustle among them, a shuffling, the particular silence of animals deciding whether to say a difficult thing. Then a young hen at the edge of the group bobbed forward and said, brightly, helpfully, disastrously:

*"Is the wolf coming back tonight? We saw him at the wall again at dawn."*

The words went through Juniper like a blade finding the exact gap in her armor, the exact missing tooth, the exact hollow room.

"Wolf," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her. "What wolf?"

The young hen blinked. *"The wolf. Your wolf. The one who—"*

*"Enough,"* the old cock snapped, and there was something in his voice — pity, terrible pigeon pity — as he looked at her. *"She does not remember. Can't you see? The price took it. Took him out of her."*

The rooftop seemed to tilt. Juniper gripped the parapet. "Took *who* out of me," she said. "Tell me. Tell me what I lost."

The pigeons looked at one another. And then, one by one, they began to speak — hesitantly at first, then all together, tumbling over each other, a gray chorus of testimony: the wolf on the roof in the early snows, the wolf who walked as a man, the wolf named Ash who came south for the packs and stayed for her, the wolf who fought through frozen warden-guard, the wolf who carried her out of the burning quarter, the wolf who loved her, the wolf she loved, the wolf who waited, the wolf who waited, the wolf who was waiting still.

Juniper listened to all of it standing very straight, with her hands flat on the parapet, and understood that she was weeping without feeling sad, which was somehow worse than any grief — tears falling steadily down her face while the hollow place behind her sternum stayed exactly as it was, a wound that could not bleed because it had been cauterized by magic, sealed by the price she had paid gladly and could not regret and could not even mourn properly, because mourning requires knowing what you buried.

When they finished, the old cock said gently: *"Do you want us to stop telling you?"*

"No," Juniper said. Her voice cracked on it. "No. Tell me again tomorrow. Tell me every day. Tell me everything, all of it, however small — what he liked, how he stood, what he called me. I'm a ledger-keeper." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, hard. "If my own heart won't hold the record anymore, then by every god in the pantheon, my books will."

* * *

That night she cleared her desk of everything else and set down a fresh ledger, new-bound, its first page blank and white as the snow that had nearly eaten the world.

At the top, in her careful clerk's hand, she wrote:

**A LEDGER OF TESTIMONY, CONCERNING THE WOLF**

Then she paused, pen hovering, and beneath it added, smaller:

**Kept by Juniper Pemberton, who is told she loved him.**

She lit a lamp. Below the window, the city dripped and glistened, thawing, healing, celebrating its salvation. In her breast pocket, the charcoal wolf watched her with his devoted, asymmetrical eyes.

Somewhere out there — at a wall, at a window, at the edge of the dark — something was waiting for her, patiently, the way winter waits, the way spring waits. Something had given her up rather than take her back by force. Something loved her enough to be a stranger to her forever if that was the price of what she'd chosen.

She dipped her pen.

*Entry the first,* she wrote. *Source: the pigeons of the guild roof. They say his name was Ash.*

The pen stopped. Her breath stopped. For one suspended moment the hollow place behind her sternum rang like a struck glass, a single clear note of *almost* —

—and then it faded, and the gap was a gap again, and Juniper Pemberton sat alone in the lamplight with a name that meant nothing and everything, and began, entry by entry, testimony by testimony, to write her way back toward a heart she could not remember owning.
