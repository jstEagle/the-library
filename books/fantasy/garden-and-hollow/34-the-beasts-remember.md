## 34. The Beasts Remember

The first testimony came from a pigeon with a chipped beak and the manner of an old soldier.

Juniper had been restocking the restitution ledgers in the rebuilt guildhall's ground floor—new shelves, new ink, the old sigil sanded off the door and replaced with an open hand—when the pigeon landed on her windowsill and said, in the flat, gravelly way of birds who have outlived their beauty: *You knew him.*

She did not look up from her column of figures. "I know many someones."

*The wolf,* the pigeon said. *The one who waited at your window through the whole white month. You fed him the ends of your bread and pretended you didn't smile when he caught it midair.*

Her pen stopped.

The trouble with forgetting was that it was not like a page torn out. A torn page left ragged edges; you could feel where it had been, could run your thumb along the stubs of what remained. What she had lost had been cut clean, cauterized by the rite itself, so that there was no wound to map—only a smooth place in her chest where something enormous used to live, and a strange reflexive warmth whenever she passed the north window of her rooms.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she told the pigeon.

*No,* the pigeon agreed, preening. *That's rather the point.* And it flew off before she could ask it anything else, because pigeons were pigeons, even the ones who testified.

She sat for a long time with her pen hovering over the ledger, leaving a small spreading blot on the page. Then, because she was Juniper Pemberton, and because a life spent keeping accounts does not simply stop keeping them, she turned to a fresh leaf and wrote at the top, in her smallest, most careful hand:

**A New Ledger: Testimony Concerning a Wolf Whom I Am Told I Loved.**

Underneath, she entered the date, the witness, and the claim.

*Witness: pigeon (chipped beak). Claim: subject fed wolf bread at north window; smiled involuntarily. Corroboration: pending.*

It was, she thought grimly, the strangest audit of her career. And she had once audited a bottled echo.

* * *

They came to her after that. She never asked them to—the beasts simply began arriving, as though word had passed along whatever invisible wires connected sparrow to sparrow, cat to cat, the great shaggy cart-horses of the delivery yards to the rats in the guildhall walls. Word traveled among beasts the way rumor traveled among clerks: fast, embellished, and impossible to fully trace.

The baker's dog came first, a brindled lurcher named Crumb who sat in her office doorway with the gravity of a magistrate.

*You loved a wolf,* Crumb said. *He slept under your desk during the thaw. He let me put my head on him. He smelled like snow and pine and, underneath, like you—he'd been near you so long he'd taken on your scent. That's how I knew he wasn't just any wolf.*

"Did he," Juniper said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "Have a name?"

Crumb tilted his head. *Ash. But you know that.*

"I don't."

*You do,* the dog said, not unkindly. *You just can't reach it. There's a difference. Ask any bone buried too deep—it's still there. The digging is the hard part.*

She wrote it down. *Witness: Crumb (lurcher). Claim: name "Ash"; scent-mingling indicates prolonged proximity; subject permitted physical contact.* Her hand shook only slightly on the last line.

The cats were less helpful. Cats always were. The guildhall's mouser, a one-eared tortoiseshell called Duchess, delivered her testimony while grooming herself with theatrical indifference.

*A wolf,* Duchess confirmed. *Enormous. Yellow-eyed. Terribly possessive of you—he once chased me across three rooftops for sitting on your coat.*

"That sounds like a dog problem, not a wolf problem."

*He didn't catch me,* Duchess said, which Juniper understood to mean the wolf had not actually been trying. *But here is what your dogs won't tell you, clerk, because dogs only remember the good parts. In the cold days—the bad days, when the ice was on the inside of the windows—I saw him carry you. You were asleep or near it, and he carried you through the frozen gardens in his arms, in his man-shape, and his face over you was the face I make over my kittens. I have seen kings. I have seen the thing that wore the king. That was neither. That was a creature holding the most precious thing in the world and knowing he might lose it by morning.*

Juniper's pen had stopped moving again.

"What happened by morning?" she asked quietly.

Duchess looked at her then, one green eye and one gold, with an expression of vast feline pity. *By morning you had forgotten him. And he stayed anyway. That's the part you should write down, clerk. Not the carrying. The staying.*

*Witness: Duchess (tortoiseshell, one ear). Claim: subject observed carrying witness-in-question in human form during Long Winter; expression characterized as parental/tender. Post-forgetting behavior: remained present regardless of absence of recognition.*

She read that entry back three times that night, by candlelight, in her rooms above the guildhall. Then she opened the north window, because she could not have said why, and stood looking out at the city until the cold drove her back to bed.

There was no wolf below. Of course there wasn't.

Except that the frost on the sill, when she checked in the morning, bore a single broad pawprint, already half-sublimed, as if something had sat there a long while in the dark and then thought better of being found.

* * *

The horses were the worst.

Not because they knew more than the others—they knew less, really, horses being honest creatures with little talent for nuance—but because they were so plainly grieved on her behalf. The delivery teams in the yard behind the guildhall would go quiet when she passed, all their great heads swinging toward her at once, and the oldest of them, a gray gelding called Barnaby who had pulled carts through the Long Winter and come out the other side with ice-scars on his hocks, made it his business to speak to her every single day.

*Good morning, keeper,* he'd say. *Still no memory?*

"Still none."

*Pity. He liked you best of anyone. He told me so. Wolves don't usually talk to horses—we're too much like dinner—but he talked to me. Said you were the only person in the world who had ever heard him properly. Said everyone else heard a wolf and flinched, but you heard *him*. Do you understand what that means to one of us, keeper? To be heard as yourself?*

"No," Juniper said honestly. "I don't think I do."

*Well,* Barnaby said, stamping one scarred hoof. *Now you know what you gave away.*

She wrote it down that evening with a steadier hand than she felt. *Witness: Barnaby (gray gelding). Claim: subject stated witness-in-question was "the only person who ever heard him properly." Interpretation: gift of beast-speech constituted foundation of bond.* She stared at that last line a long while. It had not occurred to her—not once, in all these weeks of testimony—that the love the beasts described might have grown from the same root as her treason. The secret she had hidden her whole life, the gift that could have gotten her killed, had been, to one creature in this world, not a crime but a homecoming.

She had traded away the memory of being known. No wonder the hole in her chest was shaped like that.

* * *

The rats, unexpectedly, provided the dates.

Rats kept excellent calendars—grain shipments, festival scraps, the rhythms of a city's hunger—and the colony under the guildhall had a historian, an ancient female with half a tail and a memory like a trap, whom the other rodents referred to only as Grandmother. Juniper found her one night in the archive cellar, sitting atop a box of confiscated ledgers like a small gray queen.

*You want the timeline,* Grandmother said, without preamble. *All the others give you feelings. Feelings are cheap. I'll give you sequence.*

"I would be grateful."

*Very well. First frost in harvest month: we remember, because the grain froze in the granaries and feasting was easy that year. Your wolf arrived in the city four days later, ahead of the packs, asking questions of the crows. The crows will confirm; they gossip worse than we do. He found you within two days. From that point he was never more than a street away. When they took you to the Cold Room, he went north and came back with an army. When the book burned, he carried you out of the fire quarter. And on the last night—"*

Grandmother paused. Even her rodent composure flickered.

*"On the last night, we were in the walls of the throne room. We heard everything. We heard you tell the crown what you would pay. And we heard him beg you not to. Did you know that? He begged. He offered himself instead—his memories, his life, anything the crown would take in trade. And you told him—"*

"Stop," Juniper said.

Grandmother stopped. Her whiskers trembled.

"I—" Juniper pressed the heel of her hand against the smooth place in her chest, the place that ached without an object, the place that had ached every day since spring. "I need to enter this in order. Witness, claim, date. I can't—if you keep talking I'll lose the shape of it."

*The shape of it,* Grandmother repeated softly. *Yes. You are very like him. He also needed things in order. It's why you suited.* She gathered herself, ancient and precise. *Witness: Grandmother of the guildhall colony. Claim: subject attempted to substitute himself as payment during the unbinding rite; subject refused by witness-in-question; subject restrained by packmates during final phase. Date: the last night of the Long Winter.*

Juniper wrote it all down. Her handwriting, when she finished, looked like someone else's.

* * *

She built the ledger into an institution, because that was the only way she knew how to survive a thing: give it columns, give it rules, give it a locked drawer and a regular hour. Every evening after the restitution work was done, she climbed to her rooms, lit two candles, and took testimony. Sparrows reported on his habits (he did not like bridges; he crossed them at a run). The palace ravens reported on his movements (he lived now in the northern hills, but came south; he came south often; he was seen on the guildhall roof more nights than not). A moth-eaten circus bear who had wintered in the city reported, at length and with many digressions, that the wolf had once bought a honey cake in human shape and eaten it standing in an alley, staring at her window the entire time, and the bear had thought this very sad, and also had wanted the rest of the honey cake.

Page by page, the ledger grew. And page by page, Juniper noticed something she should have noticed sooner, because noticing patterns in entries was the one thing she had never been bad at.

Every witness, eventually, said the same closing thing.

The pigeon: *He waits.*

Crumb: *He's still waiting.*

Duchess: *He has never once tried to make you remember. Ask yourself why, clerk.*

Barnaby: *He said if you came back to him, it had to be because you chose it. Not because he dug you up. He was very firm about that. Horses respect firmness.*

Even Grandmother, dry as dust and twice as old: *He could have told you everything in a single night, keeper. One conversation. Instead he sends nothing, says nothing, sits on your roof in the dark like a gargoyle with better posture. There is a word for a love that refuses to collect its debt. I am a rat and I know the word. Why don't you?*

Juniper knew the word. She had known it since the third week, since the pawprint on the frosted sill. She simply could not say it aloud yet, because saying it aloud would make the ledger what it truly was—not an investigation, not an audit, but a courtship conducted entirely through intermediaries, a woman reading herself into love like a clerk reconstructing a burned archive from fragments, and she did not know if what she was rebuilding was real or whether she was simply the kind of person who fell for a well-documented stranger.

So she kept the ledger. She verified claims. She sought corroboration. She noted discrepancies (the sparrows insisted he was grim; the circus bear insisted he laughed at least once, over the honey cake) and resolved them as best she could.

And every night, before she blew out the candles, she read the whole ledger through from the beginning, the way she had once read the prophecy book—hunting for the ending that had been torn out, except this time the missing page was in her own skull, and no black auction in the world sold those.

* * *

On the first of the autumn month, exactly one year to the day since the first impossible frost, the testimony changed.

She was in the yard at dusk, checking the delivery manifests against Barnaby's account of the day's routes—a habit now, a ritual, the old horse pretending to find it tedious and fooling no one—when Barnaby lifted his head sharply toward the gate and went silent in a way horses rarely did.

Juniper turned.

At the mouth of the alley beyond the guildhall wall stood a wolf. Enormous, gray-and-ash, yellow-eyed, exactly as every witness had described him, exactly as her own smooth blank chest had somehow known he would be. He did not approach. He did not speak. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with the stillness of a creature who had rehearsed this moment for a year and was determined not to ruin it.

*That's him,* Barnaby said gently. *In case the heart you can't reach needs it confirmed.*

"I know," Juniper said. "I mean—I don't know. But the ledger knows. Every entry matches."

The wolf's ears tipped forward. He had heard her. Of course he had heard her; he had probably heard every word of every testimony session through the roof beams for months, and had stayed away anyway, because staying away was apparently what love looked like when it refused to collect.

She walked toward him slowly, the way one approaches a wild thing, although every animal in the yard was telling her—in the pressure of Barnaby's breath, in the sudden hush of the sparrows—that this was no wild thing, that this was the tamest thing in the city, tamed a year ago and exiled for it and returned regardless.

Ten feet away, she stopped.

"You're Ash," she said. It was not a question. It was an entry, read aloud. *Name: Ash. Source: corroborated across eleven witnesses.*

Something moved through his whole body at the sound of his own name in her voice—a shiver, a flinch, a joy too large for a body that size—and he sat down in the alley mouth like a dog at a hearth, like a suitor at a door, and spoke for the first time.

His voice was exactly what the ledger had prepared her for and nothing like enough.

*You've been keeping accounts on me,* he said.

"It's what I do." Her eyes were burning. She blinked it back with clerkly discipline. "I'm told I loved you. I'm told I paid for a kingdom with it. I have forty-one pages of testimony and not one memory, and I would like—" her voice cracked, finally, irrecoverably, "—I would like to know if it's true."

The wolf looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight moved in his yellow eyes.

*It's true,* he said. *And I'll tell you all of it—every page, every date, everything the beasts missed. But not tonight.* He rose, and there was something in his stance she recognized from Duchess's testimony, from the ravens', from her own unreadable heart: the posture of a creature holding the most precious thing in the world and refusing to rush it. *Tonight I only came to say the waiting is done, if you want it done. Tomorrow, at moonrise, I'll be on the roof. If you come, I'll tell you our whole story from the beginning. And if one word of it cracks the seal the rite put in you—even one—then you'll know it's true for yourself, and not from a ledger.*

"And if it doesn't crack?"

*Then I'll tell it again the next night,* Ash said simply. *And the next. I'm told I'm patient.*

He turned and flowed into the dark of the alley, gone between one heartbeat and the next, and Juniper stood alone in the yard with her heart hammering against the smooth place like a fist against a sealed door.

That night she opened the ledger and made a new entry, the shortest one she had ever written:

*Moonrise. The roof. He will tell it all.*

And beneath it, in a hand that shook only a little:

*Hypothesis: the seal was made to hold against grief. No one thought to test it against being loved out loud.*

She closed the book, snuffed the candles, and sat in the dark watching the north window, counting the hours until moonrise.
