## 36. The New King's Debt

* * *

The regent had rehearsed this audience three times, and each rehearsal had failed differently.

Lady Vessa Corrane stood at the tall window of the council chamber and watched the thaw-water run silver down the glass, carrying winter out of the kingdom one drop at a time. Below, in the courtyard, workmen were prying frozen warden-guard armor off men who had been standing in it since the Long Winter fell. Some of them were alive. Vessa had stopped counting which.

On the table behind her lay the instrument that had brought her here: a single sheet of vellum, sealed twice, drawn up in the last lucid hour of a dying reign. *The crown-debt of Aldous, King,* it read, in a hand that shook only once, near the bottom, where the true king had surfaced long enough to sign his own name instead of letting the cold sign for him. *Owed to Juniper Pemberton, ledger-keeper of the Hollow Guild, who unbound what was bound. To be paid in whatever she asks, so far as the crown may give it.*

Whatever she asks.

Vessa had spent thirty years in the machinery of the palace and she knew what that phrase was worth when spoken by chancellors and ambassadors: nothing, and less than nothing, because it invited appetite. She had prepared herself for appetite. She had drafted responses to six likely demands — gold, titles, the guild charter rewritten in Pemberton's favor, the wardens' confiscated estates, a seat on the council itself. She had argued each one aloud in the empty chamber until her voice went hoarse, sharpening refusals like knives.

She had not prepared for the possibility that the woman would ask for something Vessa did not know how to refuse and did not know how to grant.

A knock. Her steward, Pellam, gray-faced as always, gray-souled as always, though even he had begun to smile again now that the fountains ran.

"She's come, my lady."

"Alone?"

"Alone." A pause; Pellam's mouth did something complicated. "Though there is a wolf sitting in the outer courtyard, my lady. Sitting. Like a hound at a door. The guards have been told three times to move it along and three times they've come back saying it looked at them and they decided against it."

Vessa closed her eyes briefly. "Show Mistress Pemberton in. And tell the guards to leave the wolf alone. If it wanted anyone dead, we'd know by now."

"Yes, my lady." Pellam hesitated at the door. "My lady — the guild sent a gift ahead of her. A crate. It's in the antechamber."

"What is it?"

Pellam consulted a slip of paper with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant. "It says: *one ledger, complete, being the record of all shipments of 'cold' made by the Hollow Guild to the palace over the past hundred years, with names, dates, and signatures.*"

The room felt colder for a moment, though spring was winning everywhere else.

"And the card?" Vessa said.

"*So the new reign can begin by knowing everything,*" Pellam read. "*Debts you don't count are the ones that eat you.*"

Vessa looked out the window a moment longer. Then she turned, smoothed her skirts, and sat down at the table across from the empty chair, and waited to meet the woman who had saved the kingdom and intended, apparently, to hand it its own confession.

* * *

Juniper had not been inside the palace since the night the throne room burned with cold light, since she had poured everything she loved into a ring of frozen god-bone and watched it unravel like breath on glass.

She had expected the memory of it to hurt. What she had not expected was how much of the palace she simply did not remember at all — corridors she must have walked during those weeks of investigation, doors she must have opened beside a tall figure whose absence walked next to her like a second shadow. There were gaps in her the size of a person. She had learned to walk around them without looking down.

The new king was eleven years old.

She had known this from the ledgers — succession was, after all, an entry like any other — but knowing and seeing were different things. They received her in a smaller chamber than she'd expected, a room built for family rather than state, and the boy sat in a chair too big for him with his boots not quite touching the floor, and beside him stood the regent, Lady Corrane, iron-gray and iron-eyed, with the look of a woman who had spent a century's worth of caution compressed into a few months.

The boy stared at her. Juniper knew the stare. It was the stare of someone trying to see the place where a miracle had happened.

"You're the one who killed the winter," he said.

"Tomas." The regent's voice was gentle and sharp at once.

"It's all right," Juniper said. "And I didn't kill it. I paid it. There's a difference, and it matters." She bowed — not deep, not shallow; the bow of one professional acknowledging another, because that was what she was, whatever songs the streets were already singing. "Your Majesty. Lady Regent. Thank you for receiving me."

"The debt instrument," Vessa said, laying the vellum on the table between them, "names no sum. My counsel has spent weeks preparing for every demand you might bring. Gold. Land. The guild itself." Her eyes were steady and unhostile, which Juniper recognized as the most dangerous kind of eyes. "I confess we have prepared poorly. So let us not perform. Tell me what you want, Mistress Pemberton, and I will tell you honestly whether the crown can bear it."

Juniper had rehearsed this too, in her own way — not aloud, but in the ledger of her heart, where she kept accounts more carefully than any the guild held. She had turned the question over every night for two months. *What do you want?* Gold was easy to refuse. Power was easy to refuse; she had watched power eat a king from the head downward, and she had no appetite for becoming a smaller crown.

What she wanted had four legs and was currently sitting in the courtyard refusing to be moved along.

But that want was not hers to spend. Not yet. Maybe not ever. So she asked for the thing underneath it.

"Sanctuary," she said. "For the wolves."

The regent blinked. Beside her, the boy-king leaned forward despite himself.

"There are nine northern packs," Juniper went on, "perhaps four hundred animals, coming south ahead of winters that keep arriving wrong. Their hunting grounds are breaking — you'll have seen the reports, if anyone reads the border surveys anymore. They are not invaders. They are refugees with teeth. And right now every village between here and the treeline has a bounty notice nailed to its gate, because for one season the wolves fought beside the city and everyone cheered, and for two seasons before that they were monsters, and people's memories are shorter than their fear." She laid a folded paper on the table — she couldn't help it; she was a ledger-keeper; she had brought figures. "I'm asking the crown to tear down the bounty notices. To declare the northern packs under the king's protection. Hunting rights to the high forest, safe passage through the river valleys in hard seasons, and a standing prohibition on bounties — written into law, not just promised. In exchange—" she tapped the debt instrument "—you may consider your debt paid in full, and cheaply, and I'll sign the release myself."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the thaw dripped its slow arithmetic.

"That's it?" the boy said. "That's what you want? You could have asked for anything."

"Tomas," the regent said again, but softly this time, and Juniper saw the woman's gaze drop to the paper with its columns of figures, and saw the exact moment the regent understood what kind of person she was dealing with. Not a saint. Saints didn't itemize. Something rarer: a person who had power and found the thought of using it on herself slightly obscene.

"Why the wolves?" Vessa asked. It wasn't suspicion, quite. It was due diligence. "You're known as a friend to beasts. But sanctuary is a strange shape for gratitude. Why not ask for the beasts' protection generally — horses, hawks, all of it?"

Because one of them carried my whole heart and I gave it away and I can't even remember his face, Juniper did not say.

"Because the others aren't dying," she said instead. "Horses are fed whether or not anyone loves them. Wolves need law, because law is the only thing humans respect more than fear. And because—" she paused, choosing honesty carefully, like a coin counted out "—because they fought for this city when no one asked them to. Debts to strangers are easy to forget. I keep ledgers. I don't forget."

The regent was quiet for a long moment. Then she did something Juniper did not expect: she turned to the boy.

"Your Majesty," she said. "This is yours to answer. That's why I insisted you sit in on every petition this month. What do you think?"

The boy went pink. But he looked at Juniper — really looked, the way children look, without the film adults wear — and then at the paper of figures, and then at the debt instrument with his uncle's shaking signature at the bottom.

"My tutor says a king should never pay a debt cheaply," he said slowly, "because people will think the crown is weak." He frowned. "But my uncle paid everything he had for a hundred years and it made him weak anyway. It made him into *that*." He stopped, and Juniper saw the grief move through the small body like weather. Everyone in the palace had loved Aldous, she was learning. Even hollowed, even smiling wrong, there had been something in him worth loving, and the boy had loved it, and lost it to a fingerbone.

"So," the boy said, straightening in the too-big chair, "if paying cheap makes you weak, and paying dear makes you hollow, then maybe the trick is paying *true*. And she's telling us the true price. Four hundred wolves and some ink." He looked up at his regent. "Can we afford it?"

"We can afford it," Vessa said, "if we mean it. Law that isn't meant is worse than no law." She rose, and took a pen, and drew a fresh sheet toward her. "Mistress Pemberton. The crown accepts your terms. The bounties come down within the week — I'll have the notices burned publicly, in the market squares, so no one can pretend they missed the news. Safe passage and hunting rights will be drafted as a standing decree under the young king's seal, and I warn you, I will write it in language so plain that no future chancellor can wriggle out of it. I have spent my life watching clever words eat good intentions. This one gets short words." She glanced up. "Will that satisfy you as payment in full?"

"It will," Juniper said, and heard something in her own chest unlock that she hadn't known was locked.

"One condition of mine," Vessa added. "Not of the debt — of me. When the decree is proclaimed, I would like you to stand on the platform beside the king. The city needs to see that the woman who ended the winter stands with the wolves, not above them. Half the bounties went up because of panic after the frost. Panic ends faster when it sees a familiar face on the other side."

Juniper thought of standing in a market square before ten thousand people, of being seen, loudly, permanently — she who had built her whole life on being invisible, on careful ledgers and hidden gifts. Once, the thought would have terrified her.

"I'll stand there," she said.

* * *

The regent dismissed the boy kindly — he had lessons, and besides, she could see the questions gathering behind his eyes that were better asked without witnesses — and then, unexpectedly, did not dismiss Juniper.

"A moment more, if you'll bear it. Not crown business." Vessa poured two cups of wine from the decanter Pellam had left, and set one before her guest, and sat back down, and for a moment the iron of her went quiet and Juniper saw the tired woman underneath, the one who had held a collapsing kingdom together with both hands for a season. "I signed the release. The debt is paid. So this is off the record, and I'll thank you to forget it, because I'll deny it."

"Understood," Juniper said, and meant it. Some things belonged in no ledger at all. She was learning which ones.

"The night the winter broke," Vessa said, "I was in the west corridor with half the household, watching the ice come off the windows like a fever breaking. And in all the chaos, I saw something I've wondered about since. A wolf. Gray, big — bigger than any wolf I've seen outside a bestiary. It came down the servants' stair like it owned the stone, walked past forty screaming people as if they weren't there, and stood at the edge of the light in the great hall, watching the throne room doors. Watching *you*, I assumed, though the doors were shut." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "When you came out, it was gone. And I've seen it since — or one like it — at the edge of the palace grounds, always at dusk, always alone. Never with the packs. My huntsmen say the northern wolves run together, always, except one. They say there's a wolf that runs the borders of the pack lands alone, year-round, and none of the others drive it off, and none of them call to it either. As if it's neither in nor out."

The cup stopped turning.

"They say," Vessa went on, watching Juniper's face with that careful, unhostile attention, "the pack left it behind when they came south. Or it left them. No one agrees. But my master of hounds, who has read every wolf-track in this kingdom for forty years, told me something I haven't been able to put down. He said: *that wolf doesn't patrol like an exile. Exiles slink. That one stands watch. Like a man guarding a house he's been told he can't enter.*"

Juniper's hands were very still in her lap. Stillness was a skill; she had practiced it her whole life. But her heart had begun doing something it shouldn't — aching toward a shape she couldn't name, the way a tongue finds the gap of a missing tooth.

"Lady Regent," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than the whole morning's negotiation, "why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked me for sanctuary for creatures who cannot speak for themselves," Vessa said, "and I am wondering whether there might be one more creature in this kingdom who needs it, and is too proud to ask. And because—" she hesitated, and Juniper saw, with a strange shock, that the regent was embarrassed, "—because I buried a husband twenty years ago who spent our last decade not speaking to me of things that mattered, out of pride, out of protection, out of some fool idea that silence was a gift. It was not a gift, Mistress Pemberton. It was just a longer way of losing him." She stood, briskly, iron restored. "The decree will be ready in five days. The proclamation at noon in Coronation Square. Do come. And—" almost at the door, she paused, not turning around, "—the wolf in the courtyard followed your carriage here. It's following it home, I'd wager. Whatever it is to you, or was, or isn't — the crown's protection extends to stubborn animals. Consider that noted in the ledger."

The door closed behind her.

Juniper sat alone in the small warm chamber for a long time, in the house of a dead king who had begged her to let him die, in the silence where a memory used to live.

Then she took out her little black book — the new one, the one titled in her own hand *Testimony of the Beasts* — and wrote, beneath the fox's account and the crow's account and the old cart-horse's account, a fresh entry:

*The regent says there is a wolf who stands watch outside a house he cannot enter. The regent says exiles slink, but this one guards.*

She looked at the entry for a while. Her pen hovered. Beneath it, without deciding to, she wrote one more line, small, as if the book might not notice:

*I keep feeling like someone is waiting for me to remember. I wish I knew what.*

Outside the window, far below in the courtyard, a gray shape rose from beside the carriage, shook the thaw-water from its coat, and fell into step at the wheel as the horses pulled away — close enough to guard, far enough to keep a promise, patient as winter, faithful as spring.
