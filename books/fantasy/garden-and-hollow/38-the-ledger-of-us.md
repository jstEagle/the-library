## 38. The Ledger of Us

The candle had a habit of guttering at the same page.

Juniper noticed it on the fourth night, then the ninth, then every night after—how the flame leaned toward the ledger as though it, too, wanted to hear. She had stopped believing in coincidence somewhere around the time she'd learned that gods had fingerbones and crowns had appetites. So she let the candle lean. She opened the book to where it wanted, and she read aloud.

"Entry one," she said to her empty room above the rebuilt Guildhall. "Testimony of the magpie called Sixpence, taken in the third week of thaw. *The wolf came to the garden wall at dusk. He carried a sparrow's feather in his mouth, though he did not eat sparrows. He left it on the sill beneath her window.*"

Her voice was steady. Her hands were not.

She had made a discipline of this, the way she had once made a discipline of hiding. Every night, after the ledgers of restitution were balanced and the lamps in the lower hall were snuffed, she climbed the narrow stair to her rooms, lit one candle, and read herself a stranger's love story in which she was the fool at the center who could not remember the plot.

It was, she had decided, a kind of accounting. The beasts had given their testimony; testimony was only useful if it was reviewed. And so she reviewed it—aloud, because the words behaved differently in the air than on the page. On the page they were entries. In the air they were almost memories, hovering just out of reach like names on the tip of the tongue, and every night she reached for them, and every night they slipped, and every morning she got up and did it again.

Hope, she was learning, was not a feeling. It was a practice. It was ink and repetition and showing up to a page that gave nothing back, night after night, the way a farmer shows up to frozen ground.

* * *

There were forty-one entries now.

Sixpence the magpie had the most, because magpies gossip the way rivers run—constantly and in all directions. The old cart-horse Bess at the Tanner's Gate stable had given three entries, delivered in her slow, grave way, each word placed like a stone in a wall. The rats of the undercity had contributed two, which Juniper had cross-referenced twice before accepting, because rats would say anything for bread. Even the palace cats had deigned to speak, in the imperious tone of creatures doing her a favor.

And there was the wolf himself. Ash came some nights—not often, never asking—and sat below her window in his four-footed shape, silent, while she read. He had made her a promise without words: he would not tell her what she had lost. He would not court her memory back by force of his own wanting. If she ever asked, he would answer. Until then, he waited the way winter waits, patient and enormous and everywhere at once.

Some nights she hated him for it. Most nights she loved him for it, in the abstract, secondhand way one loves a character in a book—one of the forty-one entries, a fact about a stranger.

Tonight the candle guttered toward the middle pages, and she followed it.

"Entry nineteen," she read. "*Testimony of Bess, the cart-horse. The woman came to the stable in the snow-month with frost in her hair and blood on her sleeve that was not hers. She put her forehead against my neck and said, 'Bess, I have done something terrible or something wonderful, and I cannot remember which.' I said, 'Both, little clerk. Both.' She laughed until she cried, and then she slept standing up against me for an hour, and I stood very still.*"

Juniper stopped reading.

She knew that laugh. Not the moment of it—but the shape of it, the way you know a room you've dreamed. It rose in her chest and found nothing to attach to, a kite string cut from its kite, and she sat with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if she could hold the feeling down long enough to make it land somewhere.

"Both," she repeated aloud. Her voice cracked on the small word.

That was the cruelest arithmetic of it: the rite had taken her memories of Ash, but it had not taken the love. Love, it turned out, was not stored where memory was kept. It lived deeper, in the body, in the marrow, in the animal places no crown could audit. She woke some mornings with her heart already aching before her mind had produced a single reason why. She caught herself smiling at nothing on the stairs. She had cried, once, uncontrollably, over a sparrow feather she'd found on her windowsill—had held it and wept like a widow at a grave with no name on the stone.

The ledger told her why. Her heart simply hadn't been informed.

* * *

On the twelfth night of the month, Hesse's old chair creaked under her in the guildhall below, and the day's work spread out before her like a battlefield after the fighting: petitions, restitution claims, the slow unspooling of a century of complicity.

The house of restitution was not glamorous work. That was the thing no one warned you about justice—it was mostly paperwork. For every dramatic confession extracted from a guilty guildmaster, there were nine hundred ledger lines to correct, forty shipments of "cold" to trace to their buyers, and an endless procession of people who had profited quietly from the Long Winter's approach and now arrived at her desk wearing expressions of wounded innocence.

"Guildmaster Pemberton," said a fur merchant that afternoon, wringing his hat, "I hardly think a few shipments of insulation wool constitute—"

"You sold frost-warding charms at triple price during a manufactured emergency," Juniper said, not looking up. "Entry six hundred twelve. Restitution is double your profits plus public correction of the guild record. Next."

Word had gotten around that the new master kept beasts. That she took testimony from rats and honored it. That she had once traded a memory of her mother's voice at a black auction and come out the other side still standing. They feared her a little, which was useful, and trusted her more, which was better, and she understood now—she hadn't, a year ago—that both were built on the same foundation: she kept accounts that could not be bribed, because she had learned exactly what bribery cost.

Still, when the hall emptied and the light went amber through the high windows, she found her eyes drifting to the shelf behind her chair, where the old prophecy book lived now—or what remained of it. Sorrel had burned it to free her. What survived was a handful of heat-warped pages recovered from the brazier, kept under glass like relics. Sorrel's handwriting was still legible on one fragment: *forgive me the ending I wrote for you.*

"I'm working on it," Juniper told the glass, as she did most evenings. Forgiveness, too, was a practice. Some nights she meant it. Some nights she didn't. She recorded both honestly, because a ledger-keeper did not falsify entries, even internal ones.

* * *

That night, the candle guttered early—to entry twenty-seven, one of the wolf's own.

He had given testimony exactly once, formally, sitting across from her in this very room with the gravity of a witness swearing an oath. She had offered him the option of speaking privately, off the record. He had refused.

"If it isn't written down," he'd said, "it can be lost. I've lost enough."

So he had spoken into the record, voice level, eyes on the table between them while she wrote:

"*Testimony of Ash, of the northern packs, given freely. She used to hum when she balanced the books—a tune she claimed she'd invented, though I heard gulls singing it in Whitecliff years before I met her, which I never told her because she was so pleased with herself. She talked to horses like they were colleagues and to mice like they were children. When she was frightened, she got precise. When she was happy, her handwriting got worse. She chose to forget me rather than let the crown keep the kingdom, and I would choose her again in every life the packs believe in, and I am not telling her any of this directly, because she paid too much for her forgetting to have it argued with. End of testimony.*"

She read it aloud now, as she did perhaps one night in three, because it was the hardest entry and therefore, she had decided, the most important one. Her voice caught precisely where it always caught—at *her handwriting got worse*—because she had checked, that week, going back through her own journals, and it was true. The spring after the Unbinding, her script had gone neat as print. Grief had improved her penmanship. She despised it.

"And I am not telling her any of this directly," she finished, "because she paid too much for her forgetting to have it argued with."

The candle flame stood straight. Outside, far off, a wolf howled—not a city dog doing an impression, but the real thing, long and low, from the direction of the palace gardens where the packs had been granted sanctuary. Where *he* lived now, in the rough stone cottage by the old gate, keeping his distance, keeping his promise.

She closed the ledger.

"Not yet," she said—to him, to the book, to whatever part of her heart was still listening from behind the wall. "But soon. I think soon."

* * *

The trouble with courting a stranger was that the stranger kept being wonderful.

That was the trap she hadn't anticipated. Reading the testimony was supposed to be diligence—reviewing evidence, weighing accounts. But somewhere over the weeks it had become something else. She had begun, without noticing the exact night it happened, to fall for him. The man in the entries. The one who carried sparrow feathers he didn't need, who stood still for an hour so a tired woman could sleep against his neck, who refused to win her back because winning her back would cheapen what she'd paid.

It felt like a betrayal of the missing year to admit it, so she admitted it in the ledger instead, in the back pages, in a section she had titled honestly: *Reconciliation Pending.*

*Entry:* *I think I am falling in love with a man I do not remember meeting. This seems either very foolish or very honest, and I cannot decide which, and Sixpence says the two are usually the same thing.*

*Entry:* *I saw him at the market today, in his human shape, buying nails. He nodded to me the way you nod to a respected colleague. My hands shook for an hour afterward. I bought nails I did not need.*

*Entry:* *Hesse's ghost would tell me to weigh the risk. But I have read the whole ledger twice, and here is what the numbers say: every account agrees. Forty-one witnesses, no contradictions, no falsifications. The beasts do not lie. And every single one of them, in their own grammar, in their own way, has testified to the same finding.*

*Finding: I loved him. Finding: he loves me still. Finding: the only obstacle is a door I locked myself, from the inside, to save everyone—including, though I didn't know it then, him.*

Doors could be unlocked. That was the whole point of keys.

But the rite's seal was not a door with a keyhole. Sorrel's last message—the scrap recovered from the burning book—had been explicit: *the trade is sealed by weight. Only weight equal to what was given can open it again. Nothing lighter will do. Do not try to cheat it, little clerk. Cheating is how crowns get made.*

Equal weight to a king's whole reign. Equal weight to her love for Ash, poured out entire. What could she possibly—

She stopped. Set down the pen.

Outside her window, the autumn wind moved through the garden below, and the first leaves came down, gold and unhurried, and she watched them and felt the calendar turn over in her chest like a page.

The anniversary. First frost was coming. One year since the night she had climbed to the throne room with winter howling at the windows and traded everything she was for everyone else.

One year since the wolves had begun to remember for her.

She looked down at the open ledger, at forty-one testimonies and a pending reconciliation, and made a decision the way she made all her best ones: quietly, completely, with no one told.

Tomorrow—or whichever morning the frost came—she would go to the cottage by the garden gate. She would knock. And she would ask him for the one thing he had sworn to give only if asked.

"Tell me," she practiced saying, alone in her room, to the candle. "Tell me our whole story. All of it. From the beginning."

The flame leaned toward her, the way it leaned toward the book.

Somewhere below, in the darkening garden, a wolf lifted his head.
