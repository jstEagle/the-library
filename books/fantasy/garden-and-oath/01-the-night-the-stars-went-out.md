## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with smuggling honey out of Thornfell was not the guards. The guards were easy. The trouble was that the bees knew.

"Keep your opinions to yourself," Rosalind Vasquez told the crate strapped to Juniper's back, and the hive inside it buzzed a low, offended chord through the wood. She could feel it in her palms where she gripped the mule's lead rope, that particular vibration that meant *we are unhappy and we want you to know it*. Most people heard nothing but a hum. Rosalind had been hearing what animals actually said since she was six years old, and in nineteen years she had never once found a use for the gift that didn't involve lying to someone about why her hair was full of burrs or why she'd been talking to a chicken.

Juniper flicked one long ear back at her.

"I know," Rosalind said. "I know you're tired. We're almost at the pass."

*You said that an hour ago,* said the ear, eloquently. Mules couldn't talk the way the cat-shaped shadows of the high country could, all teeth and opinion, but they could say plenty with an ear and a stomp. Juniper had been saying *I'm tired* since sundown, and *this road is wrong* since moonrise, and Rosalind had been ignoring both because the hidden pass through the Graystones was exactly as wrong as roads came — which was the entire point of it.

She paused on the switchback and looked back the way they'd come. Thornfell village sat in its bowl of dark pines two miles below, a scatter of lamplight around the inn's chimney smoke, the well-house roof just visible as a pale smudge. Beyond it, the fields went silver and then black, and beyond the fields rose the ward-stones — twelve of them, spaced along the old border ridge like a row of broken teeth, each one carved with the same spiral that appeared on door lintels and grave markers and the bottom of every ceramic cup in the valley. Her whole life, those stones had glowed faintly at night. Not bright. Just enough to say *you are inside something*, the way a lit window says *someone is home*.

Tonight they were dark.

Rosalind frowned. She counted them out of habit — one, two, three, down the ridge into the haze where the rest should have been. Dark, dark, dark. She'd seen them dim before, in bad storms, when the wind off the mountains made even the wardens' dogs hide under porches. But this wasn't storm-dark. There was no wind. The air had gone still as held breath, and the cold coming down off the peaks had a strange, clean taste to it, like water from a cup nobody had drunk from yet.

"You feel it too?" she asked Juniper.

Juniper did not answer, which was itself an answer. The mule stood with all four feet planted, head low, staring north up the trail toward the pass with the fixed attention of an animal watching a predator it hadn't decided how to run from yet.

"It's the honey," Rosalind said, more to hear her own voice than because she believed it. "Bees make everything strange. Come on."

She clicked her tongue and pulled the lead rope, and Juniper came, because Juniper always came, because that was the deal they'd struck eleven years ago when Rosalind had bought her out of a knacker's yard for three silver and a promise: *you carry, I feed, neither of us dies alone.* It was, Rosalind reflected as they climbed, the longest relationship of her life, and it was with a mule. Tam would laugh himself sick if she ever admitted that out loud. Which was why he'd never hear it.

The thought of Tam warmed her for half a switchback. He'd be waiting at the Hollow Stair, the smugglers' shelf where the Graystones folded over themselves like a hand closing, with his crooked grin and his ledger-brain and his endless argument that honey was a stupid cargo. *Nobody pays that much for honey, Roz.* And every time: *They pay it because it's Thornfell honey, Tam, and everyone south of the border knows our clover does something to it, and nobody south of the border can prove what, so they pay.* Three runs a season, spring through autumn, and between them she kept the roof patched and the larder full and her name clean enough that the Wardens never looked twice at the woman who walked the high trails alone.

That was the arrangement. That was the routine. Routine was a kind of armor, and Rosalind wore hers well.

The Hollow Stair opened ahead of them, a slot of deeper darkness between two shoulders of granite, and Rosalind stopped again — because the stars were wrong.

She noticed it the way you notice a room has gone quiet: not any single thing missing, but the shape of the whole changed. She knew the sky over the Graystones the way she knew her own hands. The Shepherd's Yoke hanging low in the northeast. The Seven Sisters strung like beads above the pass. The pale smear of the River of Birds pouring from horizon to horizon. She had navigated by them since childhood; Marta had taught her, back before Marta's eyes went soft, drawing star-maps in spilled flour on the inn table while Rosalind memorized and Marta recited names for constellations that existed nowhere but in her own head.

The Shepherd's Yoke was there. The Sisters were there. The River of Birds was —

Flickering.

Rosalind stood very still. High overhead, the River's brightest star pulsed once, twice, like a lamp guttering in a draft. Then a second star. Then a third, farther south, and this time the pulse didn't come back. The darkness where it had been seemed thicker than ordinary night, a hole cut in cloth rather than cloth itself.

"Tam," she said aloud, absurdly, as if he were standing behind her. "Tam, tell me you're seeing this."

The sky answered instead. All along the northern ridge, in a line running east to west as far as she could see, light bloomed — thin, blue-white, the exact color of the ward-spirals — and then snapped. There was no other word for it. Twelve threads of light, taut across the border of the world for four hundred years, and they *snapped*, one after another, west to east, each release accompanied by a sound Rosalind felt in her sternum before she heard it: a note like a harp string cut under tension, a *twang* that shivered down through the rock and up through the soles of her boots and out through the crown of her head, leaving her ears ringing.

The last stone, the easternmost, held on. She watched it burn brighter than the others had, desperate-bright, a candle flame standing up straight before the draft takes it. Then it too went out, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Juniper screamed.

Mules don't scream, usually. This one reared, tearing the lead rope half out of Rosalind's hands, hooves striking sparks off the trail stone, whites of her eyes showing all around. The honey crate lurched and the hive inside roared, a sound like a kettle coming to boil, and somewhere downslope a fox began shrieking in the bracken, and from the pine forest below came a rising tide of barking and bellowing and the high panicked bugling of some elk far up the mountain, every animal in the valley finding its voice at once.

"Hey." Rosalind got a hand on Juniper's cheek, pressed her forehead to the warm gray bone of the mule's nose, and breathed at her, slow and deliberate, the way you breathe at anything frightened. "Hey. Hey. I've got you. Nothing's coming. Nothing's here. Breathe, girl."

*I felt it die,* Juniper said, in the way she said things, which was mostly pressure and image against Rosalind's mind: the sensation of a thread snapping somewhere behind her ribs, of a fence she'd leaned on all her life suddenly not being there. *It held us in. It held everything in.*

"It held everything *out*," Rosalind corrected automatically, and then stopped, because she wasn't sure anymore which direction the wards had faced, and the not-sure felt like stepping onto ice and hearing it creak.

She talked the mule down the way she talked everything down, in a low stream of nonsense and endearments, and slowly the trembling eased, though Juniper never took her eyes off the northern ridge. The fox stopped shrieking. The dogs below fell silent all at once, which was somehow worse than the noise.

Rosalind stood in the middle of the Hollow Stair with her heart going like a drum and made herself think.

Option one: the wards had failed on their own. Old magic, old stones, four centuries of frost — things failed. Except the ward-stones weren't machines. They were fed. Every household in Thornfell sent someone up at midsummer with bread and salt and a spoken name, and the stones drank the naming and burned on. Her mother had taken her up the ridge exactly once, when Rosalind was small enough to ride her father's shoulders, and she could still remember the warmth of the stone under her palm, like touching the flank of something alive and sleeping.

Option two: someone had cut them. And no one cut the wards of Thornfell except the Wardens, and the Wardens had no reason to, because the wards were the whole point of the Wardens, the wall their whole order existed to stand on—

Her thoughts snagged on her mother, the way they always did, a splinter she'd long ago stopped trying to dig out. *Your gift is a garden, little rose. Tend it quietly, and never let the Order see it bloom.* That was the last real thing her mother had told her, at seven years old, three days before the fever took her. Rosalind had built her entire life inside that sentence: quiet, small, hidden, a smuggler's path through the world with her gift folded up small as a coin in a boot.

The stars flickered again. She looked up in time to watch the Seven Sisters go out — not all at once but in sequence, bead by bead, like windows going dark down a street at bedtime. Then the Yoke. Then, in the south, behind her, over lands she'd never seen, more lights dying in their turn, and the darkness coming down like a lid.

Not clouds. Clouds had edges, texture, movement. This was subtraction. The sky was being *forgotten*, star by star, and Rosalind stood under it with her mouth open and understood, in the wordless way she understood animals, that she was watching something enormous happen to the world, something that had perhaps never happened before, and that her whole life — the runs, the ledgers, the careful smallness — had just become a different kind of thing than it had been an hour ago.

"We need to get off the high trail," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Come on, Juniper. Down to the village. Tam can look after himself; Tam always looks after himself."

Juniper did not move.

"Juniper."

The mule turned her head and looked at Rosalind, full-on, one brown eye catching the last gray light, and what came off her was so clear it needed no words at all: *not home. Not home. Don't make me go home.*

"That's exactly where we're going," Rosalind said. "People. Walls. A fire. You'll get barley and I'll get answers, and tomorrow this will all be some ward-drift phenomenon Marta will explain over cider while Tam cheats Pip at cards—"

Juniper planted her feet. Eleven years, and she had refused a lead exactly twice: once before a bridge that collapsed an hour later, once away from a meadow where they found a Warden patrol camped in the gorse. Rosalind had learned to trust that refusal the way she trusted her own pulse.

"What is it?" she asked, quieter now. "What do you smell?"

Wind arrived, finally, the first wind in hours, sliding down off the peaks and into the pass — and with it came a scent that stopped Rosalind's breath. Cold, yes, mountain cold, but underneath it something else: green. Wet grass. Turned earth. Thaw. In late autumn, on a night dropping hard toward true winter, the wind smelled like April.

And then the snow began to fall.

It came down soft and unhurried, fat flakes turning in the dead-calm air, and Rosalind put out her hand and caught one and watched it sit on her glove. And sit. And sit. She waited for it to melt — her hand was warm, the flake had landed on wool over living skin — and it did not melt. It sat there perfect, six-sided, intricate as a carved button, cold as a coin from a well, and it did not melt, and neither did the ones settling on Juniper's back, on the honey crate, on the trail stones, gathering without melting, building a white skin over the world like frost forming on a window from the inside.

Every animal in the valley started screaming again.

This time Rosalind didn't try to calm anyone, including herself. She hauled the lead rope upslope — north, away from home, because her mule had told her not to go home and Rosalind Vasquez had survived nineteen years in the border country by listening to animals over people — and Juniper surged forward willingly, gratefully, and they climbed together into the Hollow Stair while the unmelting snow thickened around them and the last light of the last ward-stone died somewhere behind.

Halfway up the slot in the rock, Rosalind stopped once, just once, and looked back.

Thornfell village lay below in its bowl of pines, lamps still burning, chimney smoke still rising, the well-house a pale smudge. Ordinary. Whole. Asleep. But the snow was falling there too, she could see it drifting through the lamplight, and already the roofs wore a white that was too smooth, too complete, like a sheet drawn up over a face.

"Hold on," she whispered, to the village, to the mule, to whoever was listening. "Hold on. I'm coming back for you."

Then the pass swallowed them, and the stars stayed dead, and the snow that did not melt fell and fell and fell.

* * *

They climbed most of the night. The Hollow Stair wound up through the heart of the Graystones, narrow enough in places that Juniper's crate scraped both walls, and the unmelting snow followed them the whole way, sifting down through the slot of sky overhead as if the weather had marked them specifically. Rosalind stopped twice to check the hive — bees gone silent and clustered, which was winter behavior weeks too early — and once to eat a heel of bread she had no appetite for, chewing mechanically because the body kept needing things even when the world broke.

Toward what she guessed was the smallest hour, the trail opened onto the high shelf where the smugglers' waystation crouched under an overhang: a dry-stone hut, a fire ring, a cache chest bolted into the rock. And there, wrapped in two blankets with his boots off and his stockinged feet toward a fire that had burned down to orange memory, sat Tam Voss — no relation, a joke so old it had worn grooves — asleep sitting up with his ledger open on his knees.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the firelight, snow not melting on her shoulders, and looked at him for a long moment. His face in sleep was younger than his forty years, slack and trusting, ink smudged on his cheekbone from where he'd pushed his pen through his hair. The ledger's last entry read, in his tidy merchant's hand: *Honey, 12 crates, expected — R.V. — overdue.*

"Tam," she said.

He woke the way he always did, all at once, hand going to the knife under the blanket, eyes finding her, focusing, softening. "Roz. There you are. Stars, girl, you're white as a—" He stopped. He'd seen the snow on her. He'd felt the temperature of it, probably, the wrongness radiating off her clothes like a chill off a cellar floor. "How long was I asleep?"

"A few hours."

"And the snow started—" He looked up, past her, at the flakes drifting down through the overhang's shadow and settling on the bare stone beside the fire ring, where they lay in little perfect stars and did not melt an arm's length from live coals. Tam stared at them. Tam, who had an explanation for everything, who could price anything, who had once talked a toll-collector out of a confiscated wagon with nothing but arithmetic and charm, opened his mouth and closed it and opened it again.

"The wards went out," Rosalind said. "All of them. I watched it happen. And then the stars started going out, Tam, one by one, like someone pinching wicks. And the snow—" She held up her gloved hand with its single intact flake. "It doesn't melt."

Tam looked at the flake. Then at her. Then, slowly, down at his ledger, and Rosalind watched him do the thing he did when the world stopped making sense: reach for the numbers. "Twelve crates," he said. "You brought twelve?"

"Is that your question? Are you *serious*?"

"I'm asking what we have," Tam said, and his voice was very quiet and very steady, and she loved him for it, "because whatever's happening, it looks like it's going to take a while, and I find I think better with figures in front of me." He looked up at her, and in the firelight his eyes were scared in a way his voice refused to be. "Sit down, Roz. Tell me all of it. Start with the wards."

She told him. All of it, in order, while she unsaddled Juniper and rubbed the mule down and got the hive tucked under the driest corner of the overhang. Tam listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, and at the part about the wards snapping he flinched as if the harp-string note had reached him too, ten miles from where it sounded. When she finished, he was looking north, up the trail, toward the high col and the country beyond it.

"There's a story," he said slowly, "that old Marta tells. Drunk, mostly, so nobody credits it. About the founding. About why the wards were raised." He rubbed his ink-smudged cheek. "About what they were raised *against*."

"Marta's stories are ninety percent embroidery."

"Sure." Tam nodded. "But Roz — the beasts. You said every animal in the valley went mad. What are they saying *now*? You'd know. You always know."

Rosalind closed her eyes and listened.

She had been listening her whole life without letting herself call it listening — the chickens' gossip, the dogs' complaints, the mule's grumbles, all of it filed under *harmless, keep it small, keep it secret*. But tonight she opened the door all the way, and what came through was a chorus of fear so vast and so specific that her breath caught. The foxes saying *the ground is forgetting where it is*. The ravens circling the dead ward-stones saying *the fence is down, the fence is down, the old hunger will walk again*. And beneath it all, far off, up in the high rocks where nothing human lived, a voice she had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a family resemblance:

A cat's voice. Deep and scarred-sounding, calm as a drawn blade, speaking in full sentences — which no beast had ever done, not once in her life — saying to the night in general and perhaps to her in particular:

"*The Door is leaking. Find the Vasquez girl. Find her before the snow finds her first.*"

Rosalind opened her eyes. Snow was falling on the fire, on the coals, on Tam's ledger, and none of it melted, and somewhere up in the dark of the Graystones, something that knew her name was waiting.

"Tam," she said, "pack up. We're leaving."

"Leaving where? South? Back to the village?"

"No." She looked north, into the black. "Up."
