## 2. A Village That Forgets Its Own Name

The snow didn't melt.

That was the first wrong thing. Rosalind stood in the yard of the Drowned Hart with her mule's lead rope in her fist and watched flakes settle on the inn's slate roof, on the woodpile, on old Joss's turnip cart, and stay. Not melting. Not even softening at the edges. Just lying there like they'd always been there, like they had every right.

She'd been gone four days. Four days of honey runs through the pass, and she came home to a village wearing winter like it had never taken it off.

"Rosa!" The innkeeper's voice carried across the yard, warm as ever, and for one heartbeat everything felt normal. Aldous Crane had run the Drowned Hart since before she was born. He'd fed her stolen kitchen scraps when her mother was alive and extra portions after she was dead, and he'd never once asked why a girl who talked to animals needed feeding more than most.

He came out wiping his hands on his apron, smiling, and then the smile did something terrible. It faltered. It searched.

"You're—" He squinted at her. "You're the Vasquez girl. Rosalind." Relief flooded his face, and then, horribly, it drained away again. "I know your name," he said slowly, "but I can't—"

"Aldous?"

"Nettie." His voice cracked down the middle. "My daughter. She's got my sister's chin, and I can't see it anymore. I looked at her this morning over breakfast and I knew her name, her age, that she's allergic to bee stings—but her *face*, girl. Her face is just—" He made a shape in the air with both hands, something blank and smooth. "Like an egg. Like a smooth white egg where my daughter should be."

Rosalind's stomach dropped through the frozen ground.

"Have you told anyone?"

"Told them what?" He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only fear dressed up as a joke. "Half the village says the same thing. Marta can't remember the road to Lowmere, and she's walked it ten thousand times. The Hendersons' boy swears his grandmother's name is written nowhere in his head. And the well—" He stopped. Started again. "You should hear the well."

"The well."

"Come inside. Have something hot. You look half-froze." He hesitated at the door, and when he spoke again his voice was very quiet. "It's good you came back, Rosa. Somebody has to remember us."

Inside, the common room was full and silent in a way no common room should ever be. A dozen villagers sat around the fire nursing drinks nobody drank, and the talk, when it happened, happened in fragments: *do you remember—no, before that—I used to know—the name of—it's gone, it's just gone.*

Nettie Crane brought Rosalind a bowl of barley soup without being asked. Seventeen years old, freckled, gap-toothed. Rosalind knew her face perfectly, and the knowing hurt, because she could see exactly what Aldous had lost.

"Da keeps staring at me," Nettie said, setting the bowl down. "Like I'm a stranger wearing my clothes. Mam says give him time. But Mam forgot which bed is hers last night. She slept in the pantry, Rosa. On the flour sacks." Nettie's chin wobbled, and she pressed her lips together until it stopped. "What's happening to us?"

"I don't know." And then, because lying to Nettie Crane felt like kicking a lamb: "But I'm going to find out."

* * *

The well stood in the middle of the square, older than any building around it, its stones furred with moss even in the cold. Rosalind went to it because Aldous had said *you should hear the well*, and because she had spent her whole life listening to things other people couldn't hear, and some instinct older than sense told her the well was talking.

She leaned over the lip.

The water was black and still, and from somewhere far down came a sound like humming—not wind in a shaft, not echo. Humming. Melodic. Almost a song.

And beneath the song, words. Or almost-words. The well was remembering water it had never held. She could feel it the way she felt the mule's opinions or the barn cats' gossip: a mind, dim and deep, turning over memories that didn't belong to it. Water from rivers that weren't Thornfell's rivers. Rain that had fallen on mountains she'd never seen. A lake so wide you couldn't swim across it in a day.

"That's not yours," she whispered to it. "None of that is yours."

The humming paused. Then, gently, the way an old dog leans against a leg, something in the well pressed a memory against her mind: green water, reeds, a woman laughing—

Her mother's laugh.

Rosalind jerked back from the well so fast she nearly went into it. Her heart slammed. The memory dissolved even as she grabbed for it, leaving only the shape of it behind, the way a dream leaves its weight.

"No," she said aloud. "No, that's not—that's not how it works. Wells don't hold people."

But the well hummed on, serene, full of water that had never touched it, and Rosalind stood in the square with snow settling unmelted on her shoulders and understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with weather, that Thornfell was not just forgetting itself.

It was filling up with somebody else's memories. Like a house whose family had died, being furnished by strangers.

* * *

The animals were worse.

She heard them before she reached the byre—a low, continuous distress, the sound of a hundred small minds all speaking at once, and usually their voices were as distinct to her as faces. Hen-talk was clucking and trivial. Dog-talk was blunt and loyal. Goat-talk was insults and appetite. She'd grown up inside that chorus the way other children grew up inside a language, and she'd learned early to keep it secret, because the one time she'd demonstrated it—age nine, telling Farmer Odell his sow was sick before the sow showed symptoms—his face had changed, and her mother had gripped her shoulder hard enough to bruise and said, *never again, Rosa, never let them see.*

Now the chorus wasn't a chorus. It was a scream held at a whisper's volume.

The dogs were the worst. Old Biter, the smith's mastiff, was pressed flat against the bottom of his kennel, and his mind was a loop: *smells wrong smells wrong smells wrong.* The horses in the common stable wouldn't stop walking their stalls in circles. The barn cats had abandoned the granary entirely and sat in a row on the fence like featherless crows, staring south, all of them, all facing south.

And the mule—her mule, Hobb, stubborn as sin and twice as opinionated—stood in Aldous's yard with his ears pinned and refused, absolutely refused, to enter his own stable.

*Not mine,* Hobb said, when she tried. His mind-voice was always grumpy, always hers, and now it trembled. *That's not my stable. There's no me in it. Rosa, there's no me in anything here. The smells have been swapped. The ground's been swapped. Everything's wearing the shape of itself and it isn't itself.*

"Hobb—"

*I want to go north,* he said. *Away. Where the snow hasn't finished falling yet. Please.*

A mule saying *please*. Rosalind put her forehead against his long gray nose and breathed, and he breathed back, and for a moment they were just two frightened creatures in a yard full of wrongness.

"You feel it too," she murmured. "The forgetting."

*Everything's coming loose,* Hobb said. *The village is a coat and somebody's unpicking the seams. And underneath—* He shuddered through his whole long body. *Underneath there's a smell I don't have a word for. Old. Hungry. Patient.*

"Where's it coming from?"

Hobb turned his head. North. Toward the mountains. Toward Mount Cael, white and distant above the tree line, looking exactly as it always had and somehow, today, like a closed mouth.

* * *

She found Tam Verrick where she knew she'd find him: in the back booth of the Drowned Hart, hood up despite the indoor warmth, a cup of Aldous's worst ale untouched in front of him, watching the door the way he always watched doors—which was to say, watching everyone who came through them and pricing them instantly.

Tam was a fence, a smuggler, a liar, and the closest thing Rosalind had to a friend, in the way that two people who share secrets and never share feelings can be friends. He'd taught her which wardens took bribes and which took fingers. She'd taught him which passes the goats could hear collapsing before men could see it. Between them existed a ledger of favors so tangled that neither had ever tried to balance it.

"Vasquez." He didn't smile—he never smiled first—but something in his shoulders came down half an inch, and for Tam that was an embrace. "Heard you were running honey. Thought the stars going out might've kept you home."

"They didn't keep you home."

"I'm never home. Home's a rumor." He nudged the opposite bench with his boot. She sat. Up close she could see what the common room's gloom had hidden: Tam looked wrung out. Shadows under his eyes like thumbprints. "You've seen it, then. The forgetting."

"Aldous can't picture his daughter's face. The well's remembering water it never held. The animals are climbing the walls." She wrapped her hands around the untouched ale just to hold something. "You?"

Tam was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had none of its usual polish.

"I keep a ledger. Every debt, every favor, every name that owes me and every name I owe. Twenty years of it, all up here." He tapped his temple. "Last night I read through it, the way I do. And Rosa—there are pages missing. Not smudged. Missing. Whole people I know I dealt with, and when I reach for them, there's nothing. One of them I owed money to. I can feel the shape of the debt, the weight of it, but the man himself—" His jaw worked. "I might have invented him. I might be a cheat who invented a creditor to feel honest. That's what my own head is offering me now. That I'm a liar even to myself."

"Tam."

"And the worst part?" He laughed, short and bitter. "The worst part is I can't tell if it's getting worse or if I'm just noticing. Because that's the trick of it, isn't it? When a thing erases you, it also erases your ability to notice the erasing. Some morning we'll all wake up and Thornfell will be a village of strangers who've always been strangers, and none of us will ever know there was anything to mourn."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, Nettie was humming to herself—the same tune, Rosalind realized with a chill, that the well had been humming.

"So I'm leaving," Tam said. "North. There's talk on the smugglers' road—waystations going quiet, whole valleys where the traffic just stopped. And there's talk of something else. Something about the border wards failing, and the Order's Wardens crawling all over the high country asking questions." He turned the untouched ale in a slow circle. "I need a guide who knows the passes better than the Wardens do. Goats' ears, if you'll forgive the phrase."

"No."

He looked up. "No?"

"No." Rosalind stared into the fire. Every sensible bone in her body was screaming the same word. Leave. Run south like the cats were staring. Take Hobb and a pack of provisions and get out while her own name still fit her. "Tam, I've spent my whole life keeping my head down. My mother made me promise. One secret, kept perfectly, and nobody ever looks at you twice. If I guide you north—if I go anywhere near whatever's doing this—the Wardens will find me, and they'll find out what I am, and—"

She stopped. Because even as she said it, she heard it: the well humming another country's water. Aldous reaching for his daughter's face and finding an egg. Hobb saying *there's no me in anything here.*

Staying safe in Thornfell meant staying in a place that was being unwritten. There was no down-low left to keep her head beneath.

"Your mother's dead eleven years," Tam said quietly. "Promises to the dead are heavy. I know. But they're also the easiest promises in the world to keep, because the dead don't ask new questions." He leaned forward. "The living ones do, Rosa. Mine is: will you guide me north or not?"

She should say no. She wanted, desperately, to say no—to keep her secret, her solitude, her small careful life, all the walls she'd built brick by brick since the funeral.

Instead she heard herself say: "For pay."

Tam blinked. "What?"

"For pay. Full rate. Half up front." She pulled the ale toward her and drank, finally, because her throat was dry as ash. "And you carry your own pack. I'm not a porter."

Something moved across Tam's face—relief, quickly disguised as a merchant's satisfaction. "Full rate. Half up front. Done." He spat in his palm and held it out, and she shook it, because that was how deals were sealed between people who trusted each other exactly as far as the deal went.

"We leave tomorrow, dawn," she said. "There's a pass above the falls the Wardens haven't found. We'll be in the high country in three days."

"Good." Tam stood, dropped a coin beside his untouched ale, and then paused. "Rosa. Why'd you say yes? Really."

She looked at the fire. At the villagers sitting silent around it, holding names like cracked cups. At the window, where the snow lay unmelted on the sill, and beyond it, north, where Mount Cael stood with its mouth closed.

"Because everybody in this room is forgetting," she said, "and I'm the only person in Thornfell who can still hear what the animals are saying. And they're all saying the same thing." She drained the ale. "They're saying run north. Toward it. Not away."

Tam went pale. "Toward what?"

"I don't know yet." She stood, slinging her pack over her shoulder. "But a scarred mountain cat followed me down from the pass yesterday, and it's been sitting at the edge of the village ever since, watching my window. And this morning, when I passed it on the road—" Her voice caught, and she hated that it caught. "This morning, it said my mother's name."

Outside, the snow went on falling, and did not melt, and the mountain waited.
