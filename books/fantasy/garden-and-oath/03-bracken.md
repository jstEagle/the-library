## 3. Bracken

The snow that didn't melt had stopped falling by morning, but it hadn't gone anywhere either. It lay on the rooftops of Thornfell like a shroud laid over a body the village refused to look at, and Rosalind walked through it with her boots crunching a sound too loud for the hour.

She hadn't slept. She'd tried. She'd lain in her loft above the tannery with the blanket pulled to her chin, listening to the goats in the yard below make sounds she'd never heard them make before—not distress exactly, not hunger, but something like the noise a person makes when they've woken from a nightmare and can't remember which room they're in. Around midnight the well had started murmuring again. Around two, every dog in Thornfell had begun to howl at once, held one long note for what felt like an hour, then stopped together as if someone had cut a string.

So when the light came gray and wrong through her window, Rosalind got up, dressed, and went out into a country that was holding its breath.

* * *

The market square was half-empty, which was itself strange, because fear in Thornfell usually looked like crowding—everyone pressed together at the baker's or the well, trading rumors louder than they needed to. This was the other kind. Doors shut. Shutters drawn. An old man sweeping snow off his step even though the snow didn't move under the broom, just rippled and settled again, like water pretending to be solid.

Rosalind kept her head down and her hood up. That had been the rule her whole life: keep your mouth shut around anything with ears, because if the wrong person heard you talking to a chicken, you'd end up on the Warden's list, and nobody came back from the Warden's list. Her mother's name was still on some list somewhere, probably. People didn't talk about Maren Vasquez much anymore, but they talked about her the way you talk about weather that killed someone.

She was cutting through the alley behind the smithy when she heard the cat.

Not a cat sound. A voice.

"You walk like someone being followed by her own name."

Rosalind stopped so fast her boots skidded on the unmelted snow. The alley was empty. Then it wasn't—a shape detached itself from the woodpile shadow, and there sat a mountain cat on top of a split rail fence, tail wrapped over its paws, watching her with eyes the color of wet slate.

It was big. Bigger than any cat had a right to be, though mountain cats always were, out past the tree line where nobody counted them. Its coat was the gray-brown of winter bracken, and its face was a map of old violence: one ear notched clean through, three pale scars raking from brow to jaw, a nick out of its lower lip that gave it a permanent sneer. It should have been terrifying. It was terrifying. But it was also, unmistakably, the thing that had spoken.

Rosalind's whole life, her gift had been a matter of pressure and guesswork. Animals pushed feelings at her—the way a dog pushes its nose into your hand—and she'd learned to read those pressures the way other people read faces. A goat's anxiety felt like static. A mule's stubbornness felt like a wall. She could nudge, soothe, ask in her own way and sometimes be answered in kind: a direction, a refusal, a flicker of image. But never words. Never once, in twenty-two years, had a beast said a single word to her, and she'd privately come to believe the gift simply didn't work that way, that the stories about speakers who held conversations with crows were smugglers' lies, the same as the ones about fish that granted wishes.

The cat said, "You're staring. Your mother stared too, but she had better manners about it."

The world tilted. Rosalind put a hand against the smithy wall because the stones seemed suddenly unsure of their job.

"My mother," she managed, "has been dead eleven years."

"Yes." The cat's tail-tip twitched once. "I was there."

* * *

They ended up behind the tannery, in the yard where Rosalind kept her mule and her goats, because she couldn't have this conversation in the open and she couldn't not have it anywhere. She'd checked the lane twice, thrown scratch grain to the hens to occupy them, and then stood with her arms crossed in front of the largest wild predator she'd ever seen at a distance of six feet, feeling like a fool and not caring.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. I want to be sure I'm not dreaming."

"You are not dreaming. You slept badly, but you slept." The cat—she needed a name for it, her mind kept snagging on *it*, and the not-knowing made everything worse—sat with the patience of stone. "And before you ask the next obvious question: yes, all beasts speak. We always have. Most humans hear nothing but weather. You are not most humans. Neither was she."

"Maren Vasquez."

"Maren." Something moved behind the slate eyes—not softness exactly, but the place where softness would go if the cat allowed such a thing. "She called me Bracken. Because I was young and brown and hiding, and she said I reminded her of herself."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "I don't— I barely remember her. I was eleven. I remember her hands. I remember she hummed when she cooked. I remember—" She stopped, because what she remembered most was the night the Wardens came, the lantern-light in the lane, her mother kneeling to hug her and saying, very quietly, *whatever happens, don't stop listening.* And then gone. Taken east to the Order's stronghold at Corvane's Reach, and returned eight months later in a box, and no one ever told her why.

"She spoke to beasts," Rosalind said. "Like me. Nobody ever said. Everyone pretended my family was just—strange."

"Your village pretended many things." Bracken rose, stretched fore and aft with the unhurried arrogance of his kind, and dropped soundlessly to the frozen mud. Up close he was even more scarred than he'd looked on the fence; the fur along his flank grew in whorls over old burns. "Pretending is how small places survive inside large countries. But pretending time is over, Rosalind Vasquez. That is why I am here."

"The stars," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been carrying the words since last night like a stone in her chest. "The wards snapped. The snow won't melt. The animals are terrified, and the well is remembering water it never held, and Old Tammis called his daughter by a stranger's name this morning and then wept without knowing why."

Bracken regarded her for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—lower, flatter, stripped of the dry irony he wore like a coat.

"The border wards did not snap. They were cut. And what they were holding back is coming through the wound." He turned his scarred face toward the north, toward the black shoulder of Mount Cael rising above the valley's end, white-capped even in autumn until now, when the new snow had climbed down its slopes like something descending. "There is a Door beneath that mountain. The oldest thing in Thornfell, older than the villages, older than the Order, older than the names. Your mother sealed it. The seal is failing. The Door is leaking."

"Leaking what?"

"Forgetting." He said the word carefully, as if it might bite him. "The hunger behind the Door eats memory. It has tasted through the crack, and everything it touches comes loose. Names first. Then faces. Then roads. Then the land itself forgets where it keeps its rivers." His ear—the notched one—flicked toward the village. "You have seen the beginning of it. The well. The innkeeper. The dogs howling at what they cannot name. It will get worse faster than you can imagine. In a season, Thornfell will not be a dying village. It will be a place that never existed, and everyone in it will be a stranger to themselves, wandering a blank."

Rosalind became aware that she was shaking. Not from cold—from something older than cold, a vibration in her bones like the moment before the wards had snapped.

"Why me?" she said. "Why tell *me*? I'm a honey-smuggler. I talk to chickens. I'm nobody."

"You are the daughter of the woman who sealed the Door," Bracken said, "and you have her gift, untrained, untested, and unclaimed—and the Order knows it. That is the second reason I am here. There are men riding out of Corvane's Reach already. Wardens. They carry a list, and your name is at the top of it, and what they want is not your safety."

The yard seemed to shrink. Rosalind thought of the Warden's lists, the lanterns in lanes, the box coming home. Eleven years of careful silence, of keeping her mouth shut around anything with ears—and it had never mattered, had it? She'd been born onto a list.

"I can't fight Wardens," she said. "I can't seal anything. I don't even know what the Door looks like. My mother never taught me anything—she was taken before—"

"No," Bracken said. "She wasn't."

The word landed like a dropped stone.

"What do you mean, *no*? I saw them take her. I saw the wagon."

"You saw what she wanted an eleven-year-old to see." The cat began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps, his claws clicking softly on the frozen ground. "Maren Vasquez went to the Door willingly, child. The Order believed they were arresting a rogue speaker. She let them believe it, because a prisoner is escorted where she asks to be escorted, if she asks cleverly enough. She needed to reach the mountain. The Wardens were her carriage." He paused at the fence line and looked back at her, and for the first time the sneer in his torn lip looked almost like grief. "She spent eight months in their stronghold learning the shape of their ignorance. Then she walked into Mount Cael singing, and she sealed the Door with her own memory as the lock, and what came home in that box was everything she chose to leave outside it."

Rosalind couldn't breathe. The yard, the goats, the gray sky—all of it receded, and she was eleven again, standing in a lane, hearing *don't stop listening,* and understanding for the first time that it hadn't been comfort. It had been instructions.

"She gave up—" Rosalind's voice cracked. "What did she give up? What did she leave out?"

"Herself. Mostly." Bracken's tail curled slowly around his feet. "She asked me to guard the approaches until a Vasquez came north. I have waited eleven winters, and I am old, and my patience is nearly spent, and the Door is leaking *now*. So I will say the rest plainly, because plainly is all I have left."

He stepped closer. This near, she could see her own reflection doubled in the slate of his eyes, small and wavering.

"Come with me to the bears' cave below Cael. The beasts hold what the land is losing—every road, every name, every song. Your mother's voice is among them; the bears kept it; they will show you. Learn there what she learned. Because the seal will not hold another turning of the year, and when it fails, the choice she made will fall to the only other person alive who can make it." A beat. "That is you, Rosalind. It was always going to be you. She built it that way."

* * *

Afterward—she couldn't have said how long after—Rosalind found herself sitting in the doorway of the goat shed with a bucket of water she had no memory of fetching, while Bracken drank from it with delicate, terrible courtesy, like a king slumming it.

Her mind kept snagging on the same splinters. *The bears kept her voice.* *She let them take her.* *Don't stop listening.*

"I used to hate her," Rosalind said quietly. It was the first time she'd ever said it aloud. "For leaving. For being taken. For making me the strange girl who had to hide what she was. I used to sit up at night and rehearse all the things I'd say if she came back." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "None of them were questions about doors."

"Grief is a door too," Bracken said. "Most people nail theirs shut and call the silence peace."

"That's very wise for a cat."

"I ate a philosopher once. Some of him stayed with me."

Despite everything—despite the dead stars and the weeping dogs and the name at the top of a Warden's list—Rosalind laughed. It lasted two seconds and hurt the whole way down, but it was real, and Bracken watched her do it with an expression she'd spend years trying to name and never quite manage.

Then his head came up. Every muscle in his scarred body went still, the stillness of a thing built for ambush, and his notched ear swiveled toward the lane beyond the tannery wall.

Hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast, in formation, the way soldiers ride and smugglers don't.

"Wardens," Bracken said, low. "Three riders, gray cloaks, at the far end of Mill Lane. They are asking at each house for a girl who speaks to beasts." His eyes found hers. "Decide quickly, Rosalind Vasquez. The north road leaves this village in one direction, and it does not stay open forever."

Rosalind stood. Her legs were unsteady and her heart was hammering and somewhere behind her ribs, eleven years of grief and shame and hiding were cracking apart like river ice in spring—and underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, she could feel the goats pressing their simple frightened minds against hers, and the mule, and the hens, and beyond the walls the whole frightened animal country calling out in the dark.

She picked up her satchel. She didn't pack it. There was no time to pack it, and besides, everything she owned was a life she was fairly sure she wouldn't be coming back to.

"All right," she said to the cat. "Show me the way to the bears."

Bracken bared his teeth in what might, in a gentler face, have been a smile.

"Then run," he said, "and whatever you hear behind us—do not look back until the trees close over the road."
