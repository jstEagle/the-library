## 4. The Warden Without a Past

He woke on his back in the middle of the road with snow settling on his eyelashes and no name in his mouth.

He knew this: he was a Warden of the Order of the Unbroken Line. He knew the oath—every word of it, carved somewhere behind his eyes where memory should have been. *I am the line that does not break. I am the wall that does not sleep. What is entrusted to me I keep; what is forgotten by the world I remember.* He knew his orders, too, written in a hand he almost recognized, folded into the breast pocket of his grey coat beside a badge of black iron:

*Find the Vasquez girl. Rosalind Vasquez, seventeen years, last seen Thornfell village. She carries an inheritance the Order requires. Do not let her fall into other hands.*

Everything else was white.

Not just the snow—the world itself had gone thin at the edges, like cloth worn through. The road under him ran north and south, but when he looked east toward where hills ought to be, there was only a pale nothing, smooth as unmarked paper. When he looked west, the nothing flinched, as if it noticed him looking, and rearranged itself into a forest that seemed embarrassed to exist.

He sat up. His body worked. That was something. Sword at his hip, travel-worn boots, three days of rations in his pack—or so the pack's weight suggested; when he opened it, the bread inside was stale past any three days he could account for. A water skin. A coil of silver cord, Warden-issue, for binding wards. And a small tin box that rattled.

He did not open the tin box. Some part of him, deeper than memory, said *not yet*, and he had learned—he could not say how—that a man with no past should trust whatever instincts survived the loss.

"Name," he said aloud, to hear something human. "Your name."

The wind took the words and gave nothing back. He tried the oath instead, and it came whole, steady, comforting as a hand on the shoulder. So: not everything was lost. Whatever had been taken from him, the Order had built him well enough that the important parts held.

He stood, brushed off the snow—it did not melt on his coat, he noticed; it sat there like salt—and turned north, because the orders said Thornfell, and some instinct said Thornfell lay north, and because a man who cannot remember must walk toward something or lie down forever.

He walked.

* * *

Near midday he smelled woodsmoke, which meant people, which meant questions he might answer. The village appeared out of the thin western forest all at once, the way things did now—not approached but *arrived at*, as if the space between had been folded away. A dozen houses around a green. A well. A shrine with a stone owl whose face someone had worn smooth with centuries of touching.

And everywhere, the forgetting.

He saw it first in the gardens. Rows of winter kale stood half-faded, their green going grey at the leaf-tips like ash working inward. A dog chained by a doorhouse barked at him with a sound like a bell rung underwater. And in the doorway of the largest house, a woman stood perfectly still, staring at her own hands, turning them over and over as though she'd never seen them before and wasn't sure they were hers.

"Warden," the woman said when she saw his coat. Relief flooded her face, then confusion, then something worse than either. "Warden—you're here about—we sent word about—" She stopped. Her brow creased. "I've forgotten what we sent word about."

"The border wards fell," he said. It came out of him easily, a fact stored with the oath. "Three nights ago. The stars went out. You may have noticed—"

"We don't look up anymore." She said it simply, terribly. "It's easier."

He understood that more than he wanted to. He hadn't looked up either.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She smiled the way people smile when a question is finally easy. "Brenna Tull. I keep the shrine, and my husband is Aldous, and we have—" The smile cracked down the middle. Her hands went to her mouth. "We have a—there was a—I know there was a child. I can feel the shape of where a child goes in my heart and it's empty, Warden, it's empty and I don't know if I ever had one or if I'm mourning a daughter who grew up and moved away or—"

"Breathe," he said, and put a steadying hand on her shoulder, and hated how practiced the gesture felt, how many times some version of him must have done exactly this. "Fading children are the Order's first charge. If you had a child, and the child is young enough, they'll be near the shrine. They always are. The old stones hold names longer than flesh does."

Her eyes went wide and wet. "The shrine," she whispered, and ran.

He followed.

* * *

The child was behind the shrine, curled into the hollow between the owl-stone's feet, and she was nearly gone.

He would not have seen her at all except that the dog had come with them, still belling its drowned-bell bark, and the dog looked straight at the hollow and would not stop. Elias knelt. In the shadow of the stone there was a girl of perhaps six, and she was *thin*—not starved-thin but faded-thin, her edges uncertain, her hair the color of unwritten paper. Where her left hand pressed against the stone, the stone showed through her fingers.

She was humming. A small tuneless hum, the kind children make to prove to themselves they're still making noise.

"I'm here," Elias said softly. "I see you."

"You can't," the girl said. Her voice was faint, like a voice heard through a wall. "Mama walked right past me this morning. She looked right at the place where I was sitting and she said 'I used to sit there when I was small' and she cried and didn't know why." The faded face lifted. "Am I dying?"

"No." He said it with the full weight of the oath behind it, and meant it. "You're being *forgotten*, which is different, and different means it can be fought. Hold out your hand."

She did. Her fingers passed through his once, twice, and on the third try caught—a sensation like gripping smoke that decided, out of politeness, to be solid.

"What's your name?" he asked.

A flicker of fear crossed the small face. "I keep almost losing it. It's Sable. Like the color. Mama named me that because I was born in a night so dark the midwife couldn't see to cut the cord." The words tumbled fast, hoarded, a child guarding coins. "Sable Tull. Born under the Hunter's stars, which aren't there anymore. Sable Tull, six years old, I lost a tooth in autumn and Mama kept it in the blue jar—"

"Sable Tull," he repeated, firmly, the way Wardens repeat ward-words. "Born under the Hunter's stars. Tooth in the blue jar. Say it with me."

They said it together, four times, five, and each time her grip on his hand firmed a little, her edges pulled in from the wind. It was old craft—the most basic thing a Warden learned, older than swords and wards: *a name spoken aloud, witnessed, is a name anchored.* Memory kept alive is memory shared aloud. He didn't know where he'd learned it. He knew it the way he knew the oath.

By the time Brenna Tull came around the shrine at a dead run, calling a name that broke in the middle—"*Sable—Sable—*"—the girl was solid enough to be scooped up, and her mother's arms closed around actual weight, and Brenna made a sound Elias had never heard before and would spend the rest of his life trying to describe: grief and joy arriving at the same instant and canceling each other into something rawer than either.

He stepped back and gave them the moment. That, too, felt practiced.

Then he took out his journal—Warden-issue, oilcloth, the first page bearing his own handwriting in a hand he didn't recognize: *Day 1. No name recovered. Oath intact. Orders intact. Proceeding north.*—and wrote beneath it:

*Day 4(?). Village unnamed on my maps, west of the north road. One child recovered from fading: Sable Tull, age six. Method: spoken name, repeated, witnessed. Note: the mother had forgotten the child entirely; the child remembered herself. The young forget last. Or: the forgotten forget others first.*

He stared at that last line for a long time.

Because it raised a question his orders did not answer. The Order had taught him—had it? he assumed so; the knowledge was there—that the collapse of the wards was a catastrophe, that Thornfell's people must be gathered and protected, that the Vasquez girl carried an inheritance vital to that protection. Fine. But standing in this half-eaten village, watching a mother memorize her daughter's face again like a woman drinking after a long thirst, another thought arrived and would not leave:

*If the Order remembers everything—if remembering is our whole craft and our whole purpose—then why is there no Warden here? Why am I the first grey coat these people have seen? Why are my orders about one girl, and not about this village, this green, this dog, this shrine?*

He looked down at the journal. He looked at the tin box in his pack, the one that rattled, the one that said *not yet*.

He left it shut.

* * *

He stayed two days. He could afford two days; the orders said find the girl, and the girl had been seen in Thornfell village, and Thornfell village—so the fading innkeeper told him, a man who kept apologizing for having forgotten his own daughter's face and then forgetting he'd apologized—was a day's walk north along the road.

Two days of work. He walked the village bounds and re-sung what wards remained, using the silver cord to stitch the fraying edges of the shrine-stone's protection, buying the place time the way you'd pack a wound. He taught Brenna and three other adults the name-anchor craft until they could do it half-asleep, until saying each other's names became a grace said over meals. He wrote down every name in the village in his journal, all forty-one of them, and read the list aloud at the well each evening while the whole village listened with the desperate attention of the drowning.

On the second evening, the innkeeper's daughter—the one whose face he'd lost—came to Elias privately. She was maybe twelve, sharp-eyed, and she looked at his badge of black iron with an expression far too old for her.

"You're not like the stories," she said.

"What stories?"

"About Wardens." She glanced over her shoulder. "My gran used to tell them, before she forgot them. She said the Order doesn't protect memories. It *collects* them. She said when the forgetting comes for a place, the Wardens come after, and they take what's left to the vaults under Corvane's mountain, and nobody ever gets anything back. She said they call it harvest." The girl's chin came up, defiant, terrified. "Is that true?"

"That is not what the Order is," Elias said.

It came out steady. It came out instantly. And it came out, he realized a heartbeat later, exactly the way the oath came out—from that same carved place behind his eyes where certainty lived without evidence. He had no memory of the Order's founding. No memory of its vaults, its mountain, its High Warden. Only the oath, and the orders, and the reflexive denial.

The girl watched his face and saw the hesitation, because children always do.

"That's what a man says," she observed, "when he hasn't checked."

After she left him standing there, he opened his journal and added a line beneath the day's entry, in small letters, as though writing it quietly would keep it from being insubordination:

*Note: verify the disposition of harvested memory. Verify the existence of return.*

He did not write the next sentence, the one underneath the thought, the one that had been forming since Sable Tull's fingers had passed through his hand like cold smoke. But it was there, patient as snowfall:

*A Warden who keeps what is entrusted to him is a wall. A Warden who takes what was never entrusted to him is only a thief with better doctrine.*

* * *

He left on the third morning, north, toward Thornfell and the Vasquez girl. Brenna pressed a bundle of food on him and made him swear—swear, a grown woman making a Warden swear—to come back through and check on them. Sable Tull gave him a gift: the tooth from the blue jar, wrapped in wool.

"So you don't get forgotten," she explained gravely. "It's easier if somebody's holding a piece of you."

He carried it in his breast pocket, opposite the orders, and told himself the arrangement meant nothing.

The road north climbed through country that grew thinner with every mile. By afternoon the eastern hills had stopped pretending altogether; there was simply a direction in which the world declined to be. Twice he passed houses that stood at odd angles to roads that no longer led anywhere, doors open, tables set, chairs pushed back as if the families had stepped out a moment ago—which, he understood, they might have. Not fled. *Subtracted.*

Toward dusk, on a rise above a frozen stream, he found the tracks.

Cat tracks. Enormous—no lynx made prints like these, no lynx had a stride this long—and beside them, smaller, boot prints of a young walker, uneven, hurrying, days old at best. And there, snagged on a hawthorn branch like a flag of surrender, a thread of red wool.

Something in him that was not memory, not oath, not order—something older and less explainable—tightened at the sight of it.

He crouched and touched the wool. Far to the north, faint as a bell across a valley, he felt—felt, the way the beasts must feel, the way no Warden training he could recall had ever mentioned feeling—a pulse of attention turn toward him and consider him, and pass on.

The Vasquez girl knew he was coming. He was certain of it, in the place where his name should have been.

Elias stood, settled his pack, and spoke his orders aloud to the emptying landscape, testing them like ice before stepping onto a frozen lake: *"Find the Vasquez girl. She carries an inheritance the Order requires."*

The words held his weight. Barely.

"But required for what?" he asked the north. "And required by whom?"

The snow answered by continuing to fall—snow that did not melt, gathering on his shoulders like a debt. He walked on, a man made of oath and orders and one child's tooth, following a red thread and a giant cat's prints toward a girl who was, he suspected, the only person left in Thornfell who might remember what he himself had been before the world forgot him—

—and whether he had been someone worth finding, or someone worth running from.
