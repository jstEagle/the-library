## 5. What the Beasts Remember

Bracken walked all night and never once looked back to see if I followed.

That was the thing about him. A dog would have checked. Even the mule—stubborn, opinionated Halvard—would have flicked an ear at me every hundred paces, keeping count. But the mountain cat moved through the snow like a debt being collected, and I was expected to keep up or be left. He'd said *the Door is leaking*, he'd said he knew my mother, and then he'd simply turned and gone, as if that were enough.

It should not have been enough. Every sensible bone in my body said turn around, go back to Thornfell, bar the door of the empty house on Cooper's Lane and wait for the world to make sense again. But the world had stopped making sense three nights ago when the stars went out, and sensible had nothing left to offer me.

So I kept up.

We climbed north out of the valley while the snow-that-doesn't-melt deepened around our legs. It wasn't cold the way snow should be cold. It didn't bite. It just lay there, patient, like it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. My breath hung in the air too long after I exhaled, little ghosts of me trailing behind, and once I could have sworn one of them kept walking after I'd stopped.

*Don't look at it,* Bracken said without turning. His voice in my head was low and rough, like a stone rolling downhill. *Things that trail you are collecting material.*

"Collecting my—" I stopped. Arguing with a cat about metaphysics seemed like a poor use of breath. "Where are we going?"

*The bears,* he said, as if this explained everything.

"What bears?"

*The ones who remember.* He paused at a bend in the trail, and for the first time since we'd left Thornfell behind, he looked back at me. His face was a map of old violence—one ear half-gone, three pale scars raking from brow to jaw, eyes the color of wet slate. *You want to know what your gift is for, girl. You've wanted to know since you were small enough to hide it. The bears will show you better than I can tell you. They sleep close to the root of things.*

"I don't want to know what it's for," I lied. "I want to know how to turn it off."

He made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter could be filed down to its bones. *No, you don't. You've never wanted that. You want it to be safe. Those aren't the same wish, but they wear each other's coats.*

I hated him a little for that. I climbed faster so he wouldn't see my face do whatever it was doing.

* * *

The cave mouth opened in the shoulder of a hill no map I'd ever seen bothered naming—a green fold of country between two ridges, the kind of place that exists only because nobody important ever looked at it. Pine trees crowded down to the rocks. The snow here lay undisturbed, unmarked by any track, which should have been impossible given what lived inside.

Bracken stopped at the threshold and would go no further.

*They know you're coming,* he said. *They've known since the stars died. Go in alone. What they give, they give to Vasquez blood only.*

"You knew my mother." I grabbed at it before he could slide away into the dark. "You said that. In the pass. You said she—"

*I said I'd let them tell you.* His tail moved once, slow, a metronome counting out his patience. *Some things must come from the deep sleepers. She trusted them with her voice. Trust them with yours.*

And then he sat down in the snow like a carved thing and closed his eyes, and I understood I would get nothing more from him tonight.

The cave breathed warm air against my face. That was wrong too—caves in winter breathe cold—but this one exhaled a smell of earth and fur and something older underneath, something like honey left too long in the comb, sweet and slightly fermented. I went in with my hand on the wall and my heart going like a smuggler's heels.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of the Thornfell inn's common room, and it was full of bears.

Seven of them. Maybe eight—the dark made arithmetic unreliable. They lay in a great loose ring around a floor of bare stone, enormous brown shapes rising and falling with breath so deep and slow it sounded like the hill itself sleeping. Their fur steamed faintly in the warm dark. None of them opened their eyes.

But all of them knew I was there. I felt it the way you feel a hand hovering over the back of your neck.

*Come in, little speaker.* The voice came from everywhere—from the ring of them, from the stone, from under the stone. It was female and vast and unhurried, a voice used to having winters to finish a sentence. *Come into the circle. Mind the paws; Ursa the younger twitches when she dreams of bees.*

I stepped over a paw the size of my head and sat down in the middle of the ring, cross-legged, on warm stone. Warm stone. In February. Under a sky with no stars.

"You know who I am," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

*We know your name the way we know the salmon run and the turning of the moon,* the great bear said. *Vasquez. Daughter of Maren Vasquez, who came here twenty-two years ago with a scarred cat at her heel and a song she needed us to hold.* A pause, long as a season. *We have held it ever since. Would you hear it?*

My throat closed. I nodded, then remembered they weren't watching, then said, "Yes," and the word cracked in the middle like river ice.

*Then listen. And understand first what we are, so you understand what you're hearing. Humans keep their memories in their heads, and heads are small rooms with thin doors. When the forgetting came—and it has come before, little one, though your elders' elders sang it out of their songs—it poured through those thin doors and emptied the rooms. But beasts keep memory differently. We keep it in the body. In the den, the migration, the scar, the song passed nose to nose across generations. The forgetting cannot find our doors. We do not keep them where it looks.*

The nearest bear shifted, and I saw that its chest fur was gray with age, its muzzle silvered.

*Your mother could not stop what was coming. She could only prepare. So she gave us her voice to keep.*

And then they sang.

I don't know how to write down what happened next without making it sound smaller than it was. Seven sleeping bears began, all together, to hum—a sound below sound, felt in the ribs before the ears—and out of that hum rose a melody, and out of the melody rose words, and the words were in my mother's voice.

Not an imitation. Not an echo. Her voice, whole, the way water is whole, with the exact grain of it I remembered from one fragmentary memory I'd carried since I was four: a woman singing over a cradle with the window open to summer, stopping mid-verse because someone laughed downstairs. I had spent my whole life rationing that memory like the last of the salt. And now here it was, multiplied, unfrozen, singing verses I'd never heard:

*Lay it down, lay it down, the long night's come,
seal the seam where the starlight ran,
hold the hunger under stone
till a keeper comes who can.*

I was crying before I knew it, tears freezing in my lashes, and the bears sang on, gentle as snowfall, and the song went on to verses about oaths and doors and a daughter not yet born, and every verse ended the same way: *till a keeper comes who can.*

"That's me," I whispered. "She meant me."

*She meant whoever came,* the old bear said, kind and terrible at once. *She hoped it would be you. Hope is not certainty. But you came, and you can hear us, and you asked the right question at last—not how to turn it off, but what it is for.* The chamber seemed to lean toward me, sevenfold breathing. *Shall we show you?*

"Show me what?"

*What she sealed. Where she sealed it. What leaks.* A rumble that might have been sorrow rolled through the ring. *You cannot keep an oath you cannot see. Sleep a little, little speaker. We will walk you there.*

* * *

I did not fall asleep so much as get taken.

One moment I was sitting in the warm dark among breathing mountains, and the next I was standing on a mountainside under a sky blazing with stars—more stars than I'd ever seen, stars in drifts and rivers, a sky so crowded with light it cast shadows. Mount Cael rose ahead of me, black against the blaze, and I was moving toward it without deciding to, my feet finding a path that wound down through scree and pine into a valley that hummed.

The humming got louder as I descended. It was the same below-sound the bears had made, but vast now, geological, coming up through the rock itself. And threaded through it, faint and steady, was my mother's song—the sealing song, sung not in memory but in real time, worn smooth by repetition, a woman somewhere ahead singing the same verse over and over the way you'd sing to keep a fire banked.

The path ended at a cliff face, and the cliff face was not a cliff face. Up close I could see it was a Door—a seam in the living rock, tall as a chapel and narrow as a grave, edged in a thin line of light that was not firelight or lamplight but *starlight*, the actual stuff of the vanished sky, pressed flat between two halves of the mountain like a flower in a book.

And beneath it, under the stone, under everything, something turned over in its sleep.

I felt it the way the animals had felt the wards snapping—I felt it in the gift I'd spent nineteen years pretending I didn't have. It was hungry. Not wicked, not cunning, just hungry the way a winter is hungry, the way forgetting is hungry, an emptiness so large it had eaten its own reasons. It pressed up against the underside of the Door and the seam of starlight flickered, and my mother's voice wavered—

—and I understood, suddenly, completely, why she had stayed. Why she had left a four-year-old daughter in a village and walked up a mountain and never come home. Not because she was brave. Because someone had to stand at this seam and sing, forever, or the thing below would push, and the Door was not built to hold forever. It was built to hold *long enough*. Long enough for what?

*Till a keeper comes who can.*

The vision shuddered. The starlit seam widened by a hair's breadth, and through the gap I heard—not words, nothing so kind—a feeling, an attention, turning toward me like a head turning toward a smell. It noticed me. It noticed the shape of what I was, the open door of my gift, and it leaned toward that openness with the whole weight of its hunger, and it said, in a voice made of every forgotten thing at once:

*you remember. give.*

I woke gasping on the warm stone with seven bears watching me—actually watching, eyes open now, dark and patient—and Bracken standing over me with his scarred face very close, his breath hot on my cheek.

*You screamed,* he said. *For an hour. The bears held the dream steady or it would have taken more than your voice.*

"It knows I'm coming." My hands were shaking. I pressed them flat against the warm stone to stop them. "It talked to me. It said—" I swallowed. "*Give.*"

The old bear exhaled, long and slow, stirring the hair off my forehead.

*Then the leaking is worse than your mother feared,* she said. *It has begun to reach for speakers. Little Vasquez, hear the last of what we hold, and take it with you: your mother's song has kept the Door these twenty-two years, but a song held in sleeping bodies grows quiet with every winter. We are old. Our daughters' daughters sing it thinner. The seal does not need a voice anymore.* Her dark eyes held mine, and in them I saw reflected a small, bedraggled girl with frozen tears on her face. *It needs a keeper. One who walks in daylight. One who can carry the song out of this cave and into the world, where the forgetting cannot follow.*

"Where do I carry it?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's. Somewhere above us, muffled by miles of stone and snow, the false winter went on falling. "Where's the Door?"

*Under Mount Cael,* the bear said. *Three weeks' walk north, if the roads still exist. If they don't—* a rumble that was almost, almost humor, *—then you will need friends who remember roads, and a boy no one remembers, and perhaps even a Warden or two. Your mother needed stranger things.*

I stared at her. "How do you know about—"

*Little speaker,* she said gently. *We are bears. We know everything that sleeps outdoors.*

Outside the cave, dawn was trying and failing to lighten a sky the color of dirty wool. Bracken stood waiting in the snow, and when I came out he fell into step beside me without being asked, heading north, always north.

"Well?" I said. "You've known all along. Say it."

He glanced up at me, slate eyes unreadable.

*Say what? That your mother sang a hole in the world shut and left you the needle?* He padded on three strides before adding, quieter, *Or that she loved you enough to spend twenty-two years arranging for you to be ready? Both are true. You may carry whichever fits today.*

Ahead of us, far off on the white horizon, Mount Cael stood with its head in the clouds—and around its summit, where no cloud had any business glowing, I could see a faint seam of light, thin as a knife cut, flickering.

Like something breathing.

Like something *leaning on the door*.
