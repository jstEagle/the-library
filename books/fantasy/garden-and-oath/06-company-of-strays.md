## 6. Company of Strays

The waystation was called the Kettle, because that was what it looked like: a squat stone pot half-buried in a hillside, with a crooked chimney for a spout and a door so low you had to bow to enter. Smugglers had been bowing through it for three generations. There was a superstition about it—Marta claimed she'd started it herself, decades back, to keep the Wardens out. *Bow to the house or it won't remember you.* These days the superstition felt less like a joke.

Rosalind came down off the ridge at dusk with Bracken flowing beside her like a shadow that had decided to have edges. The snow still hadn't melted. It lay on the pines in perfect unbroken sheets, and where her boots punched through, the holes stayed crisp, as if the world were too tired to heal even its small wounds.

*They are inside,* Bracken said. His voice arrived in her head the way cold air arrives under a door—suddenly, and with a smell attached. He smelled, always, of granite and old blood. *The fence-man. The map-woman. And something else. Something thin.*

"Thin?"

*A boy-shaped absence. Be careful of it. Things that have been erased leave a hole, and holes pull.*

She'd learned not to ask how he knew things. She'd also learned that ignoring his warnings was a good way to lose sleep.

She bowed through the low door into warmth and woodsmoke, and there was Tam Voss—no relation, though he'd been claiming otherwise for years whenever it got him a discount—sitting at the long table with his boots up and a cup of something steaming in his fist.

"Well," Tam said. "Look what the snow dragged in."

He looked worse than when she'd left him in Thornfell. Thinner around the eyes. His fingers drummed the table in a rhythm that never quite resolved, the way a man drums when he's counting things he's lost.

"You're supposed to be south," Rosalind said, shaking snow off her shoulders. "You told me the Kettle was a death trap and anyone who stopped here deserved what they got."

"I say a lot of things." He pushed a second cup toward her without being asked. He'd always known exactly how she took it—honey, too much—and the familiarity of it caught in her throat like a fishbone. "Roads south aren't roads anymore, Roz. You'd know that if you'd come the long way like I did. Half of them just... stop. Not washed out. Not blocked. They stop, and then they were never there, and your feet remember walking them but your head starts arguing."

"I know."

"Yeah." He studied her face. "Yeah, I suppose you would. Heard the stars went out over Thornfell the same night the wards snapped. Whole village saw it. Half of them have already forgotten they saw it." He laughed, but it was a bad sound, a coin bitten and found hollow. "That's the part that gets me. Not the forgetting. How *fast* it goes. Old Hobb forgot his own wedding before the funeral cakes were eaten."

An old woman spoke from the corner without looking up from her work. "Hobb forgot his wife's face first. The wedding came later. Memory goes from the inside out, boy. Faces before names, names before events, events before the ground itself gives up." Marta Quill was seventy if she was a day, bent over a table covered in paper, drawing with a stub of charcoal held in a fist knotted like tree root. "Which is why the roads go last. Roads are the outside of us."

"Marta," Rosalind said. "You're still mapping?"

"Still mapping." Marta turned a sheet toward her, and Rosalind's breath caught. It was Thornfell province—the whole of it, every road, every bridge, every footpath, drawn in a hand so fine it looked woven rather than drawn. But here and there whole sections were smudged gray, as if the charcoal itself had faded, and Marta kept going back over them, again and again, pressing harder each time. "I've walked every one of these since I was nine years old. If I can hold them in my head, they hold. For a while. That's the trick nobody wants to hear—a place only lasts as long as somebody remembers walking it."

"That's not true," Rosalind said quietly. "It can't be true."

"It's true enough to be useful." Marta tapped the map. "And false enough to be survivable. Both at once. That's the world now, girl. Get used to holding two things."

Bracken padded to the fire and lay down with the enormous entitlement of a cat who has decided a room belongs to him. Tam stared at him. "That's a mountain cat."

"He is."

"They kill people."

"He hasn't yet."

Tam moved his chair six inches farther away anyway.

* * *

The third member of the company didn't introduce himself, because no one asked. That was the thing about Pip—people didn't ask, didn't notice, didn't remember he was there. Rosalind noticed him the way you notice a draft: a slight wrongness by the pantry shelf, a shape the firelight kept sliding off. When she finally looked directly at him, he flinched like a man expecting a blow.

He was maybe twelve. Brown hair, brown everything, the kind of face that would be hard to describe five minutes after leaving a room. He wore a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled eight times.

"You can see me," he said. It wasn't a question. It was the question, the only one he had left.

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"You're sitting by the pantry. You've got crumbs on your chin."

He wiped his chin with his sleeve, and his eyes went bright and wet, and he grinned like she'd handed him a kingdom. "Nobody sees me. Not since—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a village. I think there was a village. My mum baked bread, I think, or I wanted her to, or—I don't know which anymore. Everyone who ever knew me forgot me all at once, and now I'm like a word on the tip of everyone's tongue except nobody's tongue." He shrugged, too casual, a boy who'd practiced casual until it fit like his coat. "Anyway. You see me. So either you're broken or I'm real."

"Both," said Marta, not looking up. "Welcome to the club."

*The hole,* Bracken murmured from the fireside. *See? A boy-shaped hole. But the hole is warm. Strange.*

* * *

They ate stew that tasted mostly of turnip and argument. It was Tam who started it, because Tam couldn't sit in silence longer than an hour without filling it with trouble.

"South," he said, slapping the table. "That's the play. Always was. We go south, past the old toll bridges, down to the river country where the wards were built thick. Whatever's eating Thornfell's memory, it started at the border—it'll hit the thin places first and the thick places last. We get ahead of it."

"The river country is four hundred miles," Marta said. "Through terrain I can't map because I've never walked it, and if I can't remember a road, boy, it doesn't exist. You'd be asking me to lead you off my map entirely."

"So we make new roads."

"With what? Wishes?" She jabbed her charcoal at the window, at the blue-black dark beyond. "You've seen what's happening. Places don't fade because we walk away from them. They fade because something is *drinking* them. You want to run ahead of a flood, fine. But don't pretend running is a plan."

"It's more of a plan than anything else on this table."

"There's something else on this table," Rosalind said.

They all looked at her. Even Bracken opened one green eye.

She hadn't meant to say it yet. She'd meant to keep it folded up inside her where she kept everything that mattered, everything about her mother, everything the bears had shown her in their cave of sleeping breath. But Tam was drumming his fingers and Marta was wearing herself down to charcoal dust holding roads together by stubbornness alone, and Pip was watching her with those eyes that said *you see me, please keep seeing me,* and Rosalind had spent her whole life keeping secrets and look where the country was now.

"There's a Door," she said. "Under Mount Cael. The Last Door. It's the way out of Thornfell—not south, not through the wards. Out. Past them entirely. The beasts know about it. The old ones. And my mother—" Her voice did something embarrassing, and she steadied it. "My mother sealed it. Before I was born. Or when I was small. I don't remember which, and that's the problem, isn't it? I don't remember, and she's gone, and the only ones who remember her are animals."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the Kettle creaked like an old man settling into a chair.

"A Door," Tam repeated flatly. "Out of Thornfell. Under a mountain. Your dead mother built it."

"Sealed it. Built it, maybe. I don't know. The bears showed me—" She stopped, because how did you explain the vision? Standing inside a bear's dream, seeing a seam of starlight buried in living rock, hearing her mother's voice singing a song with words like *stay, stay, stay.* "They showed me enough."

"And you believe beast-dreams now." Tam rubbed his face. "Roz. Sweet Roz. I've known you since you were stealing apples off my cart. You're the most level-headed liar I ever met. This isn't like you."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

"Mount Cael is north," Marta said slowly. Her charcoal had gone still. "Into the teeth of it. Whatever's drinking the memory, it's stronger up there—the erasure moves like weather, and weather comes off the peaks." She looked at her map, at the gray smudges spreading across the high country like mold across bread. "If I take you there, I'm leading you off the edge of everything I know."

"Yes."

"If I go off my map, I might forget myself. Did you know that? An old woman's mind is a road too, girl. It needs walking."

"I know."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then she snorted, which Rosalind was learning was the sound Marta made instead of crying. "Well. At least you don't pretend it's a good idea."

"It's a terrible idea," Rosalind said. "But the south is a slow idea. We run, and the country dies behind us, and eventually whatever's doing this catches up, because it's faster than old women and smugglers and boys nobody remembers. The Door is the only thing I've heard of that's *outside* it. Outside the wards. Maybe outside the hunger. My mother thought it mattered enough to seal it with a song. I want to know why."

"Why?" Tam threw up his hands. "Why does it matter? Open it, walk through, done."

"Because nothing sealed shut stays shut forever," Bracken said.

Everyone froze. Tam knocked his stool over getting up. "The cat talked. The cat *talked.*"

"I talk constantly," Bracken said. "You simply weren't worth talking to." He rose, stretched until his spine curved like a drawn bow, and sat again. "Listen to me, fence-man, map-woman, boy-shaped hole. I am old. Older than your Order, older than your roads. I knew Maren Vasquez. I walked beside her for eleven years, and I stood at the Door when she sang it closed, and I heard what was on the other side of it knocking." His tail curled around his paws, precise as a signature. "The seal is failing. That is why the stars went out—that is why the wards snapped like harp strings. The Door is leaking, and what leaks out first is forgetting, and what leaks out last will be much worse. Running south buys you a season. Perhaps two. Going to the Door buys you a chance."

"A chance at what?" Pip asked. Small voice, steady eyes.

Bracken looked at Rosalind.

"At answers," she said. "At whatever my mother left behind. At a way out that doesn't mean abandoning everyone who can't run." She thought of the innkeeper in Thornfell, patting his daughter's shoulder with the polite bafflement of a man touching a stranger. She thought of the well remembering water it had never held. "I'm done running from my gift. I'm done pretending I don't hear what I hear. If my mother left a lock on this country, then I'm the closest thing to a key it has, and I'd rather die finding out than live another winter not knowing."

"That's a horrible speech," Tam said. "Genuinely. No craft at all."

"But?" Rosalind said.

He groaned and dragged his hands down his face. "But I once watched you talk a mule out of a ravine using nothing but insults, and I've decided your gift is the least crazy thing on the table. Fine. North. To the mountain. To the magic death Door." He pointed at her. "For pay. I want that noted. I am a professional and this is a job."

"Noted," Rosalind said, and something in her chest loosened for the first time since the stars went out.

Marta was already pulling fresh paper toward her, already sketching, her charcoal whispering. "North by the drover's road until the ford at Harrow Beck—if Harrow Beck still exists, which it won't, so north by where Harrow Beck should be. Then up through the black pines." She glanced up, fierce and ancient and suddenly smiling. "Off my map, then. Into blank paper. Well. I always wondered what was out there."

Pip tugged Rosalind's sleeve. "Can I come?"

"You don't need permission."

"I do, though." He said it simply, without self-pity, the way you'd state that water was wet. "If nobody says yes to me, I stop being anywhere. That's how it works. I've tested it." He swallowed. "Will you say yes?"

"Yes," Rosalind said. "Loudly, so the whole room hears it. Yes, Pip. You come with us. You carry the honey jars—you're lightest on your feet—and you sit next to me at every fire, and every night I'll tell the others something about you until they can't help remembering. Your name is Pip. You had a mother who baked bread. Say it back."

"Pip," he said. "Bread." His voice cracked on the second word, and he grinned through it. "Bread. Yeah."

* * *

Later, when the others slept—Tam sprawled boneless, Marta still scratching faintly at her maps even in dreams, Pip curled so close to the fire he was nearly in it—Rosalind stepped out the low door into the blue snowlight.

Bracken materialized beside her, silent as falling powder.

"You could have told me sooner," she said. "About my mother. About the Door. You let me stumble around blind for days."

"You were not ready."

"And now?"

"Now you have a reason bigger than your fear." He looked up at her, and in the starless dark his eyes were the only green left in the world. "Your mother made the same speech you made tonight. Word for word, almost. *I'd rather die finding out.* It frightened me then, too."

"What happened to her, Bracken? Really."

The cat was quiet for a long time. Snow gathered on his scarred shoulders and did not melt.

"She went down to the Door," he said at last, "and she did not come back up. And tomorrow, little Vasquez, we start walking the same road."
