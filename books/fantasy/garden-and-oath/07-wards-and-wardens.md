## 7. Wards and Wardens

The road remembered Elias even when he could not remember it.

That was the only way he could describe the sensation. He had been walking since before dawn, following the trail of five travelers and one impossible cat through snow that refused to melt, and somewhere past the second burned mile-marker his boots began to know things. They turned left where the drifts argued for right. They avoided a stretch of ground that looked solid and was not. Twice he stopped, looked down at his own feet, and felt something like gratitude toward them—strange, misplaced, as if they belonged to a boy who had once walked this road every day of his life.

He did not remember being that boy. The Order had seen to that, or the forgetting had, or both. What remained was the oath, carved into him deeper than any name: *Keep the borders. Guard the memory. Serve the country above the self.* And beneath the oath, folded small like a letter in a coat pocket, the orders: *Find the Vasquez girl.*

Elias pulled his hood lower against the wind and kept walking.

The tracks were easy to read. Five sets of boots, unevenly spaced—the fence limping on the right, the boy drifting in short distracted loops, an old woman's careful prints beside a mule's round ones. And threading through all of them, light as punctuation, the prints of a great cat who walked where it pleased and cared nothing for concealment. Elias crouched over one such print and measured it against his spread hand. Too large for a lynx. Far too large. He had reported the beast to Corvane three days ago and received no reply, which from Corvane was itself a reply: *I already know. Keep moving.*

The girl was moving north. That much was clear, and that much troubled him. North lay Mount Cael, and beneath Mount Cael lay the Door, and everything in his training said the Vasquez line would go there eventually—it was in their blood the way rivers were in valleys—but everything else in his training said no one should ever reach it. The Order's first law, older than its name: *the Door stays shut.* If she reached it, he was to stop her. If he could not stop her—

He found he did not know what came after *if I cannot stop her.* There was a blank space there, smooth as river ice, and when he pressed on it he felt the oath shift uncomfortably, like a man rearranging himself in a chair.

*You've doubted before,* he told himself. *Doubt is not betrayal.* But the words rang hollow, and he noticed, with a Warden's precision, exactly how hollow.

* * *

The waystation appeared out of the snowfall the way things had begun appearing everywhere lately: suddenly, half-formed, as though the world were sketching rather than building. A long low building of dark timber, roof bowed under old snow, smoke rising thin and true from the chimney. A stable attached at an angle, as if added by someone in a hurry. A sign hanging by one chain, its letters worn to ghosts.

Elias knew the sign.

Not recognized—*knew*. His hand rose before he decided anything and touched the wood, tracing grooves his fingers seemed to have counted before, and a word assembled itself in his mouth without permission:

"Harrowgate."

He stood very still. Snow gathered on his shoulders. The letters on the sign were illegible, erased like everything else, but under his palm he could feel them—not read them, feel them—the way you feel a scar through cloth. *Harrowgate Waystation.* And beneath that, smaller, another ghost: *Marta keeps good beer and bad company.*

A laugh tried to climb out of his chest and died halfway, strangled by confusion. He was twenty-six years old or thereabouts; the Order had told him so. He had been taken young—that much the novices all shared, the common wound, taken young and given new names and taught that names were anchors and anchors were weaknesses. Whatever life he'd had before the taking had been filed away in the vaults below the Citadel, along with everyone else's.

So why did his hand know this sign? Why did the smell of woodsmoke and tanned leather rising from the stable make his throat tighten with a homesickness so specific it had a shape—a kitchen, low-beamed, a woman humming off-key, flour on her knuckles?

*Marta,* the sign had said. The old woman with the maps. He had watched her through his spyglass two days ago, unrolling oiled leather sheets covered in inked roads, and something about the set of her shoulders as she bent over her work had made him lower the glass too fast, heart hammering, like a man who has seen his own face in a stranger's window.

He should report this. It was precisely the kind of thing Wardens reported: anomalous memory response, possible pre-Order connection, subject Marta, recommend assessment. The words formed themselves in the trained cadence and he heard, with horror, how easy they were. How smoothly the machinery of his obedience still turned, even now, even here, standing in the snow with his hand on a sign that remembered him better than he remembered himself.

He did not report it. Not yet. He told himself *not yet*, which is the lie doubters tell, and went to the door.

* * *

He meant to observe only. Circle the building, count windows, note the state of their supplies, withdraw to the tree line and wait for Corvane, who had sent word at last that morning—a single line, *hold position, I am coming to you*—and when Corvane came, Elias wanted to bring him something more than a trail. He wanted to bring him a question. *Why does the waystation know me, sir? Why do I know it?*

Instead he made his first mistake: he looked through the window.

They were gathered around the long table in the main room, lamplit, and for a moment he simply watched them the way you watch a fire—without agenda. The fence, Tam, was propped in a chair with his bandaged leg stretched out, arguing cheerfully about nothing. The boy, Pip, sat cross-legged on the bench turning something over in his hands—a carved wooden bird, Elias saw, crude and beloved. The old woman, Marta, was bent over her maps again, and the cat lay across the hearthstones like a fallen shadow, enormous, watching the room with eyes that caught the firelight green.

And the girl. Rosalind Vasquez sat with her back half to the window, a bowl in her hands, and she was doing something so ordinary it struck him like a blow: she was feeding scraps to the mule through the doorway, talking to it under her breath, and the mule had its ears tipped toward her voice the way animals only tip their ears toward one thing in the world.

*Find the Vasquez girl.* He had imagined the finding a hundred ways—arrest, escort, negotiation, if it came to it force—and in none of those imaginings had she been feeding a mule scraps in a lamplit room while an old woman laughed at something the fence said. In none of them had they looked like this. Like a family assembling itself out of spare parts, out of strays, out of people the country had dropped and forgotten.

His mother had hummed off-key. Flour on her knuckles. The thought arrived uninvited and he gripped the windowsill.

That was when Marta looked up.

Not at the window—at nothing, at the middle distance, the way old women look up when a door opens somewhere far away. Then her eyes came around, slow, and found him through the glass, and Elias understood with total clarity that she had known he was there since before he reached the sign. She did not startle. She did not alert the others. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she lifted one hand—not a threat, not a welcome, something older than either—and pointed, deliberately, at the sign outside.

Then she went back to her maps.

Elias stepped back into the snow. His breath came ragged. *She knows me,* he thought, and then, worse, quieter, the thought underneath: *I want her to.*

He retreated to the tree line as instructed. He built no fire. He sat with his back against a pine and watched the lit windows and told himself he was holding position, following orders, serving the country above the self, and the oath recited itself in him dutifully, over and over, like a prayer whose god had stopped answering.

* * *

Corvane came at moonrise, and he brought the wagons.

Elias heard them first—axles complaining on frozen ruts—and stepped out to meet the column: six sledges drawn by mules that wore the blank, disciplined calm of Order stock, and a dozen Wardens in grey, and High Warden Corvane riding at the front with his lantern unshuttered, because Corvane believed darkness was for other people.

He was not what strangers expected. Not tall, not armored, a compact man gone silver at the temples, with a scholar's stoop and a voice pitched for lecture halls. He dismounted stiffly, pressed his gloved hands together, and smiled at Elias with genuine warmth, and that warmth was the most frightening thing about him, because it never wavered, not ever, not for anyone.

"Elias." He said the name like a benediction. "You've done well. The trail is clean work."

"Sir." Elias saluted. "There's something you should—I've found something. About myself. The waystation, the sign, I think I may have—"

"Yes." Corvane was already walking past him toward the wagons, gesturing for the others to follow, and Elias fell in step because he had always fallen in step. "I read your file before I came. Novice intake, northern circuit, age approximately seven. Village of origin unknown." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Unknown to us, Elias. Not necessarily unknown to you. The Order has always suspected the northern villages produced strong candidates. Strong roots make strong trees."

"You're saying I'm from here."

"I'm saying it doesn't matter." Corvane stopped at the lead wagon and rested a hand on its rail, and his smile deepened into something almost tender. "But watch. You wanted to understand your orders. Understand them now."

The wagon's canvas cover came back, and Elias looked inside, and for a moment his mind refused the contents.

Glass. Rows upon rows of glass vessels, packed in straw like eggs—flasks, jars, bottles of every size, each one sealed with grey wax, each one holding something that was not quite liquid and not quite light. A slow silver swirling, like fog trapped in a bottle, like milk stirred into water, like—like memory. Because he had been raised on the theory, everyone had, the doctrine every novice memorized: *memory is the substance of a country; when the vessel breaks, decant the wine.* The Order preserved. The Order saved what the forgetting would take. Every child in Thornfell learned it as comfort.

He had never seen the harvest itself.

"How many?" he asked, and his voice came out wrong.

"Four hundred and eleven," Corvane said, with the satisfaction of a man reporting a harvest yield. "Villages along the southern arc. Ashford. Millbrook. The ferry towns. All of it—names, faces, roads, songs, grievances, recipes, the lot. Decanted, sealed, catalogued." He patted the wagon rail the way a farmer pats a good ox. "Safe. Nothing lost. That is the mercy of our work, Elias. Everything the country forgets, we keep. Perfectly. Forever."

"Kept where?"

"The Citadel. The vaults." Corvane turned to him, and the lantern-light made his eyes kind and bottomless at once. "Where your childhood sits in a jar, if we're fortunate enough to have harvested your village in time. Perhaps you'll be allowed to visit it someday. Perhaps you'll even recognize it." The smile again. "Wouldn't that be a gift?"

Elias looked at the bottles. Four hundred and eleven villages' worth, and the wagons were not full. There were more wagons behind. There would be more wagons after. And north of here lay a dozen villages still living, still remembering, still singing to their children in kitchens that smelled of flour—and the column had come north, following him, following the girl.

"Sir," he said carefully, "the southern villages—they were dying anyway. The wards failed. I understand the necessity. But the people here are still—" He searched for the word and hated how hard it was to find. "Still *here*. Still themselves. If we evacuate them south, if we move them out before the forgetting reaches—"

"Evacuate them *to what*?" Corvane asked, gently, reasonably, the way you speak to a promising student who has asked a foolish question. "South of the border is not a place, Elias. You've seen the edges. When a country forgets itself, there is no *outside* waiting with open arms. There is only what we save and what we lose. The land is dying. We are the will." He spread his hands, and the lantern threw his shadow huge across the snow. "Thornfell has perhaps a season. Less. Every day we delay, more of it pours away like water through cupped hands. So we cup our hands. We harvest while the harvest is sweet. And yes—" the tenderness again, unbearable, "—yes, some of it must be taken from the living. Memory held in a head is memory at risk. Memory in glass is memory forever. Which would you choose, for your village? For your mother's kitchen? The fading, or the flask?"

Elias thought of flour on knuckles. Of a woman humming off-key in a low-beamed kitchen, a memory he did not own, that lived nowhere he could reach—unless it sat in one of these bottles, sealed in grey wax, swirled silver, catalogued. Owned by the Order. Visitable by permission.

"Memory isn't wine," he heard himself say.

"No?"

"It doesn't keep. It *lives*. It lives by being told, being shared, being—" he thought of the lamplit window, the girl feeding the mule, the family of strays, "—being *used*. You put it in glass and it stops being memory. It becomes specimen."

Corvane regarded him for a long moment. Around them the Wardens had gone very quiet, the way soldiers go quiet when two officers disagree within earshot. Then the High Warden laughed—warmly, genuinely, horribly.

"There he is," he said. "There's the mind I chose you for. Do you know why I picked you out of the novices, Elias? Not your swordwork. Your questions. A Warden who never doubts is just a lock; useful, but dumb metal. A Warden who doubts and *stays*—that's a hinge. That holds the door." He stepped close, and lowered his voice to the register he used for truths. "Doubt all you like. Doubt is permitted. Doubt is even useful. But the girl goes into custody, the Door stays shut, and the harvest continues. Those are the load-bearing walls, and you will not pull them down. Am I understood?"

Elias saluted. "Understood, sir."

It was the truth. That was the worst of it. He understood perfectly, and he meant it, and some hinge-deep part of him had already begun, quietly, without his permission, to calculate how much weight those walls would bear.

* * *

They camped in sight of the waystation, which Corvane called *containment posture* and Elias called, privately, a siege. Through the trees he watched the lit windows, and near midnight he saw a figure come out onto the step—the old woman, Marta, wrapped in shawls, standing in the cold looking directly toward the tree line, toward him, as if she could pick his particular darkness out of all the other darkness.

She raised her hand again. Not a wave. She held up three fingers, then closed them slowly into a fist, then opened the hand flat, empty.

Elias stared. Three. Closed. Open.

He ran it through every signal code the Order used and found nothing. Then he stopped running codes and simply looked at it the way his hand had looked at the sign—with the knowledge that lived below the memory. Three fingers. A fist. An open hand.

*Three days,* it might mean. Or three of something. Or: *what you had, what was taken, what remains.*

Or, simplest and worst: *count, child. Count what's left.*

Corvane's voice drifted across the snow from the command tent, warm and unhurried, giving orders for the morning. *"At first light we begin with the outlying farms. Small holdings first—good practice for the men. Then the waystation itself. The old woman's maps will be valuable. Catalogue everything."*

Catalogue everything. Elias stood in the dark between the two lights—the Order's lanterns behind him, the waystation's hearth ahead—and felt the oath turn over in his chest like a key that no longer fit its lock.

Tomorrow they would come for the kitchen that smelled of flour. For the maps. For the laughing fence and the boy with the wooden bird and the girl who spoke to mules.

Unless someone warned them. Unless someone who knew the Order's timing, its formations, its methods—someone trusted, someone load-bearing—walked up to that door in the dark and knocked.

Elias looked down at his hands. They were steady. They had already decided something; he could feel it in them, the same knowing his boots had, the same boy's knowledge, rising.

He took one step toward the waystation lights.
