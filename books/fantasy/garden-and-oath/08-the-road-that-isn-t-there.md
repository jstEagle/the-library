## 8. The Road That Isn't There

Marta woke them an hour before dawn by striking a kettle with a spoon.

"Up," she said. "The road won't wait, and neither will I. I'm seventy-one years old and my knees have opinions."

They stumbled out of the waystation into a darkness that felt thicker than ordinary night. No stars—there had been no stars for six days now—and the snow fell straight down, unhurried, as if the sky had all the time in the world to bury them. Rosalind pulled her coat tight and watched her breath hang in the air like something reluctant to leave her.

Bracken sat on the woodpile, tail curled over his scarred paws, eyes catching what little light there was. *She means it,* he said. *Old women who hit kettles mean everything they say.*

"Thank you for that insight," Rosalind muttered.

Tam came out last, favoring his left side where he'd wrenched it hauling crates off the mule two days back. He looked at Marta, then at the blank white field beyond the waystation fence, then at Marta again.

"There's no road out there," he said. "I've run this stretch a dozen times. There's the pass road south, and there's nothing north but rockfall and old forest. You're going to walk us into a snowdrift and call it a shortcut."

Marta smiled the way she did when she was about to be insufferable. She unrolled one of her maps—not on a table, just held flat against her chest with both hands—and turned it so Tam could see. The parchment was dense with ink, roads crossing roads, little names in her cramped handwriting: *Miller's Bend. The Hollow Bridge. Kettleman's Rise.* None of them appeared on any map Rosalind had ever seen in Thornfell village.

"This is the Old Drover's Way," Marta said. "Ran from the waystation to the foot of Mount Cael before your grandfather's grandfather was born. It's not gone, boy. It's only forgotten. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Tam said. "A thing nobody remembers is a thing that doesn't exist."

"That," said Marta, rolling up the map with a snap, "is exactly the kind of thinking that got this country into trouble. Get your pack."

* * *

Pip walked beside Rosalind at the rear of the line, which was where Pip always walked, close enough that his shoulder occasionally bumped her elbow. He was eleven, maybe twelve—he wasn't sure himself—and he had been erased from every memory in Thornfell except his own. His mother had forgotten him mid-sentence, he'd told Rosalind once, in the middle of telling him to come in for supper. She'd looked right through him and shut the door. He'd slept in the waystation loft ever since, and no one had thought to wonder about the boy in the loft, because no one could.

He carried a slingshot, three smooth stones, and a wooden bird he'd carved himself. He'd shown Rosalind the bird on their second day out. Its wings were wrong—one longer than the other—but he'd insisted it was a kestrel, and she'd agreed it was a very fine kestrel, and that had been the whole conversation, and somehow it had mattered more than most conversations she'd ever had.

Ahead of them, Marta stopped at the edge of the field, took a breath like a woman about to dive into cold water, and stepped forward onto open snow.

And the road rose up to meet her.

It didn't appear so much as *remember itself*. First the packed earth beneath the snow, dark and firm where everything around it lay soft and trackless. Then the wheel ruts, faint as scars. Then, along both sides, the ghosts of drystone walls, knee-high, half-suggestive, stones that would be solid if you looked at them directly and were mist if you didn't. A mile ahead, the road simply stopped, dissolving into unbroken whiteness—but underfoot, where Marta stood, it was as real as the floor of the waystation.

"Well?" Marta called back, not turning around. "Are you coming, or do you want to stand there proving Tam's point?"

Tam made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer, and stepped onto the road. It held him. Bracken flowed past them all, unimpressed, though Rosalind noticed his ears were flat.

*The old ones are like this,* he said quietly, so that only she heard. *Roads, wells, hearthstones. They hold on longest. People forget fastest.*

"What about me?" Rosalind asked under her breath. "What holds on for people?"

Bracken glanced back at her, and for once the mountain cat looked almost gentle. *You're asking the wrong question, kit. Ask what you can hold on for.*

* * *

They walked all morning, and the road walked with them.

That was the only way Rosalind could describe it. The road existed exactly as far as Marta remembered it, and Marta remembered it one stride at a time. Behind them, she could feel it fading—the walls thinning, the ruts filling with snow, until when she looked back there was only the field again, pristine, as if no one had ever crossed it. Ahead, it assembled itself out of Marta's certainty, stone by stone, rut by rut, sometimes hesitating, sometimes flickering when Marta frowned and murmured to herself, counting milestones under her breath.

"You're doing this," Rosalind said, falling into step beside the old woman. "All of it. Every foot of it."

"I'm holding the door open," Marta corrected. "The road's been here eighty years. It knows its own shape better than I do. I'm just—" she waved a gnarled hand— "insisting. Politely. That it keep existing while we use it."

"And if you stop insisting?"

Marta gave her a look over the top of her scarf. "Then we'd best hope you've learned to insist too, girl. That's rather the point of this whole expedition, isn't it?"

Rosalind didn't answer. But she tried, after that, walking with her eyes fixed ahead, picturing the road continuing—picturing the walls solid, the bridge at Miller's Bend whole. And whether it helped or whether Marta simply needed less help than she let on, the road kept rising to meet them.

At noon they passed a milestone. It read **KETTLEMAN'S RISE — 2**, and the letters were freshly cut, sharp-edged, still smelling faintly of stone dust. Tam ran his thumb across them and shook his head slowly.

"My grandmother used to sing a song about Kettleman's Rise," he said. "Something about a wedding. I always thought she'd made it up." He looked at Marta. "She hadn't, had she."

"She married a Kettleman's boy," Marta said. "Elias Kettleman. Drove honey, same as you. Died young." She started walking again. "Keep moving. Grief keeps too, but I'd rather not carry any more of it than I have to."

* * *

It happened in the afternoon, at the place where the road climbed through a cutting between two hills, and it happened fast.

One moment Pip was beside her, chattering about the kestrel—he'd decided it needed a name, and he was workshopping options—and the next he had stopped dead in the middle of the road, and the snow was coming down through him.

Not around him. Through him. Flakes passing through his shoulder like he was smoke, and his outline going soft at the edges, the way the road went soft when Marta frowned.

"Pip." Rosalind grabbed for his arm and her fingers met resistance, then didn't, then did again, like gripping water that couldn't decide whether to be ice. His face was slack. Not frightened—worse than frightened. Empty. Looking at her without recognition.

"He's fading," Tam shouted from ahead. "Why's he fading? We're on the road!"

"It's not the road." Marta had turned, and her voice cracked like a whip. "Boy! Look at me! What's your name?"

Pip's mouth moved. Nothing came out. And Rosalind understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the weather, what was happening. The forgetting wasn't just out there, eating villages and stars. It was in Pip, had been in him since his mother closed the door, and it worked on him from inside. Everyone else held him up without knowing it—a glance, a greeting, someone saying *mind the boy*. Out here, on a road that existed because one old woman refused to let it go, there was nothing holding Pip up at all. Except—

Except what people said to him. Except his story, told aloud.

*Rosie,* Bracken said, appearing at her side, fur bristling. *Now. Tell him who he is.*

So Rosalind knelt in the snow on a road that wasn't there, took Pip's half-solid face in her hands, and began to talk.

"Your name is Pip," she said. "Pip. You're eleven, maybe twelve—you're not sure, and you get cross when people guess wrong, and you always guess yourself older. You sleep in the loft at the waystation even though nobody remembers putting a bed up there, because the bed is yours and you put it there yourself, and you made the ladder too, out of crate slats, and it creaks on the third rung."

His eyes focused. Slightly. Snow still drifting through his sleeve.

"You have a slingshot," she went on, faster now, words tumbling. "You can hit a tin can at thirty paces. Tam taught you, two winters ago, behind the stable, and you practiced until your fingers bled because you wanted to show him you could beat his record, and you did, and he sulked for a whole day and then bought you honey cake and never mentioned it again."

"That's—" Pip's voice was thin, distant, like a voice calling up from a well. "That's true."

"You carve," Rosalind said. "You carved a kestrel. One wing's longer than the other and you told me it was fine, it was *aerodynamic*, and I believed you, because you believe things so hard that other people can't help believing them too. That's your gift, Pip. Not the slingshot. The believing. You believe in things nobody else remembers, and that's why you're still here, and that's why you're going to *stay* here—"

"I forgot my mother's voice," Pip whispered. His edges flickered. "I've been trying to remember it all week and I can't, and if I can't remember her then maybe she was right to forget me, maybe I'm not—"

"*Listen to me.*" Rosalind's hands tightened on his face, and she felt the strange double sensation of him, solid and not-solid, and she was crying now and didn't care. "Your mother loved you. Do you hear me? Whatever the forgetting did to her, whatever it took out of her head—that wasn't her choice, and it wasn't proof of anything except that this country is sick. She was calling you in for supper. She was calling you in for supper, Pip, because that's what mothers do, because you were hers, and somewhere under the sickness part of her is still calling, and when we get through this, when we find the Door, I swear on my mother's grave and on Marta's map and on every road we walk today—I will help you find whatever's left of her, and you will tell her your name until she remembers it. But first you have to stay. Do you understand? Staying is the fight. Right now, today, on this road, staying is the bravest thing anyone has ever done."

The snow hung suspended around him. For a heartbeat nothing moved—not Pip, not the flakes, not the wind.

Then Pip breathed. A real breath, ragged and loud, and the snowflakes that had been passing through him broke apart against his shoulders instead, and his edges firmed up like wax cooling, and he collapsed against Rosalind and sobbed into her coat with the whole ugly relief of a boy who had almost stopped existing and hadn't.

Rosalind held him and looked up at the others. Tam had his hat in his hands. Marta's eyes were bright and fierce.

"Now you know," Marta said quietly. "Now you all know what we're carrying, and what it costs, and why the Door matters. Say his name when you see him. All of you. Every day. Names are ropes, and we are all of us hanging over something."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a wall that Marta swore had been a shepherd's hut once, and which obligingly remembered enough of itself to give them a roof of sorts and a hearth that lit on the third try. Tam cooked barley and dried apples. Pip sat pressed against Rosalind's side the entire evening, and every time one of them spoke to him—*pass the pot, Pip; watch the fire, Pip; good throw today, Pip*—she saw him flinch a little, then settle, like a man being handed weights to carry who had expected to float away.

Later, when the others slept, she sat by the hearth with Bracken's warm weight against her legs and watched the darkness where the road should be.

"It ends tomorrow, doesn't it," she said. "Marta's memory. There's only so much road in one person."

*There is,* Bracken said. *But you noticed something today, kit. When you talked to the boy, the fire burned brighter. Did you feel it?*

She had, actually. She'd assumed it was imagination.

"Stories burn," she said slowly.

*Stories burn,* Bracken agreed. *Which means the hunger under the mountain isn't the only thing that feeds on memory. It's just the only thing that's learned to steal. Your mother knew that. It's why she sang instead of fighting.* He tucked his nose under his tail. *Sleep, Rosie. Tomorrow the road runs out, and after that we walk on faith and maps. You'll want your strength.*

Rosalind lay back and stared at the smoke-hole, where no stars showed. But just before sleep took her, she heard it—faint, from the direction of the hearth, where the embers had settled: a thread of melody, wordless, patient, like a song remembering itself.

Marta, asleep in her blankets, was humming.

And the road, outside, held.
