## 9. Corvane's Bargain

The shrine appeared out of the snow like something surfacing from deep water.

One moment they were following Marta's memory of a shepherd's track along the shoulder of the fells, and the next there it was: a low ring of standing stones, half-buried, their carvings worn to suggestions. A stone basin at the center held a skin of black ice, and beneath the ice—Rosalind had to kneel and look twice to believe it—a single green leaf, perfectly preserved, refusing to rot the way everything else in Thornfell had stopped being allowed to change.

"Wayshrine," Marta said, breathing hard. She leaned on Pip's shoulder more than she'd ever admit to. "The Old Road shrines. Travelers left a memory here before crossing the high passes. You gave the stone something you couldn't afford to lose, so the mountain would hold it for you."

"Did people come back for them?" Pip asked.

"Some did." Marta looked at the basin a long moment. "Some decided the mountain could keep them."

Tam laughed—the easy, crackling laugh that had gotten them through three days of white silence—and clapped Rosalind on the back. "Well, I've got nothing worth keeping. Let's warm up and move on."

Bracken didn't laugh. The mountain cat stood at the edge of the shrine with his ears flat, tail low, staring into the trees on the far side as if the snow itself had said something rude.

*What is it?* Rosalind asked him, in the silent way they'd fallen into, thought shaped toward thought.

*Warmth,* Bracken said. *Where there should be none.*

She felt it then too. Not heat exactly—more like attention. The particular pressure of being watched by something patient. The animals had taught her that feeling years ago; a rabbit under a hawk knows the hawk before it sees the shadow.

"Everyone up," she said quietly. "We're not alone."

Marta's hand went inside her coat. Tam's grin faded into something older and harder, the smuggler surfacing through the man. Pip moved to stand between Rosalind and the treeline without being told, which broke her heart a little, because he was twelve and weighed nothing and would do it anyway.

The snow between the pines did not crunch. That was the worst part. Something crossed thirty yards of fresh powder and made no sound at all, and then High Warden Corvane stepped into the shrine as if he'd been waiting there all along, dusted white, his grey cloak blending with the fell, six Wardens fanning out behind him with the smooth economy of people who had done this a thousand times.

He was not what Rosalind expected. She'd built him in her head as a monster—Elias's stories had given her plenty of material—but the man who stopped ten feet away was tired-looking, silver at the temples, with kind lines around his eyes that had clearly once been used often. He looked at her the way her mother must have looked at her, if she remembered her mother, which she mostly didn't.

"Rosalind Vasquez," he said. Her name sounded different in his mouth. Weighed. Catalogued. "You have your mother's chin. And her stubbornness, I'd wager, though I only knew her a short time."

"You knew my mother?"

"I trained her." He said it simply, no flourish. "I was the one who found her, when she was your age, hiding a gift she'd been taught to be ashamed of. I offered her what I'm about to offer you. She refused. It cost us both dearly." His gaze went to Bracken, held there, unafraid. "That animal should be dead nine times over. Interesting."

"He says hello," Rosalind said.

Something flickered across Corvane's face—not anger. Hunger. "So the gift is strong. Stronger than hers, perhaps." He clasped his hands behind his back, a lecturer's posture, and Rosalind realized with a chill that this was a speech he had rehearsed. Maybe for years. "Let me be plain with you, girl, because you've earned plainness. Everyone else has lied to you. Your village lied about what was coming. The smugglers lie about where the roads go. Even the beasts lie, in their way—they remember a world that no longer exists and call it truth."

"And you don't lie?"

"I don't have the luxury." He nodded at the standing stones, the frozen basin, the buried country around them. "Thornfell is dying. You've seen it. Villages forgetting their own names. Children fading mid-step. The wards are down, the stars are gone, and every day the borders eat a little more of themselves. There is no stopping it. There is no Door that saves everyone—I know what your new friends have been whispering to you, and I'm telling you it's a story old women tell to make the walking bearable."

"The story is true," Rosalind said. "I've seen it. Under Mount Cael. In the bears' remembering."

"I believe you saw something." Corvane's voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "But here is what I know, from forty years of watching this country forget itself. Memory does not vanish. Nothing vanishes. It goes *somewhere*. The Order learned to catch it—to bottle it, store it, keep it safe from the cold. Every name we harvest is a name that survives. Every face we take from a fading mind lives on in our vaults, whole and warm, instead of dissolving into snow." He spread his hands. "I am not destroying Thornfell, child. I am its undertaker, and an undertaker is the most merciful profession there is."

"That's not mercy," Pip said, small and fierce. "That's picking pockets at a funeral."

Corvane looked at the boy, and for one naked moment something ancient and wounded moved behind his eyes. "Yes," he said softly. "It is. I've made peace with that. Have you noticed, boy, that everyone who objects to mercy never offers a better one?"

Then he turned back to Rosalind, and the full weight of his attention settled on her like snowfall.

"Now. My offer, and I'll make it only once. Come with me. Bring your cat, even—he's a marvel, and the Order protects marvels. In exchange for your gift, freely given, you will have training worthy of it. You will learn what your gift actually *is*—not beast-whispering, girl, that's the crude surface of it. You will learn to read the deep memory of the land itself. And you will be warm. Fed. Safe. When Thornfell finishes dying, you will walk out of it carrying more of it than anyone alive, because you'll be the vessel we pour it into." He paused, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "Your mother refused me because she believed the country could be saved. Look around you. Tell me she was right. Tell me what her refusal bought, except twenty years of delay and a daughter raised in shame and secrecy."

The words went in like splinters, because each one was true-shaped. Rosalind had spent her whole life hiding the gift, flinching from it, treating it as a sickness. And here was a man offering to make it a crown.

And underneath the offer, quiet as a fishhook, was the other thing: *surrender your gift.* Give it up. Hand it over. Be poured full of a dead country until there was no room left for Rosalind Vasquez.

"She wasn't wrong," Rosalind said. "She was early."

Corvane sighed, and for a heartbeat he looked genuinely sad. "They always say something clever at this point. Then—"

Bracken hit him.

There was no warning growl, no coiling pause. One instant the cat was at the shrine's edge; the next he was a streak of scarred muscle crossing the snow, and Corvane went down under ninety pounds of fury with a sound like a door breaking. The Wardens shouted. Steel came out. Rosalind was already moving, dragging Pip by the collar, screaming "*North! The pass!*"

The world became noise and white. Marta hurled something from her sleeve—a fistful of ground glass and ash, smuggler's trick, blinding two Wardens who'd circled ahead. Tam grabbed Rosalind's arm and hauled her uphill as a crossbow bolt buried itself in a stone where her head had been. Behind them, impossibly, Bracken was still alive, still fighting, flowing between blades like water between rocks, taking a slash across the shoulder that would have killed anything else and simply using the pain to move faster.

*GO,* he snarled at her, blood bright on the snow. *I am RIGHT BEHIND you. Do not look back.*

She looked back anyway. She saw Corvane rise, cloak torn, throat bleeding shallowly, and she saw that he was not furious. He was watching her run with an expression of terrible patience, the way you watch weather come.

Then the pass swallowed them.

* * *

Marta's road was a goat track in summer and a rumor in winter, switchbacking up between walls of rock that leaned together overhead like conspirators. They climbed it at a dead run, breath tearing, boots punching through crust, and the whole time the mountain groaned above them—a long, tectonic complaint, as if the country were shifting in its sleep and didn't care what rolled off.

"It's unstable," Marta gasped. "The forgetting gets into the stone. Rock that isn't remembered properly doesn't hold together—"

The first collapse came behind them. Not an avalanche—a *dissolution*, a shelf of cliff quietly deciding it had never been solid, dumping itself across the track in a roar of powder and boulders. Dust swallowed the path. Somewhere in the whiteout, voices: Wardens, cursing, cut off by the slide.

"They're buried or they're not," Tam panted. "Either way, keep moving."

They climbed for an hour that felt like a day. The pass narrowed to a slot barely wide enough for one. Frost feathered the rock walls in patterns almost like writing. And Tam, who had been joking steadily since the shrine—joking about the crossbow bolt, joking about Corvane's face when Bracken hit him, joking because that was how Tam carried fear—went suddenly quiet.

"Ros." His voice was strange. Flat. "Don't panic."

She turned. He was leaning against the rock wall, and his left side was dark. During the fight, during the flight, sometime—she hadn't seen it happen, that was the horror of it—a blade or a bolt or a jagged edge of collapsing stone had opened him from hip to ribs. The wound didn't bleed properly. That was the thing she'd remember afterward, in the bad hours: it didn't bleed right. The blood came slow and sluggish, more grey than red, as if the cold were drinking it before it could finish leaving him.

"It's nothing," Tam said, and smiled his smile, and swayed.

Rosalind caught him. Together she and Pip lowered him against the stones, and Marta knelt and pushed back his coat, and for a long moment nobody said anything at all, because Marta had bandaged wounds for sixty years and her face said everything.

"Cold-rot," she said finally. "Wound won't close in air that's forgetting itself. Flesh can't knit when the body can't remember being whole." She was already working—stripping her scarf, packing the gash with the herbs she hoarded like dragon-gold. "I can slow it. I can't stop it. He needs fire, and rest, and a place that remembers itself. There's a bothy—" she closed her eyes, digging through decades, "—a shepherd's bothy, two miles on, past the col. If it's still there. If it still *is*."

"Then we carry him," Rosalind said. "All of us. Now."

"Ros." Tam caught her wrist. His grip was weaker than it should have been, and his eyes were clear and terrified and amused all at once, which was the most Tam thing she had ever seen. "Listen. If I start forgetting things—you'll tell me, yeah? Don't let me walk around thinking I'm someone else. I hate that bloke. Never met him. Sure he's dull."

"You're not going to forget anything," Rosalind said, and her voice cracked on it. "Because I'm going to talk at you so relentlessly you'll beg for the cold to take you. Every job. Every botched deal. The honey job. The time you tried to fence a stolen harp to a blind merchant—"

"*That harp was a fair price—*"

"Move," Marta said, "or argue forever."

They moved. Pip took one arm, Rosalind the other, and Tam walked between them, mostly under his own power, telling them about the harp, insisting on the details, and Rosalind understood suddenly what he was doing: he was spending his own memory as fast as the cold could steal it, pouring himself out loud so there'd be less of him left loose to lose.

Above them, on the ridgeline, crows gathered. Not many. Seven, maybe eight, sitting in a row along the wind-scoured rock, heads tilted, watching them climb with an attention no ordinary bird had ever owned.

Bracken limped beside Rosalind, blood freezing black along his flank, and followed her stare upward.

*Those are not crows anymore,* he said quietly. *Those are what the Order makes when it feeds beasts on harvested memory. They are hungry, and they are his.*

The crows did not attack. They just watched, keeping pace along the ridge, patient as bookkeepers, all the way to the col.

And at the col, where the wind screamed through the notch and the bothy was supposed to be, Rosalind looked back one last time down the pass. Far below, tiny with distance, torches were moving. Wardens, climbing. Not hurrying.

Corvane hadn't sent them to catch her. He'd sent them to keep her moving north, exactly where she was already going, straight toward whatever waited beneath Mount Cael—and now Tam was bleeding grey into the snow, and the sky held no stars, and the only warmth left in the world was the small circle of people holding each other upright against the wind.

"Hold on," she whispered to Tam, to all of them, to herself. "Hold on till morning."

Behind them, soft as snowfall, the first crow left its perch and began to follow.
