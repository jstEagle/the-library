## 10. Tam's Long Sleep

The bothy sat in a fold of the hills like something the country had already half-forgotten—stone walls furred with lichen, a roof of old turf sagging under new snow, a door that hung on one hinge and complained about it. Marta said she'd slept here twice in her life, forty years apart, and that the second time the shepherd who owned it had been dead and buried under the rowan by the wall. She said it the way she said everything now: quickly, before the saying could be taken from her.

We got Tam inside as the light went.

He walked most of the way on his own feet, which I told myself was a good sign. He joked about the wound, too—the gash along his ribs where the collapsing pass had opened him up, a long red seam that should have been knitting shut and wasn't. "I've had worse from a badger," he said, leaning on Pip's shoulder at the door. "Badger held a grudge over some cheese. Long story."

"Tell me later," I said.

"Promise." And he smiled at me, easy as ever, and for about an hour everything seemed like it might hold.

Then the hour ended.

* * *

It started small. He called Pip "the boy" when he meant Pip by name, and Pip's face did something complicated and then smoothed itself flat, because Pip knew better than anyone what it looked like when someone's name stopped fitting them. Then Tam asked, mildly, why we were stopping at a bothy when the Hollow Ford job needed doing, and there hadn't been any Hollow Ford job, not ever, not between us. Marta caught my eye across the fire and shook her head once: don't correct him yet.

By midnight he didn't know me.

He came back to himself sitting up by the fire, blinking at the flames, and his hand went to his ribs and found the bandage and he frowned at it like it belonged to a stranger. Then he looked around the bothy—at Marta dozing against her pack, at Pip curled with Bracken's warmth at his back, at me—and his face went through courtesy, confusion, and a kind of polite alarm, all in the space of a breath.

"You're—" he began, and stopped, because there was nothing to finish the sentence with. "I'm sorry. Have we met?"

The snow outside made no sound. That was the worst part of this whole winter; snow should sound like something. This snow fell silent as ash.

"We've met," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You're Tam. You're the best fence in three valleys and a liar in four languages. You owe me eleven crowns from the Marrowbridge run."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not recognition exactly. More like a door rattling in its frame.

"Marrowbridge," he repeated. "That's..." He shook his head slowly. "There's no Marrowbridge."

"There is," I said. "There was. It's north of the Sedge, two days' walk if the ferry's running. You got us out through the tanner's yard with the guards close enough to count the buttons on their coats, and you were so pleased with yourself you talked the whole way home, and you never paid me back."

"I would have paid you back," he said—and then he heard himself say it, heard the shape of the debt, the shape of the joke inside it, and his face changed. "Wait. Wait, I know that—I know how that goes. That's how it always goes. I never pay anyone back." He laughed, a thin startled sound. "Rosalind?"

"That's right."

"Rosalind Vasquez." He said it like a man testing ice. "Honey smuggler. Terrible at cards. Worse at losing at cards." His hand pressed flat against the bandage and stayed there. "Why does my side feel like it's full of winter?"

* * *

Marta woke and looked at the wound without touching it, which was its own verdict. In the forgetting cold, she said, flesh forgot how to mend. She'd seen it before, years ago, in the first little erasures—a shepherd's frostbitten fingers that never healed, a child's scraped knee that stayed raw for a season. The body kept its own memories too, she said, and when the country's forgetting reached deep enough, even the body's memory thinned.

"So we keep him warm and we keep him talking," she said, "and we hope the road south is faster than whatever's eating him."

"And if it isn't?"

Marta didn't answer. She was good at not answering. It was one of the things I liked least about her and relied on most.

Bracken sat near the door with his scarred face turned toward the night. *The Warden is coming,* he said, in that voice only I could hear, low as rockfall. *Two hours, maybe less. He has been circling since the pass.*

"Coming to finish the job Corvane started?" I asked him aloud, and everyone looked at me, and I realized I'd spoken beast-speech in front of them again, which I still did when I was tired, which was always now.

*No,* Bracken said. *He walks unarmed. He carries something in his pack that smells of iron and bitter leaf. He is afraid.* The cat's ears turned toward the dark. *Wardens are not often afraid. When they are, it means the thing they fear is bigger than their orders.*

I looked at Tam, asleep again by the fire—or not asleep, exactly. Drifting. His lips moved sometimes, shaping words I couldn't catch, and once I leaned close enough to hear him counting, softly, over and over: names, coins, debts. A ledger. He was keeping his own books against the dark, and the numbers wouldn't stay put.

Two hours. I spent them getting ready to refuse a gift.

* * *

Elias knocked.

That was the thing I couldn't get past, afterward—that he knocked. A Warden of Thornfell, six feet of grey wool and steel discipline, standing in the snow with his hands empty and visible, knocking on a bothy door like a peddler.

Pip had the knife out before the second knock. Marta put herself between the door and Tam without seeming to move, the way old women can. Bracken rose, and the growl that came out of him was the sound the mountains make before they slide.

"It's Elias," I said. "I know his tread."

"You know his *tread*?" Marta said.

"He followed us for three days through the high passes. You learn a man's tread or you learn you're dead." I crossed the room and lifted the bar, and I will admit my hands weren't steady, and I will also admit I didn't care who saw it.

He stood in the doorway with snow in his hair and his sword left behind somewhere—he made a point of showing us his belt, bare of blade, which was either honesty or theater and I genuinely could not tell which. Up close he looked worse than he had at the shrine. There was a bruise along his jaw shaped like Corvane's signet ring, and his eyes had the look of a man who has read his own orders twice and understood them properly for the first time.

"I'm not here for the girl," he said. "I mean—for you. I'm not here for you." He winced at himself. "I'm bad at this. I was trained to arrest people, not ask them things."

"Try asking," Marta said, dry as tinder.

He knelt. Actually knelt, in the doorway, snow melting off his knees onto our floor, and unslung his pack, and drew out a flat tin and a bundle of clean linen and a stoppered flask that caught the firelight green.

"Warden medicine," he said. "Cael-water distilled with mountain sage. It doesn't heal anything. What it does is remind the body of itself. The Order uses it on Wardens hurt in the field, because a Warden who forgets his training is worse than useless." He set the tin down on the threshold, between us, like a man laying down cards he wasn't sure he had the right to play. "It works on other kinds of forgetting too. Sometimes. For a while."

"And the price?" I asked.

"No price."

"Everyone has a price. You people taught me that."

He flinched. Good, some uncharitable part of me said. He looked at the fire instead of at me, and when he spoke again his voice was lower.

"When I was a novice, they gave me an assignment. Watch a woman named Vasquez. Report anything unusual." His jaw worked. "I reported everything. Every kindness, every secret, every friend she trusted. I was very thorough. I was very good at my work." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "So no, Rosalind. No price. Consider it the first installment on a debt I've been carrying since before you could walk."

Behind me, from the fire, a voice cracked and thin and utterly itself:

"The novice." Tam had pushed himself up on one elbow, and his face was pale as the snow outside, but his eyes were sharp, and they were looking at Elias with something that wasn't quite hatred and wasn't quite forgiveness. "The quiet one. Always at the edge of the market. Always writing." He laughed, and the laugh cost him. "She used to leave extra honey out for you. Said nobody watched a person that closely unless they were lonely."

Elias closed his eyes. "Yes," he said. "She did."

* * *

We let him in. I want to record, honestly, that it was not a noble decision. It was arithmetic. Tam's color was going grey, the wound had begun to smell faintly wrong, and Marta's silences had gotten longer, which meant her answers had gotten shorter, which meant she thought we were losing. A medicine that reminded the body of itself was worth more than my pride. Pride doesn't keep anyone warm.

But I made him do it my way.

"You sit there," I said, pointing at the far wall, "and you talk while you work. If your hands go anywhere near that harvest-glass, or you say one word about the Order's mercy, Bracken takes your throat out and I spend the rest of my life feeling bad about it. Understood?"

"Understood," Elias said, and there was—gods help me—the ghost of a smile. "You bargain like your mother."

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare use her like that."

He bowed his head. "Fair."

And so while he worked—cleaning the wound with water heated over our fire, packing it with the bitter-smelling sage paste, coaxing Tam to drink the green flask in small sips while Tam made jokes about the taste that got weaker each time—I talked.

Because that was the other condition. Tam fading in and out, surfacing and sinking, and every time he surfaced the world had rearranged itself and he had to find his footing again, and I saw what it cost him, the reaching, the polite blankness, the terrible courtesy of a man asking strangers who he was. So I decided that if the country was going to take his memories, it would have to take them from both of us. I would hold one end of every story, and he could hold the other, and maybe the rope between us would hold.

"The Marrowbridge run," I said, when his eyes fluttered. "Tell me the tanner's yard."

He frowned. "I don't—"

"You got us out through the tanner's yard. Guards behind us. Go on."

"I... hid the honey in the lime pits," he said slowly, wonderingly, like a man reading a letter from himself. "Nobody searches lime pits. Everyone thinks they'll ruin the goods, but honey doesn't care about lime, honey doesn't care about anything, honey is the most stubborn substance in creation—"

"—which is why you smuggle it," I said, "and why you're insufferable about it."

"—which is why I'm insufferable about it," he agreed, and his color came up a shade, and Marta made a small sound that from Marta was equivalent to cheering.

So I fed him the jobs one after another, all night, like spooning broth into a sick man. The winter we ran salt up the frozen Sedge and I fell through the ice and he pulled me out and swore me to secrecy and then told everyone anyway, because Tam couldn't keep a secret, Tam could only keep a *price*. The summer of the false tolls at the stone bridge, when he dressed Pip—no, not Pip, that boy came later—dressed a miller's son in a Warden's cloak and the real Wardens saluted him. The night we got drunk on festival wine and he told me about his sister, the one who'd gone south before the borders closed, the one he wrote letters to that he could never send, and how he kept them anyway, a bundle of unsent letters tied with red string, because—

"Because a letter you don't send is still a letter," Tam murmured, surfacing again, his hand finding the bandage. "Still proof you wrote. Still proof somebody was there to write to." His eyes focused on me, clear and present, and stayed. "Rosie. How long have I been gone?"

"Hours. You're back now."

"For how long?"

I didn't lie to him. "I don't know."

He nodded, slow, and looked at the ceiling beams, and I watched him decide to be brave about it, the way he decided things, quickly and completely and with a joke ready. "Well. If I go wandering off, at least I'll go owing you money. You'll never let me hear the end of it. You'll haunt *me*."

"That's the plan," I said, and my voice broke on the second word, and he pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone did all night.

* * *

Near dawn, the medicine did what Elias promised—it reminded Tam's body of itself. The wound's edge went from wrong to merely angry. His breathing eased. He sank into real sleep, ordinary sleep, the kind with dreams in it, and Pip curled up against his good side and was asleep in a breath, the way children and cats can do.

Elias cleaned his tools. He did it thoroughly, quietly, a man glad to have a task that required nothing of his soul. Then he sat back on his heels by the dead fire and looked at Tam sleeping, and at Pip, and at Marta, and last at me.

"The harvest-glass," I said. "Corvane's wagons. Bottled memories. I saw them at the shrine."

"Yes."

"What happens to a village after the Order bottles it? Does it get to keep anything?"

He was quiet a long time. Snow ticked softly against the window—no, it didn't even tick. It fell past the glass without a sound, and somehow that silence was louder than any question I could have asked.

"They keep the shapes of things," he said at last. "The Order calls it preservation. A preserved village keeps its roads and its roofs and its routines. It just..." He searched for the word and hated every word he found. "It stops meaning them. I watched a town greet its own returning sons with perfect politeness and no joy whatsoever. Everything in its place. Nothing in its heart." His hands closed into fists on his knees. "Corvane believes that's mercy. That a country kept like a specimen is better than a country lost. I believed it too. I was *trained* to believe it, and training goes deeper than doubt."

"And now?"

"Now I watched you talk a dying man back into the world all night with nothing but stories." He looked up, and the dawn-grey light through the window caught his face, and he looked younger than a Warden should, and more tired than a young man should. "No bottle does that. No ward, no rite, no Order in all its history. Just—you. Telling him who he was until it became true again."

Outside, far off across the white hills, a crow called. Once. Twice. Then a third call that was not quite a crow's call at all—too long, too human, almost like a word.

Bracken's head came up off his paws. Every line of him went taut as wire.

*They've found us,* the cat said. *Not the crows. Something older, walking behind them. Something that knows this house.* His eyes found mine, and for the first time since I'd known him, the mountain cat sounded uncertain. *Girl. The one coming up the valley remembers your mother's voice. And it is singing her song—but it is singing it backwards.*
