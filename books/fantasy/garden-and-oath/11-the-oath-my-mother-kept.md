## 11. The Oath My Mother Kept

The bothy smelled of wet wool and old smoke, and Elias sat in the corner with his hands empty on his knees, waiting for the girl to decide whether to kill him.

She hadn't yet. That was something. She'd taken the Warden medicine from his pack—feverbark and ghostmoss, the good stuff, harvested from the Order's own gardens—and fed it to Tam in slow spoonfuls while the smuggler shivered under every blanket they owned. Now Tam slept a real sleep instead of a fading one, his breath coming even, and Rosalind Vasquez stood with her back against the door as if she could hold the whole night out by leaning on it.

"You'll sleep by the fire," she said. "Where I can see you."

"I don't sleep much," Elias said.

"Then you can watch me watching you."

He almost smiled. He didn't. There was something about her face when she was being fierce—the set of her jaw, the way her dark hair fell across one eye—that snagged on something deep in him, like a fishhook catching on sunken timber. He'd felt it before, in fragments. A woman's laugh in a stone corridor. Hands folding paper cranes from Warden dispatches because she was bored and brilliant and couldn't sit still during briefings. *Liria,* someone had called her. Liria Vasquez.

The name came with no face attached. Just the laugh, and the ache that followed it everywhere, like a dog that had lost its master and followed him anyway.

* * *

He dreamed, or half-dreamed, dozing by the fire with one eye open as instructed. The fragments came the way they always did now—not as pictures but as weight. The Order's training hall at Vael Corrin, all white stone and cold light. The oath spoken at fourteen with his hand on the Ledger of Names: *I will hold what others cannot hold. I will remember what others are made to forget.* He'd believed it then. He believed it still, mostly, which was the problem—because he was beginning to understand that the Order had stopped believing it first.

And beneath the oath, older than it, warmer: a memory of a garden.

Not a real garden. A courtyard in the Warden citadel where nothing grew, and a young novice—himself, younger, all elbows and earnestness—carrying water up four flights of stairs because a prisoner had asked him to. Not a prisoner, exactly. A guest who was not permitted to leave. A woman with ink-stained fingers who had been caught speaking to wolves at the border and brought in for study, and who had looked at the boy assigned to watch her and said, "You have kind eyes. They'll grind those out of you eventually. Let's grow something before they do."

So they grew things. Beans in a cracked basin. Mint in an old helmet. She taught him the names of weeds that were not weeds, and she sang while she worked—a low looping song with words in no language the Order recognized, and when the senior Wardens passed the courtyard they frowned, and when they were gone she sang louder.

"Liria," Elias said aloud, into the dark of the bothy, and Rosalind's head snapped around.

"What did you say?"

"Nothing." But the name had opened something, the way a key opens a lock whether you want it opened or not. More came through. Her hands pressing a sealed letter into his sixteen-year-old palms. *If anything happens to me, find my daughter. She'll be small. She'll be angry. She'll pretend she doesn't need anyone—that's how you'll know her.* And then the morning she was gone, and the courtyard plants pulled up and burned by order of High Warden Corvane himself, and Elias standing in the ash holding a watering can, fourteen years old and understanding for the first time that the Order did not keep memories. It caged them.

* * *

He woke before dawn with tears frozen on his cheeks and did not wipe them away, because Rosalind was awake too, sitting cross-legged by the fire, watching him with an expression he couldn't read.

"You knew her," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Four years." His voice came out rough. "I was assigned as your mother's watcher when I was thirteen. Youngest novice they'd ever given a detail. I think they thought it would be good practice—watching someone gentle. Someone who wasn't dangerous." He laughed, and it hurt. "She was the most dangerous person I ever met. Not because of the gift. Because she was kind in a place designed to cure people of it."

Rosalind's hands were fists in her lap. "They told the village she died in custody."

"She did die in custody." Elias looked into the fire. "But not the way they said. Corvane came for her the last winter. He wanted her gift for the harvest—you've seen the bottles, so you know what that means. She refused. And then she did something none of us understood until much later. She asked to be taken to Mount Cael. She told them she would seal the Door herself, willingly, if they let her go alone to do it."

"And they believed her?"

"They believed she had no choice." He shook his head slowly. "I went with her as far as the lower galleries. She made me swear to stay there. The last thing she said to me—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "The last thing she said was, *Tell my daughter I'm sorry I made her wait. Tell her the waiting is the work.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow breathing.

"That's it?" Rosalind's voice was very quiet. "That's all she left?"

"No." Elias leaned forward. This was the part he'd carried blind for fifteen years, a shape without meaning, and only now—in this bothy, with this girl, with Bracken's green eyes glinting from the shadows near the door—did it assemble itself into sense. "For years I thought she sealed the Door to imprison the hunger. That's what the Order teaches. Containment. The Vasquez line as jailers. But I heard her sing that sealing song a hundred times in that courtyard, and it was never a cage-song, Rosalind. It was a lullaby. You don't lullaby a prisoner. You lullaby a child."

Bracken rose from the shadows and came to sit between them, tail wrapped over his scarred paws. "Tell her the rest," the cat said. "You've earned the telling."

"The Door was never meant to be shut forever," Elias said. "She told me once—I thought it was just her talking, she talked constantly, she filled silence like other people fill graves—but she said the hunger beneath Cael isn't a monster. It's a wound. It's everything Thornfell ever lost, pooled and starving, and you can't kill a wound. You can only heal it or feed it. Sealing it bought time. Time for what, she wouldn't say. She'd smile and touch her own belly and say, *Time for the right ears.*"

Rosalind had gone very still. "She was pregnant."

"With you." Elias nodded. "The strongest beast-speaker born in Thornfell in three hundred years. Maybe ever. Your gift isn't a shame, girl. It's a key that took a generation to forge. Your mother didn't seal the Door to trap the hunger. She sealed it to buy time for *you*—for someone whose gift could do what hers couldn't. Not cage the hunger. Not starve it. Answer it."

Rosalind's breath came out shaky. "Answer it how?"

"I don't know. She never finished teaching me. But I know she believed it was possible, and I know she spent her last year singing to something under a mountain so it wouldn't wake hungry before her child was ready." He met her eyes. "That's the oath she kept. Not the Order's oath. Hers. And I have kept it badly, because I forgot it, because the Order took it out of me along with everything else. But I'm telling you now, and I'm telling you true: I was sworn to your mother before I was ever sworn to Corvane. And I choose her oath."

* * *

They sat with that a long time. Outside, the snow fell straight down, unhurried, the snow that did not melt. Tam murmured in his sleep—not fading words now, but dream words, ordinary ones, a man dreaming of roads.

It was Pip who broke the silence, creeping in from the loft where he'd clearly been listening to every word. "If the Door needs answering," the boy said, "and she's the answer—then Corvane wants her to open it? Or wants her not to?"

Elias felt the cold settle into him, the old institutional cold he knew too well. "Corvane doesn't care about the Door. He cares about the harvest. If the girl opens the Door and the hunger floods out, the country dies fast and clean, and the Order collects every loose memory before it scatters. If she reseals it, the slow dying continues, and the Order harvests at its leisure. Either way, Corvane wins. Either way, Thornfell is inventory." He stood, joints cracking, fifteen years suddenly heavy on his back. "Which means we have to be faster than either option. We have to reach the Door and do the thing your mother actually intended. The third thing. The one she died believing you could do."

"And you're going to help us get there." Rosalind's voice was flat, testing.

"I'm going to carry you if I have to."

"Why?" The word cracked out of her harder than she probably meant. "You woke up with no past. Why would you spend it on me?"

Elias looked at her—at the jaw set exactly like her mother's, at the anger worn exactly like her mother's armor—and the ache that had followed him since Vael Corrin finally lay down at his feet like a dog come home.

"Because I watched her once," he said. "From the wrong side of a duty. I watered her beans and I wrote my reports and I let them take her, and I have been a man missing a piece ever since. Your mother forgave me. I know she did, because forgiveness was what she did instead of breathing. But I never forgave myself, and I'm done pretending that's humility." He picked up his pack. "This isn't duty, Rosalind. Duty is what Corvane sells. This is the other thing. The thing she grew in a cracked basin in a stone courtyard. Call it what it is."

Rosalind held his gaze a long moment. Then, gruffly, in a voice that reminded him painfully of the woman in the courtyard: "You sleep by the fire again tonight. Where I can see you."

"Yes," said Elias, "Warden's honor," and for the first time since waking on a frozen road with no name but his oath, the oath and the man agreed with each other.

Bracken purred, low, like distant thunder deciding to be kind. "Rest," the cat said. "Tomorrow we climb. And Elias—" the green eyes caught the firelight, "—she sang the last verse of the sealing song to me once, the part she never sang in the courtyard. When we reach the Door, you'll hear it. And you'll understand why Corvane is afraid."

Outside, far off across the sleeping valley, a crow called once—a wrong-sounding call, hollow, like a bell with the voice scooped out of it—and did not call again.
