## 12. Crows on the Col

The col above the Shepherd's Teeth was the kind of place the wind used as a whetstone. It came off the high snowfields in long gray blades, honing itself against the rocks, and by the time it reached us it had learned to cut. We climbed into it single file, hoods up, heads down, Tam's arm slung across my shoulders because his wound had gone bad in the night—not festering in any way Marta could name, just *fading*, the flesh around it going pale and quiet, like a thing being forgotten from the outside in.

"Almost there," Marta called back. Her voice was thin against the wind but it carried anyway; old women who have spent their lives shouting over storms don't lose the habit. "There's a shelter cairn past the saddle. Stone walls, old shepherd work. We make that and we rest."

Pip walked ahead of me, small and stubborn, refusing help on the scree because he was nine and nine-year-olds would rather fall than be carried. Bracken flowed along the slope above us like a shadow that had decided to have substance, moving through rock fields no cat should have crossed so easily.

I felt the wrongness before I heard anything.

It came through the gift—the one I'd spent my whole life hiding and now couldn't seem to put down, the way you can't put down a limb that's grown onto you. The beasts of Thornfell had always been frightened since the stars went out, but this was different. This was fear with a hole in the middle of it. Fear shaped like an absence.

I stopped. "Rosalind?" Tam said.

"Get down," I said. "All of you. Get behind the rocks."

They were fast for smugglers—faster than I'd ever seen them, because months of the forgetting had taught everyone in Thornfell to move when someone with knowledge said move. Tam dropped, dragging Pip with him. Marta pressed herself against the boulder. And I stood there like a fool with my head tipped back, listening to the sky.

They came off the ridgeline in silence.

That was the first horror of them. Crows should be loud. Crows are the gossips of the air, the hecklers, the ones who tell every hawk within ten miles exactly where you are and what you're doing wrong. These came down the wind without a sound at all, wings beating, beaks open—and nothing. No calls. No cries. Just forty black shapes falling out of the white sky like torn pieces of it.

"No," I breathed, because I could hear them now, and what I heard was worse than silence.

*Cold. Cold cold cold where the warm was. Something took the warm. Something reached in and took the warm and left the shape. Fly. Find. Take what they carry. Take the remembering. Then maybe the warm comes back. Maybe if we fill ourselves enough the warm comes back.*

"They're not hunting us," I said aloud, stupidly, uselessly. "They're starving."

The first one hit Marta's pack.

She screamed—not in pain, though its talons went through wool and canvas and skin in one motion—but in fury, because the crow wasn't after her flesh. It had its beak buried in the leather flap of her satchel, in the maps, and when it wrenched its head back it was dragging something out of the bag that had no weight and no color, some thread of her, and I watched old Marta's eyes go briefly, terribly blank, the way a room goes blank when someone takes the picture off the wall.

Then Tam was up with his knife and Pip was screaming and throwing stones, and Bracken came off the rocks like a thrown spear, and the col turned into a black storm with knives in it.

I did the only thing I knew how to do. I opened myself all the way and shouted into the flock with everything I had:

*I know what you lost!*

The effect was instant and awful. Every crow within reach of my voice faltered mid-air. Three of them dropped like stones before remembering their wings. Because no one had spoken to them—no one had spoken *to* them—in whatever span of time had passed since something hollowed them out and pointed them at the living world like arrows.

*Who says that,* one of them thought at me, close, too close, hovering a wingspan from my face with its beak open and its eyes wrong—eyes like wells with the water taken out. *Who says knowing. Knowing is gone. Warm is gone. Only taking is left.*

"What's your name?" I asked it.

That stopped it harder than Bracken's claws had stopped its fellows. A crow's name is a real thing among their kind—I'd learned that much from sparrows and magpies over the years, the way you learn things you never meant to learn, secretly, shamefully, under blankets at night. Crows name themselves. They trade names like coins. And this creature hung in the freezing air turning the question over like a beggar turning a coin it couldn't remember earning.

*I had,* it thought slowly. *I had a—a word. A word that was me. There was a fence post. There was a field with—there was a field.* Its thoughts frayed at the edges even as it reached for them. *The tall one in the dark coat gave us the hunger instead. He said it was mercy. He said we would keep everything forever if we ate it.*

Corvane. My hands curled into fists inside my gloves. He hadn't just built himself an army. He'd built it out of ordinary birds, the scavengers and squabblers of a hundred farmyards, and filled the space where they lived with appetite.

"You can't eat your way back to warm," I told it. "You know that. You've eaten and eaten and you're still cold."

*We are still cold,* it agreed, and there was such a bottomless tiredness in the thought that my throat closed. *But stopping is dying. Taking is the only flying left.*

"Not the only—" I started, and then a talon raked my hood half off my head, and the moment broke.

Because the rest of the flock hadn't been listening. The rest of the flock was hungry, and hungry things don't pause for conversations.

What happened next happened fast, the way the worst moments do, all of it arriving at once. Two crows got inside Marta's guard and she went down under them, swinging her walking stick, protecting the map case with her body because to Marta the map *was* Thornfell, every road and grave and name, and she would die before she let it be drunk dry. Tam fought with his knife in short ugly efficient strokes, keeping three birds off Pip, and he was graying around the mouth from the wound and still he kept between the boy and the black wings. Bracken was a demon in the rocks, and I heard—for the first time ever—him making noise while he fought, a snarling yowl that sounded almost like language.

And one crow got past all of them.

I didn't see it coming. I was standing in the open with my arms half-raised, trying to talk to a sky full of starving things, and the hollowed one came down at my face with its beak aimed not at my eyes but at the soft place behind them—at memory, at the self, at the part of Rosalind Vasquez that was Rosalind Vasquez.

Something small and furious hit it sideways.

Pip. Ninety pounds of nine-year-old in an oversized coat, tackling a crow out of the air, both of them rolling across the frozen gravel with the boy's fists full of black feathers and his voice cracking with terror and rage: *"Not hers! You can't have hers, she knows my name, SHE KNOWS MY NAME!"*

The crow tore free, leaving feathers and a stripe of blood on Pip's cheek, and rose shrieking—that was the second horror, that they could shriek now, that the sound finally worked its way out of them, raw and rusty as an unoiled hinge—and the whole flock wheeled toward the sound of it.

Toward Pip. Toward the smallest, brightest, most freshly-remembering mind on that col.

Bracken hit the lead bird out of the air. Tam grabbed Pip by the collar. Marta was on her knees with blood running down her wrist, clutching the satchel. And I understood, in one cold flash, that talking wasn't going to be enough, that these creatures had been broken too far down to be argued with—

Except the one hanging in front of me. The one whose name I'd asked.

It was looking at Pip. At the boy who had thrown himself bodily between a monster and a stranger because the stranger knew his name. And its hollow well-eyes had something in them. Not warmth. Not yet. But the *shape* of warmth, the outline of it, the way an empty house still holds the shape of the furniture that was carried out.

*He remembered for her,* it thought. *Out loud. In front of everyone.*

"Yes," I said gently, carefully, the way you speak to a wild thing you're afraid will bolt. "That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. Memory isn't a thing you take. It's a thing you give."

*I am so cold.*

"I know. Come here. Both of you—all of you who can hear me—come down, and I'll give you something better than stolen warmth. I'll say your names back to you until you remember them yourselves."

For a heartbeat the whole col held still, the wind included, or so it seemed. The crow hovered. Below it, the flock milled in confusion, drawn two ways—toward the hunger Corvane had planted in them, and toward the voice that was the first voice in their ruined lives to offer rather than demand.

Then the one I'd spoken to folded its wings and landed on the rock in front of me, deliberately, heavily, like a bird setting down a burden it had carried too far.

*Try,* it thought.

So I tried. I closed my eyes and reached for it the way I'd reached for sparrows all my secret life, deeper than words, into the place where a crow keeps its sense of itself—and found the wreckage, and started picking through it. Fragments. A fence post whitened by weather. The smell of a dung-fire and barley. A woman's voice calling a word across a field—a word, a *name*, worn nearly smooth: *Kess? Kess?*

*Kess,* I said to it, out loud, in the old way, the way beast-speakers must once have named every creature in Thornfell before the gift became a shame and a secret. "Your name is Kess. You had a field with a fence post, and a woman who called you home at dusk, and you liked—"

I searched. Found it. A bright hard little knot of preference that the hunger hadn't managed to dissolve.

"—you liked the shine of nails. Any shiny nail. You stole them off the fence all summer and hid them in the wall."

The crow made a sound. Not a shriek. Something smaller and more broken than that, and its well-eyes overflowed—not with tears, crows don't cry, but with something else, some pressure releasing, and around its hollow center the fragments began to knit. Not whole. Nowhere near whole. But *held*. Held together by being said aloud, witnessed, given back.

Two more crows landed. One was too far gone—it looked at me with nothing behind its eyes at all, and my heart cracked for it, and I said its fragments anyway, the few I could find, a ditch and a dead mouse and a summer, and whether it helped I couldn't say. But the third one—oh, the third one. It came down ragged and shaking and I found a riverbank in it, and a stolen egg, and a flockmate whose name had been a chattering sound like pebbles in a tin cup, and when I finished speaking it preened once, twice, straightened, and looked at me with eyes that had water in the wells again.

*Ash,* it offered. *My name was Ash. I kept it hidden even from myself. Under everything. He couldn't dig that deep.*

"He couldn't," I agreed, and my voice shook. "Some things go too deep to dig out."

The rest of the flock circled overhead, shrieking, hungering, leaderless now that Kess had landed. I felt them wavering—felt the hunger in them fighting the memory of what they'd been, and losing, because hunger is a poor substitute for a name and somewhere under all of them the same buried selves were waiting.

Kess lifted its head and made a sound I can only describe as a command, rough and croaking and utterly crow: a single harsh note that meant, in whatever grammar birds use, *enough*.

The flock scattered. Not all at once—they went in ragged spirals, reluctant, some of them diving once more at our packs before thought overrode hunger—but they went, streaming east over the col in straggling lines until the sky was empty except for weather.

We stood in the aftermath. Blood on the gravel—Tam's, Marta's, mine, a little of Pip's. Black feathers everywhere, drifting. Bracken sat washing a scratch on his foreleg with intense dignity, as though he had planned the entire battle.

Marta got up slowly, tested her wrist, and looked at the two crows standing on the rock beside me with an expression I'll remember the rest of my life.

"Well," she said. "In sixty years of mapping this country, I never once put a talking crow on a map."

"It talks to me," I said. "Mostly it complains."

*Accurate,* Kess thought, and Ash made the pebbles-in-a-tin-cup sound that I realized, delighted, was laughter.

Tam slid down against the boulder, gray-faced, holding his side. The wound had spread in the fight—another finger-width of pale, quiet flesh, as if the exertion had fed the fading. Pip knelt next to him, still bleeding from his cheek, still gripping a fistful of black feathers he wouldn't drop.

"We can't stay on the col," Marta said. "Weather's turning. And those crows will find Corvane and tell him everything—where we are, which road we took." She paused, and looked at Kess and Ash again, and revised. "Or they won't. If they've truly come free."

*We will tell him nothing,* Ash thought at me, fierce as a struck flint. *Let him send his hunger after us. We know now what he takes. We will show other flocks. We will teach them to hide their names deep.*

I looked north, past the saddle, toward Mount Cael, a white shoulder of darkness under the last of the gray light. Somewhere beneath it, the Door. Somewhere beneath it, whatever my mother had died buying time for.

"Then fly ahead of us," I said to the crows. "Watch the passes. Tell us if his Wardens move." I hesitated, then added, because it seemed important: "And when you find other flocks—when you find the ones too far gone to save—say their fragments to them anyway. Even if they can't hear. Someone should."

Kess bobbed its head, a jerky, formal, ancient-seeming gesture, and then the two of them lifted off the rock and climbed the wind in spirals, east and then north, black flecks against the gray, until the snow-haze took them.

We made the shelter cairn by full dark. Tam slept by the fire and woke twice not knowing my name, and both times I said it back to him, and both times it held. Pip sat pressed against my side, feather still clutched in his fist, and just before sleep he asked me, drowsily, why the crow had saved him.

"It didn't save you," I said. "You saved me. It just noticed."

He smiled and was asleep before the smile finished.

I stayed awake longest, watching the fire, feeling the gift sit in my chest like a coal that refused to cool. Compassion, I thought. Marta's maps, Pip's name, Kess's buried self. All day long the weapons had been stories, and stories had beaten claws.

Near dawn, Ash returned alone, landing on the cairn wall with wind-ruffled feathers and urgency pouring off it like heat off a stone.

*The tall one,* it thought at me. *The tall one in the dark coat. He is coming. Not behind us—*ahead*. He knows about the shrine pass. He is already at the mountain's knee, and he has bottles with him, Rosalind-of-the-beasts. Rows and rows of little glass bottles, and they are all whispering.*

I stood up so fast the fire scattered sparks.

Bottles. Bottles that whispered.

Harvested memories. Corvane wasn't chasing us anymore.

He was waiting at the Door.
