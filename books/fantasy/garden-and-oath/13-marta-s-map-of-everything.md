## 13. Marta's Map of Everything

Marta had been dying for three days, and she was annoyed about it.

Not the dying itself. She was seventy-one years old, her knees had been filing complaints since the war years, and a body was a thing you eventually paid off. What annoyed her was the *manner* of it. The forgetting had gotten into her the way damp gets into a stone wall—slowly, then all at once. First the name of the river south of Hallowmere. Then the smell of her mother's bread. Then, this morning, she had looked at Tam across the bothy fire and thought, *who is that large kind man and why does he keep calling me by my own name like he's checking.*

She knew her own name. That was the last coin in her purse, and she meant to spend it well.

So when Rosalind Vasquez came to sit beside her pallet with that careful face young people wore around the dying—the face that said *I am here but I am also terrified*—Marta said, "Fetch me my satchel. And ink. And don't look at me like I'm already in the ground, girl, I've buried better men than you and they complained less."

Rosalind went. Good girl. Obedient under protest, which was the only obedience worth having.

* * *

The satchel came heavy into Marta's lap, and her hands knew it even if her eyes were starting to argue with her. Forty years a mapmaker, and not the kind the Order licensed, with their tidy borders and their roads drawn where roads ought to be. Marta drew roads where roads *were*. There was a difference, and the difference had kept her one step ahead of Warden patrols through most of two decades.

"Open it to the back," she said. "The blank leaves."

Rosalind did. Her breath caught a little, which pleased Marta. A mapmaker who couldn't make people gasp wasn't doing it right.

The pages weren't blank anymore. They hadn't been blank for forty years, not really—not since the night Marta had sat down at her kitchen table in Hallowmere and understood, with the cold clarity that comes before grief, that the country was going to start eating itself, and that somebody had better write down what it tasted like.

Every road she'd ever walked. Every ford, every sheep-track, every smuggler's cut through the fells. Every grave—she'd marked those in brown ink, little crosses with names beside them, because a country is mostly its dead and anyone who tells you different is selling something. Every name. Villages, farms, standing stones, the pool below Cael's north face where the trout ran fat in June, the widow Anthe's croft with its blue door, the shrine at Marrow Ford where you left a button and took a wish.

Inked from memory. All of it. Because paper could be burned, but a memory had to be *taken*, and Marta had spent her whole life making herself too stubborn to rob.

"This," Marta said, laying her palm flat on the page, "is Thornfell."

"It's beautiful," Rosalind said quietly.

"It's *accurate*. Beauty is what happens when you don't cheat." Marta looked up at her. The girl's face swam a little at the edges now; the forgetting had reached Marta's eyes too, blurring the present while the past stayed sharp as a knife point. That was the cruelty of it. She could draw the ford at Marrow from memory, every stone of it, and she could not reliably remember whether she'd eaten breakfast.

Which she had. Tam had made her eat it. Large kind man. She was almost sure of him now.

"I'm going to tell you something," Marta said, "and then I'm going to give you an order, and you're going to hate the order, and you're going to do it anyway."

"All right," Rosalind said.

"The Order thinks this country is dying. Corvane thinks it's a larder—he wants to bottle everything before it spoils. But he's wrong about what's happening, girl. I've watched it longer than he has." Marta tapped the page. "A country doesn't die. A country *sleeps*. It pulls its roads in like a hedgehog pulling in its spines. It folds its names away so nothing can steal them. Everything I've put on these pages—it isn't gone out there. It's gone *inward*. Down. Toward whatever's under Mount Cael that your mother knew about and the rest of us were too busy living to ask after."

"You think the land is hiding," Rosalind said slowly.

"I think the land is *keeping*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Marta's hand shook, and she hated that, and she kept talking anyway. "And when someone walks out of it someday—and someone will, because I've mapped every door and crack and seam in these borders, and there's always a way out for the stubborn—they'll need to know what we were. Not what the Order harvested. What we *were*. The blue door on Anthe's croft. The buttons at Marrow Ford. The graves. Especially the graves."

Rosalind's eyes were wet. Young people cried so easily. Marta envied them.

"So here's your order." She pushed the satchel into the girl's arms. "You carry this. You don't let Tam carry it, he'll lose it in a card game. You don't let Pip touch it, he'll improve it. You carry it, and when you get wherever you're going—through whatever Door is waiting under that mountain—you speak it. Out loud. All of it. Names are seeds, Rosalind Vasquez. They don't grow in drawers."

"And you?" Rosalind asked, though Marta could see she already knew.

"I'm staying with my map," Marta said. "All of it. Every road I ever walked, I walked twice—once with my feet and once with my memory. I'm nearly done with the second walking." She smiled, and felt how thin the smile was, and didn't care. "But I'm not quite done. Which brings me to the part you'll hate."

* * *

They moved her to the window seat that afternoon, because she asked for it and because Tam had learned long ago that arguing with Marta cost more energy than lifting her. Through the bothy's small square glass: the fells, white to their shoulders now, and the dark seam of the pass beyond, and above it all a sky with no stars in it, just a smooth grey nothing like the inside of a closed lid.

Pip sat on the floor at her feet, cross-legged, watching her with the fierce attention of a boy who had been forgotten once and intended to be the one who did the remembering from now on. Tam stood by the hearth with his arms folded, wounded shoulder bound, saying nothing, which for Tam was a great deal of saying.

Bracken lay across the doorway like a fallen rafter, amber eyes open, watching. The cat had barely spoken since they'd come down off the col. Cats, Marta thought, understood death better than priests did, and wasted fewer words on it.

"Now," Marta said. "Here's the hateful part."

"Marta—" Tam started.

"Hold your tongue, you great soft smuggler, or I'll have you written out of my map." She waited until the corner of his mouth twitched. Good. He remembered her well enough to be threatened by her. That was worth something. "I want the girl to read it back to me. The whole map. Every name, every grave, every road, beginning at Hallowmere where I was born and ending at the Last Door where I won't ever go. While she speaks, I'll walk it. One last time, with my feet on every mile of it. And when she's finished—" her voice caught, just once, and she pressed on, "—I'll be at the end of it, whole. Every piece of me accounted for. No gaps for the forgetting to live in."

"That's not how it works," Rosalind said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Speaking it won't stop the—"

"No," Marta agreed. "It won't stop anything. Nothing stops. That's the first lesson of being old and the last lesson of being alive. But there's a difference between being erased and being finished, girl, and I mean to be *finished*. Erased is when nobody knows what you were. Finished is when everybody does." She fixed Rosalind with her best stare, the one that had made licensed cartographers apologize. "So. Begin at Hallowmere. My father's house had a green door. Put that in first. If you skip the green door I will haunt you specifically and none of the rest of them."

Rosalind laughed—a wet, broken sound—and opened the satchel.

* * *

She began at Hallowmere, and Marta walked.

"Your father's house, green door, at the foot of Mill Hill, with the rowan tree over the gate."

There it was. The green paint, peeling at the bottom where the dogs had scratched. Marta was nine years old, barefoot in June, and the rowan was in berry.

"Mill Hill road runs east to the ford at Marrow, past the hanging field where they used to dry flax."

Flax smell, green and bitter. Her mother's hands working the stalks. The ford's brown water sliding over her ankles, cold enough to make her gasp and laugh.

"At Marrow Ford, the shrine. Buttons left on the sill, wishes taken. Widow Anthe's croft beyond, blue door, east-facing."

Blue. Not green, not grey—blue, the particular blue of a door painted with whatever pigment a stubborn woman could trade three hens for. Marta had sat on Anthe's step at nineteen with her heart broken over a boy whose name was already fading, thank the stars, and Anthe had said, *girl, hearts mend crooked but they mend, and crooked ones hold more.* Marta had put that on no map and never needed to.

Rosalind's voice went on. The low road to Thornfell. The shepherd's bothies, each with its name—Crag End, Lamb Fold, the one everyone called Sorrow Bothy because of the winter of the red snow. The graves came one by one, and Marta stopped at each one and stood a moment. Old Joss, who'd taught her to read stars. The miller's twins, taken in the fever year, two small crosses side by side because even in death they wouldn't be parted. Her husband, Aldous, under the rowan at Hallowmere because he'd loved that tree and she'd loved him, and love is just a map you keep drawing after the territory's gone.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Tam sat down on the floor next to Pip, and Pip leaned against him, and neither of them pretended they weren't crying, and Marta loved them both so much her chest hurt, and she kept walking.

Because that was the secret, the one she'd never told a soul: the map wasn't ink. It never had been. The ink was just the trail the walking left behind. Forty years of roads, and every single one of them was still under her boots, right now, in this room, in this failing body, in this voice that was getting quieter and quieter inside her head and louder and louder outside it, in the girl's mouth, in the air of the bothy, in the ears of a smuggler and a boy and a cat and—

"—and at the high col, the cairn where travelers add a stone for luck, and beyond it the pass down to the Last Door, and the Door itself, which Marta saw once, from above, at dawn, and swore never to mark precisely on any copy but this one."

Marta opened her eyes. When had she closed them?

Rosalind was looking at her, tears running freely down her face, the open book balanced on her knees. Behind her, Bracken had risen and come close, and the great cat's head was bowed, and Marta would have sworn on every grave in her map that the animal was grieving with perfect dignity.

"Well?" Marta said. Her voice came out small. She didn't mind. Small voices carried far in quiet rooms. "Did I miss anything?"

"No," Rosalind whispered. "It's all there. Every road. Every grave. Every name."

"Then I'm finished," Marta said, and found that she was smiling, and that the smile wasn't thin at all. It was the widest thing in the room. "Not erased. *Finished.* Do you see the difference now?"

"I see it," Rosalind said.

"Good. Then listen, because I'm only saying this once and I'm spending my last coin on it." Marta reached up—her arm was light now, lighter than it had been in fifty years—and touched the girl's wet cheek. "Your mother sealed something to buy time. I mapped the time she bought. Every road, every grave, every name—that's what time looks like when it's been *lived in*, girl. That's the cargo. Don't let Corvane bottle it. Don't let the hunger eat it. Carry it out and plant it somewhere with good soil and bad weather, which is the only soil anything worth having grows in."

"Marta—"

"And one more thing." Her eyes were going, the room going soft and gold at the edges, but the past had never been clearer, and the past was full of roads, and all of them led here, to this moment, to this girl. "The green door. My father's house. When you speak the map out loud someday, wherever you end up—start with the green door. Promise me. Countries begin at doors."

"I promise," Rosalind said. "Green door first."

"There's my girl." Marta settled back against the window frame. Outside, the starless sky was turning the color of pewter, and she thought: *dawn soon, probably, somewhere, for somebody.* "Now. Everyone stay where you are. I've one more road to walk and I mean to walk it slow, and I'll not have you lot tramping about in my head kicking the gravestones."

Tam made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. Pip took her hand. Bracken lay down across her feet, warm as a hearthstone, heavier than any grief, exactly right.

Marta closed her eyes and started at Hallowmere, at a green door in June, and walked.

* * *

Rosalind spoke until her voice was raw, and then she spoke anyway, and Marta walked every mile twice, once with her feet and once with her memory, and at the end of the last road there was a seam of starlight in living rock, and Marta stepped through it whole, every piece accounted for, no gaps for anything to live in.

When the bothy fell silent, the snow outside had stopped falling for the first time in weeks.

And far to the north, beneath Mount Cael, something that had been sleeping with its names folded carefully away heard, faint and far, the sound of a green door being spoken aloud—and turned toward it, listening.
