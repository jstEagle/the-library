## 14. Beneath Mount Cael

The mountain had a mouth, and Marta's map knew the way in.

We found it at dusk on the third day past the col, a seam of black in the mountainside where the snow refused to settle—snowflakes drifting over the entrance and vanishing, as if the mountain were swallowing weather itself. Old dwarven work framed it: pillars of grey stone carved with rows of names, worn shallow by centuries, but not dead. When I pressed my palm against one, it glowed. Faintly. A coal under ash.

*Halla daughter of Vund*, said the stone, in a voice I felt through my skin rather than heard. *She kept the lamps.*

"They're still lit," Pip whispered beside me. He had his hand on another pillar, eyes wide. "Rosalind, they're still *lit*. Somebody's been paying attention to them for a thousand years."

"Or the stones remember being paid attention to," Tam said from behind us. He was walking better now—the Warden medicine had done its work—but he kept his hand near his knife out of habit, and his color was still wrong, a kind of grey that the forgetting cold had left in him like frost in a wall. "Either way, I don't love standing in a doorway that eats snow."

Bracken flowed past all of us and into the dark without hesitation.

"The Door is below," the mountain cat said. His voice in my head was gravel and patience. "Two days' walk, if the roads hold. If they don't—" his ears flicked back toward the pass we'd come through, where Corvane's crows had scattered but not, I thought, gone far "—then less."

Elias looked at the pillars a long moment before following. He'd been quiet since the shrine, quiet since he'd turned his badge-face away from Corvane's harvest wagons, and I kept waiting for him to change his mind the way you wait for a storm to turn. He hadn't yet. But storms are patient too.

I touched Halla's name once more—*thank you for keeping the lamps*—and went in after my mother's cat.

* * *

The dwarves had built their roads to last, and last they had, mostly. Wide galleries cut through living rock, ceilings high enough for the mule—we'd brought the mule, because the mule had opinions and also carried our food—and lined with carvings so dense they made the entrance pillars look like practice work. Names. Names everywhere, floor to ceiling, spiraling up the columns, marching along the walls in ranks. And every few dozen paces, a lamp niche where a flame should have burned, and instead a faint glow that brightened when we passed, dimming behind us like a held breath released.

Marta walked with her fingers trailing the walls, reading.

"Vund the Lampwright," she murmured. "Bren of the Deep Gallery. Osk, who argued with a river and lost." She laughed, a dry sound. "I mapped Thornfell's roads for sixty years and never knew there was a whole country under it. All these names, and no one left alive to say them."

"Say them anyway," I said.

She glanced at me. "What?"

"That's what the hunger is, isn't it? What my mother—" I stopped. That was still new, saying *my mother* and meaning something other than a blank space and a scarred cat's grief. "That's what it wants. To be remembered. The stones glow because someone's reading them. So read them."

So Marta read. Her voice went down the gallery ahead of us like a lamp of its own, naming names, and the carvings brightened where her voice passed, and I told myself the shiver down my spine was just cold.

It wasn't. Something far below had noticed us. I felt it the way you feel a stare across a dark room—not a sound, not a sight, just a pressure at the edge of knowing, patient and enormous and *hungry*, turning its attention toward five small warm lives descending through its mountain.

Bracken felt it too. He stopped, tail low, and looked back at me with those green eyes.

"It knows a Vasquez walks its halls," he said. "It has waited a long time to speak with one."

"Good," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "It can wait a little longer. We're not ready to talk."

"No," Bracken agreed softly. "But it will not wait for readiness. It will speak in whatever voice you cannot refuse."

* * *

We camped that night in a round chamber where four galleries met, a place the dwarves had clearly meant for rest—benches carved into the rock, a fire pit, and above us a dome pierced by a single shaft that let in a thread of night sky. Except there was no sky anymore. The shaft showed only black, starless, the same nothing that had replaced the heavens the night the wards fell.

Pip stared up at it until I made him eat.

"Is it looking at us?" he asked. "Through there?"

"Nothing's looking at you," Tam said, then ruined it by adding, "probably."

"It spoke to me already," Pip said quietly, and we all went still. "On the road. When I almost faded. It sounded like—" his jaw worked "—like the innkeeper's wife. From the village that forgot me. She used to give me rolls. It sounded like her, telling me I could stop walking. That nobody would miss me anyway, so why fight it?"

The fire cracked. Elias fed it another stick, movements careful, a man handling something explosive.

"And what did you do?" Elias asked.

"I ate the roll she gave me," Pip said. "In my head. And then I remembered Rosalind telling me my own story, and I thought—that's a better roll." He shrugged, trying to be tough about it, twelve years old and holding his own soul together with bread metaphors. "It didn't like that much."

No one slept well after that. We took watches in pairs. I took mine with Bracken, sitting shoulder-to-shoulder against his warm flank while the others dozed fitfully around the coals.

"You knew her," I said. Not a question. I'd known it since the bears' cave, since her voice rose out of the hibernation dream singing the sealing song, but I wanted it said aloud—or as aloud as anything got between us. "My mother. You knew her."

The cat was silent long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer.

"I was her companion," he said at last. "As I am yours. Bound to the Vasquez line, one cat for one speaker, generation upon generation, until the gift fails or the line ends. Your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother called me kitten. I have guarded the Door through six Vasquez women, and I watched each of them walk down these same galleries, and I watched your mother be the first to walk back up alone."

"What happened to her? Down there?"

"She sealed the Door," Bracken said. "And sealing it cost her. Not her life—the hunger doesn't take lives, it takes *keeping*. She gave it her keeping of herself. Pieces of her memory, freely offered, to feed it and lull it. She walked out of this mountain with holes in her where a childhood should have been, where your father's face should have been, where—" his voice roughened "—where the plan should have been. Because the seal was never meant to hold forever. It was meant to hold until a stronger speaker was born. A Vasquez who could do more than patch the wound. One who could end the hungering altogether."

The shaft above us showed nothing. The fire settled. Somewhere far below, vast and slow, something turned over in its sleep and murmured.

"Why didn't anyone tell me?" I whispered. "Why did she leave me a secret instead of a lesson?"

"Because the Order was hunting speakers even then," Bracken said. "Because a secret keeps. A lesson can be taken from you." He lowered his great head and pressed it briefly against my shoulder, an enormous weight, an older tenderness than I knew how to carry. "She meant to teach you herself, when you were grown. The forgetting came early. It always comes early. That is the nature of hungers—they do not respect schedules."

I sat with that. My mother, holed and hollowed, carrying me out of this mountain in her arms maybe, or carrying me before that, in her body, the way you carry a promise. Building a life in Thornfell village with gaps in it she papered over with routine, smuggling honey through hidden passes, teaching me herb-lore and never once letting slip that our blood was a lock and a key.

"She sang," I said. "In the bears' dream. The sealing song."

"She sang it every day of her life," Bracken said. "You'll sing it better. You were born for the verse she never learned."

* * *

The whispers started an hour into the second day's descent.

At first I thought it was water—dwarven cisterns, air moving through old pipes. Then I realized the galleries had gone silent around us. No drip. No echo of our footsteps. The rock itself had gone quiet, listening, and into that listening came voices.

Tam heard his brother.

I knew about the brother the way you know things about smugglers—half a story, told drunk, never finished. Now Tam stopped mid-stride and turned toward a side gallery as if someone had called his name, and his face did something I'd never seen it do: it opened completely, hope and grief with no fence around either.

"Denny?" he said.

"Don't," Marta said sharply, grabbing his sleeve.

"He's *there*." Tam's voice cracked wide. "He died eight years ago, drowned off the Sorrow coast, and he's *there*, he's calling me, he says he's cold, Rosie, he says he's been cold for eight years—"

"He's not there." I took his arm. Under my hand he was shaking. "Tam. Listen to me. The thing below doesn't have Denny. It has your *memory* of Denny, and it's wearing him like a coat. That's all any of them are. Coats."

"Then how do you know?" he demanded, rounding on me, and it was the wound talking, the fever-cold talking, the forgetting talking. "How do you know it isn't true? How does anyone know anything anymore? The stars went out, the roads forget themselves, my own brother—"

"Because," I said, and pulled the truth up from wherever Bracken had planted it in me, "because if Denny were really down here, he wouldn't want you to follow him into the dark. He'd want you to walk out. That's how you know. The real ones always want you to walk out."

Tam stared at me. Then at the empty gallery. Then his face closed again, carefully, plank by plank, the smuggler's shutters going back up—and behind them, wet-eyed and furious and alive.

"He hated the cold," Tam said thickly. "Denny. Always complained. So no, he wouldn't be down here." He wiped his face with his wrist. "Let's keep walking."

But the voices didn't stop. They moved down the line like a wind finding cracks.

Marta heard her husband, dead forty years, asking if she'd finished the map. She answered him aloud, steady as bedrock: *"Not yet. Ask me tomorrow."* And kept walking.

Pip heard the innkeeper's wife again and put his hands over his ears and recited his own name over and over—*Pip, Pip, my name is Pip and Rosalind knows it*—until the whisper gave up on him and slid elsewhere.

Elsewhere was Elias.

I saw it hit him. He stopped so suddenly the mule bumped him. And whatever he heard, it emptied his face of everything except a single terrible yearning, and he said, very quietly, in a voice I'd never heard him use:

"...Corvane?"

Then he shook himself like a wet dog, hard, twice, and when he looked at me his jaw was set.

"It wore his voice," he said. "Told me the harvest was mercy. Told me I'd always known that, deep down. Told me to bring you to him and end this cleanly." His fists flexed. "It sounds like him. That's the worst part. It argues exactly like him."

"Everyone's hearing their worst door," Marta muttered. "Their easiest way out."

"Not everyone," Bracken said grimly. "It saves its best voice for the one it wants most."

And then it came for me.

* * *

It didn't use my mother's voice. That's what I expected, braced for, walking the last gallery with my heart hammering and Bracken pressed close against my leg. I expected her singing, her arms, her saying *come down, little wren, I'm here, I'm here.*

Instead the whole corridor filled with the smell of honey.

Warm honey, sun on wax, the smuggler's pass in high summer—and then her voice, yes, but not calling me down. Just talking. Ordinary. The way she'd talked when I was small and couldn't sleep, rambling about nothing, herbs and weather and which neighbors owed whom, a voice made entirely of safety.

*"You're so tired, wren. Look how tired. You've carried them all this way—Tam's wound, Marta's years, the boy's whole self in your two hands. You've done enough. Put it down. Come lie down. I'll keep watch. Mothers keep watch. You never have to keep watch again."*

I stopped walking.

The others were ahead, their torchlight bobbing away down the gallery, and the dark behind me was warm, warm as a kitchen, warm as being seven years old and safe, and every muscle in my body ached to sit down right there on ten thousand years of stone and let someone else be the grown-up.

Bracken's tail brushed my ankle. Once. Deliberate.

"That's not her cadence," he said quietly. "Your mother never let you rest from a task half-finished. She'd have said *finish the row first, wren.*"

I laughed—a broken, wet sound—and wiped my face with both hands.

"Yeah," I said. "Yeah, she would." I looked down the dark gallery, toward the pressure below, the vast patient wanting, and I put every scrap of steel I owned into my voice. "Finish the row first. Hear that? Whatever you are down there—*we finish the row first.*"

The warmth peeled away grudgingly, like a hand unclenching finger by finger. The smell of honey faded. The whispers withdrew, sullen, and somewhere beneath miles of rock something enormous sighed and settled in to wait for the next crack in us.

I caught up to the others. Nobody asked. They'd all heard their own version; they recognized the shape of mine in my face.

An hour later the gallery ended.

Not in rubble or collapse—in architecture. We stepped out onto a ledge overlooking a cavern so vast our torches couldn't find its ceiling or its far wall, a cathedral of stone where the dwarves had carved terraces and stairways and balconies in rings descending toward the center, and every surface was covered in names, millions of them, glowing faint gold in the dark like a buried sky.

And at the bottom, at the exact center, visible even from this height:

A seam of light. Thin as a knife cut. Star-white.

The Last Door.

Beside me, Bracken went utterly still, and when he spoke, his voice was the oldest thing I'd ever heard.

"We're here, wren. Whatever happens next—" his green eyes reflected the seam's pale glow "—happens quickly."
