## 15. The Last Door

The mountain stopped pretending to be stone two hours before we found the Door.

We had been walking through dwarven ruins since dawn—corridors of black granite where carved names still glowed faint along the walls, thousands of them, a census of the dead that refused to be forgotten. Pip kept stopping to touch them. Tam, walking again but gray as ash under his bandages, read some aloud in a low voice, like a man saying grace. Marta's map rode against my chest in its oilcloth wrapping, and I could feel it there the way you feel a heartbeat that isn't yours.

Then the corridor simply ended, and the rock ahead of us curved inward like the inside of a throat, and Bracken said: *Here.*

He had barely spoken since the ruins began. A mountain cat who talked in full sentences should have been a marvel every time he opened his mouth, but I'd learned his silences meant more than his words. Now he sat down on the cold floor, tucked his scarred paws beneath him, and looked at me with those yellow eyes that held nine lifetimes of watching.

"Here what?" Tam asked. "I see a wall."

"You're not looking," Bracken said.

I was. And because my gift is what it is—because I have spent my whole life hearing things the world pretends are silent—I could see what he saw. The wall wasn't a wall. It was a seam. A hairline crack running floor to ceiling in a slow spiral, and out of that crack came light. Not torchlight. Not the pale gruel-light of the long winter. It was *starlight*, actual starlight, silver and cold and alive, leaking through living rock like water through a dam.

The last piece of the vanished sky.

My knees nearly went. I don't mean that as poetry. I put a hand against the granite and stood there breathing while the others caught up, because I remembered that sky. I was seven when the stars went out for good over Thornfell—I'd thought they were clouds, everyone did—and before that, before the wards started failing, my mother used to lift me onto her shoulders on clear nights and name constellations until I fell asleep against her hair. The Bear. The Harp. The Wagon with its crooked wheel.

All of it was here, pressed flat behind a seam in a mountain, shining through a crack thin as a knife's edge.

"It's beautiful," Pip whispered. He reached toward it and Marta caught his wrist—not roughly, just firmly, the way she did everything now, her hands going papery and certain at once.

"Beautiful things in this country kill you politely," she said. "Ask it what it wants first."

* * *

The seam hummed when I got close. Not the ward-hum from the night the stars died—that snapping, harp-string wrongness. This was deeper. Older. A note held so long it had become part of the stone, and underneath the note, faint as a voice through a floor, something else.

Something listening.

*You came,* it said. Not in words. In the feeling of words, the way beasts speak to me, except this was no beast. It spoke in the shape of my own longing. *You came back. She said someone would come back.*

My mother. Of course. Everything in this mountain came back to her eventually.

"Not yet," I said aloud, because the others needed to hear me say it, and maybe because I needed it too. "We look. We don't touch."

Bracken padded up beside me and pressed his shoulder against my leg—a gesture I'd never felt from him, weight and warmth and something that was almost apology. "Your mother sealed this with a song," he said. "The bears remember fragments. I remember more. But Rosalind—the seal was never meant to hold forever. It was meant to hold until *you*. She told me that the last night I saw her. She said: *When my daughter comes, tell her the Door is not a wall. It is a wound. And wounds are not closed by hiding them.*"

"What does that mean?" Tam asked. He'd lowered himself onto a fallen pillar, one hand pressed to his side. The forgetting-cold had gotten into the wound; we all knew what that meant, even if none of us said it. "Wounds get closed. That's what wounds do."

"No," Marta said quietly. "Wounds get *tended*. There's a difference." She looked at me across the starlit dark. "Your mother bought time, girl. Time for what?"

"For me," I said. It didn't feel like pride. It felt like standing at the edge of a well and hearing your own name come up from the bottom. "Elias said—for someone with a stronger gift to be born. Except I don't feel stronger. I feel like a girl who talks to animals standing in front of the biggest thing she's ever seen."

"You feel exactly right," Bracken said. "Strength isn't how it feels. Strength is what it's *for*."

And then the mountain shook.

* * *

It wasn't an earthquake. Earthquakes don't come with footsteps.

Dust sifted down through the starlight. The glowing names on the walls flickered, dimmed, steadied. Bracken rose from his haunches in one liquid motion, ears flat, and a growl came out of him that I felt in my teeth.

"Crows?" Pip whispered.

"Worse," Bracken said. "Boots."

They came around the spiral corridor in formation—six Wardens in their slate-gray cloaks, blades drawn but lowered, faces lit from below by the seam's glow so they looked like drowned men rising. And at their center, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a granary, walked High Warden Corvane.

Behind the six came twelve more, and behind them, carried on a litter between two Wardens, came glass.

Dozens of bottles. Jars. Flasks sealed with wax and wire, packed in straw like eggs, and each one full of light—but not starlight. This light was warm-colored, domestic, the gold of lamplit kitchens and harvest fires, and inside some of the bottles shapes moved. A woman dancing. A dog chasing a stick. Hands kneading bread. Whole lives, decanted, corked, labeled with little tags in Corvane's neat hand.

Marta made a sound I'd never heard from her. Old Marta, who mapped roads that no longer existed and never flinched at anything. She made a sound like a hinge tearing.

"That's Millbrook," she said. "That's—those are *people*, that's the whole of Millbrook in jars—" Her voice climbed and cracked. "You bottled them. You bottled them while they were still *alive*—"

"While they were still *forgetting*," Corvane corrected, and his voice was mild, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing drainage. He stepped forward into the starlight and I saw his face properly for the first time up close: lined, tired, eyes like wells with the bottom scraped out. "Memories fade either way, Mistress Vasquez. Faded memories feed nothing and no one. Bottled memories can be carried somewhere they'll matter. Somewhere the sky still works." He gestured at the seam with one open hand, almost courtly. "And you've found it for us. I confess I hoped you would. Your mother made us wait twenty years. You've been far more cooperative."

"I haven't done anything for you."

"You walked here. That was the hard part." He turned to face the Door fully, and for a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his scraped-out eyes. Hunger? Grief? With him I genuinely couldn't tell, and that frightened me more than the blades did. "Do you know what this is, girl? Truly? Not the story the beasts tell. Not your mother's story. The truth."

"Tell me," I said, and kept my hand on Marta's shoulder, because she was shaking.

"This is the last exit out of a dying country, and behind it stands everything Thornfell ever was. Every road, every name, every grave, every song—including the songs nobody wrote down, including the names of villages that are already gone." His voice stayed level, but it gathered force like snow sliding off a roof. "The Order has spent three hundred years preparing for this night. We can open this Door in an hour. We can carry out every bottle, every jar, every saved scrap of this land, and plant them in living soil beyond the mountains. Thornfell dies—but Thornfell *persists*. Nothing ends. Nothing is allowed to simply end." He looked back at me, and now I saw it plainly: it was grief. It had always been grief, worn down to a tool. "That is mercy, Mistress Vasquez. It is the only mercy left. All I require is that you open the Door. Only a Vasquez can. Only the blood that sealed it can unseal it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we wait." He smiled, thin and patient as frost. "Your friend is dying. Your guide is old. The hunger below grows stronger every day you stand here arguing. Refusal is just a slower yes."

Tam laughed. It cost him—I heard the wet edge in it—but he laughed, pushing himself up off the pillar to stand beside me. "She doesn't bargain well," he told Corvane. "Believe me. I've tried selling her things for years. She always finds a third option nobody priced."

"There is no third option," Corvane said.

"There's always a third option," Marta said. "That's what maps are *for*."

Bracken said nothing. He was staring at the seam, and his tail had gone utterly still, and I realized he was listening to the same thing I was—the note beneath the note, the listener beneath the stone—and what I heard now, clearer than before, was not longing anymore.

It was appetite. Patient, enormous, and waking up, because the Door hadn't been this close to opening in twenty years, and it knew. Whatever my mother had trapped down there, whatever the hunger truly was, it could smell the argument above it. It could smell the bottles. Twenty years of silence, and now the whole mountain was full of memory again—glowing names, a dying woman's map, jars of stolen lives—and the hunger was *salivating*.

Corvane wanted to open the Door and carry the country out.

The hunger wanted the Door opened and the country *served*.

And I stood between them, the only person in the world whose blood fit the lock, and understood with terrible clarity that both of them were about to get exactly what they wanted unless I found something neither of them had imagined.

"Rosalind," Bracken said softly. "Whatever you choose—choose fast. The seal is thinner than it was this morning."

Above us, far away, the mountain groaned. The first bottle on the litter flickered, and inside it, a woman mid-dance seemed to look directly at me, mouth shaping a word I couldn't hear.

I think it was my mother's name.
