## 17. The Hunger Speaks

The seam of starlight hummed against the dark of the cavern like a struck harp string that refused to finish its note.

Rosalind stood at the edge of the light it cast—a pale, cold radiance that did not flicker the way firelight flickered but pulsed instead, slow and patient, like something breathing behind a wall of stone. The Door was not a door in any way she had imagined as a child listening to smugglers' stories. It was a wound in the mountain's living rock, tall as three men and narrow as a coffin lid, and through its edges leaked the last true sky in all of Thornfell: stars, hundreds of them, pressed flat against stone like flowers kept between the pages of a book.

She had been walking toward it for eleven days. She had lost blood to crows and sleep to grief and Marta to the long dark, and now she was here, and she found she could not take another step.

*You came,* said the hunger.

It did not speak aloud. It spoke the way Bracken spoke—inside her skull, behind her eyes—but where Bracken's voice was gravel and warmth, this voice was every voice at once. It was the innkeeper forgetting his daughter's name. It was Pip's mother, whoever she had been before erasure took her. It was snow falling on a village that no longer remembered it was a village.

"I came," Rosalind said. Her breath fogged. "I don't know why I came."

*Yes, you do.*

Behind her, Tam shifted against the cavern wall, his bandaged shoulder seeping in the cold. Elias had his hand on his blade out of habit, though they both knew steel meant nothing here. Pip pressed close to her skirts. Only Bracken was still, utterly still, the scarred mountain cat sitting on his haunches with his amber eyes fixed on the seam of light, and there was something in his posture Rosalind had never seen before.

Fear.

Bracken, who had faced down Warden hounds and Corvane himself, was afraid.

"Talk to me," Rosalind said to the darkness. "If you want something from me, say it plainly."

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. And then the cavern changed.

* * *

She was standing in Thornfell village on a summer evening, and the stars were out.

Not the stolen stars pressed into the Door—real stars, wheeling overhead in their slow ancient orders, so many of them the sky looked salted. Below them the village glowed gold at every window. She could smell bread. She could hear fiddles. Someone was laughing near the well—the well that remembered water it never held—and the laugh was high and bright and young, and Rosalind knew it, knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, knew it before her mind caught up with the impossible fact of it.

"Mama?"

Her mother turned from the inn door, flour on her hands, a smudge of it on one cheek, and smiled at her the way the sun comes up.

"There you are," Sera Vasquez said. "I wondered when you'd find your way home."

Rosalind's legs nearly went out from under her. She had been four years old—maybe five—when the Wardens took her mother away, and everything she had left of her was a voice heard through a door, a song half-remembered, a shape in a dream that faded by morning. Now here was the whole woman, solid in the doorway, flour-dusted, alive, and Rosalind made a sound she didn't know she could make and crossed the square at a dead run.

Her arms closed around her mother and her mother was warm. Warm and real and smelling of yeast and woodsmoke and the particular soap she'd used, the one with the crushed nettle, and Rosalind wept into her shoulder without shame because there was no shame here, there couldn't be, this was—

"This isn't real," she whispered.

"It's as real as anything," her mother said, stroking her hair. "Realer than most things. I'm every memory anyone ever kept of me, love. That's more than most people get to be."

"The hunger." Rosalind pulled back, holding her mother's face in her hands, memorizing it even as she understood what she was doing—that she was being played, that this was bait, and memorizing it anyway because some hungers are worth being fooled by. "You're the hunger."

"I'm what's under the mountain," her mother agreed gently. "And I am very, very hungry, Rosalind. Do you know what I am? Truly?"

Rosalind shook her head.

"A country," said the thing wearing her mother's face, "is a story a people agree to keep telling. The roads. The names. Which field floods in spring, which grandmother cursed which grandfather, where the bodies are buried and why. I was born when the first person first said *this is ours*, and I have grown fat on every telling since. For six hundred years Thornfell told itself to itself, and I wanted for nothing." The smile faltered. The flour-smudge cheek seemed suddenly thinner. "And then the borders started dying. The wards snapped. The stars went out. People began to forget, and Rosalind—when they forget, *I* starve."

The square around them flickered. For half a heartbeat Rosalind saw through it: black stone, cold air, the seam of light. Then the illusion reasserted itself, straining, like a singer holding a note too long.

"You're doing this," Rosalind said. "The forgetting. You're eating the country."

"No!" The word cracked like ice on a pond. Her mother's hands seized hers, grip desperate. "No, child, no—I would never. The forgetting is the *disease*. I am only the starving animal it's turning me into. Something is killing Thornfell from outside—something beyond the borders, something that wants this land empty—and as the memories die, I grow weaker, and a starving thing does terrible things. I have begun to eat. I hate it. I hate myself. But I am so hungry, Rosalind, and you—" the grip tightened, "—you can end it. You can feed me properly. One time. One act. Open the Door."

"And let you out."

"Let us *both* out. Me into the wide world where there is so much story to eat I'd never go hungry again—and you and yours out of this dying trap before the last of it goes. Everyone lives. Everyone remembers. Your mother gave six years of her life sealing me in, and it bought time, and the time is spent, and the only mercy left is the Door." Her mother's eyes were wet. They were her own eyes; Rosalind had seen them in her own reflection all her life and never known whose they were. "Open it, and I will give you back everything. Every memory of her the years took. Her whole life, start to finish—you'll know her better than she knew herself. And the village. I can hold the village together, keep it remembering itself until you're safely out. No one else has to fade. Not Tam. Not Pip. Not one more soul."

The square blazed around them, golden and perfect. Somewhere the fiddle played. Somewhere the bread smelled of home.

"Say yes," her mother whispered. "Please. I've waited under this mountain for twenty years to ask someone who could actually hear me. Say yes, Rosalind."

And God help her, every part of her wanted to.

* * *

"Yes," Rosalind heard herself say.

The word came out of her small and hoarse, and the moment it left her lips the whole false evening shuddered with joy—the fiddle swelled, the windows flared brighter, the stars above wheeled faster, drunk on her consent.

"Yes," she said again, stronger, already turning toward the seam of light, toward the Door, toward the mountain's heart. "I'll open it. But I want her first. All of her. Before I touch the Door, you give me—"

*Rosalind.*

Bracken's voice cut across the illusion like a claw through cloth.

He was walking toward her through the golden square, and the square hated him for it—the cobbles seemed to soften under his paws, trying to swallow him, the fiddler's music souring as he passed. He was limping. She hadn't noticed before, in the dark of the cavern, how badly he was limping. Eleven days of hard road on old wounds.

"Stay out of this," Rosalind said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "This is my mother."

"That," Bracken said quietly, "is exactly what it is counting on."

"She's offering to save everyone. Tam, Pip, the whole—"

"She is offering you the one thing she knows you cannot refuse, and she has been studying you since the moment you entered the mountain to learn precisely what that is." He stopped an arm's length away. Up close, his scars stood white against his fur, each one a story he had never told her. "Rosalind. Ask yourself one question. In all these years sealed beneath the rock, with all its power—all its *hunger*—has it ever once tried to be less than it is? Or only more?"

"What difference does—"

"Ask it," Bracken said, "what happened to the last person who opened a door for it."

The golden square went silent.

The fiddle stopped mid-phrase. The laughter died. And her mother's face—her beautiful, impossible, flour-cheeked mother's face—did something Rosalind had never seen a face do. It hesitated.

"There was no one," her mother said.

"Liar," said Bracken.

"There was no one," the hunger repeated, and now the voice was layering, doubling, her mother's voice over a hundred other voices over a sound like wind through a dead orchard, "no one, no one came, no one ever—"

"Sera Vasquez did not seal this Door alone," Bracken said, eyes never leaving Rosalind's. "There were others. Wardens. Villagers who believed her. When the sealing failed the first time—when the Door had to be closed a second time, from the inside—someone stayed behind to hold it shut while the rest ran." His voice dropped. "Your mother sang the sealing song from the outside, Rosalind. Someone else sang it from within. It has worn that voice for twenty years. It has been saving it for you."

Rosalind looked at her mother.

Her mother looked back—and for one unguarded instant, before the mask came down again, the expression on that beloved face was not love. It was appetite. Patient, bottomless, ancient appetite, the look of a thing that had learned to wear tenderness the way a crow wears another bird's feathers in its nest.

Then it was gone, and the warmth returned, and the square blazed, and her mother said, "Don't listen to the cat, love. Cats lie. Come and open the Door."

And Rosalind—grieving, exhausted, twenty years hungry herself—took one step toward the light.

* * *

Bracken moved faster than she had ever seen anything move.

One moment he was beside her; the next he was between her and the seam, planted squarely in the starlight, and the light poured through him and turned him to a silhouette of silver fire. He was an old cat, gray-muzzled, scarred, half-lame—and he stood in front of the Last Door of Thornfell like a wall.

"Move, Bracken."

"No."

"I have to do this. Everyone dies if I don't—"

"Everyone dies if you *do*," he said. "Just slower. Just later. Just somewhere far from here, thinking themselves safe, while the thing they freed learns the taste of new countries." He sat, deliberately, the way cats sit when they intend to be immovable. "So. If you mean to open it, you'll have to move me first."

"I can't fight you."

"I know." Something flickered in his amber eyes—not quite a smile. "That was always your mother's problem too."

"Then step aside."

"No. Instead, hear my offer." He raised his chin toward the seam, toward the hunger behind it, and his voice carried into the dark like a thrown stone. "*You.* Yes, you, the great starving story underneath. You want lives? You want memory? Here is mine. Nine lives, cat's-bright, every one of them lived full—hunts and winters and a woman's voice singing in a cave, twenty years of them. Take them. Take them all, tonight, and let the girl walk away unfed-upon."

"Bracken, *no*—"

"Nine lives," he repeated, louder, "against one soul. A bargain even a starving thing can't refuse."

The silence that followed was total. Even the Door's hum seemed to hold its breath.

And then the hunger laughed.

It was the worst sound Rosalind had ever heard—not cruel, exactly, but *tired*, the laugh of something that had heard every offer the desperate could make and found them all wanting. The golden square dissolved at last, unable to hold its shape around the force of it, and they stood again in the raw black cavern, the seam of stars blazing between them and the dark.

*Nine lives,* it said, almost gently. *Little guardian. I ate nine hundred lives before your grandmother's grandmother was whelped. What are nine more?*

"Then why haven't you taken mine already?" Bracken asked. "Why bargain at all? Why dress yourself in her mother and beg?" His tail curled around his paws. "Because you can't take. Not anymore. Taking is what's killing you—you've been gulping down half-digested memories for months, villages' worth, and none of it feeds you, because memory *stolen* is just meat, and you were never a meat-eater. You were built on something else."

The light pulsed. Harder. Angrier.

*Careful, beast.*

"You were built on *telling*," Bracken said. "On people choosing to share what they remember. Freely given, freely received. That's the feast. That's what you've been starving for. And no theft, no trick, no opened door will ever fill you, because everything you grab turns to ash in your mouth—and deep down, under all that hunger, you know it."

"*Enough.*"

The word hit the cavern like a landslide. Stones fell from the ceiling. Tam cried out; Pip screamed; the seam of starlight flared white-hot, and the hunger's voice became a storm of every stolen voice at once, roaring—

*I AM THE COUNTRY AND THE COUNTRY IS DYING AND I WILL NOT DIE ALONE—*

And Rosalind finally understood.

Not because Bracken had argued her into it. Not because the rage proved anything. But because in that roar, beneath the fury, she heard it—clear as a bell, clear as her mother's voice through an inn door twenty years ago:

Fear.

Pure, simple, animal terror. The terror of the dark and the empty and the alone. The same fear she'd heard trembling in the horses the night the stars went out. The same fear in the crows on the col, hollowed and screaming, birds that remembered being ordinary. The same fear, she realized, that had lived in her own chest her whole life—in the secret shame of her gift, in the loneliness, in the nights she'd lain awake certain that if anyone truly knew her, they would leave.

The hunger wasn't a monster.

It was a country, afraid of being forgotten. Gone ravenous, gone cruel, gone mad with it—but afraid, underneath, the way every frightened thing is dangerous, and every dangerous thing is, first, frightened.

"Oh," Rosalind said softly.

The roar faltered.

"You're not evil," she said. "You're *scared*."

*Do not—*

"Twenty years under a mountain. Six hundred years being told stories, and then everyone stops telling them, and you can feel yourself thinning out like breath on glass." She took a step toward the seam—not toward the Door, toward *it*, toward the thing behind the light. "My whole life I thought my gift was a curse. A shameful thing to hide. And the whole time, the loneliest creature in Thornfell was the one thing I was born able to hear."

*Stop,* said the hunger, and the voice shook. *Stop talking like that. Stop—*

"My mother didn't seal you in to punish you." Rosalind's eyes were burning. "Did she? She sealed you in because she didn't know what else to do. Because she was scared too. And you've been down here ever since, wearing her voice, asking the dark the same question over and over: *does anyone remember I exist?*"

Silence.

Long, ringing, terrible silence. The light dimmed to embers. And when the hunger spoke again, all the borrowed voices were gone, and what remained was small, and cracked, and horribly young:

*Does anyone?*

And Rosalind, standing at the edge of the last starlight in a dying country, with her wounded friend bleeding behind her and her oldest companion trembling before her, felt the truth of the next chapter of her life arrive whole, the way Marta's map had arrived, the way her mother's song had arrived—like an inheritance she hadn't known she was carrying.

"Not yet," she said. "But that can change. Not with a key. Not with nine lives. With something better."

She turned to Bracken. He was watching her with an expression she had no name for yet.

"How many voices do we have left?" she asked him. "Count them for me. Every survivor. Every crow we freed. Every name in Marta's map."

Bracken stared at her. And slowly, slowly, the fear went out of him, and something else took its place—the thing that had carried a promise for twenty years across a frozen country to keep.

"Enough," he said. "If they're willing. Enough."

Behind them, in the dark, Tam pushed himself upright against the wall, one-armed, white-faced, and said the words that set the next hour burning:

"Well then," he rasped. "Somebody fetch my good stories. I've got a lifetime of them, and I've never once told them right."

The seam of stars pulsed—once, softly, like a held breath beginning to release.

Rosalind rolled up her sleeves and turned to face her family.
