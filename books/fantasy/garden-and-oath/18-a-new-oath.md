## 18. A New Oath

The hunger's voice was still in my ears when I turned from the seam of starlight.

*Everything you lost,* it had said. *Everything she took from you.* It had worn my mother's voice like a stolen coat, and for one terrible moment I had believed it, because grief will believe anything if you hold it up to the right light. Bracken stood between me and the Door now, his scarred body low, his nine lives offered up like coins on a table. Behind him the seam pulsed—starlight pressed into living rock, the last piece of Thornfell's sky, and beneath it something vast and starving turning over in its sleep.

"No," I said to Bracken. "Not your lives."

"Then whose?" His voice was rough as bark. "The Door opens or the Door seals, girl. Those are the only doors there are."

I looked at him. Then past him—at Tam, grey-faced against the wall with his wound-bound leg, at Pip with his fists clenched like a boy daring the world to erase him again, at old Marta's map rolled under my arm because we had carried her this far even after she died, because you don't leave the dead behind on a road that isn't there. At Elias, Warden badge gone from his coat, standing beside Corvane—the High Warden on his knees in the dwarven dust, his bottled memories shattered around him, glass and starlight scattered like spilled water.

And I understood what my mother had never had. She'd been alone down here. One woman, one song, one seal. She'd bought time for someone stronger to be born—and everyone assumed that meant someone who could shut the Door harder.

But strength isn't a wall. I'd learned that from beasts. Strength is a pack. A flock. A village that remembers its own name because everyone says it together.

"It doesn't want to be fed," I said slowly, working it out aloud, the way Bracken had once taught me to read bear-sign. "It wants to be *remembered*. That's all hunger is, down here. A country afraid of being forgotten, gone ravenous." I touched the seam. It hummed against my palm, warm as a sleeping animal. "So we don't open it. We don't seal it. We feed it."

Tam laughed, wet and weak. "With what? We haven't got so much as a heel of bread left."

"With us," I said. "With everything we've got. Marta's map. Your stories. Pip's name. My mother's song." I turned to face them all, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised me more than anything else that night. "We tell it Thornfell. All of it. Out loud. Together. Not stolen memory in glass bottles—shared memory, spoken memory, memory that gets *bigger* every time it's told instead of smaller. That's the third path. That's the oath renewed."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"That's mad," said Tam.

"Yes," I agreed. "So was walking a road that wasn't there. So was trusting a Warden. So was every single thing that's kept us alive since the stars went out." I knelt by the seam. "Bracken. You knew her best. You start."

* * *

He started badly, which I think surprised him. Mountain cats are not made for speeches.

"She was—" he began, and stopped, and his tail lashed once. "She was stubborn. She smelled of juniper and ink. She sang off-key and pretended she didn't know it." He paced closer to the light, hackles half-raised, as if the words cost him something physical. "She found me caught in a snare on the Cael slopes and instead of taking my pelt she sat in the snow for three hours talking to me until I stopped being afraid of her. Nobody had ever done that. Beasts don't get talked to. Beasts get *managed*." His voice dropped. "She said a country is just a promise people keep saying out loud. And when they stop saying it—" He looked back at me. "She said someone would come who understood that. She waited thirty years to be right."

The seam brightened. Just a little. Like an animal lifting its head at a familiar footstep.

I felt it through my gift—not words, but pressure easing. Attention turning. The vast hungry thing below shifting its weight toward the sound of being spoken to.

"It's listening," I whispered. "Keep going. Everyone. Anything true."

Elias stepped forward before anyone else could. He cleared his throat like a man about to recite doctrine, and then abandoned doctrine entirely.

"I don't remember my childhood," he said. "But I remember roads. There was a road out of a valley—I think it was this valley—with a crooked mile-marker shaped like a thumb, and I used to race it on foot, and I always lost, because I was small and the road was long." He swallowed. "If that road was here, then I'm from here. And if I'm from here, then Thornfell raised me whether I remember it or not. So: there was a road with a marker shaped like a thumb. Let that be true somewhere forever."

The seam flared gold-white. Dust fell from the cavern roof. Somewhere far below, something exhaled.

Pip went next, because Pip had been waiting his whole erased life for someone to ask. He planted his feet the way he did before telling a lie to a fence, except nothing he said was a lie.

"My name is Pip," he said, loud, defiant, tears running freely down his face. "My name is Pip and my mother baked honey-bread on feast days and burned it every time and we ate it anyway. My name is Pip and I slept in the loft above the tannery and it smelled awful and it was home. My name is Pip and I am *not nobody*, I am somebody from Thornfell, and you can't have me, do you hear? You can't have me because I'm giving myself away free!"

He was shouting by the end, and the shout rang off the dwarven stone, and every carved name along the walls glowed a little brighter, as if the dead dwarves had leaned in to listen.

Tam dragged himself upright against the wall. "Fine. Fine, you absolute lunatics." He winced, hand pressed to his bound leg. "I ran honey over the pass eleven times. Twelve, counting the time the mule ate half the cargo. I cheated Rosalind Vasquez on our first job and she made me carry her pack for a week as penance, and I'd do it again, because it was the best week of smuggling I ever had, because for once I wasn't doing it alone." His voice cracked. "Thornfell's a mean little country full of mean little people and I love every mean inch of it. Write that down."

"I don't need to write it down," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Corvane spoke last.

He had been kneeling in the shattered glass of his harvest, and I had expected silence from him, or fury, or the hollow stare of a man watching his life's logic die. Instead he crawled forward—one knee, then the other, a High Warden of the Order moving like a beggar—and stopped at the edge of the light.

"You should know what I put in these bottles," he said. His voice was ash. "Thirty years of harvest. Villages. Weddings. Faces. I told myself it was mercy—that better stored than lost, better kept by the Order than eaten by the dark." He opened his empty hands. "But kept *how*? Locked where no one speaks them. That's not keeping. That's just a slower forgetting. I built a warehouse of ghosts and called it salvation."

"Corvane—" Elias started.

"Let me finish." The old man's eyes were wet. "I lost a family to a forgetting. I have spent my whole life making sure no one else would feel that, and I made everyone feel worse instead, and I cannot undo it. But I can say this." He turned to the seam, and his voice steadied into something almost liturgical. "There was a village called Lowmere. The mill wheel sang in a high wind. My daughter had a gap between her front teeth and laughed through it. My wife grew beans up strings in the south window. Their names were Alsen and Bree. Say it with me or don't—but it will be said."

And Elias said it with him. "*Alsen and Bree.*"

And I said it. And Tam, gritting his teeth. And Pip, who had never met them. And Bracken, in his rough cat-voice, because beasts keep promises too.

The seam blazed.

Not the ravenous flare of the Door straining at its seal—something gentler, deeper, like dawn coming up inside the mountain. The light poured along the dwarven carvings until every name on every wall burned steady, and the warmth of it reached us where we stood, and I felt the thing below do something I hadn't felt from any beast or any person in all these terrible weeks.

I felt it settle.

Like a dog circling three times and lying down. Like a child finally hearing the story end the way it should. The hunger didn't vanish—it would never vanish; it was Thornfell's fear, and fear lives as long as love does—but it curled itself around the sound of us and was quiet. Fed. Not on stolen memory. On shared telling. On the difference between a bottle and a voice.

"Oh," I said softly. "Oh, Mum. This is why. This is what you were waiting for." Because she couldn't have done this alone. One voice is a candle. A hundred voices are a hearth-fire. She'd sealed the Door to buy time, and the time had bought *us*—all of us, smugglers and Wardens and erased boys and freed crows perched blinking in the high dark, two of them still here, the ones I'd unmade from monsters on the col, tilting their heads at the light.

"Rosalind." Bracken pressed his shoulder against my leg. "The song. Finish what she started. Not the sealing verse—the whole of it. She never got to sing the welcome."

I didn't know I knew the whole of it. But I opened my mouth and it was there, the way Marta's map had been there, the way a country lives in its people: my mother's melody, off-key, exactly as Bracken had promised, and the verses below the seal that she'd never sung because no one had ever been safe enough to hear them. Verses about waking. About spring. About a door that opens gently when the ones outside have learned to carry the house inside them.

I sang. And one by one they joined—not harmonizing, none of us could harmonize, we sounded like a cart with a bad axle—and the seam drank it in, and the mountain held us, and for the first time since the stars went out I wasn't afraid of the dark.

When the last verse faded, the light dimmed to a steady glow. A heartbeat-light. A sleeping-animal light.

"It'll hold," Bracken murmured, "as long as someone tells it true. Solstices. Letters. Whatever ritual you make of it. Memory kept alive is memory shared aloud—you learned that on a road that wasn't there."

I looked around at them: Tam grinning through exhaustion, Pip asleep on his feet mid-triumph, Elias with his hand on Corvane's shoulder like a man helping a brother up, the crows preening starlight out of their feathers. Marta's map tube under my arm, heavy with every road and grave and name of home.

"The Door," I said. "Can it open now? Gently?"

Bracken looked at the seam for a long moment, ears forward, listening to whatever cats listen to beneath the world.

"Yes," he said. "I think it's been waiting for exactly this. Spring on the other side, girl. Stars. Go and see."

I laid both palms flat against the warm stone, and pushed—not hard, the way you push open a garden gate.

* * *

Light. Clean air. The smell of thawed earth and green things growing.

Beyond the threshold, the valley opened wide under a sky scattered thick with returning stars, and the first pale suggestion of dawn touching the peaks, and a wind that carried no cold in it at all. Behind us, through the narrowing seam, Thornfell slept under clean snow—not dying, not starving, just dreaming, holding everything we'd given it like a bed holds warmth.

Tam leaned on my shoulder at the doorway, breathing the air like a man surfacing from deep water. "Well," he managed. "You said you'd guide me north for pay. Technically this counts."

"Invoice me later," I said, and stepped through, and my family came with me—all of them, even the broken Warden, even the crows—carrying seeds and maps and stories and each other, carrying Thornfell inside them where no border ward could ever touch it.

Behind us, soft as a closing eye, the Last Door drew shut.

Ahead, down in the green valley, Pip pointed at a bend of the river and asked the question every founding asks:

"So—what do we call it?"

I smiled, and opened my mouth to answer.
