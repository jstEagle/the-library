## 19. Walking Out

The Door did not open so much as exhale.

That was how Rosalind thought of it afterward, when she tried to describe it to the children who would one day ask her what it had been like. Not a grinding of stone, not a shattering of seals, nothing like the violence she had braced for through every mile of the descent and every hour of the telling. The seam of starlight in the living rock simply widened, the way a sleeper's eyes open in the morning—slowly, without alarm, as if it had only been waiting to be certain the room was safe.

Light spilled out across the cavern floor. Not the cold harvested glow of Corvane's bottled memories, not the hungry shimmer that had whispered to them all in their dead's voices. This was warm light, gold-edged, smelling of wet grass and thawing earth.

Spring.

Rosalind stood at the threshold with Marta's map pressed against her chest, ink-stained and soft as cloth from handling, and she could not make herself take the first step. Behind her, the survivors of Thornfell were quiet—a hundred and forty souls, give or take the ones still being counted, wrapped in every blanket they owned, carrying what remained of their country on their backs. Seeds in oilcloth packets. A cradle taken apart and lashed to a sledge. Pip's hand fisted in her coat hem. Bracken sat at her right side with his scarred face lifted toward the light, and his tail moved once, slowly, like a metronome counting them through.

"Go on," he said. His voice was rough gravel softened by years of speaking only to himself and, once, long ago, to her mother. "It won't close on you. She made sure of that."

"My mother sealed it shut," Rosalind said. Her voice came out strange in the vast hush of the cavern, where the dwarven names still glowed faintly along the walls like embers banked for the night. "She spent her whole life keeping this thing closed."

"She spent her whole life keeping it *fed wrong*," Bracken said. "There's a difference. You found it." He looked up at her, and his green eye and his clouded one both held her without flinching. "The Door was never a prison, girl. It was a mouth. Your mother taught it to fast. You taught it to eat at a table."

Rosalind laughed—or something between a laugh and a sob, the sound coming out cracked and unrecognizable, and Pip squeezed her hem tighter as if to hold her together.

Behind them, deeper in the mountain, the hunger lay quiet. She could feel it the way you feel a sleeping animal in a dark barn—not gone, never gone, but settled. Fed on a hundred voices speaking Marta's roads aloud, on Tam's stories told twice through, on Pip's name spoken until it rang, on her mother's sealing song finished at last in a new key. It dreamed now instead of starving. And dreams, Rosalind had learned, could be tended.

"All right," she said, mostly to herself. "All right."

She stepped through.

* * *

The morning on the other side was so ordinary it hurt.

Grass—real grass, green going silver with dew—sloped away from the threshold down toward a valley threaded with a river the color of hammered tin. The sky overhead was enormous and blue at its eastern edge, and scattered across the rest of it, pale as breath on glass but unmistakable, were stars. Dozens of them. Fading in the growing light but there, holding on, the way a song holds on in your head after the singer stops.

Rosalind stood very still and let the sun land on her face. It had been winter for so long. Snow that didn't melt, cold that got into memory itself, a country forgetting its own name village by village—and here was warmth on her skin like a hand laid gently on a fevered forehead.

"The stars," Pip said beside her. He was squinting up, his small face scrunched against the light. "They came back."

"Some of them," Rosalind said. "Maybe they were never gone. Maybe they were just waiting for someone to look."

Bracken padded past them both and descended three steps into the grass, then stopped, and did something Rosalind had never seen him do. He rolled onto his back, all four paws in the air, and wriggled like an oversized kitten, his scarred belly to the sky.

Pip burst out laughing—the first true laugh Rosalind had heard from anyone in weeks, high and startled and infectious—and then she was laughing too, and then the people coming through behind them were laughing, or weeping, or doing both at once, which amounted to the same thing on a morning like this.

Marta's map rode against Rosalind's heart. She had promised herself she would not open it until they were safely through, and they were through, and still she waited, because some things deserved ceremony and some griefs needed a moment alone before they became shared ones. Old Marta had died two days' walk beneath the mountain, whole, her name spoken back to her syllable by syllable until the last of them, and her last words had been practical as ever: *Don't fold it against the crease, girl, you'll crack the ink.*

"I won't," Rosalind murmured to her now, in the sunlight, in the country Marta had never seen. "I promise I won't."

* * *

They counted themselves on the valley floor, because counting had become a ritual of survival and rituals outlive their reasons.

A hundred and forty-three. Tam's smugglers with their pack-frames of contraband honey and salt, grumbling on principle even in paradise. The fading families from the erased villages, solid now, their edges no longer bleeding into the snow. Two freed crows perched on the sledge shafts, preening their ragged feathers, refusing to be more than arm's length from Rosalind and pretending otherwise. Corvane sat apart on a stone, his Warden grey stripped of insignia, his hands empty in his lap, watching the refugees with the expression of a man reading a book in a language he almost remembers. Elias kept half an eye on him and half on the horizon, and Rosalind chose not to wonder which half worried him more.

And Tam lay on a litter near the fire ring, asleep.

He had been carried through the Door unconscious, the wound in his side finally closing in the last hour of the passage—as if the country's sickness had been holding the cut open all this time, and once they crossed, his body remembered what bodies knew how to do. But remembering how to heal was not the same as waking, and Rosalind had spent the first hour in the valley kneeling beside him, telling him about the grass, about the stars, about Bracken rolling in the dew like a fool, in case hearing was the last thing to go.

She was mid-sentence—"and then Pip fell in the river, which honestly he deserved for laughing at me"—when Tam said, "You're a terrible liar, Vasquez. I slipped. There's a difference."

Rosalind's head snapped around.

Tam was awake. Gray-faced, hollow-eyed, thinner than she'd ever seen him, but awake, and looking at her with both eyes clear and focused and *his*, no fog swimming behind them, no name slipping off his face when she reached for it. He blinked at the sky. He blinked at the sun. He frowned.

"That's not Thornfell," he said.

"No," Rosalind said. Her throat was tight. "It isn't."

"And those are stars. In daytime." He worked through it with the slow deliberation of a man checking a ledger twice. "We went under the mountain. The Door. You did the thing with the talking." His gaze drifted over the camp—the sledges, the crows, the children chasing each other through grass taller than they'd seen in a year. "Everyone's here."

"Everyone we could carry."

"Corvane?"

"Sitting over there being miserable."

"Hm." Tam tried to sit up, failed, accepted Rosalind's shoulder, and managed it on the second attempt. He pressed a hand flat against his bandaged side and stared at it as if it had betrayed him by working. "Doesn't hurt."

"It will," Rosalind said. "Give it an hour. Then you'll complain about everything, same as always."

"Wouldn't be me otherwise." He was quiet a moment. Then, without looking at her: "How much do I owe you? For the guiding. You insisted on pay, if you remember. Very businesslike. Broke my heart."

Rosalind thought about it. She thought about the hidden pass and the contraband honey, about the innkeeper who couldn't remember his daughter's face, about the cave of bears and her mother's voice singing in the dark, about Marta's map and Pip's name and the seam of starlight and a hundred voices speaking a country back into one piece. She thought about what any of that could possibly cost, and what currency it might be paid in, in a world with no borders left to cross.

"One story," she said. "When we're settled. A good one. Something I haven't heard."

Tam grinned, and it was the old grin, crooked and shameless, and Rosalind had to look away fast because her eyes had gone hot again and she refused to spend the whole morning crying.

"Deal," he said. "I know one about your mother and a tax collector that'll change how you think about her forever."

"Tam."

"Later. When you've eaten. Never tell a story to the starving; it curdles."

* * *

They built the first fires in midafternoon, and the smoke rose straight up into a windless sky, and nobody spoke about how smoke rising straight meant weather coming, because for once nobody wanted to be right.

Rosalind walked the perimeter as the light went long and gold. It was habit—she had walked a smuggler's perimeter for years, checking sightlines and escape routes—and she let herself have the habit while knowing, with a strangeness that kept catching in her chest like a fishbone, that there was nothing out there to escape from. No Wardens hunting them. Corvane's Order was a day's march behind them inside a country that was sleeping, not dying, and whatever the Order became next was a problem for a self that hadn't been born yet. No wards humming and snapping. No snow that wouldn't melt. Just the river talking to itself over stones, and the smell of green things growing, and somewhere upslope a bird singing a song she didn't recognize, which felt like the richest gift the new world had given her yet: a song she didn't already know.

Bracken found her at the high point of the rise, where the whole camp lay below—fires, litters, sledges, the tangle of a hundred and forty-three lives unpacking themselves onto open ground.

"You'll want to go back," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Not yet."

"But you'll want to."

Rosalind turned her face west, where the mountains of Thornfell stood blue and distant beyond the valley's rim, their peaks white-capped, familiar as her own hands. From here they looked peaceful. They looked like they were sleeping, which was the truth of it now—no longer dying, no longer forgetting, but dreaming, the whole country folded under clean snow with its memories held safe in the mouths of beasts and the pages of a smuggled map and the minds of everyone standing on this grass.

"Yes," she said. "I'll want to go back. Every day, probably. For years."

"Good," Bracken said. "Wanting to go back is how you know it was home. Wanting is the leash that runs both directions." He sat down beside her, his shoulder warm against her leg, and together they watched the camp. "Your mother wanted it too. That's why the sealing song has a second verse. Everyone thinks it ends with the locking. It doesn't. It ends with the returning."

Rosalind went still. "You never told me that."

"You weren't ready to hear it. You are now. I'll teach it to you tomorrow." He yawned hugely, showing teeth that had killed wolves and crows and once, memorably, a Warden's horse. "Tonight, sleep. You've been carrying a country for weeks. Put it down for one night. It'll keep. That's rather the point of everything we did."

She laughed, tiredly, and scratched him behind the ear in the place that made his back leg twitch, undignified, and he permitted it with the weary tolerance of the very old and the very loyal.

Below them, Pip was showing the smaller children how to skip stones, badly, with tremendous confidence. Tam had commandeered two blankets and was holding court from his litter, gesturing grandly, no doubt lying about something. Elias stood at the fire's edge turning his Warden badge over in his fingers, over and over, like a coin he hadn't yet decided to spend. The crows argued over a crust. The stars were coming out, one by one, patient as ever, and the first evening of the rest of all their lives settled over the valley like a blanket pulled up to the chin.

Rosalind took out Marta's map at last, unfolded it carefully against the crease, and spread it on the grass.

Every road. Every grave. Every name. Inked from memory by a dying woman so that a country could survive its own death—and now spread out under alien stars, waiting to become the founding document of something that didn't have a name yet.

"We'll need to call it something," she said.

Bracken's ear flicked. "Will we?"

"A place needs a name. Otherwise it starts forgetting itself. I've seen what happens." She studied the blank margin at the bottom of the map, the empty space where a cartographer would write the title. "And I think I know exactly who should choose it."

Down by the river, Pip wound up for another throw, missed the water entirely, hit Tam in the shoulder, and stood frozen in horror while the whole camp roared.

Rosalind smiled, and picked up a stick, and held it over the blank space, ready to write down whatever the boy said next.
