## 20. The Naming

The first thing Pip did in the new valley was fall over.

Not from weakness—though he was weak, they all were, stumbling through the Door like sleepwalkers into a morning so green it hurt to look at—but because the grass was real. He put his hand flat against it and the ground came up soft and warm and *there*, and his knees simply quit on him. He sat down hard in a meadow that had never been part of any map Marta ever drew, and he laughed until Tam thumped him on the back and said something about boys and weak stomachs.

The sky went on forever. That was the thing nobody could stop talking about. Stars at night, yes—the stars had come back, or maybe they'd never left, maybe they'd only been waiting on the other side of a seam of light under a mountain—but also clouds. Real clouds, fat and slow, dragging their shadows across the hills like patient animals. Pip had spent his whole remembered life under a sky that was forgetting itself. This one wasn't forgetting anything. It just hung there, enormous and indifferent and full of weather, and Pip loved it with a fierceness that made his chest ache.

"Up," said Bracken, appearing out of nowhere the way cats do, even mountain cats the size of a hound. "You're sitting on the best ground for a fire pit."

"You're sitting on nothing," Pip said. "You're a cat."

"I am a cat with opinions about drainage." The scarred old beast settled beside him anyway, tail curled over his paws, and looked out across the valley with eyes that had watched Thornfell's whole long dying. "It's good ground," he admitted, quieter. "Water close. Timber on the slopes. Defensible, if defensible still matters." He flexed one paw, sheathing and unsheathing claws. "I hope it stops mattering."

Behind them, the Door stood open in the hillside—a seam of starlight in living rock, gentle now, breathing almost, letting people through in twos and threes. Rosalind had said it would open gently. Pip hadn't believed her until he'd walked through it himself and felt it part around him like a curtain instead of tearing like a wound.

They came through all day. Refugees from the high villages, from the passes, from places whose names had already gone soft and unspoken. They came carrying what they could: seed sacks and copper pots, a loom broken down into beams, an entire beehive wrapped in wool with the bees humming indignantly inside. Old Corvane came through near dusk, walking alone, his Warden grey gone ragged, and no one spoke to him and no one stopped him either. He found a spot by the stream and sat there looking at his hands, and Pip decided that was somebody else's problem.

By full dark there were two hundred fires in the meadow, and the valley smelled of woodsmoke and someone's attempt at bread, and Pip lay on his back listening to the sound of two hundred people not dying.

* * *

It was three days before anyone thought about a name.

Three days of digging latrines and raising the first real shelter—a long low hall of cut sod and salvaged beams that everyone immediately called the Long House without discussing it—and of Rosalind going fire to fire with her gift, listening to what the beasts thought of the water and the grazing and the wolves on the far ridge. Three days of Elias hauling timber with his shirt off and his badge still hanging at his throat, because he hadn't figured out yet where else to put it.

On the third evening, Tam stood up on a crate outside the Long House and cleared his throat with the theatrical misery of a man who'd rather be doing anything else.

"Right," he said. "Council of the whole, or whatever we're calling it. First order of business." He scratched under his hat. "We can't keep calling this place 'the camp.' Marta would rise from her grave, and she'd haunt us *specifically*, and she'd be right to."

A low rumble of agreement. Somewhere a baby cried and was shushed. The Door glowed faintly up the slope, a coin of stars in the darkening hillside.

"A name," Tam went on. "For the settlement. Something to put on the first marker, when we cut one. And—" he hesitated, and Pip saw him glance sideways, hunting for someone, "—and I think it shouldn't be chosen by the likes of me. I've fenced stolen goods in four countries. My judgment on names is suspect." A ripple of laughter. "I think it should be chosen by someone who knows what it costs to lose a name. Someone who's been on the wrong side of forgetting and come back."

Every head turned toward Pip.

Pip, who had been sitting on the woodpile picking at a splinter, went hot all over. "No," he said. "No, absolutely not, you've got the wrong—"

"Pip." Rosalind was sitting cross-legged by the nearest fire, Bracken a warm weight against her side. She didn't get up. She just looked at him the way she did—straight on, no pity in it, which was the only reason he could bear it. "You're the only person here who was erased and came back. You know both sides of it better than anyone alive. If anyone gets to name a town, it's you."

"It should be a grown-up," Pip said desperately. "It should be Marta's—someone should say a word Marta liked—"

"Marta named forty-one things in her life," said Tam quietly. "Roads, crossings, graves. She told me once the naming was the best part, and the worst, and that she'd have given it away years ago if she'd found anyone fool enough to take it." He spread his hands. "Congratulations, lad. You're the fool."

Pip looked around the firelight. Two hundred faces, more or less—he'd counted them the second night, compulsively, the way he counted everything now, terrified of finding gaps. Faces he knew: Tam with his crooked hat. Rosalind with dirt on her cheek and her mother's stubbornness in her jaw. Elias standing at the edge of the light with his arms folded, watching. Old Corvane apart from everyone, but watching too, hungry-looking, like a man outside a window in winter. Faces he didn't know yet—a shepherd woman from Highvale, twins who'd carried their grandmother through the Door between them, a Warden deserter named Sef who'd started following them somewhere on the road and never explained why.

All of them waiting on a boy nobody had remembered existing six months ago.

Pip stood up. His legs felt like borrowed legs. He walked to the front of the Long House and turned around, and the splinter he'd been picking at dug into his palm and he was grateful for it, for the small sharp proof that he was solid.

"I had a name once," he said. His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "Before. In Thornfell village. And then the forgetting took it, and my mum's face, and our house, and I used to stand in the street where our house had been and feel the *hole* of it, like a tooth gone bad." He swallowed. "And then Rosalind told my story back to me on a road that wasn't there, and I learned something. I learned that a name isn't a thing you own. It's a thing other people hold for you. Like a coat. You can lose your own, but if enough people remember you, somebody'll always lend you theirs."

He looked up at the sky, where the first stars were coming out, unhurried, certain.

"So I'm not naming this place after anything new," Pip said. "Because new names are easy to lose. Nobody's roots go down into them yet. I want a name with roots. A name that two hundred people already carry inside them, that they brought through the Door in their pockets along with their seeds and their spoons." He took a breath. "I name this settlement Thornfell."

Silence. Then a sound went through the crowd—not a cheer, not at first, something lower and stranger, a collective exhale, like a wound being dressed. The shepherd woman from Highvale began to cry without any noise at all. One of the twins said, "Thornfell," out loud, testing it, and her sister said it back, and then it moved through the crowd like fire through dry grass, people saying it to each other, handing it back and forth, *Thornfell, Thornfell*, until the whole meadow was murmuring it and the name was no longer Pip's at all. It belonged to everyone. Which was the whole point.

"Countries live in people," Pip said, when he could be heard again. He hadn't planned to say it; it just came up out of him like water from the well. "Not borders. Not wards. Borders are lines on Marta's maps, and maps burn. But as long as we say the name—if our kids say it, and their kids—it doesn't matter what the old country does behind us. We're it. We're Thornfell now. All of us. Even the ones who don't deserve it." His eyes flicked, before he could stop them, toward the figure sitting alone by the stream. "Even him, probably. Ask me again in ten years."

That got a laugh, rough and surprised, and the moment broke the right way, and people started drifting back to their fires talking about a meeting hall and a well and where the schoolhouse should go. Pip stepped down off the crate with his heart hammering and found Rosalind waiting.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"How does it feel? Naming a country."

Pip considered. "Heavy," he said finally. "Like holding a door open. Somebody's always got to do it, and your arm gets tired, but you can't let it slam on the people coming through behind you." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Marta would've said it better."

"No," Rosalind said. "She really wouldn't have." She bumped her shoulder against his, and Bracken, passing, pressed his great scarred head briefly against Pip's chest—the cat equivalent, Pip had learned, of a medal ceremony—and stalked off into the dark after rabbits.

* * *

Elias couldn't sleep.

He walked the edge of the camp while the fires burned down, past the picket line where the mules dozed, past the stream where the water talked to itself in the dark. The badge hung at his throat, a disc of grey steel stamped with the Order's sigil: the tower and the eye. He'd worn it since he was nine years old, since the Wardens had taken a boy from a burning waystation and given him an oath in place of a family. He had believed, for twenty years, that the oath was the family.

Then Corvane had shown him the glass bottles. Then the shrine. Then everything.

He stopped at the stream and took the chain off over his head.

It should have been harder. He'd rehearsed this moment, actually—lying awake in the bothy while Tam faded, imagining the grand renunciation, some speech, maybe. But it wasn't a speech kind of thing. It was quiet, like setting down a pack after a very long march. The badge sat in his palm, catching starlight, and the sigil looked smaller than it used to. Just a stamp. Just a man's idea, pressed into metal, that memory could be owned.

"Elias."

Rosalind. Of course. She moved quietly for a girl raised on smuggling runs; he was getting used to being startled by her. She stopped a few paces off, arms crossed against the cold, and looked at the badge in his hand.

"You heard the boy," Elias said. "Even me. Probably."

"He meant it kindly. For Pip."

"He meant it exactly right, is what he meant." Elias turned the badge over. On the reverse, worn nearly smooth, was the number they'd assigned him at intake instead of a surname. He'd forgotten it existed until tonight. "I've been trying to work out what to do with this. Bury it. Melt it. Throw it in the stream." He crouched. "None of those are right. Throwing it away says it never mattered, and it did matter. It kept me walking straight for twenty years when walking straight was the whole job. It's just—" he exhaled, "—it's finished. The oath ran out. Not because I broke it. Because the thing it pointed at stopped existing. There's no more Thornfell border to ward. There's just people."

"So plant it," Rosalind said.

Elias looked up.

"Here, at the edge of camp," she said, nodding at the ground between the last fire ring and the wild grass. "Badge-down, like a signpost. Let the grass grow over it. So it's not thrown away and it's not worn—it's *planted*. Retired. Doing one last piece of honest work, holding a bit of earth together." A ghost of a smile. "Marta would've approved. She liked things that changed jobs instead of dying."

Elias knelt. He worked the badge into the soil edge-first, pressing it down with the heel of his hand until only a sliver of grey showed, like a tooth of stone. Then he stood, and brushed off his knees, and discovered that his chest felt strange—lighter and emptier at once, the way a room feels after the furniture's gone.

"What will you do?" Rosalind asked. "The Order won't forget you. Corvane's broken, but the Order isn't. They'll send someone, eventually, to see what happened to their harvest."

"Let them come," Elias said. "They'll find a valley with no borders to drain and two hundred people who know their own names. And they'll find me." He rolled his shoulders, feeling the old trained strength settle into its new shape—not a weapon anymore, or not only. "I was a Warden because I wanted to guard something. Turns out I still do. It's just smaller now, and closer, and it sleeps in the Long House and steals honey." He glanced toward the Long House, where Tam's snoring was audible even from here, deep and steady and gloriously alive. "I'll build. I'll teach the young ones what I know about watching the passes. And when the Order comes asking, I'll tell them the truth: that you can't harvest a country that lives in people. You'd have to take us one at a time, and we'd have to let you."

Rosalind studied him for a long moment. "My mother trusted you," she said. "Bracken told me. She said you were the only Warden who ever asked her questions instead of writing answers down."

Elias's throat tightened. "She deserved better than me," he managed. "Than all of us."

"She got you," Rosalind said simply. "In the end, she got you. That counts." She touched his shoulder once, brief and hard, and walked back toward the fires.

Elias stayed a while longer by the stream. When he finally turned in, he passed the planted badge, and in the starlight the sliver of grey was already gathering a fine dust of soil, beginning its slow disappearance into useful ground. He nodded to it, the way you nod to a colleague at the end of a shift.

Then he went to sleep, and for the first time in twenty years, he dreamed of nothing but morning.

* * *

Pip woke before dawn to shouting—but happy shouting, which took him a confused moment to parse—and stumbled out of the Long House into cold gold light.

The wolves had come down in the night. Six of them, lean and winter-thin, standing at the meadow's edge just beyond the picket line, and half the camp was awake and armed and Rosalind was pushing through the crowd saying *wait, wait, wait*, and Bracken was already out there, sitting calm as a fencepost in front of the pack like a small striped ambassador.

"They're not raiding," Rosalind called back, laughing, breath steaming. "They're—hold on—" She cocked her head, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, and her face did something complicated. "They're from the other side of the mountains. From the old country. They followed the scent of the herd animals through the Door." She turned to the crowd, eyes shining. "They're asking permission to stay. They say—" she listened again, and her voice softened, "—they say the old country has gone quiet and green and asleep, and they were lonely."

Pip stood in the doorway with the whole waking camp around him, and watched Rosalind kneel in the frost-gold grass and hold out her hand to a wolf, and watched the wolf—after a long, long moment—step forward and touch its nose to her fingers.

Thornfell's first morning was starting. There was so much to do: a well to dig, a schoolhouse to site, a marker to carve with a name two hundred people already knew by heart. Pip pulled on his boots and went to join it.

But at the edge of hearing, so faint he might have imagined it, he thought he felt the seam of the Door up the hillside give a small, contented sigh—like a sleeper turning over, dreaming of gardens.
