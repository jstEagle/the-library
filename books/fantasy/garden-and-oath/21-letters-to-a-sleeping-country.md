## 21. Letters to a Sleeping Country

The first letter took me three tries.

I sat on the cold stone of the Door's threshold with the paper on my knees and the wind coming up out of the seam like breath, and I wrote *Dear Thornfell* and then stopped, because I didn't know how to talk to a country. I knew how to talk to mules and crows and one scarred mountain cat who pretended not to listen. Countries were new.

Bracken solved it for me, in his way. He was sprawled across my feet in the sun that slanted down into the valley—real sun now, spring sun, the kind that made the snow on the far peaks blush pink—and he said without opening his eyes: "You're overthinking it. She never thought about it either."

"She?"

"Your mother. When she sang to the Door." He stretched one paw, claws pricking my boot, then sheathing them again, an old cat's lazy punctuation. "She just talked. Told it about her day. The weather. What Tam's mother said at market. She treated it like a neighbor, not a monster."

So I crossed out *Dear Thornfell* and started again.

*I saw a fox today on the ridge above the camp,* I wrote. *Red as a coal. The children chased it until it got bored of them and vanished like smoke. Pip swears it winked.*

That was better. That was true. And truth, I had learned, was the only food the thing beneath the mountain could digest without going strange.

* * *

The valley beyond the Door had no name when we came through it. It had grass, a river running cold and quick with snowmelt, stands of pine on the shoulders of hills that rolled south toward a horizon none of us could claim. Marta would have known its name. Marta had known every name. But Marta was a map folded shut now, buried under a cairn on the col where the crows had come for us, and so the naming fell to a boy everyone had forgotten, which was, Pip said later, the funniest joke the world had ever played.

He named it Thornfell. Of course he did.

Some of the older refugees argued. Tam, mostly, once he was on his feet again—the Warden medicine Elias carried had pulled him back from the fading cold by inches, and he'd spent his recovery being insufferable about it. "You can't just carry the name over," he'd said, gesturing with his good arm at the valley. "Thornfell has an inn with a crooked door. Thornfell has the well that remembers water wrong. This is—" he'd waved vaguely, "—grass."

"It's ours," Pip had said, twelve years old and immovable, "and countries live in people, not borders. Rosalind says."

I hadn't said it first. Bracken had, one night on the road that wasn't there, in his flat growl, while I lay awake listening to the wind try to remember how to be wind. But I'd said it enough times since that it had become mine, the way borrowed things become yours if you carry them long enough.

So the valley was Thornfell now. Second of its name. And we built.

* * *

The schoolhouse went up in the second summer, because the children needed somewhere to be, and because I needed somewhere to put what I knew before it curdled into secrecy again. That was the lesson of my whole life, I think—my mother's gift hidden in a cellar, my gift hidden under my tongue, and look what hiding had bought us: a sealed Door, a starving dark, a country eating itself. Secrets kept alone go rancid. Secrets shared stay bread.

So I taught them to listen.

Not all of them could hear the way I heard. Most never would. But every child can learn to be quiet properly—to sit still in the grass until the rabbits decide you're furniture, to watch a crow's shoulder before it turns its head, to know the difference between a dog barking at strangers and a dog barking at nothing, which is never nothing. And two or three in each class, every season, would go still in a way that made the hair rise on my arms, and look at me with their mouths open, and say, *Miss Vasquez, the goat says the storm's coming early,* and be right.

Elias helped, though he'd deny it. He stood at the back of the schoolhouse the first month with his arms crossed like a man guarding prisoners, and then one gray afternoon a girl named Sera—eight years old, born after the wards fell, the first child of the new Thornfell—tugged his sleeve and said, "Warden, your horse is lonely," and something in his face came unfastened. After that he taught the older ones trail-craft and how to read weather, and he never once mentioned the Order, except once, to me, by the river.

"I keep waiting for them to come," he said. "Corvane's people. Someone from the Chapterhouse. It's been four years."

"And?"

"And I've decided to stop waiting standing up." He turned a stone over in his hands, looking at the underside of it like it might have writing. "I planted my badge, Rosalind. I meant it. But a man doesn't stop being a wall just because he walks away from the garrison. I keep listening for boots."

"You should teach them to listen too," I said. "For boots. For anything. Fear shared stays small."

He gave me one of his rare almost-smiles. "You sound like your mother."

"I'm told that constantly," I said, "by a cat."

* * *

Which brings me back to the letters, because the letters were Bracken's idea too, though he'd never admit that either.

It happened the first winter after we came through. Snow came down soft and clean on the new valley—not the forgetting snow, never that, just ordinary winter doing honest work—and I dreamed badly for three nights running. Dreams of the seam of starlight in the rock, of the hunger coiled beneath Mount Cael, patient as frost. In the dreams it wasn't raging. It was waiting. And waiting things, I knew, could turn.

On the fourth morning I packed a satchel—bread, a flask of Tam's terrible plum brandy, paper and ink—and walked up through the pass alone. Bracken fell into step beside me before I'd cleared the treeline, because of course he did. He had been bound to guard the Door since before I was born. He did not consider my trips there optional company.

"Why are we going?" he asked, in the tone he used for questions he already knew the answers to.

"To check the seal."

"The seal held while you slept. It will hold while you stand next to it. Your feet are not load-bearing, girl."

"Then why am I dreaming about it?"

He was quiet for half a mile, which for Bracken was a speech. Then: "Because it's hungry. Quieter than it was—your oath-keeping feeds it, the telling, the naming—but quiet hunger is still hunger. And your mother's seal was a lid, not a cure. Lids rattle."

At the threshold I knelt and put my palm against the stone, and felt it: the faint pulse beneath, the seam of starlight dimmed but present, the vast slow attention on the other side turning toward me like a sunflower toward light. Not malevolent. I'd learned that much. A country's fear of being forgotten, gone ravenous in the dark. It wanted, the way the abandoned want. Wanting that size could crack mountains.

So I did the only thing I could think of. I sat down on the cold stone, opened the satchel, and read aloud.

I told it about Sera and the horse. About Pip's fox. About Tam complaining that the new valley's plums made worse brandy than the old ones, and Marta's cairn wearing its first cap of snow, and Elias planting beans of all things, a Warden of the realm bent double in a garden patch. I told it the names of everyone in camp, one by one, slowly, the way you feed someone spoonful by spoonful. And when I ran out of news I told it older things—Marta's roads, the well that remembered water wrong, the innkeeper's crooked door—until my voice went rough and the light through the pass had gone amber and long.

When I finished, the pulse beneath the stone had slowed. Evened. Like a frightened animal breathing itself calm against your hand.

"There," Bracken said, curling around my knees. "Now you understand the job."

"That's it? That's the great oath? Reading the mail to a hole in a mountain?"

"Your mother sang. You gossip. Every keeper works with what she has." He yawned hugely, showing teeth that had ended crows. "Do it every season. Solstices. Hunger forgets itself when it's fed on schedule. Ask any barn cat."

* * *

Four years of solstices now.

Summer letters and winter ones. The summer letters were easy—harvest counts, weddings, the year the river trout came back and half the camp stood in the shallows shouting like fools. The winter letters were harder, because winter was when the dreams came, when the pulse under the stone beat a little fast, when the hunger remembered it had once been everything and been left alone with the dark. Winter letters I wrote slower. Winter letters I read twice.

I kept them all, or tried to. The first few blew away—one memorable solstice a gust took the entire page mid-sentence and Pip, who had insisted on coming that year, chased it down the scree slope swearing with a fluency I chose not to correct. After that I weighted them with stones, tucked them into the crevice beside the seam, and let the mountain keep its mail. Some winters I'd find last year's letter still there, ink faded to ghost-gray, and some winters the seam had taken them, and I chose to believe that was eating, not losing.

Tam came with me most years now. He called it "running one last job—delivery to a very difficult client." He'd sit on the threshold drinking his own bad brandy and heckling gently while I read, and somehow the letters got better when he was there, funnier, more alive, because the hunger didn't only need comfort. It needed proof of life. Proof that memory, shared aloud, grows instead of wearing thin.

Pip came every single time. He was sixteen now, tall as Tam, still carrying his own name like a lantern he refused to set down. He always read the same closing line himself, in his cracking voice, leaning close to the seam:

"My name is Pip. You remember me. Nobody else did, and you do, and that means remembering is a thing people choose."

The pulse always slowed after that line. Every time. I checked.

* * *

This autumn, the children asked to come.

Sera led the delegation, of course—she was twelve now and had the governing instincts of a much larger animal. They stood in front of the schoolhouse in a row, seven of them, scrubbed and solemn, and Sera announced: "We want to hear the Door."

"It's a long walk," I said.

"We walk long walks."

"It's cold up there."

"We own coats."

"Bracken will complain."

"He complains anyway," said the boy Joss, who heard goats. "Miss Vasquez, please. You say the country's asleep, not dead. Sleeping things wake up mean if nobody talks to them while they dream. My gran said so about my grandad."

I looked at Bracken, sprawled in the schoolhouse doorway, golden in the afternoon light, one ear tipped toward the children the way it tipped toward everything.

"Well?" I said.

"They should come," he said. "The telling shouldn't live in one throat. One throat is how you get another lid." He opened one eye. "Also I require the small one who scratches correctly."

So they came. Seven children and a Warden-turned-gardener and a fence with a limp and a boy with a lantern-name and a cat who complained the entire way up the pass about the pace, the mud, the modern decline of youth. At the threshold I handed out the letters—each child had written one, dictated to me or scratched out themselves in pencil, messages to a sleeping country: drawings of the schoolhouse, lists of favorite foods, Sera's careful account of a lamb born during a thunderstorm that the ewe named Thunder despite Sera's objections.

And one by one they knelt at the seam and read, or recited, or in Joss's case simply pressed both palms to the stone and *listened*, eyes wide, reporting afterward in a whisper: "It's big. It's sad. It's not scary, Miss Vasquez. It's just lonely."

"Yes," I said, and found my voice had gone rough. "Yes, that's exactly it."

The pulse beneath the mountain slowed like a sleeping child's breathing. Above us, through the high gap of the pass, the evening stars were coming out—real stars, the sky's own, returned to us the night the Door opened onto spring. But the seam in the rock still held its own faint starlight, a piece of sky kept safe in stone, and I thought: *we'll bring you back someday. Not open you. Wake you. There's a difference, and we've learned it.*

* * *

We camped that night below the pass, and I woke near dawn to Bracken's weight settling against my side, which he only did when he had something to say that didn't fit in daylight.

"You're writing one more letter tonight," he said. "I can tell. You've got the face."

"I always have the face."

"This one's different." He looked up at the pale sky, where the last stars were dissolving into blue. "Solstice is past. The feeding's done. Whatever you write next isn't for the hunger."

I didn't answer right away. Down the slope, the camp was stirring—smoke from the fire, Tam's voice raised in complaint about porridge, Sera already hauling Joss out of his blanket by the ankle. Four years of building. A schoolhouse with my name chalked on the slate. A valley wearing an old country's name like a coat cut for someone bigger, waiting for the wearer to grow.

"Every year," I said finally, "the seam feels a little less like a wound and a little more like a window. This year, when I put my hand on it, I swear I felt something on the other side. Not the hunger. Under the hunger. Older. Patient in a different way." I looked at him. "Green, Bracken. It felt green."

His whiskers twitched. For a long moment the only sound was the wind coming down off Mount Cael, and under the wind—if I wasn't imagining it, and I was learning to trust that I wasn't—a thread of melody, half-familiar, rising from the deep places like sap in spring.

A sealing song, sung slow. Sung like a welcome.

"Your mother's voice?" I whispered.

"No," Bracken said softly, and stood, and shook the frost from his coat. "Yours. It's time you went and met it."
