## 22. Garden and Oath

The Door had grown a handle.

That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, standing before the seam of starlight in the living rock of Mount Cael with her pack on her shoulder and frost in her beard-shadowed breath—no, not beard; she'd never managed one, though Tam teased her about it every winter. A handle. A loop of pale wood grown straight out of the stone, smooth as driftwood, shaped exactly for a hand like hers.

Years ago it had been only a seam, something you pressed your palm against and pushed and hoped. Now it waited, patient as a well, as if the mountain had spent all this time learning what doors were supposed to be.

"You're staring," Bracken said. He sat beside her on the threshold stone, gray-muzzled now, his scar pulling at one eye when he squinted up at her. "You've been staring for an hour. I counted."

"Cats can't count."

"Mountain cats count excellently. We simply choose not to, as a matter of dignity." He rose, stretched until every joint popped, and butted his head against her knee—the old greeting, unchanged since the night he'd first spoken to her in full sentences and turned her whole life inside out. "Go on, then. You didn't walk three days through the pass to admire the carpentry."

She touched the handle. It was warm.

* * *

She went through alone.

That had been the argument at the camp—gentle, worn smooth by years of practice, the way all their arguments were now. Tam wanted to come. Pip wanted to come worse than anyone, because Pip had been born on the wrong side of a border that forgot him, and no one who'd been erased ever quite stopped wanting to see the place that tried to swallow him. Even Elias, gray at the temples, his Warden badge long since buried under the schoolhouse floor where it could do more good as a foundation than a symbol—even Elias had stood there with his arms crossed and said, "I could carry the tools."

"No," Rosalind had told them, the same way she'd told them every solstice for nine years. "This one's mine. The last one. After this, anyone can come."

They'd let her go because they understood something she'd taken years to learn: some inheritances have to be carried the whole way by hand, or they don't take root.

So she stepped through the seam, and the starlight closed over her like water, and she came out the other side into—

Green.

She stopped so suddenly she nearly fell over her own feet.

Thornfell had been snow when she left it. Thornfell had been snow for nine years—a clean, deep, dreaming snow, the country sleeping under white while its people built a new life in the valley beyond, carrying it inside them the way you carry a song. Every solstice she came back through the Door and walked the white roads and read her letters aloud to the sleeping land, and every year the snow was exactly as she'd left it. Patient. Preserved. A country held between pages.

Now she stood ankle-deep in grass.

It lapped at the Door's threshold like a tide coming in. Wild thyme, by the smell of it, and something she didn't recognize—some flower gone from Thornfell since before her mother's time, maybe, blooming back into existence because somewhere in the dark beneath the mountain, the hunger that had once been a country's fear had finished digesting everything they'd fed it and was dreaming the land back together. Green ran down the valley in ribbons. The pines on the lower slopes had put out new growth, bright as struck matches. Somewhere below, water was moving—actually moving, un-frozen, talking to itself over stones.

Rosalind sank down onto the grass and laughed until she cried, and then cried until she laughed, and Bracken, who had followed her through despite explicit instructions, sat down beside her and pretended not to notice either.

"The snow," she managed finally. "Where's the snow?"

"Melting," Bracken said. "What do you think spring is? It's the world remembering warmth. Your letters worked, girl. All those stories you told it, year after year. You taught a sleeping country how to want morning." He sniffed the air, and his whiskers trembled. "And it woke up hungry—but the good kind of hungry. The kind that eats sunlight."

* * *

She walked down through her childhood the way you walk through a house you dreamed once and half remember.

The road to Thornfell village existed again—not Marta's remembered version, inked from a dying woman's devotion, but the road itself, wheel-rutted and real, with foxglove leaning over the walls. The village was empty, of course. Its people lived in the valley now, in the settlement Pip had named, and no one had decided yet whether they would ever come back. That decision, Rosalind suspected, was why she was here. Why the Door had grown a handle. Countries don't wake up on their own schedule. They wait to be invited.

The inn still stood. She pushed open its door and found the common room exactly as forgetting had left it—tables askew, mugs on the boards, a card game abandoned mid-hand—and also not exactly, because moss had come up through the hearthstones and swallows were nesting in the rafters, and the whole room smelled of green things growing through the bones of the old life. On the wall by the bar, someone had scratched words into the plaster long ago: *HODGE KEEPS THE BEST ALE AND THE WORST SECRETS.*

Hodge. The innkeeper who had forgotten his daughter's face on the worst night of the world. Rosalind traced the letters with her finger. His daughter Maren was twenty now, teaching the little ones their letters in the valley, and she had her father's laugh and her grandmother's stubbornness, and when Rosalind got home she was going to tell Hodge that his inn remembered him, that the wall still kept his joke, that the ale barrels in the cellar had held.

That was the work now. Not sealing. Not fleeing. *Telling.* Carrying the country back to itself, name by name, scratch-mark by scratch-mark, until the people and the land recognized each other again.

She heard the wind pick up outside, moving down off the high col where the crows had once ambushed them—two of those crows roosted on the schoolhouse roof these days, fat and opinionated and terrified of nothing—and the wind came through the village streets and it was singing.

Rosalind went very still.

She knew the song. She'd known it since the cave of hibernating bears, since the vision of her mother's voice rising out of beast-memory, a sealing song meant to hold a Door shut and a hunger down. She'd sung it herself at the seam, nine years ago, leading a ragged line of survivors and freed crows and one broken High Warden in speaking a whole country's memories aloud into the light. She'd sung it every solstice since, at the threshold, her voice cracking on the high notes because she'd never been the singer her mother was.

But the song the wind sang was different. The old verses were there—the names, the graves, the roads, Marta's map set to melody—but the ending had changed. The ending that had once been a lock, a warning, a *stay down, stay down, stay down*, had unwound itself into something with a major key. Something that sounded, unmistakably, like *welcome*.

And underneath the wind, threaded through it like a second voice harmonizing, was her mother.

Rosalind stepped out into the street. The wind circled her, tugging at her hair, and the voice in it was warm and tired and amused in the way Rosalind remembered from exactly two memories—one of hands braiding her hair by firelight, one of a kiss pressed to her forehead through the crack of a door, the night her mother went up the mountain and did not come back.

*"You grew,"* the wind said, in the space between one verse and the next. *"I only ever knew you small."*

"I grew," Rosalind said aloud, to the wind, to the green country, to whatever her mother had become when she gave her memory into the seal and let the mountain keep it. "You should see the schoolhouse. You should see Pip—he's taller than me now, and insufferable about it. You should see Elias planting turnips. A Warden, Mother. Turnips."

The wind laughed. It was her mother's laugh, and it broke her heart and mended it in the same breath.

*"Is the oath kept?"*

Rosalind looked around—at the village remembering itself, at the green climbing the slopes of Mount Cael, at the far glint of the Door's seam high above, holding steady, holding warm, no longer a wound in the world but a door in the truest sense, something with two sides.

"It's changing," she said. "The way oaths should. I'm not keeping yours anymore. I'm renewing it. In my own words. That's what you bought time for, isn't it? Someone strong enough to write a new one."

The wind went quiet, listening. Even the swallows hushed.

* * *

She planted the garden that afternoon, at the Door's threshold, because that was where her mother would have planted it and because thresholds are where the important things grow.

She'd brought seeds from the valley in oilcloth packets sewn by Marta's granddaughter—beans and barley and sweet peas and a row of apple pips from the tree that grew over Marta's grave, because Marta would have wanted her map to bear fruit. Rosalind knelt in the thawing earth at the mouth of the Last Door and worked the soil with her bare hands, and Bracken supervised from a sun-warmed rock with the air of a foreman whose crew is finally showing competence.

The earth was good. Nine years of snowmelt and slow dreaming had made it black and rich, and when Rosalind pressed the first seed in, she felt the land lean toward her touch like a cat—like a scarred mountain cat who had chosen her long before she understood she was being chosen.

She talked as she planted. That was the whole ritual, really. That had always been the whole ritual.

"This row is for Hodge, who kept the worst secrets and the best ale, and whose daughter laughs exactly like him. This one's for Tam, who owes me eleven favors and has paid back four, and who smuggled hope across a dead border when hope was the most contraband thing in Thornfell. This one's for Marta, who drew roads that didn't exist until we walked them, which is just another way of saying she made them exist." Her fingers worked. Her voice carried down the valley. "This one's for Pip, who was erased from every memory but his own and refused, absolutely refused, to stop existing, and who named our new home after the one that tried to forget him, because countries live in people, not borders. He said that. Write it down somewhere permanent."

Bracken's tail curled and uncurled. "The mountain is writing it down," he said. "Mountains are excellent at permanent."

"And this row—" Rosalind paused, seed in her palm, apple pip brown and small as a knuckle. "This one's for my mother. Who sealed a Door to buy time, and whose time bought me, and whose song the wind sings now as a welcome instead of a warning. I don't know if you can hear this. But the beasts always could, and you're in the beasts, and you're in the wind, and you're in the dirt, so I'm going to say it anyway: I'm not afraid of you anymore. I never should have been. You weren't a warning. You were a hand-me-down."

She pressed the pip into the earth and covered it, and the wind came down off the col and passed over the garden once, gently, the way a hand passes over a sleeping child's hair, and the sealing song finished its verse on a note that sounded like consent.

Then Rosalind stood, brushed the soil from her knees, and spoke the oath.

Not her mother's words. Her own. She'd been drafting them for nine years in the backs of ledgers and the margins of maps, and she knew them the way she knew her own heartbeat:

*"I am Rosalind Vasquez, daughter of Thornfell, speaker to beasts, keeper of the Door. I swear to remember this country out loud—to tell its stories until the telling and the land are the same thing. I swear that no one in my care will be forgotten while I have breath, and that nothing under this mountain will be starved of memory while I have voice. What my mother locked away, I will feed. What my mother bought with her silence, I will spend on songs. And when the last of us walks out under returning stars, we will carry Thornfell inside us so completely that wherever we plant a garden, there Thornfell is. This is my garden. This is my oath. Grow."*

The ground under her hands shivered—just once, softly, like a sleeper turning over—and every seed she'd planted sent up a green shoot before her eyes, fast as a struck match, unfurling leaves in the starlight that was just beginning to show through the evening sky.

Because that was the other thing. She looked up.

The stars were back. Had been back for years beyond the border, but here, over Thornfell, they were arriving one by one like guests taking their seats—the same stars that had flickered and died the night the wards snapped, filling back in overhead, constellations reassembling themselves out of shared memory. The Bear. The Harp. The ones Marta had named and the ones the dwarves had carved glowing into the ruins beneath the mountain. The sky was remembering itself, and it had started here, at the threshold, above the garden.

"They're waiting for you," Bracken said quietly. He'd come to stand beside her, old bones and old scars silhouetted against the growing dark. "In the valley. The tall boy is pretending he wasn't watching the pass all day. The fence is cooking something he'll claim was your favorite. The ex-Warden is mending a roof that doesn't need mending because his hands get restless when he worries." He looked up at her, one eye bright, one eye clouded with age, both of them fond. "Nine years you've made this walk alone. The Door has a handle now. Spring is here. The country knows its own name."

Rosalind took a long breath of Thornfell air—thyme and pine and thaw and woodsmoke drifting faintly from a world away—and felt the last knot inside her, the one she hadn't even known was tied, come loose.

"One more night," she said. "I want to sleep under the stars here, just once, in the country that kept me. Then we go home."

"We?" Bracken's whiskers twitched. "You're claiming me now?"

"I've claimed you since the pass, you impossible cat. You just took nine years to notice."

He huffed—the laugh he'd deny to his dying day—and settled himself against her side as she spread her bedroll among the fresh-turned rows, and above them the stars came out in their hundreds and their thousands, and the wind moved through the young garden singing her mother's song, and the last line of it, the new line, the welcome, followed Rosalind down into sleep:

*Come home. Come home. The garden is planted. The door stands open. Come home.*

And far to the south, past the pass, past the col, in a valley full of lamplight and cooking smoke, a boy who had once been erased from the whole world looked up from his chores at the mountains, and smiled, and called the others outside to watch the sky over old Thornfell fill with stars.
