## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a room with no windows, because windows let in weather and weather let in doubt. Ansel Quintana had worked there for six years, and in that time she had learned three things: that ink was cheaper than blood but cost more in the end; that the guild's ledgers never lied, only waited; and that the safest place in any building was the one where nobody looked.

She was very good at not being looked at.

Her desk sat in the third alcove of the Cataloguing Hall, between a woman who appraised bottled storms and a man who had once tried to sell his own shadow and been rejected on grounds of quality. From dawn bell to dusk bell, Ansel transcribed. That was the work. That was all the work. The guild acquired impossible things—bottled tides, borrowed years, the last word of a drowned man sealed in amber—and someone had to write them down before they could be owned. Writing made a thing real. Writing made a thing *theirs*.

Ansel wrote, therefore things were theirs.

"Quintana." The head cataloguer, Mistress Vell, stopped at her alcove without slowing, the way she always did, dropping a ledger onto the desk like a stone into still water. "Lot forty-one came in overnight. Salt from a sea that no longer exists. Full provenance required by noon."

"Yes, Mistress."

"And your hand is slipping. Page twelve of yesterday's folio. The descender on your *g* wavered."

Ansel kept her eyes on the fresh ledger. "It won't again."

"It had better not." Vell moved on. Her footsteps had the particular rhythm of a woman who counted everything, including steps.

Ansel opened lot forty-one and did not think about page twelve, or rather she thought about it the way you think about a splinter—constantly, uselessly, from every angle that offered no relief. Her handwriting was the one thing she owned outright. Not her name—the guild held that, collateral against her father's debts, a debt so old it had passed through two deaths and arrived at her like an inheritance nobody wanted. Not her time—the guild bought her days at a rate they set and she signed for. But her hand, the small disciplined architecture of her letters: that was hers. When Vell said it was slipping, what she meant was *you are slipping*, and they both knew it, and neither of them would say it, because saying things aloud was how people became interesting, and interesting people got audited.

So Ansel bent over the salt.

It was good salt, as impossible salts went. Gray-pink, coarse, smelling faintly of a storm that had happened before her grandmother was born. She recorded its weight, its color, its provenance—a sea called the Vellimir, drained in the Year of the Dry Quill—and entered it into the master catalogue in her perfect hand:

*One (1) measure, salt of the Vellimir Sea (extinct). Acquired from the estate of —*

She paused. The estate line was blank. The acquisition record showed no seller, no price, no chain of custody. The salt had simply appeared in the guild's vault, the way some things did, the way the guild preferred not to discuss.

*Acquired from the estate of unrecorded origin,* she wrote, and felt the small familiar shame of it. Somewhere in this building there was a rule for everything except the things that mattered.

* * *

At midday she ate her bread in the alcove, as she always did, and catalogued the morning's remaining lots between bites. A jar of borrowed years—someone's childhood, thirty-one days of it, sold to pay a gambling debt and now resold twice, the contents gone thin and gray like wine poured too often. A tide in a bottle, corked with wax stamped by a harbor authority that had burned down a century ago; when she tilted it, something inside pressed against the glass like a cheek. A letter that delivered itself, addressed to a woman who had died in the plague year, still trying, arriving every morning at a door that no longer existed, until the guild had bought the door too, just to give the letter somewhere to go.

Ansel wrote them all down. *One letter, self-delivering, addressee deceased.* Her pen did not shake. Her *g* descended straight as a plumb line.

This was the trick of surviving the guild: you let the strange things pass through you without catching on anything inside. You did not wonder who had sold their childhood or why. You did not tilt the bottle more than once. You wrote the entry, closed the book, and went home to your rented room above the chandler's, where nothing was impossible at all, only small and quiet and yours.

Except tonight, going home was not what happened.

* * *

Dusk bell rang, and the hall emptied the way water empties—all at once and then gradually. Ansel stayed to finish lot fifty-three, a minor item, a key that unlocked whatever it was pointed away from. She was blotting the final line when she heard footsteps behind her, unhurried, and turned expecting Vell with another correction.

It was not Vell.

He stood at the mouth of her alcove holding a clay jar against his chest with both arms, the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken. He was salt-flat born—you could tell by the sun-cracks at the corners of his eyes and the white dust that never fully left the creases of his knuckles. He wore a peddler's coat gone soft at the elbows, and he looked at her the way people look at a counter they've approached a hundred times and a person they've never once seen.

"You're the writer," he said.

"I'm a cataloguer."

"That's what I said." He came forward without asking, which nobody did, and set the jar on her desk beside the ledger. It was plain clay, unglazed, stoppered with a twist of cloth. Through the neck she could see salt—coarse, gray-white, ordinary. Completely ordinary. That was the first wrong thing about it. Everything else in this room was impossible. This jar was just salt.

"There's no entry slip," she said automatically. "I can't accept an item without—"

"I'm not giving it to the guild."

"Then I can't—"

"It's for you." He said it simply, the way he might have said *it's raining*. "You look like someone who hasn't been given anything in a long time."

Ansel stared at him. Six years in this hall, and in all that time no one had brought her anything that wasn't a lot number. She opened her mouth to explain the rules—no personal items on guild property, no undocumented goods, no conversations longer than the transaction required—and found that the rules had all gone somewhere and left her standing there without them.

"What's your name?" she asked instead.

"Wren."

"Wren what?"

"Just Wren. Salt doesn't have two names." He almost smiled. There was dust in the lines around his mouth too. "You won't find me in any book. I checked. I checked before I came, actually. Wanted to be sure."

"Sure of what?"

"That you'd have to write me down yourself." He tapped the jar once, lightly, with one finger. "Or not. Your choice. That's the whole point of it. Nobody's ever given you a choice, have they? Everything about you is already written somewhere. Your debt, your hours, your hand." He glanced at the ledger, at her perfect descending letters, and something crossed his face—not pity, exactly. Recognition. "I thought maybe you'd want one thing that isn't."

"The guild will count it," she said. "Anything in this room gets counted. If it has no entry, they'll make one, and then it's theirs, and then—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing how much she knew about how things became owned.

"Then don't put it on the desk," Wren said. He was already backing toward the aisle, hands empty now, lighter. "Put it under. Put it anywhere they don't look. You're good at places they don't look. I watched you for a while before I worked up the nerve."

"You *watched*—"

"Market days. You buy bread at Ferro's stall and you never haggle, even though you could, even though you count better than he does. You walk home along the canal and you stop at the second bridge, every time, and look at the water for exactly as long as it takes to feel like a person, and then you stop feeling like one and go home." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, the dust at his eyes crinkling. "I sell salt. I notice what people are missing. Salt shows you what's missing—that's half the trade. It draws out the flavor of the thing that isn't there anymore."

"That's not what salt does."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "But it's what I do." At the aisle's mouth he paused, and for a moment the lamplight caught him full, and Ansel saw that under the peddler's coat and the flat-born squint he was younger than she'd guessed, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Eat well this winter, writer. That's good salt. Best I ever pulled."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the long hall, past the vaults, out through the door that only opened outward after dusk, and Ansel sat alone in the third alcove with a jar of ordinary salt and no entry in any book.

* * *

She should have reported it. That was procedure. Undocumented goods, found on guild property, were logged, appraised, and absorbed. She should have carried the jar to Vell's desk and said *a seller left this, no provenance, please advise*, and by tomorrow it would be lot fifty-four and hers never at all.

Instead she sat in the darkening hall and turned the jar in her hands. The clay was warm. That was the second wrong thing—clay cooled fast, everyone knew that, and this jar was warm as if it had been held against a chest for a long walk across a city, which, she supposed, it had.

She pulled the cloth stopper and smelled it. Salt, yes—but under the salt, something else. The Vellimir sea in lot forty-one had smelled of a dead storm. This smelled of a living one. Hot wind off flats, brine drying on wood, the particular mineral breath of shallow water shrinking in summer. It smelled like a place she had never been and yet her chest tightened as if she were remembering it, which was impossible, and she was a cataloguer of impossible things, and she knew exactly what the guild would call that feeling.

*Unprovenanced memory. Value: negligible. Disposition: absorb.*

She stoppered the jar. She looked at her ledger, at the waiting blank line beneath lot fifty-three, at her pen lying parallel to it like a thing asleep.

Here was the truth she had built six years of perfect handwriting on top of: everything she was existed in someone else's book. Her father's debt lived in the guild's great folio. Her hours lived in the attendance rolls. Her name—she had checked once, years ago, drunk on stolen wine and self-pity—her name appeared in eleven separate records in this building, and in none of them was she anything but a sum outstanding. Ansel Quintana, balance remaining. Ansel Quintana, interest accrued. Nowhere: Ansel Quintana, person, who liked the second bridge and hated haggling and had not been given a gift since she was nine years old.

Wren had walked into the one room in the world where everything becomes a record, and left the one thing in it that wasn't written down.

And he had told her to hide it. He had looked at her—actually looked, the way nobody looked, the way she hadn't known she wanted to be looked at until this moment, which was its own kind of wound—and he had said *you're good at places they don't look*, and he had meant it as a kindness.

That was the part that cracked her. Not the gift. The accuracy.

Ansel slid the jar off the desk and into the drawer beneath, the one where she kept her own pens, her own ink, the few objects the guild had never bothered to claim because they had never bothered to look. She closed the drawer softly. Then she picked up her pen, drew the master ledger toward her, and wrote, in her most flawless hand, the lie that would keep it safe:

*No further lots received this date.*

Her *g* descended perfectly straight. She looked at it for a long moment, the first crooked thing she had ever made on purpose, and understood that she had just done something the guild had no column for.

Outside, the mountain stood black against the stars, the way it always did, wearing its court of kept things somewhere in its deeps like a stone wears a fossil. Ansel did not know yet that Wren's name was already in a book down there. She did not know that hers was coming.

She only knew that for the first time in six years, something in her life had no entry—and that she had chosen, with her own perfect hand, to keep it that way.

She blew out the lamp and went home along the canal, and at the second bridge she stopped, and looked at the water, and took as long as she liked.
