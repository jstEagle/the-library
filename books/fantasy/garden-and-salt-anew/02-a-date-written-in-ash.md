## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The folio arrived at my desk at the hour when the guild's lamps burn lowest and the ink in every well goes slightly thick, as if even it is tired of being asked to mean things.

It came wrapped in oilcloth and chained shut, which was my first warning. Guild property travels by basket, by runner, by whatever junior clerk can be bribed with a warm bun. It does not travel in chains. The second warning was the note pinned to the cloth, written in the Guildmaster's own hand—a hand I knew from a hundred rejection letters, all of them addressed to other people's petitions and none, ever, to me:

*Restore. Do not read.*

I sat with that for a long moment. Restoration *is* reading. You cannot mend a page you have not looked at; you cannot stabilize ink you have not identified. Whoever wrote this note either did not know my trade or knew it perfectly and wanted, very specifically, for me to hold the book without understanding it.

Both possibilities were insulting. Only one of them was interesting.

I broke the seal anyway. Of course I did. A woman who spends her days cataloging bottled tides and borrowed years develops a certain professional relationship with the word *forbidden*. We are old acquaintances. We nod to each other across crowded rooms.

Inside the oilcloth was a folio bound in grey leather gone soft as ear skin, its corners worn round by a century of careful hands. The guild's mark was stamped on the cover, but faintly—stamped, I thought, the way you stamp something you'd rather not claim too loudly. No title. No catalog number. Just a small brass plate on the spine with a single letter worn nearly smooth: **P**.

Prophecy. The guild kept exactly one such collection, and it was not supposed to exist. We dealt in impossible *goods*, not impossible *knowledge*. Bottled weather, yes. A widow's second chance, decanted and stoppered, yes. But foreknowledge was temple territory, temple law, temple terror. The Guild of Impossible Things had survived two hundred years precisely by never touching what the temples called *the third theft*—stealing not from a person, but from time itself.

And yet here it was on my desk, chained and breathing dust.

I cleared a space. I laid out my tools: bone folder, soft brushes, the little pot of wheat-starch paste I mix myself because the guild's paste smells of vinegar and lies. I worked under lamplight while the tower slept, and the first hour was ordinary enough—rehumidifying brittle leaves, flattening a cockled margin, lifting a stain of ancient candle grease with a warmed spatula. My hands know this work the way Wren's hands used to know the weight of a full salt jar. The body remembers its usefulness even when the ledger doesn't.

That's what I told myself, anyway, right up until I turned to the gathering near the center of the book and saw that the pages there had been written in ash.

Not ink. Ash. Someone had ground charcoal fine as flour and written with it in a hand so controlled it made mine look like a child's scrawl—each letter placed as if it had been set there with tweezers. And the entries were not prophecies in the temple style, all thunder and seven-headed beasts. They were bookkeeping. Dates. Names. Amounts. A ledger, written forward instead of backward.

*Third frost, 99 years past: the cooper's daughter drowns. Paid in full.*

*Midwinter, 97 years past: the bridge at Vell takes the flood. Two hundred souls, entered as one line item.*

Page after page. A century of deaths, recorded before they happened, in the flat, patient voice of someone doing arithmetic. I restored three pages before I let myself understand what I was restoring. My brushes moved. My mind stood very still, the way you stand still when you realize the floor of your house has been hollow underneath you all along.

Then I reached the leaf dated for tonight.

I want to say I knew immediately. I didn't. I noticed the date the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've heard a thousand times—something in me flinched before my mind caught up. Third frost. Tonight's date, rendered in the old calendar, the one the guild still uses for contracts because the new one keeps getting revised by governments that don't last.

My eyes went to the entry beside it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

*Third frost, this year: the soul beneath the mountain dies. Collected by the court. Witnessed by —*

And then my own name.

**Ansel Quintana.**

Ink—ash—dried a hundred years before I was born, and it spelled me out in letters more perfect than any I have ever managed. Not *Ansel Q.* Not a guess, not a common name held up against chance. *Ansel Quintana*, whole and entire, in a book that predated my grandmother's grandmother.

The lamp guttered. Or I did. I honestly could not tell you which.

Here is the thing about living invisibly: you get to believe it's a skill. That you have made yourself small through discipline, through good handwriting, through never asking for anything loud enough to be denied. I had built an entire life on that belief—the way a snail builds a shell out of its own back and calls it architecture. Keep your head down. Keep your lines straight. Nothing sees you, and therefore nothing can take from you.

But invisibility only works if no one is *watching*. And a prophecy is watching. A prophecy had been watching since before I existed, sitting in a chained folio in a cellar of the guild that owned my debt, recording my name beside a death with the same unbothered patience it gave to floods and drowned coopers' daughters.

I had never been invisible. I had been *filed*.

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough for the lamp to need trimming twice. Long enough for my hands, which had been steady through ten thousand restorations, to leave a thumbprint of sweat on a page older than the mountain road. When I finally breathed, it came out sounding like someone else.

"Close the book."

The voice came from the doorway, and I did not startle, which surprised me. Perhaps there is a limit to how much a person can be startled in one night. Head Archivist Maren stood in the frame with a candle she hadn't lit, holding it like a club she'd forgotten the use of. She is older than the east wing and shaped by the same weather. She had been kind to me once, years ago, over a mislabeled crate of bottled fog. She was not kind now. She was worse than not kind. She was afraid.

"You were told not to read," she said.

"The note said restore."

"Restoration is reading." She crossed the room—not quickly, archivists never move quickly, we simply arrive—and took the folio from my desk with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping animal. "Which is why the note was written for you and no one else. Because everyone else would have read it and understood nothing. You—" she stopped. Looked at the open page. Looked at my face. Something in her expression folded closed, like a ledger snapping shut.

"You found your name," she said. Not a question.

"It's tonight," I said. My voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside me. "There's an entry for tonight. There's a death, and there's my name next to it, and the ink is a century old, and nobody—" I heard my own voice climb and hauled it back down. Nobody had told me. Nobody had *warned* me, except a note that said do not read, which is a warning shaped like an invitation, and we both knew it.

Maren set the folio down on my desk again, gently, and turned it so the cover faced me. As if I needed reminding what I'd touched.

"When I was young," she said, "a scribe found her name in this book. Do you know what happened to her?"

"No."

"No one does. That's the answer. No one does." She lit her candle at last, and the small flame made her face look carved. "She was erased. Not killed—erased. Every record, every contract, every memory in every head that held her. The guild's books have a hole where she was, and holes in our trade are worse than deaths, girl. A death you can enter in a ledger. An erasure leaves nothing to enter."

The candle flame leaned toward the folio, though there was no draft.

"So close it," Maren said. "Close it, restore the binding, log the hours, and forget the name you saw. That is the only mercy this building offers, and I am offering it to you personally, which I have done perhaps four times in fifty years."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll find out why the book is kept in chains." She paused at the door, and for one moment the fear in her cracked and something older showed through—grief, maybe, or guilt. "Ansel. The entry isn't only a death. Read the marginalia sometime, if you must. The hand that wrote that page knew the person it named. You can tell, in the letters. People write differently about the ones they—" She stopped herself. "Close the book."

She left. The candlelight went with her.

I stood alone with a century-old book and my own name drying in ash, and I did the most dangerous thing available to me, which was nothing. I thought. About the soul beneath the mountain—I had heard the phrase before, in market gossip, in temple warnings, always vague, always *down there somewhere*, the way people speak of things they've agreed not to look at. About the salt-seller who used to bring jars to my desk and never entered them in any book. About the way he'd looked at me last week, like a man reading a letter he already knew the ending of.

About whose handwriting, exactly, would write differently about someone they loved.

I picked up my brush. I turned back to the leaf dated for tonight. And in the margin, in tiny letters I had missed on the first pass—letters that were not ash but true ink, added later, in a hand I almost recognized—

*He sold himself for her debts. She will come down the salt stair. Let her.*

Let *her*.

The lamp went out on its own.
