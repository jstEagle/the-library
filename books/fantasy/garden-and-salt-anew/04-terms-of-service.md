## 4. Terms of Service

The petition took Ansel three days to draft, and she wrote it in her best hand, which was the problem. Every clerk in the Guild of Impossible Things knew that a perfect hand meant a settled mind, and a settled mind had no business petitioning anything.

She wrote it anyway. *Petition for the Voidance of Sale: one soul, male, sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain by Wren Corvane, salt-seller of the Lower Flats, in the year the granaries failed. Grounds for voidance: the sale was made under duress, the debt it cleared was not his own, and the buyer is a court that keeps rather than pays.*

She read it eleven times. On the twelfth reading she noticed she had written *keeps* where she'd meant *kills*, and she left it, because *keeps* was worse and truer, and because she had seen the word *soul* in a book a century old beside tonight's date and her own name, and there was no settling a mind after that.

The Guildmaster's office sat at the top of the guildhouse, above the counting rooms and the archive and the long gallery where impossible goods were cataloged behind glass: bottled tides with their little weather inside, borrowed years coiled like sleeping eels, a jar of last light from a village that no longer existed. Ansel passed them every morning on her way to her desk. She had cataloged most of them herself. She had never once been allowed to touch them without gloves.

The Guildmaster was a small woman named Odile Vane who had held her post for longer than any record could confirm, because the records concerning her own tenure were, per guild policy, sealed. She received petitions standing at her window, looking down into the courtyard, so that petitioners spent the whole interview staring at the back of her head and their own courage.

Ansel stared at both.

"Read it to me," said Odile Vane, without turning.

"I've written it out—"

"The guild trusts its scribes' hands, not their papers. Papers can be forged." A pause. "You would know."

Ansel's throat went dry. She read the petition aloud, all of it, her voice steady until the last line, where *the buyer is a court that keeps rather than pays* came out slightly cracked, like ice on a pond in early spring.

Silence. Below, in the courtyard, an apprentice was wheeling a crate of bottled fog toward the vaults, and the fog pressed against the glass of its bottles like faces.

"You found your name in the Ash Folio," Odile Vane said. It was not a question. The head archivist had reported her; of course he had. "And you have spent four nights since then in the archive, which we permitted, and two days drafting this, which we also permitted. Do you understand why we permitted it?"

"No," Ansel admitted.

"Because a scribe who is hiding something writes carelessly. You have written nothing careless in six years, Quintana. Until now." The Guildmaster turned at last. Her face was unremarkable, gray-eyed, forgettable—the face of a woman who had perhaps once paid the guild to make her so. "Your petition is denied."

"The sale was made under duress—"

"The sale was made under love, which is a stronger duress and a more binding one. The guild does not void sales made in love. We'd be here all century." Odile Vane crossed to her desk, which held nothing but a bell, a knife for cutting quills, and a single sheet of blank paper. "The prophecy guarantees collection. The court beneath the mountain has never defaulted on a debt in nine hundred years. Would you have us break contract law to spare one soul? What would we tell the other souls?"

"They're dead," Ansel said. "The other souls. The prophecy says the soul beneath the mountain dies tonight, and I am named beside it, and instead of telling me what that means, everyone tells me to close the book."

Something moved behind the gray eyes—not sympathy. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman reading a ledger entry she has seen before.

"Yes," the Guildmaster said. "That is the difficulty."

* * *

They walked together through the gallery, past the glass cases, and Odile Vane talked the way bankers talk when they are about to take something from you: slowly, kindly, showing you each step of the arithmetic.

"The court collects tonight. That is fixed—it is written, and what is written in the Ash Folio has never once failed to occur. Not the flood of the Salt Year. Not the burning of the twin abbeys. Not the death of the soul beneath the mountain." She stopped before a case containing a single feather, cataloged as *one wingbeat, unsent*. "But a death clause, Quintana, is only ink. And ink has always had a second function."

"Which is?"

"Transfer." The Guildmaster smiled, and it was almost warm. "A debt recorded can be reassigned. A death recorded can be—amended. But only by the hand that holds the pen at the moment of writing. The final page of the Ash Folio is blank. It has been blank for a hundred years, waiting. Transcription of that page requires a senior hand. A promoted hand."

Ansel felt the shape of the trap before she could see its edges, the way she could feel weather in old scars. "You're offering me a promotion."

"I'm offering you the pen."

"And if I transcribe the page as it comes to me—as it will come to me—"

"Then the prophecy completes itself, the court collects, the guild's contracts hold, and you will be the youngest senior scribe in guild history, with full access to the vaults, the archives, and the sealed records." Odile Vane looked at her gently. "Including, in time, the ones concerning your own family's debt. Which, I think, is what you actually want. Not the salt-seller. The truth about why a salt-seller loved you enough to sell himself."

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Ansel stood very still among the impossible things, the bottled tides ticking softly behind their glass like small trapped storms, and hated how well the woman had read her, and hated more that she was right—that some traitorous part of her did want the sealed records, wanted the whole architecture of her life explained, wanted the ledger balanced and legible even if the balance was against her.

"That's not an offer," she said quietly. "It's a purchase. You're buying my hand."

"I'm promoting it. There's a difference, and the difference is salary." The Guildmaster returned to her window. "You have until sundown. The transcription begins at dusk, in the archive, under seal. Wear gloves."

"And if I refuse?"

Odile Vane did not turn around. "Then you remain a debt-bound junior scribe with an imperfect understanding of her own affairs, and the page gets transcribed anyway, by someone else, and you will spend the rest of your life cataloging goods you may never touch, wondering what was written in a room you weren't allowed to enter." She rang the small bell on her desk, once. "I have found, Quintana, that people rarely refuse. They only negotiate the terms of their own complicity. It's cheaper that way, for everyone."

* * *

Ansel walked back down through the guildhouse as the light went long and amber in the high windows. Past the counting rooms, where clerks murmured numbers like prayers. Past the long gallery, where she stopped, without meaning to, before a case near the end.

Inside, under glass, sat a jar of salt.

It was cataloged in her own handwriting, six years ago, her first week: *Item 4471. One jar sea-salt, provenance unknown, submitted by anonymous donor. No entry in any book. Disposition pending.*

Disposition pending. Six years. She had cataloged everything else in this building—bottled tides, borrowed years, the wingbeat of an unsent letter—and this one jar had sat in limbo the entire time, waiting for someone to decide what it was.

Wren had left it on her desk the day before she found her name in ash. She had told herself it was coincidence, a merchant's sample, a courtesy between acquaintances who had once been children together on the flats. But jars do not sit six years in a guild vault awaiting disposition unless something in them refuses categorization, and she had lifted the lid once, alone, gloved, and smelled not salt but the flats themselves—the hot white glare, the wind off the shallows, a boy's voice calling her name across the crusted pans—and understood that Wren had not sent her seasoning.

He had sent her a receipt. Proof of purchase. *This is what I bought you with. Remember it.*

She pressed her palm flat against the cool glass, and the salt glittered up at her like a field of tiny accusations.

The Guildmaster thought she was negotiating with a scribe's ambition. The temple thought she was negotiating with a scribe's fear. Both of them had read her petitions, her perfect hand, her six years of keeping her head down, and concluded that Ansel Quintana was a woman who wanted to belong to something.

None of them had read the jar.

She thought about the blank page. About a pen offered like a gift, a promotion dressed as mercy, a seat at the table where the writing was done—so long as she wrote what came to her, so long as the prophecy completed itself through her hand and called it duty. They didn't want to save her. They wanted her signature. Complicity, notarized in her own famous hand, was worth more to the guild than any junior scribe's loyalty, because loyalty could waver and a signature could not.

Sundown was coming. The archive waited. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a man who had loved her was being kept, the way the guild kept tides in bottles and years in coils—carefully, permanently, with excellent records.

Ansel took her hand off the glass.

There was a drawer in her desk, third from the top, where she kept the tools of her trade: knives, nibs, sealing wax, the small brass stamps of guild authority. She had spent six years learning to forge nothing and copy everything, and a guild that trusted hands over papers had taught her, incidentally, exactly how hands worked.

She had ledgers. She had writs. She had a merchant stair beneath the mountain, and gates that took tolls, and a memory she was suddenly, coldly willing to spend.

At her desk, she pulled a fresh sheet toward her and uncapped the ink.

She did not write a petition.
