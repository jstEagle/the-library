## 5. Down the Salt Stair

The writ took me four hours and cost me everything I had ever believed about my own honesty.

I wrote it at my desk after the guild emptied, by lamplight I kept low enough that the wardens on their rounds would take me for a scribe finishing a late entry. Which was true. It was only the *entry* that was a lie. I copied the seal from the Guildmaster's writ of collection — the one authorizing the court's annual tithe — tracing it three times on scrap before I trusted my hand. A seal is just calligraphy with authority, and calligraphy is the one thing in this world no one can take from me.

In the ledger of small debts, under impossible goods, I entered: *one writ of passage, merchant class, bearer authorized to descend.* Then, because the books must balance or the books will notice, I entered the price: *paid in full.*

The ink dried black as a closed door.

I told myself the forgery was a technicality. The guild owned my debt; the debt was mine; therefore any instrument signed in my own hand was, in some lawyer's hell, legitimate. The Guildmaster would have laughed at that reasoning. He would have laughed, and then he would have taken my hands.

I folded the writ into my coat, took Wren's salt jar down from the shelf where it had sat for three days like an accusation, and went out into the night to sell the last thing I owned that couldn't be catalogued.

* * *

The merchant stair begins behind the tanneries, where the mountain's root breaks through the street like a knuckle of bone. There is a door in it — there is always a door in it, if you know which grain of stone to knock against — and above the door, cut deep and worn shallow by centuries of traffic, the old trade-tongue: **NOTHING CARRIED DOWN THAT IS NOT PAID FOR.**

Merchants used this stair when the mountain still traded openly. They carried silk down and brought up ore, or the reverse; the histories disagree, and the histories were written by merchants. What everyone agrees on is the toll. Not coin. Coin means nothing underground. The gates want weight of a different kind.

I had read about the tolls in a marginal note in a folio I wasn't supposed to touch, in a hand so cramped it might have been written falling. *The stair eats upward,* the note said. *It takes what you will not miss until it is gone.*

There are nine gates between the surface and the court. I counted them on the map I'd memorized and then burned. Nine payments. I stood before the first gate for a long time, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

What does a person have, if you strip away the guild, the debt, the handwriting? She has her past. That's all a self is — a ledger of what happened to you, kept in a hand only you can read.

I knocked on the stone.

* * *

The first gate opened onto a landing no wider than a coffin stood on end, lit by salt-crusted lamps that burned without flame. In the wall, a hollow shaped exactly like a human hand. I understood without being told. You put your hand in. The gate reads you the way I read a folio — quickly, thoroughly, without mercy — and then it names its price.

The voice, when it came, came out of the stone itself, dry as pages turning:

*FIRST TOLL. NAME WHAT YOU BRING.*

"I bring nothing," I said. "I pay as I go."

*A WISE BEARER. THE GATE CHOOSES. THE BEARER MAY REFUSE ONCE PER GATE. REFUSAL TWICE AND THE STAIR KEEPS YOU.*

My heart was going like a stamp-press. I put my hand in the hollow.

Cold, then not cold — a sensation like being read, which I can only describe to you as what it would feel like if someone opened your skull to its middle page and ran a finger down the columns. The gate hummed. Somewhere far below, something vast shifted its weight.

*TOLL ACCEPTED: THE SMELL OF BREAD IN YOUR MOTHER'S KITCHEN. THE MORNING OF IT. DO YOU CONSENT?*

I almost refused. It was such a small thing, such a *stupidly* small thing — and that's how it gets you, I realized. It starts with bread-smell. It teaches you the shape of losing.

But I had made a list, walking down through the tannery district. I had spent three sleepless nights ranking every memory I owned by what it would cost me to lose it versus what it protected. My mother's kitchen held her face, her voice, her deathbed, the debt she signed with shaking hands. If the gate wanted the kitchen, let it take the kitchen whole. Better it than the pieces I needed sharp.

"I consent," I said.

The smell arrived one last time — warm crust, woodsmoke, the particular sweetness of scorched honey — vivid enough to make my mouth water and my eyes sting. Then it left, the way a tide leaves, and where it had been there was a smooth blank place, like a page washed for reuse. I could remember that there had been a kitchen. I could not remember being in it.

The gate opened. I stepped through, lighter by one morning, and discovered the terrible secret of the stair:

It doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. Losing feels like *relief.*

* * *

By the fourth gate I had learned the rhythm, and the rhythm was seduction. Hand in the hollow. The reading. The offer. One refusal allowed, and I hoarded my refusals like coins, spending them only when the gate reached for something on my protected list.

Gate two took my grandmother's lullaby, melody and all — I consented fast, before it could find the woman herself. Gate three wanted the name of my first schoolmistress and I gave it gladly; the woman had beaten penmanship into me with a ruler and I felt no loyalty owed. Gate four reached deeper. It circled for a long time, humming, and offered terms:

*TOLL PROPOSED: THE DAY YOU FIRST SAW THE SEA.*

"No," I said, and my refusal rang in the landing like a struck bell.

The gate paused. Considered. I felt it paging through me, patient as an archivist.

*ALTERNATIVE TOLL: THE REASON YOU CRIED AT YOUR FATHER'S FUNERAL.*

That stopped me. Because I knew why I'd cried, and it wasn't grief, and it was a shameful thing, a small crooked thing I had never told anyone — I'd cried because his death meant the debt passed wholly to me, and some starving part of me had felt *claimed*, finally, entirely, and the feeling had been indistinguishable from love.

"Take it," I said.

And it did, and I stood on the landing afterward feeling strangely clean, and understood that the stair doesn't only take. It *edits*. Every soul that walks down arrives at the bottom a little more legible than it left the top. The court likes its records tidy. Of course it does. So do I.

That thought should have frightened me more than it did. I was four gates down and already thinking like the thing I was robbing.

* * *

Between gates five and six there is a long gallery where the salt has grown in columns floor to ceiling, translucent, veined with pink and grey, and each column holds a light of its own. I stopped there despite myself. Despite the clock ticking somewhere in my chest — because there was a clock now; the writ named tonight, and prophecies are punctual.

The columns weren't salt. Or they weren't only salt. When I pressed my palm against the nearest one, warmth moved under the surface, and deep in the crystal something turned over, slow, like a sleeper.

Memories. The stair keeps what it takes. It hangs them here in the dark like lamps, and lights the way down with other people's yesterdays.

I found one close to eye level that flickered with a summer I recognized — not mine, but adjacent to mine, a salt-flat summer, a boy running the white ridges with his shirt off and his arms spread like wings. Wren. Someone else's Wren, some cousin or neighbor who had sold himself decades ago, and here was their memory of him, burning quietly in a pillar of salt, lighting strangers' steps forever.

I stood in front of it longer than I could afford.

Then I did something I hadn't planned. I took out the little knife I use for trimming quills, and I chipped a fragment from the base of the column — no bigger than a thumbnail — and wrapped it in cloth and put it in my pocket beside the salt jar.

If the stair could keep things, then things could be stolen back. I intended to prove the principle at scale.

* * *

Gate seven nearly ended me.

The reading took longer. The humming went on so long I began to suspect the gate had found something it liked too much, and when the voice finally spoke, it spoke gently, the way the Guildmaster speaks when he wants something:

*TOLL PROPOSED: THE NAME ANSEL QUINTANA.*

"No." Instant. Reflex. "Absolutely not."

*THE BEARER MAY REFUSE ONCE. THIS WAS HER REFUSAL.*

"Then propose something else."

Silence. Long enough that I heard my own blood. Then:

*ALTERNATIVE TOLL: EVERY MEMORY OF THE FACE THAT GOES WITH THE NAME.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and cracked, echoing down the gallery behind me. "You want the face but not the name? What kind of bargain—"

*THE COURT FINDS NAMES HEAVY AND FACES USEFUL. MANY BEARERS HAVE AGREED TO LESS.*

And there it was, laid out plain: the mechanism of Wren's sentence. He walked down a stair like this one, and at some gate he paid and paid and paid, and what remained at the bottom was a man-shaped record with no one inside it. The court didn't erase him out of cruelty. It *invoiced* him.

I gripped the salt jar through my coat. "No face. No name. Propose again or I turn around, and your prophecy goes unwritten, and whatever happens to the court when its own book fails — let that happen instead."

Another silence. Longer. And then, with something I chose to hear as respect:

*TOLL PROPOSED: THE SOUND OF HER LAUGHING. ALL INSTANCES.*

I stood very still.

Her. Whose? The gate didn't say, and I suddenly couldn't be sure — the memory it meant was warm and specific and *mine*, a laugh I could hear that instant if I tried, bright and unguarded, someone laughing at something I'd said, in sunlight, in a place with salt in the air—

Wren. It was Wren laughing. It had always been Wren laughing; I had made him laugh maybe a hundred times across our childhood and I had never once counted those moments as wealth, and now a gate in a mountain was appraising them at the price of my identity.

All instances. Every one.

"Yes," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out.

They went the way tides go, all together, a whole shoreline draining at once — and what was left was a fact without music: *he laughed, sometimes, near me.* I knew it the way I know entries in ledgers I've never read. Correct. Empty. Paid in full.

The seventh gate opened onto the last descent, and the air coming up from below was warm and smelled of ink.

* * *

Two gates remained. I paid them in a daze, guarding the core of myself with both refusals — gate eight wanted my ability to weep and I refused, and it settled for my memory of ever having been afraid of the dark, which I considered a fair trade and a tactical advantage. Gate nine asked for nothing I noticed losing. That frightened me most of all. I checked my pockets like a woman counting fingers: writ, jar, quill-knife, salt-chip. All present. Whatever ninth-gate took, it took from rooms in me I no longer knew I had.

And then the stair ended, and there was no gate at all, only an archway of black stone and beyond it a hall lit by ten thousand hanging ledgers, swaying gently on their chains like lanterns, each one glowing faintly, each one alive.

A court of kept things. I had imagined a throne room. It was a *library* — the kind of library I had spent my whole life serving, scaled up past sanity, past mercy.

Somewhere in those racks hung a ledger with Wren's soul bound in its spine.

I smoothed my coat, squared the forged writ in my hand, and walked in wearing the only armor I have ever owned: perfect handwriting, and a straight back, and a lie signed in my own name.

Halfway across the floor, a figure detached itself from between the shelved ledgers — tall, robed in vellum-white, its face a page of fine print — and spoke in a voice like a stamp coming down.

"The bearer of the writ," it said. "We have been expecting you for a hundred years, Ansel Quintana."
