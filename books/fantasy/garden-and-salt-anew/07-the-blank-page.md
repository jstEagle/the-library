## 7. The Blank Page

The Magistrate received her in a room with no walls she could find.

Not that the court beneath the mountain lacked rooms—Ansel had passed a hundred of them on her way here, each one hung with ledgers like meat in a smokehouse, each one humming faintly with the sound of kept things. But this chamber was different. The stone simply stopped, and beyond it was a darkness so even it might have been paper. A desk stood in the middle of it, black wood, unblemished. On the desk lay a single sheet.

The Magistrate sat behind it with his hands folded, and Ansel understood immediately why the guild's records described him only as *a figure of account*. He had no face she could hold onto. Every time her eyes tried to fix his features—a jaw, a brow, the gray of age or the smoothness of youth—the details slid apart like wet ink. He was not hiding his face. He had simply never bothered to have one, because no ledger had ever required it of him.

"Scribe Quintana," he said. His voice was dry and precise, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "You are four gates deep and carrying perhaps half of what you were when you entered my mountain. Sit."

There was no chair. She remained standing, which felt correct.

"You know what I am," he said. "Let us not spend your remaining hours on ceremony. You came to steal a soul from my collection. I have watched you descend. I let you." He turned one hand over, palm up, an accountant's gesture: *behold the figures*. "Do you wonder why?"

Ansel's throat was dry. Somewhere behind her, three gates back, she had left the memory of her mother's laugh, and its absence ached like a pulled tooth. "Because you couldn't stop me."

"Because stopping you was never the point." The Magistrate drew the single sheet toward him but did not lift it. "You are a scribe. You understand debt better than anyone alive. So let me show you the oldest entry in my keeping."

He turned the page around.

It was the final page of the prophecy folio—the one the Guildmaster had ordered her to transcribe, the one she'd been told was blank. It was not blank. It was *waiting*. The paper had the particular pale hunger of a surface prepared for ink, sized and burnished, and across its top, in handwriting Ansel knew better than her own heartbeat, ran a single line:

*On this night, the scribe comes down to finish what she began.*

Her own hand. Her own slanted descenders, her habit of crossing t's with a slight upward flick at the end, the tiny hesitation-mark she made before a word she wasn't sure of. A century old. Unmistakably hers.

"That's impossible," she said.

"Nothing is impossible in my court," said the Magistrate. "That is rather the entire business model. What is impossible is merely expensive." He folded his hands again. "You wrote that line, Scribe. Not will write it. *Wrote* it. One hundred years ago, in this room, at this desk, with the pen I am about to offer you. The folio in your guild's archive is not a prophecy. It is a receipt. And receipts, as you know, are written after the transaction."

The floor did not move, but Ansel felt it tilt anyway. She thought of the archivist's trembling hands. Of the Guildmaster's too-gentle voice: *transcribe the final page.* They had known. They had always known—not the future, just the entry, the way a merchant knows a shipment will arrive because he has already signed for it.

"If I wrote it," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through any ledger, line by line, "then it's already done. I already came down here. I already—" She stopped. "Then Wren is already free. Or already lost. Which?"

"Neither yet. Time in my court is kept like everything else here: in arrears." The Magistrate produced a pen. It was black, and long, and it drank the light of the room without reflecting any back. He laid it on the desk between them, parallel to the page, like a knife set before a duelist. "Here is the mercy you came for, offered plainly. Complete the page as written—as you already wrote it—and I release the soul called Wren. Whole. Unerased. He walks out of my mountain tomorrow with no debt, no binding, no memory of the price. He will live a long life selling salt under an open sky. This is not a trick. I keep my accounts immaculate; ask anyone I have ever ruined."

"And the cost."

"The cost is the completion itself." He tapped the empty space below the line. "The page requires its ending. The death clause stands. *The soul beneath the mountain dies tonight*—that is what your century-old hand recorded, and that is what must be true for the receipt to balance. But hear me, scribe, and hear me carefully, because this is the part your guild never understood." He leaned forward, and though he had no face, she felt him look at her. "The clause does not say whose soul dies. It says *the soul beneath the mountain*. Tonight, there are two souls beneath this mountain. His. And yours."

The silence that followed was the kind Ansel had only ever heard in the guild's deepest vaults, where debts so old they'd gone feral were stored away from honest books.

"You're offering me a trade," she said. "His life for mine. Written in my own hand, witnessed by my own ink."

"I am offering you what you already chose." The Magistrate spread his hands. "I do not invent entries, Scribe. I merely collect them. A hundred years ago, a woman sat where you stand, holding everything you are holding now—grief, guilt, love worn down to a load-bearing structure—and she picked up that pen and wrote the fair version. The merciful version. The version where the salt-seller goes free. She paid the clause herself and considered it a bargain, and then she walked out of my mountain lighter than you are now, because she left nearly all of herself behind as toll." He paused. "She did not remember writing it. She did not remember him. She lived out her days as a stranger to her own life, and she died owing nothing to anyone, which in my experience is the closest thing to peace a debtor ever gets."

Ansel's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs.

"Who was she?" she asked, though she knew. She knew the way you know a mirror.

"Her name was Ansel Quintana," said the Magistrate. "Guild of Impossible Things, debt-bound, third of her name in the ledgers and last. She was very good with numbers. She loved someone foolishly and completely, the way salt loves water." He slid the pen an inch closer to her. "You are standing in the middle of a circle, child. There is no way out of a circle. There is only the way through it, and the way through is the page."

* * *

She made him wait while she thought, because thinking was the one thing no one could repossess.

She walked the edge of the dark until she found the wall of it—or the place where wall should be—and put her palm against the smooth nothing there. Behind her, the pen waited on the desk like a held breath. Somewhere above her, past five gates and a merchant stair, Wren sat in his alcove being a record of himself, and he did not know her name, and he would not know it tomorrow either, if she finished the page. That was the fine print. The Magistrate had been scrupulous about the fine print: *whole, unerased, no memory of the price.* No memory of her. No memory of the salt flats, or the jar on her desk, or the boy who had once traded his whole self for her arithmetic mistakes.

And she would die. Here, tonight, in whatever way a soul dies when a clause closes over it. And the woman who walked out would be—no. There would be no woman who walked out. There would be a blank where Ansel Quintana had been, and a freed salt-seller who grieved someone he couldn't name, and a folio in a guild archive with its final page complete, balanced, immaculate.

Duty dressed as mercy. Mercy dressed as duty. She turned the offer over and over and found the same watermark on every side: *this is fair, this is fair, this is what you already agreed to.*

That was the trap's true teeth, she realized. Not the death clause. The *already*. The Magistrate wasn't asking her to choose—he was asking her to obey a choice she'd supposedly made, to sign her own past as her own future. It was the guild's whole philosophy rendered in a single page: that a debt, once written, owns the debtor more thoroughly than any chain could. That the book is always right.

But she was a scribe. And there was one thing every scribe knew that magistrates and guildmasters preferred to forget.

A book is only right until someone writes in it.

She turned back to the desk. The Magistrate's faceless attention settled on her like weather.

"You said I wrote this a century ago," she said. "At this desk. With that pen."

"I did."

"Then the tolls I paid coming down—the memories at the gates. Those were hers first, weren't they? Hers, paid by her, carried down by her, and paid again by me. The same coins, circulating." Her heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth. "Memory doesn't vanish in your court. Nothing vanishes. You *keep* things. It's the entire business model—you said so yourself."

Something shifted in the darkness behind the Magistrate. If a faceless creature could go still, he went stiller than still.

"Clever," he said, and for the first time his voice was not entirely dry. "Yes. Your tolls are held in escrow against the completion of the account. When the page is finished, they are released to the ledger as fuel. Ink needs something to burn, Scribe. Even prophecy is written in something."

"Then give me the pen," Ansel Quintana said, "and open the escrow."

She reached for it—and stopped, her fingers a hair's width from the black lacquer, because the last thing she saw clearly was not the page or the pen but a memory she had chosen to keep at the second gate, paying almost everything else to hold onto it: Wren at nine years old on the white flats, cupping seawater in both hands, letting it evaporate in the sun, saying, *look, Ansel—it keeps. Everything keeps, if you're patient enough.*

Everything keeps. Even a choice. Even a loop.

But a loop, she thought, picking up the pen, is just a circle drawn by someone who believed the line had to close.

She set the nib to the page below her own century-old handwriting, and the mountain held its breath.
