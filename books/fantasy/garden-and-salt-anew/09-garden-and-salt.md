## 9. Garden and Salt

Wren woke with dirt under his fingernails and no idea how it got there.

He was lying in a garden—someone's garden, walled and small, the kind that clung to a hillside terrace above the lower market. Tomato vines sagged on their strings. A fig tree dropped its overripe fruit into the grass. The morning smelled of green things and, underneath, faintly, of salt.

He sat up too fast and the world tilted. His body felt wrong in a way he couldn't name—not injured, not sick. *Empty*, some quiet part of him said, and then the word slid away like water off glass.

He checked himself methodically, the way you check pockets after a fall. Two hands. Ten fingers, calloused from rope and crate-work. A coin purse with eleven coppers and a bent silver bit. A jar.

The jar sat beside him in the grass, stoppered with wax, half full of coarse grey salt. It was warm. Not sun-warm—*held*-warm, as if someone had been carrying it against their chest for a long time and had only just set it down.

He turned it over. No label. No maker's mark. Just salt, the good flake kind that came off the pans at low tide, the kind his own hands had raked for years back when—

Back when what?

The thought dissolved before it finished forming. He sat there in the wet grass, heart hammering at nothing, grieving something, and could not have said for what or whom. It was like waking from a dream where someone had died, except the dream wouldn't come back and neither would the name.

"Oi." An old woman leaned out a shuttered window above him, arms crossed. "You can't sleep in Master Ivo's garden."

"No," Wren agreed. He stood, brushed off his knees, and found his legs knew the way down the terrace stairs even though his head didn't. "Sorry."

"You one of the salt-sellers?" she called after him.

He stopped. The word fit him like a coat cut to his shoulders. "Yes," he said, surprising himself. "I think I am."

"Well, don't stand there thinking about it. Market opens at bell."

He went down toward the bells.

* * *

The lower market took him in without questions, which was its way. Stalls of river fish and dyed wool, a knife-grinder working his wheel, a woman selling candied ginger who shouted prices at him familiarly, as if he'd been buying from her for years. Maybe he had. Maybe he hadn't. There was a hole where his life should have been—a Wren-shaped absence—and everyone else seemed to remember around it just fine.

He set up at an empty stretch of wall near the fountain, because his feet carried him there and refused to be argued with. He had no stock. He had a jar.

He unstoppered it and pinched a few flakes between his fingers. Good salt. The best. He remembered—no. He *knew*, the way you know your own hands, that this salt came from pans he'd tended himself, that he'd raked it at dawn, that somewhere behind the knowing there had been a reason he'd made this particular jar, filled it particular-full, sealed it with wax pressed by his own thumb.

It was for someone.

That certainty arrived whole, like a stone dropped into still water. *This is for someone.* Not a customer. Not a debt. Someone who needed it, or needed him, or needed the delivering of it more than anyone needed anything.

But no name came. He held the certainty up to the light and turned it, and there was nothing written anywhere on it.

So he did what salt-sellers do. He sold salt. Word got around that the young man by the fountain had honest flakes at fair weight, and by midday he'd traded most of a second jar—he didn't know where the second jar came from either; it was simply in his satchel when he looked—for bread, a blanket, a razor. He shaved at the fountain and caught his own eye in the water and thought: *you look like a man who has been crying for a very long time and has finally stopped.*

His face was dry. His eyes were not red. But the look was true all the same, and he couldn't account for it, and he let it go, because there was no ledger in his head to write it down in.

That phrase drifted through him—*no ledger*—and left a strange lightness, like a tooth pulled. He almost missed whatever had been there. Almost.

* * *

She watched him from the fruit stall across the lane, holding an apple she had no intention of eating.

Ansel had told herself she would not come. She had told herself it in the mountain's shaking dark, walking out through gates that no longer asked tolls of her because there was nothing left in her to take. She had told herself it on the road down, in the inn, at dawn. And then she had walked to the market anyway, because a person can lose everything except the wanting to see.

He looked well. That was the cruelty of it. The court had kept him beautiful the way it kept everything—unchanged, unlived-in—and now the morning had him back, and the morning suited him. He laughed at something the ginger-seller shouted. He weighed salt with quick, sure hands. He was a stranger doing a stranger's work, and every gesture was one she knew.

The jar sat on his table. Her jar—the one she'd pressed into his sleeping hands in the garden, because the court's last law had followed them both out of the dark: *what was taken must be returned by hand, unnamed.* She had spent her final coin of memory on the wording. She no longer remembered writing it. She only knew the rule the way you know a scar's shape with your tongue.

Deliver it yourself, Wren. Come find me. I'm allowed to be found. I'm just not allowed to be known.

She had explained none of this to him. There had been no one to explain it to. The guild's books no longer held her name—the debt struck through in ink that burned itself out as she climbed. The temple rolls were blank where she'd been. Even the prophecy folio, when she'd dared check the archive one last time, showed a final page bearing a single line in handwriting that was hers and also not hers anymore:

*A soul returned above ground. A debt paid by its author. Let no record bind them.*

And beneath it, where her own name had been, smooth blank vellum.

She had wept then, once, briefly, in the archive dust—not for the name but for the fact that she would never again know why the name had mattered enough to burn. Then she'd bought an apple and gone to watch a man sell salt.

He looked up.

Not at her—at the middle distance, the way a man looks when a thought almost lands. His gaze crossed the lane, crossed the fruit stall, crossed her, and kept going, snagging on nothing. But his hand had gone still on the scale, and his brow had furrowed, and for three long seconds he stared at the empty air beside her as though the air owed him something.

Then the ginger-seller bawled a price, and he blinked, and the moment closed over like water.

Ansel set down the apple. This was the bargain, she reminded herself. This was the price she had chosen, line by line, in ink made of everything she'd ever been. He lived. He was free. He would wake tomorrow and the day after and never feel the mountain's cold again, and if a grief rode him that he couldn't name—well. Grief without a name is just weather. It passes.

Mostly she believed that.

She turned to go, and that was when she saw him pick up the jar.

He held it in both hands, thumb finding the wax seal, finding the dent her own thumb had left in the wax a hundred years before she was born—or however the loop had gone; the arithmetic of it was lost to her now, along with so much else. He turned the jar slowly, frowning at it, and then he did the thing that stopped her heart against her ribs:

He tucked it under his arm, stepped out from behind his stall, and started walking.

Not toward the fountain. Not toward the ginger-seller. Toward the fruit stall. Toward her—with the unhurried, stubborn stride of a man following an instruction he can't read, drawn across a crowded market by a jar of salt and whatever the salt remembered.

Ansel stood very still among the apples.

*Don't,* she thought, with no conviction at all. *Don't ask my name. Don't—*

He stopped an arm's length away. Up close he smelled of brine and morning, exactly as he always had, exactly as she had paid everything to preserve. His eyes were the same grey-brown as the flats at dusk. They rested on her face with a polite, puzzled warmth, the warmth of a man greeting weather he half-remembers.

"Forgive me," Wren said. "This is going to sound strange."

"Most true things do," Ansel said, and heard her own voice shake.

He held up the jar. "I'm supposed to deliver this. I've known it since I woke up this morning in a stranger's garden, and I don't know how I know it, and I don't know who it's for." He turned it over; the salt whispered inside. "But I've been standing here trying to leave, and my feet won't take me anywhere but to you. So." He offered it out, careful as a man offering water to a wounded bird. "Is it yours?"

The market roared on around them—bells, bargains, the grinder's wheel. Somewhere a child laughed. The world, indifferent and enormous, giving them room.

Ansel looked at the jar. At the dent in the wax. At the man holding it, who had sold his soul to pay her debts and would now, if she let him, begin paying again in the only coin left: time, attention, a question asked across a fruit stall by a stranger.

"It might be," she said.

He waited. He was good at waiting; she could tell that about him still, the way you can tell a door used to open here.

"I'm passing through," she said carefully. "I'll need salt, eventually. Everyone does."

Something flickered in his face—not memory, memory was gone, gone, paid for—but its shape. Its outline. The place where a thing had been, aching like a healed break before rain.

"Then you'll want it from someone honest," he said. "I'm Wren."

And Ansel Quintana, whose name existed nowhere in the world—not in any book, any roll, any ledger, any hand but her own—discovered that being unwritten meant she could answer however she liked.

She smiled, and it cost her everything, and it was worth it.

"Ansel," she said. "My name is Ansel."
