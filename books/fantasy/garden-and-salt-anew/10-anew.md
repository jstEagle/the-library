## 10. Anew

The ledger I bought cost three coppers and a lie.

The lie was that I had no name to give the stationer, which was true in every way that mattered. He'd asked, casual as weather, and I'd opened my mouth and found nothing there — not a blank, exactly, but a smooth place, like a stone worn by water. *Ansel*, said something deep in me, a fish turning under ice. But when I reached for it, it slipped. So I shrugged, and he shrugged, and I paid my three coppers, and walked out into the morning with a book of blank pages and no name anyone could find.

That was the trade. I had known the terms before I made them. Every toll I'd paid on the salt stair, every memory I'd surrendered gate by gate — those had gone into the ink. And the ink had needed more than tolls in the end. It had needed the author. The guild's records no longer held an Ansel Quintana; I had checked, twice, standing in the archive with my heart going like a trapped bird while a junior archivist paged politely through the rolls and found nothing, nothing, nothing. The temple's census: nothing. The debt-rolls of the Guild of Impossible Things, where my handwriting had once been famous for its perfection: nothing at all. Even the prophecy folio, the old one bound in ash-grey leather, was clean now — the death clause struck through in ink the color of burnt salt, and beneath it, in a hand I recognized as mine and could not remember writing, a single new line:

*She pays her own debts.*

I had gone to see it the way you visit a grave. I had stood there a long time. Then I had bought a ledger from a man who didn't know my name, because nobody did, and carried it home.

Home was a room above a chandler's shop in the low town, rented with coin earned honestly and stupidly — copying letters for merchants who couldn't write, three coppers a page, no guild seal required when the work is small enough to be beneath the guild's notice. Invisibility, it turned out, was a skill I still had. Some things survive erasure the way roots survive fire.

On the first page of the new ledger, before any entry, I wrote the rule:

*Nothing written here can bind anyone.*

Then, underneath, because I am who I am even without remembering who I am:

*A ledger is a promise, not a cage. If an entry cannot be crossed out, do not make it.*

It felt good, the pen moving. That hadn't changed. Whatever else the mountain had taken — and I would spend years, I suspected, finding the shaped absences, the doorways in my memory that opened onto walls — it had not taken this. My hand still loved the work. Ink still smelled like beginning.

* * *

I set up my stall in the market on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were slow and I wanted to fail quietly if I was going to fail.

The stall sold what I could provide: fair copies, letters written and read aloud, contracts explained in plain words so that no one signed what they didn't understand. It was, I realized somewhere in the second week, the exact inversion of everything the guild did. The guild wrote things down so they could be held forever. I wrote things down so people could hold them lightly — a letter home, a bill of sale, a recipe, a will — and then let go.

Business was thin. Word spread anyway, the way word does. A woman whose mother had died came to have a letter read that she couldn't bear to read alone, and I read it, and she wept, and I found I was weeping too, though I had no idea why the phrase *the garden needs less water than you think* should undo me so completely. She paid me double. I tried to refuse. She put the coins in my hand and closed my fingers over them, and said, "You look like someone who used to give things away."

"I don't remember," I said.

"Neither do I," she said, "most days. It doesn't stop it being true."

I thought about that for a long time after she left. About the difference between a record and a truth. The guild had never understood the difference; that was their whole sickness, the belief that what is written is what is real. But the woman was right. Things could be true with no record at all. Things could be true *against* the record. Somewhere under the ice, the fish turned again.

* * *

He came on a Thursday, in the sixth week, carrying a jar of salt.

I saw him before he saw me — of course I did, though I didn't know why my chest went tight, why my hands went still on the page mid-word. He was taller than something in me expected and moved the way something in me expected, quick and careful both, a man used to narrow places. Salt-seller. I knew it the way you know rain coming: not seeing it, just feeling the pressure change. He worked the flats, or had. There was white dust in the seams of his knuckles.

And he was looking at his jar of salt like it had betrayed him.

I watched him stop three stalls short of mine and turn the jar over in his hands. Watched him frown at it. Watched him start toward the chandler's, change direction, end up at my stall anyway, wearing the expression of a man who has been arguing with himself for six streets and lost.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning." My voice came out steady. Something under the ice was hammering on it, wanting to say something else entirely, and I kept it steady out of pure spite toward whatever that was. "Letter? Copy? Contract explained?"

"No." He turned the jar over again. "I don't — this is going to sound mad."

"Most true things do."

That startled a laugh out of him, short and surprised, and the sound of it went through me like a hook through cloth. I knew that laugh. I had never heard it in my life. Both things were somehow true at once, and I was getting used to living in the space between them.

"It's the salt," he said. "I've been selling salt since I was a boy. I know my stock like I know my own hands. And this jar—" he set it on my counter, and even upside down, even sealed, I could see the grains inside had settled into a pattern, deliberate, a spiral, the kind of thing that doesn't happen by accident — "this jar I can't account for. It was in my pack two months ago and I don't remember packing it. I've sold salt off and around it for weeks and I can't bring myself to touch this one. And then this morning I woke up and knew I had to bring it here. To you. To this stall. Which I've never been to." He looked up. His eyes were grey-green, sea-glass, and something behind them was reaching for something behind mine and not quite touching. "Do you know anything about it?"

I looked at the jar. Inside the spiral of grains, faintly, pressed into the salt itself the way you press a seal into wax, was a single word. I had put it there — I knew that the way I knew the laugh, without knowing how I knew — back when I still remembered making it, back on the last morning, when I'd stood in the market and watched him wake above ground with grief in him and no name to attach it to, and I had not been able to bear leaving him with nothing.

The word was *Ansel*.

My name. The one no record held anymore. The one that existed now in exactly two places: carved in salt in a jar a man couldn't explain, and — maybe — somewhere under the ice in me, if I ever got warm enough to reach it.

"It's salt," I said.

"It's a message," he said. "Or it's a debt. I've been carrying it like a debt. Like I owe somebody the delivering of it, only the delivery is—" he gestured helplessly at me, at the stall, at the whole unreasonable morning— "*this*. Standing here. Talking to you. And I don't know why."

"You could sell it," I offered. "Salt keeps."

"I can't." Flat, immediate, no thought in it at all, and then he heard himself and blinked. "I can't. Huh." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Look. I'm Wren."

The name landed in me like a bell struck in an empty room. Everything rang. Nothing fell. Under the ice the fish went still, listening.

"That's a good name," I managed.

"It's just a name." He hesitated. And then, quieter, with the particular courage of a man stepping off a ledge he's already fallen from once: "What's yours?"

And here is the thing I want to set down properly, because it is the only entry in this whole ledger that matters. I could have said *I don't know.* It was nearly true. It was true in every way the world could verify. But the woman with her mother's letter had taught me the difference between a record and a truth, and I was done letting records — even missing ones, even erased ones, even my own sacrificed past — decide what was real.

"Ansel," I said.

The word came up from under the ice whole. Not remembered — *chosen*. Mine, because I said it, because I would keep saying it, because a name spoken freely to someone free is the one kind of writing that binds no one and holds everything.

Something happened in his face. Not recognition — recognition was gone, paid for, burned as fuel for ink against a mountain. But something older than recognition, something that lives below memory the way bedrock lives below soil. He breathed out. His shoulders came down half an inch.

"Ansel," he repeated, testing it, keeping it. "Why does that feel like — " He stopped. Started over. "Would you think I was mad if I said it feels like coming home?"

"Not at all," I said, and my eyes were stinging, and I was smiling, and none of it needed a reason on record. "I'd think it was the truest thing anyone's said to me all year."

He stayed. He bought nothing and stayed anyway, and I let him, and the morning went on around us, ordinary and enormous. At some point he turned the jar over once more, and the spiral caught the light, and he said, almost shyly, "Could I come back tomorrow? Not to buy anything. Just — here."

"Here," I agreed.

That night, upstairs, I opened the new ledger and made its first real entry, in a hand that was still perfect, because some things survive anything:

*Bought: nothing. Sold: nothing. Received: one name, returned to its owner. One visitor, expected again tomorrow. Debts outstanding: none. Debts permitted: all, provided both parties choose them freely, in daylight, with the option of crossing out.*

Below that, after a moment, I added one line more. Not a rule. Just a note to whoever I would be when I read it again — tomorrow, or in fifty years, with or without the ice melted:

*Some things are true with no record at all. Start there.*

Outside, the market was quieting. Somewhere below my window, salt was settling in a jar into shapes that meant something, and a man who had been given back his life was sleeping on the question of a name, and tomorrow he would come back, having chosen to, which is the only way anyone ever really comes back at all.

I dipped my pen. The page waited, blank and unbinding, wide open as a morning.

I began to write.
