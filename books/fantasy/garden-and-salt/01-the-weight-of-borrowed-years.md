## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The magistrate dreamed in green.

Emrys stood at the edge of the dream the way they always did, one hand on the doorframe of the man's sleeping mind, and let it wash over them. It was a garden—of course it was a garden, it was always a garden with these people, some sunlit courtyard from before the weight of office settled on their shoulders—and this one was magnificent. Wisteria gone feral over a stone wall. A well with a rope so worn smooth by generations of palms that it gleamed like something carved. A woman's voice calling a name that wasn't *Magistrate Hallow* but something smaller, softer: *Tam. Tam, come in for supper.*

Emrys had never had a garden. They'd grown up above a tannery in the river district, where the only green thing was the scum on the water barrels, and where names were shouted, never called gently. So they stood in Tam Hallow's childhood for as long as the harvest allowed, breathing air that had stopped existing forty years ago, and told themselves—as always—that this was simply good practice. Know your goods. Inspect the vintage.

It was a lie they'd polished until it shone. The truth was that Emrys Vasquez was a glutton, and memory was the only feast they could afford.

The extraction itself took eleven minutes. That was the craft of it—the part Corvin had drilled into them until it lived in their hands instead of their head. Find the seam. The seam in Hallow's mind ran along his fear of his own son, which was convenient; fear made generous doorways. Emrys slipped through, found the garden nested behind it like a pearl in an oyster, and cut around it with the fine silver hooks of the trade—not hooks you could hold, but hooks all the same, made of attention and intent. The garden came away clean. The magistrate would wake tomorrow knowing he'd loved wisteria once, knowing there had been a well, knowing a woman had called him home—but the color would be gone from it. He'd have the bones of the memory without the marrow. He'd assume he was simply getting old.

They all assumed they were simply getting old.

Emrys withdrew, sealed the dream behind them, and sat up in the guest room of the Hallow townhouse with the harvested memory coiled in their chest like a warm meal. Below, the real Magistrate Hallow snored on, dreaming now of ledgers and mild anxieties. Emrys dressed in the dark, left the payment-for-nothing exactly where the broker's letter had specified—three hundred crowns in a purse under the loose floorboard, because even thieves in this trade paid for what they took, just not to the people they took it from—and went out through the window the way they'd come in.

The city of Vail was still drunk on its own festival. Lanterns swung from the magistrates' row, and somewhere a fiddle was being played badly by someone who loved it anyway. Emrys moved through the side streets with the hood up and the walk of a person belonging nowhere, which was its own kind of belonging. At the corner of Tanner's Cut they paused out of old habit, checked the dead drop, found it empty, and felt the small cold satisfaction of a clean job.

Then they walked out the west gate before dawn, and kept walking, and did not stop until the city was a smudge of smoke behind them and the mountain road had narrowed to a track only smugglers and goats remembered.

The cabin sat at the tree line like a tooth left in an old jaw. Emrys had built it themselves, seven years ago, from timber they'd hauled up in pieces disguised as firewood, because a memory-thief needed a place that existed on no map. No neighbors. No roads that remembered them. Just stone and pine and the long silence of the high country, where the wind came down off the Thornfell passes speaking a language of its own.

Inside, the cabin smelled of cedar smoke and lamp oil. Emrys hung their coat, banked the fire, and set about the ritual that made the theft worth doing: the tasting.

This was the part no broker understood, the part that couldn't be sold. You didn't just pocket a memory like a coin. You poured it into yourself and let it steep. Emrys sat in the chair by the window with a cup of nettle tea going cold, closed their eyes, and walked back into Tam Hallow's garden—this time slowly, this time as a trespasser who intended to stay a while.

They learned the smell of the wisteria, which was sweeter than any flower Emrys had ever smelled in life, honeyed and faintly rotten at the edges, the way beautiful things are when they're allowed to overgrow. They learned the sound the well-rope made, a creak-and-splash rhythm like a slow heart. They learned the woman's voice—Hallow's mother, dead these thirty years—and the way she said *Tam* with the second syllable rising, a question and an answer both.

And they learned, because memory is honest even when its owner isn't, that Tam Hallow had been happy here, and that he had spent fifty years since trying to buy the feeling back with offices and titles and a stone house in the capital, and that he had failed, and that he didn't know why.

Emrys opened their eyes. The tea was cold. Outside, the light had gone from grey to gold, which meant midmorning, which meant they'd been sitting in a dead woman's voice for three hours.

"Getting soft," they told the empty room. Their voice sounded strange after two days of silence—rough, underused, theirs alone. That was the other cost of the trade nobody mentioned. Every hour you spent inside someone else's head, your own got quieter. Emrys sometimes went whole weeks saying nothing but *yes*, *no*, and *the usual terms*.

They were reaching for the ledger to log the harvest—*Hallow, T., magistrate, 1 garden (wisteria), quality: exceptional*—when the wind shifted.

It came down off the passes the way it always did, but tonight—or rather this morning—it carried something underneath it. A thread of sound too regular to be birdsong, too small to be a voice carrying from the valley. Emrys froze with the quill above the page.

There was no one within twenty miles. There was no one *within twenty miles*. That was the entire point of the cabin.

They took the knife from the woodbox—they weren't a fighter, but Corvin had taught them that a thief who couldn't threaten was just a victim with inventory—and went to the door, and eased it open.

The child was asleep on their doorstep.

A girl, maybe eight years old, maybe younger—you couldn't tell with the hollows her cheeks had gone. She lay curled against the door itself as if she'd walked until the door was the only thing left in the world worth leaning on. Her clothes were valley wool, good once, ruined now by weather and distance. Her feet were bare. Bare feet, in the high country, in the turning season. The skin was split and blue at the toes, and Emrys thought, with a professional's detachment that disgusted them even as they deployed it: *she'll lose two of those if she doesn't warm soon.*

And she was talking. Murmuring, rather—words spilling out of her sleep like water finding a crack, soft and ceaseless and utterly wrong.

Because Emrys knew languages. It came with the trade; you couldn't harvest a memory you couldn't read, and over the years they'd collected scraps of a dozen tongues the way other collectors collected coins. Low Vailish, High Vailish, the marsh dialects, the trader-pidgin of the salt roads, enough old church Cant to follow a funeral. And this was none of them. This was older. The sounds had a shape Emrys's ear kept reaching for and missing—a grammar built backward, vowels that fell where modern speech put consonants, a rhythm like a litany, like something recited rather than spoken.

Like something memorized.

Emrys stood in the doorway with the knife hanging forgotten at their side and listened to a child speak a language that should have been dust. Because they *had* heard it before—not live, not from a mouth, but once, years ago, in a memory they'd stolen from a dying antiquarian in the capital: a fragment of a phonograph-wax recording, or the magical equivalent, of a scholar reading from a stone. The antiquarian had wept while he listened, and Emrys, young and greedy, had skimmed past the tears to get to the rarer memories underneath.

The stone had been from Thornfell. Pre-kingdom. The scholar had called the tongue *the first speech of the mountain folk*, and he'd called it dead, and he'd said the word *dead* the way you say a word when you're glad it's dead, because dead things can't argue with the histories written over them.

The child on the doorstep murmured on, her breath fogging in the cold, her bare feet twitching with dreams, and every word she said landed in Emrys's chest like a stone dropped down a well they hadn't known was inside them.

She should not exist. This language should not exist. And yet here both were, delivered to the one door in the mountains behind which lived a person whose entire livelihood depended on things that should not exist staying buried.

Emrys crouched, laid the knife on the step—empty hands, no threat—and gathered the girl up. She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of woodsmoke and road dust and, beneath it, something mineral, something like the air inside caves. Her murmuring didn't stop. If anything it deepened, settling into a cadence, and though Emrys couldn't parse a single phrase, they caught one sound that repeated, over and over, at the end of each cycle like a bell:

*Aldric. Aldric. Aldric.*

No. Not quite. Older than that. A root-word, maybe, from which a king's name had grown like a weed over a grave.

Emrys carried her inside, wrapped her in the good blanket, the one they'd stolen the money for and never bought, and fed her broth spoonful by spoonful while she slept on. She never woke. But when the broth touched her lips, her murmuring changed key—softened, slowed—and Emrys sat back on their heels and looked at this impossible cargo and felt the walls of their carefully built life develop their first crack.

Because there were only three kinds of people who came up this mountain: lost ones, sent ones, and bait.

And every instinct Emrys owned—the instincts Corvin had honed, the instincts that had kept them alive through nine years of harvesting the sleeping rich—was screaming that this child was not lost.

She had been sent. Or she had been *let go*, the way you open a cage door and pretend the bird flew free.

Outside, far below in the valley, so distant it might have been imagination, a hunting horn sounded once and stopped.

Emrys barred the door, sat down facing it with the knife across their knees, and waited for morning, listening to a dead language breathe.
