## 2. A Tongue Older Than Crowns

The memory wouldn't sell.

Emrys had tried three times in the week since the child arrived. Three times they'd sat at their worktable with the girl's recollection coiled in a wax-sealed vial, turning it in the lamplight like a jeweler appraising a stone of dubious provenance. Collectors paid for legible lives—a magistrate's first kill, a duchess's wedding night, a soldier's last morning. What they did not pay for was noise.

And that's what it was. Emrys had sampled it once, pressing a fingertip to the opened seal and letting a single thread of it crawl up into their mind. It wasn't images. It wasn't even language, not any language Emrys knew, and after twenty years in the trade they could parse High Court drawl and dockside gutter-argot and the hand-signs of the salt-guilds. This was sound shaped like meaning, cadence without content—*shel athu renn, shel athu renn*—repeated with the metronome patience of a child who has said it ten thousand times and will say it ten thousand more.

It made their teeth ache. Not metaphorically. Their molars throbbed for an hour afterward, deep as a toothache, and when they spat into the basin the water tasted faintly of iron and old stone.

So: unsellable. Which meant, by the cold arithmetic Emrys had lived by since Corvin first put a harvesting needle in their hand at fourteen, the child was dead weight. Worse than dead weight—she ate like a grown man, slept twelve hours a day, and had the unnerving habit of standing at the window at dawn, watching the treeline, as if she were waiting for something to come out of it.

"You can't stay," Emrys told her on the fourth morning, in the flat tone they used for closing deals. The girl sat at the table, hands folded around a bowl of barley porridge, watching them with eyes the gray of river ice. "I'm taking you down to Hollowmere. There's a man there who collects oddities. He'll feed you. Probably."

She didn't cry. She didn't argue. She simply looked at Emrys for a long moment, then lifted one small hand and touched her own throat, and shook her head once.

Mute. Of course. Emrys had almost forgotten, which was itself unsettling—the child was so *quiet*, so complete in her quietness, that silence pooled around her like water around a stone.

"Fine," Emrys said. "Hollowmere anyway."

They told themselves it was efficiency. A scholar might identify the memory's origin, and provenance raised price even on strange goods. That was all. That was the whole of it.

The lie sat comfortably enough for the two-day walk down out of the high passes, the girl riding on Emrys's pack like she'd been born to it, her small weight warm between their shoulder blades.

* * *

Brannoc's shop occupied the ground floor of a leaning house in Hollowmere's tanner quarter, wedged between a chandler and a shuttered countinghouse, its windows so thick with dust and stacked folios that the interior glowed brown even at noon. Brannoc himself was a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers and the myopic squint of someone who had read himself nearly blind, and he greeted Emrys with the wary courtesy he reserved for customers who paid in stolen goods.

"Vasquez." He didn't offer his hand; he never did. Memory-thieves made scholars nervous, the way crows made farmers nervous. "What have you brought me."

"Not goods." Emrys set the girl down on a stool amid the clutter. She sat perfectly still, hands in her lap, surveying the towers of books with what might have been approval. "A question. Listen to this and tell me what tongue it is."

Brannoc sighed the sigh of a man whose afternoon had just been ruined, but he leaned in when Emrys nodded to the child.

The girl looked at him. Looked at Emrys. Then she spoke.

"*Shel athu renn. Shel athu renn, kor vel issa. Shel athu renn, kor vel issa, dan morath kel.*"

Emrys had heard it a dozen times now and it still landed wrong, that voice coming out of so small a body—level, unhurried, old. Brannoc went rigid. Emrys watched it happen: the scholar's shoulders climbing toward his ears, his breath stopping somewhere in the middle of his chest, the color draining from his face in stages, like water leaving a lock.

"Where," Brannoc said, when he could speak again, "did you get this child?"

"She got herself onto my doorstep. What is she saying?"

"That is Thornfell." Brannoc wiped his palms on his robe, though they weren't visibly sweating. Emrys noted it anyway. "Not the Thornfell of the border lords, not the court-Tongue your grandmother might have sung to you. Pre-kingdom. First-speech. It died before Aldric's grandfather wore the crown. There are perhaps four inscriptions in the whole mountain range that use it, and I have spent eleven years failing to translate two of them." He laughed, a thin, unhappy sound. "Eleven years. And now a child walks in off the road conjugating it fluently."

"What is she saying?" Emrys asked again, slower.

Brannoc didn't answer immediately. He came around his desk, moving carefully, the way men move near things they're afraid of breaking—or things they're afraid will break them—and knelt in front of the girl. She regarded him without fear.

"Child," he said gently, "do you know what you're saying? Do you know the words mean anything?"

She tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she reached up and touched him—one fingertip against his temple, the way Emrys had seen harvesters touch a sleeper's brow before the needle goes in—and Brannoc shuddered like a man stepped into cold water.

When he stood, he did it in stages, using the desk.

"It's a litany," he said. His voice had gone hoarse. "A binding-litany. Vasquez, do you know what the crown of Thornfell actually *is*? Not the gold. The thing under the gold. Every king who wears it loses something—ask any court physician, they'll tell you it's melancholy, it's the burden of rule. It isn't. The crown eats. One soul per reign, devoured slowly, and the binding that chains it—that keeps it from eating past the king, from eating the whole court, the whole city, the whole—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. Started again, quieter. "The binding was laid in the first speech. By the first keepers. Before there was a kingdom, there were keepers, and they bound the thing in the crown with words, and those words are what she is saying. Over and over. Like a prayer. Like a *lock being turned*."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled past; somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up.

"She's mute," Emrys said. It came out stupidly. "She doesn't talk. Except that."

"No." Brannoc was staring at the girl with an expression Emrys recognized from collectors seeing a memory they wanted: hunger, terror, and greed fused into one face. "No, I don't think she's mute at all. I think everything else has been taken out of her, and that litany is what's left. I think she may be the last vessel of it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? If the binding weakens—if the crown needs renewing—they would need a vessel. Someone who carries the litany in their bones. And if she escaped whoever was keeping her—"

He didn't finish, because the knock came at the door.

Three knocks. Measured. Official. Emrys knew the rhythm before Brannoc's face finished changing, because every town in the mountains knew that rhythm: the knock of people who expected doors to open for them.

Through the dusty window, Emrys saw the gray-and-white livery. Crown wardens. Four of them, and a fifth holding the horses, and the tall one at the front wearing a captain's badge worked in silver thread.

"Brannoc," Emrys breathed. "Back room."

"There's no back—"

"*Now.*"

The girl moved faster than Emrys expected—slipped off the stool and through the curtain behind the desk without a sound, and Emrys followed her into a closet of a room that smelled of mildew and binding-glue, pulling the curtain shut a half-second before the front door opened.

"Scholar Brannoc." A woman's voice. Clear, unhurried, carrying the particular boredom of authority. "Captain Sera Voss. Crown wardens. We're seeking a runaway. A child, perhaps eight years, dark-haired. May have passed through Hollowmere."

"A child." Brannoc's voice was admirably steady. "I see few children. Books don't write themselves."

"We've been told otherwise." Paper rustled. "We're asking at every house. If you see her, you'll send word to the garrison. Immediately." A pause, and then, lighter, almost pleasant: "You look pale, scholar. Unwell?"

"Seasonal humors."

"Mm." Footsteps. Then, closer to the curtain—close enough that Emrys could hear the leather of a glove creak—Voss's voice dropped into something softer and far more dangerous. "One more thing. There are rumors of a memory-thief working the high passes. Steals from sleepers. Sells to collectors. If such a person were also harboring the child... well. The crown pays very well for both, scholar. Very well indeed. Think on it."

The footsteps retreated. The door closed. Hoofbeats faded down the street.

Emrys let out a breath they'd been holding since the knock and found the girl standing pressed against their side, her small hand fisted in their coat. When Emrys looked down, she was looking up—not afraid, Emrys realized. Watching. Reading their face the way Emrys read a sleeper's.

"She knows about me," Emrys murmured. "She's not hunting a runaway. She's hunting us. Both of us. Together."

From beyond the curtain, Brannoc's voice, shaken: "Vasquez. You should know—I couldn't help overhearing, and you should know—there's a collector in Vell. Offers extraordinary prices for pre-kingdom artifacts. Living ones, if the rumor holds." A pause. "You could be free. Both problems solved, one transaction."

Emrys looked at the girl. The girl looked back, and did the thing she'd done on the doorstep the first night: touched her own throat, then pointed, deliberate as a signature, at Emrys's chest.

*Shel athu renn,* she mouthed. No sound at all. But Emrys had heard it enough times now that the shape of it lived behind their teeth, and for the first time it didn't feel like noise.

It felt like a question.

Dread settled over Emrys then, cold and specific—not the diffuse dread of danger, but the dread of a decision already forming, hardening in the chest like cooling wax. They thought of Voss's gloved hand inches from the curtain. They thought of the price on two heads instead of one. They thought of freedom, priced and purchasable, one transaction away.

"Brannoc," Emrys said, pushing through the curtain, the girl's hand still knotted in their coat. "Tell me about the salt-cathedral."
