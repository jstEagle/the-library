## 3. What the Magistrate Forgot

The magistrate's memory had a smell to it.

That was the first thing Emrys noticed when they unstoppered the little lead vial and let the recollection breathe. Most memories were scentless—clean as rainwater, or faintly of the sleeper's sweat—but this one came off the stopper like a cellar door opening: ink, tallow smoke, and underneath it something sharper. Wax. Sealing wax, hot and sweet.

Emrys sat at their worktable with the vial cupped in both hands and did not drink it yet. Three days they'd been telling themselves they'd sell it whole, untouched, the way they sold everything. A magistrate's private recollections fetched a fine price from collectors who liked their vicarious lives respectable. *A man of consequence*, the listing would say. *His secrets included.*

But the sealing-wax smell was wrong. Memories didn't carry objects in them—not like that, not so loud. Unless the object had been pressed against the mind hard enough, recently enough, to leave an impression through the skull.

Unless the magistrate had been carrying something he'd been ordered never to forget.

"Damn it," Emrys said quietly, to the cabin, to themselves.

They drank the memory.

* * *

It opened the way all stolen memories opened: a lurch, a falling-into, and then Emrys was looking out through another man's eyes at another man's life.

Magistrate Odo Ferran, fifty-one years old, gout in his left foot, a wife who spoke to him only about accounts. Emrys knew all this already; they'd walked his sleeping mind a week ago in the town of Harrowmere and lifted three memories out of him like plums from a basket—a first kiss behind a tannery, a funeral he'd wept at more than he should have, and this one, the newest, still warm. He'd slept badly. Guilt made for shallow sleep, and shallow sleep made for easy theft. Emrys hadn't asked what the guilt was about. That was the rule: take, don't read too deep. Reading deep left fingerprints.

Now they read deep.

The memory assembled itself around them. A government office in Thornfell's administrative quarter, all grey stone and greyer light. Ferran standing before a desk, hands clasped, while a woman with close-cropped iron hair slid a document across the blotter toward him.

Captain Sera Voss. Emrys had never seen her face before, but they knew her by reputation the way thieves know the heads of guilds that hunt them. Crown warden. The crown's long arm, its long eye, its long knife.

"Ferran," she said, and her voice came through the borrowed ears flat and precise, "you will receive a rider within the fortnight. A child, traveling alone on the mountain roads. You will hold the child. You will send word to me and no one else. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand it's irregular, Captain."

"It is a matter of state." Voss set a second object on the desk: a small lead tube, sealed at both ends with black wax stamped with a sigil Emrys recognized without wanting to—the crowned mouth, the royal seal of Aldric's line. "These are your orders. Memorize them. Then burn them."

Ferran broke the seal. And here the memory did something strange—it *brightened*. Because Ferran, obedient little clerk of a magistrate, did exactly as instructed: he read the orders once, twice, and burned them, and the act of memorizing pressed the words into his mind so hard they glowed. Emrys felt them now, written across the inside of the stolen skull in letters of fire:

*Subject: female, approx. eight years. Speaks the old tongue. Do not harm. Do not let her speak to anyone. She is the vessel. The King requires her alive and unspoiled. Failure is treason.*

And beneath that, in Voss's own hand, one line more:

*If she reaches the salt country, kill her where she stands.*

The memory ended there—or rather, Emrys ended it, tearing themselves out of Ferran's head so fast they nearly knocked the vial off the table. They sat shaking in their own body again, in their own dim kitchen, with the taste of someone else's terror in their mouth.

*A matter of state.* A child. *She is the vessel.*

Emrys thought of the girl asleep on their doorstep two nights ago, murmuring syllables older than any kingdom, and felt the floor tilt under the cabin.

They hadn't just robbed a magistrate. They'd robbed the trap. Every hour Ferran spent unconscious and emptied, every hour he woke foggy and slow to send his letters—that delay was theirs. Emrys's greed had gummed up the machinery of a royal manhunt, and somewhere in Harrowmere a magistrate was waking each morning with a hole in his week and no idea why, and eventually—soon—he would go to Voss and confess that his memory had been tampered with, and she would know exactly what that meant.

She would know a thief had the child's trail.

Worse. She would know the thief had *her orders*.

"Oh, you fool," Emrys said, and couldn't tell which of them they meant.

* * *

Brannoc's shop stood at the crook of the river road in Lowmarsh, wedged between a cooper's yard and a chapel nobody used, its windows crammed with books stacked spine-out like cordwood. Emrys had been coming to him for eleven years—the only buyer who never asked where a memory came from, because Brannoc cared about content, not provenance. Old, half-deaf, cheerful as a grave-worm. He'd identified the girl's language in ten minutes flat and gone the color of tallow doing it.

Emrys arrived at midday with the lead tube hidden under their coat and found the street full of smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Not cookfire smoke. It had a chemical sweetness to it, oil and pitch, and it was pouring from Brannoc's windows in fat black ropes. Two figures in warden grey stood at the door checking a list against the burning inventory—one book, one scroll, one jar of memory-salts at a time, cataloguing before consigning. Methodical. Thorough. Wardens didn't burn things in anger; they burned them in *procedure*.

Emrys pulled their hood down and crossed to the far side of the street, heart hammering, and watched.

Through the broken window they could see the interior going up row by row. Eleven years of Brannoc's collection—memories he'd bought fair, memories scholars had traded him, memories of harvest festivals and dead wives and the last speaker of a dozen dying tongues—all of it curling and blackening. And Brannoc himself was not in the flames. He was kneeling on the cobbles outside, wrists bound, flanked by a third warden, and he was not struggling. He was talking. His mouth moved steadily, cheerfully even, and the warden beside him kept leaning away from whatever he was saying, and Emrys would have given a year of stolen youth to hear it.

Then Voss stepped out of the shop.

She moved like the office memory had promised—no wasted motion, no heat. She stood over Brannoc and said something short. Brannoc shook his head, smiling. Voss studied him for a long moment with the patience of someone reading a difficult page, and then she turned her head, slowly, deliberately, and looked directly across the street at the hooded figure by the cooper's fence.

Emrys went very still.

Voss held the look for three heartbeats. Four. Then she raised one hand—not pointing, not gesturing, just lifting it slightly, the way you'd acknowledge a debt—and turned back to her work, and the wardens mounted up, and the shop burned on behind them, and they rode north without hurry, because they knew exactly where they were going and saw no reason to gallop.

Emrys stood in the smoke-shadow until the hoofbeats faded, then crossed the road fast and knelt by Brannoc.

"They cut the bindings when I told them I'd scream the whole street awake otherwise," the old man rasped. Soot streaked his face; his eyes were wet, but his voice was steady. "Took the ledgers. Left the rest." He laughed, a dry crackle. "Eleven years of inventory. Well. It was only ever other people's lives, wasn't it? Easy come."

"Brannoc—"

"You're the reason, boy." He said it without venom, which was worse. "The litany-girl. Voss asked me who brought her in. I told her nothing, but she'd already read my purchase records—your name's in them, spelled wrong, but in them. So." He looked past Emrys at the fire eating his life's work. "You stole from the wrong sleeper. Or the right one. Depends how the story ends."

"What did the orders say?" Emrys asked. "In the tube. Did they show you—"

"I don't need to read sealed orders." Brannoc gripped Emrys's wrist with surprising strength. "I read the language she speaks. I told you: pre-kingdom Thornfell. Binding-litany. Now think, thief, use that cold clever head of yours—what does a binding need after a hundred years?" His grip tightened. "A key. Or a lock. And the crown has been hungry a long, long time."

Behind him the roof beam gave way in a fountain of sparks. Brannoc watched it fall with the expression of a man closing a ledger.

"There's a collector in Harrowmere," he said, "who's been asking after a child who talks in her sleep. Offering enough coin to buy a pardon. Word travels faster than wardens, boy. Decide which road you're selling her down before someone decides for you."

He pushed himself up, brushed ash from his knees, and shuffled off toward the chapel that nobody used, carrying nothing.

Emrys stayed in the street until the fire burned itself down to a red skeleton, and the whole time their hands wouldn't stop shaking, because they kept seeing it: the magistrate's desk, the black wax, the words pressed into a sleeping man's skull like a brand. *She is the vessel.* Emrys had lifted that memory out of pure habit, pure appetite, priced it, and carried it home to sit on a shelf—and every day it sat there, a magistrate forgot a little harder, a captain waited a little longer, and a child slept on in a stranger's cabin, murmuring the one language in the world that could unmake a king.

Greed had lit the fuse. Emrys had always known that about themselves. They'd just never stood close enough to feel the heat before.

They walked back toward the mountains through the afternoon, and by the time the cabin's chimney came into view, they had stopped shaking and started counting—which was, for Emrys, the same thing as deciding.

There was a collector in Harrowmere offering a fortune. There was a captain riding north with all the time in the world. And there was a girl sitting on the doorstep waiting for them, watching the treeline, humming her dead language to herself like a lullaby.

Emrys looked at her for a long moment.

"We need to talk about money," they said.
