## 4. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

Emrys did the math on the back of a smuggler's ledger, in pencil, because pencil could be erased and ink was a confession.

*Item: one child, mute, pre-kingdom tongue, recites binding-litany. Provenance unknown. Condition: frightened, underfed, watches me like I'm the weather.*

*Buyer one: Captain Sera Voss, crown wardens. Price: my freedom. Possibly a pardon. Certainly no more looking over my shoulder on mountain roads.*

*Buyer two: Master Quillon of the Amber House, collector of rare memories. Offer received by courier hawk two days ago: three hundred crowns for "any intact first-tongue recitation, unspoiled." He didn't know what he was bidding on. Three hundred crowns is a cabin with a new roof. Three hundred crowns is ten years of not caring who's climbing the switchbacks.*

*Item: what happens to the child afterward.*

Emrys set down the pencil.

That was the flaw in every ledger they'd ever kept. There was always a line at the bottom that wouldn't balance, and they'd learned to write *afterward* in small letters and look away from it. The magistrate's memory had sold clean because the magistrate would never know what he'd lost. A child sold to Quillon would know. A child handed to Voss—

They looked up. The girl sat on the floor by the cold hearth, knees drawn up, turning Emrys's spare wool sock over in her hands as if it were an artifact from a drowned civilization. She'd been there four days now. Four days of porridge and silence and the litany murmured into her pillow at night, soft as snowfall, in a language that made the cabin timbers creak if she said it too close to the walls.

Emrys had tested it once more, carefully, through the door while she slept. The child's mind was not like other minds. Other minds were houses with doors; you picked the lock, walked in, took what you wanted off the shelves. This child's mind was a well. Emrys had leaned over the edge and seen their own reflection looking back up at them, and beneath the reflection, far down, something vast and salt-white turning slowly like a wheel.

You couldn't steal from a well. You could only fall in.

"Three hundred crowns," Emrys said aloud, just to hear how stupid it sounded.

The girl looked up. She held out the sock, offering it back, solemn as a magistrate returning a seal.

"I don't want the sock."

She put it down between them anyway, a gift formally tendered, and went back to watching the fire Emrys hadn't lit yet because firelight showed through the shutters and Voss's riders were combing the valley. Four days, and the wardens had already been to Brannoc's. Four days, and the smoke over the low country still hadn't fully cleared in Emrys's imagination.

That was the other line item. *Cost: Brannoc's shop.* Emrys had replayed the warden captain's memory again last night—the stolen one, lifted off her sleeping mind at the Wayhouse Meridian a season back, back when she'd been just another rich officer with interesting nightmares. In it, Sera Voss stood in a burning archive and read a sealed order aloud to her lieutenant, her voice flat with duty and something underneath the flatness:

*"Recover the vessel alive. The litany must be preserved intact. Casualties among intermediaries are authorized."*

*Intermediaries.* That was Brannoc. That was the ferryman at Grey Ford, probably, and the hawk-courier, and anyone else who'd touched the chain. And when the chain was gathered up and burned, the last link would be the thief in the mountain cabin who'd pulled the child out of the snow.

Sell the child to Voss, and Emrys bought a pardon with a shop-ash smell on it. Sell the child to Quillon, and Emrys bought a roof and a decade of sleep, and somewhere in Thornfell's records a line item marked *vessel: dispersed, value realized.*

Or.

There was always an *or*, and the *or* never paid.

* * *

It rained that evening, a thin cold rain that turned the world outside the windows into grey wool. Emrys boiled barley and the girl ate hers in small precise spoonfuls, wasting nothing, a hunger managed rather than indulged. Someone had taught her that. Someone who hadn't been able to feed her.

Afterward she did the thing she'd started doing on the second night. She crawled close—not touching, never touching—and pressed two fingers against Emrys's wrist, right where the pulse lived, and waited. Asking permission in the only grammar she had.

Every time before, Emrys had pulled away. Tonight, running the arithmetic and hating every column of it, they didn't.

The well opened.

Not this time the dark wheel turning far below. This time she gave Emrys something, deliberately, the way you hand someone bread across a table: a memory-vision, whole and bright-edged, laid into Emrys's mind like a coin into a palm.

Salt.

A cathedral built of salt, or something like salt—translucent, faintly gold at the edges, its spires dissolving upward into light instead of ending. It stood on a plain of white crystal that cracked and sang underfoot. Inside, beneath a vault like frozen spray, stood a king.

He was young. That was the wrongness of it, the thing that made Emrys's breath catch even inside the vision—young, broad-shouldered, wearing a crown that did not sit on his head so much as *grip* it, filaments of pale metal running down past his ears into his jaw, into his throat, vanishing beneath his collar like roots into soil. He was kneeling at an altar of white stone, and he was weeping, and the tears cut channels through the dust on his face, and his mouth was moving.

He was reciting. The same cadence the girl murmured into her pillow. But where her voice was snowfall, his was a man being unmade politely, syllable by syllable, and the crown drank each syllable as it left him, and grew, very slightly, brighter.

Around the altar stood others—robed, hooded, faces shadowed—and their hands were raised, and Emrys understood with a certainty that arrived whole, like a memory of their own, that these were not tormentors. They were witnesses. They were *recording*. Binding something so enormous required witnesses, and the litany was the record, and the record had to survive somewhere the crown could never reach.

Somewhere small. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere passed mouth to mouth down generations of the overlooked, in a tongue the kingdom had buried.

The vision folded closed like a book.

Emrys came back to the cabin with wet eyes and rain on the roof and the girl's fingers still resting on their pulse, patient, asking nothing.

"That king," Emrys said. Their voice came out scraped. "In the crown. Is he still alive in there?"

The girl tilted her head. Then she did something new: she pointed at Emrys's chest, then at her own, then made a slow closing motion with both hands—a door shutting, a ledger closing, a thing swallowed.

*Inside. Like you. Like me.*

Then she opened her hands again, slowly, and looked at Emrys with an expression no child should have to wear: the expression of someone showing you the only valuable thing they own and waiting to see if you'll take it.

Emrys had spent fifteen years teaching themselves that every transaction had a price and every price could be paid by someone else. They had a ledger full of columns proving it. And here was a child offering up the one thing the crown wanted, the one thing collectors would burn counties for, laying it on the floor between them next to the returned sock, and the arithmetic wouldn't work. There was no number. Every buyer's price came back *insufficient*, and Emrys sat there understanding, with the particular nausea of a professional confronted with amateur honesty, that some things were not inventory.

"You're not merchandise," Emrys said. It sounded foolish in their own mouth, a smuggler's oath in reverse.

The girl smiled. Small, crooked, the first one. It transformed her face entirely and Emrys hated how much it cost them to see it.

* * *

They packed by lamplight, quietly, telling themselves a story as they worked. That was the last skill Corvin had taught them, though he'd never named it as such: *the job isn't done until you've told yourself the story that lets you sleep.* Steal from a magistrate? You're redistributing. Burn your mentor's ledger? You're pruning. Walk a mute child down out of the mountains toward the one place in the world the crown wants her most?

You're delivering goods. High-risk cargo. There's a fee involved. It's still a transaction.

Emrys wrote the terms in the ledger, in pencil, because pencil could be erased:

*Item: escort services, one vessel, destination salt-cathedral ruins, southern passes. Fee: to be negotiated upon delivery. Risk: extreme. Buyer: none. Seller: none.*

*Note: this is still a transaction.*

They erased the note. They wrote it again. They left it.

At dawn the rain had stopped and the mountains stood washed and merciless under a sky like rinsed glass. Emrys dressed for the road—soft boots, layered grey, the knife they'd carried since Corvin put it in their hand at fourteen and said *this is the only honest tool, everything else lies*. The girl watched from the doorway, wearing the too-big coat Emrys had cut down for her, holding the sock.

"We're going south," Emrys told her. "Down through the Thornfell passes. There are people who can help—or people we can avoid. Either way, we move today, because Voss's riders found the ford at Grey Rock yesterday and this valley has maybe two days left in it as a hiding place."

The girl nodded as if she'd understood every word. Perhaps she had. Children who memorize dead languages tend to understand more than they say.

"One condition." Emrys crouched to her height. "If it comes to choosing—me or the mission—I walk away. I've spent my whole life surviving. It's the one trade I'm good at. Don't make me bad at it on your account."

The girl considered this gravely. Then she reached out and pressed her fingers, briefly, to Emrys's wrist—pulse to pulse—and shook her head.

*No,* the gesture said. *That term is rejected.*

"Negotiation's over," Emrys muttered, standing. "Worst counterparty I've ever dealt with."

They took the south trail as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, two figures small against the scree, and behind them the cabin sat empty with its ledger on the table, one line item left open, fee blank, the pencil lying beside it like a dare.

By noon they reached the first of the old waystones, a leaning finger of granite crusted with lichen, and the girl stopped beside it and went rigid, her lips parting around the first syllable of the litany—and far off, deep under the mountain's skin, something answered.

A hum. Faint, mineral, patient. Like a cathedral clearing its throat after a thousand years of silence.

Emrys put a hand on the girl's shoulder and scanned the pass ahead, and saw, on the ridge line to the west, a single rider where no rider should be—still, watchful, wearing the crown's grey.

"Not yet," Emrys breathed. "We're not ready for *yet*."

They turned east, off the map, into the passes that remembered.
