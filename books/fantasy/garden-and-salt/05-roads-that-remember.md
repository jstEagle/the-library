## 5. Roads That Remember

The Thornfell passes did not forgive. Emrys had known that since they were nineteen and Corvin first dragged them up a goat track in the dark, saying: *The mountains keep what the cities lose.* They'd thought it was a lesson about hiding. It took them three days of climbing with a mute child on their back to understand it was a warning.

The girl—Emrys still called her *girl*, because she hadn't given them anything else—rode in a sling against their chest like contraband, which was what she was. Her weight was nothing. A sparrow's weight. But her silence pressed down on them heavier than any pack they'd ever carried out of a rich man's house.

She hadn't spoken since Brannoc's shop burned. She hadn't needed to. When Emrys had lifted her over the back fence while the wardens' torches turned the scholar's books to drifting black snow, she had looked at them with those gray eyes and made one small sound—not a word, not quite—a note, low in her throat, like a struck bell heard through a wall. And the fire behind them had guttered, just for a breath, as if something enormous had inhaled.

Emrys didn't let themselves think about that too long. Thinking led to questions. Questions led to the arithmetic, and the arithmetic only worked if you didn't look at the numbers too closely.

They climbed north through the spine of the old kingdom, where the smuggler roads ran. Not roads, really—Corvin would have laughed at the word. Threads. Paths worn into the rock by feet that had never been counted by any census, mapped in no warden's ledger, remembered only by the people who used them and the stone itself. Emrys knew eleven ways across the Thornfells. They had bought six of them from dead men's memories and learned five with their own blisters.

On the third morning, near a waystone, the girl began to hum.

* * *

The waystones were older than the kingdom. Everyone said so; nobody knew who said it first. Knee-high pillars of gray granite standing at intervals along the passes, each carved with a spiral that wound inward like water going down a drain. Farmers left bread at them. Smugglers left nothing, because smugglers knew better than to owe debts to things older than debt.

This one stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut, furred with lichen, its spiral worn nearly smooth. The girl squirmed out of the sling before Emrys could stop her and put both palms flat against the stone.

The humming stopped. What replaced it was worse.

Words. Low, rhythmic, in that dead first-tongue that had come out of her sleeping mouth on Emrys's doorstep—the language Brannoc had identified with shaking hands before he went pale as tallow. The binding-litany. She spoke it now with her eyes open, her small voice steady as a plumb line, and the words were not just sounds anymore. Emrys felt them in their teeth.

Then the mountain answered.

There was no better word for it. A vibration came up through Emrys's boots, deep and slow, a pulse under the rock like a heartbeat in a chest the size of the range. Lichen shivered off the waystone. Somewhere above them, snow let go of a ledge in a long white sigh. The girl's voice rose, and the pulse rose with it, and for three full seconds Emrys stood inside a sound that seemed to come from everywhere at once—from below, from the stone, from the deep places where salt and root and dark water tangled together.

Then it stopped. The girl took her hands off the stone. She looked up at Emrys, and there was something in her face that was not fear and was not joy but some third thing Emrys had no name for, and she pointed north. Up the pass. Toward the salt country.

"No," Emrys said, out loud, to a child who couldn't hear them. "We're going west. West is the coast. West is a collector named Halvard who pays in unmarked silver and asks no questions."

The girl kept pointing north.

Emrys walked west. The girl sat down in the middle of the path and did not move.

It took an hour. Emrys paced, swore, tried bribery with dried apricots, tried the cold merchant's logic they'd rehearsed—*you're cargo, cargo goes where I say*—and got nowhere, because the girl simply waited, patient as the waystone itself, until Emrys ran out of arguments and found, to their considerable disgust, that they had run out of will too.

They went north.

*Telling yourself it's still a transaction,* they thought grimly, hauling her back into the sling. *Fine. New route. Longer route. Route with a detour through whatever the hell that was. Still a transaction.*

The mountains, very faintly, under everything, seemed to be keeping time.

* * *

For four days they followed the old litany-road. Emrys told themselves it was strategic—the northern passes were less patrolled, the river crossings shallower, the warden garrisons thinner. It was even true. It was also true that every time they passed a waystone, the girl touched it, and the litany came out of her a little stronger, and the stone under their feet answered a little louder, and Emrys felt something in their own chest shift like furniture being moved in a locked room.

Because here was the thing Emrys did not say aloud, not even to the wind: the memories were moving.

A thief learns their hoard the way a miser learns his coins—by touch, by weight, in the dark. Every stolen memory Emrys carried had its shelf, its shape, its weight. The magistrate's garden with the crooked pear tree. A fisherman's knowledge of thirty years of tides. A bride's terror and delight walking down a nave. Emrys could close their eyes and walk the aisles of their own skull and know exactly what was where.

Since the Kestrel Cut, the shelves were rearranging. Not losing anything—nothing gone—but shifting, settling, the way a house settles on its foundations after an earthquake. And when the girl chanted near the stones, Emrys could have sworn certain memories grew warm. The magistrate's garden. Always the garden. As if something in the deep rock knew that memory was there and was reading its spine.

"You're doing this," Emrys muttered on the fifth evening, crouched by a fire no larger than two fists, watching the girl sleep. "Somehow. Whatever's in you, it's leaking."

The girl slept on. In her sleep her lips moved, shaping silent syllables, and the fire leaned toward her like a listener.

Emrys banked the coals and did not sleep much.

* * *

The river crossing at Sorrel Ford was the last good place to turn west before the high salt country closed the options down to one. Emrys knew the ford the way they knew their own hands—which was to say, someone else's hands, once. A drover's memory, harvested off a sleeping cattle-man in Lowmark seven years ago: forty summers of crossings, every sandbar, every sinkhole, every season the water lied about its depth. Emrys had bought that memory cheap and used it maybe twice, and it sat on its shelf fat and reliable as a purse of gold.

They came down to the ford at dusk, meaning to cross at first light. Which was the mistake. Dusk was when you scouted, not when you arrived; Corvin had taught them that too, and like most of Corvin's lessons, Emrys had only ever learned it properly by breaking it.

The lanterns came out of the alder brake on the far bank. Six of them. Then the shapes behind the lanterns: gray cloaks, the crossed-keys badge of the crown wardens catching the light. And in the center, a tall figure who did not need a lantern, standing with the stillness of someone who has already won.

Captain Sera Voss.

"Thief!" Her voice carried flat across the water, unhurried. "You're carrying crown property. Set the child down and walk away, and I'll write you down as a witness instead of a traitor. You know I keep my word. You've been in my head—you know exactly how I keep my word."

That was true, and it was the worst part. Emrys had stolen from Voss once, two years ago, in a border inn—a routine lift, a captain's memory of terrain and troop rotations. What they'd gotten instead was Voss entire: her father's forge, her brother's drowning, the day she'd taken the warden oath and meant it. Emrys had sold none of it. Some memories you couldn't sell, because selling them felt like letting someone else wear a person's grief as a coat.

Voss didn't know that. Voss only knew her secrets had been read by a stranger.

"I'm not giving her to the crown," Emrys called back.

"The crown isn't what wants her." For the first time, something moved in Voss's voice—something almost like doubt, quickly killed. "Give her up, thief. Before you learn what actually wears the crown."

The girl's hand found Emrys's collar. Her grip was iron. And low, barely audible, she began the litany—not loud, not calling the mountain this time, just holding on to the words like a rope.

Emrys did the math. Six wardens, a ford, a hundred yards of open gravel, night falling. Even with the drover's memory—even knowing every stone under the black water—six arrows beat one thief. Unless.

Unless they spent something.

Every thief knows the technique. Burning a memory—really burning it, not trading it, not selling it, but feeding it whole into the body's furnace—is the oldest trick in the trade and the one Corvin forbade. *You burn a memory, you don't get the life back,* he'd said. *You get the skill without the man. And a little less of you every time, because the fire takes fuel from both sides.* Emrys had burned exactly one memory in nine years: a pickpocket's fingers, to escape a debt collector in Marshport. They'd missed the pickpocket afterward like a missing tooth.

The drover's memory weighed more than a pickpocket's fingers. Forty summers of a man's craft, his pride, his patience, his slow love of a river he'd worked his whole life. If Emrys burned it, they'd have the crossing—every sandbar, every lie the water told—burned into muscle and nerve for the next ten minutes.

And then it would be ash, and the man would be ash, and Emrys would carry the hole where he'd been.

"Hold on," Emrys whispered to the girl. "Whatever happens, hold on."

They reached into the hoard, found the shelf, and set it alight.

It didn't hurt, exactly. It hurt like cold does—like something leaving. Forty summers poured through them in a single breath: ice-fords and spring floods, a wife's name (Maret, her name was Maret), the taste of river water in a wooden cup, the particular way cattle sang when they were tired. Emrys felt the man go—felt him go *through* them, generous to the last—and then their boots were moving, and the river was no longer a black unknown but an old friend with all its flaws showing.

Left of the big stone, the bar runs true. Third channel lies about its depth. The far cut is waist-deep but the bottom holds.

They crossed like a rumor. Arrows came—two, three, slapping into the water where a man without forty years of river-sense would have been. The girl clung, silent, her litany a thread of warmth against Emrys's neck. On the far bank Emrys ran up through the reeds until the lanterns were sparks and the sparks were gone, and then, only then, they stopped and bent double and breathed.

The drover was gone. Emrys reached for the shelf and found smooth wall. No Maret. No river-song of tired cattle. Just a hole, clean-edged, and around the hole a terrible new understanding:

It had been easy. That was the horror of it. Nine years ago they'd wept over a pickpocket's fingers. Tonight they'd burned a whole man and their hands hadn't even shaken.

*Habit becoming choice,* Corvin's voice said somewhere, smug and dead. *That's how thieves are finished, boy. Not caught. Finished.*

Except. Except the girl was looking up at them in the dark, and her small hand was pressed flat over Emrys's heart, right where the ache was, and she held it there a long moment—deliberate, solemn, like a bandage—before pointing north again.

Toward the salt country. Toward the cathedral ruins that stood over the caves where, if Brannoc's dying notes were true, the first king had knelt and let a crown drink.

"All right," Emrys said hoarsely. "North. But hear me—I'm not spending anyone else. Not you. Not me. Whatever's coming, we walk in with what we've got left."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she nodded once, and pointed again, and this time Emrys noticed her finger wasn't aimed at the pass.

It was aimed at the road behind them. Downriver. Where, far off in the black, a single lantern was moving—not following the ford, not following the bank, but cutting straight across country, unerring, toward them.

Someone who knew exactly where they were. Someone who'd always known.

Emrys knew only one person who could find a thief across a hundred miles of dark by the smell of what the thief carried.

"Corvin," they said, and the word fell into the cold like a coin down a well.
