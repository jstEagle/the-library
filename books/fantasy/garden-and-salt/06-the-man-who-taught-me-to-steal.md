## 6. The Man Who Taught Me to Steal

The watchtower found us before Corvin did.

It stood on the shoulder of the pass like a broken tooth, older than the kingdom, its stones furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Issa stopped when she saw it and would not go around. She walked straight to it, small hand trailing along the wall, and pressed her palm flat against the gate-stone the way you'd greet an old dog.

"It remembers her," Emrys told herself. Not me. Her.

We sheltered in the tower's ground room because the wind had teeth and my ribs still ached from the river crossing, where I'd burned a memory of my father's face to buy us thirty seconds of terrain I no longer knew. That's the arithmetic they never teach you: every escape costs a past.

I woke near midnight because the fire had gone quiet.

Not out—quiet. The flames burned straight up, unnaturally still, and the cold came in anyway, sliding under the door like something that had been invited. Issa was awake too, sitting up with her blanket clutched at her throat, staring at the stairwell that climbed into the dark.

I knew that cold. I'd learned to steal inside it.

"Corvin," I said, to the dark, because there was no point pretending otherwise. He'd never once surprised me by arriving. He arrived the way weather arrives—you felt it coming for days and pretended you didn't.

He came down the stairs without hurry. He looked exactly as he always had, which was the first obscenity of it—a man sixty years old wearing a face that hadn't aged since I was nineteen, smooth as a coin rubbed by too many hands. His eyes were the same gray as mine. He'd given them to me, in a way; the first thing he ever stole from me was my reflection, and he kept it.

"Emrys." My name in his mouth sounded like a debt being called in. "You've gotten sentimental. A child. A scholar's funeral pyre. You're leaving footprints a blind man could follow."

"You burned Brannoc's shop."

"The crown burned it. I held the lantern." He said it without shame, which was worse than shame. "That's the lesson you never learned, girl. Tools don't grieve."

"She's not a tool."

"No." He looked at Issa then, and his expression changed—not softened, *focused*, the way a man looks at a strongbox he's been hired to open. "She's a key. And keys are worth more than tools. Which is why I'm going to take her, and you're going to let me, because somewhere in that hoard of yours is the memory of what happens to people who don't."

* * *

Here is what Corvin taught me, in the ten years I was his: memory is a weapon before it is anything else.

A stolen recollection isn't a story. It's a blade with someone else's blood already on it. You can cut a man with his own childhood. You can gut him with his mother's voice. I have watched Corvin end negotiations by simply *giving*—pressing into a magistrate's mind the memory of the day he wept at his father's coffin, unasked, unwanted, until the man broke just to make it stop.

So when he sat down across my dying fire and folded his hands, I knew the duel had already begun. The talking was just the whetstone.

"You remember the night I found you," he said. "Of course you do. It's yours now. But it was mine first—I took it from your mother while she slept, the night before she sold you to the workhouse. Would you like it back? Her face, the night she decided?"

"Don't."

"She didn't cry, you know. That's the detail everyone gets wrong in the retelling. She was relieved."

He pushed it at me—not gently, Corvin never did anything gently—and my own mind betrayed me by opening. I saw her: thin, tired, sitting on the edge of a bed in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and feeling *lighter*. I heard her think, clear as a bell: *one less mouth.*

I staggered. The fire spat. And Corvin watched me with the patience of a man who has done this a thousand times, waiting to see if I'd break or bite.

I bit.

I gave him back the night he taught me to harvest—the real one, not the version he remembered. In his telling, he was kind that night; in mine, I kept the truth he'd made me burn: that the sleeping woman whose memories we took was his daughter, that he'd sold her dreams for eleven years and told himself each time it was medicine, that the last thing she ever said to him before her mind went soft was *"Papa, I can't find myself in here anymore."*

He flinched. Actually flinched, the great Corvin, the crown's hollow knife, and for one heartbeat the firelight showed me the old man underneath the coin-smooth face.

"There she is," I said. "There's the man who taught me."

"I taught you survival."

"You taught me theft and called it mercy. You taught me that taking is easier than keeping. You—" I stopped, because he was pushing again, harder now, and this time it wasn't a wound he offered.

It was a gift.

My nineteenth winter. A tavern in the lowlands. A boy with flour on his hands who laughed at my terrible card tricks and asked my name, and I gave him a false one, and for four months I was almost happy, and then Corvin found me and whispered *you know what you are* into my ear, and I left before dawn without saying goodbye, and I have spent fifteen years telling myself I left to protect him.

Corvin pressed the rest into my skull: the boy grown into a man, running a bakery two towns over, a wife, a daughter, a scarred knuckle from the time he punched a wall after I vanished. And underneath it all, laid bare where only a thief could see: he still checked the road some mornings. Still watched for a woman with quick hands who could never be bothered to learn a card trick properly.

"He asks about you," Corvin said softly. "Every year. On the anniversary. I know because I've been to him. I've stood over his sleeping family and read him like a ledger, and Emrys—he keeps a box of your things. A ribbon. A crooked die. Fifteen years."

"Why are you showing me this?" My voice came out wrong.

"Because I want you to understand what I'm offering." He leaned forward, firelight crawling on his too-young face. "Give me the girl. Walk away clean. And I'll give you this life. All of it. Every morning in that bakery, every ordinary year—I'll pour it into you whole, and you'll live it as if it were yours, because it will be, because I'll have taken it from the man who's living it now and he'll wake up tomorrow with a hole where his happiness used to be and never know what he lost."

The horror of it took a moment to arrive, and when it did, it arrived completely. He wasn't threatening me. He was *bribing* me—with theft, with the exact crime that built me, dressed up as rescue.

"That's not a life," I said. "That's a costume."

"It's more than you have."

And the worst part—the part I will carry longer than any wound—is that for three full heartbeats, I wanted it. I stood in that ruined tower and let myself want a warm kitchen and flour-dusted mornings and a child calling someone *mother*, and I understood, finally, with total clarity, what Corvin had made me: a person so empty that happiness itself looked like something to be stolen rather than grown.

Issa moved.

She crossed the room between the fire and Corvin, small and silent, and put her hand on his knee. And she *pushed*.

* * *

What she gave him, I caught only in fragments, because it passed through the air between them like heat off coals:

A king on a salt-white throne, weeping. A crown descending toward his head like a mouth opening. Generations of devoured souls screaming behind his eyes, and at the center of the screaming, a voice that said, in Thornfell's dead first-tongue, in a tone almost gentle: *I am patient. I am hungry. Bring me the vessel.*

Corvin went white. Coin-smooth face cracking like dropped porcelain.

"You've seen it," he whispered. "You've actually seen it. Then you know it can't be fought. You know what it does to the ones who try." He looked at me, and for the first time in twenty years, Corvin looked afraid. "I'm not its thief, Emrys. I'm its *larder*. It eats through me the way it eats through everything, and it has been eating me for forty years, and I am so tired—"

"Then stop feeding it."

"I can't. You don't stop being a knife by deciding to be a hand." He rose, and the cold came with him, and his face reassembled itself into the mask I'd known all my life. "Salt-cathedral. I know where you're going. I'll be there ahead of you. When you arrive—and you will arrive, because you're mine, Emrys, I made you, and everything I made comes back to me—one of us will finally settle what we owe."

He paused at the stairwell.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the boy in the bakery. I lied about the box. There's no box. He married, he forgot, he's happy. I needed leverage, and lies are cheaper than truths."

He climbed into the dark, and the cold went with him, and the fire remembered how to move.

I sat shaking. Issa climbed into my lap, and I held her, and I told myself the relief I felt was because he was gone and not because there was no box.

But even as I held her, I was doing the arithmetic I'd been doing since the river: Corvin would reach the cathedral first. Corvin would tell the crown. Whatever waited for us in the salt-dark below those ruins would be waiting *for* us, arranged, prepared—

And Voss. Where was Voss?

I looked at Issa. She looked back, and in the firelight her lips moved, shaping words in the dead tongue, and though I couldn't translate them, I understood their shape the way you understand a held breath.

Dawn was four hours away. The pass beyond the tower led down toward the coast, toward the salt-caves, toward the place where a king had wept and a crown had learned to eat.

We would walk into it anyway. Some debts you pay walking forward.
