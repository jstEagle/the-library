## 7. Salt in the Wound of the World

The wound went bad on the second day.

Emrys had taken a warden's blade through the meat of their left shoulder at the river crossing, and though they'd stitched it with gut-thread and willow-bark by firelight, by morning the flesh around it had gone hot and tight, the skin flushed a wrong, glossy pink. They walked anyway. There was no other option. The child walked beside them, small hand occasionally finding Emrys's belt when the trail steepened, and neither of them spoke about the way Emrys's vision kept going gray at the edges, like a page left too long in the sun.

The cathedral ruins announced themselves before they appeared: a smell. Salt, vast and mineral, rising out of the mountain's bones like breath from a sleeping animal. Then the ruins themselves—white ribs of stone jutting from a green shoulder of the pass, so weathered they looked less built than grown, the way antlers grow or coral. Thornfell's first cathedral. Every map called it abandoned. Every map was written by people who'd never listened to the ground beneath it.

Emrys knew where the entrance was because they'd stolen the knowing—from a dying smuggler, years ago, one of the few harvests that still sat clean in their memory. A crack behind the fallen bell-tower, wide enough for a thin person and a determined child.

Inside, the dark smelled of centuries.

* * *

The caves beneath the cathedral were not natural. Emrys understood that within the first hundred paces, as the passage widened into vaulted chambers whose walls glittered faintly, crystalline, catching what little light their lantern gave and returning it bruised and gold. Salt, all of it salt—the whole under-mountain packed with it like marrow in a bone. Someone had hollowed these halls deliberately, worshipfully, and the work had the patience of people who expected to be at it for generations.

The child stopped at the third chamber and pointed.

There, carved deep into the salt wall, ran lines of script. Emrys couldn't read them—but they didn't need to. They'd heard those shapes. For six nights now the child had murmured them in her sleep, that dead first-tongue rolling off her like water off oiled cloth, and here they were, cut into the living rock, worn shallow in places by what could only have been touching. Thousands of hands. Generations of pilgrims reaching up to press their palms against the litany.

The child pressed hers there now.

And the wall *sang*.

Not sound, exactly. It moved through the salt and up through the floor and into Emrys's teeth—a low harmonic, patient and enormous, like the drone of a bell heard from inside the bell. The lantern flame stood straight up. The child's eyes had gone somewhere else entirely.

"Stop," Emrys said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it. "Hey. Stop."

She let her hand fall. The hum faded reluctantly, like a tide going out.

They made camp in a side chamber that still held the ghost of a hearth—soot ancient and fossilized in a ring of stones—and Emrys did what they could for the wound, which wasn't much. Boiled water. Cleaned it. Packed it with salt from the walls, which burned so badly they bit down on a strap of leather until they tasted blood, but which left the flesh feeling, afterward, marginally more like theirs.

Fever came with the dark.

It came the way fevers do—not all at once but in waves, each one pulling Emrys further from the chamber, from the lantern, from the small figure sitting cross-legged across the fire watching them with those old, old eyes. At the worst of it Emrys lost the thread of where they were and woke shouting once, certain that Corvin was in the room, certain they could smell his cologne, that particular blend of cedar and something colder underneath, and had to grip the salt floor to remember that he was three days behind them at most, not three feet away.

Through all of it, the child stayed.

When Emrys surfaced between waves, she was always there. Once she'd found water. Once she'd draped Emrys's own coat over them, awkwardly, upside down, and hadn't fixed it because fixing it would have meant moving them. Once, in the deepest hour of the fever, Emrys felt a small cool hand rest against their burning forehead—the same gesture, they realized through the delirium, that the child had made against the singing wall—and felt something pour through the contact that was not heat and not cold but *recognition*, the way a tuning fork answers its twin.

The fever broke near dawn on the third day.

Emrys woke wrung out, hollow, weak as wet paper, but clear. The child was asleep sitting up, chin on her chest, and the lantern was burning low, and the salt walls glimmered around them like the inside of some vast pale throat.

"You saved my life," Emrys said hoarsely. "I don't even know your name."

The child opened her eyes.

And spoke.

"Issa."

One word. Her voice was rusty, unpracticed, a door opening on hinges that hadn't turned in years—but it was a voice, an actual voice, and Emrys forgot how to breathe. She watched them react, and something almost like satisfaction crossed her face, and then she said more, slowly, feeling her way through the common tongue like someone wading into cold water.

"Issa. My name is Issa." A pause, gathering words like scattered coins. "I could not... speak it. In the lowlands. The words—" she touched her throat, frowning, hunting for the shape of the thought. "The words of here come easier. This is where they were made."

"The salt," Emrys said. "You can talk down here."

"Here I am *loud*." And she smiled, briefly, and it transformed her face into something heartbreakingly ordinary—a child's smile, gap-toothed, quick. Then it faded. "You are dying slow," she said. "I gave you what I could. But the litany needs a vessel that is not already spent."

"What does that mean? What vessel?"

Issa pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, and when she spoke again the words came easier, as if the cave itself were lending her fluency.

"There were seven of us. Vessels. Born with the litany already in us, the way some children are born with songs stuck in them. We were made after the binding—made *for* it—to keep the crown's chains oiled, generation over generation. Each vessel carries a piece of the severing-words. Alone, we are nothing. Together, in the place where the binding was done..." She looked up at the script-carved wall. "We finish it. We unmake what was made."

"Seven," Emrys said. "Where are the other six?"

The silence answered before she did.

"The crown ate them," Issa said simply. "Or the wardens did, on the crown's orders. I was hidden. My mother put me on a smuggler's cart when I was four and told me never to speak above a whisper, never in the lowlands, never where stone is quiet." She touched her throat again. "So I didn't. For six years. I am the last unbound vessel, and I have been carrying my piece of the litany alone since before I could tie my own boots."

Emrys stared at her. Six years of silence. Six years of a child swallowing the only language that came naturally to her, holding a king's ransom of destiny behind locked teeth, walking through a world that wanted her dead or caged, and saying nothing.

"Why tell me?" Emrys asked. "You've seen what I do. You know what I am."

"Yes," Issa said. "I know exactly what you are."

* * *

Later—after Emrys had eaten, after the salt had done what it could for the wound—they sat facing each other across the dead fire, and Emrys found, to their surprise, that they wanted to say it. Not needed to. Wanted to. Which was worse.

"When I take a memory," Emrys said, "the person doesn't miss it. That's the lie I tell myself. They wake and the garden's still there, the childhood's still there, everything's still there—just... thinner. Faded at the edges. Like a book read too many times." Their hands wouldn't stay still; they kept flexing, opening, closing. "But that's not true. I know it's not true. I've *seen* what's left behind. I took a woman's wedding day once, sold it to a collector in Grayharbor who wanted to know what being loved felt like. Three weeks later I saw her in the market, standing in front of a baker's stall, just standing there, crying, and she didn't know why. She'd lost the word for happy and didn't even know she'd lost it."

Issa said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing; six years of practice.

"I have a hoard," Emrys went on, because now that the dam had cracked they couldn't stop. "Years of it. Hundreds of lives. First snowfalls. Mothers' voices. The smell of a house you grew up in. Whole childhoods I bought off desperate people and stole from sleeping ones, filed away and catalogued, because someday I might need them, because someday they might buy my way out of something." A laugh came out of them, ugly. "I told myself I was a librarian. A custodian. Keeping precious things safe from being forgotten. But librarians don't sell the books, Emrys. Librarians don't get rich off other people's weddings."

"How many?" Issa asked quietly.

"I stopped counting at two hundred."

The salt walls glimmered. Somewhere far off in the cave system, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"My mentor taught me that memories are the only honest currency," Emrys said. "Corvin. He said a coin can be counterfeit but a lived moment can't—that when you hold someone's memory, you're holding the only version of it that ever existed, and that makes you responsible for it. He said that like it was a sacred trust." They shook their head. "He sells to the crown now. He's coming for you, Issa. Not to kill you. To *harvest* you. The thing in the crown wants the litany, wants it whole, and Corvin is very good at taking whole things from people."

"Then we should not be here when he comes."

"No," Emrys agreed. "We shouldn't." But they didn't move, and Issa didn't either, because both of them understood there was a question still hanging in the chamber air, and it belonged to Emrys.

"If I give up the hoard," Emrys said slowly, working it through aloud, tasting how insane it sounded, "all of it—every stolen life, every borrowed year—it might be enough to fuel the severing. The litany needs power. Living memory is power. That's the whole rotten engine of my trade, and it might be the whole salvation of the kingdom, and the joke is that I'd finally be spending it honestly." They looked at their hands. "Two hundred lives. Gone. Everything I am except the parts nobody paid for."

"Would it hurt?" Issa asked. Not cruelly. Curiously, the way children ask.

"Like cutting off a limb. Like cutting off two hundred limbs." Emrys met her eyes. "I don't know who I am without it. That's the truth. I've been so many people that I'm barely anyone. When it's gone, there'll just be... me. Whatever that turns out to be. Probably not much. Probably a thief with empty pockets."

Issa unfolded herself and crossed the space between them and took Emrys's face in her two small hands, the way you'd hold something broken to see if the pieces fit.

"In the first tongue," she said, "there is a word for what you are. It has two halves. One half means *one who takes*. The other half means *one who keeps*. Same word. Both true at once." Her thumbs were cool against Emrys's cheekbones. "I have known you nine days, and you have not yet decided which half you are. But you are closer than you think."

Outside, far above, through a hundred feet of salt and stone, Emrys heard—or imagined they heard—a sound that did not belong to caves or cathedrals.

Boots. Many of them, on the ridge above the ruins.

And threaded beneath the crunch of gravel, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind down through the crack behind the fallen tower: a man's voice, dry and amused, giving orders in a tone Emrys had obeyed for ten years.

Corvin had found them.
