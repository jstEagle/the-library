## 8. The Court of the Hollow King

The capital smelled of roasting meat and crushed flowers, and beneath both, faint as a stain, of salt.

Emrys had not come back to Thornfell City in nine years. They had told themselves they never would. But the jubilee changed the arithmetic: every gate stood open, every warden's eye turned toward the parade route, and the palace kitchens had hired two hundred extra hands for a feast meant to feed ten thousand. Nobody looked hard at a lean woman with a boy's cropped hair and a child who kept her eyes down and her mouth shut.

Issa walked beside Emrys through the press of the crowd, her small hand hooked in Emrys's belt like a burr. She hadn't spoken since the caves. She didn't need to. The litany lived in her now the way a fever lives in a body—Emrys could feel it radiating off her skin when they slept pressed together in ditches, a warmth that was not warmth.

*Three days,* Brannoc's dying note had said, smuggled out through a baker who owed him. *Jubilee ends. After that, the crown feeds publicly.*

They passed a wine-seller's stall where a fat merchant was telling anyone who'd listen that the king had appeared on his balcony at dawn and smiled at the crowds for a full hour without once blinking. His companions laughed. One of them said the old stories were true, that Aldric hadn't aged since the war, and another said don't be stupid, and the first one said, *then explain the teeth*, and nobody laughed after that.

Emrys bought an apple with a stolen coin and gave it to Issa.

"Stay close," she signed to the girl, and Issa nodded, and for a moment her eyes were old, old, old—the eyes of something that had watched kings be crowned and unmade—and then she blinked and she was seven again, biting into fruit.

* * *

The servant corridors were called memory-blind because that was what they were.

Every palace had them—warrens built in the bones of the building, behind the marble, where the architects of the old kings had carved wards into the stone so that no scrying eye, no dream-walker, no memory-thief could find what moved there. Emrys knew them because Corvin had known them, and Corvin had taught Emrys everything worth knowing about moving through other people's houses unseen.

*The servants are invisible by design,* he used to say, walking Emrys through a duke's manor with his hand light between her shoulder blades. *So become the design. Don't hide from the house. Belong to it.*

The entrance was a laundry chute behind the tannery row, exactly where it had been nine years ago, because palaces did not renovate their shame. Emrys lifted Issa through first, then followed, dropping into steam and lye-smell and the roar of a hundred washwomen who had no time to look up at anything.

For six hours they were ghosts in grey. Emrys carried linens. Issa carried a basket of candle-ends. They moved with the flow of the corridors, down and inward, past the ward-stones set in the walls—black river-rock, humming faintly, drinking the light. Emrys felt them the way you feel a toothache coming: a pressure against the hoard inside her skull, all those stolen lives packed tight behind her eyes. The stones wanted memories. The whole palace wanted memories. It was a building built to feed a thing that ate them.

"You feel it too," Emrys murmured, crouching beside Issa in a shadowed junction where three corridors met. "Don't you."

Issa pressed her palm flat against the floor. Then she drew her hand slowly up her own throat, to her mouth, and closed her fist.

*It's hungry here.*

"Yes," Emrys said. "So we move fast."

* * *

They came up into the gallery above the throne room through a musician's stair, and that was where Emrys saw the king.

Not from across a square. Not on a coin or a banner. Close. Twenty feet, maybe less, through the carved screen that hid the choir loft from the hall below.

The jubilee court was assembled—all of it, every lord and lady of Thornfell in cloth-of-gold, the ambassadors, the generals, the high priestess of the Salt Faith standing rigid with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. Trumpets. Ten thousand candles. And at the far end, ascending the steps to the throne with the slow, ceremonial gait of a man who had done this four thousand times:

King Aldric.

He was wrong.

That was Emrys's first thought, thief's instinct, the part of her that read people the way merchants read ledgers. He was wrong the way a puppet is wrong, the way a body animated by something else is wrong. He walked beautifully. He smiled precisely. And none of it was him.

Up close, the crown was worse than the stories.

It sat low on his brow, black iron worked into a circlet of thorn-shapes, and it *moved*. Not visibly—not in any way the court below could see—but Emrys had spent her life watching the borders of things, the seams where one mind ended and another began, and she could see the crown breathing. Pulsing. A slow rhythm, in and out, like a mouth working at something soft. With each pulse, a thread of pale light ran from the king's temple up under the iron, and with each pulse the king's face went a little more distant, a little more smoothed-over, a face being worn away like a cliff worn by tide.

And around him—this only Emrys could see, this made her grip the screen until her knuckles went white—there were others. Faint shapes standing in the air near the throne, translucent as breath on glass. Old men. Old women. A young soldier with half his head gone. A little boy in a prince's clothes, holding the hem of the king's cloak.

The devoured kings. Eight generations of them, still standing attendance on the thing that wore their lives.

Aldric reached the throne and sat, and the crown pulsed, deep and satisfied, and somewhere in the hall a woman gasped—whether at the majesty of it or at some flicker of the man underneath, some last expression crossing the ruined face like a fish surfacing before it drowned—Emrys couldn't tell.

"He's still in there," Emrys breathed. "Gods. He's still *in there.*"

Beside her, Issa had gone rigid. Her lips were moving. No sound came out, but Emrys could see the shape of the litany forming on them, word after word after word, and the ward-stones in the walls below began, very softly, to hum.

Emrys pulled her back from the screen. *Not yet. Not here. Tomorrow, when the hall is full and Voss is looking elsewhere—* but even as she thought it, she knew tomorrow might not exist. Three days, Brannoc had written. This was day two.

Down in the hall, the crown pulsed again, and the little ghost-boy holding the king's cloak looked up—directly up—at the choir screen.

Directly at Emrys.

Its mouth opened. No sound crossed the distance. But Emrys read the shape of it easily enough, because she had spent nine years reading mouths through windows, reading the words people thought no one saw.

*Help us.*

* * *

They almost made it out.

Almost.

The corridor behind the choir loft was empty when they entered it and not empty when they tried to leave it. Captain Sera Voss stood at the far end in her warden's grey, helmet under her arm, and she did not draw her sword. That was the first strange thing. The second was that she was alone.

"Thief," she said.

Emrys put herself between Voss and Issa and calculated, fast and cold: one door behind Voss, one grate behind Emrys too narrow for a grown woman, a knife at her hip, a hoard of forty-one harvested lives in her skull and perhaps three seconds to choose which one to burn.

"I wondered," Voss said, walking forward slowly, boots silent on the stone. "Since the river crossing. Since you burned that terrain-memory—I felt it go. Like a door slamming somewhere in my own head." She stopped six feet away. "You know what I am. You've been in my memories. So you know I'm not going to pretend I don't know what you are either."

"The mountain pass," Emrys said. "You were nineteen. Your father was selling you to a border lord and you were memorizing the passes so you could run." She didn't know why she said it. Cruelty, maybe. Or the thief's oldest trick: make them flinch.

Voss didn't flinch. "I know," she said quietly. "Because someone left that memory where I'd find it. In my own quarters, sealed in wax, no note. Two nights ago." Her jaw tightened. "I thought it was a threat. I thought you were telling me you'd been inside my head again. But that wasn't it, was it. You were showing me—" Her voice caught, just barely, a crack in the officer's cadence. "You were showing me who I was before the wardens filed me down."

"I show people receipts," Emrys said. "What they do with them is their business."

"And the child?" Voss's eyes went past Emrys's shoulder, to Issa, small and silent in the dark. "The orders say she's a vessel. Say she's dangerous. Say bring her to the crown unharmed." A pause. "But orders are sealed, thief. Sealed things can be opened. I opened mine." She held up a folded paper, and even in the dim corridor Emrys could see the crown's seal broken on it, and could see—thief's sight, the sight that read the residue of handling—that Voss had read it more than once, and that each reading had left a deeper mark of horror in her.

"They're not orders," Voss said. "They're a shopping list. Do you know what happens at the end of the jubilee? Do you know what the crown does with a vessel?" She laughed, short and ugly, at nothing. "I've served it eleven years. I told myself it was the kingdom. The kingdom needs a king; the king needs the crown; the crown is the price. Everyone pays something." Her hand drifted to her sword hilt—not drawing it, resting on it, the way a man rests his hand on a wound. "I watched him on the balcony this morning. He blinked twice in an hour. Twice. And the second time, thief, I swear on my mother's grave he was *looking out*. From inside. Asking."

The corridor hummed. Somewhere below, the trumpets swelled for the closing ceremony of the jubilee's second day.

"So here is my hesitation," Voss said, and now she did draw the sword, slow, and laid it flat across both palms like an offering or a surrender. "Eleven years of service, weighed against one sealed page and one memory of a girl memorizing mountain passes. I don't know yet which way I fall. Understand me: I may still take you both. If the crown calls, I answer—it's not a choice I get to refuse, not entirely, not with the oaths in my blood." She set the sword down on the stone between them and stepped back from it. "But right now, in this corridor, I am hesitating. And I have never hesitated in my life."

Emrys stared at the sword. At the woman. Forty-one lives crowded behind her eyes, and not one of them had ever taught her what to do with an enemy laying down arms.

"The salt-cathedral," Emrys said finally. "Tomorrow. When the hall is full."

"I know what tomorrow is." Voss's face was grey as her uniform. "I'm assigned to the throne steps." She turned, paused, and said, without turning back: "The grate behind you opens onto the east stair. The sentry changes at the bell. Go."

Emrys took Issa's hand and backed toward the grate, and at the mouth of it she stopped.

"Why?" she asked. "Why let us go?"

Voss stood very straight in the dark, and for a moment she was nineteen again, memorizing passes.

"Because I opened the sealed page," she said, "and now I can't close it. None of us can. Go, thief. Before I stop hesitating."

They went. Behind them, faint through the stone, the crown pulsed once—deep, long, hungry—and the whole palace seemed to lean toward the sound like a field leaning toward storm.

Tomorrow, the throne room. Tomorrow, the litany.

Tomorrow, Emrys would find out what she was willing to burn.
