## 9. Unbinding

The servant corridors behind the throne room were built for people no one was meant to see. Emrys had walked them for three hours, learning them the way they'd once learned a magistrate's sleeping face—by touch, by repetition, by the small betrayals of light. Now they stood in the dark of an alcove behind the great eastern pillar, and through the gap they could see the whole glittering horror of the jubilee court.

Aldric sat on the Salt Throne with his hands flat on his knees, like a man at his own funeral trying to look pleased about it. The crown sat on his head the way a leech sits on flesh. It had grown since Brannoc's drawings. Veins of black metal ran down from the circlet into the king's temples, into his jaw, threading him like a root through soil. His eyes were open. That was the worst part. There was still someone home in there, deep down, watching through the bars of his own skull.

*Issa.* Emrys signed it against her shoulder, the private shorthand they'd built on the road: *now, or never.*

She looked up at them. In the torchlight her face was calm in a way no child's should be, and Emrys hated that they had taught her nothing of that calm—that she had carried it out of Thornfell on her own, through whatever had burned the first-tongue back into the world.

Then she stepped out of the alcove.

The hall noticed her the way water notices a dropped stone. Conversation thinned. A lordling laughed too loudly somewhere near the wine tables. Issa walked down the center aisle between the pillars, small and straight-backed, and when she reached the foot of the dais she did not kneel.

She opened her mouth, and spoke.

Emrys had heard the litany a hundred times on the road—murmured at waystones, hummed in sleep, scratched into frost on window glass. But never like this. Never *full*. The first-tongue came out of her like water finding an old channel, and the words were not sounds so much as weights. Emrys felt each one land in their chest. Around the hall, nobles clutched their ears, though there was nothing loud in it. The candles guttered toward the throne, all of them, all at once, as if the room had begun inhaling.

And the crown answered.

It began as a vibration in the floor. Then the screams started.

Not Issa's. Not anyone living. They poured out of the crown like smoke forced through a keyhole—the devoured kings, every one of them, generations of men who had worn the circlet and been eaten by it, screaming all their deaths at once. Emrys had heard harvested memories scream before; they knew what a mind sounded like when it was pulled apart. This was worse. This was *old*. The sound had weight and history and grief so thick it curdled the air.

Nobles fled the hall in a rustling tide. Guards dropped their spears or didn't—some stood rooted, weeping without knowing why. On the throne, Aldric convulsed against his restraints of root and bone, and his mouth moved, and beneath the chorus of dead kings Emrys heard one thin living voice:

*"Do it,"* the king said. *"Please."*

*Issa.* Emrys signed. *Hold.*

She held. Her voice climbed the litany's spine, and the crown screamed louder, and the black veins in Aldric's skin began to pulse faster, feeding, fighting—

—and then the crown found her.

Emrys saw it happen. Saw the pressure turn, the way a thief turns on the mark who bites back. The crown reached for Issa the way Corvin had taught Emrys to reach for sleeping strangers: gently, expertly, finding the seam of her and beginning to peel. Issa's voice stuttered. Her hands flew up to her head. She went to her knees on the marble, and the litany broke off mid-word, and the silence where her voice had been was the loudest thing in the hall.

No.

Emrys had done the arithmetic a hundred times on the smuggler roads, and every sum had come out the same: the hoard was worth more than their life. Every stolen garden, every purchased childhood, every borrowed decade of some stranger's joy—they had told themselves it was capital. Leverage. A retirement plan measured in other people's yesterdays.

They had also, somewhere in the last few weeks, stopped believing it.

Emrys stepped out of the alcove and opened their archive.

It was not a place. It was not a thing. It was everything they were—the hoard they'd spent fifteen years building, sealed and sorted and catalogued like a miser's vault. The magistrate's garden with its crooked pear tree. A fisherman's daughter's wedding day. A dying woman's memory of her mother's hands kneading bread. Hundreds of lives. Thousands of moments. All of it theirs, none of it earned.

They let go of all of it at once.

The severing took them like a river takes a bridge. Emrys felt each memory leave—not fade, not drift, but *burn*, torn out and fed into the litany like fuel into a furnace. The magistrate's garden went first, and Emrys screamed, because they had loved that garden more than anything they'd ever owned honestly. Then the rest, in a flood, a cascade, a conflagration—every version of who they might have been pouring out of them and into the air, into the salt, into Issa's broken litany, which caught fire with it and rose.

Issa's voice came back.

She rose off her knees. She planted her feet on the marble like roots, and the first-tongue poured out of her doubled now, tripled, carrying the weight of every stolen life Emrys fed it—a fisherman's daughter's joy braided into a binding-word, a baker's morning woven through a curse-breaking syllable. The crown shrieked. The veins in Aldric's skin turned white, then gray, then crumbled like old mortar.

The last thing to burn was the oldest thing in the hoard. Emrys had saved it without meaning to—the first memory they ever stole, back when Corvin's hand had guided theirs, a stranger's memory of being forgiven. Of being *kept*. It went up like a struck match, and Emrys felt it go, and understood, finally, what Corvin had taken from them all those years ago, and what they were choosing, freely, to give away now.

The crown cracked.

Not shattered—cracked, once, clean, like ice letting go of a branch in spring. And from the crack came not a scream but a sigh, long and vast and relieved, the exhalation of nine dead kings released from a prison they had each, in turn, mistaken for a throne.

Then ash. The crown fell apart on Aldric's brow into gray powder, and the powder slid down his face like snow, and King Aldric—what was left of him, a thin old man with hollow cheeks and wet eyes—looked out at the ruined hall and whispered something in the first-tongue. Emrys didn't know the words. But Issa did. She translated later, though by then it hardly mattered:

*Thank you. I remember my name.*

Emrys sat down on the marble. Their hands were shaking. Their head was—empty. Not quiet. Empty, the way a house is empty after the furniture is sold, every room echoing with the shape of things that used to be there. Fifteen years of harvest, gone. They tried to reach for the magistrate's garden and found only a hole where it had been, smooth-walled and clean.

They couldn't remember why they'd loved it.

That was the price. That was the whole price, paid all at once, and Emrys found—to their genuine surprise—that they could breathe under it. The weight of borrowed years, gone. What remained was thin and poor and entirely, unmistakably their own.

Footsteps on the marble. Emrys looked up, too drained to run, and expected Voss.

It was Issa. She knelt in front of them, took their face in both small hands, and studied them with those old, old eyes. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead to theirs, and spoke—one word, in the first-tongue, soft as a waystone's hum.

Emrys didn't know it yet. But they would learn it by morning, and carry it the rest of their life: a word that meant *thief*, and meant *keeper*, and had never before needed both meanings at once.

Behind her, at the far doors of the hall, Captain Sera Voss stood with her wardens' swords still drawn—and did not move.
