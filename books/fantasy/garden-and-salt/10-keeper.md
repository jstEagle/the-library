## 10. Keeper

The crown came apart the way a fever breaks.

One moment it was screaming—the devoured kings pouring out of it like smoke from a house fire, filling the throne room with every voice Aldric's parasite had ever swallowed—and the next there was only silence, and ash drifting down through the shafts of morning light like gray snow.

Aldric lay on the steps below his own throne. He was smaller than he'd looked from the gallery. Smaller than any king should be. The thing that had worn him for forty years was gone, and what remained was just an old man with a hollowed face and eyes that were, for the first time in living memory, entirely his own.

He looked at Emrys. He looked at Issa. His mouth moved, and no sound came out, and then—because the crown had taken his voice long before it took everything else—he shaped the words with his lips alone.

*Thank you.*

Then he died, quietly, the way ordinary men die, and the court stood around him in their jeweled foolishness and did not know yet that the world had changed.

* * *

Voss found them at the servants' door.

Emrys heard her boots before they rounded the corner—that measured warden's stride, unhurried, which was worse than running. They put themselves between her and Issa without thinking about it, hands empty, hoard burned to nothing, nothing left to fight with except the truth.

"Captain," Emrys said. "I'd offer you a memory of my surrender, but I don't have one."

Something flickered across Voss's face. She had seen it too—in the flood when the crown broke, everyone in that hall had seen it: the parade of kings, the feeding, the slow devouring dressed up as succession. She had spent her life guarding a mouth.

"The magistrate's orders said bring you in alive," she said. "The magistrate is dead. The orders died with the crown." She looked past Emrys at Issa, who stared back at her with those old, old eyes. "There will be wardens who don't accept that. There will be lords who want a new crown made by morning. You should be far from here when they remember what you did."

"You're letting us walk."

"I'm telling you where the east gate's guard changes." Voss's jaw worked. "I served a lie for twenty years because I thought it was order. Don't make me watch you get hanged for fixing it." She turned, then paused. "The child spoke. In the hall. Before the end."

"She named me," Emrys said. "I didn't understand the word."

"You will." Voss walked away, and her stride, for the first time, sounded tired.

* * *

They went out through the east gate at dawn, two small figures among the carts and pilgrims flooding in for the jubilee that would now be a funeral. Emrys kept waiting for the shout behind them, the whistle, the net. It never came.

The strange part wasn't the fear. The strange part was the lightness.

Emrys had carried stolen lives for fifteen years—a magistrate's garden, a merchant's first love, a shepherd's whole childhood of hills, hundreds of them, thousands, a warehouse of other people's yesterdays stacked in the dark behind their eyes. Now the shelves were bare. When Emrys reached for a memory of their mother's face, they found only the fact of having once known it, like a book title with the pages torn out.

It should have been agony. Instead it felt like setting down a load so heavy they'd forgotten they were carrying it.

"I can't remember her name," Emrys said on the third day, on the smuggler road above the salt-caves. "My mother. I know she hummed when she cooked. I don't know her name."

Issa took their hand. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to; since the throne room she spoke rarely, as if words were also something you could spend, and she was being careful with hers.

But that night, in the shelter of the caves where the binding-litany had first been sung into salt, she sat across the small fire and looked at Emrys for a long time.

"*Thievra*," she said.

"What does it mean?"

She pressed her fist against her chest, then opened it, palm up—an taking, a giving. "It means thief," she said. "And keeper. One word. The old tongue doesn't split them." She tilted her head. "You kept me. You gave everything away. Both are true. So both are your name."

Emrys laughed, or something near it, and found their eyes wet. "*Thievra*," they repeated, clumsy on the old vowels. It fit better than any name they'd ever owned—which was none, really, because who were you when every version of yourself had been sold?

Someone who stayed. Apparently that was enough.

* * *

Spring came late to the mountain, but it came.

They built the cabin wider than the old one—two rooms, because Issa was growing like a weed and deserved walls of her own—and they planted the garden above the salt-caves, in soil that remembered grief but not spite. Potatoes and beans, practical things. And flowers, because Issa insisted, dragging seeds from a trader's cart in Thornfell with the fierce authority of someone who had ended a dynasty and therefore knew what mattered.

Emrys learned to farm without memories of farming. Every skill had to be grown fresh, from seed, the slow way. Their hands blistered. Their back ached. They forgot things daily—small things now, the price of bread in the capital, the layout of Brannoc's shop—and each loss hurt less than the last.

Some nights, travelers climbed the smuggler road and knocked. Wardens mostly, or scholars hunting fragments of the litany, or occasionally someone hollow-eyed who'd heard rumors of a thief who could take pain out of a head and wanted theirs gone. Emrys sent most away. For the ones whose suffering was a wound rather than a life, Emrys learned a new trade: not stealing, but carrying. Taking a memory only to give it back lighter, or holding it awhile until its owner grew strong enough to bear it again.

Keeper work. Thievra work. The word held both, and Emrys was learning to.

One evening in early summer, Issa came up from the caves with dirt on her knees and something cupped in her hands. A waystone shard, broken off in some ancient quake, its dead runes faintly warm.

"It's singing again," she said. "Down deep. Not the litany. Something else. Something older, under the salt." She looked up, and her eyes had that old weight in them, but also—new this season—something like excitement. "Thievra. I think the mountains have memories too."

Emrys wiped their hands on their trousers and looked out over the garden, the small green defiance of it, the road winding down toward a world still full of crowns and the people who missed them.

"Well," they said. "Then we'd better learn to listen."
