## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The fog came off the river at dusk the way debt came due: slowly, then all at once.

Bram Nakamura watched it swallow the Vellum Guild's countinghouse from the third-floor window of the Map Room, where the light was rationed by law and the ink was rationed by fear. Below him, Lamplighters' Row dissolved into grey wool. The gas lamps burned halos around themselves and nothing else. Somewhere in that blindness, a hundred clerks were walking home with their collars up, carrying in their heads the day's arithmetic—sums owed, sums owed for, sums owed because—and not one of them could have said what Bram Nakamura looked like if you put a knife to their ribs.

That was the point. That had always been the point.

He turned back to his desk. The desk itself was a lesson in misdirection: scarred oak, second-hand, deliberately ugly, positioned beneath the window where the Guild's floor-wardens could see him working whenever they liked. Diligence on display. A man who worked where he could be watched was a man with nothing to hide, or so the theory went. Bram had spent eleven years making the theory true in every direction but the one that mattered.

On the desk lay two documents.

The first was official: a commission slip stamped with the Guild's triple-seal, assigning one cartographer—unnamed, identified only by the workmark *VX-9*—to complete a survey of the Meridian Steps district for the Assessor's Office. Ordinary work. Honest work, if you didn't look too closely at what the Assessors did with honest maps.

The second document was a single sheet of good rag paper, unruled, unstamped, belonging to no ledger in the building. On it, in Bram's own hand, was the coastline of a country that existed only on Tuesdays.

He had been mapping Tuerday—the name was his own joke, and he guarded jokes the way other men guarded gold—for six years now. It began, as most of his best work began, with an error someone else made. A Guild surveyor named Ollensby had filed a report complaining that his instruments gave different readings in the Meridian Steps depending on the day of the week, and that on Tuesdays the readings gave nonsense entirely: streets that bent, distances that doubled, a stairwell on Coriander Lane that went down forty steps and arrived at a door he'd passed going up. Ollensby had blamed damp. The Assessors had blamed Ollensby. The report was shelved, and would have stayed shelved, except that Bram read everything that got shelved. Reading what got shelved was, in fact, most of his job description, though no line in any contract said so.

Six years of Tuesdays. He'd learned the country's grammar the way you learn a language: badly at first, then all at once. Tuerday was small—a few dozen streets folded into the seams of the Meridian Steps, visible only when the week turned over, populated by nobody he'd ever managed to meet, though there were signs of habitation. Doors worn smooth at handle-height. Window boxes with dead flowers replaced by living ones between one Tuesday and the next. Someone swept those streets. Someone drew water from that well. And whoever they were, they had never once noticed Bram watching them, because Bram had built his observation the way he built everything: to leave no mark behind.

He dipped his pen—not the Guild's iron-gall, but a private mixture of walnut and soot that dried brown and aged convincingly—and continued the coastline. The trick to mapping Tuerday was to map it backwards. You started from where the country ended and worked toward its heart, because the heart moved. Any surveyor who began at the center found the center had relocated by the time his compass settled. Begin at the edges, let the edges anchor you, and the middle would come to you eventually, like a shy animal to a patient hand.

It was beautiful work. He allowed himself, privately, in the privacy of his own skull, which was the only vault he trusted, to admit that it was the finest thing he had ever drawn. The Guild's own master charts—those vellum monuments to imperial certainty that hung in the Long Gallery downstairs, gold-leafed and glass-cased—were children's scrawls beside it. They mapped what was easy. Bram mapped what was true.

And no one knew. That was the part he savored the way other men savored brandy. Not the beauty alone, but the secrecy wrapped around the beauty like a hand around a candle flame. Somewhere in this building were ledgers that recorded every guildsman's name, apprenticeship, commission, wage, debt, and sin. Bram appeared in none of them. Officially, the countinghouse employed a cartographer designated VX-9, hired through a broker in Saltmarket who had since emigrated, whose records had since burned, whose existence rested on paperwork so old and so dull that no clerk had ever wanted to look at it twice. Bram had written that paperwork himself, eleven years ago, in three different hands, across four different decades. He had forged his own history backward until it was older than he was. There was a version of him in the archives who had joined the Guild before Bram Nakamura was born.

His real name lived nowhere. Not in the ledgers. Not in the rent-book of the room he kept in Cinders Ward under another dead man's name. Not in the memory of anyone who could be made to give it up—he paid his debts promptly and his friendships never, and he had taught himself, long ago, to be forgettable in person: medium height, medium build, a face that was a corridor rather than a room. People remembered corridors poorly.

Untraceable. The word sat in him like a warm stone. Other men had reputations, and reputations were leashes. Other men had names, and names were handles, and anything with a handle could be picked up and carried somewhere it didn't want to go. Bram had watched it happen to better cartographers than himself: Master Ellery-Vane, who'd mapped the drowned quarter and been drowned in turn by men who wanted his charts; young Peth, who'd signed a commission for the wrong ministry and spent two years explaining himself to people who never explained why they were asking. Every ambitious soul in this city was walking around with their name stitched to their collar like a laundry tag, waiting for someone to grab it.

Not Bram. Bram was the fog. Bram was the margin note no one read. He finished the eastern coastline of Tuerday, blew the ink dry, and felt the particular satisfaction of a lock clicking shut—one more piece of a country only he could enter, entered by a man only he could find.

* * *

The floor-warden came at half past eight, as floor-wardens did, on his rounds, with his lamp and his list.

"VX-9." The warden's voice had the flatness of a man reading his own boredom aloud. "Commission status."

"Meridian Steps survey," Bram said. "Two-thirds complete. Delivery by month's end." He turned the official sheet toward the lamplight so the warden could see the neat rows of measurements, the respectable blankness of a map being made correctly.

The warden glanced at it the way he glanced at everything, which was barely. "Assessor's office sent word. They want the boundaries marked for *disputed parcels*. All of them." A pause with weight in it. "You know what disputed means now."

Disputed meant the Assessors had decided someone's land was worth taking, and needed the map to say the land had always been ambiguous. Bram had drawn disputed boundaries before. He drew them well, which was a thing he tried not to think about at night.

"I know what it means," he said.

"Then mark them." The warden made his tick on his list—*VX-9, compliant*—and moved on down the row of desks, and Bram watched him go and thought, not for the first time, that the whole Guild was a machine for turning geography into theft and calling it bookkeeping. And thought, also not for the first time, that the machine had never once turned its gears on him, because the machine required a name to grip, and he had spent eleven years sanding his down to nothing.

He worked another hour after the wardens' rounds ended. The fog thickened against the window until the glass might have been frosted, might have been papered over; the countinghouse shrank to its lamps and its scratching pens. Down the row, old Mistress Quill—no one remembered her real name either, though hers was lost to age rather than art—snuffed her lamp and shuffled out. One by one the lights died until only Bram's remained, and then, because even invisibility needed sleep, his too.

He locked Tuerday in the false bottom of his desk drawer, beneath a layer of genuinely boring tax assessments, and pulled his coat on. The stairwells of the countinghouse at night were a study in echoes: his boots sounded like two men descending, then four, then a crowd, and he had long ago stopped flinching at the acoustics and long ago started using them, varying his step so that anyone listening would count more feet than were there. Small habits. Cheap insurance. A life assembled from small habits and cheap insurance, mortar-brick by mortar-brick, until the whole edifice was proof against weather, against inquiry, against want.

Outside, the fog took him the way it took everyone, which was to say completely, which was to say safely. He walked home through streets he could have drawn blind—did draw, in fact, in private folios he'd have burned before showing anyone—and let the city's anonymity close over his head like water over a swimmer. In Cinders Ward he climbed to his rented room, ate bread and hard cheese standing up, and lay down on his narrow bed with the deep, untroubled satisfaction of a man whose books all balanced.

He was asleep within minutes. He dreamed, mildly and pleasantly, of coastlines.

* * *

The knocking came at an hour when knocking is a threat.

Bram woke with his hand already inside the loose floorboard where he kept a knife, a purse, and nothing else worth stealing, listening. Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. Pause. One knock. Not a pattern any friend used; not quite a pattern any enemy used either, which was somehow worse. His room in Cinders Ward had one door and one window, and the window overlooked a drop into a neighbor's yard that he'd measured precisely, once, for exactly this kind of hour.

The knocking came again. Weaker.

He crossed the room without lighting a lamp—light was a signature; he'd taught himself to move in the dark the way other men taught themselves to whistle—and put his eye to the peephole he'd drilled through the door's warped panel years ago. The hallway beyond was the usual murk of tallow smoke and bad plaster. And slumped against his door, half in and half out of the shadows, was a boy in a courier's coat.

Guild livery. Or something very like it—Bram's eye went to the buttons first, because buttons were how lies were told in this city, and these buttons were right, or right enough, or wrong in a way that made his skin prickle because he couldn't say how. The boy was young, sixteen perhaps, and the color of tallow himself, and there was something spreading under him on the floorboards that the dark made black and that Bram's nose, arriving a half-second later, made worse than black.

Ink. The smell of ink. But wrong—too rich, too sweet, with an iron undertone like a mouthful of coins.

"VX-9." The boy's voice came out cracked, used up. He wasn't looking at the peephole; he couldn't have known where it was. He was looking at the door itself, at the space just above the handle, as if he could see through it. "I know you're there. I carried it three days. Please. Before they—" A cough, wet and terrible. "Before *it* finds the map again."

Every sensible instinct Bram owned stood up in a body and voted to stay silent, wait the boy out, open the door at dawn onto an empty hallway and a plausible absence. Sensible instinct had kept him alive for thirty-one years. Sensible instinct had built every wall of his invisible life.

But the boy said, in a whisper fraying apart at the edges: "The king is not the king. Do you hear me? The king is *not the king*, and I'm dying on the doorstep of the only man alive who'll believe me, because Master Corvess said—you were his student, he said you'd know the hand, he said *no one else maps like you*—"

The world tilted, quietly, the way Tuerday tilted.

Corvess. His master. Dead nine years, unmourned by the Guild, unmapped by anyone—which for Corvess would have been the only fitting monument. And a dying stranger on his threshold saying *your craft, your hand, your secret*, saying it in a voice running out of time like sand through split fingers.

Bram unbolted the door.

Later—he would have a great deal of *later*, though he didn't know it yet—he would try to reconstruct why. Curiosity, probably. The cartographer's original sin, as it would come to be called by people with better hindsight: the inability to leave a blank space blank. But in the moment there was no reasoning at all, only the boy's weight falling forward into his arms, heavier than a boy should be, and the folded paper pressed against Bram's chest with the last deliberate strength of a dying man's hand.

"The map," the boy breathed. His lips were stained black to the root of the tongue. "It's drawn in blood. Mine doesn't matter. *His* does. Keep it moving, keep it—never let it lie still, it hates—" His eyes found Bram's face in the dark, and focused, and widened slightly, as if seeing something there he hadn't expected. "Nakamura," he said. Wonderingly. "You're *Nakamura*. Corvess said you'd hidden the name so deep even the ledgers forgot it. He said—" The cough took him then, fully, and what came up was black as the inkwell of hell, and Bram held him while the boy's grip on his collar tightened once, twice—

—and the last thing the courier said, so softly that only a man trained to live in margins could have caught it, was:

"He's seen the map. Now he's seen *you*."

Then the hand went slack, and the fog pressed in against the open door, and somewhere far off across the sleeping city, in a palace Bram had never had reason to enter, something vast turned over in a king's borrowed sleep and opened its eyes.
