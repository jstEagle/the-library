## 4. The Guild Sends Regards

The knock came at the hour when fog thickened into something almost solid, when the countinghouse lamps burned low and honest men had gone home to lie to their wives about where they'd been.

Bram knew before he opened the door that it was trouble. Not because of the knock itself—three raps, measured, courteous—but because of what the knock was *not*. His visitors did not shuffle. They did not clear their throats or mutter apologies for the lateness of the hour. The three raps landed with the precision of a signature on a contract, and beneath them he could hear the particular quality of silence that enforcers carried with them like luggage: a silence that expected to be filled.

He looked down at the blood-map, spread across his drafting table, its margins crawling with that sigil that moved only when unwatched. He had spent two days trying not to look at it and failing, the way one fails not to look at a wound.

The map went under the floorboard first. The ledgers—the ledgers were the problem.

* * *

Every cartographer who worked the Guild's gray margins kept ledgers. It was the great hypocrisy of the trade: a profession devoted to recording the world, practiced by men and women desperate to leave no record of themselves. Bram's ledgers were his sins made orderly. Eleven volumes, bound in calfskin and cheap buckram, each one a small country of debts and commissions, of names he'd mapped for and names he'd mapped *against*, of payments routed through three false factors so that no thread ran from Bram Nakamura back to Bram Nakamura.

He kept them in the safehouse above the chandler's shop on Wren Lane, behind a loose brick that smelled of tallow and old smoke. He had told himself for years that the ledgers were insurance—that if the Guild ever came for him, the books would prove he'd been useful, that burning him would cost more than tolerating him.

Standing in his rooms with the knock still echoing, Bram understood for the first time that the ledgers were not insurance. They were evidence. Every careful entry was a confession written in advance, waiting only for a reader.

The knock came again. Three raps. Patient as tide.

He wrapped the blood-map in oilcloth and shoved it into the cold hearth's flue, wedged between soot-blackened brick, then pulled on a coat as though he were a man interrupted at nothing in particular. In the glass over his washstand, his own face looked back at him with an expression he recognized and hated: the face of a man rehearsing innocence.

*You are a mapmaker,* he told his reflection. *You have lied to archmages and customs men and one very persistent duchess. You can lie to three enforcers.*

His reflection did not believe him either.

* * *

There were three of them, and they filled his doorway the way water fills a hull—not all at once, but with the certainty of eventual drowning.

The one in front was tall and gray as the fog itself, with the Vellum Guild's seal worked into his collar in thread so fine it was nearly invisible. That was the Guild's way. Their power lived in things you almost didn't see. Behind him stood a woman with ink-stained fingers and the flat, patient eyes of someone who read documents for a living and found most of them disappointing. The third stayed half in shadow, and Bram's professional eye noted, with a lurch, that the third one cast a slightly wrong shadow—too long for the lamplight, angled against the light's logic.

A shadow-reader, then. Or something worse. The Guild did not send its ordinary hounds for ordinary errands.

"Master Nakamura." The tall one smiled, and it was a good smile, well-made, the kind of smile that had been fitted to the face like a door fitted to a frame. "Forgive the hour. May we come in?"

"You may not," Bram said, "but you will."

The smile deepened by a degree. "We've been looking for you all evening. Your neighbors said you were home." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Your neighbors know your name, Master Nakamura. That must be inconvenient for you."

*It is,* Bram thought. *It has been for eleven years.* Aloud, he stepped aside and let them in, because refusing twice would have been a second kind of confession.

They entered the way auditors enter: touching nothing, missing nothing. The woman's eyes went over his drafting table, his ink pots arranged by viscosity, the pins in his wall maps marking realms that existed on Tuesdays and Thursdays and one memorable stretch of last autumn. The gray man—Bram decided privately to think of him as the Gray Man, since names were currency and he refused to spend his own—drifted toward the window and stood with his back half-turned, which Bram knew was a technique. Men talked more freely to a shoulder than to a face.

"We're investigating a theft," the woman said. She produced a document but did not open it, holding it the way a priest holds a book he doesn't need to read. "From the royal archive. A ledger. Older than the current reign. Perhaps older than the Guild's charter, though we'd thank you not to repeat that speculation."

"I'm a cartographer," Bram said. "I don't deal in ledgers."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You deal in maps. And ledgers are merely maps of money, aren't they? Of debts and territories and who owes whom. We thought—who better to ask than a man who understands how things are recorded?" Her flat eyes lifted to his. "And how things might be *un*recorded."

The room felt smaller. Bram became aware of the flue above the cold hearth, of the oilcloth bundle wedged there like a swallowed secret, of the fact that the fire had been out for two days and any inspector worth his salt would wonder why a working mapmaker's chimney held anything at all.

"A courier died three nights ago," the Gray Man said to the window, conversationally. "In the Sumpward district. Black fever, the watch ruled. Though the watch rules black fever whenever a body bleeds from places bodies shouldn't bleed." He turned his head, and in the lamplight his face was smooth and forgettable, a face designed to be described badly. "The courier had made deliveries to this building. Twice in the past month. Our records show it."

"My records show rent," Bram said. "I receive parcels. Paper stock. Pigments. I'm a tradesman; tradesmen receive parcels."

"Of course." The Gray Man nodded slowly. "And the contents?"

"Ink. Vellum offcuts. Nothing that would interest the Guild."

"Nothing," the woman repeated softly, "that would interest the Guild." She finally opened her document, and Bram saw with a cold slide in his stomach that it was not a warrant but a ledger page—a single page, copied in a fair hand, listing transactions. "Then you won't mind confirming that none of these accounts touch you. Factor Halloway, in Saltmarket. The counting-house of Ferren and Doss. A certain arrangement through the Tuesday market—" her finger stopped on the page, and she looked up with something almost like kindness, which was infinitely worse than malice, "—which exists, as far as we can determine, only on Tuesdays."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, in the chandler's shop, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"That's clever work," Bram said, and heard his own voice come out wrong—too quick, pitched half a tone high, the voice of a man whose invisibility had just been peeled back at one corner. "I mean the accounting. Very thorough. But I don't know those factors. I buy my supplies locally, cash, like anyone."

"Cash," the woman said. "Yes. Cash leaves no trail. How admirable." She closed the ledger page. "Master Nakamura, we are not here to accuse you. The Guild values its freelancers. You are, by reputation, the finest realm-surveyor of your generation—and by *absence* of reputation, we note, the most discreet. Discretion is a commodity. The Guild pays well for commodities."

"What do you want?"

"The courier carried a ledger out of the archive. We want it back. Whoever holds it is in possession of information that destabilizes thrones." She said *thrones*, plural, without inflection, and Bram's skin crawled because he understood suddenly that she meant it literally—that whatever was in that ledger had unseated kings before, or seated them, and the Guild had been the hand on the scale. "If it came to you, even incidentally, even by accident—returning it would be remembered favorably. Generously compensated. The Guild remembers its friends."

"And its others?"

The Gray Man turned fully from the window then, and for just a moment his forgettable face forgot to be forgettable, and what looked out of it was old and patient and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with appetite. "The Guild's others," he said, "become entries."

* * *

They left the way they'd come, unhurried, certain, and Bram stood in his own doorway listening to their footsteps fade with the particular rhythm of men who would return.

Then he moved.

He had planned fires before—in the abstract, the way a sensible man plans for floods. Now the plan unfolded with terrible clarity. The ledgers first. All eleven. The loose brick gave up its hoard and he stacked the volumes on the hearthstone, hands steady in the way hands go steady when panic passes some threshold and becomes purpose. The blood-map he left in the flue; it could not burn—it had already survived worse than fire, if the courier's death meant anything—but it could stay hidden among the soot where no searcher would think to distinguish it from ash.

He struck the tinder. The calfskin caught reluctantly, then greedily. Buckram curled. Ink, that faithful servant of his whole hidden life, bubbled and smoked, and the smell of it filled the room—his debts, his commissions, eleven years of careful erasure going up in a stink of burnt bone and iron gall.

*This is survival,* he told himself, watching the flames. *This is what survival costs.*

But the thought rang hollow, and he knew why. Because the woman had read his Tuesday-market account off a copied page. Because the Gray Man had known about the courier's deliveries. Because they had come not with suspicion but with an *offer*, which meant they already believed they knew everything, and the offer was simply courtesy extended to a man they considered already caught. His ledgers had never been insurance. They had been a rope he'd braided himself, strand by strand, year by year, and tonight the Guild had shown him the loop at the end of it.

He fed the last volume into the fire and watched the cover warp. *Nakamura, B.,* the inside flyleaf said, in his own younger handwriting, before he'd learned better. *Commissions, Year One.*

He had kept that page out of vanity. Some buried part of him had always wanted proof he existed.

The fire ate it.

* * *

Near dawn, with the ashes cold and the blood-map re-hidden in the lining of his traveling coat, Bram sat at his drafting table and tried to do the only thing he knew how to do: make a map. Not of territory—of exits. Routes out of the city. The river gates and their bribed wardens. The mountain paths that existed in fog. He drew them with mechanical skill and no conviction whatsoever, because every line now felt like an entry in someone else's ledger, every route a corridor built by hands he couldn't see.

That was when he noticed the sigil.

It had migrated. Two days he had studied the blood-map's margin, and the moving sigil had always kept to its corner, circling its small orbit like a moth around a lamp. Now it sat in the lower margin, directly beside the notation for the palace grounds, and it had changed shape. Where before it had been a closed figure—a knot, a lock—it now resembled a keyhole. An open one.

As he watched, it pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat felt through a wall.

Bram sat very still. The ashes of his ledgers grayed the hearth behind him. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows, and somewhere in the city the Guild was writing his name into columns he would never be permitted to read, and on a throne across town something vast and patient wore a king like a coat.

*Hiding is surviving,* he had believed for eleven years. He tested the belief now, the way one tests a rotten floorboard, and felt it give.

A soft sound at the window made him turn.

Perched on the sill outside, in defiance of four stories and a locked stairwell, crouched a woman in a travel-stained coat, and she was holding up against the glass a piece of paper on which, in large furious letters, was written:

*I KNOW YOU HAVE MY BROTHER'S MAP.*

Below it, smaller: *Open the window or I open the door.*

Bram looked at the paper. He looked at the sigil pulsing gently in the map's margin, patient as a held breath. He looked at the woman's face—grief-hardened, sleepless, absolutely certain—and understood that the day of being unread was over, and that everything after this moment would happen in full view.

He opened the window.
