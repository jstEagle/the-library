## 5. A Witch Named Sorrel

Sorrel Vance had been waiting in the stairwell of Bram Nakamura's safehouse for six hours, and in that time she had learned three things about the man who was about to die.

First: he was careful. He had come home twice through different doors, once wearing a fishmonger's apron and carrying a basket of herring he clearly had no intention of eating, and both times he had stood in the fog outside for a full quarter hour before entering, reading the street the way other men read letters.

Second: he was afraid. Not of her—she had made sure her presence left no trace a hedge-cartographer could feel—but of something larger. She had watched him burn papers in his own hearth at three in the morning, feeding them page by page like a man administering last rites, his face lit orange and utterly without comfort.

Third: he had her brother's map. She could feel it through two floors of rotting timber and lath, the way you can feel a struck bell through a wall—a low, wrong hum, ink that was still alive, still remembering being blood. It sat somewhere above her head like a coal that refused to cool.

Sorrel adjusted the sigils stitched into the lining of her coat—concealment, silence, the small ugly one for pain that she hoped she would not need—and settled deeper into the shadow beneath the staircase, and waited the way her order had taught her to wait before they expelled her: patiently, precisely, and with a specific destination in mind for all her anger.

* * *

The door opened at dawn, because of course it did. Men who lived by hiding came and went with the light, when shadows were thickest and eyes were slowest.

Bram Nakamura climbed the stairs with the herring basket again—he actually ate them, then; that was almost funny—and Sorrel let him get four steps past her hiding place before she spoke.

"The map," she said. "Give it back."

To his credit, he did not scream. He went very still instead, the stillness of a man doing rapid arithmetic with his life, and then he said, in a voice carefully sanded smooth of anything useful, "I think you have the wrong house."

"I don't." She stepped out of the shadow and let him see her—all of her, the cropped hair, the burned-off sigil scars at her wrists where the Order of the Unwritten Line had removed her knighthood in ink rather than flesh, the knife she held low and easy. "And neither does anyone else, which is why I've been standing under your stairs since midnight listening to you burn your ledgers. You're burning evidence, cartographer. That means someone's coming for it. I'd like to be gone before they arrive."

His eyes moved—the knife, her wrists, the window, the window again. She watched him price every exit in the room and find them all too expensive.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Sorrel Vance." She let the name land and watched it mean nothing to him, which stung more than she expected. Six years ago that name had opened doors in three cities. "Wren Vance was my brother."

Something shifted behind his eyes. Grief has a particular weather, and she had learned to read it in faces the way she read sigil-light: the small contraction, the recalibration. He knew the name. He knew the courier.

"He died on my doorstep," Bram said quietly.

"Yes. I know. I found the blood." Her voice came out flatter than she intended, and she hated that, so she pushed it sharper. "He bled black for half a street and then someone scrubbed the cobbles, badly, because the Guild's cleanup crews are cheap. I followed the residue. It took me two days. He was dead for one of them."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She took a step closer and saw him weigh the knife against the window again. "The map he carried. The folded thing, drawn in living ink. You have it. I can feel it humming through your floorboards like a dying animal, so don't insult us both by lying."

For a long moment Bram Nakamura simply looked at her, and Sorrel realized she was being measured—not as a threat, though he was certainly doing that too, but as a problem. A variable. She recognized the look because it was her own: the look of a person whose survival depended on knowing exactly what they were holding.

"You're a sigil-witch," he said at last. "But your marks are burned off. Expelled?"

"Struck from the rolls. There's a difference. Exiles can petition for return." She showed her teeth. "I can't. They cut the guild-sigils out of my skin and salted the lines so they'd never take ink again. So now I do my work the old way, or the new way, depending on which gets me arrested faster."

"What did you do?"

"That's not a question you've earned." She flicked the knife up and caught it. "Here's what you've earned: context. Wren didn't steal that map to sell it. He stole it from the royal archive—the sealed archive, the one under the Hall of Records that officially doesn't exist—to save the king. Do you understand? Our king. Aldric the Third, the Hollow King, the man who smiled at his coronation like a portrait of himself. Wren found out what's actually sitting on that throne, and he stole the only document that proves it, and he ran for nine days with half the Guild's enforcers and worse things behind him, and he died getting it to the one person alive who could read it."

Bram had gone very pale. Not the theatrical pallor of a guilty man—something realer. Something that looked like recognition.

"Why me?" he asked. His voice had lost its sanded smoothness. "Why would he bring it to me? I'm nobody. My name isn't in any ledger in this city."

"No," Sorrel agreed. "It isn't. I checked. I spent four days checking, because I wanted to know who my brother died reaching for, and I couldn't find you anywhere. No guild registration. No tax roll. No birth record. Either you don't exist, or—" and here she leaned forward, and let him see that she had done her homework, "—or you're very, very good at making sure no one ever wrote you down. Which is its own kind of signature, isn't it? Only one kind of person erases themselves that thoroughly. The kind with something worth hiding. The kind whose craft is hiding."

The silence stretched between them, taut as sigil-thread.

"Wren talked about you," she said, softer now, and the softness cost her more than the knife-work had. "In his letters. Never by name—he wasn't stupid—but there was a teacher, he said. Years ago. Someone who taught him that a map is a sentence the world says about itself, and that most mapmakers only transcribe, but a few—" her throat closed, and she made herself finish, "—a few *compose*. He said if anything ever went truly wrong, truly kingdom-wrong, there was one person who could read what needed reading. He wouldn't say how he knew. He just knew. Little brothers are like that. Certain of their heroes."

"I was never his teacher."

"No. But you were his standard. The bar he measured craft against." She sheathed the knife—not because she trusted him, but because her arm was tired and the point was made. "And now he's dead in a pauper's drawer at the Chapel of Rest because the bar got him killed, and you're standing in your little hidden house burning your little hidden books, and the map that could save a king is humming upstairs like it wants to be wept over. So. Give it to me, or tell me why I shouldn't take it."

* * *

He didn't give it to her. Of course he didn't. Instead he did something she hadn't predicted, which after six hours of studying him felt almost personal: he sat down. Right there on the bottom step, in the gray dawn light, and put his face in his hands, and for a moment the careful anonymous shape of him collapsed into something younger and much more tired.

"They'll come for it," he said, through his fingers. "Whoever killed him. Whoever sent him. They'll trace the map the way you traced the blood. And everyone it touches dies—you know that, you said it yourself, he ran nine days—"

"He ran nine days *carrying it wrong*." Sorrel crouched down to his level, and up close she could see the exhaustion on him, days deep, the sleeplessness of a man who had been dreaming in someone else's geography. She'd seen that look before, on apprentices who'd handled blood-ink without protection. Her stomach dropped. "You've been reading it."

"I've been *looking* at it. There's a difference."

"There really isn't." She reached out and took his wrist before he could pull away, turned his hand palm-up. The lines of his palm had gone faintly dark, ink-shadowed, like veins under thin ice. Blood-maps bound their readers; every first-year witch learned that before they learned to write their own name in light. "Oh, you idiot. You beautiful, doomed idiot. How long?"

"A few days. I dream in it." He said it flatly, the confession of a man reporting symptoms to a physician he doesn't expect to cure him. "Roads. Archives underwater. There's a garden in the middle of it that's been dug up and replanted so many times nothing grows right. And at the edges—" his jaw tightened— "at the edges things are missing. Eaten. The map is smaller than it was when I unfolded it the first time."

Sorrel let go of his wrist and sat back on her heels. Well. That changed the arithmetic entirely.

A blood-map of a mind, degrading while it was read. A reader already dreaming its roads. A brother dead in a gutter with black ink in his veins. And underneath all of it, the thing she had confirmed three nights ago from the gallery of the Grand Basilica, watching the king bless a crowd of ten thousand with a smile that moved exactly right and meant absolutely nothing.

"It's eating him," she said. "The parasite. While it wears him, it eats what's left of him inside, and the map is showing you the meal."

"You know what it is." Not a question. Bram was watching her with those careful cartographer's eyes, and she understood suddenly why Wren had trusted this stranger with the most dangerous document in the kingdom: because this was a man who, having been handed an impossible thing, immediately began mapping it.

"I know pieces. I know what the archive seals said, because Wren copied them into his last letter and I've read that letter until the ink went soft." She pulled the letter from her coat—water-stained, blood-spotted at the corner, carried against her heart for eleven days—and held it between them without letting go. "I know that whatever sits on the throne came in through a door, and doors are built, and builders sign their work. And I know that the Order of the Unwritten Line expelled me for asking exactly one question too many about royal warding contracts, which means either I was right and they buried it, or I was wrong and they panicked. Both options interest me."

"And what do you want from me?"

"The same thing Wren wanted." She heard her own voice crack on it, grief finally breaking through the fury like water through a bad seal, and she let it, because some things should be seen. "Finish it. Read the map. Find the door. Get our king back—or prove to me there's nothing left to get back, and help me kill the thing wearing him. Those are the only two endings I'll accept. Everything else is just waiting to be eaten too."

Bram looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at his own darkened palm, turning it over slowly, a man reading a country he'd never consented to enter.

"If I give you the map," he said, "you'll die. If you leave without it, I'll die. If we keep it between us, we might both die, slightly later." He laughed, short and joyless. "Those are terrible terms."

"Those are the only terms on offer."

"Then I want your lore. Sigil-lore. Whatever the Order taught you before they untaught you—I want it, all of it, because reading that map is one thing and *acting* on it is another, and I am a maker of maps, not a breaker of crowns." He stood, and offered her his hand—palm down, she noticed, hiding the inked lines, some habits already forming. "Partnership. Unequal, uncomfortable, and honest about both. Do we have terms?"

Sorrel looked at the offered hand. Somewhere above them the blood-map hummed its low wrong note, patient as hunger. Somewhere across the city, enforcers were tracing black residue along cobblestones, and in a palace on the hill, something vast and patient was smiling with a borrowed mouth.

She thought of Wren, certain of his heroes. She thought of her own burned wrists, and the years she had spent being no one, and how being no one had saved her life every single day and never once been *living*.

She took his hand.

"Terms," she said. "And cartographer—if you dream in that map again, you wake me. Whatever walks those roads, you don't walk them alone. That's not sentiment. That's tactics."

"Of course," said Bram Nakamura, who was lying, and she let him lie, because she was lying too, and two liars holding the same secret was, she suspected, the closest thing to trust either of them would ever manage.

They went upstairs together, toward the humming.

Neither of them saw the gray feather on the doorstep, placed there sometime in the night—nor the faint sigil scorched into the wood beneath it, in a hand the Order of the Unwritten Line had burned off Sorrel Vance's own wrists six years ago.

Someone had found her first.
