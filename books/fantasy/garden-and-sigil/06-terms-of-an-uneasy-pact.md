## 6. Terms of an Uneasy Pact

**Bram**

She had made tea in his kitchen without asking, which was either a declaration of war or a truce, and Bram Nakamura could not yet tell which.

The kettle was his—the dented copper one he'd bought three years ago from a pawnbroker who never asked names—and she handled it as though she'd always known where he kept it. That was the trouble with sigil-witches, he decided. They read rooms the way he read maps: instantly, greedily, and without permission.

"Sit down," Sorrel Vance said. She didn't look at him. She was arranging three cups on his scarred table with the deliberateness of someone laying out surgical instruments. "You've been up for two days. I can smell it on you. Ink and no sleep. You've been reading it."

"I've been *studying* it," Bram said. "There's a difference."

"Not to the map." She poured water over the leaves, and the smell of blackthorn and something sharper—juniper, maybe, or burnt rosemary—filled the narrow room. "Blood doesn't like to be studied. It likes to be *read*. There's a difference too."

He sat, because standing suddenly seemed like a great deal of effort, and because refusing would have been its own kind of confession. The blood-map lay between them on the table, folded into its careful eighths, and even folded he could feel it. That was new. Two days ago it had been paper. Now it felt like a sleeping animal.

"You dreamed about it," Sorrel said. Not a question.

Bram wrapped his hands around the cup she pushed toward him. The ceramic was hot enough to hurt, and he held on anyway. "Roads," he said finally. "A road that remembers being walked. And archives under water, but the water doesn't drown anything. The books just... wait down there." He looked up. "How did you know?"

"Because my brother described the same dreams, the week before he died." Her voice didn't waver. That was the frightening thing about her—everything else about her trembled slightly, hands, breath, the flicker of her eyes toward the window every time a cart rattled past below, but her voice was iron laid over all of it. "Tobin said the map started dreaming *him* before he ever finished drawing it. He thought that was normal. He thought that was what it meant to be a good cartographer." A short, bitter laugh. "Our mother taught us both to draw. Only one of us learned when to stop."

Bram drank the tea. It tasted like medicine and honesty, two things he had spent thirty years avoiding. "You want me to stop reading it."

"I want you to understand what you've already done." She turned the cup in her own hands, not drinking. "Blood-maps bind the reader. That's not a flaw; it's the mechanism. You can't chart a living mind from the outside—you have to be *tied* into it, the way a surveyor ties his chain to a fixed point. Tobin tied himself. Look what it bought him." Her jaw tightened. "And now you're tied, and you're dreaming its roads, and in another week you'll start losing hours. Walking somewhere and not remembering the walk. Writing notes in your sleep. And after that—" She stopped.

"After that?"

"After that, whatever is wearing the king starts using your legs."

* * *

**Sorrel**

She watched him absorb it, and she hated how well he absorbed it.

Most men, told they were being slowly hollowed out by a parasite's geography, would have gone pale or loud. This one just went still, the way deep water goes still, and behind his eyes she could see the ledger-brain working: *cost, risk, exit, cost again.* He was pricing his own dissolution. She had seen merchants do it with horses.

"You're wondering if you can cut yourself loose," she said.

"I'm wondering whether cutting loose kills the map." He set down the cup. "If the binding runs both ways—if I'm anchored to it and it's anchored to me—then severing might damage the original. And the original is the only record of what's inside the king's head. Possibly the only record of *the king*."

"The king," Sorrel said flatly, "is a corpse with good posture."

"No." He said it quietly, but with the stubbornness of a man who had checked his measurements twice. "I've been inside the margins of this thing for two days. The parasite has its own signature—it maps the way mold grows, boundary-eating, everything smoothed into itself. But underneath there are older lines. Roads that were built by someone who loved building them. Gardens. Someone planted gardens in there, Mistress Vance. Parasites don't plant gardens."

Sorrel looked away, because her eyes had gone hot and she refused to let him see it. Tobin had said nearly the same thing, the last time they'd argued, standing in her doorway in the rain with the stolen map already hidden against his ribs. *It's still him in there, Sorrel. Someone has to go in after him.* She had called him a fool. She had been right, and it was the most useless thing she had ever been.

"Tobin thought so too," she said. "About the gardens."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Sorry is cheap and I've had a lot of it lately." She pulled her satchel onto the table, next to the map, and began unpacking it with the crisp violence of someone who needed her hands busy: chalk sticks wrapped in waxed linen, a bundle of iron styluses, a jar of silver powder that whispered faintly when it moved, a strip of dried skin—not hers, she didn't explain whose—that bore a sigil inked in fading violet. "So here are my terms, cartographer. Listen carefully, because I'll only say this once."

* * *

**Bram**

Her terms were these:

One. He would read the map—all of it, fully, deliberately, completing the binding rather than letting it happen to him in fragments. "A half-tied reader is worse than none," she said. "Like a half-anchored boat. You'll drag."

Two. She would supply the sigil-lore. Everything he found in the king's mind, she would translate into workings—doors, wards, anchors, if it came to it, blades. "You can't fight what's in there with lines on vellum. Lines need teeth. I have teeth."

Three. They would do it within nine days. "The binding deepens daily," she said. "By the tenth day you won't be reading the map anymore. You'll be *obeying* it. And I will not stand in this kitchen and watch a second person I—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. "Watch a second person be eaten."

"And in exchange?" Bram asked. "What do you get out of this arrangement? Besides revenge, which costs you nothing to collect through me."

Sorrel went very quiet. Outside, the fog pressed against the window like something listening.

"My brother stole that map from the royal archive to save a man he'd never met," she said at last. "He was expelled from his order for less than that—for asking questions about why the archive keeps blood-works locked beneath the treasury instead of the library. He died in a doorway bleeding ink, and the last thing he did with his life was put that map in *your* hands, of all the hands in this city." She leaned forward. "I get to finish it. That's what I get. Don't dress it up as anything nobler and I won't either."

"Agreed," Bram said.

"That fast?"

"You expected haggling."

"I expected a coward. Everyone says you're a coward. The Guild says you don't exist. Half the countinghouse thinks you're three different people." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "But cowards haggle. Cowards ask for guarantees. You just said *agreed* like a man signing his own death warrant because the alternative was waiting for it."

"I don't sign things," Bram said. "That's rather the point of me."

"No," Sorrel said softly. "I don't think it is anymore."

* * *

**Sorrel**

They worked through the afternoon, and it was the strangest collaboration of her life, and she had once bound a storm-sigil into a lighthouse during a hurricane.

He unrolled the map only as far as each section required, and she watched his hands—quick, precise, reverent in a way he probably thought he was hiding—and she understood, watching them, why Tobin had chosen this man. Not because Bram Nakamura was brave. Because he was *careful*, in the way that only people who have spent their whole lives one mistake from ruin can be careful. Tobin had been brilliant and generous and reckless as weather. This one counted every step twice.

"Here." She chalked a warding circle around the map's edge, then a second inside it, mirror-reversed. "The inner ring is yours. If the map tries to pull more than reading out of you, the ring takes the strain. It'll hurt. Pain means it's working."

"And if it doesn't hurt?"

"Then run."

He almost smiled. It transformed his face so completely that she had to look back down at her chalk. *Oh,* she thought, with irritation. *Oh, no.* He was one of those. One of the ones who kept themselves locked up so thoroughly that the first crack of light came out blinding.

"Show me the sigil in the margin," she said, to give her hands something to do. "The moving one."

He turned the map's edge toward her, and there it was: a small coiled figure, drawn in blood darker than the rest, sitting in the lower margin like a printer's mark. When she looked directly at it, it was a knot of interlaced lines. In her peripheral vision, it was breathing.

"It moves when unobserved," Bram said. "I've tried catching it mid-motion with mirrors. It knows the difference."

"It's not moving *despite* being observed. It moves *because* of it." Sorrel crouched until her eye was level with the page. "Observation feeds it. Attention is a door, and doors are how that thing travels. My order's oldest texts say the Hollow Court's founders used attention-doors to walk between minds. They called it the Courtesy." She spat the word. "We were taught it was a fable. Then Tobin started pulling archive records, and the fables had ledgers attached."

"So the thing in the king's head is old."

"Older than the Guild admits. Older than the charter, maybe. Maybe the charter exists *because* of it." She straightened, dusted chalk from her fingers, and caught him watching her with that still, weighing look. "What?"

"Nothing. You talk like someone who's been reading forbidden books since childhood."

"I *was* someone who's been reading forbidden books since childhood. It's why I'm disgraced." She shrugged. "My order teaches that sigils are prayers. Safe little invocations, approved forms, nothing wild. But a sigil isn't a prayer. It's a *sentence*—a command spoken in a language reality is obliged to obey. And nobody asks what happens to the speaker of commands." She touched the faded violet mark on the strip of dried skin, briefly, like touching a scar. "Somebody should ask."

* * *

**Bram**

Night came down over the fog-bound city, and neither of them lit lamps, because light drew eyes and eyes were now, apparently, doors.

They worked by witchlight instead—a small green flame hovering above Sorrel's palm that cast no shadows and, she claimed, registered to scrying as ordinary damp. Bram mapped aloud while she transcribed: the drowned archives, the road that remembered feet, the garden terraces climbing a hill that appeared on no horizon. As he spoke, the words came easier than speech should come, flowing up out of him like water finding a channel already cut, and part of him noted that with distant professional alarm, and part of him simply *sang along.*

"—and the western stair descends eleven steps but returns you having climbed fourteen," he heard himself say. "The discrepancy accumulates. By the ninth descent you arrive somewhere else entirely—"

"Bram." Sorrel's hand closed over his wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "Stop. Breathe. You slipped."

"Slipped?"

"Your voice changed. It went—" She hesitated. "Smooth. Like something reciting rather than speaking. That's the third time tonight."

He looked down at his own hands, resting on the table beside the open map, and realized they had been tracing the western stair's contour in spilled tea without his noticing. The line was accurate. He hadn't seen that section in six hours.

"Well," he said, and heard his voice come out dry and steady and entirely false. "The binding is progressing on schedule."

"Nine days," Sorrel said. "Maybe eight now."

"Then we shouldn't waste any." He began folding the map, carefully, along its original creases, and found his fingers knew the creases better than his eyes did. "There's a question I haven't asked. Why me? Tobin bled for this map, ran the Guild's whole gauntlet to reach my door—and he was a trained archivist with a sister who actually knows the lore. Why hand it to a stranger who hides his name?"

Sorrel was quiet long enough that the witchlight guttered.

"Because of what he found in the archive records," she said finally. "Every blood-map in the royal collection has a maker's mark. Every one except the first. The first blood-map—the one that taught the parasite how to wear a king—is unsigned. But the *technique* in it, the surveying method, the way it ties reader to terrain—" She met his eyes across the small green flame. "It's your technique, Bram. Your secret methods. The ones only you use. Tobin was certain. He said whoever drew the first map invented what you do—or learned it from whoever did. Either way, he said, there's exactly one living person whose craft fits that map like a key fits a lock."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way his dream-stairs tilted, returning him having climbed more than he descended.

"He died saying my name," Bram said quietly. "At the door. I assumed he was delirious. People name strangers when they're dying. Priests, mothers, anyone."

"He wasn't delirious." Sorrel reached across the table and, with a gentleness that startled them both, closed his fingers around the folded map. "He was delivering it. To the only locksmith in the city."

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to toll—not the hour, not any hour Bram knew. Nine strokes, then silence, then nine again. Sorrel's face went white in the witchlight.

"That's the Court bell," she said. "It hasn't sounded in sixty years. It only rings for one thing."

"What thing?"

"A summons." She was already snuffing the flame, already moving, gathering chalk and iron. "The king is calling for a cartographer."
