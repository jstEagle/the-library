## 9. Hollow Court

The summons arrived the way Bram had always feared such things would arrive: correctly.

Not a mob of enforcers kicking down his door, not a knife in a fog-bound alley—those were the instruments of people who had already decided. This was worse. A cream-colored card, sealed with the royal sigil in green wax, delivered by a footman who did not meet his eyes and did not wait for an answer. *His Majesty requests the presence of Master Nakamura, cartographer, to discuss a commission of some delicacy.*

*Master Nakamura.* The name sat on the card like a bird of prey on a fence post, patient and certain of its perch.

Bram read it eleven times by candlelight before he let himself understand what it meant. He had spent nineteen years making sure no ledger held that name. He had paid rent through intermediaries, sold maps through cutouts, signed nothing but false initials in a hand he practiced until it stopped resembling his own. He had been a rumor with excellent penmanship. And now the crown itself had plucked him out of the fog and set him down on cream cardstock, spelled correctly, honorific included.

There was only one creature in the capital with both the reach to find him and the reason to want him found. It had looked at him across a procession square three days ago, through the eyes of a king, and it had *noticed*.

"You could run," Sorrel said. She stood at his window with her back to the room, watching the street below with the flat attention of someone counting exits. Her sleeves were pushed up; the sigil carved into her left forearm had scabbed over into a thin dark line, like a seam in leather. "Tonight, before dawn. I know six ways out of this city that don't touch a gate."

"And then what? It sends for me again. Or it stops sending." He turned the card over. Blank. Even the paper felt expensive in a way that seemed deliberate—an insult dressed as courtesy. "A thing that eats boundaries doesn't need to chase anyone, Sorrel. It just redraws the walls."

"Then don't go."

"It already knows where I sleep." He said it lightly, and hated how much effort the lightness cost. "Besides. It's inviting me in. That's not what you do with prey. That's what you do with a tool you haven't finished assessing." He almost smiled. "I've been assessed my whole life. I'm very good at being underestimated."

Sorrel turned from the window. In the candlelight her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion, grief worn down to something functional. "My brother thought the same thing," she said. "Right up until the ink came out of him."

The words landed exactly where she meant them to. Bram folded the summons along its original crease and put it in his coat, over his heart, where the blood-map's folded weight had lived for two weeks now and where he sometimes imagined he could feel it ticking.

"I'll wear my good coat," he said. "If I'm going to be eaten, I'll be eaten well-dressed."

She didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to. But as he reached the door she caught his sleeve—not the sigil arm—and held it a moment longer than accident required.

"The palace walls whisper," she said quietly. "Old stones remember. If it gets bad in there—if the rooms start lying to you—look for the seams. Every lie has one. My order taught us that before they expelled me for teaching it to other people." Her grip tightened. "Come back out, Nakamura. I've buried one person who carried your craft. I decline to make it a habit."

He went out into the fog, and the city closed behind him like water.

* * *

The palace did not look wrong. That was the first thing Bram understood, standing in the forecourt while a chamberlain with a voice like dry vellum recited his name to guards who did not blink: the palace looked *magnificent*. Pale stone, colonnades, fountains running silver in the grey morning light. Everything was symmetrical. Everything was correct.

That was how he knew.

Nineteen years of mapping realms that existed only on Tuesdays taught a man to stop trusting his eyes and start trusting his feet. Bram walked where he was led, past tapestries and through vaulted galleries, and he counted. Steps between doorways. Turns. The rhythm of the architecture, which every honest building keeps the way a heart keeps time.

By the third gallery, the rhythm broke.

It was nothing a courtier would ever notice. A corridor that should have taken forty paces took forty-three. A right turn was followed, four rooms later, by another right turn, which geometry forbade unless the building had quietly grown a fold. A window on the east side showed a garden that, when he passed its twin on the west, was the same garden—the same broken sundial, the same blackthorn—but seen from the same angle, not the mirrored angle. As if the palace had been copied rather than built. As if somewhere beneath the stone there was an original, and this was a tracing done from memory by something that understood buildings the way a magpie understands jewels: brilliantly, and without any comprehension of why they were made.

And everywhere, under everything, the sense of edges being *chewed*. Not destroyed—consumed, digested, incorporated. A wall here bore a hairline crack that wasn't structural damage; it was a scar, the way skin scars around something swallowed. A doorway had been bricked up so recently the mortar was pale, and the bricks were laid in a pattern that hurt to look at directly, because the pattern was almost a sigil and the almost was the point. Something had gotten in once. Something had been patched after.

The chamberlain paused before a set of double doors banded in bronze. "His Majesty will receive you in the Long Gallery," he said. "You will speak when spoken to. You will not approach the throne unbidden." His eyes flicked up, briefly, and Bram saw in them something he recognized from his own mirror on bad nights: a man performing calm over a cellar full of dread. "Master Nakamura. May I say—it is an honor. The Guild speaks highly of you."

"Do they," Bram said. "How strange. I've never given them anything to speak of."

Something moved behind the chamberlain's expression—a flicker, quickly smoothed. *Everyone in this building knows more than they're saying,* Bram thought. *Or less than they think they are.*

The doors opened onto the Long Gallery, and Bram stopped trusting his feet entirely.

It ran the length of a city block, hung with portraits of dead monarchs, lit by windows tall enough to admit weather. At the far end, on a dais that had no business being in a gallery, sat the throne, and on the throne sat the king.

Between them, the portraits were wrong.

Bram had studied the royal line for a commission years ago—he'd needed the genealogy to date a border dispute—and he knew these faces. Here they were subtly, horribly altered. King Aldous the Third had been lean and long-chinned; the portrait showed him soft, blurred, his features running toward a generic handsomeness like wax left near a fire. Queen Maren's famous crooked smile had been straightened. Portrait after portrait, the same drift: individuality sanded away, each face pulled toward the same center, the same mild, pleasant, forgettable template. Twenty kings rendered as one king, rendered as no one.

*It doesn't just eat boundaries,* Bram thought, cold spreading through his chest. *It eats distinction. It's been sanding this dynasty down for generations, and the paint is still wet.*

Then he reached the dais, bowed as low as protocol demanded without kneeling, and looked up into the living face of the Hollow King.

The body was magnificent. Tall, broad-shouldered, dressed in grey and silver, crowned with iron worked into the shape of intertwined branches. The face was handsome in the way the portraits were handsome—generic, smoothed, a face designed by committee. The eyes were open and looking at him.

And behind those eyes, vast and patient and *amused*, something looked back.

Bram had felt it once before, across a procession square: the sensation of being read like a document. Now, at ten paces, it was a physical pressure, a thumb pressing down on the pages of him. The blood-map stirred against his chest—not the paper, the *map*, the thing that had soaked into his dreams for two weeks—and for one vertiginous moment Bram saw the throne room twice: once with his eyes, and once as the map showed it, as a hollow space shaped exactly like the inside of a skull, with a small bright thing curled at its center like a seed.

He kept his face still. Nineteen years of practice. He bowed again.

"Your Majesty honors me."

"Honors you?" The voice was warm, resonant, perfectly pitched. Too perfectly. It had the evenness of water poured from a jug. "No, Master Nakamura. I *need* you. There is a difference, and men like you always know the difference." A hand gestured, gracious, unhurried. "I am told you map places that do not exist. Places that exist only under conditions. Tuesdays, I believe, is the current fashion."

The word *Tuesdays* in that mouth made Bram's skin crawl. "Rumor exaggerates, Majesty."

"Rumor is the only honest historian." The king leaned forward, and the movement was fluid in a way bodies aren't, like a current shifting under ice. "I require a map of my own kingdom. All of it. Including the parts that have been... mislaid." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You will find my palace instructive. It has changed hands, in a manner of speaking. New management values accuracy."

Every instinct Bram owned screamed at once. He heard himself answer in the flat, modest tones of a tradesman: "Accuracy is my trade, Majesty. Though I warn you, I charge by difficulty."

The thing behind the eyes smiled, and the smile used the king's mouth beautifully. "Difficulty," it said, "is the only currency I respect."

* * *

They gave him a workroom.

Of course they did. A fine one, third floor, north light, drafting table of seasoned oak, inks laid out in a row like soldiers. A guard outside the door who was either protection or a lock, depending on the hour. Bram was shown to it by a steward who spoke in the chamberlain's same smoothed-over cadence, and left alone with instructions to begin surveying the palace at his leisure.

At his leisure. In a cage with a view.

Bram spent the first hour doing what he did best: pretending to work while actually working. He paced the corridors with a measuring line and a notebook, sketching, muttering measurements aloud for the benefit of whatever listened through the walls. And underneath the performance, he mapped the truth.

The truth was worse than he'd guessed. The palace wasn't merely misbuilt—it was *remapped*. Whole wings existed in the wrong relationship to one another. Staircases descended to floors above their starting point. The chapel, when he finally located it, sat precisely where the map in his coat said the king's memory of his mother should be. He checked that correspondence three times, hands shaking, before he accepted it: the blood-map and the floor plan overlapped. The palace had been rebuilt as a mirror of the mind it contained. Every corridor outside corresponded to a corridor inside. The parasite hadn't just moved into the king's skull. It had renovated the country around the skull to match.

*It's building a shell around the shell,* Bram wrote in cipher in his notebook. *Why does a thing that owns the inside need to own the outside too?*

Because, answered the part of him that had spent a lifetime studying how hiding works, *it's afraid of something getting in.*

On the second day, the whispers started.

Sorrel had told him: old stones remember. He'd assumed she meant it as lore. She'd meant it as instruction. The stones of the oldest parts of the palace—the foundation levels, the service passages where the original masonry still showed—whispered when no one official was listening. Not words, exactly. Echoes. Coronation oaths repeated in voices fifty years dead. A woman singing a lullaby in a dialect extinct for a century. And once, very clearly, in a young man's voice, ragged with terror:

*"It wasn't supposed to take everything. I only asked it to keep the body alive—"*

Bram stood in that passage for a long time, measuring line dangling forgotten from his hand, feeling pity arrive in him like floodwater. Because he knew that voice's logic intimately. Had built his whole life on its foundations. *Hide, and survive. Give up the visible self, keep the essential one.* The king had made Bram's bargain at the scale of a nation, and the price had come due with interest.

On the third evening, the trap he'd half-hoped for and fully dreaded sprang—or rather, unfolded, gently, like a hand opening.

He was summoned to the Long Gallery again, alone, at dusk. The guards withdrew. The portraits watched with their sanded faces. And the king came down off the dais himself—came *down*, which Bram gathered from the whispering stones was nearly unprecedented—and walked beside him through the gallery as the light died, discussing survey methodology in a voice like poured water.

"The eastern wing first," the thing said. "Begin with the eastern wing. It is least... settled."

"As Your Majesty wishes."

They passed a window. Dusk. Their two reflections floated together in the glass, and Bram, watching the reflections instead of the man—that was an old trick, watch the reflection, the reflection can't perform—saw it.

For one frame of eternity, the reflection lagged. The king's reflected head turned a fraction behind his real head, and in that fraction the reflected face was not smooth. It was *cramped*. Features crowded, fighting for room on the skull, a face trying to be twenty faces at once and failing, eyes stretched wide around something enormous packed behind them. Then the glass caught up, and the handsome mask resumed, and they walked on.

But the mask had slipped, and something behind it had *seen him see*.

The king stopped walking. The gallery went very quiet—the particular quiet of a held breath. When the voice came again, it was still warm, still pitched like water, but Bram heard now what lived under the warmth: a vast boredom, the patience of a thing that has worn a hundred years like a coat.

"You are wondering," it said, "why I invited a cartographer into my house."

"Majesty—"

"Because I collect maps of myself." The king resumed walking, and Bram fell into step because his legs decided independently that falling out of step would be fatal. "Every ruler does, in the end. Mine are simply more literal." They reached the end of the gallery. The guards reappeared at the far doors, distant as figures painted on porcelain. "Your work begins tomorrow. Survey thoroughly, Master Nakamura. I want every room accounted for." A pause, and the amusement surfaced again, thin as oil on deep water. "Especially the rooms I have forgotten."

Bram bowed. Turned. Walked toward the doors with his pulse hammering in his throat and his mind screaming at him to look at nothing, change nothing, *be no one*—

—and the king's hand closed on his forearm.

The grip was gentle. That was the horror of it. Gentle as a father's. And leaning close, so close the crown's cold iron nearly touched Bram's temple, the king's mouth moved, and what came out was not the poured-water voice.

It was cracked. Rusty. A voice that had not been used in years, a voice speaking around something huge that filled its chest, forcing each word out through gaps in the possession like a prisoner passing notes through bars. Four words, breathed against Bram's ear, so quiet that only a man trained to hear what rooms tried to hide could catch them:

"*Find me. I'm here.*"

Then the grip released, the mask flowed back over the face like water closing over a drowned lamp, and the Hollow King stepped back onto his dais and said, in the warm resonant tone, "Good evening, Master Nakamura. Mind the stairs on your way down. They have been unreliable lately."

Bram walked out of the Long Gallery with his legs obeying him through sheer professional discipline. Past the guards. Down the unreliable stairs, which he mapped as he descended, automatically, helplessly, noting that they now descended to the second floor instead of the ground. Through the corridors whose lengths lied. Out into the fog, where the city received him without comment.

He made it three streets before he had to stop and put his hand against a wall and breathe.

*Find me. I'm here.*

The king was alive in there. Buried, starved, pressed flat under decades of occupation—but alive, and lucid enough to spend his single hoarded coin of stolen motion on a stranger. On *him*. Because Bram was the only living soul who could read the map that showed the prison, and somewhere in the dark behind that handsome borrowed face, the true king had worked that out too.

Pity and horror rose in Bram together, indistinguishable, the way grief and relief rise together at a sickbed. He thought of Sorrel's brother bleeding ink into the gutter. He thought of the drowned archives at the edge of the blood-map's margins, the roads remembering being walked. He thought of nineteen years of his own careful erasure, and of a man who had erased himself so completely that he now had to smuggle messages out of his own mouth.

He had come to the palace believing he might find a monster wearing a king.

He was leaving knowing he'd found both—and that the monster was the jailer, and the king had asked for it, and the king wanted *out*.

Bram straightened, buttoned his good coat over the blood-map, and turned toward the lodging where Sorrel waited with her knives and her sigils and her brother's ghost. Tomorrow he would survey the eastern wing, as instructed. He would measure every lying corridor and chart every chewed boundary, and he would do it smiling, the perfect hired tool, because the tool was the only disguise left to him.

And somewhere between the second floor and the ground, he had already begun to understand the shape of the problem no one had spoken aloud yet:

To free the king, someone would have to go in after him.

The palace had shown him, in its wrong staircases and mirrored wings, exactly how the door worked.
