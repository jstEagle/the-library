## 10. The Price of the First Door

The sigil had to be carved into living skin.

Sorrel had known this since the third night of preparation, when she'd finally stopped lying to herself about the alternatives. She had spent those three nights the way she spent most nights now: in the rented room above the chandler's shop on Vetch Street, surrounded by her brother's stolen notes and Bram Nakamura's infuriatingly precise marginalia, looking for a loophole. There was always a loophole. That was what sigil-craft was, at its heart—not power but accounting, every working balanced against something, every door paid for in some coin. Find the cheaper coin. Find the lesser toll.

There was no lesser toll.

*The first door opens from the body that bears it,* Wren had written, in his cramped left-slanting hand, on the last page of the notebook he'd carried out of the royal archive and died carrying to Bram's door. *Blood-map for blood-key. The reader goes in through the reader.*

She had read that line perhaps two hundred times. She had hoped, each time, that it would mean something different. It never did.

Now she sat cross-legged on the floorboards with her sleeves pushed past her elbows, an awl of guild-steel—iron would not hold the lines; iron refused them—resting against the inside of her left forearm, and she was waiting for her hands to stop shaking.

Bram stood by the window with his back half-turned, which she understood was his way of giving her privacy without leaving. He had offered, in his flat cartographer's voice, to do the carving himself if her hand proved unsteady. She had told him exactly where he could put his offer. He had accepted this with a small nod, as though she'd given him useful surveying data, and gone back to studying the map.

That was the thing about Bram Nakamura that Sorrel could not decide whether to hate or admire: nothing seemed to cost him anything. He moved through the world like a man walking a route he'd memorized years ago, avoiding all the places where feeling might pool. She had watched him burn his own ledgers—years of work, his whole hidden architecture of safety—and his face had shown nothing but concentration, like a man watching a fire to make sure it caught evenly.

But then, she reminded herself, she had watched him dream. Because that was happening too, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it aloud. He slept four hours a night now instead of six, and he woke speaking in a cadence slightly too formal, describing roads he had never walked in a city that existed nowhere except inside a king's skull. The blood-map was binding him already, thread by thread. Every night he went further in without meaning to. Which meant every night, the door they were building became less a choice and more a necessity—if they waited too long, he would fall in entirely, and there would be no one left outside to pull him back.

So: tonight. Her arm. Her memory. The toll.

"Tell me again," she said, because saying it again made it real, and real things could be survived. "Tell me the sequence."

"Anchor-sigil on the bearer." Bram did not turn from the window. "That's you. The carved lines take your blood, your blood carries the pattern, the pattern meets the map's ink and opens a passage between the mapped country and the mapping body. One door. One passage. It holds as long as the sigil holds."

"And the toll."

"The toll is taken by the door, not by me." Now he turned. In the candlelight his face was all planes and shadow, a face drawn economically, no wasted line. "I don't choose what it takes. Neither do you. The sigil weighs what you're carrying and takes what will balance the opening."

"A memory," Sorrel said. "It takes a memory."

"It took a memory from everyone who ever opened a first door. Wren's notes are clear on that. Three documented passages in Guild history, three bearers, three gaps." He hesitated, and she saw—the smallest flicker—that he hated the next sentence. "The notes don't say what kind. The archive records only say the bearers reported losses afterward. A name they couldn't recall. A year gone dark. One woman forgot how to swim."

"One woman forgot how to swim," Sorrel repeated. "And the Guild filed that under *acceptable expenditure.*"

"The Guild files everything under acceptable expenditure. That's rather their defining feature."

She almost laughed. That was the other thing about Bram: buried somewhere under all that careful blankness was a dry, sharp humor that surfaced only when things were worst, like plants that bloomed after fire. She had caught herself liking him twice this week and had scolded herself both times. Liking people was how you ended up carving your own arm open in a chandler's spare room. Liking people was how you ended up kneeling in an alley holding your brother while the black ink drained out of him faster than blood should.

She flexed her fingers. The shaking had mostly stopped. It would come back during; she knew that; she would work through it.

"Sorrel." Bram crossed the room and crouched down to her level, which he almost never did—he was a man who kept altitude, kept angles, kept exits in view. "You should know I've been through Wren's notes eleven times looking for another way. There isn't one. But also—" he paused, choosing words the way he chose contour lines, sparingly and exactly, "—you don't have to be the bearer. We could find someone. Hire someone. Someone who doesn't—"

"Someone who doesn't love him," Sorrel finished.

"I was going to say someone expendable."

"No, you weren't. You're a bad liar. It's honestly remarkable, given your profession." She held out her left arm, palm up, forearm bared to the elbow. "It has to be someone who loves him. The anchor-sigil keys to intent. Wren wrote that too. The door opens toward what the bearer wants. If we hired some cutpurse who wanted payment, the door would open toward payment. Toward nothing. Maybe toward the parasite itself, if the parasite promised more." She looked at him steadily. "It has to be me. You know it has to be me. Say it has to be me so I can stop arguing with you in my head and start arguing with my arm."

"It has to be you," Bram said quietly. Then, after a moment: "For what it's worth, I argued with you in my head too. I lost there as well."

* * *

She worked by candlelight, because moonlight made the guild-steel glint and glinting made her flinch, and flinching made crooked lines, and crooked lines in an anchor-sigil meant a door that opened sideways, into whatever lived between the walls of the world. She had seen what came out of sideways doors, once, in her final year before the order expelled her. She did not think about that. She thought about straight lines.

The design covered her inner forearm from wrist to the bend of the elbow: seven interlocking gates, each one smaller than the last, spiraling inward toward a central character that Wren had transcribed from the oldest stratum of the royal archive—a glyph so old it predated the order's orthography, a character that meant, roughly, *let the true thing through.* Around it, in her own hand, she had added the modifications the order would have forbidden: a ward against the parasite following the passage outward, a limiter to keep the door from widening once opened, and a small, vicious counter-sigil of her own devising that would seal the whole arrangement permanently if anyone tried to carve it out of her skin. The order had called her work reckless. The order had called her a great many things in the tribunal hall, most of them accurate.

She set the awl's point at the outermost gate and breathed out.

"You can look away," she said to Bram.

"I'm going to watch," he said. "If something goes wrong with the lines, I need to see it immediately. And—" the pause again, that economical man spending words carefully, "—you shouldn't be alone for this part."

So he watched, and she carved.

The first gate was the easiest, because the pain was still abstract. The awl bit and dragged, and the line welled red behind the steel, and she spoke the syllable that fixed it—*vess*, the sound of a held breath—and the line sealed itself, raised and glistening, a scar in the shape of intention. The second gate was harder. By the fourth, her vision had narrowed to a tunnel with the sigil at the bottom of it, and sweat ran off her chin onto the floorboards, and Bram's voice came steady from somewhere above her: "Line's true. Gate five is clean. Breathe, Vance. Breathe."

"Don't call me Vance like we're colleagues."

"What should I call you?"

"Nothing. Be quiet. Gate six."

Gate six was the long horizontal that crossed the old break in her forearm, the one from the winter she was nineteen, and the awl found the scar tissue and skidded, and she swore with a fluency that would have gotten her expelled from the order a second time, and steadied, and drew the line again, true.

Then gate seven. Then the center. Then the old glyph, *let the true thing through*, and her hand was cramping so badly she had to brace the awl in both fists, and Bram knelt beside her without being asked and held her wrist steady, his grip cool and exact, a compass needle of a grip, and she carved the last character in one unbroken stroke.

The sigil woke.

She felt it the way you feel a bell struck in the next room—not in the ear but in the chest, in the teeth. The carved lines flushed dark, drank the blood welling along them, and settled into a color that was not quite red and not quite black, the color of the map's ink, the color of what had leaked out of her brother. The seven gates seemed to recede into her arm even as they rose from it, depth and surface trading places, and the room grew very quiet, the way rooms do when something large has begun paying attention.

"Oh," Sorrel said. "Oh, it's awake. It's early. Wren's notes said the toll comes at the opening—"

"The notes said the toll comes when the door decides," Bram said. He had not let go of her wrist. His hand had tightened. "Sorrel. Look at me. Whatever it takes, it takes it now, and you stay here, and you come back. Do you understand? You come back."

The door reached into her.

There was no sensation she could properly describe, because it was not a sensation—it was an absence moving through her, a finger drawn along a shelf, considering. She felt it pause over things. Her mother's kitchen, the smell of scorched barley porridge: paused over, passed by. The tribunal hall, the order's white robes, the word *expelled*: paused over, passed by. She had a mad impulse to argue with it, to shout *not that one, take anything but—* and understood, distantly, that everyone who ever paid this toll must have shouted the same useless words at the same indifferent weight.

It settled on her brother's face.

Not her brother. Not Wren—all of Wren, his stubbornness, his terrible handwriting, his habit of stealing honey cakes and leaving the crumbs as evidence of innocence. Those remained. What it lifted, precisely, cleanly, surgically, was his face. The specific assembly of features that had been Wren Vance's face, the thing she had known before she knew his name, the face she had last seen gray and slack in an alley with black ink drying at the corner of his mouth.

It took it the way a tide takes a footprint.

And then the door was open, and the sigil on her arm burned cold, and Sorrel Vance sat on the floorboards of a chandler's spare room knowing—with absolute, archival certainty—that she had a brother, that his name was Wren, that he had stolen a map from the royal archive to save a king and died delivering it—and unable, no matter how she reached, to see his face.

She knew his hands. She knew his laugh. She knew that his left eyebrow had a scar through it from the time he fell out of the tanner's loft at age nine. She knew all of these the way you know entries in a ledger, correct and complete and lifeless. The face itself was a smooth pale oval where a person used to be.

"Sorrel." Bram was still kneeling, still gripping her wrist, and his face—for the first time since she'd met him—was doing something unplanned. Something raw. "Sorrel, talk to me. What did it take?"

"His face," she said. Her voice sounded fine. That seemed obscene. "It took his face. I have everything else. I have his whole—I have the eyebrow scar, Bram, I have the scar but I can't see the face around it." She heard herself start to laugh, high and wrong, and clamped down on it. "The order warned us about this. First-year lecture. *Never pay a toll you haven't counted.* I counted. I counted everything except—I didn't think to count his face. Who counts a face? You just have it. You just have it your whole life."

Bram did the last thing she expected. He sat down on the floorboards beside her, folding his long frame awkwardly, and stayed there, shoulder nearly touching hers, saying nothing. He was not good at comfort. She could see him assembling possible sentences and discarding them, one after another, like draft maps. Finally he said:

"When we get him back—the king, when we get the king back—you'll have done the thing Wren died trying to do. That won't give you the face back. Nothing gives it back. But it means the toll bought something."

"That's the most Bram Nakamura consolation imaginable."

"I know." A pause. "It's the only kind I have."

She looked down at her arm. The sigil had gone quiet, the seven gates resting in her skin like a road waiting for a traveler, the old glyph at its center dim and patient. *Let the true thing through.* Somewhere across the city, behind palace walls that were subtly, terribly wrong, a man sat on a throne wearing his own body like a borrowed coat, and a small true self lay buried in the drowned archives of his mind, and tonight—for the first time in however long the thing had worn him—there was a door.

"How long do I have?" she asked. "Before the binding pulls Bram all the way in. Before we have to use it."

Bram was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he looked at the blood-map spread on the table, and she followed his gaze, and saw what she had been refusing to see for two days: the margins of the map had crept inward. The blank paper at the edges was narrower than it had been. The mapped country was growing, or the paper was shrinking, or there was no difference.

"Tomorrow night," Bram said. "Maybe the night after. No later."

Sorrel closed her eyes. Behind them, where a face should have been, there was a smooth pale oval, and she made herself a promise in the dark: *I will finish this, and then I will sit down and draw you, brother, from the hands and the laugh and the eyebrow scar, and it will be wrong, and I will keep it anyway.*

"All right," she said, and opened her eyes, and began wrapping her arm. "Then tomorrow night we go in. Get your ink, cartographer. And Bram—"

He looked up.

"When we're inside, if the door starts taking more from me—from the outside end—you cut the passage. Don't hesitate. Don't negotiate with it. Promise me."

"I'm a very bad liar," Bram said. "But I'll try to be a worse one about this."

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell rang the hour, and beneath its bronze note—faint, unmistakable, wrong—came a second sound, like something vast turning over in its sleep. The sigil on Sorrel's arm pulsed once, cold, in answer.

It knew they were coming.
