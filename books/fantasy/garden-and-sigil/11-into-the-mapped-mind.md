## 11. Into the Mapped Mind

The sigil-door opened the way a wound opens: reluctantly, and with a great deal of blood.

Bram had expected darkness, or light, or some theatrical threshold. Instead there was a moment of vertigo so complete that he forgot which way his feet pointed, and then he was standing on a road.

It was a good road. That was his first thought, professional and absurd in equal measure—*good road*, well-laid, cambered for drainage, running straight as a ruled line through country that had no business existing anywhere but on paper. The stones were pale as bone and fitted so precisely that no weed grew between them. On either side stretched fields of tall grass gone silver with seed-heads, bending in a wind he could not feel against his own skin.

He looked down at himself. His boots were his boots; his coat was his coat; the inkstains on his right cuff were the same three stains that had survived every laundering since he was nineteen. But when he lifted his hand, the light came through it wrong—thinned, like lamplight through vellum. He was a drawing here. A figure in the margin.

"Right," he said aloud, and his voice sounded flat, unechoed, as though the country had not yet decided whether to permit sound. "Right. You've walked stranger maps than this."

He hadn't, actually. But lying to oneself was a cartographer's oldest tool, and Bram Nakamura had never once drawn an honest map in his life.

He unfolded the blood-map at his breast and did not need to. It was already unfolded in the world around him. That was the thing he had failed to understand until now, studying Sorrel's carved sigil still raw and glistening on her forearm, hearing her recite the tolls and thresholds: the map was not a guide to this place. The map *was* this place. Every fold of parchment was a valley; every crease a fault line; every drop of the courier's living blood a river or a road or a walled town with lights in its windows. He stood inside the courier's final act of craft, and the craft was dying even as he breathed it.

Because the edges were wrong.

Bram turned slowly, reading the horizon the way other men read faces. A well-made country has a confident edge—a coastline, a mountain wall, something that says *here I end*. This one ended in nothing. Not darkness exactly. Darkness would have been honest. This was a soft erosion, a blurring, as though someone had taken a wet thumb to the margins of the world and rubbed. Beyond the last field the grass simply stopped being grass, became suggestion, became a grayness that was not empty but *hungry*—he could think of no better word, and hated how easily it came.

And the grayness was closer than the map said it should be.

He checked anyway, out of habit, holding the folded map up against the landscape. The road beneath his feet ran to a town marked in faded umber: *the Archive of Roads*, the courier's cramped hand had labeled it, back when the courier still had hands. On the map, the gray erosion lay a full day's walk beyond it. Here, he could see the blur from the road itself, patient as tide.

"It's faster than you drew it," Bram told the dead man quietly. "You should have said."

But perhaps the courier *had* said, and the map had changed since. Blood-maps bound their readers—that was what Sorrel had warned him, night after night, while she carved and bled and forgot her brother's face one memory at a time. Bound them how? He was beginning to learn. He had dreamed in this geography for three weeks. He knew the turn of this road before his boots reached it. Somewhere behind his sternum, a small cold voice observed that if the map was dying, and he was bound to the map—

He put the thought away the way he put away all thoughts that did not serve the next mile. There would be time for dread later. Dread kept.

* * *

The fields gave way to orchards, and the orchards were drowning.

Not flooded—drowned. There is a difference, and Bram felt it in his teeth. Floodwater moves; it carries leaves and light and the smell of rain. This water stood perfectly still among the trees, black and mirror-calm, rising by increments he could almost hear, the way you can almost hear a candle burning down. The apple trees stood waist-deep, then chest-deep, their fruit heavy and dark above the surface. And under the water—if he crouched at the edge of a drowned row and looked—he could see shelves.

Shelves. Ranks of them, receding into the black depth below the orchard rows, laden with books and scrolls and ledgers whose spines gleamed faintly, gold-tooled titles legible through fathoms of standing water. An archive, sunk whole beneath an orchard, and the orchard planted on top of it as though the planting were a kindness. As though someone had decided that growing things were more bearable than remembering them.

Bram knelt and touched the water.

It remembered him. That was the only way to say it. The surface shivered at his fingertips and then went still again, but the stillness afterward was a *considered* stillness, the stillness of something that had taken his measure. In the reflection, for half a heartbeat, his face was not his face—it was older, wearier, crowned with something heavy—and then it was only Bram again, ink-stained and afraid, which was somehow worse.

"The king," he murmured. "This is him. All of this is him."

The roads remember being walked. The courier had written that in the margin, in a note Bram had read a dozen times without understanding: *the roads remember being walked, follow the ones that flinch.* Now, standing in the country itself, he understood. These were not representations of roads. They were the accumulated fact of a life's walking, pressed into form—the coronation road, the mourning road, the long dull roads of council meetings and harvest festivals and one specific road, worn deeper than all the others, that ran from the palace gates to a modest garden and back, twice a week, for thirty years. A king who walked to a garden. Bram filed that away the way he filed everything: carefully, in the dark, where it might be useful.

He chose a road that flinched.

It happened like this: he would stand at a junction, and the roads would lie there under his gaze like animals under a hawk's, and most of them held still, pretending to be mere ground. But one—a narrow lane running west toward hills he could not name—would tighten, almost imperceptibly, the way a man tightens when a stranger reads the letter over his shoulder. Those roads led somewhere someone did not want found. Which meant they led somewhere worth finding.

The lane took him through the drowned orchards for an hour, past trees whose fruit, when he finally dared touch one, was soft and warm and beat faintly, like a heart grown too large for its casing. He did not eat it. He was not a fool, whatever else he was.

Then the water began to fall behind, and the hills rose ahead, and the grayness at the edge of the world rose with them.

* * *

He saw the erosion eat a village.

There had been no village on the map—or rather, there had been a smudge where a village might be, the courier's hand unsteady, as though he had not wanted to look at it directly. It sat in a shallow vale below the lane: a cluster of stone cottages, a well, a small square church with a bell in an open tower. Lights in the windows. Smoke from the chimneys. People—figures, at least, moving with the unhurried economy of people doing evening chores, fetching water, closing shutters.

They were made of memories. Bram understood that the way he understood perspective: instantly, and with his stomach dropping. The woman at the well was a memory of a nurse who had bandaged a boy-king's knee. The old man splitting wood was thirty years of harvest festivals. The children chasing a dog between the cottages were birthdays, dozens of them, bright and quick and already fading. This was the king's past, given flesh because a mind gives its past flesh when it can afford to, and the mind of Aldric the Hollow King had once been rich enough to keep a whole countryside of yesterdays.

The gray reached the vale at dusk—which was strange, because dusk had not otherwise been falling; the light had held steady, sourceless, the flat illumination of a page. But where the erosion came, it brought its own ending, its own soft dark, and the village lit its windows against it the way villages always do, which is to say: too late, and beautifully.

Bram stopped on the hillside and watched, and could do nothing else, and knew with perfect clarity that he was watching a man forget.

The gray did not crash or roar. It arrived the way fog arrives, the way forgetting arrives. The woman at the well set down her buckets and became less—a little less, then much less, then a shape, then a suggestion of a shape, then a smooth patch of gray with the faint ghost of a bucket still in its hands. The church bell rang once, a single clear note that hurt somewhere behind Bram's eyes, and then the tower was gone, and the note went on ringing in a place where there was no longer any tower to ring from, which was a sentence Bram's mind refused to finish.

In eleven minutes, by the small silver watch he carried because it had belonged to his mother and was the only object in the world he had ever allowed himself to be traced to, the village was a smooth gray hollow in the vale. No ruins. No ash. Just absence, rounded at the edges, like a socket after the tooth is gone.

The erosion moved on. It did not pause. It was not cruel. Cruelty requires attention, and the grayness did not attend to anything; it simply proceeded, thorough and impersonal as arithmetic.

Bram discovered he was gripping the lane's low stone wall hard enough to hurt his hand, and that his face was wet, and that he had been saying "no" under his breath for some time, quietly, like a man haggling.

Thirty years of garden walks. A nurse's hands on a boy's scraped knee. Gone, and going, everywhere, at the pace of a rising tide. And somewhere in the middle of this dissolving country was the true king, buried alive in his own skull, waiting for a rescue that had taken weeks to organize because two liars needed time to gather their courage.

"Move," Bram told himself, and his voice cracked on it, and he moved.

* * *

The lane climbed. Behind him the gray followed—not fast, but without any of the merciful pauses that ordinary disasters allow. Ahead, the hills gathered themselves into a single steep mass crowned with a building that made Bram stop and stare with his whole body, the way he had not stared at anything since he was a boy first shown a map of the sea.

It was a library. Of course it was a library. But it was built upside-down into the hillside, its foundations in the air and its towers descending into the earth, buttresses of pale stone arching downward like the roots of a tree, windows glowing warm along every descending level. The Archive of Roads. The courier's label. A library planted headfirst in the ground, keeping its deepest rooms deepest, guarding whatever lay at the bottom the way a fist guards what it holds.

The doors stood open at the top—doors of dark wood, iron-banded, sized for a man, not a giant. Someone had left them open. Recently. The hinges were bright.

Bram climbed the last stretch with his heart going like a trapped bird, and paused at the threshold, and did the thing he had done at a thousand thresholds before entering a thousand rooms he had no right to enter: he listened.

From far below, faint through the inverted shafts of the library, came a sound. Small. Rhythmic. Patient.

Someone was digging.

* * *

The stairs went down and down, spiraling inside the hill, past shelf after shelf after shelf, and every shelf was drowning. Water seeped from between the books themselves, weeping out of the spines, trickling down the stone in thin black runnels that fed the flood in the orchards above. Bram passed galleries where the water stood ankle-deep, knee-deep, waist-deep, and the books floated in them gently, opening and closing like slow fish, their pages flashing white in the gloom. He caught glimpses as he descended: a coronation oath, the gilt letters dissolving even as he watched. A child's drawing of a garden, the crayon colors bleeding. A treaty. A love letter. A list of names, hundreds of names, each one struck through in a different hand, and the last hand still writing.

He did not stop. Stopping was how the gray got you. He was fairly sure of that now, in the way you are sure of things your skin knows before your mind consents.

At the very bottom, where the roots of the library gripped the bedrock of the mind, there was a room. Small. Round. Lit by a single lamp with a shade of green glass, the kind of lamp a clerk uses, the kind of lamp Bram himself had burned through ten thousand nights of secret work. And in the floor of the room, a hole—a fresh-dug hole, its edges crumbling, its depth unknowable—with a mound of excavated earth beside it, and kneeling at the hole's rim, digging with his bare hands, a small gray man in a plain coat.

The man did not look up. "You're late," he said. His voice was quiet, sanded down, the voice of someone who has been talking to himself for a very long time. "I started without you."

Bram stood in the doorway of the bottom of a king's mind, and his throat closed, and when he finally spoke it came out as the only honest thing he had said in years.

"I didn't know where to dig."

The small man sat back on his heels. He was unremarkable in every particular—thin, stooped, with ink-stained fingers and tired eyes and a face like a clerical error—and it was the unremarkableness that undid Bram, because he had expected a king, and instead he found a man who looked exactly like what he was: someone who had spent thirty years being careful, and hidden, and small, and had dug himself so deep a burrow that even he had nearly lost the way back.

"You're the cartographer," the true King Aldric said. Not a question. "Wren told me you'd come. Before the end, he wrote it down. He wrote everything down." The king gestured at the walls, at the drowning shelves, with hands that shook slightly. "That's the trouble with my memory. It writes everything down, and the water rises anyway." He looked at the hole in the floor, and then up at Bram, and his tired eyes were suddenly, terribly clear. "I've been digging toward myself for six years, Master Nakamura. I'm close now. But the water's faster than I am."

Above them, faint through miles of descending stone, came a sound like a wave that never breaks.

"And," the king added, conversationally, "I'm afraid the thing wearing me has noticed the door you came in by. It's on its way down. So—" he wiped his earthy hands on his plain coat, and smiled, and it was the smile of a man inviting a guest into a flooding cellar, "—shall we talk quickly?"
