## 13. What Sorrel Gave Up

The sigil-door was a wound in the air, and Sorrel Vance was the knife that kept it open.

She knelt on the cold flagstones of the abandoned countinghouse cellar, both palms pressed flat against the carved circle in her own forearm, and held the door with everything she had left. Through it—through the shimmering membrane of light that showed nothing so much as a window into deep water—Bram Nakamura walked a country made of memory, hunting for a king who had been buried alive inside his own skull.

The sigil itched. That was the first sign, always. The carving along her arm—a spiral of interlocking gates, each one a word in the old tongue of binding—had stopped itching an hour ago and started *humming*, low and patient, like a tuning fork struck against bone.

*Payment comes due,* her instructors at the Order of the Silver Thread used to say. *A sigil is a loan against yourself.*

They hadn't mentioned the interest compounded by the hour.

* * *

She had lost things already. She kept a list, scratched into the dust beside her knee with a stylus, because writing them down was the only way to know what was gone. You couldn't miss a memory. It didn't ache like a missing tooth. It simply wasn't there, and the world rearranged itself around the absence so smoothly that only the list could tell her the truth.

*My mother's voice singing on washday.* Gone. She knew she had once known the song; the list said so; the silence where it should have been said otherwise.

*The name of the river in my home village.* Gone. There was just "the river," generic as any river, and a small cold horror every time she reached for it.

*How to whistle.* That one had frightened her most, absurdly. Her body had simply forgotten, lips shaping around empty air, and she'd sat there for a full minute laughing and crying at once because of all the things to lose, the Order's toll had taken *whistling*.

The door flickered. She pressed harder. Blood welled fresh along the carved channels of the sigil, and the light steadied.

"Stay with me, cartographer," she muttered. "You owe me a brother's worth of answers."

* * *

Footsteps on the stairs above. Not Bram's—he'd gone through the door three hours ago, leaving his body slumped against the far wall like a coat hung on a hook, breathing slow, eyes moving beneath closed lids as though reading something enormous. These footsteps were many, and booted, and they did not try to be quiet.

Sorrel closed her eyes and let the sigil speak through her.

The old order had called it *threading*—pulling a working out through your own skin instead of drawing it on paper or stone. Forbidden in her third year, after the Incident with the Novice Hall, which was a story she still told well even if half its details had been paid away by now. Threading cost more than ink ever did. But she had no ink here, no chalk, no time, and enforcers of the Vellum Guild were coming down her stairs.

She threaded a ward of misdirection into the doorway: a simple knot, *what you seek is elsewhere*. Cheap, if you paid in attention rather than memory. The world would slide off the enforcers' eyes like rain off waxed cloth.

Four of them descended anyway. Of course they did. The Guild wrote wards too—they'd written half the ones she'd learned—and they carried counter-sigils stamped into their badge-tokens, little brass discs that ate misdirection for breakfast.

"Well," said the first one, a broad woman with a face like a closed ledger. "The disgraced Vance. Still carving on yourself, I see."

"Ilva." Sorrel didn't get up. Getting up meant letting go of the door. "Come to collect me for the archive theft? Take it up with my brother. Oh—wait."

Ilva's expression didn't change, but something behind it did, a flinch folded small. "We're not here for you. We're here for what's under the city. The Guild has held the Hollow Court's charter for two hundred years, witch. Whatever Nakamura thinks he's found in the king's head, it belongs to us. Step away from the working."

"It's not a working." Sorrel bared her teeth. "It's a door. And he's not stealing anything. He's *returning* something."

"The king is stable," Ilva said, and the word *stable* came out with the flat reverence of scripture. "The realm is stable. Two hundred years of peace, bought and paid for. You'd unmake it for sentiment."

"For a *person*," Sorrel said. "Your ledgers call him an asset line. I call him Wren's king, the one my brother died carrying proof about. And I am not stepping anywhere."

The enforcers moved. Sorrel moved faster.

Threading combat sigils while holding a door open was like fighting with one hand tied behind your back and the hand that remained slowly catching fire. She threw a lash of force that caught the nearest man across the chest and sent him into the wall hard enough to crack plaster. She wove a blind of darkness over the second's eyes. The third reached her before she could finish the fourth working, and his boot came down toward the sigil-circle itself—

She caught his ankle with a hook of force and *pulled*, and he went down sprawling across the flagstones, and then there was a sound like tearing silk and a lance of white-gold light, and all four enforcers were suddenly, thoroughly entangled in threads of burning script that dropped from nowhere and bound them where they stood.

Sorrel knew that handwriting. She hated that handwriting.

"You always did fight like someone apologizing," said Maren Cade, stepping past the struggling enforcers with her hands still trailing golden thread. Senior Sigilist of the Silver Thread, first chair of the Conclave, and the girl who had beaten Sorrel in every examination for six straight years until Sorrel got herself expelled for threading. "Hello, Sorrel."

"Maren." Sorrel's arms were shaking. The door flickered again and she slammed her will back into it. "Here to arrest me too?"

"Here to make you an offer." Maren crouched down to her level, and up close Sorrel could see the years on her—the silver at her temples, the fine tremor in her thread-hand that came from decades of paying the Order's prices. A woman who had spent herself carefully and completely. "And you should hear it, because you're dying by inches on this floor, and the man in the door can't help you, and I can."

* * *

Maren talked while the enforcers strained against their bindings and the sigil-door hummed and dripped light.

"The Conclave knows what the Guild did," she said quietly. "Has known for generations. We signed the accord alongside them—we hold the *counter-ledger*, the record of every price the parasite's stability has cost. Every mind smoothed. Every inconvenient heir retired into pleasant vacancy. We keep the receipts, Sorrel. That's our great moral achievement: meticulous bookkeeping of atrocities." Her mouth twisted. "I've spent thirty years keeping those books. And now there's a chance to burn the whole library, and it's being attempted by a disgraced witch kneeling in a cellar losing her own past one hour at a time."

"Then help," Sorrel said. "Thread me relief. Hold the door with me."

"I can't hold *that* door. No one can but the one who opened it—that's the nature of a flesh-carved gate. But I can do something better." Maren reached into her coat and drew out a slender case of grey leather, and inside it, when she opened it, lay a sigil drawn on vellum so old it had gone the color of tea. Sorrel recognized the work instantly. Pre-accord. First-generation Silver Thread. A restoration rite.

"Every payment you've made tonight," Maren said, "and every payment you made at your expulsion trial, and every one since—it's all recoverable. The Order keeps what we take. Nothing is destroyed, only *held*. This rite calls it back. All of it. Your mother's song. Your brother's face." She let that land, watching Sorrel flinch. "Yes. I know what you paid to open the first door. Wren's face is in a vault in the Conclave tower right now, filed and catalogued. I can put it back where it belongs. Tonight. Before you lose anything else."

The cellar was very quiet. Even the enforcers had stopped struggling to listen.

"And the price?" Sorrel asked, because there was always a price, and Maren respected her enough not to pretend otherwise.

"Walk away. Now, while the door still closes cleanly behind you. Let Nakamura finish alone—if he finishes at all. The Conclave's position is that the possession must be ended *by the book*: formal rite, conclave authority, the parasite contained rather than bargained with. If you withdraw, we proceed properly, and when it's done, you stand restored before the full Conclave. Reinstated. Your expulsion annulled. Your brother's name cleared in our records, whatever the Guild says." Maren's voice gentled, which was worse than any threat. "Sorrel. You've already given more than anyone asked. Nobody would blame you. *I* wouldn't blame you. I've watched people pay these prices for thirty years and I am telling you that the ones who stop are not cowards. They are survivors. Come be a survivor with me."

Sorrel looked at the vellum. At the restoration rite, in the beautiful dead hand of the order's founders. She thought of her mother's song, the shape of it waiting somewhere in a vault like a bird in a cage. She thought of whistling. She thought of Wren's face—which she had already lost, which she had paid without hesitation at the first door and grieved without knowing exactly what she grieved, only that the grief had a name-shaped hole in it.

She thought of Bram Nakamura, asleep against the wall, a man who had built his entire life out of not being known, walking voluntarily into a stranger's skull because a dying courier had pressed a map into his hands and he hadn't been able to put it down. He'd argued with her for a week before he agreed to come. He'd stood at the threshold of the door and said, *I've never done anything worth being seen doing,* and stepped through anyway.

If she walked away, he would come back—or he wouldn't—and either way he would come back to an empty cellar, and either way she would have her mother's song, and she would hum it for the rest of her life in a world where the thing wearing the crown had been *contained* rather than freed, filed rather than named, managed forever by committees of careful women keeping meticulous books.

"No," Sorrel said.

Maren closed the case slowly. "Sorrel—"

"No. Listen to me, Maren. You want to know why they expelled me? It wasn't the threading. It was because I told the Conclave that a sigil paid for in memories is a lie you tell yourself—that you can't buy change with pieces of other people and call the change yours. The Guild learned that lesson two hundred years ago and decided they liked the lie." She lifted her chin, and her arm screamed, and the door blazed. "I'm not taking back the pieces. I'm spending the rest. That's the whole difference between me and everyone in your tower. That's *always* been the difference."

For a long moment Maren Cade looked at her—looked at her the way you look at a position on a board you have just realized you miscalculated twenty moves ago.

Then she laughed, short and helpless, and rose to her feet. "Gods. You haven't changed at all."

"Neither have you. You brought the offer because part of you hoped I'd refuse."

"Part of me hoped you'd refuse," Maren admitted. She turned to the bound enforcers, and her hands began to move, weaving something new, threads of gold gathering thick around her fingers. "Because if you refused, then I finally have permission to do what I've wanted to do for thirty years." She glanced back over her shoulder, and her eyes were bright and fierce and terrified. "I'm holding your cellar, witch. Enforcers, reinforcements, whatever the Guild sends—I'll hold it until you and your cartographer walk back out of that king's head. Consider it the Conclave's first act of treason in two centuries. Try not to die, or I'll have committed treason for nothing, and I'll *never* forgive you."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the countinghouse's front doors giving way. Many boots. Many more than four.

* * *

Sorrel turned back to the door.

The humming in her arm had become a ringing, and the ringing was becoming a silence—not pain exactly, but the particular hush of a room after a piece of furniture is carried out. She looked at her list in the dust. Three new lines had appeared in her own handwriting that she had no memory of writing:

*The smell of rain on the Order courtyard.*
*Wren's laugh.*
*Why I became a sigil-witch.*

She stared at that last one for a long time. She could feel the hole where it had been, smooth-sided, clean-cut, recent. She didn't know anymore why she had chosen this life. She only knew that she had chosen it, that the choosing was real even if its reason was gone, the way a riverbed proves the river.

"Fine," she whispered to whoever kept the accounts. "Take it. Take all of it. I'll be a stranger holding a door, and I'll still hold the door."

Through the membrane of light, far off in the drowned green distance of the king's mind, she saw a figure on a road that remembered being walked—small, dark, unmistakably Bram—stop, and turn, and look back toward the door as if he could see her through it. As if he knew.

His mouth moved. No sound crossed. But she read the shape of it easily enough, having spent a week learning how the man spoke when he wasn't hiding: *Almost. Hold on.*

"Hold on yourself," Sorrel said, and pressed her bleeding palm harder against the sigil as the stairs filled with boots, and Maren Cade's golden threads rose around them both like a garden of light, and somewhere beneath the city a buried king turned his face toward a door that was still, barely, wonderfully, open.
