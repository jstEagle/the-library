## 16. Sigil and Counter-Sigil

The safehouse Sorrel had chosen was a defunct chandler's shop in Tanners' Row, three streets from the river, where the fog came in off the water thick enough to chew and the neighbors had long ago learned that asking questions was a habit that got you drowned. She liked it for the same reasons Bram would have: no one looked at a place like this twice. The difference between them, she was beginning to understand, was that Bram had built his whole life out of places like this, and she had only ever used them as ammunition.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of the old workroom with her brother's blood-map spread before her on a sheet of oilcloth, and she hated it, and she loved it, and she could not stop looking at it.

Wren's handwriting. Wren's careful little marginalia, the notes he'd always made in the corners of everything—grocery lists annotated with weather observations, letters to her with postscripts about the price of candles. He'd drawn this map of the Hollow King's mind the way he'd done everything: thoroughly, precisely, and without any idea it would kill him.

*Don't trust the margins,* one note read, in his cramped hand. *They move.*

She knew. She'd been watching the margins for two days now, out of the corner of her eye, never directly, because direct attention seemed to freeze them the way frost froze a window. When she looked away and looked back, the sigil in the lower border had shifted—a quarter-turn clockwise, a hair's breadth closer to the map's edge, like something testing the walls of its cage.

"It's still moving," she said.

Bram looked up from the far side of the room, where he'd been scratching figures into a wax tablet by lamplight. He had the particular stillness of a man who had spent years being overlooked and had turned it into a discipline. Even now, knowing what she knew about him—that his name was in the Guild's ledgers after all, written there since his birth, unread but not unwritten—he moved like someone trying to take up less space than a body required.

"Has it changed shape?" he asked.

"Rotated. Maybe a degree." She didn't touch it. You didn't touch a sigil you couldn't parse; every witch learned that before she learned to write her own name in light. "It's patient. Whatever's in there with the king, it isn't clawing at the borders. It's measuring them."

"Cartographers measure," Bram said quietly. "That's how we were taught."

*We.* He still did that sometimes—said *we* as if the craft were a country they both held citizenship in, when really it was a country that had expelled her and swallowed him whole. But she let it go. There were larger grievances in the world tonight than the way a man said *we*.

She smoothed the oilcloth flat and made herself look at the map properly, all of it, the way she hadn't dared in the first days. A mind rendered as terrain: drowned archives where the king's memories should have been, roads that remembered being walked, a garden gone to seed at the center where—according to everything they'd learned in the mindscape—the true king sat buried alive inside his own skull. And coiled through all of it, threaded into the rivers and the boundary-stones and the roots of the hills, the parasite. The thing wearing the crown. The thing whose name they did not know.

"You've checked the rite again," she said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd checked it eleven times; she'd counted.

"The unbinding needs three things." Bram recited it the way he recited everything, flat and exact, a man reading coordinates. "A door into the king's mind that the parasite doesn't control. A reader who can hold the map's geography steady while the rite works—because if the mindscape shifts mid-rite, the binding collapses inward and takes the king with it. And the parasite's true name, spoken by someone standing inside the boundary."

"Two of three we can do."

"Two of three we can do badly," he corrected. "But yes. The name is the wall."

Sorrel pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. In the old days—in the order, before the expulsion, before Wren's face became a wound she carried instead of a face she remembered—she'd have had a chapter house full of sisters to consult on a problem like this. Elders who'd spent centuries cataloguing names. Now she had a smuggler-cartographer, a dead brother's map, and a sigil carved into her own forearm that itched whenever the parasite moved in its sleep.

"Every binding has a true name," she said. "It's the first law. You can't unmake what you can't call. So the question is—who named it?"

Bram set down the stylus. For a moment he just looked at her across the lamplit room, and she saw him decide something, the way she'd seen him decide things twice before: once when he'd shown her the courier's warning, once when he'd told her about the Guild's ledger and the name written in it that he'd never known was there.

"The Guild," he said. "It has to be. Think about what we found in the mindscape archive. My master drew the first blood-map. He taught the thing how to wear a king. He didn't do that alone—you don't hand a parasite a crown without institutional backing. The Guild brokered the possession. Which means the Guild performed the binding. Which means—" He tapped the wax tablet. "The naming rite is in their records. The original ledger. Not a copy. Copies get sanitized; I've sanitized enough of them myself. The true name exists exactly once, in the burned original, in the vault under the countinghouse."

"The vault you told me about. The one that isn't on any map."

"No map can hold it. That's rather the point of it." He almost smiled, and it was a strange thing to watch, like seeing frost form on moving water. "I spent nine years making sure I never needed to go down there. Now I need to go down there more than I've ever needed anything."

*

They worked through the night, and the work felt, absurdly, like honesty.

That was the thought Sorrel kept circling back to as the candles burned down and the fog pressed against the shutters. She had spent her whole adult life lying—with sigils, with false faces, with the careful blankness the order had taught her to wear like a second skin. Bram had spent his whole life lying too, with omissions, with absence, with a name kept off every page. Two liars, bent over a table covered in stolen craft and forbidden lore, planning a theft from the most heavily warded vault in the capital.

And yet nothing she'd ever done had felt so clean.

Because the lie they were building toward wasn't a lie at all. It was a truth so heavy it needed a crowbar. *The king is not the king. Here is his name. Here is the door. Here is how we put him back together.* Every line of the plan pointed at something real, and she hadn't aimed at something real in years.

"Say it again," she said, around the fourth candle. "The sequence. Slowly."

Bram didn't sigh, though she could tell he wanted to. Instead he drew the diagram again on the wax tablet, his stylus moving with the economy of a man who'd drawn ten thousand maps and wasted no motion on any of them.

"I enter the vault ahead of you—I'm the only one who can find it blind, and the wards key to perception, not passage. If you can't perceive the door, the door can't perceive you. I map it by feel. Touch, sound, air pressure, the way the stone remembers weight. It'll take time. An hour, possibly two."

"While I hold the enforcers off."

"While you hold the enforcers off," he agreed, and here his voice did something complicated, a small fracture running through the flatness. "Sorrel. The counter-sigils I've drafted will cost you—"

"I know what they'll cost."

"—you don't, actually, because you haven't cast them yet. Reading about memory-toll and paying it are different countries." He set the stylus down and looked at her with an expression she was learning to hate, because it was care, and care was a debt, and debts were how people got hold of you. "You've already paid once. Your brother's face. You told me yourself you can't—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and the sigil on her forearm flared hot in sympathy, ink remembering anger. She breathed until it cooled. "I know what I paid. I know what I'll pay. Wren paid with everything, and he didn't even get to choose the manner of it. I get to choose mine. Do you understand what a luxury that is? Do you understand that I would carve my whole arm bare if it meant finishing this?"

Bram was quiet for a long moment. The candle guttered; shadows ran along the ceiling like water finding its level.

"That's the part I keep failing at," he said finally. "Choosing to be seen. Choosing to pay in public. I've hidden so long that hiding feels like breathing. And you're over there volunteering to lose pieces of yourself, and I keep thinking—if I were better at what you're doing, none of this would be necessary. I'd have seen the parasite years ago. I'd have blown the Guild's secret wide open. Instead I mapped realms nobody could reach and called it survival."

"It was survival," Sorrel said. "For a while. Survival always is—for a while." She reached across the table and turned the blood-map so it faced him. "Then the while ends, and you find out what the hiding cost. Wren hid in plain sight for six years, right under the archivists' noses, copying that map out of the royal archive one inch at a time. It worked beautifully. Right up until it killed him." Her voice caught on the last word, and she let it, because some catches deserved to be heard. "So yes. We pay. We pay in advance, we pay in full, and we pay each other's debts when we can. That's the whole of the plan, Bram. Everything else is cartography."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he picked up the stylus and said, "Third candle-burn. I map the inner vault by feel while you run interference. We meet at the sump-gate below the countinghouse, dawn minus one hour. We speak the name together or not at all—one voice can be denied, two voices make a witness."

"Two voices make a target," she said.

"Yes," Bram said. "That too."

And there it was again, that terrible new thing in him: a man agreeing, deliberately, to be a target. To be *known*. She'd watched it happen over the past weeks the way you watch ice break up on a river in spring—slowly, then all at once, the whole frozen surface of him groaning and shifting and coming apart into moving water. She wasn't sure she trusted it. She was absolutely sure she admired it.

*

Near dawn, when the fog outside had gone from grey to the color of old vellum, they laid out the final draft of the plan on the oilcloth beside the blood-map, and Sorrel did the last thing she had been avoiding.

She drew the counter-sigils.

There were four. She'd designed them over three sleepless nights, working from lore she'd sworn never to use again, the expelled chapters of her order's teaching that lived in her hands whether or not her hands were permitted to use them. Each one was a ward against the Guild's tracking-craft, and each one took its payment from a different room of her memory.

The first ate small things: the taste of specific meals, the color of specific doors. Cheap toll. She'd pay it gladly.

The second ate skills—not permanently, but for the duration, which might be forever if she died holding the line. She'd lose, temporarily, her finest gradework, the delicate single-line sigils that were her signature. Casting it was like amputating a finger to save the hand.

The third ate people. Not faces—she'd already lost the one face that mattered—but the *feel* of people. The warmth. She would remember that Bram Nakamura was her ally the way you remember a fact from a book, and not the way you remember a friend.

The fourth she wrote small, at the bottom of the page, and did not explain aloud, because explaining it would mean admitting what it cost, and admitting that would give Bram grounds to argue, and she could not afford an argument. The fourth ate the plan itself. If she cast all four, she would stand in the fog outside the countinghouse defending a door she could not fully remember the reason for, trusting a man she could not fully remember trusting, on the strength of instructions she'd have sewn into her own sleeve in advance.

*Trust the sewing,* she wrote in the margin of her own notes. *Past-me is the only witness future-me gets.*

"What's the fourth one?" Bram asked. Of course he asked. He noticed everything; it was his curse and his craft.

"A reserve," she said. "You'll know it if you see it."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide—visibly, consciously—not to press. Another new thing. The old Bram, the ledger-Bram, the man who'd survived by knowing more than everyone, would have pried. This one folded his hands and said, "When this is over, you'll tell me."

"When this is over," she agreed, "I'll tell you whatever's left to tell."

The joke landed wrong and they both knew it, and neither of them laughed, and somehow that was its own kind of honesty.

*

She slept two hours in the chandler's back room, on a pallet that smelled of tallow and river-damp, and dreamed of the vault.

In the dream it was not a vault at all but a library, endless, and every book was a name, and every name was screaming softly, the way names scream when they've been locked away from the mouths that ought to speak them. She walked the stacks looking for one book in particular. She knew its color. She'd known its color her whole life, though she couldn't have said why. Green, the green of garden leaves, the green of—

She woke with the sigil on her arm burning and Bram's silhouette in the doorway, already dressed, already packed, already the shape of a man walking into the deepest room of his enemies' house.

"Dawn minus two hours," he said. "Fog's thick. Best cover we'll get."

Sorrel rose, bound her hair back, and rolled up her sleeves. On her left forearm, the door-sigil she'd carved weeks ago had healed into a pale silver scar, and beneath it, waiting, she inked the first counter-sigil in washable chalk-base—ready to fire when the enforcers came.

At the door, Bram paused. He didn't turn around. She watched his shoulders rise and fall once, the breath of a man standing at the edge of the unmapped country and choosing, for the first time in his life, to walk in with his name pinned to his coat.

"Sorrel," he said. "If the fourth sigil fires—if I come out of that vault and you don't know why you're standing there—look for the green book. I'll be carrying it."

Her skin went cold. "I never told you about the dream."

"No," Bram said, and now he did turn, and his eyes in the pre-dawn gloom were calm and terribly certain. "You dreamed it. I've been dreaming the same library for three nights. The vault is answering us, Sorrel. It knows we're coming—and either that's a door opening, or it's a trap closing. Either way, we're out of time."

He pushed open the door onto the fog, and the fog took him the way it took everything, gently and completely, and Sorrel stood alone in the chandler's shop with her brother's map, her burning arm, and the growing certainty that somewhere beneath the countinghouse, something ancient had just begun to read *them*.
