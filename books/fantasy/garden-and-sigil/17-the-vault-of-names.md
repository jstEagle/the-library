## 17. The Vault of Names

The Vellum Guild's deepest vault did not exist.

That was not a figure of speech. Bram had spent three nights with the Guild's public floor plans spread across Sorrel's kitchen table, and three nights finding the same absence: below the countinghouse on Candlewick Row, where the ledgers of debts and deaths were kept, the plans simply stopped. The stairwells ended in blank vellum. The surveyors' marks trailed off like footprints in snow that had stopped snowing. Somewhere beneath the fog and the counting floors was a room the Guild had paid good money to leave unmapped—and the Guild did not pay for anything it could get for free.

"They call it the Vault of Names," Sorrel had said, her finger resting on the blank space without touching it, as though even paper could be dangerous. "My brother called it that too. He said every name the Guild has ever erased goes down there. Every person they've made unreadable." She had looked at him then, and Bram had felt the look like a hand pressed flat against his chest. "People like you, Bram. People who thought they were invisible."

Now he stood in the dark at the bottom of a stairwell that ended in blank vellum, and he understood why no one had ever mapped this place.

It wasn't warded. Wards were legible; wards were *arguments*, and arguments could be answered. The vault was something older and more arrogant than that. It was simply *unmappable*—a space that declined, with the serene confidence of an institution that had never once been audited, to have edges. When Bram closed his eyes and reached out with the cartographer's sense his master had beaten into him—the sense of walls as lines, of rooms as shapes, of distance as a kind of grammar—he got nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. His mind slid off the space ahead of him the way a pen slides off oiled parchment.

"Feel anything?" Sorrel whispered behind him. Her voice was tight. She had already begun paying for tonight, and they both knew it, and neither of them had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a debt, and debts were the one currency the Guild understood better than anyone alive.

"No," Bram said. "That's the point."

He knelt and put his palm flat against the stone floor. Cold came up through his skin like water through cloth. And then, because there was nothing else—because the eyes were useless here and the trained mind was useless here—he did the thing he had sworn never to do again since the night he'd found his master's first blood-map folded inside a book of psalms.

He opened a vein.

Not dramatically. A cartographer's lancet, a shallow cut along the pad of his thumb, the kind of wound a man gets from a bad penknife. He pressed his bleeding thumb to the stone and began, by feel, by touch, by the slow blind patience of a man reading braille with his whole body, to draw.

The blood-map was not a picture. It was a question asked in the only ink that could not lie. *What is here?* The stone drank the blood the way dry ground drinks rain, and for a long moment nothing happened, and Bram's heart counted out its frightened arithmetic in his throat.

Then the floor answered.

It came up through his palm not as sight but as *shape*—a pressure, a contour, the ghost of geometry. There was a corridor ahead. No: not a corridor. A throat. The vault was built like something swallowed—a passage that narrowed, turned twice against the natural run of the architecture, and opened into a chamber whose dimensions were wrong in a way Bram's fingers understood before his mind did. The chamber was larger inside than the building above it could possibly contain. It had been mapped into bigness, stretched like taffy, by someone who had known exactly what Bram knew and had used it for exactly the purpose Bram had spent his life refusing.

His master's work. Or his master's master's. The lineage went down into the dark alongside the stairs.

"There's a door," Bram breathed. "Two turns past the second turn. Iron, but old iron—older than the Guild's charter." He wiped his thumb on his sleeve and stood, swaying slightly. Blood-loss made the dark swim pleasantly, like the deck of a ship. "Sorrel. The moment we go through, they'll know."

"They already know," Sorrel said. "They've known since we burned the Candlewick safehouse. This isn't stealth anymore, Bram. It's a race." He heard her roll her shoulders, heard the small wet sound of her flexing her carved hand—the sigil cut into her palm from the first door, the one that had cost her her brother's face, still faintly warm, still faintly hungry. "How long do you need in there?"

"To find the ledger? Minutes. To read it?" He hesitated. "I don't know. Names are heavy. Reading one true name might take as long as reading a city."

"Then I buy you minutes." She said it the way other people said *pass the bread*. "Go."

* * *

The throat of the vault swallowed them.

Bram walked it by the map in his mind, one hand trailing the wall, counting turns in the dark. Twice the passage tried to be longer than it was—to stretch, subtly, the way the chamber above had been stretched—and both times he caught it, because he had spent twenty years catching rooms in the act of lying about themselves. He laughed once, low and breathless, and Sorrel shot him a look he couldn't see.

"What?"

"It's cheating," he said. "The vault is cheating. It's using my own craft against me—making itself unmeasurable so no one can hold it in their head." His hand found the iron door, cold as a grave lid, furred with rust. "But a lie about distance is still a lie, and every lie has a shape. You just have to feel for the seam."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're standing in a wall when the enforcers arrive, and honestly, Sorrel, that might be kinder."

The lock was a sigil-lock, and it nearly killed them. The moment Sorrel's fingers touched it, the old iron woke—not with light or sound but with *recognition*, the way a dog wakes. It tasted her. It tasted the sigil carved into her palm. And it began, patiently, to read her back: her order training, her expulsion, her brother's name, the shape of her grief. Bram felt the air change temperature and grabbed her wrist.

"Don't let it finish a sentence about you," he snapped. "Sigil-locks feed on identity. Give it nothing true."

"So I give it something false."

"You're a sigil-witch. Lie in your own language."

She grinned in the dark—he couldn't see it, but he heard it, heard the wild bright edge in her breathing—and pressed her ruined palm to the lock and spoke a word that was not a word, a counter-sigil she must have stitched together from forbidden fragments and pure spite. The lock shuddered. Something in the iron *believed her*, briefly, gloriously, the way a mark believes a forged coin. The door swung inward on a darkness deeper than the dark around it.

Beyond lay the Vault of Names.

Bram had imagined shelves. He had imagined ledgers in their thousands, dust and vellum, the great grey sediment of the Guild's centuries. What he found instead was a garden.

Rows of white stakes grew from a floor of packed earth, and from each stake hung a single strip of vellum, and on each strip was written a name in ink that moved—slowly, like insects under bark. The room breathed. That was the horror of it, the thing his blood-map had failed to warn him of: the names were not recorded here. They were *kept*. Alive. Each strip of vellum trembled faintly, the way a held bird trembles, and the air was thick with a murmur too low to parse, ten thousand names whispering themselves over and over so they would not be forgotten by the only thing that remembered them.

"There," Sorrel whispered, and her voice cracked on it. She had gone pale in the gloom. She was staring at a stake near the far wall, and Bram knew, with sick certainty, whose name hung there. "Wren. Wren Vance. He's—" Her hand rose toward the strip and stopped. "He's still *here*. They didn't erase him. They're keeping him. Why would they keep—"

"Because a kept name can be sold back," Bram said quietly. "Everything in this room is inventory, Sorrel. Grief included." He hated how steady his voice was. He hated more that steadiness was the only gift he had to give her right now. "Find your brother later. We have minutes. Where would the original charter ledger be?"

She dragged her eyes away from the trembling vellum. Pointed, hand shaking, to the center of the room, where a single lectern of black wood stood apart from the rows like a headstone stands apart from beds. On it lay a book so old its covers had gone the color of dried liver. The First Ledger. The one the Guild claimed had burned in the Charter Fire two hundred years ago, the lie upon which every other lie was mortared.

Bram crossed the earth-floor garden of stolen names and lifted the book, and the moment his fingers closed on the cover, every strip of vellum in the vault went silent at once.

Ten thousand names, holding their breath.

He opened the ledger. The pages were not paper. They were skin—no, he corrected himself, pressing down his revulsion, they were *vellum*, calfskin, ordinary calfskin, and it only looked like skin because everything in this room wanted him to think of bodies. The script inside was his master's. Of course it was. His master's hand was all through this place, in the stretched geometry, in the sigil-lock, in the very idea of a vault that refused to exist. The old man had not merely drawn the first blood-map. He had been the Guild's gardener here, pruning names, planting parasites, teaching the thing that wore the crown how to fit itself to a human shape.

Bram turned pages. Debts. Coronations. Centuries of quiet transactions in which the Guild had traded stability for sovereignty, selling kings their own survival at compound interest. And there, near the end, in ink fresher than the rest:

*The tenant in the royal house. Bound by invitation, sustained by charter.*

And beneath the entry, its true name.

Bram read it once. The name was not words—it was a shape, a folded geometry of sound, and reading it hurt the way looking at the sun hurts, not because it was bright but because it was *true*. The name sat in his mind like a nail driven into a beam. He would never get it out. He understood, with a lurch, that this was by design: the ledger did not record the parasite's name. It *held* it, the way the strips of vellum held the stolen ones. By reading it, he had become part of the vault.

"Bram." Sorrel's voice, sharp with fear. "The vellum. Look at the vellum."

Every strip in the garden had begun to flutter. Not gently now—frantically, like leaves before a storm, like birds against glass. The whispering resumed, faster, higher, and Bram realized with cold clarity what it meant. The names weren't whispering to themselves anymore.

They were screaming. Because the vault had noticed that its most dangerous prisoner's name had just walked out of it, folded up small and burning, inside the skull of a cartographer who had spent his whole life learning how to carry things unseen.

"The ledger stays," Bram said. "We take the name. The name is enough." He slammed the book shut and the silence that followed was worse than the screaming. "Sorrel—we need to be moving *now*."

"I know." Her face had changed. Above them, muffled through fathoms of stretched stone, boots were starting to thunder. "Bram, listen to me. There are at least six enforcers on the stairs and more coming through the countinghouse. I can hold the throat of the passage—I can fold it, make it long, buy you time to get clear—but the sigil I need—"

"How much?"

She didn't pretend not to understand. That was one of the things he had come to love about her, in the fierce and inconvenient way he had lately admitted to himself: she never pretended the bill was smaller than it was.

"A year," she said. "Maybe two. Of memory. I don't get to choose which." Her jaw set. "I've already lost his face. Whatever else goes, at least it won't be that twice."

"Sorrel—"

"*Go read your name-shape somewhere safe,*" she said, and pressed her carved palm to the doorway, and began to speak.

* * *

Bram ran.

He ran through the throat of the vault while it screamed behind him—not the vault, *her*, Sorrel's voice bent into syllables that made the passage groan and lengthen and remember being a mile wide instead of forty feet. He ran past enforcers who had entered a corridor that now had no end, heard their shouts thinning into confusion as the miles accumulated between them and the door. He ran with the parasite's name nailed into his mind and the First Ledger's truths burning behind his eyes, and somewhere in the running he began to laugh, or sob, or both, because they had done it. Against the Guild, against the crown, against the unmappable itself—they had done it.

He burst out through the blank-vellum stairwell into the fog of Candlewick Row, lungs full of knives, and turned to wait for her.

The passage exhaled. Sorrel stumbled out of it a moment later, gray-faced, blood running freely from her carved palm, and looked at him with eyes that searched his face a half-second too long.

"Did we get it?" she asked. And then, before he could answer, with a small frown, as if consulting a shelf and finding it dusty: "I'm sorry—who—"

Her gaze cleared. She blinked. "Bram. Right. Bram Nakamura." She said his name carefully, like a word in a language she was relearning. "I know you. I know I know you. Give me a moment and I'll remember why."

Above them, in the dark, the vault began—very softly, very patiently—to sing the parasite's name back to itself, calling it home.

Bram took her hand. "Walk first," he said. "Remember later. We have a king to save, and I finally know how—but Sorrel, the rite only works from *inside* him."

She stared at him through the fog, and he watched the meaning land, watched her understand what he had already begun, in some cold cellar of his heart, to plan.

"Inside," she repeated. "Bram. How in God's name do you get *inside* a king?"

He looked down at his thumb, still bleeding, and said nothing at all.
