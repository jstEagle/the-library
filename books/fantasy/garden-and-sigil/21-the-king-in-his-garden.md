## 21. The King in His Garden

The garden had been dead for eleven years, and Sorrel Vance could tell because the stones remembered it alive.

She stood at the edge of the palace's eastern terrace, her hands still wrapped in linen bandages where the sigils had been carved, and watched the king kneel in the dirt like any common gardener, turning over black soil with his bare fingers. Around him, the beds lay fallow—rank with weeds gone woody, hedges grown wild and leggy, a stone fountain choked with leaves. But beneath it all, under the neglect, the bones of something beloved. Rose walks. A knot garden laid out in the old style. An arbor that had once, she was certain, been trained with wisteria.

The stones whispered it to her. They had been whispering since the coronation, since before—murmuring the true history of the Hollow King through the soles of her boots whenever she walked the capital's old quarters. Now, standing this close to the man himself, the whispers had changed their tune. They no longer spoke of what had been done *to* the garden.

They spoke of who had planted it.

*A boy king,* said the stones of the terrace steps. *A boy of nine winters, given seeds by his mother before the regents took her away. He planted them himself, badly, in rows that wandered like a drunkard's handwriting. The roses came up crooked and he loved them best.*

Sorrel's throat tightened. She looked at the man kneeling in the dirt—the Hollow King, Aldric Vane, thirty-one years old now, his hands black to the wrist—and tried to see the boy in the wandering rows.

It was hard. Not because he seemed grand. Because he seemed *small*, in the way that people are small after they have survived something enormous: shrunken around the absence of it, like a garment worn too long by someone larger.

Eleven years. Eleven years the parasite had worn him, and the court had applauded its performance, and the Guild had collected its fees, and no one—not one soul in all the marble splendor of the capital—had noticed that the king had stopped gardening.

He looked up as if he'd heard her thinking. His eyes were ordinary brown eyes, unremarkable, human. That was the thing that undid her every time she saw them now. She had spent weeks staring into those eyes when they were windows with something vast and patient looking out, and she had learned to read the wrongness of them the way sailors read weather. And now they were just... eyes. Tired ones.

"Master Vance." He said it formally, but there was a wry note underneath, an apology for the formality. "You're hovering."

"I'm supervising," Sorrel said. "There's a difference. One of us has sigil-lore and the other has dirt."

"And which is which?" He smiled—a real smile, lopsided, a little uncertain of itself, like a muscle remembering an old shape. "I'm told I used to be good at dirt. The records disagree about everything else."

"The records," Sorrel said, "were written by the thing that ate you."

Something flickered across his face—not pain exactly, but the place where pain would go if he let it travel. He brushed off his knees and stood, and Sorrel was struck again by how carefully he moved, how every gesture seemed to be tested against some internal ledger before it was permitted. *Is this mine? Did I do this, before? Would I have done this?* She recognized the habit. She had caught herself doing the same thing since the vault, since the memories began coming back in pieces—reaching for a recollection and finding a hole shaped exactly like it, then building the day around the hole as if the hole were furniture.

"You slept badly," the king said. It wasn't a question. "You were walking the north colonnade at the third bell. I heard your footsteps stop at the same spot four times."

"You hear footsteps from the east terrace?"

"I hear everything now." He said it lightly, and it landed heavily anyway. "Eleven years of listening from inside a locked room. I find I haven't learned how to stop."

Sorrel came down the steps and joined him at the nearest bed. Up close, the soil smelled turned and living, and there was something almost obscene about it—that anything should smell this much like spring after what they had crawled out of. She crouched, wincing, and pressed her bandaged palm flat against the earth. Old habit. Grounding. Her order had called it *listening with the skin*, before her order expelled her, before the order itself was revealed as the Guild's oldest glove.

The stones under the bed murmured. *He asked the head gardener to teach him grafting. He was twelve. The parasite let him keep it—it cost nothing, the body did the work while the self sat in the dark and watched. He grew a rose with petals like torn paper and named it for his mother. The parasite ordered it dug out the following spring, calling it sentiment. The gardener wept. The king, inside, memorized the weeping, because it was the only honest sound left in the palace.*

"He kept a rose," Sorrel said quietly. "Named for his mother. It was dug up."

The king went very still.

"How do you know that?"

"The city tells me things. It's a bad habit I picked up using forbidden sigils in old streets." She glanced up at him. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—I shouldn't have said it like that."

"No." He exhaled slowly. "No, say it. Say all of it. Everyone in this palace has spent two days asking me how I feel with their eyes fixed just past my shoulder, waiting for me to be either broken or magnificent. You're the first person to hand me back a piece of myself I didn't know I'd lost." His voice roughened. "What else do they say? The stones."

"That you planted crooked rows at nine years old. That you loved the crooked ones best."

He laughed—or made a sound adjacent to laughing, brief and startled out of him, and then pressed the back of his hand to his mouth as though the sound had escaped without permission. When he lowered his hand his eyes were wet, and he did not apologize for that either.

"Crooked rows," he repeated. "Yes. I remember now. It comes back the way yours must—doesn't it? In fragments. Wrong order. With the edges burned off."

"Worse than burned off," Sorrel said. "Mine come back *damaged*. I got my brother's face yesterday."

The words fell between them, and she watched him understand what she meant—not the fact of it, which he knew, but the shape of the wound.

"Tell me," he said.

So she told him, because he had earned it, and because saying it aloud was the only way she had found to make the fragments bearable—as if memory were a splinter and speech the tweezers.

"I was holding the door open. The sigil-door into your mind. Every hour it stayed open, the carving took another payment out of me, and I could feel them going—memories, whole seasons of my life, sliding out like water through fingers. And then Wren Ashgrave found me." She named her old rival flatly, without heat; the heat had burned down to ash days ago. "Offered me restoration. Everything the sigil took, returned whole, if I walked away and let you and Bram finish it alone. The Order's own restoration rite, she said. Perfect recall. All I had to do was stop."

"And you refused."

"I refused." Sorrel flexed her bandaged hand. "And here's the joke, Your Majesty. Here's the punchline I've been turning over at three in the morning. If I'd taken her offer, I'd remember my brother perfectly right now. Every detail. The exact gray of his eyes, the way he hummed when he copied maps, the stupid joke he made the last time I saw him alive. Instead I paid it all to hold the door, and what came back—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "What came back is a face like a portrait painted by someone who loved me and never looked closely. Warm. Vague. *Approximately* Tam. I can't remember his voice anymore. I know that I knew it. I know it mattered. But the sound itself is gone, and it's not coming back, because you can't restore what was freely spent. Wren even told me that. She thought it would break me. She thought I'd realize I'd traded my brother for a stranger in a crown."

"And did it break you?"

Sorrel considered the question honestly, the way she now tried to consider everything honestly, having spent most of her life in disguise and finding the habit harder to break than expected.

"No," she said finally. "Because Bram said something to me. After. When we were both sitting in the wreckage of everything, covered in ink and blood and each other's mistakes. He said: *a memory held perfectly isn't a gift, it's a cage with the bars on the inside.* He said Tam wouldn't want to be a perfect picture on a wall. He'd want to be missed properly—messily, incompletely, by someone still moving forward." She shrugged, and the shrug trembled. "I don't know if it's true. But it's the only version I can live inside, so I've decided to believe it, and I'm discovering that deciding to believe things is apparently what being a person is."

The king knelt back down in the soil. For a long moment he said nothing, working his fingers through the dirt, and when he spoke again his voice was low.

"When I was in the dark—all those years, watching through my own eyes—I used to think the worst part was the watching. It isn't. The worst part is that I *agreed*. I opened the door myself. I invited it in, to survive the coup, and I told myself it was temporary, and eleven years passed while I told myself that." He looked up at her. "Your brother died carrying the truth of me out of the archive. Bram unbound me at a price I'll spend the rest of my reign repaying. And you held a door open with your own skin until it took your brother's face." He shook his head slowly. "I keep waiting for someone to present the bill. That's what crowns do, in my experience. Someone always presents the bill."

"The bill was presented," Sorrel said. "We already paid it. That's rather the point of the last month."

"Then why does it feel like the paying hasn't started?"

"Because you're a king again and kings are terrible at receiving gifts." She stood, brushing soil from her skirts, and nodded toward the far end of the terrace, where a figure had appeared in the arched doorway—tall, dark-haired, moving with that particular careful lightness of a man who had spent his whole life ensuring that no surface remembered him. "Speaking of which. There goes the man who won't accept protection from a monarch, a guild charter, or the weather. You might as well try your speech now. He'll refuse it. But I think he needs to hear it anyway."

* * *

Bram Nakamura crossed the terrace the way he crossed every room: as if the room were a formality and his presence in it a clerical error he intended to correct quietly. Even now—even with his name spoken in the Guild's halls, entered in their ledgers in fresh ink, hunted openly by the enforcers who had survived the purge—he walked like a rumor.

But Sorrel had learned to read him, these past weeks, the way she read sigils: by what was absent. And what was absent now was the smugness. The man who had once prided himself on being unreadable walked like a man who had discovered that being known was survivable and hadn't yet decided what to do with the discovery.

"Your Majesty." He stopped at a respectful distance. "The eastern archive inventories are finished. The Guild's copies of the blood-map techniques are catalogued for burning—your marshal has the list. And the last of the enforcers withdrew from the city at dawn. You're free of them."

"I was free of them the moment you spoke that thing's name," the king said. "Sit down, Master Nakamura."

"I'd rather stand."

"I know. Sit down anyway." There was steel under the mildness now, and Sorrel hid a smile; eleven years of captivity had apparently cured Aldric Vane of any shortage of stubbornness. Bram, after a pause that communicated volumes, lowered himself onto the terrace step—perched, really, like a bird on a wire, ready to be gone in an instant.

The king remained standing. He looked out over his ruined garden, and when he spoke, it was to the garden as much as to them.

"I spent eleven years in a locked room inside my own skull, watching a stranger rule in my name. Do you know what sustained me? Not hope. Hope rots, in the dark. What sustained me was *specificity*. Small, precise observations. The parasite liked honey in its wine and I hated honey, and I held onto that hatred like a rope. The Guild's envoys wore grey gloves and changed them hourly, and I counted the changes. I built a map, Master Nakamura. In the dark, with nothing, I mapped my own prison, boundary by boundary, because mapping was the only act of selfhood left to me." He turned. "And then a courier bled a map of my mind into the world, and a cartographer who hides his own name from every ledger in creation read it, and came for me. You understand what I'm telling you. You saved me with the exact craft you've spent your life pretending doesn't belong to you."

"I didn't pretend it doesn't belong to me," Bram said evenly. "I pretended *I* don't belong to it. There's a difference."

"Is there?" The king's voice gentled. "Then here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I respect you enough not to insult you twice. Full pardon, retroactive and perpetual. A seat at court—Warden of Maps, if you like the title, or nothing at all if you don't. Guards. A house. A name written in the royal rolls, protected by the crown, so that no ledger anywhere can touch you again." He paused. "Protection, Bram. Freely given. All you have to do is stop running."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below the terrace, a thrush was testing a song against the ruins of the wisteria arbor.

"You dismantled the charter," Bram said at last. It wasn't an answer, and everyone present knew it wasn't an answer.

"This morning. The Guild's charter is dissolved; its assets seized; its surviving principals summoned to answer for eleven years of brokering possession and a century of worse before that. The ledgers are being opened. Every name they ever bought or sold or erased." The king studied him. "Including yours. Entered the week you were born, Master Nakamura. You were never invisible. You were only unread. I'd have thought, of all people, you'd want to read what they wrote."

"I read it," Bram said quietly. "That's how I know I don't want your protection."

"Bram—" Sorrel started.

"No. Let me say this properly, because I've been composing it since the vault and I'd like to get it out before I lose my nerve." He stood, and for once he didn't move like a rumor; he moved like a man planting his feet. "Your Majesty. Protection is a debt with the interest hidden. I've spent my whole life watching what happens to people the crown protects—they become instruments, then precedents, then footnotes. I watched it happen to my master. I watched it happen to the Guild's founders, who started as mapmakers trying to keep realms safe and ended as landlords of other people's souls." His jaw tightened. "I will not be kept safe. I've *been* kept safe. It nearly ended with a parasite wearing a king because men like me preferred tidy ledgers to messy truths."

"Then what will you be?" the king asked.

Bram looked at Sorrel. Just for a moment—but it was a long moment, the kind that contains whole conversations, and Sorrel felt her face warm and resolutely did not look away.

"Visible," he said. "On my own terms. I'll take the pardon—refusing a pardon is vanity, and I've had enough vanity to last a lifetime. But no title, no house, no guards. I'll keep my own rooms. I'll walk my own streets. And if the Guild's ghosts come for me, I'll meet them in daylight, named, where anyone who wants to can watch." He almost smiled. "You offered me a cage with the bars on the outside, Your Majesty. I've lived in one with the bars on the inside. Neither suits."

The king regarded him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he knelt—knelt, the King of the Realm, in the dirt of his dead garden—and pressed his palm flat to the soil, the way Sorrel had done, listening with his skin.

"Then I'll ask only one thing," he said. "Not protection. Permission. Permission to plant this garden back the way it was. Crooked rows and all. And permission to ask you both—one gardener and one witch and one cartographer, when the work allows—to come and put your hands in the dirt with me. No debt. No ledger. Just hands, and dirt, and the outrageous presumption of spring."

Sorrel felt something crack open in her chest—not painfully, the way the sigil-carving had cracked her, but the way ice cracks on a river in thaw: with a sound like relief.

"Your Majesty," she said, "you may presume outrageously at me anytime."

All three of them looked at Bram.

Bram looked at the soil. At the crooked ghost-rows only he could probably see, mapped in his head from the stones' whispering. At his own clean hands, which had drawn a map in his own blood to save a stranger, and which had not yet stopped trembling from it.

"One condition," he said finally. "The roses go in crooked. On purpose. I'll survey it myself if anyone tries to straighten them."

The king laughed—really laughed, the first one, Sorrel suspected, in eleven years—and the sound went up through the dead wisteria like a bird released.

* * *

She found Bram at dusk on the terrace steps, when the garden had gone blue and gold and the first honest stars were out. He had a stick in his hand and was drawing in the dirt of the path—absently, unconsciously, the way he did everything: a survey line, a boundary marker, the outline of beds not yet planted.

Sorrel sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not pretend it was an accident.

"My memories came back a little more today," she said. "In the bath, of all places. Tam humming. Just four notes of it, over and over. It's not his voice. But it's *mine*—my memory of it, damaged and mine, and I cried until the water went cold, and then I got out and felt..." She searched for it. "Lighter. Isn't that obscene? Grief that weighs less than it should."

"It's not obscene." Bram didn't stop drawing. "It's accurate. Nothing returns unmarked. That's not damage, Sorrel. That's proof it was carried."

She watched his stick trace the crooked rows, and after a moment she said, quietly: "You know they'll come for you. The Guild's remnants. The ones who slipped the purge. Your name is in every ledger now—readable, findable, *huntable*. You gave up the only armor you ever had."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway." He set down the stick. In the fading light, his face was open in a way she had never seen it—not unguarded, exactly, but *choosing* to be unguarded, which was braver. "Do you know what I figured out, in the king's mind, in the drowned archives? Hiding was never survival. It was just dying slowly somewhere no one could hear the noise." He turned to look at her, and the stars were coming out above the ruined garden, and his eyes were steady. "I'd rather be hunted in daylight than safe in the dark. Especially if the daylight has you in it."

Sorrel Vance, disgraced sigil-witch, sister of a dead courier, keeper of a door carved into her own skin, discovered that she had absolutely nothing clever to say.

So instead she leaned over and kissed him—briefly, imperfectly, getting the angle slightly wrong, nothing at all like the perfect sealed recollections the Order used to promise—and when she pulled back, Bram Nakamura was smiling like a man whose name had just been written somewhere he chose.

Below them, in the dark, the garden waited. And beneath the garden, the old stones began—very softly, very patiently—to whisper of spring.
