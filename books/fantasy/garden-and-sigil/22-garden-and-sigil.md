## 22. Garden and Sigil

The king's offer came in spring, when the fog over the capital had finally learned to lift before noon.

Bram stood in the rebuilt audience chamber—the one whose walls he had once mapped by feel, hunting the wrongness that ate boundaries—and listened to Aldric Vane, King of the Hollow Throne no longer, offer him a title.

"Cartographer Royal," the king said. "With lands, a stipend, a seat at court. The Guild's charter is ash, Bram. Someone has to draw what comes next."

The chamber was full of light now. They had torn out the panels where the parasite's influence had pooled like standing water, and Sorrel had sigiled the new stone herself—clean geometry, honest lines, nothing hidden in the margins. Bram had checked. Twice. Old habits.

He looked at the king—a broad man with a gardener's hands and a face that had aged ten years in the months since his own mind was returned to him—and felt the old reflex rise: *decline, deflect, disappear.* For fifteen years, every gift had been a hook, every honor a ledger entry, every name spoken aloud a debt accruing interest somewhere he couldn't see.

"I'm grateful," Bram said. "But no."

Aldric studied him. The king had gotten good, since his unbinding, at looking at people until they stopped performing. "You'd rather teach children to fold maps in a rented hall off Cooper's Lane?"

"I'd rather teach anyone who walks through the door," Bram said. "Under my own name. On no ledger but mine." He paused. "You of all people know what it is to have your interior claimed by an institution. I spent my whole life making sure mine couldn't be read. Turns out unread isn't the same as free. A title would just be a prettier cage."

"And the school isn't?"

"The school has a door I can lock from the inside," Bram said. "And windows."

The king laughed—a real laugh, rough at the edges, the laugh of a man still relearning what his own voice sounded like without something else shaping it. He extended his hand, and Bram took it, and neither of them pretended the gesture was simple. It was the first time Bram Nakamura had shaken a king's hand as himself, named, witnessed, entered into the record of the realm whether he liked it or not.

"Then I'll commission maps from you like any other customer," Aldric said. "Starting with the palace gardens. I'm told they've gone to ruin."

"They've gone to ruin because you keep pulling up the flowerbeds looking for parasites."

"One learns vigilance," the king said mildly. "I'm trying to learn gardening instead. I hear it's the same discipline, practiced with better faith."

* * *

The school opened on the first of Thawmonth, under a sign Bram painted himself: **NAKAMURA SCHOOL OF CARTOGRAPHY — ALL REALMS CONSIDERED**. Below it, smaller, in letters he almost didn't paint and then did: *No question too strange. No student turned away for want of coin.*

His first student was a fisherman's daughter named Wren who wanted to map the shifting sandbars of the estuary. His second was a former Guild apprentice, a hollow-eyed young man called Pell who arrived with a satchel of stolen instruments and a confession he couldn't finish. Bram hired him as an assistant and never asked him to finish it. His third was a noblewoman's son who only wanted to draw dragons, and Bram taught him projection and scale anyway, because even dragons deserve accurate coastlines.

He taught them everything.

That was the part that still startled him, weeks in—that he simply *told* them. The Tuesday-realm technique, the method of mapping a place that exists only when observed. The trick of drawing a coastline so the coastline becomes slightly more true. The blood-ink warning, delivered plainly on the first day of advanced study: *this craft can be turned inside out; here is how you recognize when it has been; here is the counter-sigil Sorrel Vance will teach you herself if you ask.*

For years those secrets had been his walls. Now they were windows, and people kept walking through them, and the strange thing—the thing he turned over at night like a stone with a warm underside—was that none of them used the knowledge to find him. Being known had not made him owned. The ledgers of the world had closed their books on the Vellum Guild, and it turned out most people had never wanted a ledger at all. They'd wanted a map.

Pell found him one evening after class, coiling surveyor's cord by lamplight.

"You were famous," Pell said. "Before. In the Guild. They talked about you like weather. *Nakamura can map anything, don't give Nakamura your true name.* And now you're—" he gestured at the classroom, the chalk dust, the child's drawing of a dragon with accurate latitude lines pinned to the wall, "—*this*."

"Does it disappoint you?"

"It terrifies me," Pell said honestly. "It also—" He struggled. "My master in the Guild told me knowledge was leverage. That everything I learned was a knife I held over someone or they held over me. You just... gave me the estuary technique. For free. I don't know where to put it."

"In your hands," Bram said. "That's where tools go."

* * *

Sorrel planted the garden in high summer, over the vault.

The Guild's deepest archive had been unsealed by royal decree, its ledger-vaults emptied into public record, its stones left standing like a pulled tooth's socket in the hillside below the old countinghouse. Most people avoided the place. The grass grew wrong there, they said. The shadows leaned.

Sorrel went with a spade, three sacks of loam, and a basket of seeds she'd traded half her restored memories' worth of patience to collect—marigold, borage, feverfew, and a climbing rose called *Remember Me* that the seed-wife swore bloomed black at first and red only after a full year.

Bram came to help, which meant he stood holding the sacks while she worked, because Sorrel Vance did not let anyone else touch her soil.

She dug with the focus he'd seen her bring to sigil-work, carving the earth instead of her skin this time, turning the vault's cold capstone into the base of a raised bed. She hummed while she worked—old order hymns, mostly, and once, unexpectedly, a lullaby she didn't seem to recognize singing. Her brother's, maybe. Something that had come back to her in fragments, imperfect but hers.

"You know what's down there still," Bram said. "Under the flagstone. The original ledger burned, but the binding-stones don't burn. The parasite's name is cut into them."

"I know."

"It could be recovered. Someone could try to use it."

"Someone could try to do a great many things," Sorrel said, patting soil around a marigold start with more force than strictly necessary. "I could spend my life guarding a hole, or I could grow things on top of it. Names lose their teeth when you plant roses over them. Ask any witch." She looked up at him, dirt on her cheek, her carved sigil-scars pale in the sun—she'd stopped hiding them, too, wearing short sleeves for the first time since he'd known her. "Besides. I remember my brother's face now. Not perfectly. The scar over his eyebrow is missing, and I think his eyes were browner than this. But it's *mine*, Bram. Imperfect and mine. That's worth more than a perfect memory I bought back by abandoning you." She pressed a seed into the earth. "We remake our inheritances or we pass them on broken. I choose remaking."

They finished at dusk, palms blistered, the bed raw and hopeful above the sealed dark. Sorrel watered it from a rain barrel, and Bram—feeling slightly foolish and doing it anyway—drew a small map of the garden in his notebook, marking each seedling's position, dating it, titling it in his own unmistakable hand: *Garden over the Vault of Names, first planting, by Bram Nakamura, who was present.*

Signed. Witnessed. Real.

"What's it feel like?" Sorrel asked, watching him write. "Being legible."

Bram considered. "Like setting down a weight I forgot I was carrying. Like the difference between hiding in a house and living in one." He closed the notebook. "Some nights I still reach for the old tricks. The urge to be no one. But then Wren shows up at dawn asking questions about tidal flats, and I'm someone specific enough to answer her, and—" he shrugged, "—it turns out being found isn't the same as being caught."

Sorrel smiled and threw a handful of borage seed at his head.

* * *

Autumn came. The garden's first blooms opened—black-petaled roses, exactly as promised, ugly and gorgeous against the marigolds. The king visited incognito, badly disguised, and weeded for an hour, and said afterward that it was the best afternoon he'd had since coronation. Wren mapped the estuary so well that the harbor authority paid her, and she brought the coin to school and nailed it to the wall rather than pay tuition, out of principle, and Bram let her.

On the last warm evening of the year, Bram walked home past the garden and saw a figure standing at the gate.

Small. Hooded. Waiting.

The figure turned, and in the dying light Bram saw a face he knew from a map drawn in living blood—from the margins, from the moving sigil, from a courier's final breath. Not the parasite. Never the parasite; that name slept under roses now, honestly bounded in a realm of its own, per the trade. This was older. This was the face from the drowned archive, from the mind-country's farthest shore, from the first blood-map's signature line.

His old master's face.

"You're dead," Bram said. "I watched the archive take you."

"Did you?" the figure said, and its voice was kind, and terrible, and familiar as his own handwriting. "Come walk with me, Bram. There's a realm on the edge of the map that neither of us has ever surveyed. And I think—" the smile widened, patient as tide, "—you should see what's been growing there."

Bram's hand went to his notebook, to his pen, to the name stitched openly on the sign behind him.

He followed.
