## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The border of the valley announced itself the way a held breath announces itself — by its absence.

Ivo Eastwick felt it first in her teeth. For eleven years she had lived inside her master's magic the way a fish lives inside water: unnoticed, ambient, inescapable. Aldous Wicker's power soaked into the cottage beams and the well water and the very bread they ate, so that even on the days he did not touch her — the days he only *looked* at her, measuring, weighing, the way a merchant looks at stock — she could feel him in everything, a low hum beneath the world. Now, three days' hard walking from the cottage, that hum thinned, frayed, and stopped.

She stood at the mouth of the valley with her pack straps cutting her shoulders and listened to the silence where magic should have been.

It was not an empty silence. It was the silence of a room after a loud argument ends — full of everything that had been said before it began. She could feel the shape of what was missing, the way her tongue found the gap of a lost tooth. And into that gap poured something she had not felt since childhood, something she had almost forgotten the name of.

Freedom tasted like cold water. Like the first bite of bread when you didn't know you were hungry.

Ivo laughed — one short, startled bark of sound that the valley walls caught and returned to her, hers alone, belonging to no master's design. Then she laughed again, properly this time, until her ribs ached, until she had to sit down on a warm stone with her hands over her face because eleven years of held breath were leaving her all at once and it hurt going out.

*He can't follow me here.* The thought arrived slowly, disbelievingly, like dawn through shutters. Whatever else the valley was — and the shepherds' stories said it was cursed, godless, a place where prayers went unanswered and hedge-charms curdled in the casting — it was *hers*. No scrying could reach into it. No binding laid outside its borders would hold within them. She had run toward the one place in the world where Aldous Wicker's long arm went numb at the shoulder.

She walked down into it as the sun climbed, and the valley received her without ceremony. It was beautiful in a blunt, unmagical way: grey-green slopes stitched with sheep tracks, a river running shallow and quick over slate, hawthorn hedges gone feral along the old drovers' road. Everything here grew crooked and stubborn and alive. Nothing glowed. Nothing whispered. A kestrel hung over the far slope doing nothing but being a kestrel, and Ivo loved it for that.

By evening she had found a shepherd's bothy, roof half-collapsed, smelling of old peat smoke and long-departed sheep. She built a fire without witchcraft — flint and steel and patience, the way ordinary people did — and ate hard cheese and an apple, and lay back against her pack, and thought: *this is the rest of my life now. Small and quiet and mine.*

That was when the stranger came down from the pass.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him — a scrabbling, uneven sound on the scree above the bothy, like a man walking on legs that had forgotten how. Ivo was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up to her body; eleven years under Aldous Wicker taught you that sounds in the night were never innocent.

He came around the boulder at the edge of the firelight and she saw at once that he was dying.

He was perhaps forty, lean, dressed for travel in good wool gone dark with blood — his own, she understood, though there was too much of it and it came from everywhere. His face was grey as the slate in the riverbed. One hand pressed against his side; the other reached for her, or for the firelight behind her, or for nothing at all.

"Please," he said, and his voice cracked like dry kindling. "Please — are you — is anyone—"

"No one," Ivo said stupidly. "There's no one here but me."

Something moved across his face that might have been relief. He took two more steps and his legs simply resigned their office, and Ivo dropped her knife and caught him — or mostly caught him, enough that his head did not strike stone — and lowered him down beside the fire with her arms shaking under his weight.

"You're hurt," she said, and wanted to bite the words off her tongue. Eleven years of herb-lore and charm-work, and all of it useless here, in the valley where her craft died at the border. She pressed her hands against the worst of the bleeding anyway, out of pure animal refusal to let a man die under her hands, and felt nothing answer her. No warmth gathering in her palms. No hum. Just blood, warm and human and unstoppable.

He laughed — a wet, terrible sound. "You're a witch," he said. "I can smell it on you. Bad luck. Bad luck for both of us." He coughed, and the cough went on too long. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't matter here. That's why I came this way."

"Why?" Ivo asked. "Who did this to you?"

"Inquisitors." He said the word flatly, the way a man says *weather*. "Three days behind me. Maybe four. They don't cross into the valley either — they're not fools. But they'll wait at the pass. They're patient." His eyes found hers, and they were clear, horribly clear, the eyes of a man spending his last coin carefully. "Listen to me. There isn't time for the whole of it, so I'll give you the spine of it and trust you to grow the rest."

"I'm not—" Ivo started. "I can't help you. I don't even know your name."

"Tobin," he said. "Tobin Vael, courier, late of Merrowgate." He fumbled at his breast with the hand that wasn't holding himself together, and Ivo helped him, hating herself for helping, and drew out an oilskin packet stained nearly black. Inside was a folded square of heavy paper. He pressed it into her hands and closed her fingers over it with both of his, and his grip was still shockingly strong.

"This goes to my sister," Tobin Vael said. "Or it goes to someone who will finish what I couldn't. You're the only someone there is."

"I'm nobody," Ivo said. "I'm a runaway apprentice. I have nothing. I want nothing."

"Nobody," he repeated, and smiled with bloody teeth. "Yes. That's exactly what it needs. Listen." He pulled her closer, and his breath was iron and salt. "Twenty years ago, the free cities made a bargain. A foul one. They paid the court under the mountain in the only coin it wants. Souls, girl. Living souls, signed away by deed, taken whole. My sister among them. Sarelle. She was nineteen." His voice broke on the number and he mastered it. "They bought peace with her. Everyone eats well off that peace. Everyone agrees not to remember the price."

"The mountain court is a story," Ivo said. "A tale to frighten children."

"So was I, this morning." He laughed again, and this time it turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened, there was blood on his lips and less of him behind his eyes than there had been a minute ago. "The map knows the way. It will show you more than roads if you let it. Don't let it use you — do you hear me? Whatever you are, whatever your master sold—" He stopped. Frowned. Something like wonder crossed his ruined face. "Ah," he breathed. "Oh, that's why. That's why it had to be you."

"What had to be me? Tobin — *what* had to be me?"

But he was looking past her now, at the fire, or through it, and his hand had gone slack in hers. She shook him. She shouted at him. She pressed her ear to his chest and heard the slow, irregular drumming of a heart deciding whether to bother, and then, some minutes later, heard it decide.

The valley kept its silence around them. Even the wind seemed to hold off, courteous as a mourner.

Ivo sat beside the dead man for a long time, her hands sticky with his blood, the folded map lying in her lap like something sleeping.

* * *

She buried him at dawn, up the slope where the ground was soft under the hawthorns, because the dead deserved better than the bothy floor and because she could not bear to look at his face any longer than she had to. She dug with the fire-iron and a flat stone, and it took hours, and she wept partway through without quite knowing why — not for Tobin Vael, whom she had known for a quarter of an hour, but for something larger and less nameable. For the fact that a man could die alone in a silent valley asking a stranger for help, and the stranger could give him nothing but a hole in the ground.

She marked the cairn with his knife, driven into the turf. *T.V.*, scratched into the handle. It was little. It was what there was.

Then she picked up her pack, and told herself, clearly and firmly, the way you talk to a child or a fool: *Walk away. Walk west, or south, anywhere but east. Find a town. Find work. Start the small life. The map is not yours. The dying wish of a stranger is not a debt. You owe him a grave and you've paid it.*

She walked west.

By midday she noticed she was hungry in a way food didn't fix, and by late afternoon she realized the map was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm from inside, like a bird's egg, like a sleeping animal, and it sat against her back through the oilskin and the canvas and the spare shirt wrapped around it, and it warmed her spine between her shoulder blades the way a hand might rest there — patient, familiar, *expectant*.

She stopped on the drovers' road and took it out and held it at arm's length, meaning to throw it into the river.

The paper sat in her palm, folded small, ordinary-looking, ink-brown, corner-worn. Warm. She could feel a faint pulse in it, or imagined she could — no, not imagined; the valley was empty of magic, that was the whole point of the valley, and yet here was this thing beating gently against her skin like a second heart that had decided to move in without asking.

"You're not mine," she told it.

The warmth deepened, very slightly, the way a dog leans into a hand.

Ivo stood on the road a long while. West lay towns and work and the small life, and somewhere behind her, eventually, the long arm of Aldous Wicker feeling about for her like a blind man in a dark house. East lay a mountain out of children's tales and a dead man's sister and inquisitors waiting at the pass, patient as weather.

She looked west. She looked east.

The map warmed against her palm, and Ivo Eastwick — runaway, nobody, free for four entire days — sighed the sigh of the condemned, turned her face into the evening wind, and started walking east.

Behind her, the cairn under the hawthorns kept its silence. Ahead, the road ran down out of the valley toward country she had never seen, and the thing in her pack beat softly, patiently, like a heart waiting to be given back.
