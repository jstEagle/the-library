## 2. What the Map Wants

The valley let her go on the third morning, and Ivo discovered she had no idea what to do with that.

For eleven years, leaving had been impossible. Mistress Harewell's wards had ringed the cottage like a fence of thorns made of thought — step past the well, past the leaning gate, past the crooked rowan, and something in your chest would simply turn you around, gentle as a hand on your shoulder, until you'd walked yourself home without ever deciding to. Ivo had spent a decade telling herself she stayed because she chose to. It was easier than admitting she'd never once tested it.

Now she stood at the valley mouth where the river went thin and grey over stones, and behind her the hills folded shut like hands closing over something precious, and ahead of her lay country she had only ever seen in Harewell's stolen books. No pull in her chest. No turning. Just the wind coming down off the pass, smelling of pine and distance.

She laughed out loud, once, and it startled a heron up from the shallows.

She might have gone anywhere. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it. Eleven years dreaming of this exact moment, and when it came she had no destination at all — except the one pressed into her hands by a dying man whose blood had soaked through his coat and into her sleeve before she even understood he was falling.

The map sat against her ribs where she'd tucked it inside her shirt, folded into eighths, warm as a loaf pulled from an oven.

It had been warm since the stranger died. She kept telling herself it was her own body heat. She kept not quite believing it.

* * *

She walked east because east was where the pass went, and told herself that was the whole reason. The road unwound through high meadows going gold at the edges of summer, then down into pinewood shadow, then along a river that widened as it went, gathering tributaries the way a rumor gathers detail. Three days. Four. She slept under hedges and in the mouths of culverts and once, extravagantly, in the hayloft of a barn whose owner never knew she was there. She ate berries she identified from a book she'd memorized at fourteen while pretending to dust the shelf it lived on. Her feet blistered and hardened. Her shoulders, used to stooping over mortar and pestle, straightened by degrees.

And every morning the map was warmer than the night before.

On the fifth day she took it out at noon and studied it properly for the first time, sitting on a flat stone above the water with her boots off. It was drawn on something that wasn't quite paper and wasn't quite skin — a pale membrane, faintly translucent, that caught the light wrong. The ink was brown-black and fine as hair. It showed mountains she didn't recognize, a river system that matched none of the maps in Harewell's library, and at its eastern edge, rendered in strokes so small they were almost suggestion, a mountain shaped like a closed fist with a crown of peaks above it.

Beneath the fist-mountain, in handwriting cramped and urgent, someone had written: *the stair the miners sealed. ask for Tam Vess at Coldharbour. tell him Corvin kept faith.*

Corvin. That was the dead man's name; she'd found it stitched inside his collar after she'd buried him. She'd said it aloud over the grave because it seemed wrong to put a man in the ground nameless, and the word had felt strange in her mouth, like borrowing a coat that fit too well.

*Corvin kept faith.* Kept faith with whom? With Tam Vess, whoever that was. With Sarelle, the name he'd whispered last, the name that had come out of him like the last coin from a emptied purse. With her? She hadn't known him four minutes. He'd come stumbling down the pass with an arrow-slit in his side and a look on his face like a man who has run out of road, and he'd seen her — a hedge-witch's runaway in patched men's clothes, mud to the knee — and he'd chosen her. Pressed the map into her hands with both of his, folding her fingers over it like a priest giving communion.

*Take it east,* he'd said. *Don't read it where there's light.* And then, softer, almost gone: *Sarelle. Tell her—*

He hadn't finished. She'd knelt there in the scree holding a stranger's hand while the life went out of it by inches, and the worst part — the part she couldn't stop chewing at, nights, staring up at unfamiliar stars — was that some cold practical corner of her mind had already been thinking: *I don't know this man. This isn't my trouble. I could walk away right now and no one would ever know.*

She hadn't walked away. She'd dug his grave with a flat stone and her bare hands, which was how hedge-witches buried people when there was no spade, and she'd laid him in it facing east, and she'd hated herself a little for noticing how much lighter her pack was without him in it.

Now she held the map up to the sun — deliberately, spitefully, because it had asked her not to — and the membrane went transparent as horn, and beneath the ink she saw a second layer of lines, finer, older, a different hand entirely. Roads within roads. A design beneath the design.

She dropped it like it had bitten her.

That night, at a crossroads inn called the Waymeet, she fed its fire until it roared, waited until the common room emptied, and fed the map to the flames.

It burned beautifully. That was the terrible thing — it burned like any paper should, curling, browning, collapsing into black lace, and she watched it with a satisfaction so fierce it frightened her, because burning it meant choosing. Meant the dead man's last request could go hang, meant Sarelle could stay a stranger's unfinished sentence forever, meant Ivo Eastwick was finally, finally free to be nobody's instrument.

She went to bed feeling lighter than she had since the valley mouth.

In the morning the map was in her pack.

Not ashes. Not fragments. Folded into eighths, uncreased, unscorched, lying on top of her spare shift as if it had always lived there, warm as fresh bread. She stood in the loft's dusty light holding it at arm's length for a long time, her heart going hard and slow, and understood two things with perfect clarity: first, that she could not get rid of this thing by any means she possessed; second, that it did not want to be destroyed. It wanted to be carried. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

"Fine," she said aloud, to the map, to the dead man, to whatever was listening. "East, then. But I'm deciding things as we go. You hear me?"

The map, being a map, said nothing. But it warmed against her palms like something pleased.

* * *

The man caught up with her on the ninth day, at a ford where the river spread wide and shallow and loud.

She heard him before she saw him — or rather she heard the absence of him, the way birdsong stopped along the alder bank in a moving line, and she came up out of her crouch with a handful of ready nothing, because her magic was still a hundred miles behind her in the valley and all she had here were her eyes and her feet.

He came down through the trees leading a tired grey horse, and he was wrong in a way that took her a moment to parse. Big man, broad, maybe fifty, with the kind of frame that had once carried armor easily and now carried drink instead. Grey stubble, grey-streaked hair cut short in a soldier's fashion long grown out of fashion. A sword at his hip that was far too good for the rest of him — plain guard, honest wear on the grip, but the balance of it spoke money and care somewhere in its history. His eyes were the problem. They were red-rimmed and unfocused until they fixed on her, and then they focused all at once, horribly, like a lantern turned up.

"You," he said. His voice was gravel in a bucket. "You're the one from the pass."

"I'm nobody from anywhere."

"Corvin." He said the name like setting down a weight. "You buried him. I've been following your grave-dirt for three days." He swung down from the horse, badly, catching himself on the saddle, and she saw now that he swayed even standing still, that his hands weren't steady, that underneath the soldier's carriage he was holding himself together with habit and spite. "The map. Where is it."

"Who's asking?"

"Wren." He said it flatly, without pride, and something in the way he said it told her it used to mean more. "Ser Wren Aldous. Once." He looked at her the way a man looks at a lock he intends to open. "You've got it on you. Under your shirt. You keep touching your ribs like they hurt."

Her hand dropped from her side, too late. "You knew him. Corvin."

"I knew him." Something moved behind the drunk-haze, quick and wounded. "Twenty years in his service. His family served mine before that, if you can believe anyone served my family at anything." He took a step closer and the horse shifted nervously behind him. "He rode west six weeks ago with a sealed commission and came back to me in a dream, dead, so either I've finally gone mad or someone on this road is carrying what he died to deliver. Give it here."

"No."

The word came out of her clean and flat, and she was almost proud of it. Wren blinked, as if refusal were a language he'd forgotten.

"Girl, that map is court property. Sealed under the privy signet. Carrying it is treason, and I am — I was — an officer of that court, and it is my duty—"

"Your duty." She heard her own voice climb and didn't rein it. "Your duty got him an arrow in the back on a mountain pass, did it? Your duty left him crawling downhill toward a valley where magic can't follow, looking for — what, looking for *me*, a runaway with dirt under her nails, because everyone else in your beautiful court wanted him dead?" She was shaking, and realized distantly it wasn't fear. "He put it in my hands. Mine. Whatever he was carrying, he chose who'd carry it next, and it wasn't a grey old drunk who can't mount his own horse."

Wren went very still. The drunkenness drained out of his posture without leaving his face, the way water leaves sand — and what remained underneath was worse: a soldier assessing, measuring, remembering how to be dangerous.

"That seal," he said quietly. "Describe it."

"Why?"

"Because I've seen it twice in my life. Once on the commission that made me a knight, and once on the order that broke me. If it's what I think it is, then everything I did to earn my disgrace was for nothing, and I need to know that today, not tomorrow." His voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "Please."

The *please* undid something. Not enough to hand anything over. Enough to answer.

"It's a tower," she said. "A tower with a door at the bottom instead of the top. And a star over it with seven points, and the seventh point is broken off."

Wren closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like a drunk and more like a man standing in a house fire, watching twenty years burn.

"The Seal of the Inner Door," he said. "Nobody's used that since the old king died. Nobody *should* have it." He opened his eyes and looked at her, and there was something new in his face now, something that had replaced the demand and frightened her more: certainty. "You're going east. To the mountain."

"I'm going east," she allowed. "Where I go after that is mine."

"The mountain will kill you. Or worse — the mountain will *keep* you. I've seen what comes out of that court. I helped seal the miners' stair myself, ten years ago, and I've drunk every night since trying to forget why." He hauled himself back onto the horse, movements stiff, dignity assembled out of sheer stubbornness. "So here's how this goes, girl-with-no-name. You won't give me the map, and I'm too old and too honest to take it off you, though God knows I've done worse. So I'll do the only thing left. I'll follow you. Every mile. All the way to the fist-mountain if that's where you're walking. And when you get in trouble — and you will, because you walk like someone who's never been hunted — I'll be there, and you can decide then whether you trust me."

"And why would you do that?"

He gathered the reins. When he answered, he was already turning the horse, and she had to strain to hear him over the ford.

"Because Corvin kept faith with me for twenty years, and I repaid it with obedience. I owe him one act of my own choosing." He glanced back, red-rimmed eyes level. "Keep walking, witch. I'll be behind you. Try not to set anything on fire."

He rode north along the bank, unhurried, a grey man on a grey horse, and Ivo stood in the shallows with the map warming against her heart and the cold river pulling at her knees, and understood that she had just acquired something she had never had before and never wanted:

Someone who knew where she was going.

She wrung out her skirts, climbed the far bank, and walked east — faster now, though she told herself it was only the weather turning.
