## 3. Inquisitors on the Road

Wren had known the inquisitors were coming before he saw them. He knew it the way old soldiers know weather — in the ache of an old wound, in the way birds go quiet, in the smell of smoke that isn't quite a hearthfire.

He smelled it first at dawn, three days out from the pass, when he crested a hill above the border town of Harrow's Ford and saw the market square full of people standing very still around a bonfire that was burning far too hot for autumn.

They were burning records. Ledgers, mostly — Wren could tell by the way the pages curled and flared, by the black snowfall of ash drifting over the rooftops. The town's registry of births and debts and deaths, all of it going into the fire while a man in grey stood beside the pyre with a book of his own, checking names off against some list only he could see.

Wren crouched in the bracken at the hilltop and felt something old and sour rise in his throat.

He knew that man's posture. He knew the particular stillness of an inquisitor at work — the patience of them, the terrible economy of motion, as though wasting a gesture would be a kind of sin. Twenty years ago Wren had stood in squares like this one himself, in his grey-and-silver, with his warrant and his clean boots, while clerks fed their own ledgers into flames and townspeople watched with the careful blankness of people hoping not to be noticed.

*We are not punishing you,* he remembered saying, once, to a woman whose husband had fled the mountain court with a stolen deed. *We are simply correcting the record.*

The woman had wept anyway. Records, corrected, had a way of ending in gallows.

Wren spat into the grass and turned away from the square. The courier — Aldous, the dead man whose seal now rode folded in Ivo Eastwick's pack — had passed through Harrow's Ford eleven days ago. Which meant the inquisitors were eleven days behind a dead man and closing on a living girl who had no idea what she was carrying.

He found her an hour later at a roadside shrine, arguing with it.

"—and I'm not asking your opinion," she was saying to a weathered statue of some local harvest saint, "I'm saying if you have any influence over the weather, a little rain would be appreciated, because my boots are falling apart and— oh, it's *you*."

She said it the way other people said *rats*. Wren had been following her for six days now, keeping to her blind side, and she still greeted him like an infestation.

"Girl." He stopped at the edge of the shrine's little circle of trampled grass. "There are inquisitors in Harrow's Ford."

"I know. I bought bread there yesterday." She tore a hunk of it with her teeth and chewed at him belligerently. "Grey coats, silver buttons, very polite, asked everyone if they'd seen a tall man with a limp. I said no. Everyone said no. They wrote down everyone's name anyway."

Wren went cold. "You spoke to them?"

"I *bought bread*, knight. It's not a crime yet."

"It will be." He crossed the circle and took her arm — she twisted free instantly, quick as a ferret, glaring — and lowered his voice as though the shrine itself might report him. "Listen to me. They don't ask questions to get answers. The questions are a net. Every person they speak to goes on a list, and every list gets compared, and anyone whose answer differs from their neighbor's becomes interesting. You told them no. What did the baker say?"

Ivo paused, bread halfway to her mouth. "That he'd seen him. That the tall man limped through two days before and paid with a mountain coin."

"Then you're already marked. You're the one person in Harrow's Ford who didn't see a man everyone else saw." He was already walking, fast, back toward the road east. "They'll come back for you within the day. Possibly tonight."

Behind him, silence. Then her footsteps, hurrying, catching up. "Marked for *what?* I'm a hedge-witch's runaway with holes in her stockings. I don't even have magic anymore — the valley took it—"

"The valley doesn't take anything." He said it without thinking, and immediately wished he hadn't, because she stopped dead in the road.

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. Walk."

"Wren." Her voice changed — dropped its belligerence and went sharp and narrow, the voice of someone who had spent years learning exactly which questions mattered. "You just said the valley doesn't take anything. How would you know what the valley does?"

He kept walking. "Because I helped seal the miners' stair beneath the mountain, twenty years ago, and I've read every report ever written about the borderlands. Because I served the court you're so eager to rob. Because I know what inquisitors do to marked girls, and it starts with questions and ends somewhere you don't want to imagine. *Walk.*"

To his surprise, she walked. But he could feel her thinking behind him, feel the question being filed away in whatever ledger she kept behind those grey eyes, and he understood that he had just handed her a thread, and that she would pull it until something unraveled.

* * *

They made twelve miles that day, off the high road, along drover's paths that Wren half-remembered from a campaign he'd spent thirty years trying to forget. Ivo asked nothing further about the valley. She asked instead about inquisitors — how many traveled together (four, usually, plus a clerk), whether they rode or walked (rode, but dismounted at checkpoints, because a mounted man is a threat and a walking man is a neighbor), what they did with the lists (burned them, eventually, after the corrections were made).

"You talk like a man describing his own hands," she observed, as dusk came down blue and cold over the hills.

"I told you. I served."

"And you left."

"They asked me to do something I couldn't." He built the fire small, screened, the way he'd been taught — another lesson from the grey coats — and stared into it. "So I left. And then I drank for ten years, and then Aldous found me face-down in a gutter in Merrowgate and carried me home like a brother, and sobered me up out of sheer stubbornness, and never once asked me what I'd done that I couldn't do again. That's the whole story. Are you satisfied?"

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge entirely.

"He sounds like he was worth dying for."

Wren looked up. She wasn't mocking him. She was watching him across the fire with an expression he couldn't name — something almost gentle, quickly hidden behind another bite of bread.

"He was," Wren said. "And the men hunting you killed him. So when I tell you to walk faster, girl, understand that I am not nagging. I am burying a second friend in advance and I find I lack the practice."

Something flickered across her face — guilt, he thought, the same guilt he'd been smelling on her since the pass, since she'd knelt by a dying stranger with blood on her hands and a stranger's map in her pack. She thought she owed the dead man a debt. She was right. But she thought the debt was hers alone, and that was where she was wrong, and Wren found he hadn't the heart to explain it yet.

* * *

The checkpoint caught them two days later, at the stone bridge over the River Liss, where the drover's path rejoined the high road and there was no way around for forty miles either direction.

Wren saw it from the ridge above: four grey coats, a clerk with a portable desk, a queue of farmers and carters shuffling forward one at a time to be questioned and listed. Standard disposition. Standard method. He had stood at a hundred bridges like this one, and the memory of it sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"We go around," Ivo said.

"There's no around. The Liss cuts through gorge for two days' march, and the gorge path drowned when they built the mill weir." He chewed his lip. "We could wait for dark—"

"They'll have pickets on both banks after dark. I would have." He closed his eyes briefly. *I would have.* "No. We walk through. I'll do the talking. You keep your mouth shut and look stupid."

"I *am* stupid," Ivo said promptly. "I'm a simple herb-girl traveling with her uncle to a wool fair. Uncle. Say yes, uncle."

"...Yes, uncle."

"Don't enjoy it."

They joined the queue. Wren pulled his cloak up around his ruined face and affected the stoop of a man grown old under burdens, and Ivo — gods, the girl was good — slouched and gaped and scratched, transforming herself in a dozen small ways into someone too dull to remember. The queue advanced. The clerk's pen scratched. A farmer ahead of them was waved through; a tinker was asked to open his packs.

Then the inquisitor looked up, and looked directly at Ivo, and smiled.

"Young woman." His voice was mild, courteous, awful. "Have we met? At Harrow's Ford, perhaps, three days ago? There was a girl at the baker's stall who answered a question strangely."

Wren felt the world tilt. *Eleven days,* he'd thought. *Closing.* No — they'd been *ahead*. They'd been waiting, working backward down the courier's route, and Harrow's Ford was behind them, and—

Ivo blinked at the inquisitor with magnificent vacancy. "Don't know Harrow's Ford, sir. We come from up west. Wool fair at Merrowgate." She nudged Wren. "Tell him, uncle."

"Yes," Wren said. "Wool."

It was, objectively, the worst lie he had ever told. One word. He could hear it hanging in the air, thin and ridiculous, and he saw the inquisitor's smile deepen by a single degree, the way a cat's does when the mouse stops running.

"Wool," the inquisitor repeated. "And your business at the fair?"

"Selling," Wren said. "Wool."

A pause. Somewhere in the queue behind them, a horse stamped. The clerk's pen had gone still.

"Uncle," the inquisitor said pleasantly, "you will forgive me, but I find myself curious about your niece's name. For our records. All travelers are recorded, you understand. It is not punishment. We are simply correcting the record."

And Wren — drunk for ten years, disgraced for twenty, a man who had spent half his life obeying precisely this voice and hated himself for how well he still knew its music — heard himself say:

"She hasn't got one."

The inquisitor's eyebrows rose. "Everyone has a name."

"Not her. Valley-born." Wren let his stoop deepen, let his voice crack with rustic grievance. "Valley folk give their names to the church at birth, sir, everyone knows that, it's why they can't do magic. She's been 'girl' her whole life. Ask anyone down the vale."

It was nonsense. It was worse than nonsense — it was a folk-tale stitched to a slander, and any inquisitor worth his grey coat would know it inside a minute. But the inquisitor hesitated, because the valley *was* strange, because rumors *were* useful, because a lie with a hook of truth in it is harder to dismiss than a lie without — and in that hesitation, Ivo sneezed.

Enormously. Catastrophically. Directly onto the clerk's ledger.

What happened next happened very fast. The clerk shrieked about his records; the inquisitor stepped back with distaste; a farmer's cart chose that exact moment to lurch forward and spill turnips across the bridge; and Wren seized his "niece" by the elbow, muttered "*sorry, sorry, sickly girl, sorry*" and propelled them both through the checkpoint in the confusion, walking, just walking, hearts hammering, until the bend of the road took the bridge from view.

Then they ran.

They ran through the turnip-strewn chaos, off the high road, down into the willow scrub along the Liss, crashing through reeds with the shouts starting up behind them — *stop, in the king-under-the-mountain's name, STOP* — and Wren ran the way he hadn't run in twenty years, lungs burning, old wounds singing, dragging Ivo by the wrist through water and mud until the shouts faded and the willows closed overhead and they collapsed, gasping, in a hollow banked with dry leaves.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The river murmured. Somewhere distant, a horn sounded twice and gave up.

"They'll send riders along the banks," Wren managed at last.

"Then we cross." Ivo was on her elbows, staring back the way they'd come, and her face — gods, her face. Not fear. Something older and colder than fear. "They had my description, Wren. From Harrow's Ford. They described me *to me* — grey eyes, brown hair, answers strangely — and I stood there and listened to a man read me off a list like I was a debt to be collected." She looked at her hands. "I ran away from my master to be free. This is what freedom is? Being a line in someone's ledger?"

"That's what it always was," Wren said quietly. "For most people. Most people live their whole lives as lines in ledgers and call it peace."

"Then it's a rotten peace." She got to her feet, dripping, furious, alive. "Whoever Aldous was, whoever Sarelle is — they did this to me. Put me on that list. Made me *interesting*." She kicked a stone into the river with unnecessary violence. "Fine. Fine! If I'm marked anyway, then I'm marked for something *worth* marking. Show me the map's second layer. Tonight. I want to know what we're walking into."

Wren looked up at her from the leaves — this sharp, angry, terrified girl who had lied to an inquisitor with a sneeze and was now demanding to be taught the shape of her own destruction — and felt, unexpectedly, the ghost of an old feeling. The one he'd had at nineteen, taking his oaths. Purpose, wearing duty's clothes.

"Aldous wanted you to go east," he said. "East it is. But we move at night, we sleep in ditches, and from this morning forward, girl, you are the least memorable person in the free cities."

"I was born in a valley that erases people," Ivo said grimly. "Forgettable is the one thing I know how to be."

They crossed the Liss at dusk, two shadows among shadows, and behind them the inquisitors' fires burned on the far bank like a row of patient grey eyes. Ahead, three days' ride away, Merrowgate glittered on its hills — the first of the free cities, where the council was even now debating what to do with the past.

Wren did not yet know that the council's answer would be to burn it.

But the map did. And against Ivo's ribs, through wool and linen and sweat, it had begun — very faintly, very patiently — to warm.
