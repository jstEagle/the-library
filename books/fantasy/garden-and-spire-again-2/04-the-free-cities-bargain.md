## 4. The Free Cities' Bargain

Merrowgate announced itself before they saw it: a smell of brine and tar and hot bread rolling down the coast road like a welcome that had been rehearsed too many times. Then the walls, white as gull-bone, then the harbor beyond them — a hundred masts stitched against a grey sky, cranes swinging their slow patient arcs, everything moving, everything bought and sold.

Ivo had never seen a city. She had seen the market at Harrow's Cross twice a year, four streets of mud and shouting, and she had thought that was what people meant by *crowd*. Merrowgate made her understand she had been looking at puddles and calling them the sea.

"Keep your hood up," Wren said. He had shaved on the road, badly, with a knife he clearly missed using for better purposes. Cleaned up, he might once have been handsome; now his face was a map of old weather, grey coming in at the temples like frost finding a window. "The council quarter is east of the customs house. If anyone asks, you're my sister."

"I don't look like you."

"You look like trouble. So do I. It'll pass for family."

She should have hated him still. Three days ago he'd held a knife to her throat in an alder grove and demanded the map, and only the map's sudden heat against her breastbone — hot enough that she'd gasped, hot enough that he'd felt it through her coat when he grabbed her — had stopped him from simply taking it. Since then they had walked together in the wary way of two animals drinking from the same stream, each pretending not to watch the other.

But watching him now, as the gates swallowed them into noise, she saw something she hadn't expected: fear. Not of the city. Of it. Wren looked at Merrowgate the way she looked at her master's cottage in her worst memories — the way you look at a place that was home right up until it wasn't.

*He served here,* she remembered. *Before. Before whatever disgraced him.*

"Why are we going to the council?" she asked. "You said yourself they're useless."

"They're worse than useless." He said it quietly, under the clang of a smithy door. "They're respectable. There's a difference, and it matters." He turned down a street where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. "The courier — Sarelle's brother, Aldous — carried a council seal. Whatever he found, whatever he died carrying, the council either sent him or wants very much to pretend they didn't. Either way, we'll learn which faster inside their walls than out."

"And if they decide we're the problem?"

Wren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Then you'll finally have a reason to use whatever it is your master beat into you, hedge-witch, and I'll finally get to see it."

She didn't answer. She was tired of the question everyone asked eventually — *what can you do?* — because the honest answer was *nothing, nothing, nothing*, and the valley had proven it. She had run into the one place in the world where magic couldn't follow her, and instead of freedom she had found a dying man and a map that burned like a coal against her skin whenever she thought about turning back.

It was burning now.

* * *

The council hall of Merrowgate had been built by men who wanted history to think well of them. Its dome was painted with the nine free cities as nine maidens holding up a sky of gold leaf; its floor was marble quarried, Ivo would later learn, by mountain clans paid in promises. Everything about it said *we are permanent*. Nothing about it said *we are afraid* — but Ivo could smell the fear anyway, sour under the incense, the same way she could smell rain coming. Fear had a texture. This room was upholstered in it.

They got in the way Wren said poor people always got in: by carrying something. A porter's tabard borrowed from a laundress who owed Wren a debt older than Ivo's life, a crate of ledgers between them, heads down, walking like men with somewhere dull to be. The ledgers were real. That was the trick, Wren murmured. Never carry anything worth stopping for.

They set the crate down in a records alcove off the main chamber, and through the archway Ivo watched the Council of Nine argue about the end of the world.

"—the mountain court has moved no army in twenty years," a fat man in silver-grey was saying, his voice oiled and reasonable. "No demand has been made. No border crossed. And now this courier dies in the west passes spreading rumors—"

"He didn't spread rumors," said a thin woman with ink-stained fingers, standing, though standing was apparently not done. "He carried *documents*, Chancellor Vess, and those documents were in the council's own cipher, and three days after he left this building our western liaison reports inquisitors on the roads asking after him by name. Rumors do not send inquisitors."

"Inquisitors investigate. That is what they are *for*—"

"They burn archives," the woman said. "Ask Ostmere. Ask what's left of Ostmere's records office. Ash doesn't investigate."

Ivo felt Wren go very still beside her. In the dark of the alcove his hand found her sleeve — not grabbing, just touching, the way you touch someone when you want them to feel what you're feeling because there's no time to say it. *Ostmere.* The town where the inquisitors had nearly taken her. Where Wren had lied so badly and run so well.

"The proposal," Chancellor Vess continued, unbothered, "is simple good sense. The courier is dead. His papers are lost — let us make certain they stay lost. We renew the Accord, we extend the tithe schedule another twenty years, and the mountain stays a mountain. Gentlemen, ladies — we have peace. Peace is not a rumor. Peace is a *balance sheet*, and ours balances."

A murmur of assent went around the horseshoe of seats like wind through wheat. Ivo counted faces: nine cities, nine chairs, and perhaps forty hangers-on, clerks and seconds and merchants' representatives, nodding along. Nodding at *tithe schedules*.

"What tithe?" she whispered. "What's the Accord?"

Wren didn't answer. His face had gone the color of the marble floor.

The thin woman answered for him, because she was still fighting, alone, her voice cracking with the effort of being reasonable in a room that had decided not to be. "You all speak of the Accord as if it were a trade agreement. As if it were wool tariffs. Twenty years ago this council sat in these chairs and voted — *voted*, minuted, sealed — to deliver two hundred and six of its own citizens to the mountain court. Living citizens. They were told they were going to serve as hostages for the cities' good behavior. What actually happens to them under that mountain, none of us knows, because none of us has ever been permitted to ask. And every five years since, we have renewed the delivery. We call it the tithe. We put it in the budget between road maintenance and harbor dredging." Her voice shook. "Sarelle Vane was nineteen years old. Her brother signed the deed over her head because this council threatened to take two children from her household instead of one, and then this body thanked him for his service. And now his corpse is cold in the western passes and you propose to thank him again."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears.

Then Chancellor Vess, gently, the way one speaks to a child or a madman: "Mistress Corvel. What would you have us do? March on the mountain? With what army? The moment we refuse the tithe, the moment that map surfaces, the Accord dies — and with it, every guarantee that has kept nine cities free and fat for twenty years. Two hundred souls, may the gods rest them, against two million living ones. You do the arithmetic. It is not cruelty. It is *arithmetic*."

And the vote was called, and the hands went up, one after another after another, well-fed hands with clean nails, and Ivo stood in the dark of the records alcove with the map burning against her heart and understood, finally and completely, why a man had run himself to death crossing the passes rather than bring his news home.

Not because the council would refuse him.

Because they would *file* him.

Wren's POV

He had sworn an oath in this room. That was the thing he couldn't stop hearing, under the chancellor's voice, under the rustle of the vote — the echo of his own younger voice, twenty-two years old and shining, swearing fealty to the Compact of the Nine before the painted maidens held up their golden sky. *To guard the law that keeps the peace.*

He had believed it. That was the unforgivable part. Not the things he'd done since — the evictions, the quiet arrests, the checkpoint at Ostmere where he'd once stamped a family's papers and watched them walk toward the border with everything they owned in sacks. Those were sins of the hands. But believing — believing that the arithmetic was noble, that peace purchased at any price was still peace — that had been a sin of the soul, and he had spent eleven years drinking himself grey trying to drown the creditor.

Now he stood in an alcove full of other people's ledgers, next to a hedge-witch half his age, listening to his institution vote to bury a dead man twice.

"You knew," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. Her voice was low and flat and more frightening than if she'd screamed. "You knew what the tithe was. On the road. When you recognized the seal — you knew whose it was."

"I suspected." He kept his eyes on the chamber. Watching the vote close was like watching a door shut on a person. "Aldous Vane was the western liaison's courier. I served under the liaison, before. I knew he had a sister. Everyone knew — it was the scandal of the season, twenty years ago, before it was quietly not a scandal anymore." He exhaled. "I hoped I was wrong. Men hope. It's cheaper than thinking."

"You hoped." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes in the dim alcove were the exact grey-green of the valley she'd come from, and he understood suddenly that this girl had been raised by someone cruel enough to teach her exactly how much a person could endure. "Two hundred and six people, Wren. Sold. Renewed every five years like a lease. And your council just voted to keep paying."

"Yes."

"And you still love them." She said it without contempt, which was somehow worse. "I can see it. You're standing there grieving. Like a man at the bedside of a father who beat him."

Wren opened his mouth to deny it and found nothing there to say. Because she was right, and the knowing of it went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor he'd worn so long he'd forgotten it was armor. He loved Merrowgate. He loved the painted maidens and the marble and the bells over the harbor at dusk. He loved the idea the place stood for — law instead of lords, ledger instead of sword — and the idea was good, and the institution was rotten, and he had never in his life learned how to hold both truths in the same pair of hands.

"Mistress Corvel will be arrested within the week," he said at last. "Vess won't move today — too public — but she's made herself the last obstacle. Watch. By month's end she'll be reassigned somewhere distant and damp." He straightened. "Which means the map is the only voice left saying what happened. Yours and mine, hedge-witch. We're what's left of the opposition."

"We're a drunk and a girl who can't do magic."

"Yes," Wren said. "The council should be terrified."

That startled a sound out of her — half laugh, half sob, gone as fast as it came. Then her hand went to her breastbone, and he saw her flinch.

"It's getting hotter," she said. "The map. It does that when—" She stopped. Frowned. Pulled the folded square from inside her coat, and even in the dim alcove Wren could see it glowing faintly through the leather of her glove, gold seeping between her fingers like light through a shutter.

"It's never done that indoors," she whispered.

"No," Wren said slowly. "It wouldn't have." He was remembering, with the particular horror of a man remembering too late, the layout of this building. The records alcove. The vault beneath it. "Twenty years ago, when the deeds were drawn up, the originals were lodged in the council archive for safekeeping. Sealed. Witnessed." His mouth had gone dry. "Everyone assumed the mountain court took custody of them. Everyone assumed wrong."

Ivo looked down at the burning map, and then up at the floor beneath their feet, at the marble quarried on broken promises, and he watched her arrive at it a heartbeat after he did.

The archive was directly below them. And something down there — some paper bearing a dead man's seal and a living woman's name — had just woken up, and was calling to her through six feet of stone.

Above them, faintly, came the sound of the session ending, chairs scraping, and one set of footsteps that did not join the others going out.

Footsteps, unhurried, coming toward the alcove.
