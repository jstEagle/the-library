## 5. Sarelle's Room

The map's second layer revealed itself on the third night in Merrowgate, when Ivo spread it across the floor of their rented room and held a candle low enough to catch the wax she'd rubbed into the creases. Beneath the inked roads and the mountain's spidery crown, fainter than memory, another drawing had been waiting: not roads this time, but rooms. A house drawn in elevation, like an architect's plate — three storeys, a bay window, a stair coiling up through its heart like a snail shell. And at the top, in handwriting that trembled even in ink, a single word pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the paper.

*Sarelle.*

"She drew it herself," Wren said from the doorway. He had a cup of something he wasn't drinking and the grey look of a man who hadn't slept in a city that never went dark. "Before he left. Before—" He stopped, because they both knew the shape of before. The stranger had died with his sister's name in his mouth and his blood soaking into valley earth where no magic could follow him, and Ivo had buried him under stones because there was nothing else to be done for him.

"It's a house," Ivo said. "In Merrowgate."

"Half the houses in Merrowgate look like that." But he came closer, kneeling despite his knees, and studied the little drawing with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant in a pretty hand. Then his finger stopped over the bay window. "No. No, I know this one. Candlemaker's Row. It belongs to Aldous Vey."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows him. Silk and salt and old money. He was on the council delegation twenty years ago." Wren's voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was holding something down. "He signed the bargain. They all signed it. But Vey kept souvenirs."

Ivo looked at the word again. *Sarelle.* Pressed hard enough to tear. A woman writing her own address because she knew she would forget it, or because she wanted whoever found the map to know exactly where to come and get her.

The map warmed against her ribs, patient as a heartbeat.

* * *

Candlemaker's Row smelled of tallow and money, which is to say it smelled of nothing at all beneath the perfume of its gardens. Vey's house stood behind iron gates and a hedge trimmed to mathematical obedience, and Ivo hated it before she'd touched the latch. She hated the symmetry of it, the way everything living had been bullied into shape. It reminded her of her master's cottage, of hands that arranged things because arrangement was power.

They went in as what Wren called honest work — he still owned clothes that could pass for a courier's, and Ivo had learned in three weeks of travel that her face was forgettable in exactly the useful way. A delivery of documents from a Merrowgate counting-house. Wren lied badly and Ivo carried the forged seal, and the doorman, who had the bored eyes of a man paid to see nothing, waved them toward the service stair.

The map pulled. That was the only word for it. Not heat now but a tug, gentle and insistent, like a child at her sleeve, guiding her past the kitchens, past the laundry with its steam and singing, to the back stair and up. Up past the second floor where the family slept behind doors thick enough to swallow sound. Up to the third floor, where the corridor narrowed and the wallpaper changed to something older, out of fashion for twenty years, and every door was locked except the last one, which was not locked at all.

It opened onto a room that made Ivo's stomach turn, though she couldn't have said why at first. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Beautiful the way the hedge outside was beautiful — everything arranged, nothing alive. A harp no one played. Fresh flowers in a vase, replaced so often the stems never wilted. A window with a view of the mountains, framed like a painting, positioned so that whoever sat in the chair by it would always be looking at the place where the court beneath the mountain lay hidden.

And in the chair sat a woman.

She was sewing. Her hands moved with perfect, mechanical grace, needle flashing, thread following, and she was making — Ivo saw with a lurch — a christening gown. Fine white linen, embroidered at the hem with tiny birds, for a baby that had surely grown and gone decades ago. The woman's hair was silver at the temples but her face was smooth in the wrong places, young around the eyes and old around the mouth, as if time had argued about her and settled on a compromise. She was perhaps fifty. She should have been seventy. Or she should have been thirty. There was no way to tell, and that was the wrongness of her, the thing that raised the hair on Ivo's arms.

Her eyes were empty. Not blind — they tracked the needle, followed the thread — but empty the way a house is empty when the family has gone away and left the furniture covered in sheets. Someone was home in the hands only.

"Sarelle?" Ivo said softly.

The needle did not stop. The thread did not break. The woman sewed.

Wren made a sound behind her, small and broken, and Ivo understood that he had known Sarelle once, long ago, when she'd been a whole person laughing at his clumsy sword-drill. He put his hand against the doorframe as if the room had struck him.

Ivo crossed to the chair and knelt, slowly, the way you'd approach a frightened animal, though the woman showed no fear at all. She showed nothing. Up close Ivo could see the seams of her — not literal seams, but the places where a person had been taken apart and the parts that remained had been left running. Hands that remembered craft. Eyes that remembered light. Everything else packed away somewhere, folded up and filed in a vault beneath a mountain, held as collateral against the free cities' good behavior.

"I'm looking for Sarelle," Ivo said again, gently. "Her brother sent me."

Nothing. The needle flashed. A bird took shape at the hem, stitch by stitch, wings half-finished, flying nowhere.

Ivo reached out and touched the woman's wrist.

The map flared hot against her ribs — and the world *tilted*.

* * *

She was standing in a kitchen she didn't know, in a body that wasn't hers, twenty years younger and laughing. A boy was chasing her around the table — no, a young man, gangly, with flour on his nose, and she was Sarelle, she was inside Sarelle's laugh, feeling it shake loose in her own chest, and someone was calling them in to supper and the whole world smelled of bread—

Then cold. Cold and paper and a room full of men in good coats, and her own voice — Sarelle's voice — saying words she didn't understand while a quill scratched, and a man with kind eyes and a terrible smile explaining that it was for the cities, it was for peace, it was such a small thing to give, and her hand moving anyway, signing, signing, because everyone she trusted was nodding, because the alternative was war, because— 

—and then the taking, which felt like being read aloud by someone who skipped the best chapters, like being summarized, like being *folded*, and the last thing she thought as she went into the dark was *Corvin, don't—*

Ivo came back to herself on the floor of the beautiful room, gasping, Wren's hand gripping her shoulder, the sewing woman still stitching above her as if nothing had happened at all.

"What did you see," Wren said. Not a question. His face was white.

"Her brother's name is Corvin." Ivo pushed herself upright. Her hands were shaking. "That's who died in the valley. That's who sent me." She looked up at the woman in the chair, at the half-finished birds, and grief hit her sideways — grief for a man she'd buried under stones and a woman sitting three feet away who couldn't grieve for him at all. "I saw the day they took her. She fought it, at the end. Inside. Where they couldn't see."

"Sarelle." Wren knelt beside the chair, and his voice cracked open like a door off its hinges. "Sarelle, it's Wren. Wren Halloway. You used to steal my helmet. You used to—"

The needle paused.

It was the smallest thing. A hesitation, a hitch, less than a heartbeat. But Ivo saw it, and Wren saw it, and then the woman's head turned — slowly, with the resistance of a rusted hinge — and her empty eyes found Wren's face and *focused*, and behind the emptiness something surfaced like a drowning swimmer breaking water.

"Wren," she said. Her voice was dusty, unused, a voice kept in a drawer for twenty years. "You shouldn't be here."

"Sarelle—"

"*You shouldn't be here.*" Louder now, and her hands had stopped sewing, and her face was doing something terrible, trying to make expressions it no longer had full use of, grief and terror fighting through a mask that fit badly. "There's a contract. There's always a contract. If you touch me wrong, if you take me without the deed, the clause takes hold — I've seen it happen, they brought me here to watch, they brought me to watch what happens to people who run—" Her breath hitched, ragged. "The deed. Only the original deed unbinds me. It's in the archive. Under the mountain. In the usurper's own keeping, because he doesn't trust anyone, not even his chamberlain, not even—"

Her voice was gaining strength with every word, a fire fed after two decades of ash, and Ivo could hear the woman she must have been, the woman Corvin had crossed a kingdom to reach.

"And then you mustn't come," Sarelle said, and now she gripped Ivo's wrist with fingers surprisingly strong, surprisingly desperate, and her eyes — still mostly empty, but with that one bright swimmer thrashing in them — fixed on Ivo's face. "Whoever you are. Whatever he promised you. Don't come. Don't come under the mountain. He'll want what you're carrying. He always wants. It's all he does. He'll offer you anything, and the worst of it is he'll mean it, and it will still destroy you. Go home. Forget my name. Let me keep my brother's death mean something by letting it be *enough*—"

And then she was gone. Ivo watched it happen — watched the swimmer go under, watched the emptiness close back over her face like water over a stone. Sarelle's hands returned to the sewing. The needle flashed. A bird finished its wings and began another.

But her grip on Ivo's wrist loosened slowly, reluctantly, like a hand letting go of a rope over a drop, and the last pressure of her fingers said more clearly than any surfacing could: *remember, remember, remember.*

* * *

They walked out through the gardens, past the mathematically obedient hedge, and Ivo waited until they were three streets away before she let herself stop and lean against a wall and breathe.

"We can't take her," Wren said. He was staring at nothing. "She told us how. We can't take her without the deed, and the deed is—" He laughed, short and awful. "Under the mountain. Of course it is. It's always under the mountain. Twenty years ago I helped seal the miners' stair myself, and I thought I was closing a door on all of it forever."

Ivo said nothing. She was thinking about the clause. About contracts, and how her master had loved contracts, how every lesson had ended with terms and conditions, how she'd fled that cottage believing she was escaping debt and had instead walked into a valley where magic couldn't follow — or so she'd believed. The map was warm against her ribs. It had been warm since the day a dying man pressed it into her hands, and she had told herself for weeks that she was carrying it out of guilt, out of obligation, out of the simple decency of finishing a dead man's errand.

She wasn't. She understood that now, standing in a Merrowgate street with Sarelle's desperation still printed on her wrist.

She was carrying it because it was hers. Because somewhere in a vault beneath a mountain there was a page with her name on it too — she'd felt it in the flash of contact, a certainty like a scar she'd never seen — and because a hollow man in a beautiful court had been waiting twenty years for her to walk east, and she was done letting other people write the terms.

"He offered her peace," Ivo said quietly. "Twenty years ago, they sold souls for peace, and the peace was a lie, and the souls are still paying for it. The council will debate and deliberate and choose comfort, same as they always do. Vey will keep his trophy. And Sarelle will sit in that chair sewing christening gowns until she dies of a life she never got to live." She turned to Wren. "So we don't ask permission. We don't ask whether. We go under the mountain and we take the deeds — all of them — and we burn the whole rotten archive."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the grey drained out of his face and something older and harder came up underneath it — the knight he'd been before the drinking, before the disgrace, before the door sealed on the miners' stair.

"The stair I sealed," he said, "had a flaw. I built the flaw in myself, in case I ever needed to come back." He straightened. "We leave tonight."

Above them, far to the east, the mountain wore its evening cloud like a crown, and beneath it, in a beautiful room on Candlemaker's Row, a woman sewed birds that flew nowhere — and waited, in the only place left to her, for someone to finally keep a promise.
