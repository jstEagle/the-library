## 6. The Hollow Court

The miners' stair began behind a waterfall that no longer fell.

Wren had sealed it himself, eleven years ago — three days of masonry and mortar and the particular shame of a man doing his duty well. He remembered the work of it: how the stone had sweated in the dark, how his hands had ached for a week after, how he had stood back at the end and felt *proud*. That was the part that shamed him now, not the sealing but the pride. He had been twenty-six and certain that walls were the same thing as peace.

He chiseled out the first stone with the same trowel-blade he'd once used to set it, and the mountain exhaled against his face — cold air, mineral-smelling, older than any kingdom above it.

"You've done this before," Ivo said behind him. Not a question. She had stopped asking questions about his past somewhere on the road past Merrowgate, when she'd understood that every answer cost him something and had decided, with her particular brand of mercy, to stop collecting the debt.

"I built this wall," Wren said. "That's different from having done it."

"Is it?"

He didn't answer. He went through the gap sideways, into the throat of the mountain, and heard her follow.

* * *

The stair descended in long switchbacks cut into living rock, and for the first hour there was nothing but the sound of their breathing and the drip of water finding its way down through stone that had forgotten the sky. Wren counted the landings without meaning to. Two hundred and forty steps between each turn; he had counted them once before too, carrying dispatches, carrying orders, carrying the comfortable certainty that everything below him was properly governed.

At the fourth landing Ivo stopped walking.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

He did. Music. Faint and sweet, threading up through the rock like water through cloth — strings, or something like strings, playing a melody he almost recognized and couldn't name. It was beautiful. That was the first wrong thing about it: nothing should have been playing music four hundred feet inside a mountain.

"The court keeps musicians," he said, and heard how hollow the old explanation sounded even as he said it.

"In the dark? On a stair no one uses?" Ivo pressed her palm flat against the rock wall. "There's no one up here. There's no *room* up here."

She was right. He had known she was right since the second landing, when the music had started, because he remembered now — he had always remembered, and buried it — that the stair had hummed like this on his last descent too. The mountain sang to itself. Or something in it did.

"Keep close," he said. "And whatever you see below — don't stare. Staring is how they know you're not one of them."

"And if I can't help staring?"

"Then stare at me," Wren said. "I'm used to being looked at like I don't belong."

* * *

The stair ended at a door of black wood without hinges or handle, and the door opened before Wren could decide whether to knock — swung inward onto warmth and gold light and the smell of beeswax and roasting meat, and Wren's stomach turned over, because the last time he had smelled that exact combination of things he had been nineteen years old and being fitted for his first court uniform.

They stepped through into a corridor that belonged to no architecture he knew, though he recognized every element of it. Arches like the ones in Merrowgate's council hall. Floor tiles patterned after the free cities' guildhalls. Windows — *windows*, deep inside the mountain — showing gardens that could not exist, full of flowers blooming out of season under a sun that was only a memory of a sun. The court beneath the mountain had been built by something that had seen the world above and loved it the way a thief loves a locked house.

"It's copying us," Ivo breathed. "All of it. It's copying *up*."

"Don't talk in the open," Wren murmured. "Sound carries strangely here."

Servants moved along the corridor, and Wren made himself look at them the way he'd trained himself to look at anything in the old days: briefly, completely, without flinching. A woman carrying linens, her face smooth and pleasant and *repeated* — he passed a second woman, then a third, with the same face, the same small mole at the left jaw, the same gentle half-smile, none of them acknowledging one another. A young man polishing a railing had the face of a courier Wren had known in the border towns, a man who was, as far as Wren knew, still alive and selling apples two hundred miles east. His hands were wrong, though. Too smooth. Hands that had never held anything heavier than a smile.

"Borrowed faces," Ivo whispered. "Wren. Those are *borrowed*—"

"I see them." He caught her elbow and steered her toward an alcove hung with tapestries that depicted, in loving detail, harvests that had never happened. "Don't say names. Not yours, not mine, not anyone's. Names are currency down here. Don't spend yours."

She looked at him then — really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether a thing was worth being afraid of — and nodded once.

The music swelled somewhere ahead of them, closer now, and they followed the corridor as it curved down and opened all at once into the court itself.

* * *

It was beautiful. That was the second wrong thing, and the worse one.

A great hall vaulted like the inside of a bell, lit by chandeliers of pale crystal that burned without heat. Dancers moved across a floor of black glass — dozens of them, gowned and uniformed, turning through figures of grave elegance — and there was no orchestra, no gallery, no players anywhere in the vast room, and yet the music came from everywhere, from the walls themselves, from the dancers' own bodies, note-perfect and endless. Wren watched a woman waltz past and saw that her slippers never quite touched the floor. He watched an old lord bow to a partner who was only a shimmer in the air, bowing to nothing, graciously, again and again.

"They don't get tired," Ivo said softly. "Look at them. They've been dancing a long time."

"How can you tell?"

"Because they dance like my master's candles burned." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when hedge-witch knowledge was the only armor she had. "Everything in this room is burning something to keep moving. Look at the edges of them, Wren. Look at where the light doesn't sit right."

He looked, and wished he hadn't. Every dancer had a faint blur at their outline, like a painting seen through smoke. Every servant had it. Even the flowers in the impossible gardens, when he glanced back toward the false windows, had it — beauty rendered slightly out of focus, slightly *unpaid for*.

This was what the usurper's power cost. This was the ledger under the splendor. Every borrowed face, every tireless dancer, every note of music with no musician was a soul stretched thin and spent like coin, worn translucent with use. And somewhere in this glittering machine, held as collateral against the free cities' peace, were souls that had been whole once — Sarelle among them, and the courier who had died pressing a map into a stranger's hands.

Wren had served this court for six years. He had guarded its corridors, carried its decrees, knelt before its throne. He had told himself the stories everyone told: that the mountain kept order, that order was worth its price, that the price was paid by someone else, somewhere else, in some abstract way that never touched the actual world.

Now he stood in the actual world, and it was dancing, and it was starving.

"Why did you leave?" Ivo asked quietly. "Really."

He took longer than he meant to answering. "I escorted a tithe," he said finally. "Once. Souls going down into the archive levels. They walked. They could walk, then — new enough, still shaped like people. One of them asked me for the time of day. Just that. Like a person would." His jaw tightened. "I gave it to her. And then I handed her over anyway, because the writ said to, and she looked at me the whole way down the last flight of stairs like I was the answer to a question she'd stopped asking. Three weeks later I couldn't remember her face. Couldn't. Something had — filed it away from me. And I realized the court does that. It takes the remembering too. You serve long enough and you can't hold on to why any of it was wrong." He looked at the dancers, at their blurred and patient grace. "I ran before it finished taking mine. That's the whole of it. Not honor. Arithmetic."

Ivo touched his sleeve — just briefly, just two fingers, there and gone. "Then let's go do some arithmetic," she said.

* * *

They kept to the edges of the hall, behind pillars veined with crystal, following the map's pull — Ivo carried it now against her ribs, and Wren could tell by the way she tilted, subtle as a compass needle, which direction the archive lay. Down. Everything down here was down. The court's splendor was a lid, and beneath it the contracts waited in the dark like roots.

They had nearly reached the far archway when the music stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped*, mid-phrase, the way a heart stops, and in the silence Wren heard every dancer's slippers settle to the black glass floor at once — a single soft click, like a lock turning. The dancers stood still. The servants stood still. The false sunlight in the false windows dimmed to a courteous dusk.

"Guests," said a voice behind them, "are announced."

The chamberlain was tall and grey and immaculate, in livery Wren remembered better than his own mother's face. His face was his own — that was somehow the most frightening thing about him, the only unblurred face in the hall, lined and human and utterly empty behind the eyes, like a house with all the furniture sold off. He smiled at Wren with genuine, terrible warmth.

"Ser Wren of the Vale Guard," the chamberlain said. "Eleven years absent, and the mountain remembers its own. We kept your rank open. We keep everything open, here. Nothing is ever truly given away." His gaze slid to Ivo, lingered, and returned. "And your companion. How industrious of her, to bring herself."

"She came on her own," Wren said.

"Of course she did." The chamberlain folded his hands. "Here is what will happen, Ser Wren. You will take her to the guest wing, and you will return to your post, and within the month you will wear your old insignia, and the grey will go out of your beard, and the drink will lose its taste for you, and you will sleep at night — do you understand? Sleep. As you did before. All of it restored, all of it *forgiven*, because the mountain forgives everything except absence. You need only give her to us. She is expected. She has been expected for a very long time." He tilted his head, kind as a physician. "You have been hollow a long while yourself, ser. We know the feeling. We are generous with what fills a man back up."

Wren felt it move through him like a tide — the wanting, huge and simple and shameful. His rank. His name in the rolls. The years given back, the shame lifted off him like a coat he could finally put down. He thought of his rooms above the border town, the bottle, the grey mornings, the arithmetic of a life that had never balanced since the stairwell and the woman who had asked him the time.

Beside him, Ivo said nothing. She didn't plead. She just stood there, watching him with those level eyes, already knowing — he could see it in her face — that whatever he chose, she would not hate him for it. And that, more than the chamberlain's whole speech, was the thing that broke the spell. No one in this mountain had ever offered him a choice without a price. She was offering him nothing at all, and it was worth more than everything the chamberlain had.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out rough and quiet and it was the easiest thing he had said in eleven years.

The chamberlain's smile did not change, but the hall changed around it. The dancers turned their heads — all of them, together, a hundred faces rotating toward the two of them with the smooth synchrony of weathervanes finding wind. The music resumed, but wrong now, fast and thin, a tune like running.

"A pity," the chamberlain said, and snapped his fingers. "Take them. Gently, if it costs less."

"*Run!*" Wren shouted, and shoved Ivo toward the archway, and the run began.
