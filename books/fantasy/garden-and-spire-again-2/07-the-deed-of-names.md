## 7. The Deed of Names

The archive breathed cold.

Ivo felt it before she saw it — a stillness in the mountain's marrow, the kind of hush that lived only in places where nothing was permitted to decay. The chamberlain's servants had left them at a door of black iron worked with vines that were not vines, and Wren had gone ahead to watch the corridor, jaw set, hand never far from his sword. So she crossed the threshold alone, into a room that had no right to exist beneath a mountain.

Shelves rose out of the dark like cliff faces. Not wood — something paler, grown rather than built, and every shelf held ledgers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Their spines were unmarked, but as Ivo walked the central aisle she began to hear them: a faint rustling, like leaves in wind, though there was no wind here. Like breathing. Like a great many people trying very hard to be quiet.

She stopped walking.

*They're in the books.* The understanding arrived whole, the way her master's lessons used to — not reasoned toward but simply *known*, settling into her bones. Every ledger on those shelves was a life. She could feel them the way she'd once felt weather coming, pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper and rain. Somewhere in this room was Sarelle's name pressed flat between covers, kept alive the way a flower is kept alive between the pages of a dictionary — perfectly preserved and perfectly unable to move.

"Right," Ivo said aloud, because saying it made it less enormous. "Then we do this properly."

Her master had taught her that much, at least. Or she'd thought he had. She put the thought away before it could cut.

The map had gone quiet since they entered the court — no warmth, no pull, just a folded weight in her pack like a sleeping animal. But when she stood in the central aisle and let her fingers trail along the nearest shelf, the rustling rose to meet her touch, eager, terrible, a thousand small voices asking to be *found*. She snatched her hand back.

"Don't," she told them softly. "Not yet. I have to know how."

It was a promise. She hadn't meant to make one. The rustling settled anyway, reluctantly, like children told to wait for morning.

She found the catalogue by accident — or it found her. A lectern of pale stone near the room's heart, and on it an index that wrote itself as she watched, ink blooming up through vellum in letters that rearranged whenever she blinked. She searched for *Sarelle* and the index shivered, and a number appeared, and the number became a shelf, and the shelf became a book in her hands before she fully understood she'd reached for it.

The deed was beautiful. That was the worst part. Fine paper, careful script, a seal of grey wax bearing the mountain's sigil — a spire within a ring. And there, in the middle of all that loveliness, in a clerk's indifferent hand:

*Sarelle Vane. One (1) soul, entire. Sold by Sarelle Vane, freely given, in exchange for peace between the Free Cities and the Court Beneath the Mountain. Term: perpetual.*

Freely given. Ivo read that line four times. Someone had taken this woman's name and written *freely* beside it, and somewhere a council of respectable men had signed their approval below, and twenty years of quiet had been purchased with the interest.

"You didn't give anything freely," Ivo whispered to the page. "None of you did. That's the whole point of what he does."

The book warmed under her hands — just slightly, just enough. She wrapped it in cloth from her pack and did not put it down again.

* * *

It was the second thing she found that broke her.

She hadn't meant to keep searching. She had Sarelle; the sensible thing was to go, to find Wren, to run while the court still believed its guests were wandering where they'd been invited to wander. But the index was still open, still writing, and some stubborn hedge-witch reflex — the one that made her count the change twice, read the contract twice, check the latch twice — made her search one more name.

Hers.

*Ivo Eastwick,* she wrote in the space the index offered, and watched the letters take, and waited for the index to shrug and offer nothing, because she had no soul-deed, because she was nobody, because the valley had scoured her magic out of her like lye scours grease from a pan —

The index bloomed.

A number. A shelf. A book already waiting, spine unmarked, cover cool as riverstone. Her hands knew it was hers before she opened it, the way you know your own bed in the dark.

The page inside was blank.

Not empty — blank. There was a difference, and her skin crawled with it. Empty paper waits for nothing. This page *waited*. The parchment had the taut, expectant quality of a drumhead, and across its top, in the same fine clerk's hand that had recorded Sarelle's surrender, someone had written a single line:

*Ivo Eastwick. Reserved.*

Below it, a date. Twenty-two years old. Older than her memory. Older than everything.

Ivo sat down on the archive floor without deciding to, the book open in her lap, the blank page drinking the light. Reserved. Her name had been sitting in this cold room since before she could walk, filed and sealed and *waiting*, and she had spent her whole life believing herself diminished — believing the valley had burned her hollow, believing her master's sighs about wasted potential, believing that if she just tried harder, studied longer, ground finer herbs and spoke truer words, the magic would come back.

There had never been anything wrong with her.

There had been a *transaction*.

"No," she said. "No, he wouldn't —"

But even as she said it, the memories began rearranging themselves, the way a room rearranges after you learn a terrible fact about someone you love. The way her master had always known which clients would come. The way he'd steered her away from the free cities her whole apprenticeship, then, in the last year, begun speaking of the eastern passes with strange frequency. The way he'd wept when she ran — she'd thought it was grief. It might have been guilt. It might have been *fear*, the fear of a man whose arrangement had just walked off the table.

And the stranger. The bloodied courier who had died at her feet with a name on his lips. He hadn't stumbled into the valley by chance. He'd been looking for the girl with the reserved page — the one soul in the world already half-belonging to the mountain, the perfect courier to carry the map home. Her escape from her master's cottage, the one act she'd ever believed was entirely her own, had been anticipated, arranged, *purchased* years before she made it.

Even her running had been someone else's design.

Ivo laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and she pressed her fist against her mouth to stop the rest of it. Above her the shelves rustled — thousands of souls stirring at the sound, murmuring, asking what was wrong, what was wrong, what was wrong.

"I'm sorry," she told them. "I'm sorry. I'm going to fix it."

She didn't know yet how. But she knew, suddenly and completely, that she would not sign whatever page was waiting for her signature. Whatever the mountain wanted from Ivo Eastwick, it had been waiting twenty-two years for her consent — and consent was the one thing left in the world that belonged entirely to her.

She closed the book. Kept it. Wrapped beside Sarelle's.

* * *

He came without footsteps.

One moment the aisle was empty; the next, the air at its far end had assembled itself into a man, the way frost assembles on glass — gradually, then all at once. He was tall and spare and dressed in grey so fine it seemed woven from the mountain's own stone, and his face was handsome in the way of portraits: accurate, composed, and somehow *unfinished*, as if a painter had grown bored before the eyes.

Wren had described him on the miners' stair, in a voice like a man describing a wound: *He'll be courteous. That's how you'll know. Courteous men don't need to threaten.*

"Miss Eastwick." His voice was warm and dry, like paper near a flame. "You've been reading my correspondence."

"Your correspondence." Ivo got to her feet. Her legs held, which surprised her. "You mean your *harvest*."

"A harsh word." He drifted closer without seeming to walk, and the shelves leaned subtly away from him, the way grass leans from wind. "I prefer to think of myself as a custodian. Souls are fragile things, Miss Eastwick. Left loose in the world, they bruise. They grieve. They die. Here—" he gestured, and the rustling of ten thousand ledgers rose around them like applause, "—they are safe. Forever. Is safety not worth a little stillness?"

"Not when the stillness is bought with someone else's name."

"Every peace is." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a mask fits — correctly, and nothing more. "Your cities learned that. Your *master* learned that. Ah — yes." Something moved behind the unfinished eyes, something almost like pleasure. "You've found your page. I wondered when you would. You went straight to it, didn't you? Even before Sarelle's. Souls know their own paperwork."

Ivo's throat tightened. "My master sold me. Before I could walk."

"He sold your *potential*," the usurper corrected gently. "Which is a different thing, and kinder than you're making it. He was a dying man with a gifted child and no way to feed either. I offered him terms. He took them. You were fed, housed, taught — and in exchange, I received first claim on what you might become. Everyone was satisfied." He tilted his head. "Well. Nearly everyone. He did cry rather a lot, your master. Toward the end."

"I ran from him."

"You ran *to* me." The words landed soft as snowfall and cut just as deep. "Did you truly believe a hedge-witch's apprentice outran her master's wards? That a bloodied courier collapsed at precisely the right crossroads, in precisely the one valley I cannot enter, carrying precisely the map that would bring you here?" He spread his hands, and the gesture was almost tender. "I have been patient with you, Miss Eastwick. Twenty-two years patient. The valley stripped nothing from you — why would I damage my own investment? It taught you silence. Discipline. How to hold power without letting it hold you. Do you imagine that skill arises naturally? I *arranged* your education, piece by piece, through frightened men who never knew whom they served. You are the finest thing I have ever grown."

The archive rustled. Ivo realized distantly that her hands were shaking — and that the shaking had stopped mattering.

"Why?" Her voice came out low and steady, and she was grateful to whoever she was becoming. "Why go to such lengths for one hedge-witch?"

"Because willing signatures bind deepest." He stepped closer, close enough now that she could see that his eyes, up close, were not unfinished at all — they were *absent*, twin windows into a room where no one sat. "A soul taken is collateral. A soul *given* is foundation. I have built this court on the taken, and it groans, Miss Eastwick, it groans like a ship in ice. But a soul offered freely, by one trained to hold power in silence — anchored at the heart of my works —" his voice dropped to something almost reverent, almost hungry, "— this court would stand until the mountains themselves are sand. And you would want for nothing. Rank. Craft. Every spell your valley forbade you, taught properly, by me. You would be the most powerful woman beneath the sky."

"And Sarelle?"

"Would remain exactly where she is. Safe. Forever." He smiled his portrait smile. "Everyone keeps what's theirs. Isn't that justice?"

Ivo looked down at the two books in her arms — Sarelle's deed, warm as a living thing against her ribs, and her own blank page, taut and waiting, hungry for her name.

Twenty-two years of being designed. Of being a garden someone else planted, pruned, and planned to harvest. Her master's tears. The stranger's blood on her hands. Sarelle's empty eyes begging her, *don't come*.

They had all assumed she'd sign. Every one of them — the dead man, the dying master, the hollow king — had built their designs on the certainty that Ivo Eastwick, given comfort and power, would choose herself.

She lifted her head.

"You've made one mistake," she said.

The usurper's smile thinned by a degree. "Oh?"

"You grew me in *silence*." Ivo hugged the deeds to her chest and felt, for the first time since the valley, the power there — not gone, never gone, only quiet, coiled and patient as a banked fire. "Silent things listen. And I have been listening to your books since I walked in. All ten thousand of them. They've been telling me what you are."

For the first time, the court beneath the mountain went utterly, perfectly still.

Behind the usurper, at the end of the aisle, a door of black iron swung open — and Wren stood in it, sword drawn, face grey as ash, and shouted her name.
