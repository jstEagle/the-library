## 9. The Mountain's Heart

The archive was burning from the inside out, and Ivo stood in the middle of it, reading.

Not the contracts — those were ash and curling vellum now, the deeds of names flaking into black moths that rose on the heat. She read the mountain itself: the groan in its bones, the way the light guttered in the lanterns as though something vast had drawn a breath and not yet let it out. The court above them was screaming in voices too beautiful to be human, and beneath her feet the heart-chamber opened like a wound, a shaft of pale stone descending into a light that did not come from any fire.

"Tell me you've found it," Wren said at her shoulder. He was bleeding from a cut along his jaw where the chamberlain's guard had caught him, and he held his sword low and loose, the way men do when they have stopped believing the sword will save them.

"I found it." She held up the last deed — the one she'd pulled from the usurper's own coffer, the parchment that bore no name at all, only a smooth blank space where a name should be. "It's this. It's me."

* * *

She had known since the archive. Since the blank page with her name waiting on it like an empty chair at a table. Her master — old Marrow, who had fed her nettle soup and taught her which mushrooms lied — had sold her before she could walk. Sold the shape of what she might become to a hollow man beneath a mountain, and then spent twenty years teaching her to be small enough that no one would ever come to collect.

Except one man had come. A stranger with blood in his mouth and his sister's name on his lips, who had looked at a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice and seen the anchor the whole stolen court needed.

The valley hadn't stripped her magic. It had taught her to hold it silently — to carry power the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room, without spilling, without drinking. Every day she had walked through that magic-dead vale feeling nothing, she had been practicing. She just hadn't known for what.

Now the heart-chamber lay open below, and the souls hung in it like storm-light.

They came down the stair together, Wren first with his sword up, though there was nothing left up here to fight but the dark. The chamber was a hollow sphere of white stone, and within it the stolen souls turned slowly — hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small burning color, each one a person somewhere in the world walking around with a hole where their self should be. Sarelle's soul burned near the center, a deep amber, patient as honey. Twenty years in a jar, and still it glowed.

"They're waiting," Ivo said. Her voice sounded strange down here, doubled by the stone. "They've been waiting so long they've forgotten how to do anything else."

"And you can send them home?"

"I can open the door." She knelt at the chamber's edge and set the blank deed flat against the stone. "But a door needs a frame. If I just let them go, they'll scatter. They'll come apart. Someone has to stand in the doorway and hold it — a living name, anchored on this side, so every soul has something to pour back toward."

Wren went very still. "Ivo."

"It has to be a name," she said. "A whole one. Given freely — that's the oldest law, older than the mountain. And it has to stay. That's the price. You don't get to give your name and then walk off with it under your arm."

"No." He said it flatly, the way he said everything that mattered. "Find another way."

"There isn't one. Believe me, I've been looking since the archive. There's always a price and never a discount." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Wren. Listen to me. I spent my whole life being someone else's design. Marrow's design. The stranger's. Even the map — even this. This is the one thing I've ever chosen. Let me choose it well."

"You'll be nothing." His voice broke on it. "You'll walk out of here unnamed. I've seen what the soulless are. Empty houses. I won't drag an empty house up a mountain and call it you."

"Then don't call it me." She took his hand — gauntleted, shaking — and pressed it once against her cheek. "Call it a debt. You owed Tammas Eastwick your life; I'm told you pay your debts. Pay this one by getting Sarelle home. By telling the cities what their peace cost. By remembering me badly and often."

Above them, the mountain screamed again, closer now. Stone split somewhere in the upper dark, and dust sifted down through the soul-light like snow falling upward.

"Ivo—"

"Hold the door behind me," she said. "That's all. Hold it until everyone's through."

* * *

She stepped into the light.

It was warm. That was the terrible thing — after all the dread, the heart of the mountain was warm as a kitchen, warm as Marrow's cottage on a baking day, and the souls turned around her like curious birds. She felt them notice her. Felt them lean in, hungry and hopeful, a thousand faces she couldn't see all turning at once.

She knelt in the center of them and put her hand on the blank deed.

She didn't speak words, because the valley had taught her better than that. Words were how magic used you — gave it a handle to take hold of, a mouth to shout through. What Marrow had really taught her, all those silent years, was the other thing: the discipline of holding. So she held. She gathered her name the way you gather water in cupped hands — every memory, every scar, every stubborn unkind thought and sudden kindness, the girl who ran from the cottage and the woman kneeling here, all of it gathered up and offered, freely, without letting it spill sideways into anything else.

*Ivo Eastwick,* she thought, offering it like bread. *Take it. Go home.*

The door opened.

There was no sound, which was somehow worse than sound. One moment the souls circled her like trapped weather, and the next they were moving — pouring past her, through her, each one brushing her like a hand on the shoulder in passing, and going. Down and out and away, through the stone, through the miles, home to the bodies that had walked hollow for twenty years. She felt Sarelle's amber warmth pass closest of all, and it paused — one heartbeat, two — and something in it pressed against her like a sister's kiss before it went.

And with every soul that left her, a little of Ivo went too.

She had expected pain. It wasn't pain. It was subtraction. First went her mother's face — she'd never known her mother, but the wanting of her was hers, and it went. Then the taste of nettle soup. Then the word for green. Then green itself. She held the door with what remained, gripping her own unraveling edges the way you grip a rope over a long drop, and she kept saying the only thing left to say:

*Hold. Hold. Hold.*

* * *

The usurper came down the stair while the last of the souls were still pouring home.

He didn't run. Hollow men never run; running is a fear the living have. He walked into the heart-chamber with his borrowed face calm and his empty eyes full of a hunger so old it had worn grooves in him, and he stopped at the edge of the light and watched his stolen wealth fly away like starlings.

"You could stop this," he said. His voice was gentle. It had always been gentle; gentleness is cheap when you feel nothing. "One signature. Yours. Willingly given, and I let the rest go — keep only yours, keep only the strongest, and I let the mountain stand and the cities sleep and everyone live happily in the lie they built. You'd be rich beyond the councils' dreams. You'd be safe. You'd be *real*, girl — more real than anyone alive."

Ivo looked up at him through the last of the light, and what remained of her felt something she hadn't planned.

Pity.

Because she could see it now, plain as the blank page in the archive: he had signed first. Before the armies, before the courts, before any of it — he had stood in some room like this one and given his name away, and everything since had been a hollow man's hunger to feel real again. Every soul he'd taken, he'd taken the way a starving man eats pictures of food. And it had never worked. Not once in twenty years.

She reached into the light and drew out the deed she'd saved for last — his own, pulled from his coffer, bearing his true name in ink that still gleamed wet after two decades. She held it out to him.

"Here," she said. Her voice was thin now, most of it gone with the rest. "Take it back. Be small. Be mortal. Be hungry and tired and *someone*. It's not too late. It's never too late — that's the whole point of doors."

For one moment — one — the hunger in his face wavered into something almost human. His hand rose.

Then he looked at the name on the page, and whatever he saw there was smaller than he had become, and he could not bear it.

"No," he said, and his gentleness tore like paper. "No. I will not be *less*."

He struck the deed from her hand and drove his will into the mountain's bones, and the mountain obeyed him one last time. The white stone began to crack. The heart-light guttered. If he could not keep his kingdom, he would bury it — bury the door, bury the anchor, bury the girl who pitied him — and go down into the dark owning nothing, which was, Ivo understood dimly, all he had ever owned anyway.

"WREN!" she shouted, with the last voice she had. "GO!"

Hands caught her — gauntleted, bleeding, refusing. Wren hauled her out of the collapsing light with his arm around her ribs, and behind them the usurper stood in his dying court, arms spread, singing something with no music in it as the ceiling came down.

* * *

The stair was gone; they went up the miners' shaft instead, hand over hand, while the mountain groaned like a ship in a storm. Contracts burned in the chambers they passed, whole archives of theft going up in clean fire, and through the smoke Ivo saw — or the part of her that still saw — the freed souls flickering past in the rock like fish in a river, going home, going home, going home.

Her hands forgot how to grip. Wren tied her wrist to his belt with his cloak-cord and kept climbing.

Somewhere far below, the singing stopped.

Somewhere far above, there was a line of grey daylight, thin as a knife, and Wren climbed toward it dragging a woman who was mostly absence now, who had no name and no past and no reason to keep breathing except that he refused to let her stop.

"Stay," he was saying, over and over, into her hair, into the dust. "I know there's nothing left of you to hear it. Stay anyway. I'll do the remembering for both of us. I'll remember enough for ten people. Just — stay."

The daylight came down to meet them like a hand.

And in the last dark before it, in the place where Ivo Eastwick had been, something very small and very stubborn — the last crumb of a name, the part that had chosen this, the part no contract had ever listed — curled itself around Wren's voice, and held.
