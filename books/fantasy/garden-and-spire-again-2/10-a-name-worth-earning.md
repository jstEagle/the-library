## 10. A Name Worth Earning

The soulless woke in the order they had been sold.

That was how Sarelle explained it to Ivo, in the slow, patient voice of someone who had spent weeks learning to speak again. The fishwife from Merrowgate's harbor, sold first, woke first — sat up in her bed one grey morning and asked for salt, as if twenty years had been a nap. Then the cooper. Then the two brothers who had been sold together and had woken holding hands across the space between their beds, though neither could say why. Last of all, in ones and twos, the great names: the councilors' cousins, the merchants' wives, the children grown strange and tall without anyone noticing what was missing behind their eyes.

The mountain had let them go like water finding its level. No trumpets. No proclamation. Just people blinking awake in towns from Merrowgate to the coast, hungry, weeping, asking after relatives who had died believing them empty.

And the Council of Free Cities, Ivo noted over the top of her broth, was claiming every last one of them as its own doing.

"They've drafted a resolution," Wren said. He had brought the morning's broadsheet folded under his arm, and he laid it on the table with the flat distaste of a man handling something dead. "*The Council, acting through channels it is not at liberty to disclose,*" he read, "*secured the liberation of the wrongfully held.*" He looked up. "Channels. That's you. You're the channels."

"I'm the channels," Ivo agreed. Her voice still came out thin some days, like paper held to light. She wrapped both hands around the bowl and felt the warmth of it all the way into her wrists. "I'm also, according to page three, a rumor. A hedge-witch of no fixed name whose involvement has been *exaggerated by interested parties.*"

"That part's true," Wren said. "You don't have a fixed name."

She threw a crust of bread at him. He caught it out of the air without looking up, which was the sort of thing that had stopped surprising her around the second week.

Sarelle laughed from the doorway — actually laughed, a real sound with weight to it, and Ivo set down her spoon just to listen. Three months ago that laugh had been locked behind glass eyes in a merchant's front room, where Sarelle had been displayed among the porcelain like the finest piece in the collection. Now she stood in the kitchen of the little house above the tannery with flour on her hands and color in her face, and when she moved there was nothing borrowed about her at all.

"Eat," she told them both, in the tone of a woman who had lost twenty years and did not intend to lose another minute to nonsense. "Both of you. Wren, you have a trial."

The word dropped into the room and sat there.

* * *

They held the trial in the open air, in the customs yard below the council hall, because the council had done the arithmetic and decided that a public acquittal was worth more than a private conviction. Everyone understood this. Wren understood it best of all, standing on the platform in his old grey coat with the knight's badge torn off years ago, watching the same crowd he had once cleared from these streets with a truncheon now packed shoulder to shoulder to watch him be forgiven.

Ivo watched from the edge, hood back, letting herself be seen. That had been Sarelle's counsel: *Let them look at you. Let them see what came out of the mountain.* So she stood in the cold and let several hundred strangers study the face of the rumor from page three.

The charges were read. Desertion of post. Abandonment of sworn service. Aiding an unlicensed practitioner. Consorting with forces hostile to the lawful court beneath the mountain — and here the herald stumbled, because there was no longer a lawful court beneath the mountain, only a hollow full of burnt contracts and rubble, and everyone knew it, and the law had not yet caught up to the fact.

"How do you answer?" the magistrate asked.

Wren answered honestly, which Ivo had learned was the only way he knew how, even when honesty cost him. "I deserted," he said. "I broke my oath. I served a court that bought souls and called it peace, and I policed the streets of the cities that signed those contracts, and I did it for eleven years before I found the courage to stop." A murmur went through the yard. He let it pass. "I won't ask pardon for what I did. I'll ask only this: judge the man who came back down the miners' stair dragging a dying witch toward daylight, and if that man deserves hanging, hang him. But don't confuse him with the one who left."

Silence. Somewhere in the crowd a gull screamed, absurd and ordinary.

It was the fishwife — the first one woken, the harbor woman with salt-cracked hands — who settled it. She pushed forward through the press before any official could stop her, planted herself below the platform, and said in a voice built for shouting over wind: "This man carried my sister's deed out of the burning. Half the souls walking free today walked past him on the way out. If the law wants to hang him, the law can start by hanging us too, and good luck finding enough rope."

The crowd roared. The magistrate, a small dry man who had clearly rehearsed this moment and now watched it escape his control entirely, conferred briefly with the councilors on the bench, then raised his hand.

"The charge of desertion is set aside," he declared, "in recognition of services rendered to the free peoples. Acquitted." And then, quieter, almost humanly: "Imperfectly done. But done."

Justice outside the law, Ivo thought. It wasn't clean. It never would be. The council got its spectacle; the crowd got its mercy; Wren got his life back and no apology from anyone for the eleven years they'd let him rot inside his oath. But Sarelle had her laugh back, and the brothers had each other's hands, and somewhere a fishwife's sister was alive to shout down a courtroom. Imperfectly done. Done.

Afterward, in the churn of the dispersing crowd, Wren found her. His badge was still gone; he'd refused the offer to have it re-struck. "They want me to train the new watch," he said. "Reform it. Teach them what I learned the hard way instead of the easy way."

"Will you?"

He looked out over the yard, at the fishwife still arguing triumphantly with a council clerk, at the ordinary machinery of a city grinding back into motion. "Someone should," he said. "Might as well be a man who knows exactly where the rot starts." He glanced down at her. "And you? The council will come asking again. They want a story with a hero in it. Preferably one with a name they can put on a plaque."

"I'm working on that," Ivo said.

* * *

The names came back like scar tissue. That was Sarelle's phrase, and she would know: hers had returned in patches, a childhood nickname surfacing first, then her mother's voice saying it, then the smell of the house she'd grown up in, arriving whole one afternoon while she was kneading bread until she had to sit down on the floor with her arms around the bowl. The deep names came last. The ones other people had called you. The ones you had called yourself.

Ivo's were slower. Whatever she had paid in the mountain's heart, she had paid thoroughly — the archive had taken her name-soul entire, and what grew back grew crooked and new, like bone healing wrong but healing. Some mornings she woke knowing things about herself she hadn't known the night before: that she liked rain better than sun, that she had once hated her master's cat on principle, that the valley's silence had never frightened her, even at seven years old, even the first time. Other mornings there was nothing, just a clean white blank where a girl used to be, and she lay in it and breathed and waited.

Sarelle sat with her through the waiting. Neither of them spoke much during those hours. There was no need. They had already exchanged the only confession that mattered — Sarelle's whispered *don't come*, Ivo's coming anyway — and everything since had been aftermath, and aftermath, Ivo was learning, was mostly just company.

On the last day of autumn, the final piece arrived.

She was in the garden behind the tannery house, turning the frozen earth where Sarelle wanted herbs come spring, when it came: not a memory exactly, but a shape of self, arriving like a key turning. She straightened, spat the hair out of her mouth, and knew — suddenly, completely, the way you know your own hands — what she wanted to be called.

She went inside still wearing the dirt. Sarelle took one look at her face and set down the kettle.

"You've got it," she said. "All of it?"

"All of it." Ivo laughed, and the laugh sounded like someone, sounded like *her*. "There's a hole where the old name was. I can feel the edges of it. But the rest—" She spread her dirty hands. "The rest is mine. All of it mine. Nobody sold it, nobody wrote it in an archive, nobody offered it to me as a bargain. I get to choose."

"So choose," Sarelle said gently. "Out loud. Names need air."

Ivo stood in the warm kitchen, in the house that wasn't hers but was, among people bound to her by a dead man's map and a living woman's gratitude and a knight's stubborn loyalty, and she chose.

"Bramble," she said. "Ivo Bramble Eastwick. The bramble was the first plant my master ever taught me — worthless weed, she said, grows anywhere, fights back, feeds the birds nobody else bothers to feed." She grinned, and it felt like trying on a coat cut exactly to her shoulders. "I've decided to take it as a compliment."

"Bramble," Sarelle repeated, tasting it, and nodded once, decisively, the way she approved of bread dough. "It suits. It'll scratch anyone who grabs at you carelessly."

"That," said Bramble, "is the general idea."

The door banged open and Wren came in on a gust of cold air, snow in his greying hair, a bottle of something celebratory under one arm and a broadsheet under the other. He stopped when he saw their faces. "It happened," he said. Not a question. He'd been waiting on the doorstep of this moment for weeks, she realized, the way you wait outside a birthing room.

"It happened," Sarelle confirmed. "Say hello to Bramble."

Wren set down the bottle, crossed the kitchen, and shook her hand with grave formality, which lasted approximately two seconds before he pulled her into a rough hug that smelled of snow and woodsmoke and the long road east. "Bramble," he said into her hair. "Good name. Thorny."

Over his shoulder, through the frost-furred window, Bramble saw the boy from next door sprinting up the lane toward the tannery house, waving a paper, shouting before he even reached the gate — and the words, when they arrived, breathless and tumbling, landed in the warm kitchen like a stone dropped into still water:

"There's riders on the coast road — flying the old mountain colors — they're asking after the witch who burned the court — they say the mountain's *breathing* again—"
