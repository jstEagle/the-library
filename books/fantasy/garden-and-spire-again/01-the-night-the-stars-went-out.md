## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The Ledgerkeepers' Guild had exactly one rule that mattered, and it was not *be honest*. It was *record everything, judge nothing*. Honesty was a virtue for temples and children. We dealt in what was true, which was often a very different animal.

I had been a clerk of the guild for eleven years, and in that time I had learned three things: never ink an entry before you've counted it twice; never ask what a client intends to do with a purchased impossibility; and never, under any circumstances, volunteer during the annual audit.

I volunteered anyway. Master Corvane's hands had been shaking for a week—badly enough that he'd spilled tea on the March ledger, which for Corvane was roughly equivalent to weeping in public—and someone had to hold the tally-slates steady while he read out the year's balances. I told myself it was loyalty. In truth I think I already knew something was wrong, the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air, and I wanted to be standing next to the truth when it arrived.

The audit was held in the Vault of Records, a round chamber beneath the guildhall where every contract the guild had ever brokered slept in lead-lined drawers. The air tasted of cold iron and old paper. Forty clerks sat in concentric rings with their quills poised, and above us all hung the Founding Contract itself, displayed behind glass on its iron easel like a saint's fingerbone. It was the oldest document in the building. Older than the kingdom, some said. It was the paper on which the first truce between human and fae had been written down and made binding—the reason the border wards held, the reason the stars over the marches stayed lit as a covenant-fire visible to both realms.

Corvane read the balances in his dry, papery voice. Grain futures sold to a drought-stricken duchy: recorded. A widow's purchased hour of her dead husband's voice: recorded. Three years of borrowed luck sold to a racing stable at ruinous interest: recorded, judged nothing.

Then he reached the fourth ledger, and his voice did something I had never heard it do.

It stopped.

"Master?" I said, because someone had to.

He cleared his throat and read on, but I was already looking at the mirror-slate in front of me, where the audit figures were supposed to appear as he spoke them. The slate showed me what his voice would not: entries for starlight. Not candlelight, not moonlight, not any of the small luminances the guild legitimately traded. Starlight. Covenant starlight, drawn off the border wards themselves, sold in quantities that made no sense—barrels of it, tons of it, whole constellations' worth, quarter by quarter, for two years.

Two years. I had been keeping the secondary ledgers for two years. I would have known. I *should* have known.

Unless the entries had been written to look like something else. Unless they'd been hidden inside freight manifests and tithe schedules, dressed up as "clarified tallow" and "silver sand," routed through shell buyers whose names dissolved when I traced them backward, like footprints in snow.

My quill had gone still. Around me, forty other quills scratched on, oblivious. Corvane's eyes met mine across the rings of desks, and what I saw in them was not guilt. It was fear—the specific, terrible fear of a man who has just discovered the hole in his own books and does not know how deep it goes.

He mouthed one word: *later.*

There was no later.

* * *

It happened at the eleventh bell, while the audit was still grinding through the third ledger. The lamps guttered—not the way lamps gutter when there's a draft, but the way they gutter when something is drinking the light out of the room. Every clerk looked up at once. I felt it through the floor first: a hum in the lead lining of the vault drawers, a note too low to hear properly, like the building itself clearing its throat to scream.

Then the glass case above us cracked from corner to corner, and the Founding Contract went dark.

Not burned. Not torn. *Dark*, the way a window goes dark when the house behind it goes empty. The ink was still there—I could see it, faint as breath on glass—but the binding in it, the living thing that made the words law, had been drained out. Drawn off, piped away, sold, entry by entry, barrel by barrel, for two years, by someone using my guild as a spigot.

And overhead, muffled by six floors of stone but unmistakable to anyone who knew what they were hearing, the bells of the capital began to ring wrong. All of them. At once. The alarm peal reserved for the fall of the border wards.

We poured up the stairs and out into the courtyard, forty clerks and one master, and we stood in the rain and looked up, and the sky over the city was black in a way sky is not supposed to be black. No stars. Not dimmed—*absent*, as if someone had reached up and taken the pins out of heaven. To the north, toward the marches, there was a glow that was not dawn, because dawn was four hours away and this glow was the color of a wound.

Someone in the crowd behind me began to recite the guild's neutrality oath, voice cracking on every line. *We record. We judge nothing. We serve the balance.* It sounded, for the first time in my life, like a prayer being said over a corpse.

That was when we went back inside and found Master Corvane dead.

* * *

He was in his study, seated upright at his desk, which was the first wrong thing. The second was that there was no wound, no poison cup, no sign of anything except that his hands—those precise, unshakeable hands that had taught me to count twice—were folded neatly around a blank sheet of paper, as if he'd died mid-thought about something he couldn't bring himself to write.

The third wrong thing was the open drawer where the yearly ledgers lived.

Empty. All of them. Two years of entries, including every disguised sale of starlight, gone—as cleanly lifted as the binding out of the Founding Contract. Whoever had killed the stars had also killed my master and robbed the evidence, and they had done it inside a locked guildhall full of forty witnesses, none of whom had seen or heard a thing.

The senior clerks did what senior clerks do. They argued about jurisdiction. They sent a boy to fetch the city watch, then countermanded it for fear of scandal, then sent him again. Someone suggested the fae had done it, and someone else said the fae *couldn't* have done it, the wards were the fae's half of the bargain too, and everyone talked louder because talking loudly meant not thinking about the obvious question:

Who profits?

I stood in the doorway of Corvane's study and thought about the mirror-slate downstairs, and the entries I had already memorized without meaning to—eleven years of habit will do that, the numbers live in your head whether you invite them or not. Names of shell buyers. Dates. Quantities. A routing pattern that funneled everything, eventually, toward one destination in the capital's records district: the Office of the Royal Inquisition.

I am not a brave person. I want that on the record, since I was, at that moment, the only record left. I am a quiet clerk with ink-stained fingers and a bad knee, and everything in me wanted to sit down in the corridor and wait for the watch and let larger people sort it out.

But the guild's law was *judge nothing*, and I judged. For the first time in eleven years, I stood in my dead master's study and judged, and the judgment was this: whoever emptied those ledgers had murdered the sky, and they were going to come back for the only document that could prove how.

The Founding Contract still hung in the vault below, dark and drained—but drained things can be refilled. That was rather the point of the guild. Everything we ever traded, we traded on the principle that nothing impossible is ever truly lost, only moved.

So I took it.

Unhooking the case felt like blasphemy. Carrying it up the servant stairs felt like theft. Slipping out the postern gate into the rain, with the founding law of two realms wrapped in oilcloth under my coat and pressed against a heart going far too fast, felt like the first honest act of my entire career.

Behind me, the guildhall bells were still ringing their broken alarm. Ahead of me, the undercity waited, along with whatever buyer had grown fat on two years of stolen night.

I had spent my whole life recording what others did. Now the ledger was empty, the guild was masterless, and the only entry that mattered was the one I was writing with my own feet, running south through the wet streets:

*One founding contract, removed. One clerk, responsible. Judgment: pending.*

I just hoped I'd live long enough to close the account.
