## 2. A Debt Paid in Ash

The undercity of Vellsmark smells like a ledger nobody balanced—wet stone, tallow smoke, and the faint copper tang of debts that will never be collected. I had been down there for three days, and I'd learned two things: the smugglers' tunnels run truer than any royal road, and fear is a currency that inflates fast.

I should explain how I got to be a person who knows what smugglers' tunnels smell like. For eleven years I was a clerk. I sat in the third chair from the window in the Ledgerkeepers' hall and I recorded impossible things in very possible handwriting. A widow's grief, weighed and sold by the dram. A king's forgotten name, kept in trust. Starlight, once—a single vial of it, entered and exit-tallied, back when starlight was rare enough to be precious rather than merely gone.

The guild's law was neutrality. *We keep accounts; we do not spend them.* Master Ondrell said it so often it wore smooth as a river stone. And I believed him, right up until the night the stars went out and he turned up cold with his ledgers stolen and his hands folded like a man who'd finally finished balancing something.

Right up until I found the founding contract hidden in my own desk, with a note in his hand: *You always check the margins. Run.*

So I ran. With the contract sewn into the lining of my coat and the guild seal burning a hole in my pocket like a coin I couldn't spend.

* * *

Serah Vane did not run. Serah Vane walked.

She walked through the Inquisitors' Hall at dawn with her boots ringing on marble that had been polished by three centuries of other people's kneeling, and she read the report of the guild master's death without changing expression.

"Stolen ledgers," she said.

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"And the apprentice?"

"Missing. Felix Yarrow. Clerk, third rank. No family. No record of violence. Unremarkable."

Vane almost smiled. She had a face built for not smiling—the kind of handsome that made people confess just to fill the silence. "Unremarkable people don't steal founding contracts," she said. "Find them. Quietly. And find out who's been buying starlight on the black market before I do, because if it isn't me, we have a problem."

The junior inquisitor blinked. "Before you—"

"I've been buying starlight," Vane said, "on behalf of the Crown. Which is to say, on behalf of me, which is to say, on behalf of the Crown. The wards are down. The fae are coming. I intend to be ready." She closed the report. "That is all."

It was not all. It was never all with Vane. But the junior inquisitor wrote it down anyway, because that is what clerks do, and somewhere in the capital there was always another clerk writing down what power said, and calling it truth.

* * *

By the fourth day I found Maren.

Maren Cobb ran the Gutterlight, a tavern wedged under an aqueduct where the water hadn't flowed since before the truce, and she was the closest thing I had to a friend outside the guild, which is to say she tolerated me twice a year when I came to audit her smuggling accounts—which were immaculate, incidentally, because Maren believed crime should be conducted with professional standards.

"Felix Yarrow," she said, pouring me something that claimed to be wine. "You look like a man being audited by Death."

"Close. By an inquisitor."

"Same thing, worse manners." She leaned on the bar. "Word down here is the stars went out because somebody sold them. That true?"

I set the guild seal on the bar between us. Her eyes went flat and careful.

"I need passage through the deep tunnels," I said. "And I need to know who's been buying glowstock in quantities that don't make sense. Vials of starlight, moving through your channels."

"My channels move brandy and unlicensed prayer books."

"Maren."

She looked at the seal a long moment. Then she laughed, short and bitter. "Eleven years I keep clean books for you people, and now you come down here asking me to dirty mine. You know what neutrality gets a woman like me? It gets me ignored. Ignored is fine. Ignored is *safe*."

"It wasn't safe for Master Ondrell."

"No," she said quietly. "It wasn't." She took the seal, turned it over once, and slid it back. "There's a buyer. Big orders, no names, paid in royal scrip. Scrip like that only comes from one pocket. You want the drop point, it's the old candleworks off Tanner's Row. Tomorrow, midnight. I'll get you through the tunnels as far as the sluice gate. After that you're on your own, because after that, whatever you're doing stops being smuggling and starts being war, and I don't do war."

"You might already be in it," I said. "They burned the guild hall, Maren. Whoever has those ledgers knows every name the guild ever kept. Including yours."

Something moved behind her eyes—fear, quickly filed away under a more useful expression. "Then I'd best sell fast," she said, and poured herself a drink with a steady hand, and I loved her a little for it, and hated myself for bringing the war to her door.

* * *

Inquisitor Vane received her intelligence at dusk: the apprentice had surfaced, asking questions about starlight purchases, last seen entering the Gutterlight.

She read it twice. Then she took four men and no warrant, because warrants left paper trails, and paper trails were for people who intended to be honest later.

"The tavern keeper?" she asked.

"Known associate of the Ledgerkeepers. Smuggler. Minor."

"Minor," Vane repeated. She put on her gloves. "Everything is minor until it talks."

* * *

I went through the sluice gate at midnight with Maren's cousin Pell, who whistled while he walked, which in the deep tunnels is either courage or a way to not hear what else is down there. He left me at the ladder to Tanner's Row with a lamp, a knife I didn't know how to use, and the advice: "If it goes wrong, go to water. Water doesn't remember faces."

The candleworks stank of old tallow and newer money. Through a broken window I watched crates come in—dozens of them—and watched a man in an inquisitor's gray open one and lift out vials that caught light that shouldn't exist. Starlight. Hoarded, bottled, sold by the crate. Enough, if my margin-math was right, to fuel ward-anchors. Or siege-lanterns. Or a private army's march against a border that no longer existed.

Royal scrip. Maren was right. One pocket.

I was still doing the arithmetic of horror when the sky over Tanner's Row turned orange.

Not dawn. Dawn was hours off, and dawn doesn't scream.

I ran. I'm not proud of how fast I ran, or how little I thought about anything except the fire and the direction it was coming from, which was wrong, which was backward, which was the Gutterlight.

They'd nailed the doors shut first. I learned that later, from the survivors, from the ones who'd jumped into the canal with their clothes alight. What I saw was the roof going up like a struck match, and the crowd forming that particular circle crowds form around disaster—close enough to watch, far enough to be blameless.

Maren didn't come out.

Pell did, half-carried, his whistling days visibly behind him. He grabbed my collar with a blackened hand. "Gray coats," he rasped. "Nailed the doors. She told them she didn't know where you were. They—" His voice broke into coughing. "They said it was a message. *Neutrality protects no one.* That's what they said. Word for word."

I stood in the crowd holding a contract full of dead guild law, and I understood, finally, in the place where understanding becomes scar tissue: neutrality was never a shield. It was a price tag. It said *this person may be bought,* and someone had finally paid.

I had spent eleven years keeping accounts for a guild that believed standing apart would save it. The guild was ash. Maren was ash. The ledgers were ash, and every name in them was a debt now owed to no one, collectible by anyone.

Except the founding contract. Except me.

I walked to the canal's edge and let the water take some of the smoke smell out of my coat, and I opened the contract to its binding clause—that beautiful, terrible paragraph where the guild bound itself to hold all things in trust and spend none of them—and I read it the way you read a eulogy.

Starlight enough to relight the sky, hoarded in vaults I could find, if I could read the contract's ciphers. Held in trust. Never spent.

"Well," I told the dead water, the dead stars, the dead woman who kept immaculate books. "Someone's going to break that rule."

Behind me, at the edge of the firelight, something stepped out of a shadow that was deeper than the night had any right to make. Tall. Wrong. Beautiful the way a blade is beautiful.

It inclined its head, courteous as a creditor.

"Apprentice Yarrow," it said, in a voice like frost forming. "We should talk about what you intend to buy."

*I* intended to buy nothing. But the emissary smiled, and I understood that in its accounting, everything I had lost had a price—and someone, something, was ready to pay it.
