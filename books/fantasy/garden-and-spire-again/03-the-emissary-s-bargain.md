## 3. The Emissary's Bargain

I had been running for six days when I finally stopped running, which is the sort of arithmetic that would have gotten me expelled from the guild if the guild still existed to expel me from.

It wasn't a choice, exactly. The salt marshes east of Harrowgate end at a cliff, and cliffs are famously poor at negotiation. I stood at the edge with the founding contract pressed against my ribs inside its oilcloth wrapping, my boots sinking into black mud, and I thought: *well. This is where neutrality gets you. A cliff, a corpse-smell of burning in your memory, and no one left to invoice.*

That was when the tide went out all at once.

Not gradually — not the polite retreat of water minding its business. The sea simply *withdrew*, like a servant dismissed, and in the wet mirror it left behind I saw the moon standing in three places at once, and I knew, with the weary certainty of a clerk recognizing a forged seal, that I was about to have company.

"You've been difficult to find," said a voice like a bell rung underwater. "Or perhaps you've been easy to find and difficult to *catch*. With your kind it's often the same thing."

Ithuriel stepped out of the air the way other people step through doorways — unhurried, entitled, trailing a scent of cold iron and hawthorn blossom. The emissary wore a human shape the way a rich man wears borrowed clothes: flawlessly, and with the constant suggestion that they could take it off. Their eyes were the color of the sky before stars were invented.

"I wasn't hiding," I said. Which was true. I'd been *moving*, which any ledgerkeeper knows is different. "Hiding implies you matter enough to hide from."

"Wounded," Ithuriel said, delighted. "Good. Wounded things negotiate honestly." They gestured, and the mud beneath my feet firmed into a path of pale stone leading away from the cliff, toward a fire that hadn't been there a moment ago and now very much was, complete with two chairs and a kettle. Fae hospitality. It's real, and it's worse than the traps. "Sit. Drink. Let us discuss what you're carrying."

"I don't drink with—"

"With my kind? Yes. Your guild wrote whole chapters on it. Chapters I have read, incidentally, more carefully than your masters ever did." Ithuriel poured tea anyway, and the smell of it — honey and woodsmoke and something older, something like the first morning of the world — reached across the space between us and tugged at me like a hand on my sleeve. "Felix Yarrow. Last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers. Found abandoned on the guild steps in winter, raised among ledgers instead of lullabies. Never bonded, never named in any contract but the ones you witnessed for others. You spent twenty years recording other people's belonging and never once signing your own."

The tea trembled slightly in my hands. I hadn't picked up the tea. I looked down; I was holding the tea.

"That's not fair," I said.

"No," Ithuriel agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Fairness is a human invention. We prefer *accuracy*."

* * *

Here is what the fae offered me, as the tide stayed banished and the impossible fire burned without consuming anything, because even their kindness doesn't know how to leave things alone:

They wanted the contract. Not destroyed, like Vane wanted — Vane, whose smuggler-fires I could still smell whenever I closed my eyes, who had burned old Tam Wicker alive in his own boat and sent me the ashes in a neat little box with a royal seal on it, as if grief could be filed. No. Ithuriel wanted the contract *turned*.

"The founding covenant binds both realms," they said, pacing the firelight with the grace of something that had never once had to hurry. "But binding clauses cut both ways, little clerk. Your guild used them to hold the fae in check for four hundred years — to tax our crossings, to cage our names, to decide which of us counted as *persons* under truce-law. Do you know what we lost? Do you know how many of my people are written into your ledgers as *inventory*?"

I did know. I'd catalogued some of those entries myself, apprentice work, copying columns of captured names into the vault indexes. I'd told myself it was just record-keeping. It's amazing what you can tell yourself when someone else holds the pen.

"So help me unmake it," Ithuriel said, and knelt — *knelt*, an emissary of the Unseelie court, kneeling in the mud-light of a conjured fire — and took my hands, and their touch felt like being remembered. "Help me turn the binding clause against the humans who broke the truce first. Against Vane, who bought starlight to fund her slaughter. Against the guild masters who sold the sky out from under both our peoples. And when it is done—" their voice dropped to something almost gentle, almost kind, and that was the most dangerous thing about it, "—you will come with us. You will have a name. A place. A hearth that knows you're coming. You will never again be the one outside the circle, writing down what belonging looks like."

And here is the shameful truth, which I will set down here because I promised myself this account would be honest: I nearly said yes.

I want that understood. Not *considered*. Nearly *said*. My mouth opened around the word like a flower turning toward a sun that had gone out six days ago along with every other star, and Ithuriel saw it, and smiled, and waited, because patience is the cruelest weapon the fae carry.

Twenty years of watching other people sign contracts that gave them families, names, futures. Twenty years of sleeping in the guild dormitory where everyone else had a patron's crest over their bed and I had a blank wall. Twenty years of *sir, who shall I make this out to?* and the answer always, always being someone else.

"Say it," Ithuriel whispered. "Just say yes. The rest is paperwork, and you're so very good at paperwork."

And that — that was the moment. Not a grand revelation. A clerical one. Because I heard myself described as *good at paperwork* by someone who wanted me to write a binding clause against an entire realm, and I suddenly understood, with the flat clarity of a balanced column, what I was actually being handed.

A pen. And someone else's name already filled in above mine.

"It wouldn't stop with humans," I said quietly.

Ithuriel's smile didn't move, but everything behind it did. "Pardon?"

"The clause. Binding clauses don't discriminate — that's the whole point of them, that's why the guild guarded them. Turn it against humanity and it won't stop at Vane. It'll close crossings, cage names, decide which of *us* counts as persons. You'd be holding the same pen the guild held. You'd write inventory columns too. Maybe you'd even believe you were keeping records." I pulled my hands free. They let me, which frightened me more than if they hadn't. "You're not offering me justice. You're offering me a better seat in the same burning house."

For a long moment the emissary simply looked at me, and the sky-before-stars color of their eyes flickered through expressions no human face could hold — surprise, perhaps, or appetite recalibrating.

"You would choose nothing," they said at last, "over everything."

"I'd choose *unowned*," I said. "There's a difference. Ask your ledgers." Then, because I am apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone: "Oh wait. You can't. Someone stole them."

The fire went out. That was my answer, I suppose. The stone path dissolved back into mud, the tide came roaring home like it had never left, and Ithuriel's voice arrived from everywhere and nowhere, stripped now of all its honey:

"Then you will die owning nothing, Felix Yarrow. And Vane will reach the spire before either of us, and she will burn the contract, and the dark will be permanent, and your precious *neutrality* will be a footnote in no one's book."

"Not neutral," I said to the empty marsh, hugging the contract against my ribs. "Just not yours."

The moon snapped back into singular. Somewhere behind me, far inland, a ward-stone flared red against the clouds — the direction of the ruined spire, the last anchor of the border wards, glowing like a wound in the world.

Vane was already moving. So, apparently, was I.
