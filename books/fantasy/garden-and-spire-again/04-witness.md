## 4. Witness

The ward-spire had been beautiful once. I knew this the way you know things about a stranger's childhood—by inference, by the shape of what remained. What remained was a blackened finger of stone pointing at a sky with nothing left to point at, ringed by scaffolding that had rotted to lace, and surrounded by a moat of dead grass that crunched like old parchment under my boots.

I climbed it anyway.

Not because I wanted to. Because both of them were coming, and if the end of everything was going to happen somewhere, I preferred it to happen on guild property. Sentimental, perhaps. But the Ledgerkeepers had taught me that every transaction needs a venue, and I had decided—somewhere between the smuggler's pyre and Ithuriel's silken offer—that this one would be mine.

The founding contract rode against my chest in its oilcloth wrapping, warm as a sleeping animal. Three hundred years of clauses and covenants and careful, cowardly neutrality, all of it folded down to something I could hold in two hands. Master Aldous had died for less weight. Tam had burned for less. I intended to spend all of it.

* * *

Serah Vane arrived first, because of course she did. Inquisitors are punctual the way avalanches are punctual: inexorably, and without regard for anyone standing below.

She came up the ruined stair with her lantern held high and her officers fanned out behind her—six of them, hand-picked, which meant either loyal or bribed, and I'd learned in the undercity that the two categories overlap more than the Crown likes to admit.

"Apprentice Yarrow." Her voice carried the particular patience of someone who has already decided how your interrogation will go and is merely waiting for the paperwork. "You've made a great deal of trouble for a clerk."

"I'm told it's called an audit," I said. "Someone sold the stars, Inquisitor. I found the entries."

"And I bought them." She said it plainly, no shame in it, which was somehow worse than a denial. "Every coin of stolen light went into arming the border garrisons. Into keeping *them*—" a flick of her chin toward the dark horizon, where the fae realms lay like a held breath—"*out*. You think the truce protected anyone? It protected a lie. I am simply collecting on three hundred years of human debt."

"You burned Tam alive."

Something moved behind her eyes—not guilt, exactly. Bookkeeping. "He was a warning. Warnings work. You're here, aren't you? Instead of running?"

I was, and I hated that she'd scored the point. But then the grass at the spire's edge began to silver, frost blooming outward in a perfect circle, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath came out in ribbons, and we both turned to watch the second avalanche arrive.

* * *

Ithuriel did not climb the stairs. The fae emissary simply was suddenly present at the top of them, the way moonlight is suddenly present when clouds move—arrived without ever having been observed traveling. Their beauty was the kind that makes your teeth ache, all sharp angles and eyes like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Inquisitor," they said pleasantly. "Clerk. How convenient. We can settle this like civilized parties." A pause, delicate as a knife being set on silk. "Or like humans."

"The contract," Vane said, "belongs to the Crown."

"The contract," Ithuriel said, "was broken by the Crown. Sold starlight, dead wards, murdered keepers. By the old law, the fae realms are owed redress—and Felix Yarrow holds the instrument of collection." Those coin-bright eyes found me, and I felt the full weight of the offer they'd made me in the alley behind the Glasshouse: *a true place among us. Belonging. You have never belonged anywhere, little ledger-keeper; we can change that.* "Felix. You know what they did. Help us collect, and you will never be a stray again."

And there it was. Both hunters, both hands outstretched, both offering me the same thing wearing different clothes: *be our weapon and we will make you matter.*

I thought about the guild hall. About thirty years of learning to keep my head down and my ink dry, about Master Aldous saying *neutrality is the highest virtue, child—the moment you take a side, you become a tool.* He'd believed it right up until someone took his side away from him permanently.

I thought about Tam, who'd never been neutral a day in his life, and who had died grinning and shoving the contract deeper into my arms while the fire took him.

I opened the oilcloth.

"Felix," Vane said sharply, officers raising their crossbows. "Don't."

"Felix," Ithuriel said softly, "don't be foolish."

"It's funny," I said, unrolling three hundred years of binding clause across the ruined altar-stone at the spire's crown. "You're both offering me a place. Vane wants me to be the Crown's clerk. Ithuriel wants me to be the fae's witness. And neither of you has once asked what the contract actually says."

I had read it. Every night since the stars died, by candlelight, with the growing horror of a man reading his own obituary in fine print. And buried in the archaic phrasing, beneath the clauses of secrecy and hoarding and *let the light be kept, not given*, was the founding clause itself—the first promise, the one the guild had spent centuries burying under amendments:

*That which is taken from the sky shall be returned to the sky, freely, in the sight of both realms, by any keeper willing to bear witness.*

Any keeper. Not the master. Not the guild. Any keeper willing.

They'd written themselves an escape hatch into the founding document and then spent three hundred years pretending it wasn't there. Cowardice, or conscience—I genuinely couldn't tell. Maybe both. Maybe that's what conscience is.

"What does it say?" Ithuriel asked, and for the first time their voice wasn't smooth.

I struck the flint.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about burning a magical contract: it screams.

The vellum caught and the flame ran up the binding clause like fire through a dry field, and the sound it made was every secret the guild had ever kept, let out all at once. The hoarded starlight—decades of it, centuries of it, locked in vaults beneath the guildhall and in caches along the border—felt the clause die and remembered what it was for.

Light came up out of the ground.

It came up through the cracks in the spire, through the dead grass, through the soles of my boots, gold-white and relentless, and overhead the sky tore open along seams I hadn't known were there. One star. Then ten. Then a constellation uncoiling like a hand opening after years clenched.

Vane's officers lowered their crossbows. All six of them, staring upward, faces washed in returned light, and I watched Serah Vane realize in real time that her soldiers were looking at the sky instead of at her target, and that whatever arithmetic had kept them loyal was suddenly, publicly wrong.

"Lieutenant," she said. "Arrest the apprentice."

The lieutenant looked at the stars. Looked at her commander. "Inquisitor Vane," she said, in the flat voice of someone reading from a warrant she'd apparently been carrying for some time, "you're under arrest for treason against the realm's covenant. By order of the Crown. Which, it turns out, includes the sky."

The guild's vaults collapsed beneath the capital with a sound like a held breath finally released—a soft, vast exhalation, three hundred years of hoarded light going home. Somewhere across the city, I knew, the guildhall was settling into rubble, and I found, to my surprise, that I didn't grieve it. You can't grieve a lock once the door is open.

Ithuriel stood at the edge of the light, and their beauty had gone strange and still.

"You've unmade the truce," they said.

"No," I said. "I've unmade the *secret*. The truce is right here." I pulled the last thing from the oilcloth: a fresh ledger, blank pages, cheap binding, the kind Tam used to buy from the stationer on Coil Street. "New covenant. Open terms. No hoarding, no selling, no neutrality dressed up as virtue. Both realms sign. Anyone may read it. And it needs a witness who belongs to neither side—which, it turns out, is the only thing I've ever qualified for."

Ithuriel looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then they laughed—a sound like ice shifting on a spring river—and extended one impossible hand, and signed.

Vane, wrists bound, watched from the stairs with an expression I will be thinking about for the rest of my life: not hatred. Recalculation. As if she were already drafting the entry.

I signed last. Witness, not master. First line of the first honest ledger:

*Returned to the sky, freely, in the sight of both realms.*

The stars burned overhead, all of them, reckless and wasteful and free, giving their light away to absolutely everyone.

And that was when I noticed, at the bottom of the new covenant's page, a line of writing that hadn't been there when Ithuriel signed—a thin, patient script in ink the color of deep water, waiting beneath my name like a hook beneath bait:

*One witness remains to be chosen for the far shore.*

I looked up. On the horizon, where the border between realms had been a dead gray seam for three hundred years, something was glowing.

Someone, it seemed, had read the new terms already—and had opinions about who should deliver them.
