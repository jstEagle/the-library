## 1. The Courier on Cold Larch Pass

The mules hated Cold Larch Pass, and Quentin Ashgrove had learned to trust the opinions of mules above most men.

They stopped at the narrows where the wind funneled between the black stone walls and screamed like a widow, ears flat, nostrils flared at something up-slope. Quentin raised a fist and the caravan halted behind him — six sledges of untaxed brandy and one very nervous smuggler named Doss Marlowe, who was currently trying to light his pipe against the wind with all the success you'd expect.

"Something's wrong," Doss said.

"Something's always wrong. That's why you pay me."

"I mean *wrong* wrong."

Quentin didn't answer. He stood in the falling snow and listened the way he'd been taught to listen twenty years ago, in a garden that no longer existed, by men who no longer lived. The wind said nothing useful. But the mules were right about one thing: there was blood on the air, faint and iron-bright beneath the pine.

"Stay here," he said. "If I'm not back in a quarter hour, take the low fork and don't wait."

"And if the hunters come?"

"Then be somewhere else when they do."

He climbed alone, boots crunching, hand resting easy on the hilt of the sword wrapped in oilcloth across his back. He hadn't drawn it in four years. That was rather the point of it.

* * *

He found the man thirty paces off the trail, half-buried in a drift, as if the mountain had tried to swallow him and lost interest halfway through.

He'd been riding hard — the horse lay dead ten feet beyond him, legs folded wrong, steam still rising off its hide. Arrow wound through the thigh, feathered in grey goose-fletching. Quentin knew that fletching. Everyone in Thornfell knew it. Crown work.

The man was young. Twenty-five, maybe, with a courier's lean frame and frost in the stubble of his jaw, and he was still alive, which was frankly rude of the mountain. His eyes found Quentin and fixed on him with the desperate clarity of someone who has decided that dying can wait until after the errand.

"You're real," the man rasped. "Good. I've been arguing with a tree for an hour and losing."

"That's usually how I'm greeted." Quentin knelt in the snow. "Who shot you?"

"Crown riders. Black cloaks." A wet laugh. "Witch-hunters who couldn't tell a witch from a wren. They wanted what I carry." His hand moved — slow, trembling, deliberate — to the satchel strapped against his chest, and Quentin's old instincts woke and snarled at him to stand up, turn around, walk away, forget this ever happened.

He ignored them. That was becoming a bad habit of his.

"What do you carry?" he asked, though some cold part of him already knew he shouldn't have.

"A map." The courier fumbled the satchel open with fingers going grey at the tips. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen gone dark with blood, was a scroll sealed with a stamp Quentin recognized and had spent thirteen years hoping never to see again: the crowned stag of Vaelmoor, pressed deep in red wax. Royal sigil. Royal business. Royal trouble, which was the worst kind, the kind that got whole valleys burned.

"No," Quentin said quietly. "No, friend. Whatever this is, find someone else. Find a priest. Find a fool."

"There are no priests left who'd take it." The courier pressed the bundle into Quentin's hands anyway, because dying men are strong in exactly the ways living men are weak. "And you're no fool. You're swordspeaker. I saw your hands."

Quentin looked down at his own gloved fingers and felt something twist under his ribs, old and unwelcome, like a door opening in an empty house.

"You're mistaken," he said. "I guide smugglers. It's honest work, or near enough."

"It's the thirteenth turn." The man grabbed Quentin's wrist with sudden shocking strength, pulling him close, breath sour with blood and fear. "Do you hear me? Thirteen turns since they bound him. Thirteen new moons, and the last one comes at midwinter. Whatever's wearing the crown — it finishes eating before spring. The map shows the way. The Spire. You have to—" A cough took him, deep and ruinous, and when it passed there was red on his lips and less of him somehow, as if the cough had carried away a piece. "You have to go where I couldn't."

"I don't have to do anything." Quentin heard his own voice go flat and cold, the voice he used on bandits and tax collectors. "I'm not an oath-man anymore. There is no order. There is no oath. There's a pass full of brandy and a man named Doss waiting on me, and none of it concerns a dead king."

"The king isn't dead." The courier smiled, and it was the worst smile Quentin had ever seen, bloody and certain. "That's rather the problem."

The wind screamed down the narrows. Somewhere below, a mule complained, and Doss shouted something cheerful and profane about the cold.

"Your name," Quentin said, because it seemed suddenly important.

"Hale. Wren's my daughter. She'll come looking. She looks for everything." His grip loosened. "Give her— no. Don't give her anything. She'll want the map. Don't let her have it until she understands what it costs. Make her understand." His eyes were going distant now, fixing on something over Quentin's shoulder, or perhaps inside it. "Thirteenth turn. Midwinter. Swordspeaker, you were made for this. You don't get to stop being made."

"I stopped years ago."

"Then start again." Hale's voice dropped to a whisper, almost gentle, almost amused. "An oath doesn't die because you stop feeding it. It just gets hungry."

And then he died, there in the snow, with the wind keening over him and the royal stag crushed between their two sets of fingers, and Quentin Ashgrove sat in the drift beside him for a long moment, feeling the hungry thing stir in his own chest and wishing, with a sincerity that surprised him, that he had argued with the tree instead.

* * *

Doss met him at the bottom of the trail with his pipe finally lit and his face arranged into an expression of elaborate unconcern, which on Doss meant terror.

"Well?" he said. "Dead?"

"Very."

"His? Ours?"

"Just his. For now." Quentin walked past him toward the sledges, the bloodstained bundle tucked inside his coat, heavy as a sin. "We move. Low fork, double pace. We camp at Greywater and nowhere else."

"We were camping at Greywater anyway."

"Then we camp there faster than planned."

Doss sucked his pipe thoughtfully. "You found something on him, didn't you. Something the black cloaks wanted."

Quentin considered lying. He was good at lying; it was the second trade he'd mastered after killing. But Doss had followed him through three winters of passes without asking questions, and there was a debt in that, small but real.

"I found trouble," Quentin said. "Crown-shaped trouble. If witch-hunters ask after me, you never guided me. You hired a quiet man named Ash who left for the coast."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you're either braver than you look or stupider than you look, and either way I'll worry about you."

Doss grinned around his pipe stem. "You worry about everyone. It's your worst quality. Right after the brooding."

"Move the sledges, Doss."

The caravan groaned into motion, mules balking then relenting, runners hissing over the packed snow. Quentin took point as always, and let the rhythm of the climb settle over him — step, breathe, listen; step, breathe, listen — the oldest discipline he had, older than the order, older than the oaths. It almost worked. It usually did.

But the bundle rode against his heart like a second heartbeat, and every time the wind gusted he heard the courier's whisper threaded through it: *an oath doesn't die because you stop feeding it.*

He had buried his oaths at nineteen, in a garden full of ash, alongside forty-one better men. He had dug that grave himself, with his bare hands, and he had sworn — a real oath, the only one he'd kept since — never to dig another.

Now here came a dying stranger pressing a king's seal into his gloves, talking of turns and moons and a hunger wearing a crown, and Quentin could feel the old grave soil shifting, feel something green and stubborn pushing up through it whether he watered it or not.

At the Greywater camp, while Doss and the others slept, he unwrapped the map by firelight. Just to look. Just to know what would kill him if he let it.

It was beautiful work — fine vellum, ink gone brown with age, mountains rendered in strokes so confident they seemed carved rather than drawn. It showed the deep Thornfell, the places even smugglers' maps marked only with warnings. And past them, in a fold of the high country where no road should be, a single tower drawn in silver: a spire, hollow at its heart, ringed in symbols Quentin had learned as a boy and prayed he'd forgotten.

Beneath it, in a cramped, urgent hand, someone had written: *the binding holds while the moon wanes. Thirteen. No more.*

Quentin stared into the fire for a long time.

Then, very carefully, he rolled the map closed, tied it, and told himself — firmly, reasonably, in the voice of a sensible man — that in the morning he would sell it, or burn it, or bury it in the deepest scree of the pass, and go back to brandy and silence and the comfortable weight of doing nothing.

Behind him, far down the southern slopes, a hunting horn sounded once and fell silent.

The mules, awake now, all turned their heads toward the sound.

Quentin reached for his sword.
