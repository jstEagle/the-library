## 2. What the Map Refuses to Show

The town of Brackenhollow sat at the bottom of the pass like a coin dropped in a beggar's cup — small, worn, and grateful for whatever fell into it. Quentin had been coming here for six years, long enough that the innkeeper knew to water his ale and not to ask why he always took the room facing the stable yard. Long enough that he'd stopped flinching when people called him *Quentin of Nowhere*, which was the name he'd given them and the only one that had never cost him anything.

He paid for three nights in advance and told Marta the innkeeper he'd drunk too much sour wine on the road and needed sleep. This was a lie so comfortable it was nearly true.

Then he locked the door, wedged a chair under the handle, and unrolled the dead man's map.

* * *

It was good work. That was the first thing he noticed, and it annoyed him, because good work meant someone important had made it, and important things were trouble wearing a better coat. The vellum was fine-grained, the ink still dark after thirteen years or more, and the cartographer had drawn the Thornfells with a lover's attention — every ridge, every scree slope, every place where the mountains pinched a traveler between stone and sky.

Quentin knew those places. He'd carried smugglers' brandy through half of them.

But the map didn't show smugglers' roads. It showed something older. A route that began at Cold Larch Pass — where the courier had died with blood freezing in his beard — and wound north and east, past landmarks Quentin had never seen marked on any chart: *the Weeping Stair. The Door That Isn't. The Garden Where Nothing Grows.*

His thumb stopped on that last one.

The garden where nothing grows. He'd heard that phrase before, in another life, in a voice like cold water. He'd sworn, along with everyone else who ever held a sword and spoke to it, that if he ever heard that phrase again he would run in the opposite direction until his boots wore through.

Because there was only one garden that nothing grew in, and it stood at the foot of the Hollow Spire, where the swordspeakers had gone to die and hadn't come back.

"No," he said aloud, to the map, to the room, to the dead courier whose face he could still see behind his eyes. "No, you stupid brave fool."

The map, being vellum and ink, declined to argue. It simply lay there, patient as a debt, showing him the way to the one place in Vaelmoor he had spent thirteen years making sure never to look at too directly, the way you don't stare at a hole in the floor for fear your feet get ideas.

And there, folded into the corner where the mountains gave way to the northern waste, was the Spire itself. Drawn small. Drawn carefully. With a little annotation in a cramped, educated hand:

*Where the king sleeps. Thirteen turns. Do not let it wake hungry.*

Quentin sat back on the bed and listened to his own heartbeat, which was doing something unsoldierly.

*Thirteen turns.* The courier's last whisper. *Before the thirteenth turn.* Thirteen years since the old king of Vaelmoor had ridden north with his honor guard and come back — changed, they said. Quieter. Hungrier-eyed. People said a lot of things about kings; most of it was the wind talking. But Quentin had been in a tavern in Vaelmoor's capital two winters past, keeping his head down over his cups, and he had seen the king across a crowded festival square, and he had felt his sword-hand ache the way it ached near things that were wrong. The way it used to ache near certain kinds of magic, back when he'd still answered to an order and an oath instead of to coin and silence.

He'd told himself it was the wine. He'd been telling himself things for thirteen years. He was very good at it. It was practically a craft.

He turned the map over.

There was a seal pressed into the vellum's corner, wax gone amber with age, cracked down the middle. Quentin tilted it toward the candlelight and felt the floor tilt with it.

A crowned tower above crossed keys. The royal sigil of Vaelmoor.

Not a forgery — he'd forged enough seals in his smuggling days to know the difference, the way a counterfeit coin betrays itself by being too perfect. This one had the small honest flaws of the real thing: a bubble in the wax, a thumbprint at the edge. Someone in the palace itself had made this map. Someone who had known what slept — or was kept — at the end of the road, and had wanted a way to it written down.

Someone who was probably dead now, because the courier carrying their work had died on Cold Larch Pass with witch-hunters' arrows in his back.

Which raised the question Quentin had been avoiding since he'd knelt in the snow: *who else knew the courier had the map?*

The answer arrived, as answers do, in the form of hoofbeats in the street below.

* * *

They came into Brackenhollow an hour before dusk, four riders in grey-and-crimson, and even before Quentin looked out the window he knew what they were, because the whole town went quiet the way a household goes quiet when the father comes home drunk.

Witch-hunters. The crown's own. Grey cloaks, crimson sashes, and faces that had forgotten how to do anything but watch. Quentin counted them from behind the shutter: two dismounting at the inn door, two walking the street slow, reading the town like a ledger of sins.

He heard Marta greet them with the bright, brittle voice innkeepers keep for tax collectors and worse. He heard boots on stairs. He did not hear them come up to his floor — yet.

So Quentin Ashgrove did the thing he was best at, which was not swordspeech, which was not heroism, which was not any of the grand doomed things his order had died doing. He did arithmetic.

One: they were asking questions, which meant they didn't know exactly who had the map. If they knew, they wouldn't ask; they'd kick doors.

Two: the courier had died alone on the pass, which meant either he'd traveled alone — unlikely, couriers didn't — or whoever was with him had run. Or been taken.

Three: Quentin had maybe an hour, possibly less, and one bag, and a decision that had been waiting thirteen years for him to stop running from it.

He looked at the map. The map looked back, in its way.

"You're not my problem," he told it. "You understand that? I guide smugglers. I don't save kingdoms. Kingdoms are what got my order killed, and nobody thanked us, and the ones who did the killing put up statues afterward." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, and kept going anyway. "I'm nobody. Quentin of Nowhere. That's the whole trick. That's how I'm still alive."

Downstairs, a chair scraped. Boots crossed the common room. Marta's voice, higher now.

Quentin moved.

He pulled the loose board under the floorboards — every room he ever rented longer than a night got one, an old habit from the order's fieldcraft, the only inheritance he'd kept — and took out the packet he'd sworn he'd burn a hundred times and never had. His commission papers, signed by a Hand of the Order of the Speaking Sword. His oath-scroll, the words still sharp enough to cut. Letters from brothers and sisters in the blade, some in hands that had gone cold years ago. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing he'd told himself was sentiment and knew was cowardice: his spurs. Swordspeaker's spurs, silver, stamped with the tower-and-key of Vaelmoor's old covenant, back when the order and the crown had trusted each other, which is to say, back before everything.

He fed the papers to the hearth first. They caught quickly, the way paper does when it has waited long enough. The oath-scroll burned blue-green at the edges — old magic in the ink, waking just enough to notice it was dying — and Quentin watched it go and felt nothing, which was itself a kind of grief.

The spurs he held a moment longer. Then he threw them in after, and the fire made a sound almost like a word, and he decided not to have heard it.

"There," he said quietly. "Now I'm really nobody."

It should have felt like freedom. It felt like amputation.

Below, the boots were on the stairs now. Two sets. Heavy. Unhurried, because witch-hunters were never hurried; hurry implied urgency, and urgency implied fear, and they never feared anything except being laughed at.

Quentin opened the window onto the stable-yard roof, slung his bag across his chest, and paused with one leg out into the cold.

Because here was the thing, the thing he'd been circling since the pass, the thing underneath all the arithmetic: he could leave the map. Roll it up, tuck it under the mattress, let the hunters find it, let it be their problem. Whatever slept at the Hollow Spire had slept thirteen years without him. The world had gotten along fine being lied to. Lies were load-bearing; he'd built his whole life on them.

But the courier had chosen *him*. Out of everyone on that pass — the smugglers, the mule-drivers, the hired blades — a dying man had crawled through snow to press a sealed map into the hands of a stranger and whisper *before the thirteenth turn*, and he'd done it because dying men see truly, and what he'd seen in Quentin's face was not Quentin of Nowhere. It was the other one. The one with the oaths.

And the seal on that map was the sigil Quentin had burned into his skin at nineteen, the sigil on the spurs now curling in the grate, the sigil of a king who might be a prisoner in his own body while his kingdom smiled and sang and called it peace.

"Damn it," Quentin said, with feeling. "Damn it all."

He rolled up the map, tucked it inside his coat against his heart, which felt less like hiding it than like feeding it, and climbed out onto the roof as the knock came at his door below.

"Marta says you're within," called a voice like a file drawn across iron. "Master Ashgrove. We only wish to ask about a man on the pass. We won't keep you long."

*We know your name,* the voice didn't say, but meant. *We've always known your name.*

Quentin crossed the stable-yard roof on silent feet, dropped into the straw behind the far wall as the door upstairs splintered, and went over the back fence into the blue dusk while the town's dogs, bless their honest hearts, barked at everything and therefore nothing.

* * *

He was three streets gone, hood up, walking at the pace of a man with nowhere particular to be, when he felt it — the prickle at his neck, the ache in his sword-hand, the old warning his body had never unlearned.

Someone was following him. Not a hunter. Hunters announced themselves; fear was their tool. This was quieter. Lighter footsteps. A shadow that matched his turns a half-beat too well, that slipped from lamplight to lamplight with a professional's patience.

Quentin turned down the lane behind the tannery, where the smell covered everything and the shadows pooled deep, and spoke without stopping, low and even.

"If you're a hunter, you're the worst one I've ever met. If you're a thief, you've picked a poor mark. And if you're neither—" he let his hand rest on the knife at his belt, an honest threat from an dishonest man, "—then you've been following me since the bridge, and I'd very much like to know why."

The shadow at the end of the lane stepped forward into the light of the last lamp, and Quentin saw a young woman, wiry and sharp-eyed, with her sleeves pushed up and a knife of her own held low and easy, and on her bare forearm, dark as ink, a tattoo of interlocking symbols that made his stomach drop through the cobbles.

Half a cipher key. He'd know it anywhere. He'd spent the evening learning the lock it belonged to.

"You've got my father's map," she said.
