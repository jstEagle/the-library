## 3. Wren of the Nine Names

Wren had nine names, and she wore them the way other people wore coats — one for every kind of weather.

There was Wren Vael, born in a courier's saddlebag on the high road to Marrowgate. There was Wren the Quick, who'd picked her first lock at six because her father's hands were shaking too badly with fever and someone had to get the medicine out of the strongbox. There was Lark, who sang in taverns for coppers and listened with both ears. There was Mistress Fenwick of the Fenwick Trading Concern, who existed entirely on paper and had excellent credit in three towns. There was the name she'd been christened with by the river-priestess when she was nine and frightened, which she kept folded away like good linen, saved for occasions that mattered.

And there was the name she was using tonight: nobody. A shape in the rocks above Cold Larch Pass, cold to the bone, watching a man make camp badly.

He was big — that was the first thing. Not tall so much as *settled*, the way old trees are settled, with shoulders that suggested he'd once carried things heavier than regret. He moved around his fire like a man who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more, which meant he wasn't running. Running men built fires differently — small, shielded, guilty. This one's fire sat out in the open like it owned the mountain.

That annoyed her. Her father had died building a fire exactly that unafraid, if the story the dying mule-driver told was true. *They found him on the pass road, girl. Knife in the ribs and a smile on his face, like he'd finally delivered something worth the postage.*

The map was in that man's pack. She was sure of it. Sure the way you're sure of your own heartbeat — not because of evidence but because everything else would be unbearable.

Her father, Tam of the Nine Roads, had carried messages across Vaelmoor for thirty years without ever once asking what was in them. That was the courier's covenant: *the seal is not mine to break, the words are not mine to know.* He'd lived by it. Wren had assumed he'd die by it, eventually, peacefully, of too much pipe smoke.

Instead he'd broken the covenant for the first time in his life — opened a sealed royal dispatch on the road from the capital, read whatever was inside, and then spent his last week of life riding hard enough to kill two horses, hiding half of something in the only place no hunter would ever think to look.

On his daughter. In ink. Under her skin, in the old courier cipher, wrapped around her left forearm in a band of fine-lined characters that looked, at a glance, like a decorative bracelet. It had hurt like a bastard. She'd cried exactly once, and only because her father had held her hand while the needle-woman worked and told her jokes in the voice he used when he was terrified.

*Half a key,* he'd said. *The map has the rest. Find the man with the grey eyes and the swordspeaker's calluses. He'll have it whether he wants to or not.*

She'd found him. Grey eyes, check. Calluses in the wrong places for a laborer and the right places for a blade, check. And a way of moving through a campsite that made her professional pride sit up and take notice — nothing wasted, nothing shown, everything accounted for except the thing she wanted.

Wren checked her knives. Checked the wind. Checked the lie she'd prepared, which was a good one, involving a lost brother and a reward poster, and discarded it, because she'd watched this man for two days now and lying to him felt like bringing a candle to argue with a bonfire.

So: the truth, mostly. Truth with the serial numbers filed off. It was amazing how far truth could travel if you changed its clothes.

* * *

She dropped out of the rocks when he was banking the fire, landing soft on the pine needles eight feet behind him, which should have been impossible and knew it.

"Don't turn around," she said. "Or do. I've got a knife at your spine either way, and honestly the knife doesn't care about your feelings."

He didn't turn around. He also didn't tense up, which was somehow worse. "You've been following me since Tarnholt," he said. His voice was low and gravelled, like a road in early spring. "You stepped on a jay's nest the second morning. The bird complained about you for an hour."

"That bird," Wren said, "is dead to me."

"You slept in a hollow under the split birch. You ate half your rations the first day, which means you planned for three days and you're now into improvisation." He set down the fire-iron and turned, slowly, hands loose and visible, and looked at her with those grey eyes that catalogued things. "You're Tam's girl."

The world went very quiet and very sharp. "You knew my father?"

"I carried with him twice, years back. Thornfell run, before the tolls went up." Something crossed his face — brief, quickly buried, but she was a professional and she caught it. Grief. Real grief, filed under a man who didn't do real grief where it could be seen. "I'm sorry. He was the best courier on the nine roads. Never asked what he carried."

"No," Wren said. "He stopped asking. Right at the end, he started again." She let the knife drift down an inch. "You have something of his. A map. Sealed in oilcloth, bloodstained on the corner, about so long." She measured it with her free hand. "He pressed it into your hands on Cold Larch Pass and you took it, and now it's in your pack, and I'm here to take it home."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we're going to have a much less pleasant conversation."

He studied her the way she imagined scholars studied dangerous old books. "You have his chin," he said. "And his habit of answering questions with threats."

"It's called *inheriting a legacy.*"

Something happened to his mouth that on another man might have been the beginning of a smile. On him it was more like the memory of one, kept in a drawer somewhere. "Quentin Ashgrove," he said.

"I know. You're the last swordspeaker. You're also supposed to be dead, or drunk, or both, depending on which veteran I bribe." She tilted her head. "Funny thing about that, Ashgrove. My father breaks his life's covenant to carry one message, gets a knife for it, and hands the prize to a man whose entire profession was *truth*, and who now spends his days pretending to be nobody. You two must have gotten along famously."

The smile-memory closed its drawer. "What do you want, girl?"

"Wren. And I want what's mine."

"The map isn't yours."

"The key is." She pulled back her sleeve in one motion and thrust her arm into the firelight — the band of cipher characters inked around her forearm, fine as spiderwork. "Half of it, anyway. Tattooed by needle and lamp-black while my father held my hand and pretended the jokes were funny. Whatever's on that map, it needs both of us to read. So you can give it to me, or you can keep carrying a locked box and pretend that counts as freedom. Your choice. I hear smugglers love choices."

For a long moment he said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which Wren felt was relatable.

Then Quentin Ashgrove did the last thing she expected. He reached into his coat, slow and deliberate, and drew a knife.

Not at her. He flipped it, caught it by the flat, and tossed it to her feet. "If we're doing this," he said, "do it properly. Come at me like you mean it, and I'll know what you are. Or take the bargain like a professional, and we'll skip the part where we both get stitches."

"What bargain?"

"You keep your arm. I keep the map. We read it together, because neither of us can read it alone, and because—" he paused, and she watched him decide to say the true thing instead of the safe one, watched it cost him visibly, "—because whoever killed your father will come looking for what he carried. They'll find me eventually. I'd rather they find two of us."

Wren looked at the knife in the dirt. Then at the man. Then at the knife again, because looking at the man was getting complicated.

"You realize," she said, "that this is the part where I tell you I don't work with partners."

"You realize," he said, "that I heard you rehearsing a lie about a lost brother for the last two days. You mutter when you scout. It's a wonder you're alive."

"I do *not*—"

"*'Dear brother Aldous, alas'*," he recited, in a fair imitation of her voice, pitched higher, which she chose to find insulting rather than accurate. "You were going to lead with *alas*. Nobody leads with alas. I'd have known inside a minute."

Heat climbed her neck, and Wren — Lark, and Mistress Fenwick, and the name folded in linen, and all nine of her — discovered she was, against every rule she owned, grinning. "Fine," she said. "Together. But I set the terms. One: I touch the map first each day. Two: when we find whoever killed my father, I get five minutes alone with them."

"Four."

"Five, and I don't ask what a swordspeaker's oath actually says, because I suspect it's embarrassing."

His jaw did something complicated. "Four minutes," he said, "and yes. It's embarrassing."

She scooped up his knife, weighed it — good balance, older than it looked, cared for the way poor people care for the few things they love — and slid it into her belt as a down payment on trust. Then she held out her tattooed arm, firelight crawling along the cipher lines.

"Read it, Ashgrove. Let's see what my father died carrying."

He took her wrist gently, turned her arm to the light, and went still. All the wariness drained out of his face at once, replaced by something that made her stomach drop — not fear exactly, but the particular dread of a man recognizing handwriting on a wall.

"This isn't a cipher key," he said quietly. "It's a warning. Half of one." His thumb traced the final character. "The thirteenth new moon. It's in four months, Wren. And this line here—" he tapped a cluster of marks she'd always assumed was decorative filler, "—says the king of Vaelmoor is already dead. Thirteen years dead. And something else is wearing his crown, and it eats a little more of him every turn of the moon."

The owl asked its question again. This time, Wren thought, somebody ought to answer.

"So," she said, when she trusted her voice. "Which way to the Hollow Spire?"

Quentin looked east, toward the white silence of the high country, and she saw the exact moment the man who wanted to be nobody lost the argument with himself.

"Three days north," he said. "We walk tonight."
