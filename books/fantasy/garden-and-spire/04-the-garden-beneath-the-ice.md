## 4. The Garden Beneath the Ice

**Quentin**

The map had been lying to them for three days.

Not lying exactly — Quentin had learned the difference over years of reading smugglers' charts, where a lie was a road drawn through a gorge that flooded every spring, and a *truth withheld* was a blank space where a valley should be. This map did the second. It showed the Thornfell range folding north past the Grey Stair, and then it simply stopped, as if the cartographer had run out of ink, or nerve.

"It's hiding something," Wren said, for the fourth time that morning. She walked with her hands jammed in her armpits against the cold, which did nothing for her dignity and everything for her warmth. "Maps don't get shy, Ashgrove."

"This one does." He turned the parchment over again. In certain light — low light, the kind that came just before dawn or just after regret — faint lines surfaced in the blank quarter like bruises rising under skin. A valley. A road. And at its heart, a small careful symbol he hadn't let himself look at directly since the pass: a sword planted hilt-up in a garden bed.

The mark of the Order of the Green Blade. His order. The one that no longer existed because he was very good at not existing either.

"You've gone quiet," Wren observed. "Quieter. Which for you is basically a eulogy."

"I'm deciding whether to tell you something."

"Decide faster. My toes are filing for independence."

He almost smiled. That was the trouble with her — she kept almost making him smile, and smiles were doors, and doors were dangerous when you'd spent six years nailing yours shut. But the valley was coming whether he told her or not, so he told her.

"There was an order," he said. "Swordspeakers. We didn't fight with steel first. We spoke to blades — woke the metal, asked it to remember what it was forged for. Most swords only remember fire and hammering. Ours remembered *purpose*." He said it flatly, the way you recite a lesson you hated. "There was a monastery where we trained. Deep in Thornfell. The crown burned it eleven years ago. I wasn't there. I was running a message south, which is why I'm the last of us instead of none of us."

Wren was silent for a long moment, which from her was practically a state funeral.

"That's why the courier gave it to you," she said finally. "Not because you're a guide. Because you're the only one alive who can read what's at the end of this road."

"Because I'm the only one alive who'll hate walking it," Quentin corrected. "Which is nearly the same thing."

Ahead of them, the mountain opened.

It happened between one switchback and the next: the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, and the rock simply wasn't there anymore — replaced by a gap in the peaks, a hidden throat of a valley floored with old snow and, impossibly, green. Even from the height they could see it: a smudge of living color in all that white and grey, sheltered by walls of stone on three sides, warmed by some deep vent of the mountain's blood. The map's blank space, refusing to be blank any longer.

Wren whistled. "Your monastery picked a nice spot to die."

"The garden survived," Quentin said quietly. "The monastery didn't." He started down. "Stay close. And Wren — whatever you see down there, don't touch the rosemary."

"Why not?"

"Because it remembers me, and it holds grudges."

* * *

**Wren**

She had followed nine names across half the borderlands — Nine Names being what she called the false identities she'd worn since her father first taught her to pick a lock at age seven, back when he still came home between courier runs and smelled of road dust and honey cakes. She'd been a merchant's ward, a tax clerk, a nun for one deeply confusing week. She was good at becoming other people.

So it unsettled her, descending into that impossible green valley, how much she wanted to stop performing.

The garden was a wound that had decided to heal anyway. Broken cloister walls ringed it, their pale stone furred with moss, their arches open to the sky like missing teeth. Snow lay at the edges and melted at the center, where raised beds ran in neat rows — herbs, winter greens, bare rose canes sleeping under straw. A stone fountain stood dry at the heart of it all, and around the fountain, worn into the flagstones by centuries of feet, were words in Old Vaelic.

*We speak, therefore the blade listens,* Wren translated slowly, tracing the letters with her eyes. Her father had made her learn dead languages the way other fathers taught fishing. *What did that even mean?*

Quentin had stopped walking. He stood at the gate with his hand on the latch and his face doing something complicated, the way a man's face does when a house he burned down turns out to have been rebuilt by someone else.

"You knew this place," she said. Not a question.

"I grew up here." His voice came out sanded smooth, like wood rubbed too long. "From age six. Mother Sable taught me letters. Brother Aldous taught me the first forms of swordspeech — you don't speak to a blade with your mouth, you speak with—" He stopped. Started again. "It doesn't matter. It burned. I watched the smoke from three ridges away and I couldn't—"

He didn't finish. Wren waited. Waiting was a skill too; her father had called it the honest cousin of listening.

"I couldn't do anything," Quentin said at last. "I was nineteen and I could wake a sword from a hundred paces and I couldn't save one person. Forty-one of them. I counted for years. I stopped counting when the number started counting *me*." He looked at the fountain, the beds, the stubborn green. "I never came back. Six years I guided people through these mountains and I always took the eastern passes. Always."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," Wren said.

His head came around, and she braced for anger, but what she saw was closer to relief — the expression of a man who has been carrying a full bucket for miles and finally meets someone willing to say *you know that's heavy, right?*

"Is it," he said.

"Ashgrove. You were nineteen. You're a *guide*, not a god." She kicked a clump of snow off the path, mostly for emphasis. "My father died two weeks ago in a ditch outside Cold Larch Pass, and I wasn't there either, because he told me to take the southern route and I obeyed him, because obeying him was the only way I knew to love him. So don't stand there marinating in guilt like it's a bath you paid for. Grief's not a debt. Nobody's collecting."

Quentin stared at her. Then, slowly, he unlatched the gate and stepped into the garden, and Wren saw his shoulders come down half an inch — the half inch a man lowers them when he sets down something he forgot he was holding.

"Nobody's collecting," he repeated, tasting the words.

"Nope. They just pile up until you find somewhere to put them down." She nodded at the nearest herb bed. "Also, seriously, why does the rosemary hold grudges?"

"Brother Aldous spoke to it for forty years. Herbs grown near a swordspeaker get... opinionated." He crouched, touched a sprig, and murmured something too soft for her to catch. The rosemary shivered though there was no wind. "See? Still sulking about the time I weeded it wrong."

Wren laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh echoed off the broken cloister walls, and it sounded — strangely, dangerously — like belonging somewhere.

* * *

**Quentin**

They found her in the east cloister, kneeling in a bed of winter kale with her sleeves rolled despite the cold, her blind eyes fixed on nothing and everything.

She was older than Quentin remembered — of course she was; everyone was — but her hands were the same, quick and sure among the plants, and when she spoke she did not turn toward them, because she had heard their footsteps forty rows ago.

"The rosemary told me someone was coming," Mother Sable said. "It said: *the boy who weeds wrong.*" Her mouth curved. "And someone else. Lighter feet. A liar's walk, that one. No offense meant, girl. It's a fine walk. Kept you alive, I'd guess."

"None taken," Wren said. "It's my best feature."

Quentin crossed the rows and knelt in the cold mud beside her, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence was not empty but full — full of forty-one names he had never said aloud, and one old woman who had buried every one of them and stayed anyway, tending a garden in a graveyard because grief needed somewhere to put its hands.

"You stayed," he finally managed.

"Where would I go?" She plucked a slug off a kale leaf with surgical precision. "The dead are bad company but they're *my* bad company. Besides." Her clouded eyes turned toward him, uncannily accurate. "Someone had to keep the garden alive until you stopped being a coward and came home."

A laugh broke out of him — startled, cracked, real. "You haven't changed."

"Grief changed me plenty. It just didn't get to make me small." She set down her basket and took his hand in both of hers, and her grip was still strong as root. "Now. You wouldn't have fought your way past your own shame to get here unless the map led you. And maps only lead here for one reason." Her fingers tightened. "Tell me about the Spire, child. Tell me about the king who isn't a king. And then tell me what you're planning to do about it — because I can hear it in your heartbeat, Quentin Ashgrove. You're planning to do something *stupid and necessary*, and I am far too old to bury another family."

Behind him, Wren went very still.

Quentin looked at the blind gardener, at the garden that had outlived its murderers, at the thief who had followed him into the white silence and hadn't left. The oath stirred in his blood, the old words waking like the rosemary, opinionated and alive.

"We're going to unbind the true king," he said. "Before the thirteenth new moon."

Mother Sable exhaled slowly. Then she reached for her basket, and her walking stick, and began, deliberately, to roll down her sleeves.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd best start walking. Winter's short, the road's long, and I make terrible time on hills."
