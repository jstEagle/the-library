## 5. Hunters in the White Silence

The glacier had a name once, back when anyone bothered to name things that wanted you dead. The smugglers called it the Long Tooth, and they crossed it only in deep winter, when the crevasses filled with snow and lied about being solid ground.

Quentin had crossed it eleven times. He'd told Wren it was twelve, because he'd wanted to sound impressive, and she had seen through him instantly, which was becoming a pattern between them.

"Eleven," she said now, trudging beside him with her collar up and her breath streaming behind her like a banner. "You said twelve last night."

"I miscounted."

"You don't miscount. You counted our tea leaves this morning. There were forty-one."

"There were forty-two. Mother Sable took one for her pot."

Mother Sable, three paces behind, raised her walking staff without breaking stride. "I did. And I'll thank you both to argue more quietly. The mountain is listening, and mountains gossip."

It was, Quentin thought, absurd how much he'd come to rely on them in six days. The blind gardener who navigated ice better than any sighted man he'd known — she read the wind against her face and the song of the snow underfoot, and she had already steered them around two crevasses that would have swallowed a horse. And Wren, who complained constantly and walked anyway, whose father's map rode against Quentin's chest like a coal that wouldn't cool.

They were two days from the far side of the Long Tooth. Two days from the hidden valley, the ruined garden, whatever waited at the Hollow Spire.

And somewhere behind them, riding hard from the border town, were the witch-hunters.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a blue wall of ice, and Quentin did what he always did: he took first watch, alone, because sleeping among people was a skill he'd let rust.

He was thinking about tea rations and nothing else, deliberately nothing else, when Mother Sable came and sat beside him without asking.

"Your heart's been loud all day," she said.

"It's been loud for years."

"Mm. But today it's saying something new." She turned her face toward him. Her eyes were pale as milk and saw nothing, and somehow always saw everything. "Horses. Four of them, maybe five. They stopped an hour ago, down-valley. They're not following our trail. They're waiting for morning."

Quentin went very still. "You can hear horses through a glacier?"

"I can hear worry through a person," she said. "The horses are a guess. A good one." She patted his arm with fingers like winter twigs. "Whatever you're deciding, boy, decide it kindly. That's all wisdom ever asks."

She left him alone with the stars, which was worse company.

* * *

Morning came white and windless, the kind of silence that pressed on the ears. They were halfway across a broad shelf of ice — the flattest stretch of the crossing, a place where a man could almost pretend the world was gentle — when Wren stopped walking.

"Quentin."

He heard it too. The creak of harness. The soft iron music of shod hooves on stone-hard ice.

They came over the ridge to the east in a wedge of five, wrapped in grey-and-white, faces masked to the eyes, and every one of them wore the crowned eye of Vaelmoor stitched on the shoulder. Witch-hunters. Not the scruffy question-askers from the border town — these were the Crown's own, the Kindly Ones, as the joke went, because they only ever asked questions once.

"Down," Quentin said quietly. "Behind the serac. All of you."

Wren didn't move. Of course she didn't. She stood there counting them, lips moving, doing whatever arithmetic liars do. "Five," she said. "We could run."

"We could die slower," Quentin said. "Get down."

The hunters spread into a line with the unhurried grace of men who had done this many times and never lost. One of them stepped forward and pulled down his mask, and Quentin's stomach dropped, because he knew the face. Halvard Vane. Once, a lifetime ago, Vane had been a recruit the Swordspeakers had turned away for cruelty. Now he carried a badge and a warrant and a long thin blade that drank light instead of reflecting it.

"Ashgrove," Vane called across the ice, conversational, carrying. "Or should I say the ghost of Cold Larch Pass? We found your papers burned in a hearth in Marrow's Ford. Very dramatic. Very guilty."

Quentin stepped out from behind the serac, hands loose at his sides. "Just travelers."

"Just travelers." Vane smiled. "Traveling with a dead courier's daughter and a blind witch who navigates by listening to things no one else can hear. Traveling toward the Spire with a royal seal in your coat." He drew the pale blade. It made no sound at all, which was the most frightening thing about it. "The king is very concerned about travelers like you."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad weather does.

Vane gave some signal, and the line moved — not charging, but *flowing*, fanning wide to cut off retreat down-glacier, and Quentin understood with cold clarity that they hadn't come to arrest anyone. Blades that drink light weren't for arrests.

Wren ran the wrong way. Of course she did — she went for the high ground, the ridge, the route a scout takes, quick and clever and exactly where the two flankers wanted her. Quentin saw it unfold the way you see a cart wheel coming loose: helplessly, in advance.

"Wren, *no—*"

One hunter cut her off at the base of the ridge. The other circled behind. And then the ice did what glaciers do, what the Long Tooth had been waiting thirteen days to do.

The shelf beneath Wren's feet spoke — a groan, a crack like a snapped bone — and opened.

She dropped so fast there was no scream, just the shock on her face, her hand slapping ice that broke like bread, and then she was sliding into a blue throat of the glacier, catching a lip of ice with both hands, boots kicking over a darkness that went down further than anything ought to go.

And Quentin Ashgrove, who had spent seven years being no one, who had buried his oaths under smugglers' coin and silence, felt the old words rise in his blood like water finding its level.

Swordspeech wasn't shouting. That was what laymen thought — that the order bellowed commands at steel. It was quieter than that. It was *true speech*, the language things used before men taught them to lie. Ice listened to it. Steel remembered it. And the order had died rather than let the Crown have it, which was why speaking it now, here, in front of five hunters, was the same as signing his own death warrant.

Wren's fingers were slipping.

Quentin knelt at the edge of the crevasse, put his palm flat against the ice, and spoke.

"*Hold.*"

Not loud. Barely a breath. But the word went down through the glacier like a bell through still water, and the whole Long Tooth *heard* it. The ice around the crevasse thickened, knit, grew teeth of fresh blue crystal that locked around Wren's wrists and hauled — gently, impossibly — until she spilled out onto solid ground, gasping, frost in her lashes, staring at him.

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind had stopped to listen.

Vane's smile was gone. "Swordspeaker," he said softly, reverently, the way other men said *heretic*. "All this time. The last one. Do you know what your head is worth?"

"I've heard figures," Quentin said. His voice shook. He couldn't stop it. "None of them flattering."

"Take him alive," Vane said. "The king wants him *alive*."

* * *

They might have taken him, too, if Mother Sable hadn't chosen that moment to be terrifying.

She walked out into the open, straight toward five armed men, staff tapping the ice, milk-white eyes forward, and began to sing. It was a gardening song, low and looping, the kind of thing you'd hum over seedlings — and the glacier answered her the way it had answered Quentin, but differently, wildly, the whole shelf shuddering and shrugging like a great animal rolling over. Crevasses yawned open between the hunters. Seracs leaned. Two of them fled downslope; one simply vanished with a cry into a seam of blue that closed behind him like a mouth.

Vane alone kept his feet, backing away, pale blade up, eyes fixed on Quentin with something that wasn't quite hate. "Thirteen turns," he called. "You're too late, swordspeaker. Whatever you think you're going to find — you're too late." Then the ice tilted, and he was gone down the slope, running, alive, which meant the story would travel.

Quentin stood in the ringing quiet with his hand still burning where it had touched the ice.

* * *

Wren wouldn't look at him.

They walked for an hour, fast, downhill, while the adrenaline curdled into something colder. She kept three paces ahead, arms wrapped around herself, jaw set, and Quentin recognized the posture because he'd invented it. It was the way a person stands when the ground has opened under something they thought was solid, and it isn't the ice they're angry at.

At midday she finally stopped and turned.

"You *lied* to me."

"Yes."

"The whole time. 'Just a guide.' 'Just a smuggler.' 'Nothing special, Wren, keep your voice down, Wren.'" Her voice cracked on his name, which hurt worse than shouting. "My father died carrying that map. I watched witch-hunters burn our house for less than what you've got in your blood. And you let me walk beside you for six days thinking you were *nobody*."

"Yes," Quentin said again, because lying had gotten them this far and it seemed rude to change habits mid-argument. Then he made himself say the true thing instead. "Because everybody who knows what I am ends up dead or hunted, and I got tired of choosing which."

"So you chose lying."

"I chose *staying alive*. It seemed like a good foundation for the other plans."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — fury fighting a laugh and losing narrowly. She scrubbed a hand over her face. Behind her, Mother Sable had tactfully wandered off to inspect a rock, humming.

"My father knew," Wren said quietly. "Didn't he. That's why he gave the map to *you*. Out of everyone on that pass. He knew what you were."

Quentin looked at the ice. "Tomas Kell and I guided together, years ago. Before. He never said. I never said. That was the arrangement — we each pretended the other was ordinary, and it was the most honest friendship I've ever had."

Wren was silent a long moment. Wind moved across the glacier, and somewhere below, the Long Tooth settled and groaned like an old man in his sleep.

"If you use that — that *speech* — again," she said at last, "you tell me first. No more miracles out of nowhere. No more strangers wearing my friend's face."

Friend. He held very still, the way you hold still when a wild thing decides not to run.

"And if the hunters come back?" he asked.

"Then we fight," she said, "and you tell me *before* the glacier eats anybody." She stuck out a mittened hand, formal as a treaty. "Truce. Amended terms."

He shook it. Her grip was fierce and freezing and the best thing he'd felt in seven years.

Mother Sable reappeared at his elbow, smiling faintly at nothing. "Good," she said. "Now walk faster, children. The gardener's song says the valley mouth is close — and it also says we're being followed by something that doesn't ride horses."

Quentin turned. Far up the white slope, at the exact place where Vane had vanished, the snow was moving against the wind.

Walking downhill. Patiently. In no hurry at all.
