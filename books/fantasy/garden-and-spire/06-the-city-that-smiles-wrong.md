## 6. The City That Smiles Wrong

Vaelmoor's capital was called Brightwater, and it had earned the name the way a liar earns trust — by being very good at what it showed you.

Wren had seen cities before. She'd pickpocketed her way through three of them before she turned fourteen, and she knew their grammar: where the light pooled and where it refused to go, which smiles were currency and which were free. Brightwater spoke that language fluently. Lanterns floated on the canals like patient golden fish. Musicians played on every second corner, and the third corners held pie sellers, which Wren considered a far better use of real estate.

"It's beautiful," Sable said, in the tone of a woman describing a very handsome snake.

"It's *loud*," Quentin muttered. He'd pulled his hood up at the gates and hadn't lowered it since, walking with his shoulders curled inward as though he could make himself into a smaller man through sheer stubbornness. "Everything smells like sugar."

"That's the festival," Wren said. "The Feast of the Long Reign. Thirteen years of the king's rule." She said it lightly, watching him. Thirteen turns. The number sat in all of them now like a stone swallowed whole.

They had come down out of the mountains six days ago, and every one of those days Wren had expected to feel different — lighter, or heavier, or something. Instead she felt exactly the same, which she suspected was the trick of grief: it didn't announce itself at borders. It just came along, quiet as a stowaway, eating your rations.

Her father's map rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth. She still caught herself reaching for it the way you reach for a hand that isn't there anymore.

"Right," she said aloud, because if she stopped moving her hands they'd start shaking. "We need eyes inside the palace. And I know exactly who we're going to annoy."

* * *

Ferro Bramble lived above a shop that sold nothing anyone needed — scented candles shaped like famous admirals, mostly — which was how he'd always been able to afford the rent. Wren found him at his writing desk, composing a letter of complaint about candle quality, and watched his face travel from confusion to recognition to naked horror in the space of a breath.

"Wren," he said faintly. "Wren Nine-Names. They told me you died."

"They tell everyone that. It saves on funerals." She dropped into the chair across from him and put her boots on his desk, because some friendships are built entirely on small desecrations. "Hello, Ferro."

"You look terrible."

"I've been climbing mountains with a man who thinks conversation is a form of tax." She jerked a thumb at the doorway, where Quentin stood trying to be furniture. "Quentin Ashgrove. Ferro Bramble. Ferro used to forge provenance papers for stolen art. Quentin used to be a legend. Now you're both just sad men in nice coats."

"Charming," said Ferro, and then, because he was Ferro, he was already pouring wine for four without being asked. Mother Sable materialized beside him the way blind people do — silently, and slightly too close, so that he startled hard enough to slosh it. "And who—"

"Sable," she said, taking the cup from his hand and finding it by smell alone. "You have good wine and bad nerves, boy. Sit down before you spill us all into ruin."

What followed was an hour of lying, which Wren conducted with something close to joy. She gave Ferro perhaps a third of the truth — a map, a spire, a king who might not be a king — and dressed it in enough vagueness that it sounded like a merchant's rumor rather than treason. Ferro listened with his pen tapping against his teeth, and when she finished he was quiet for a long moment.

"You want archive access," he said finally. "To the restricted vaults. Under the palace. During the Feast of the Long Reign, when there are four hundred extra guards and every noble in Vaelmoor drunk on honeyed wine."

"When everyone's looking at the lights," Wren corrected sweetly.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll tell your wife what happened to the admiral candles."

Ferro went pale. "*That* was a clerical error—"

"She thinks they're imported. From the Moonlit Isles. At great expense." Wren let the silence do its work. Underneath the blackmail, though, she watched him carefully, because this was the part that mattered: Ferro Bramble had been her father's friend, and her father had trusted almost no one, and that trust had been worth more than any cipher key. "I wouldn't ask if it weren't true, Ferro. You know me."

He did know her. That was the trouble with old friends; they could see straight through the performance to the frightened girl underneath, holding a dead man's map. He sighed the sigh of a man whose comfortable life had just developed a leak.

"The servants' gallery," he said. "Feast night. Wear something gray and don't touch anything with a wax seal on it. And Wren—" His voice dropped, and for a moment the foppish courtier fell away and she saw the sharp, frightened man beneath. "People have disappeared lately. Quiet people. Archivists. A lamplighter who talked too much about the king's face. If you find what you're looking for, don't stand in the street admiring it."

* * *

The Feast of the Long Reign began at dusk, and Brightwater spent its entire self on it.

From the servants' gallery high above the Grand Court, Wren looked down on a sea of silk and candlelight. Fountains ran with spiced wine. Dancers whirled until their colors blurred. And at the far end of the hall, on a dais beneath a canopy of white silk, sat the King of Vaelmoor.

She had expected — she didn't know what. A monster. Something with wrongness written on it large enough to read across a room. Instead she saw a handsome man in his middle years, silver-streaked hair, a warm smile, waving graciously at the crowd. He looked tired, maybe. Kings were allowed to look tired.

"He looks fine," Quentin murmured beside her. He was pressed into the shadow of a pillar, gray-cloaked, hating every second. "Maybe the map's wrong. Maybe we climbed a glacier for nothing and I can go back to smuggling brandy like an honest criminal."

"Shh." Wren wasn't listening. She was watching the way she'd learned to watch card tables — not at the thing itself, but at everything around it.

The courtiers approached the dais in a slow river, bowing, presenting gifts, offering jokes. The king laughed at each one. And here was the first thread she pulled: his laughter came a half-beat after the joke landed. Not the natural lag of a bored monarch. A lag like sound arriving late across water. The courtier would finish, the hall would ripple, and *then* the king's mouth would open and produce the laugh, precise as a clock striking.

"Quentin," she whispered. "Watch his shadow."

The dais was ringed with candles — hundreds of them, so that every figure cast a dozen shadows, soft-edged and overlapping. The king rose to give the traditional blessing. He lifted his arms. The hall hushed.

His shadows did not.

For one heartbeat — Wren counted it, one full beat of her own traitor heart — the king's shadows stayed folded at his sides while his arms rose toward the ceiling. Then they hurried to catch up, sliding up the silk canopy like spilled ink running the wrong way, and settled into place, and no one below saw a thing. Four hundred nobles cheered. The fountains burbled. Somewhere a child laughed.

Beside her, Quentin had gone very still. Not afraid-still. The other kind — the kind she'd seen once on the glacier, right before the ice had sung and the hunters had gone flying. Swordspeech stillness.

"Thirteen turns," he breathed. "It's been wearing him like a coat for thirteen turns, and it still hasn't learned how shadows work."

Below, the king smiled down at his adoring city, and his smile arrived exactly on time, because a smile is easy. It's the body that betrays you. It's the things you stop thinking about.

Wren's skin crawled. She thought of her father dying on Cold Larch Pass, pressing a map into a stranger's hands, spending his last breath on a warning. *He knew,* she thought. He'd known this. He'd carried this knowledge alone through the mountains until it killed him, and he'd trusted it to a smuggler with a hero's name and a coward's habits, because there was no one else left to trust.

"We're leaving," she said quietly. "Now, while the toast has them all staring at the wine. Archive's below the east wing?"

"Servants' stair behind the tapestry of the Founding Hunt," Ferro had said. "Third door. Don't touch anything with a wax seal."

* * *

The archives smelled of dust, cold stone, and tallow — the honest smell of a building, under all Brightwater's perfume. Wren picked the outer lock in nine seconds and the inner one in eleven, and pretended not to notice Quentin noticing.

"Show-off," he said.

"Survivor," she corrected. "Same skill set, better marketing."

Lamps lit, they spread out among the shelves. The restricted vault was organized by reign, and the last thirteen years occupied a single shelf — thin, suspiciously thin, for over a decade of a kingdom's history. Wren pulled ledgers at random and found them hollowed out. Budgets with lines blacked through. Correspondence with pages razored away. A census of the royal household with one name cut cleanly from the roll, leaving a gap in the numbering like a missing tooth.

"They've been eating the paper trail," she said. "Literally."

"Here." Quentin's voice came from the far corner, strange and flat. He stood before a framed document on the wall — a proclamation, gilt-edged, dated thirteen years ago. *By decree of the Crown, the Order of the Swordspoken is dissolved, its members attainted, its gardens salted.* Beneath it, in a different hand, someone had added a line in ink gone brown with age:

*The binding holds until the moon forgets its face. Thirteen times. Then the vessel is spent.*

Below that, a date. Wren did the arithmetic twice, hoping she'd made an error both times.

Four weeks. Give or take a handful of days.

"Quentin." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "The new moon. The thirteenth one. It's—"

"I know." He hadn't moved. In the lamplight she could see his face, and for once there was nothing guarded in it at all — just a man reading his own death warrant in someone else's handwriting. "I know, Wren."

Behind them, somewhere up in the glittering dark of the feast, four hundred people raised their cups to a king whose shadow moved a heartbeat late — and in the corridor outside the archive door, soft as snowfall, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Stopping.

Then a voice, pleasant and unhurried, through the oak:

"We know you're in there, swordspeaker. The witch-finder would like a word."
