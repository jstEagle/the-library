## 7. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

Ferro Bramble had spent eleven years in the Vaelmoor archives learning one essential truth: the kingdom's history was a lie told in beautiful handwriting.

He was thinking about this as he knelt in the sub-cellar beneath the Hall of Records, holding a candle stub that had seen better centuries, while Quentin Ashgrove pried at a floor stone with a letter opener and Wren — who was supposed to be keeping watch on the stairs — was instead reading over his shoulder and eating his almonds.

"Those are rationed," Ferro said.

"You rationed them to yourself. I never agreed to your rations." She took another handful. "Also, these are stale."

"They're *preserved*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between civilization and savagery."

Quentin grunted from the floor. The stone came loose with a scrape that sounded, to Ferro's straining ears, like a cathedral bell being dragged through gravel. They all three froze. Somewhere above, a clerk coughed and went on copying.

"Subtle," Wren whispered.

"I'm an archivist," Ferro whispered back. "My tools are patience and dust. You'll notice neither of those is quiet."

The cavity beneath the stone held a lead box, and the box held what they had come for: the private ledger of Aldous Crane, Royal Binder, written in the year the king stopped being the king. Ferro had found its existence mentioned in a marginal note thirteen years ago, in another book entirely, and had spent a decade telling himself it was nothing — a rumor, a scribble, a dead man's joke. He had been very good at telling himself things were nothing. It was practically his job description.

Now he opened the ledger with hands that would not quite stay steady, and the candle flame leaned toward the pages as if it, too, wanted to read.

* * *

The binding ritual took up most of the first third. Ferro read it twice, because the first time he kept losing his place — not from the difficulty of the script, which was Crabbed Court Hand, tedious but legible, but from the growing cold feeling behind his ribs.

"It's a hunger," he said finally. "The thing in the crown. Crane bound it into the king's body, but a binding isn't a cage, it's a — a loan. The thing pays rent by keeping the body alive. And the rent goes up."

"How much?" Quentin asked. He had gone very still, the way he did when something mattered, which Ferro had learned to read as easily as weather.

"Thirteen turns of the moon. Thirteen new moons from the binding, and the debt comes due all at once. The body is consumed. Whatever's left wearing the crown won't be wearing anything else." Ferro turned the page. "There's a calendar in the margin. Crane counted them off."

Thirteen small moons, inked in a neat column. Twelve of them crossed out.

Wren set down the almonds. That, more than anything in the ledger, frightened Ferro. Wren setting down food was like a river deciding to run uphill.

"The thirteenth new moon," she said slowly. "That's — "

"Nineteen days," said Ferro. "Nineteen days from tonight."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The candle crackled. In the dark beyond its reach, the archive breathed its smell of vellum and mouse and time.

"And the unbinding?" Quentin asked. His voice was flat and careful, a man walking on ice he didn't trust.

Ferro turned to the last section. "It needs three things. The blood of the binder's line — Crane had no children, so that's a problem we can set aside and panic about later. A true name spoken freely — the king's own name, given willingly, which means someone has to convince him to *want* to die, or at least to want to stop being a puppet, which may be worse. And swordspeech. The old kind. The kind that could cut a binding without cutting flesh." He looked up. "I believe you're familiar."

"I was," Quentin said. "A long time ago."

"Well," said Wren briskly, in the tone of a woman rolling up her sleeves, "then it's lucky we have nineteen days and a smuggler who remembers. What's the problem?"

"The problem," Ferro said gently, "is that the last entry isn't about the ritual at all." He turned the final page and angled the candle so they could see it. The handwriting changed here — shakier, hurried, pressed hard enough to tear. "This is Crane's last night. Listen. '*It knows I am writing this. It reads through his eyes when he sleeps. If you find this, do not trust the smiling ones. Do not trust anyone the court loves. It makes people love it. That is how it eats.*'"

The candle guttered, though there was no draft.

"Cheerful fellow," Wren said, too lightly.

"He was found the next morning," Ferro said. "Officially, his heart failed. Unofficially — well. There is no unofficially. That's rather the point of official."

* * *

They worked until the small hours, copying the ritual in three different hands so no single theft would be traceable, and it was nearly dawn when Ferro climbed the stairs to his office and found the note waiting on his desk, weighted under his inkwell where any passerby might have seen it.

He knew the hand before he touched the paper. Small, precise, a schoolmistress's letters. *Marta*, his wife wrote, even after twenty years, in the same careful script she used for market lists.

*Come home before the bells. We need to talk about Liss.*

Liss. Their daughter, who was twelve, who had been accepted last spring into the Queen's Chorus — the queen who did not exist, whose duties were performed by whatever wore the crown — and who had come home two weeks ago saying strange, glowing things about how kind the Lord Protector's men were, how they'd asked her questions about Papa's work, how they'd given her a silver ribbon for her hair.

Ferro stood in his office with the copied ritual inside his coat and the note in his hand, and he thought: *it makes people love it.*

He thought about the witch-hunters who had visited him six months ago, polite as tax collectors, asking gentle questions about old books. He thought about how he had laughed and shown them ballads and inventories and sent them away satisfied, and how he had told himself afterward, in his professional capacity as a teller-of-nothings, that it was nothing.

He thought about Marta's face at breakfast, and how she had stopped meeting his eyes sometime around the new year, and how he had told himself that too was nothing.

The bells began. Dawn bells, sweet and stupid, ringing over a city that smiled wrong.

Ferro sat down at his desk and did not go home.

* * *

He lasted four days.

In fairness — and Ferro was a great believer in fairness, in ledgers balancing, in every debt getting its line item — he lasted longer than he'd expected of himself. Four days of carrying the copies sewn into his coat lining. Four days of watching Wren pick locks and Quentin watch shadows and knowing, with each hour, exactly what price was accruing against his family's names in some hunter's book.

On the fourth evening, Wren caught him burning a scrap of paper in his office grate.

She didn't say anything at first. She just stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, and Ferro watched the last corner of the paper curl and go, and knew she'd seen enough of it to know. A list of roads. The mountain route out of the city. The safehouses. Written in his hand, addressed to no one, meant for the fire and the wind up the flue to carry its ash wherever ash goes — which, if you were a hunter who knew to look, was nowhere near far enough.

"That's my father's cipher notation," Wren said quietly. "On the corner. You copied it so they could read the rest of the map faster."

"Wren —"

"Don't." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing; it stayed low and level, an archivist's voice, a man who catalogs things. "Don't explain yet. Let me guess first, I'm good at this. Your family. Someone's got your family."

"Liss sings in the royal chorus," Ferro said. His voice cracked on the second word, which embarrassed him profoundly, and he pressed on anyway, because the truth deserved to be spoken even badly. "Twelve years old. She thinks the Lord Protector's men are her friends. My wife has dinner once a month with the wife of the captain of the hunt, and calls it neighborliness, and I let her, because refusing felt more dangerous than attending. They've been building a net around my house for a year, Wren, thread by thread, and I've been pretending it was decoration." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Crane wrote *do not trust the smiling ones*. I'm the smiling one. I've been the smiling one for eleven years. It's how I survived the purge — everyone assumed a man who jokes about filing systems can't be dangerous."

"So you sold us."

"So I was *going* to sell you," Ferro said, "and then I sat down to write the letter, and I couldn't make my hand do it, and I burned the first copy, and I've burned one every night since. Four nights. Four letters. I keep — I keep writing *they have my daughter* and then I keep thinking *and what will they have when the thirteenth moon comes?* Because it doesn't stop with a king, does it? It eats outward. It made a whole court love it. It'll make a whole kingdom. And Liss will grow up loving it, singing for it, and I will have bought her life with everyone else's, and she'll never know what I paid, and neither will I, because I'll be dead or worse, and — " He stopped. Breathed. "You asked me once why an archivist helps smugglers. That's why. Somebody has to keep the true records. Even if nobody ever reads them. *Especially* then."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Down in the street, a festival drum practiced its rhythm; the city getting ready to smile again.

"Quentin should decide," she said at last. "Not me. But Ferro — if you send that fifth letter, I'll know. And I want you to understand that I'd still get your family out. After. That's the part that should frighten you. We're apparently the sort of idiots who do that."

She left him alone with the grate and the ashes of four unsent betrayals.

Ferro sat until the candles burned low, doing the only arithmetic that had ever mattered: one girl, weighed against a kingdom, and no honest column in which both balances held.

Then he heard boots on the archive stairs — too many boots, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who already owned the building — and the lamps along the gallery went out one by one, like eyes closing.

He reached for the copies in his coat lining, and found, with a lurch, that the lining was empty.

And from the darkness at the top of the stairs, a voice he knew — the captain of the hunt, warm as a hearth, smiling audibly — said:

"Master Bramble. Her Majesty's hunters thank you for your service. Now — where is the swordspeaker?"
