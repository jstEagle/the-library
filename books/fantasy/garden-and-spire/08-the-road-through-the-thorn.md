## 8. The Road Through the Thorn

The bells of Vaelmoor were still ringing when they went over the wall.

Not the great bronze bells of the cathedral — those had gone silent at dusk, as if the city itself were holding its breath — but the smaller bells of the watch-towers, ringing the alarm Quentin had heard only once before in his life, and never with his own name attached to it.

"Left," he said, hauling Wren over a garden wall by the collar of her coat. "Not right."

"You said left last time and we nearly drowned in a cistern."

"That was Ferro's left. My lefts are reliable." He glanced back. Ferro Bramble came over the wall a moment later, breathless, his fine courtier's coat torn at the shoulder, carrying nothing but a satchel of documents he'd stolen from his own archive and a face like a man who had sold his soul and been given a receipt he didn't trust.

"They'll seal the river gates within the hour," Ferro panted. "And my house will be watched. My family—"

"Your family is already on a barge to Millbrook," Wren said. "You did that part right, at least."

"I did *everything* right until I did one thing catastrophically wrong," Ferro said, "and I would appreciate it if we never spoke of it again."

"We're speaking of it again," said Mother Sable serenely, from where she sat on a rain barrel as though she'd been waiting for them all her life. The blind gardener had crossed half the capital that day on the strength of a boy's arm and her own unshakable conviction that she would not be lost, because, as she put it, "I have never once been lost. I have merely been somewhere unexpected." She rose, brushed off her skirts, and tilted her face toward Quentin. "Well, swordspeaker. Which way is out?"

Quentin looked north, toward the dark shoulder of the Thornfell mountains rising beyond the rooftops, and felt something in his chest both dread and ease. Dread, because the hunters were behind them and the mountains were hard country in autumn. Ease, because the mountains were *his* country. Every smuggler's cut and goat-track and forgotten drover's road from here to the pass lived in his head like the lines of an old song.

"Through the tanneries," he said. "Then the drainage tunnels under the old wall. Then we run."

"Run where?" Wren asked.

He looked at her — mud-streaked, knife-scarred, seventeen kinds of trouble wrapped in a courier's coat — and told the truth, which still felt strange in his mouth.

"Home. Or close enough."

* * *

They ran all night.

The drainage tunnels smelled exactly as bad as drainage tunnels should, and Ferro complained about it with the eloquence of a man who had spent ten years being complimented in drawing rooms. Sable walked the black tunnel without hesitation, one hand on the boy's arm, humming something old. Wren moved at Quentin's shoulder, silent, watchful, and every time she glanced back at him he saw the same question in her eyes that had been there since the glacier: *what else haven't you told me?*

By dawn they were in the foothills, in the grey light and the thin cold rain, and Quentin allowed them their first rest in a hollow beneath a leaning birch. He divided out what remained of their food: half a wheel of hard cheese, a heel of bread gone stale, six apples Ferro had liberated from a market stall with the reflexes of a much younger criminal.

"Ferro," Sable said, accepting her apple, "did you pay for these?"

"I paid for them *emotionally*," Ferro said.

They ate. The rain ticked in the leaves. Somewhere far behind them, faint as a headache, a hunting horn sounded once and fell silent.

"They've given up?" the boy asked. His name was Tam; he was fourteen, he had carried Sable across the capital on his back, and he had not stopped looking terrified since the bells began, but he hadn't once suggested going home either, which Quentin privately counted as the bravest thing anyone had done all week.

"No," Quentin said. "They're regrouping. Captain Vess doesn't give up. She's the kind of hunter who treats a quarry's escape as a personal insult to the crown." He turned the cheese knife over in his fingers. "We have maybe two days before she picks up our trail. Less if the rain stops."

"So we don't stop either," Wren said.

"We don't stop for long." He stood, knees cracking, and looked at the ridge ahead — a black spine of rock and pine with a notch in it that no map marked, because he had erased it from every map he'd ever drawn. "There's a road through that notch. It isn't on any chart the crown owns. It's steep, it's narrow, and there's a stretch of two days where there's nothing to eat but what we carry. So we leave the tent. We leave the spare rope. We take food, water skins, and speed, and we trade comfort for distance."

Ferro looked mournfully at the tent. "I slept so well in that tent."

"You complained about the tent."

"I complained about the *ground*. The tent itself was blameless."

They left the tent.

* * *

The smugglers' road was not a road, properly speaking. It was a decision the mountain had made long ago and never fully committed to: a shelf of stone above a gorge, threaded through pine and scree, vanishing and reappearing like a thought you couldn't quite hold onto. Quentin had first walked it at nineteen, with a pack of contraband salt and a captain who lied beautifully, and he had walked it perhaps forty times since. His feet knew it better than his eyes did.

What he had never done was walk it with four other people, one of whom was blind, one of whom was fourteen, and one of whom kept stopping to look back down the gorge as though the hunters might come flying up it like crows.

"Wren." He caught her elbow on the third such pause. "Stop doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Looking back. It costs us twenty steps every time, and twenty steps is the difference between the high shelf before dark and sleeping on the open stone with Vess's dogs below us."

Her jaw set in the way he was learning meant she was about to argue on principle. But then she exhaled, and something else came out instead, quieter. "My father used to say that. *Don't look back, little wren, the road behind you is already paid for.*" She stared down the gorge anyway, just once more, defiantly. "He looked back his whole life, you know. At debts, at mistakes, at me. That's how they caught him. He was watching the road behind instead of the one ahead."

Quentin didn't answer right away. Above them the wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea, and somewhere in his chest the old oath stirred, the way it always did now, warm and useless and insistent.

"He pressed a dying man's map into your hands too," he said finally. "That's not looking back. That's handing something forward."

"It's a *map*, Quentin. It's ink on calfskin."

"It's the truth about a king in a cage, and everyone who touches it ends up responsible for it. Your father knew. He chose you." He hesitated, then made himself finish, because Sable had told him in the garden that grief unspoken goes rotten, and he was tired of carrying rotten things. "I know something about choosing silence instead. I watched my order burn and I walked away and I called it survival for eleven years. I told myself the oaths were dead, the war was over, and a man could buy peace with his back turned." He met her eyes. "It doesn't work. In case you were considering it. You can spend eleven years not looking back and the past will still be standing right in front of you, holding out its hand."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be honest. Comforting comes later, if we live."

Despite everything — despite the hunters and the gorge and the ache in her legs — she laughed. It was a short, startled sound, like a bird startled off a branch, and Quentin found he wanted to hear it again, which was a dangerous sort of wanting, and he filed it away with all the other things he wasn't ready to look at directly.

"Come on," he said. "The high shelf before dark."

* * *

They made the shelf as the light failed, and camped in the lee of an old boundary cairn that smugglers had built tall enough to break the wind. No fire. Cold rations. Sable produced a handful of dried mint from some impossible pocket of her robes and passed it around "for morale," and morale improved measurably.

Tam fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, and Ferro eased him down against the cairn with a gentleness that would have surprised anyone who'd only seen him at court. Sable settled beside the boy like a hen over one egg.

Which left Quentin and Wren on watch, shoulder to shoulder against the stone, the gorge breathing darkness below them and the stars sharp overhead.

"Fear," Wren said abruptly. "You talked about failure. What are you afraid of? Actually afraid of. Not the hunters. Not dying."

Quentin considered lying. It was his oldest craft, older than swordspeech — he'd lied to captains and customs men and himself with equal fluency. But the mountain was dark and honest, and she had handed him her father's ghost that afternoon, and fair was fair.

"Losing this," he said. "This exact thing. Four people asleep against a rock, sharing dried mint. I spent years making sure I belonged to no one, because everything I loved got burned. And now—" He gestured vaguely at the sleeping company. "Now there's a blind woman who hums at me when I'm sulking, and a courtier who steals apples badly, and a boy who snores, and you. And I'm terrified, because I know precisely how much it costs when it burns."

Wren was quiet. Then: "Mine's stupider. I'm afraid I'm only good at running. That if we actually reach the Spire and open it and the truth comes out, I'll just... be a girl with a tattoo and a dead father and no idea what to do next. Running is the only thing I've ever been certain of."

"The tattoo's a good start," Quentin said. "Half a cipher key on one arm. Very practical. Very romantic."

"It itched for a year."

"Truth usually does."

She bumped her shoulder against his — lightly, the way you test whether a bridge will hold — and then left it there. Neither of them said anything else for a while. Below them, far off, a single point of torchlight moved along the valley floor, patient as a spider, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it would cost them this, and they had both just admitted how much this was worth.

* * *

On the second day the road narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for one, and Sable crossed it with her hand on Quentin's sleeve and her chin high, saying, "I can hear the drop. It sounds rude." On the third day it snowed, early and mean, and they burned Ferro's spare coat for warmth, which he bore with the dignity of a martyr and the commentary of a critic. On the fourth morning, they came down out of the pines into the hidden valley, and the ruined monastery stood white-rimmed against the frost, and Mother Sable smiled at it like a woman greeting an old argument.

But Quentin stopped at the tree line.

Below, at the mouth of the valley path, planted in the fresh snow like five black nails: boot prints. Fresh. Deliberate. Heading *up* the trail toward the garden — toward the place where the map's cipher said the second key waited.

And beside the prints, pressed deep and precise into the snow, the mark of a witch-hunter's brand-iron, stamped into a stripped pine trunk as a message:

**WE KNOW WHERE YOU'RE GOING. WE'LL WAIT WHERE YOU MUST ARRIVE.**

Captain Vess hadn't followed them through the thorn at all.

She'd gone ahead.
