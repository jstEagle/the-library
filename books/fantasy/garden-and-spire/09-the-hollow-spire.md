## 9. The Hollow Spire

The spire did not look hollow from the outside. That was the first lie of it.

It rose out of the glacier's shoulder like a black finger accusing the sky, taller than any tower in Vaelmoor and older than the kingdom it overlooked, its stone so dark it seemed to drink the daylight. No door. No windows. No banner, no guard-post, no road — nothing to suggest that anything inside was worth protecting, which was of course exactly why something inside needed protecting.

Quentin stood at the edge of the ice field with Wren on his left and Mother Sable on his right, and he felt his swordspeech stir in his chest like a dog that had caught a scent after years of sleeping.

"It's uglier than I imagined," Wren said.

"You imagined it?"

"I imagine everything before I meet it. Saves time." She squinted up at the spire. "Usually I'm wrong anyway."

"Then you're well prepared," Sable said serenely. The blind gardener had walked three days across the ice without once stumbling, her bare feet somehow finding purchase where their boots slipped, and she had refused every offer of a hand with the mild firmness of a woman declining a second helping of soup.

Quentin checked the map one last time, though he no longer needed to. The bloodstained lines had rearranged themselves over the weeks of travel, ink crawling like veins toward their heart, until now the final route glowed faintly: *up the north face, through the mouth that is not a mouth, down the throat that is not a throat.* Thirteen turns of the moon since the binding. Two nights remained.

"Guardian," he said aloud, mostly to warn the others, partly to warn himself. "There'll be one."

"There's always one," Wren said. "In my experience the trick is to befriend them or rob them. Sometimes both."

"In my experience," Quentin said, "the trick is to survive them."

"Well. We each bring our strengths."

* * *

The mouth that was not a mouth opened at the spire's base as they approached — a seam in the black stone widening with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat — and the guardian came out to meet them.

It had been a knight once. That was the horror of it. Armor fused to flesh gone grey as old candle wax, a greatsword dragging a furrow through the snow, and where the visor should have shown a face there was only smoothness, as if someone had pressed a thumb into wet clay and smoothed away everything human. It wore no crown. But around its neck, on a chain thick as a ship's anchor cable, hung a small iron circlet — a collar, Quentin realized. A leash. The crown-thing kept its own hound leashed, even here, even alone.

It planted itself between them and the opening and spoke in a voice like gravel poured slowly from a sack.

"TURN BACK."

"We've come for the king," Quentin said.

"THERE IS NO KING."

"That's rather the point."

The guardian raised its sword. And Quentin, who had spent thirteen years running from exactly this moment, stepped forward and let his swordspeech rise.

It was not speech, really, though the order had named it that. It was older than language — a resonance in the bones, a truth spoken so directly that reality flinched and obeyed. He had not used it in anger since Thornfell. It hurt, the way a long-unused muscle hurts, and more than that: it cost. Every word spent was a coin taken from a purse that did not refill.

He spoke one word.

"*Stay.*"

The word hit the guardian like a wall. Its sword stopped mid-swing; its boots sank an inch into the ice; the whole vast grey body trembled with the effort of resisting, and then — slowly, joints grinding like millstones — it knelt.

But it did not stop fighting. Quentin could feel that. The command held it the way a dam holds a river, and the river pushed.

"It won't last long," he said through his teeth. Sweat froze on his brow. "Wren — the seam. Get us inside."

Wren was already moving, hauling at the stone mouth, and Sable walked past the kneeling guardian without hurry and laid one hand briefly on its smooth face as she went by, the way she might touch a grieving child.

"Poor hound," she murmured. "Poor loyal thing."

Inside, the seam closed behind them with a boom, and the guardian's muffled roar shook dust from walls that had not seen dust disturbed in thirteen years.

* * *

The interior of the Hollow Spire was not hollow either. It was a throat, all right — but a throat lined with garden.

That was what none of them had expected. Quentin stood on a spiraling stair cut into living rock and stared at greenery climbing the walls in terraces, pale and starved but alive, fed by some light with no source he could find. Ivy with silver undersides. Moss like spilled emeralds. Small white flowers he recognized from Mother Sable's monastery garden, blooming stubbornly in the dark.

"The binding didn't just imprison him," Sable said softly, trailing her fingers along a leaf. "Someone tended him. All these years. Someone came down and kept things growing."

"Who?" Wren asked.

"Perhaps the same someone who made the map," Quentin said quietly. "Perhaps the courier wasn't carrying orders. Perhaps he was carrying *watering instructions*."

They descended. The stair wound down and down, past terraces that grew lusher as they went — warmer air, richer soil, the light strengthening from ghost-pale to gold — until Quentin understood with a lurch that the deepest part of this prison was the most beautiful part, and that this, too, was mercy of a kind. Whoever bound the true king had wanted his last years to hold something green.

At the bottom was a chamber like the inside of a bell, and in the center of it, a man sat on a plain wooden chair, tending a potted rose.

He looked up when they entered, and Quentin's breath caught, because he knew that face. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew that face; it was stamped on every coin, carved over every courthouse door. King Aldous the Third, the Beloved, missing these thirteen years — except not missing at all. Here. Older. Greyer. With dirt under his fingernails from the rose pot and a blanket over his knees like any grandfather on any porch in the kingdom.

"Well," the king said, in a voice dry and warm as autumn leaves. "Visitors. It's been a while since the gardener's boy. You're not his lad."

"No, Majesty."

"Don't do that." The king smiled, tired. "'Majesty.' There hasn't been a majesty in this room in thirteen years. There's been a prisoner who keeps roses. Sit, sit — there are stools. The girl looks hungry. There's bread, such as it is. It keeps appearing. I've never asked how."

Wren took the bread. Quentin noticed she took it without hesitation, without checking it, and filed that away among the many small reasons he trusted her.

"Majesty—" he started again, caught himself, and began differently. "Aldous. My name is Quentin Ashgrove. I was swordspeaker-trained, before Thornfell. I've come to unbind you."

The king went very still.

Then he set down his watering can, folded his hands atop the blanket, and said, with terrible gentleness:

"No."

* * *

The argument lasted a long time. Quentin would remember afterward that it was conducted almost entirely in kindness, which made it worse.

"You don't understand what I am now," the king said. "I was a vain man. Ambitious. I signed papers that ruined families because they were inconvenient. I chose ministers for their flattery. Thirteen years in this chair, with nothing but a rosebush and my own company, and do you know what I discovered? I like the man I've become. He is the best work of my life, and he exists *only here*. The moment you break the binding, the thing wearing my crown unravels — yes, fine, good — and my body, which has been kept alive by the same working, goes with it. I walk out of this chair and into a grave. That is the arithmetic. Freedom equals death. You'd be trading a living man for a corpse and a story."

"Your people are ruled by a monster," Quentin said.

"My people are ruled by a monster who keeps the granaries full and the borders quiet," the king said. "I've had thirteen years to think about it, Ashgrove, and I'll tell you an ugly truth: the thing is a better king than I ever was. It only eats a courtier now and then. I started a war over a slight at a wedding."

"The thirteenth new moon," Quentin said. "Two nights from now. When it comes, the binding consumes your body entirely — the archive was clear. After that, there's no choice left to make. The thing rules forever, and you're not even a prisoner anymore. You're nothing."

The king was silent for a long moment. On the terrace above, wind moved through leaves that had no business existing, and the sound was like rain on a roof, like safety, like home.

"Then I have two nights," he said at last, "and I mean to spend them being alive. Tell me about the road. Tell me about the girl with nine names — yes, I heard her muttering on the stairs, half of those names are clearly invented. Tell me about the blind woman who touched my poor hound like he was somebody's son. Tell me everything. If I have two nights, I want them *full*."

And that was the knife-point of it, Quentin thought later. Not a battle. Not a riddle. Just an old man asking for stories, and a swordspeaker holding an oath in one hand and mercy in the other, unable to set either down.

Because here was the truth Quentin could not say aloud: the oaths of his order had never been to kings. They had been to *truth*. Speak the true word, spend the true cost, let reality flinch. And the true word — the word his magic already knew, already leaned toward, the way a dog leans toward a door — was that the binding must break, whatever it broke.

But his hands, his ordinary human hands, wanted to pour the wine and listen to the stories and let the old man keep his rose.

Sable found him alone by the stairwell near midnight, or what passed for midnight in a place without sky.

"You've decided," she said. It was not a question.

"I don't know that I have."

"Grief taught you to run. Mercy is teaching you to stay. Both are honest, Quentin. But only one of them is *true*, and you were always a truthful man. It's the most annoying thing about you."

He laughed — a cracked, unexpected sound in that green dark — and then stopped, because far above them, muffled through thirteen years of stone, the guardian began to howl.

Not in rage. In warning.

The seal on the chamber wall, the one keeping the binding intact, had begun to glow — and it was flickering. Counting down. And beneath the howl of the hound, rising up the stairwell like smoke, came a voice Quentin had heard only once before, at a festival in a city that smiled wrong:

*"I know you're down there, little swordspeaker. And I am coming to collect my throne."*

The crown-thing had left its palace.
