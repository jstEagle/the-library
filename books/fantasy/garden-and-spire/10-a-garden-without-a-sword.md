## 10. A Garden Without a Sword

**Quentin**

The king was smaller than Quentin remembered.

Thirteen turns ago, Aldric of Vaelmoor had stood a head taller than any man in the oath-hall, laughing loud enough to rattle the banners. Now he hung in the roots of the Hollow Spire like a coat someone had forgotten on a hook, gray at the edges, translucent in places where the crown-thing had already eaten through. His eyes were still his own. That was the cruelest part.

"Please," the king said, and his voice came out in pieces, like ice cracking on a pond. "Don't. I know what you've come to do. Don't."

Quentin knelt. The binding-circles glowed around him, old swordspeech older than kingdoms, and every line of them hummed against his skin like a struck bell. Wren stood behind him with the lantern. Mother Sable stood behind her, sightless eyes turned toward the king as if she could see him better than any of them.

"I have to," Quentin said. "You know I have to."

"I know." The king's mouth shaped something that might once have been a smile. "I hoped you wouldn't. That's different."

Behind them, up the long throat of the spire, the guardian screamed — the crown-thing's last servant, tearing itself apart against Ferro's barricades, against Wren's traps, against the stubborn ordinary courage of people who had no magic at all and kept fighting anyway. Time was short. Time had always been short; that was the whole shape of this story.

"Tell me what it costs," Wren said quietly. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere on the glacier, when she'd seen what swordspeech did to him, how it spent him like coin.

"It takes everything," Quentin said. "That's how unbinding works. You can't open a lock with a borrowed key. It has to be *yours*, all of it, given whole." He flexed his fingers. They were already cold. "The order called it the Last Word. We used to joke about who'd say it first. Halric always claimed he would. He got a knife in the back instead, so I suppose we're both liars."

"You don't have to be funny about it," Wren said, but her voice wobbled, and he loved her for the wobbling.

"Somebody has to. You're all terrible at it."

The king made a sound that might have been laughter, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Swordspeaker. Listen. When you open the binding, the thing comes out of me. And what's left—" He looked down at his fading hands. "What's left is thirteen turns of rot. I will die. But I will die *me*. Do you understand? For thirteen years I have been a house that something else lives in. I want my house back. Even for one breath."

"That's why I'm here," Quentin said.

He put his hands into the binding-circle. Swordspeech rose in him one last time — not words exactly, never words exactly, more like the memory of an edge, the truth a blade knows about falling. He had spent this power carefully for twenty years, hoarding it like a miser, and now he opened his fist and let all of it go at once.

It did not hurt. That surprised him. It felt like setting down a pack he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there — like standing upright for the first time and realizing how bent he'd been. The circles blazed white. The roots of the spire shuddered. Somewhere above, the guardian's screaming stopped mid-note, which was somehow worse than the screaming.

And the king of Vaelmoor breathed in, and was a man again.

He lasted eleven breaths. Quentin counted them. In the first, the king looked at his own hands and wept. In the third, he said, "My daughter — tell my daughter the garden —" and didn't finish, because kings are always leaving instructions nobody can follow. In the seventh, he looked at Quentin and said, "Thank you," plain as bread, no crown in it at all. In the eleventh, he closed his eyes, and the thing that had worn him for thirteen years unraveled out of the air like smoke deciding it had somewhere better to be, and the Hollow Spire went quiet for the first time in thirteen turns.

Quentin tried to stand. The floor came up and met him, gently, the way old friends do.

* * *

**Wren**

He weighed nothing. That was the frightening part — her father had been a big man too, and when he died he'd gone heavy as wet rope, but Quentin came up off the spire floor light as kindling, as if the magic had been most of what held him together and now there was just the man.

"He's breathing," she said, for the fourth time, mostly to herself. Sable walked beside her with one hand on Quentin's wrist, reading his pulse like scripture.

"Breathing," Sable agreed. "Faint as a mouse's apology. But breathing." She smiled her terrible serene smile. "The sword is out of him, child. What's left is only a man. Men mend."

"Do they?" Wren heard her own voice crack. "Because I've met a lot of men, and—"

"This one will. He's too stubborn to do otherwise. I can hear it in his pulse. Stubbornness has a rhythm, you know. Like a bad drummer."

They came up out of the spire into gray morning, and Ferro Bramble was waiting for them, sitting on a rock with his head in his hands and blood on his sleeve from holding the barricade. He looked up, and Wren braced herself for the courtier's smile, the smooth recalibration, the man who sold their route to the hunters to save his family and then couldn't live with it and came back anyway.

Instead Ferro just said, "Is it done?"

"It's done," Wren said.

"The king?"

"Dead. Human. Free."

Ferro nodded slowly, several times, like a man agreeing to terms. Then he stood, brushed off his fine ruined coat, and said, "Right. Well. Someone will have to explain to the capital that their king is dead and also saved, and I am unfortunately the only person alive who is good at explaining things to the capital." He paused. "Also I owe all of you approximately everything, and Brambles repay debts. It's practically heraldry."

"You sold us," Wren said. Not cruelly. Just checking.

"Yes," Ferro said. "And then I sat outside a witch-hunter camp for two days making sure they marched the wrong way, and I wrote letters that will take the hunters apart piece by piece when they surface, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life being useful in deeply annoying ways. That's the arrangement. Take it or leave it."

Wren looked at Quentin, unconscious over Sable's shoulder, looking younger than she'd ever seen him. She thought about her father pressing a map into a stranger's hands because a stranger with an oath was worth more than a daughter with a grudge. She thought about nine names and only needing one now.

"We'll take it," she said.

* * *

**Quentin**

Spring came to the monastery garden while he learned to be ordinary.

That was the strange mercy of it. He had expected the loss of swordspeech to feel like an amputation, and for the first weeks it did — he reached for the edge inside himself and found only scar tissue, smooth and silent. He woke some nights certain the world was speaking to him and he'd gone deaf to it. Wren found him once at dawn sitting among the dead rosebushes, and instead of asking, she sat down next to him and said, "I counted your breaths, you know. Eleven. I counted them too."

He didn't know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and she leaned her shoulder against his, and that was enough. It kept being enough.

Mother Sable put him to work. This, he suspected, was her actual medicine. A blind gardener ran the ruined monastery the way a general runs a war, and Quentin Ashgrove, last of the oath-bound, last of anything, spent his mornings hauling water from the spring because Sable said the roses wanted it and Sable was never wrong about what things wanted.

"They'll bloom this year," she told him in the third week, kneeling beside a bed of black soil, her hands moving over the canes like a fortune-teller's. "Grief-fed ground grows the best flowers. Everyone knows that. Nobody believes it until it happens."

"You're making that up."

"Entirely. But I'm usually right anyway."

Ferro arrived at the end of the month with a cart, three barrels of decent wine, and news: the crown-thing's hunters had scattered like startled crows, the regency council was arguing itself into irrelevance, and Vaelmoor — leaderless, frightened, alive — had begun telling stories about a swordspeaker who gave away his magic to free a dying king. "They're getting it wrong, naturally," Ferro said, pouring wine with a flourish. "In the tavern version you fight a dragon. But the bones of it are true, and true stories are rare enough these days that people hold onto them."

"Let them have the dragon," Quentin said.

Wren stayed. He hadn't asked her to. She simply started taking the east wall of the garden, clearing the ivy, and one evening he found her sitting on the wall with her sleeves pushed back, the cipher-key tattoo half-hidden under new dirt, watching the sun go down over the Thornfell peaks.

"My father charted roads his whole life," she said. "Every smuggler's path, every hidden valley. And the one place he never marked on any map was this one." She looked around at the ruins, the roses, the blind woman humming to seedlings. "I think he was saving it."

"For what?"

"For whoever needed it after him." She hopped down off the wall and dusted off her hands. "So. I'm staying. If that requires discussion, I'm prepared to win the discussion."

"No discussion required," Quentin said.

* * *

**Wren**

On the first true day of spring, the roses bloomed.

Not all of them — the garden was thirteen years dead and would take thirteen years living to come all the way back — but along the south wall, where the grief-ground was richest, a dozen white roses opened overnight, and Sable stood among them in the early light with her face turned up like a woman listening to music.

"There," she said softly. "Told you."

Quentin came out with two cups of tea, handed one to Wren, and stood beside her looking at the flowers. He moved differently now. Slower, sometimes, like a man still learning the weight of his own body. But he laughed easier. Yesterday he'd laughed at one of Ferro's jokes, an actual laugh, and Ferro had dropped an entire tray in surprise.

"Any regrets?" Wren asked. She'd been saving the question for weeks, afraid of the answer.

Quentin considered it honestly, the way he considered everything now — no edges left to hide behind, just the man. "I miss it," he said finally. "Some mornings I miss it like a limb. And then I come out here, and there's tea, and there's you, and there's a mad old woman talking to roses, and there's Ferro reorganizing our toolshed by color for reasons known only to him." He took a sip of tea. "I traded a sword for a garden. I keep waiting to feel like I lost the bargain."

"And?"

"And I don't." He glanced at her, and there was warmth in it, unhurried, real. "Turns out I was never very good at being a weapon. I'm told I make an excellent gardener."

From the gate came the crunch of boots, and Ferro appeared, breathless, coat dusty, holding a letter with a broken royal seal. Behind him on the road, Wren could see riders — a dozen, maybe more, flying no colors she recognized, and at their head a young woman with the late king's jaw and his same way of sitting a horse.

"Ferro," Quentin said slowly, "why does that letter smell like a summons?"

"Because it is one!" Ferro beamed, entirely delighted, entirely unrepentant. "The princess survived, she's taken the capital, she's read my account of everything, and she requests — requests, mind you, in writing, with her own seal — the presence of the swordspeaker who freed her father. Also his companions. All of us. Immediately." He spread his hands. "I may have mentioned you were available."

Wren looked at Quentin. Quentin looked at the roses, then at the road, then at the tea in his hand as if it might contain advice.

"Well," he said at last, and set down the cup, and there was something in his face that wasn't swordspeech anymore but was its cousin — resolve, maybe, or hope wearing work clothes. "Someone should probably tell the princess her father counted eleven breaths. And that he said the garden."

He picked up his coat. Spring was coming up the mountain behind them, and the road ahead was open, and none of them, Wren thought, would be walking it alone.
