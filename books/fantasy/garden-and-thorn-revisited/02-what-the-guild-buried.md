## 2. What the Guild Buried

Silas did not sleep.

He tried. He banked the fire, drew the shutters, sat in the dark of his own shop like a man sitting vigil over a stranger's coffin. But the boy breathed in the corner behind the counter, and each breath came out shaped—soft syllables, round vowels, a rhythm like water finding its way downhill—and every time the sound reached him, Silas felt it in his teeth, in the old scar along his forearm where his master had pressed the guild-seal into his skin with a hot iron and said, *This is not a mark, boy. It is a debt.*

The Old Tongue. He hadn't heard it spoken aloud in five years. Hadn't dared to speak it himself, even alone, even into his pillow, because words in that language were not descriptions of things—they were *handles*, and you did not pick up what you could not put down.

And this child, this mute-by-day creature who had appeared on his step at dusk with frost on his bare feet and no shadow of fear anywhere in him, was dreaming in it fluently. Not stumbling. Not reciting. *Conversing*—with someone, or something, that Silas could not hear.

Toward dawn the boy's murmuring changed. The cadence shifted from wandering to deliberate, the way a sleeper's breathing changes when they begin to wake, and Silas found himself leaning forward in the dark, straining, translating without meaning to—the way a man who has once been drowning reads water forever after.

*...the root remembers the cutting...*

*...ask the one who was left...*

*...ask the one who was left where the ledger sleeps...*

*...under the third stone, where the garden was...*

Then silence. Then ordinary child-breathing, shallow and even, and gray light seeping through the shutter seams, and Silas Reyes sitting very still with his heart going like a fist against a door.

Because he knew that phrase. *Where the garden was.* Every apprentice knew it. It was how Master Corvane had ended the last lesson any of them ever received, the night before the purges began—standing in the ruined courtyard behind the guildhouse with his hands behind his back, looking at the dead rosebushes as though they owed him an apology.

"A garden is a promise," he'd said. "Remember that when they come for us. A garden is a promise that something buried will return."

Silas had thought it sentiment. Old men were full of it. He'd been nineteen and furious at the world for ending before he'd had his turn to matter in it.

He got up, lit a candle with flint and steel like a law-abiding man, and went to the back wall of the shop, behind the shelf of harmless things—dried lavender, blessed thread, copper bells sold to grandmothers who wanted to believe in small protections. He knelt. The floorboards there had been replaced once, badly, by his own hands five years ago, and he had told himself ever since that he'd done it to repair rot.

He pried up the third board.

Beneath it: a cavity lined in lead foil, and inside the lead, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a book. Not large. Bound in green leather cracked like drought-earth, its spine stamped with the thorn-and-blossom sigil that Silas had spent five years making sure no one saw on his skin.

His master's ledger. Hidden under the third stone, where the garden was—which meant Corvane had known, years before the witch-hunters came, exactly which of his apprentices would survive, and which house he would run to, and where in that house he would kneel.

*Ask the one who was left.*

Silas unwrapped the ledger with hands that were not quite steady and did not open it immediately. He held it instead, feeling its weight, feeling the faint wrongness of it—the way certain objects feel colder than the room around them, not because they hold cold but because they hold *attention*. Magic too large to be called magic anymore. Magic that had become architecture.

"You knew," he said aloud, to a dead man, in a whisper. "You knew and you left me a hiding place instead of a warning."

The boy stirred behind the counter. Silas looked over. The child was awake—had perhaps been awake all night—and was watching him with those eyes the color of pond ice, unblinking, patient, ancient in a way that no child's eyes should be. He raised one small hand and pointed, not at the ledger, but at Silas's chest. At the place beneath his shirt where the scarred seal lay.

Then he spoke—one word, aloud, in daylight, his first since arriving, and the candle flame stood straight up like a soldier called to attention:

*"Open."*

* * *

The ledger opened to a page already marked by a pressed thorn, brown and brittle, five years dead and still sharp enough to raise blood if handled carelessly. Silas handled it carefully.

The early pages were what he expected: inventory, tuition records, the mundane bookkeeping of a guild that had presented itself to the world as makers of charms and wards and weather-luck. His master's handwriting marched across them, neat and dry as accounting.

Then, two-thirds through, the handwriting changed. Larger. Pressed harder, as though the pen were being driven by something other than a hand. And the language shifted—not fully into the Old Tongue, but laced through with it, High Common stitched with threads of the older speech the way a wound is stitched with wire.

Silas read, and the morning went cold around him, and he read on anyway, because that is what apprentices are for.

*We did not find the god,* the ledger said. *We made the prison, and the prison required a shape, and the shape we chose was a crown.*

*Understand what was done. Understand it fully, whoever reads this, because I will not be alive to soften it. In the year the harbor froze in high summer, the thing that the first kings had worshipped under the mountain turned its attention from worship to consumption. It ate the harvest-thoughts out of ten thousand minds and left them smiling. The guilds of that age met in secret and chose binding over banishment, because banishment requires a door, and we could not find a door wide enough to push it through and narrow enough to keep it closed.*

*A living binding, then. Blood-line bound to blood-line. We wove it into the royal succession itself, so that every crowned head would carry the seal unknowing, generation upon generation, a chain of living locks. The king does not merely wear the crown. The king IS the ward. And so long as the line continues, the thing beneath the throne stays beneath the throne.*

*But a lock wears, apprentice. A lock wears at the exact rate that it is used. Each coronation turns the key. Each royal death strains the weave. We built it to last a hundred reigns. I have counted. This is the ninety-eighth.*

*They will call us traitors when they learn. Let them. Traitors at least chose. But know this also, and carry it as your inheritance: we did not only bind the god. We bound ourselves to the binding. Every Thornwright sealed is a stitch in the working. When they purge us, the binding weakens by our number. They think they are killing sorcerers. They are pulling stitches out of a wound.*

*Except one. One must remain. One must be left, deliberately, the way a surgeon leaves a drain in a closing cut. I have chosen. Forgive me. Survival is not always mercy.*

Silas set the ledger down on the counter with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown through a window.

Five years. Five years of scrounging and flinching and selling luck-charms to bakers, five years of believing himself a survivor by accident—a coward, a deserter, the least of the Thornwrights, the one who'd hidden while better men burned. He had built his whole self on that foundation. He had *earned* his shame nightly, worn it like a garment that fit.

And it was engineered. Tailored. His master had measured him for survival the way a tailor measures a man for a coat, and Silas had put it on and called it his own skin.

He laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. The boy watched him without judgment, which was somehow worse.

"Ninety-eight reigns," Silas said. "So Aldric is—" He stopped. Did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the wards screamed. "Aldric is the ninety-eighth lock. And the binding is failing *now*. While he still lives."

Which meant it wasn't wearing out slowly, the way Corvane had predicted, reign by reign. Something was accelerating it. Something was *pulling*.

He turned pages faster now. Near the end, the entries grew terse, hurried, written in the ledger's final weeks before the witch-hunters came with their green wax seals and their lists. Corvane's last full entry read:

*If the binding fails early—if the lock breaks before the line ends—then the god comes loose whole, and no rite exists to rebind it. There is only the older rite, the one we swore never to write down, and I am writing it down, because oaths are for men who expect to live. It is in the drowned house, under the harbor, where we kept the things too dangerous for land. Salt keeps secrets. Go down where the garden was drowned, and read what we paid, and then decide whether you are brave or merely left.*

*The cost is written plainly. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise, including yourself. A binding needs a vessel. A vessel must be willing. Read it twice.*

Silas closed the ledger. Outside, the city was waking—carts rattling over cobbles, the baker's boy calling the hour, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary day in a capital that did not know it was a lid screwed down over something hungry.

The boy slid off his chair, crossed the floor, and stood beside Silas at the counter, small and grave. He laid one finger on the ledger's cover, then pointed east—toward the river, toward the harbor, toward the gray water where the old guildhouse had gone down in the flood forty years before anyone thought to mourn it.

"Salt," the boy said. Second word of his life offered to Silas. His voice was clear and cold and completely certain.

Silas looked at the child, at the ledger, at his own reflection ghosted in the dark shop window—thin, tired, twenty-four years old and already an heir to a century of buried crimes.

His guilt had curdled overnight into something harder. Not forgiveness—he did not forgive Corvane, not yet, maybe not ever—but resolve, dense and mineral, like a stone forming around a wound.

"All right," he said quietly. "I was left. So I'll go down."

He was reaching for his coat when he heard them: boots on the cobbles outside, several pairs, unhurried, disciplined, stopping directly in front of his shop. Through the window seam he caught the flash of a badge worked in green wax.

The Crown's witch-hunters did not knock at dusk. They knocked at dawn.

And they had knocked at his door.
