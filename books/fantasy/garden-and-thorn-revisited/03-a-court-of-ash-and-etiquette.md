## 3. A Court of Ash and Etiquette

The invitation came on green wax, which was how Silas knew it was a trap dressed as an opportunity.

*By appointment of Her Ladyship Amara Vess, Chamberlain of the Winter Court: purveyors of fine charms and curiosities are summoned to present their wares for the amusement of the court.*

Amusement. Five years ago the word would have been an insult; now it was a door. The ban had not erased magic from the capital—it had only driven it into the parlors of the wealthy, where a luck-knot sold for thirty crowns and a love-charm for a hundred, and no one called it sorcery because no one called it anything at all. The rich had always believed their sins were merely eccentricities.

Silas read the summons twice by candlelight while the child watched him from the corner, cross-legged on the floor among the dried herbs, turning a sprig of rosemary over and over in small brown fingers.

"They want charm-sellers," he said. "Which means they want something else. The crown doesn't summon tradesmen out of boredom."

The child said nothing. The child never said anything in any tongue Silas could parse, but sometimes—when the wards hummed, when the ledger's pages turned themselves in the night—the child looked at Silas with eyes too old for the face, and Silas felt understood in a way that frightened him more than any patrol.

He packed his case with care. Luck-knots braided from horsehair and river-reed, harmless as bread. A fever-warming stone, its magic so diluted it was barely a memory of a spell. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, three things that could hang him: a warding sigil pressed into slate, a sleep-thorn no longer than a fingernail, and the ledger's torn page, copied out in his own hand, describing the resonance of the royal binding.

If he was searched, he was dead. If he stayed home, the binding failed anyway, and everyone died slower.

"Watch the shop," he told the child, and then, absurdly, "Don't open the door for anyone," as though the child were an ordinary boy who might be tempted by strangers. The child nodded once, gravely, and Silas left feeling like a man who had lied to a priest.

* * *

The palace at dusk was a thing built to be looked up at.

Silas joined the line of peddlers and perfumers at the servants' gate, and even there the architecture pressed down like a thumb—buttresses like ribs, gargoyles with faces worn smooth by rain into expressions of permanent, patient hunger. The stone was pale granite shot through with veins of something darker, and Silas's skin prickled before his mind caught up to what his skin already knew: those dark veins were old work. Guild work. Thornwright mortar-magic, laid centuries ago, still humming under the flagstones like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

His masters had built this place. Or built over it. Or built it *around* something, the way a pearl forms around a wound.

Inside, the corruption of scale continued. Corridors wide enough to drive a cart through, ceilings painted with gods whose faces had been scraped off during some pious century and repainted badly during another. Servants glided past with the blank courtesy of people who had learned that noticing things was a career-ending habit. And everywhere, threaded through the incense and roast meat and beeswax, a smell Silas recognized from his apprenticeship and had hoped never to smell again: cold iron and old blood, the scent of a binding under strain.

The presentation hall held perhaps forty merchants arranged along the walls like exhibits of their own, each behind a small table, each smiling the fixed smile of people being appraised. Nobility drifted between them in silk and spite. Silas set out his wares—a modest display, deliberately second-rate beside the gem-cutters and the vintner with his impossible southern wines—and waited.

They came to him eventually, as the bored always do.

A countess bought a luck-knot for her lapdog. A young lord with wine-flushed cheeks bought two more, one for each mistress, and winked as though they shared a joke about the gullibility of the powerful. An old woman with sharp eyes and sharper knuckles fingered the fever-stone and said nothing, bought nothing, and moved on—and Silas marked her, because she had held the stone the way a person holds a thing they recognize.

Then the hall changed.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, a dropping of temperature, the way air goes still before lightning. Conversation thinned. Even the nobles felt it, though they misread it as awe, and bowed, and the crowd parted like water around a keel.

King Aldric entered at the far end of the hall.

Silas had seen coins with the king's face, woodcuts in the news-sheets, a mural on the customs house forty feet tall. He had expected a stranger. What he had not expected was to recognize the man—or rather, to recognize the *posture*. Aldric walked the way Master Ellery Vane had walked in the last year of the guild's life: spine held straight by will alone, head carried carefully level, each step placed like a word chosen not to offend. The walk of a man containing something.

The king was tall, silvering at the temples, handsome in the way of portraits. He wore the crown lightly, almost carelessly, and smiled, and spoke a gracious word to the vintner, and laughed at something the chamberlain said—and every motion was correct, every motion was *right*, and that was the horror of it. Because Silas, standing twenty feet away with a table of harmless trinkets, could feel what the nobility could not.

Behind the king's eyes there was a room. And in the room, something vast was pacing.

It pressed outward against the inside of Aldric's skull the way a moth presses against a lantern glass—patient, dimly luminous, testing the same pane of glass ten thousand times without anger, because it did not need anger. It needed only time. Silas's hands went cold on the tabletop. The ledger page burned against his chest like a coal. *The vessel holds. The vessel weakens.* His master's handwriting swam up behind his eyes. *We bought nine generations. Pray we bought ten.*

Aldric's gaze swept the hall, courteous, vacant—and snagged on Silas.

For half a heartbeat, nothing. Then the pressure behind the king's eyes *turned*, the way a great fish turns in deep water, and looked at him. Not the king looking. The other thing. It regarded Silas across twenty feet of marble and candlelight with an attention like a hand closing around his throat, and Silas understood, with the clarity of a man falling, that it knew what he was. That it had perhaps been waiting five years for a Thornwright to wander into reach.

And then—worse than the regard, worse than the cold—the king's face flickered.

It was nothing. A blink, a small furrow between the brows, gone in an instant. But in that instant Silas saw the man underneath: exhausted, drowning, *terrified*, mouth shaping a single syllable of some word the crown would not let him speak. Then the smile reassembled itself, seamless as fresh snow over a corpse, and Aldric moved on down the line of merchants, leaving warmth and grace behind him like a wake.

Silas discovered he was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten his knuckles. He made himself let go. Made himself smile. Sold a luck-knot to a baroness whose perfume smelled of lilies and something underneath the lilies, something mineral and wrong, and did not let his hands shake until she had drifted away.

*Get out,* he told himself. *You've seen what you came to see. Get out while your legs still obey you.*

But his legs had stopped obeying somewhere around the moment the thing behind the king's eyes had looked at him, and he stood rooted among his trinkets while the hall roared softly on, wondering how a kingdom could sit down to dinner every night twenty feet from a god wearing its king, and taste nothing wrong in the wine.

* * *

Captain Vessa Corrane had learned to read rooms the way other people read letters, and the presentation hall that evening read like a confession.

She stood in the shadow of the musicians' gallery with her hands folded and her uniform brushed, invisible in the way that only a woman who had spent eleven years making herself invisible could be. Below her, the merchants displayed their wares. Below her, the court performed its nightly liturgy of flattery. And below her, the king smiled.

She did not look at the king. Looking at the king for too long did something to her that she had no vocabulary for—a vertigo, a sense of standing at the top of a staircase in the dark and feeling one stair missing. So she watched the merchants instead, cataloguing hands and eyes, and that was how she found the charm-seller.

He was good. She granted him that. His wares were convincingly dull, his manner convincingly tired, his prices pitched to the exact note of a man grateful for small sales. But he had watched the king's entrance the way no merchant watches a king—not with greed, not with calculation, but with *recognition*. And when the king paused near his table, the man had gone white in a pattern Vessa knew intimately: not the pallor of fear for oneself, but the pallor of someone seeing a wound they know how to treat.

She had seen that pallor once before, on a hedge-witch in Millbrook who had looked at her dying sister and known exactly what was killing her, and had been right, and had been burned for it.

Vessa drifted down from the gallery and along the wall, unhurried, letting the crowd carry her. Up close the charm-seller was unremarkable—mid-thirties, ink stains on his fingers, the calloused hands of a craftsman. His collar sat high. High collars were common this season. High collars were also where men hid tattoos.

"Your luck-knots," she said, picking one up. "What do they actually do?"

"Nothing, Captain." He smiled the tired smile. "That's rather the point of them. Hope, braided."

"Hope is banned too, technically. Article Nine." She set the knot down. "You're new to the palace circuit."

"First summons. My patron thought the exposure worthwhile."

"Who's your patron?"

"A lady prefers discretion."

"Ladies prefer lots of things." Vessa turned the luck-knot over once more, watching his eyes rather than his hands. Steady. Too steady. Men with something to hide either sweated or went very calm; the calm ones were the dangerous ones. "Name and district for the register."

"Silas Reyes. Chandler's Lane, Lowmarket."

She wrote it down slowly, letting him watch her write it, which was its own kind of message. "Chandler's Lane. There was a raid on Chandler's Lane four years ago. Candle-shop cult, blood in the wax. You'd have been there."

"I sell candles, Captain. I try not to attend them."

Despite herself, something in her almost warmed. He had the particular dryness of men who had survived by wit, and wit was a currency she respected even when she confiscated it. She closed the register.

"Master Reyes. A piece of advice, freely given, worth more than your luck-knots. The palace buys charm, but it eats charm-sellers. Present your wares, take your coin, and don't come back." She met his eyes. "Especially don't come back on audience days."

"I'll remember that."

"No," Vessa said quietly. "You really won't. I can tell."

She walked away before he could answer, and reported to no one, and hated herself a little for the reporting to no one. In the corridor outside the hall she passed the king's procession returning to the private apartments, and bowed her head as protocol demanded, and in the half-second of the bow she felt it again—that missing stair, that drop in the dark—and straightened to find the charm-seller watching her from across the hall with an expression she recognized from mirrors.

The expression of a man who has just realized the institution protecting him is the institution he should fear.

Vessa Corrane went back to her office, took out the day's intelligence summaries, and began—without yet admitting to herself why—to pull every file the witch-hunters kept on the Thornwright Guild.

The first file was thin. The second was thinner. The third bore a seal she had never been authorized to see, and when she broke it, the paper inside smelled faintly of green wax and old blood, and the first line read: *In service of the Crown's continuity, the following arrangements have been made regarding the vessel's maintenance—*

Down in the hall, the last of the merchants was packing his case. Down in the hall, the king was laughing at something. Vessa read on, and the candles burned lower, and somewhere beneath the palace, in veins of dark mortar older than the dynasty, something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed of being free.
