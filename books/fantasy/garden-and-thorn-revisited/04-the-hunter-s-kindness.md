## 4. The Hunter's Kindness

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, the way all cells in the Bastion of the Vigilant Hand smelled, as if the building itself had been marinating in confessions for two hundred years. Vessa Corrane had walked these corridors since she was nineteen years old. She knew which torches guttered when the harbor wind turned, which floorboards announced a visitor three steps before he arrived, which of her fellow hunters drank on duty and which merely pretended not to.

She did not know why she had waited four hours to interrogate the charm-seller.

He sat on the bench across from her now, wrists bound with salt-thread cord—the cheap kind, ceremonial more than functional—and looked at her with the particular patience of a man who had already counted the exits. He was thinner than she'd expected. Softer around the eyes than his file suggested. The file said *Silas Reyes, apothecary, suspected minor thaumaturgy, no prior record*. The file was wrong about something; it always was. Files were written by men who believed magic announced itself with thunder.

"Comfortable?" she asked.

"I've slept in worse."

"I know. I've read your landlord's complaint. You sleep in your shop, on a cot behind the counter, under a window that doesn't lock." She set the folder down but didn't open it. "A man who can afford a shop sleeps upstairs like a person. A man hiding something sleeps where he can hear the door."

Something flickered behind his eyes—amusement, maybe, or respect. "You could have just asked my landlord that."

"I'm asking you."

* * *

She had been hunting for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned that everyone lied in their own dialect. Nobles lied in long sentences. Soldiers lied in short ones. Priests lied by asking questions back. But this man—this quiet, ink-stained remnant of a dead trade—lied the way water lies: smoothly, without effort, so that you only noticed the current after you'd stepped in.

"You sold a luck-charm to Master Hobben the baker," she said. "Tuesday last. Copper wire, river stone, a knot tied left over right. My people watched you do it."

"That's a trinket. Superstition. Half the city ties knots for luck."

"The knot held. Hobben's oven, which has burned every loaf since midwinter, produced eight perfect rolls Wednesday morning." She leaned forward. "I ate one. It was very good bread, Reyes. That's the problem."

He said nothing. Outside, the wind turned, and the torch nearest the door guttered exactly as she'd known it would.

"Here is what happens next," she continued. "In an hour, Sergeant Dole comes down with his irons and his questions, and Dole is not me. Dole believes what the Church taught him—that magic is a debt, and debts are collected in flesh. He'll start with your hands. Do you understand? Your hands, because that's where the work shows. Every hedge-witch, every charm-monger, every guild-trained practitioner carries the marks in the fingers first. The calluses that come from tying wards. The burns from quenching sigils in wax."

She watched his fingers curl inward, just slightly, against the salt-cord. There. The tell she'd been fishing for since she sat down.

"You're looking at them right now," she said softly. "So am I."

* * *

Her sister had had hands like that.

Not calloused—Maren had been nine, and nine-year-olds had soft hands, hands that smelled of soap and crushed grass—but there had been a summer, the last one, when Maren had come home with a red thread knotted around her wrist and a story about a woman at the well who traded wishes for secrets. Their mother had cut the thread off and burned it and told Maren never to speak to well-women again, and Maren had promised, the way children promise, with her whole heart and no intention.

Three weeks later the fever came. Not the sweating sickness, not the lung-rot that took half the dockside that autumn—a fever with no name, that burned cold instead of hot, that made Maren speak in a language their mother swore she'd never taught her, words that slid like oil, that made the candles lean toward the bed as though listening.

Vessa had been fourteen. She had sat by the bed for six nights holding her sister's hand while the words poured out of her, and on the seventh night the words stopped, and so did everything else, and the priest who came to write the death-record refused to enter the room until the windows were opened and salt was scattered.

*It took her,* the mother had whispered afterward, staring at nothing. *Whatever that woman gave her—it took her.*

The Church called it possession. The Vigilant Hand called it contamination. Vessa, at fourteen, standing in a graveyard with salt still stinging her palms, had called it the thing she would spend her life killing.

Eleven years later she understood, in the way one understands things at three in the morning, that the well-woman had probably been nobody. A beggar with a talent for stories. A hedge-charmer selling hope at a copper a knot. That the fever had been a fever, that the language had been delirium, that Maren had died of nothing more sinister than being small in a city full of bad air.

And eleven years later she still tied her boots with a double knot every morning, because Maren had died with a knot on her wrist, and some part of Vessa had never stopped believing that if she could just find the right knot to untie—

She caught herself. The prisoner was watching her watch him, and there was something in his face—not pity, worse than pity. Recognition.

* * *

"Your hands," she repeated. "Show me."

"They're tied."

"So they are." She rose, crossed the cell, and cut the cord with her belt-knife—an indulgence, a breach of procedure, the kind of thing Dole would have reported her for. She took his wrists and turned them palm-up in the torchlight.

There. Faint, old, faded almost to nothing: a pattern of fine scarring across the pads of the first two fingers of each hand, the kind that came from years of drawing characters in ash and salt and blood. And below the left thumb, mostly hidden by the cuff he'd kept tugged down all interview—

She pushed the cuff back before he could stop her.

The tattoo was old and deliberately blurred, pricked over with ordinary ink to look like a botched sailor's mark, but she had studied the guild registers until she dreamed in them. A thorned branch, coiled. The mark of the Thornwright Guild, chartered four hundred years, purged twenty-two years ago to the last recorded member.

Except one.

"Well," she said quietly. "The last apprentice."

His jaw tightened. "That mark means nothing. It's decoration. Boys get stupid tattoos."

"Boys get stupid tattoos of ships and girls. Nobody gets a Thornwright thorn unless a Thornwright put it there, and they only ever marked their own." She released his wrists and stepped back. "Every other man who wore that branch is dead or nameless. Purged in the Reckoning, when the Church decided the guild's great working was heresy. Do you know what the great working was, Reyes?"

"No."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "Neither do I. The records burned. Which is either convenient or terrifying, depending on how much you trust the people who lit the fire."

* * *

She should have called Dole. That was the procedure, and procedure was the spine of the Vigilant Hand—she had told a hundred recruits that the day they took their oaths, had told herself that every morning for eleven years. Procedure was what stood between a hunter and the thing she hunted. Procedure was the wall.

But she had sat in this cell for four hours before coming down, turning the arrest report over and over, and the thing that had snagged her wasn't the charm or the knot or the bread. It was a line in the surveillance notes, written by a junior watcher with more diligence than sense:

*Subject observed speaking to child, approx. 7 yrs, unaccompanied, outside shop. Subject appeared distressed. Child spoke briefly; subject covered own ears.*

A child. Unaccompanied. Speaking something that made a grown man cover his ears.

Maren had spoken like that. Words sliding like oil. Candles leaning in to listen.

"Tell me about the boy," she said.

Silas Reyes went very still. It was the stillness of a man whose whole body had become a locked door. "What boy?"

"Don't." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Please don't. I have spent eleven years listening to liars, and I am tired, and it is past midnight, and I am asking you—one question, honestly answered. The child on your step. What did it say to you?"

The torches guttered again. Somewhere above them, the Bastion creaked like a ship.

"I don't know what it said," Reyes said at last, and the terrible thing was that she believed him. "It wasn't a language I knew. Not one I *admitted* knowing. And it opened something in my shop that I sealed five years ago, and captain—" he looked up at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a hole he couldn't see the bottom of— "whatever it was, it wasn't the child's fault. If your people take that child, they won't be saving anyone. They'll be delivering it."

"Delivering it where?"

"To whatever is hungry enough."

* * *

She let him go at the fourth bell, before the shift changed, before Dole arrived with his irons and his appetite.

She told herself it was procedure of another kind: a controlled release, the subject unaware, a tail assigned to follow him home. She wrote the order in her own hand and signed it and felt the lie of it sitting in her stomach like swallowed lead, because she assigned no tail. She wrote *released pending further evidence* and she burned the surveillance note about the child and she watched Silas Reyes walk out of the Bastion into the gray pre-dawn like a man walking out of a dream he hadn't finished.

At the door he stopped. He didn't turn around.

"You could have handed me to Dole," he said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

She thought about Maren's hand going slack in hers. She thought about knots. She thought about the way he'd covered his ears, and the way she had once covered hers, kneeling beside a small burning bed, wishing the words would stop.

"Because I don't know yet which side of the door I'm standing on," she said. "Go home, Reyes. Stay away from the palace. Whatever you're circling—the court, the crown, whatever the guild buried that the Church burned the records over—stay away from it. The thing that sits on that throne isn't a king, and the people who feed it aren't priests, and I have seen the supply ledgers." She heard her own voice drop, heard the fear in it, hated the fear in it. "People go into the palace and don't come out, and the paperwork calls them *honored guests*. Stay away."

He did turn then, halfway, the dawn catching the edge of his face. "And if staying away isn't possible anymore?"

"Then," Vessa Corrane said, "you had better pray the hunter you meet next is as tired as I am."

He went. She stood in the doorway until the fog took him, then climbed back up through the sleeping Bastion to her office, where she unlocked the drawer she never opened, and took out the one document she had sworn never to act upon alone.

An order, countersigned twice, authorizing the transfer of *one (1) child, age approx. seven, recovered harbor district* to the palace household.

Dated tomorrow.
