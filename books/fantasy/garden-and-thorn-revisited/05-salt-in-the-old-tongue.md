## 5. Salt in the Old Tongue

The ledger's last page smelled of tallow and old iron, and it told Silas to go where the harbor kept its dead.

*Where the ninth pier forgets its name,* Master Corvane had written, in a hand gone brown as dried blood. *Descend at slack tide, when the water holds its breath. Bring salt. Bring the boy. Bring nothing you love.*

Silas read it four times by candlelight while the child slept curled in the back room, beneath the coat Silas had draped over him like a shroud too large for its body. Outside, the tide was turning. He could feel it in his teeth—a low, dragging ache, the same ache he'd felt every night for five years without knowing why, the way a dog feels weather coming.

He packed salt in waxed linen. He packed the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. He packed a hooded lantern with a shutter of smoked glass, because light on open water drew patrols, and patrols drew questions, and questions were how witch-hunters fed.

The child woke when Silas lifted him—woke without opening his eyes, the way cats wake, the way things that have died once wake—and put his arms around Silas's neck with the boneless trust of someone who had nowhere else to be.

They went down through the fish-market lanes where the cobbles wept even in dry weather, past the rendering houses with their sweet-rot stink, out along the rotting spine of Pier Nine, which had been condemned so long ago that the condemnation notice had itself rotted to lace. The water below was black and patient. Slack tide. The harbor held its breath.

Silas found the stairs behind a collapsed boathouse, stone steps descending into the dark like the throat of something swallowed. Old wards clung to them still—his guild's work, he realized, his guild's *handwriting*, the particular way Thornwrights knotted a ward into stone so it looked like mere masonry. Five years of hiding his tattoo under his sleeve, five years of selling charms too small to notice, and here was proof that the guild had built things worth hiding all along.

"Stay close," he whispered.

The child said nothing. The child never said anything—not in any tongue Silas knew. But as they descended, the boy began to hum, three notes over and over, and Silas's skin crawled, because those were the first three notes of the Drowning Hymn, the guild's mourning song, which no one had sung since the purges, which the child could not possibly have heard.

The water came up to their knees, then their waists, cold enough to make Silas gasp. The lantern showed him a corridor of black stone, doorways gaping on either side like pulled teeth. In one, shelves still stood, their contents long dissolved into silt. In another, a table with iron rings bolted to its edges. He did not let himself think about what the rings had held.

At the corridor's end: a door of green bronze, crusted white with salt bloom, and across it, in raised letters that his fingers found before his eyes did:

*WE BUILT THE GARDEN. WE PLANTED THE THORN. FORGIVE US THE HARVEST.*

Silas stood in the freezing water with the child humming against his shoulder and felt the words go through him like a fishhook. *Forgive us.* His masters had known. Had written apologies into bronze before the witch-hunters ever came, before the fires, before Silas was born. Whatever the guild had done, they had done it deliberately, and they had been sorry, and sorry had not saved them.

He pressed his palm flat against the door and spoke the counterword—the one every apprentice learned last, the word that opened guild doors and was supposed to die with the guild. Salt flaked away. The bronze groaned. Beyond lay darkness that breathed.

* * *

Inside, the drowned chapterhouse was dry.

That was the first impossible thing. The chamber sat above the waterline somehow, or below it in some way that water refused to acknowledge, and the air within tasted of dust and myrrh and something older underneath, something like a temple sealed since the world was young. Silas's shuttered lantern threw a blade of amber light across rows of benches, a lectern, an altar of black basalt—and on the altar, laid out with ceremonial precision, a book bound in grey leather that was not, Silas understood with a lurch, leather at all.

The child slid from his arms and walked straight to the altar. No hesitation. No wonder. A boy coming home.

"No," Silas said, and then, more gently, "careful."

The small hands hovered over the grey cover. And then the child spoke—spoke clearly, in the Old Tongue, in a voice pitched low with age that no child's throat should hold—and the words were not random. They were a title. Silas had studied enough fragments to know a title when he heard one, the way you know a knife is a knife even in the dark.

*Rite of the Garden and the Thorn. Being the true account of the binding of the Hollow God Vesh-Amaran into the royal line of Aldric's blood, and of the price thereof.*

Silas's mouth went dry. He opened the book with hands that shook, and the pages were dense with script in two columns—the Old Tongue on the left, the common trade-glyphs of the guild on the right, a translation, a mercy, left for whoever came after. Left, he understood slowly, for *him*. The only apprentice left. The survivor. The one they'd engineered to survive.

He read until his eyes burned. He read standing, then kneeling, then sitting on the cold bench with the child watching him with those grave, unchildlike eyes. And what he read was this:

Centuries ago, the thing now wearing King Aldric's crown had come into the world hungry—not for flesh, not for gold, but for *obedience*. It ate will the way fire eats oxygen. Whole cities had knelt until they forgot how to stand. The Thornwright Guild, then young, had fought it with everything they had and lost, and lost, and lost again—until they understood that it could not be killed, only *contained*, and that containment required a vessel of royal blood, willing, sustained by a binding renewed each generation, a garden planted around a thorn.

And the price. The price was written plainly, in the guild's own careful hand, as if they'd hoped plainness would make it bearable.

*A vessel must be living. A vessel must be willing. The binding feeds on consent as the god feeds on will; take the vessel unwilling, and the thorn withers, and the god wakes whole. Each renewal demands a new consent, freely given, from blood of the line. When no consent comes, the binding decays. Decay is measured in years, not centuries.*

The last renewal had been fifty years ago. Aldric's father. And there was no record of another.

Silas looked at the child. The child looked back.

"You knew," Silas said hoarsely. "You've known this whole time."

The boy reached out and touched the page—touched the passage about consent—and then touched his own chest, and then, with terrible gentleness, touched Silas's.

"No," Silas said again. "No, I'm not—I'm not royal blood. I'm nobody. I'm a charm-seller. I hide from patrols." But even as he said it, he remembered the ledger's line: *Bring the boy.* Remembered how his survival through five years of hunts had felt too easy, too lucky, luck-charms that worked a little too well. Remembered Master Corvane's final letter, the one that had arrived after the fires, containing only a single sentence: *Live, Silas. That is your task. You will know why when the child comes.*

He had thought it meant live as in survive. He was beginning to understand it might have meant live as in *remain alive for use*.

The child climbed onto the bench beside him and, for the first time since arriving on his step, made a sound that was not the Old Tongue and not humming—it was a small, human, frightened noise, and he pressed his face into Silas's shoulder, and Silas held him, and the two of them sat in the dry dark of the drowned house while the tide turned outside and the harbor resumed its breathing.

Above them, muffled by stone and water, came the tolling of the palace bells. Not the hour bell. Not the alarm.

The summons bell. Every registered charm-seller in the capital, called to court.

An audience with the king.

Silas closed the grey book and held it against his chest, over his heart, over the guild mark hidden beneath his sleeve, and felt the weight of it settle there like a second skeleton.

The child looked up at him, and in the lantern light his eyes were the exact grey-green of the bronze door, and he spoke one word of the Old Tongue, softly, the way you'd speak to a wounded animal.

Silas didn't need the translation column to understand it.

*Audience.*
