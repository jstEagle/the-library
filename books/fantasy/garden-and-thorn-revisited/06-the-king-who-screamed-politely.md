## 6. The King Who Screamed Politely

The summons came on green wax.

Silas had sold charms in the palace's outer court for six days before the seal found him—a small disc of wax the color of pond water, pressed with a stag's head whose antlers had been broken off at the tines. The chamberlain's clerk who delivered it did not look at him. Nobody at court looked at charm-sellers; that was the whole architecture of the arrangement. They were furniture with prices.

*His Majesty requests the pleasure of your craft. The Queen's solar. After vespers.*

The Queen's solar. Not the public rooms where merchants were permitted to breathe, but the deep rooms, the ones with names instead of numbers. Silas read the card four times and felt the ledger in his coat seem to warm against his ribs, as if the dead master's handwriting recognized its own ink coming home.

"You'll want to dress better than that," the clerk said, already leaving. "There will be lords."

There would be more than lords. Silas spent the afternoon preparing the way he imagined a man prepares for his own funeral: carefully, and with an eye toward what might be salvaged afterward. He packed his case with charms that were honest—small protections, sleep-eases, a salve for grief that worked only because grief wanted to be touched. If he was going to die in the palace, he preferred to do it having sold nothing false that day.

He also sewed a razor into his left cuff. He was not proud of it. He was not ashamed either. Five years of hiding had taught him that shame was a luxury for people who expected to survive their choices.

* * *

The palace at vespers was a held breath.

Candles everywhere, thousands of them, and yet the light seemed afraid to travel. It pooled on marble floors and died at the edges of corridors, so that walking from one room to another felt like wading through dark water between islands. Servants moved with their eyes down. Guards stood too still. Once, passing through an antechamber, Silas heard a woman laughing behind a screen—the bright, unguarded laugh of someone who did not know what building she was in—and the sound was so wrong, so human, that he had to stop and press his hand flat against the wall until it passed.

The Queen's solar smelled of lilies. That was the first cruelty. Lilies meant funerals in every village he'd ever sold in, and here they were arranged by the hundred in silver bowls, fresh every day, because the Queen had loved them once and no one had thought to stop ordering them.

She was not there. He understood, slowly, that she was never there. The chairs were arranged for company that did not come. A half-finished embroidery frame stood by the window, dust furred along its top edge like snow that had stopped falling years ago.

And by the fire, alone, sat the King of Cael Morath.

"Master Reyes." The voice was warm. Rich. A voice built for proclamations. "Come to the light. My eyes are old."

They were not old. Aldric Vane had been crowned at thirty-one, and even now, across the room, Silas could see the youth of him preserved like fruit in syrup—the strong jaw, the brown hair going silver only at the temples in a way that flattered rather than aged. But the eyes. The eyes were the thing he had glimpsed across the throne room a week ago and told himself he had imagined.

They were patient. Nothing else in the world was patient like that. Fire wasn't patient. Winter wasn't patient. These eyes had the patience of something that had waited inside stone for centuries and considered the wait a formality.

"Your Majesty honors me," Silas said, and bowed, and hated how smoothly the lie came. Five years of practice. His masters would have wept—or laughed.

"I am told you make excellent charms." The King gestured, gracious, and Silas approached and opened his case and began the performance. Sleep-ease. Luck-knots. A salve for the hands that ache when rain is coming. The King listened to each description with apparent delight, turning the little objects over in fingers that were careful, almost tender, handling them the way a child handles birds.

"Tell me," the King said, holding up a luck-knot to the firelight, "do you believe in what you sell?"

"Belief isn't required, Majesty. Only results."

"A merchant's answer." The smile widened. "I had a guild of men who gave me poets' answers, once. They are all dead."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the corridor, a door closed with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"The Thornwright Guild," Silas said carefully. "Before my time. I inherited some of their patterns. Their knots. Nothing else."

"Nothing else." The King set the luck-knot down. And then, in a voice that had not changed at all—warm, rich, kind—he said:

*Veth-ka-oren. Sul maethen ir. Veth-ka-oren.*

Kill me. Please. Kill me.

Perfect Old Tongue. Not the gutter-cant the child spoke, not the broken ritual fragments Silas had pieced together from a drowned ledger, but the language entire, the language that bound kings, pronounced with the accent of someone who had been speaking it for a very long time. Longer than a lifetime. Longer than a reign.

Silas's hands went still over his open case.

"It is rude to stare," the King said pleasantly, in Caelish again, smiling at him with those patient eyes. "But you may stare. I give you leave. I give you leave to do anything at all, Master Reyes, and I find I mean it."

"Majesty—"

"He sleeps sometimes." The King tilted his head, listening to something. "The one who wears my face when I am tired. He sleeps after the feeding days. You have seen the feeding days—the processions, the prisoners, the ones who go into the deep chapel and do not come out. He calls it maintenance. He is very fond of the word *maintenance*. He learned it from a chancellor who has been dead eleven years and does not know it yet."

The words came out in the same warm voice, the same measured cadence, and that was the horror of it—that the thing and the man shared one throat, and you could only tell them apart by what they chose to say.

"I am Aldric Vane," the King said. "I want that recorded somewhere. In case. I am Aldric Vane, crowned in the spring of my thirty-first year, and I have been awake inside this"—the hand turned over, examining itself—"for longer than I can count, because counting requires a self that ends, and mine does not end. Do you understand? No. Of course not. Your guild understood. They built the cage. They knew exactly what they were putting in it, and what it would cost to keep it fed, and they wrote it all down in a book and then they let themselves be killed so no one would ever read it."

"They didn't let themselves," Silas heard himself say. "They were purged."

"Were they?" The King smiled. "Ask your ledger. Ask it why the binding needed a bloodline. Ask it why the cage was built around a crown instead of a cell. They were not purged, Master Reyes. They were *spent*. There is a difference, and the difference is that spending implies intent."

The lilies' sweetness was suddenly nauseating. Silas became aware of his own heartbeat, loud and stupid, a village drum at a state funeral.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"I told you." The King leaned forward, and the firelight moved across his face, and for just a moment—for one flickering instant—the patience cracked, and underneath it Silas saw a young man drowning, hands pressed against the underside of ice. "*Kill me.*"

"I can't—"

"You can. Don't insult us both. You carry the last of their craft in that coat, and I can smell it, I have been able to smell it since you walked into my court smelling of harbor salt and dead men's ink. There is a rite. There is always a rite; they were very thorough people. It will hurt. Good. Let it hurt. I have earned a painful death the way other kings earn monuments."

"And if I refuse?"

The warmth returned like a lid closing. The King sat back, folded his hands, became a portrait of himself.

"Then he finishes waking," the King said gently, "and no one will need to kill anyone ever again, because wanting will be done away with entirely. He is very efficient. You would admire him. Everyone admires him. That is rather the problem."

Somewhere beyond the door, boots. Two sets, unhurried, the pace of guards who had been told to check on something at a specific time.

The King heard them too. Something moved behind his eyes—a shutter, a change of tenants—and when he spoke again the Old Tongue came fast, tumbling, urgent, the diction of a man running out of minutes:

*Sul maethen ir. Sul maethen ir—veth-ka-oren sul, ka-oren sul, ka—*

and then the word broke in the middle, snapped clean, and the King of Cael Morath looked at Silas Reyes with perfect, pleasant patience and said:

"You must forgive me. I find I'm tired. Captain Corrane will see you out."

* * *

Vessa Corrane was waiting in the corridor with two witch-hunters and a lantern, and her face was carved from official neutrality, and her eyes were not.

"Master Reyes." She fell into step beside him as the hunters took up positions behind—not escorting, *herding*, though gently, the way you herd something you intend to examine rather than slaughter. "An audience with His Majesty. How fortunate."

"He requested a luck-charm," Silas said. "For the harvest season."

"Of course." Her voice was silk over a blade. "And did you sell him one?"

"I sold him three. He has a great many seasons to worry about."

They walked. The candles guttered as they passed, all of them, in sequence, and Silas saw Vessa notice it too—saw her jaw tighten, saw her hand drift toward the iron rod at her belt and then deliberately away. She was hunting him. She had been hunting him since the outer court. And she was also, he realized with a vertigo that nearly buckled his knees, *afraid of this place*, and hiding it better than he was.

At the doors to the servants' stair she dismissed the hunters with a flick of two fingers. They hesitated. She repeated it. When they were gone she stood close, too close, and spoke low enough that the words barely disturbed the candlelight.

"There is a window in the Queen's solar," she said. "North wall, behind the tapestry. It opens onto the roof of the chapel cloister. It has been broken for nine years. No one repairs it because no one wants to admit they know about it." She met his eyes. "Whatever you sold the King tonight, Master Reyes—I suggest you deliver the rest of the order quickly, and then take a long journey, and never come back."

"Captain—"

"*Never come back.*" For a moment the neutrality cracked, and he saw the sister in her, the wound, the reason. "You don't know what you're standing next to. I do. Go home. Burn whatever brought you here. Pray it doesn't follow you."

Then she stepped aside, and the way out was open, and Silas walked through it because the alternative was dying in a corridor for a principle no one would ever learn.

But at the turn of the stair he stopped. Because behind him, faint through three doors and a wall, in a voice built for proclamations, warm and rich and perfectly polite, the King of Cael Morath was screaming.

It did not sound like pain. That was the worst of it. It sounded like etiquette—a host protesting that his guest had stayed too long, a man apologizing for the noise, *please, please, I beg you, I beg you*—begging in the Old Tongue, begging in Caelish, begging in a third language Silas had never heard and hoped never to hear again, while the lilies sweetened the air and the candles burned straight and no one, anywhere in the palace, so much as paused.

Silas pressed his back to the cold stone and put his hand over the ledger against his ribs and made himself listen to all of it. Every second. Because his masters had built this. Because the ledger was written in his inheritance. Because the man behind the door had asked him for the only mercy left, and Silas had walked away, and walking away was a choice too, and he intended to remember the sound of it for the rest of his life—which, he understood now, looking down the dark stair toward the city, might be considerably shorter than he'd planned.

He had come to the palace to spy on a monster.

He was leaving knowing the monster's name, and the name was Aldric Vane, and the monster was the smaller of the two things wearing the crown.

Tomorrow, he would find the drowned guildhouse. Tonight, he had a window behind a tapestry to climb through, a captain's strange mercy to survive, and a promise to keep to a dead king who was not dead yet.

Behind him, fainter now, the screaming resolved into words he could finally translate, and he wished he hadn't:

*Find the vessel. Find the child.*

The hunt had begun.
