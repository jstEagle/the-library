## 7. Betrayal in Green Wax

The summons came at the hour when candles gutter and honest work becomes confession.

Vessa Corrane stood in the antechamber of the Office of Vigilance, watching wax run down the throat of a candelabrum shaped like a rearing stag. Someone had designed it, decades ago, to look fierce. Now it only looked like an animal melting. She knew the feeling.

"Captain Corrane." The undersecretary's voice was dry as old vellum. "You'll want to sit."

"I'll stand."

He didn't press it. He was a small man with ink-stained fingers and the particular pallor of someone who had spent thirty years indoors making other people's horrors legible. He slid a folder across the desk—green wax seal, unbroken, heavy enough that it made a sound against the wood like a struck knuckle.

"You've been asking questions," he said. "About requisitions from the Lower Harbors district."

"I audit my own jurisdiction."

"You audited *upward*, Captain." His smile was thin and professional. "That's not your jurisdiction. That's mine."

She looked at the folder and did not touch it. "Then tell me what I'm not supposed to know, and I'll go back to not knowing it."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Pity, maybe, or its uglier cousin, embarrassment on her behalf. "Open it," he said quietly. "You were always going to. Better you read it here than in some archive where you'd draw conclusions without context."

So she broke the seal.

The first pages were ordinary enough: census tallies for the river wards, tax rolls, lists of names with occupations beside them—cooper, laundress, lamplighter, widow. It was the third document that stopped her breath. A schedule. Not a court calendar, not a grain ledger. A *schedule*, in the neat clerical hand she recognized from a hundred official communiqués, listing dates going back four years, and beside each date, a name, and beside each name, a single word:

*Rendered.*

Four years. One hundred and six names. She counted twice because her hands were shaking and she thought she must have miscounted, because one hundred and six was too many, because this was the Crown, her Crown, the thing she had bled for since she was nineteen years old—

"The crown requires sustenance," the undersecretary said, in the tone of a man reciting a supply inventory. "The binding is a mechanism, Captain, and mechanisms consume fuel. The alternative is—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the palace, toward the king, toward everything, "—instability. You've seen what happens when His Majesty has a difficult week. The riots in Candlemaker's Row last spring? That was a *good* week. That was the king holding himself together through sheer will."

"That wasn't the king."

"No," he agreed mildly. "It wasn't. Which is precisely why these arrangements exist. Stability, Captain. The city eats, the markets hold, the king reigns, and one hundred and six people over four years is a rounding error against the tens of thousands who would die in a true unraveling. You of all people should understand arithmetic like that. You've done it yourself—every time you burn a hedge-witch rather than let her craft spread. One life against many."

"That's different."

"Is it?" He tilted his head, genuinely curious, and that curiosity was the worst part. "Because you took magic from them. We take people to feed the thing you keep everyone afraid of. From where I sit, Captain, we're colleagues."

She heard herself say, very calmly, "Who selects them?"

"The Office of Records flags candidates. Indigent, unattached, unlikely to be missed. The Vigilance provides transport. There's a facility beneath the palace—the old cisterns—and a rite performed quarterly by—" he paused, choosing his word with care, "—a contractor. You don't need the name."

But she already knew. She had seen the residue of that rite once, three years ago, in a sealed chamber she'd been told to forget: scorched sigils in a circle, and the smell of salt and burnt hair, and something in the walls that had *leaned* toward her like a dog scenting meat.

"One hundred and six," she said.

"A hundred and nine now. The quarter's rendering was Tuesday."

Tuesday. She had spent Tuesday reviewing patrol rotations. She had eaten lunch. She could not stop thinking about lunch—she had eaten lunch while they fed a lamplighter or a widow or someone's forgotten uncle into the dark under the palace, and the city above had gone on being stable, and she had called that stability a victory.

"Dismissed, Captain," the undersecretary said gently. "And Corrane—this stays in this room. For your own sake. Grief makes people careless."

*Grief.* As if it were grief. As if her sister were anything like this—as if the thing that took Maren had been anything but a hungry accident, wild and mindless, while this was *clerical*. This had a schedule. This had a filing system. This had an undersecretary with ink-stained fingers explaining it to her in the tone of a man discussing grain tariffs.

She saluted. Her arm moved because she told it to, the way you tell a dog to heel.

* * *

She walked the city for hours without deciding to.

Her feet took her down through the merchant terraces, past the fountain where she'd taught Maren to skim copper coins, past the shrine where her mother had prayed for safe births and gotten half of what she asked for. The lamps were being lit. Somewhere a baker was pulling loaves, and the smell of it drifted warm across the street, and Vessa thought: *he doesn't know. None of them know. They think the fear of witches is what keeps the dark out, and all the while we've been carrying the dark our dinners.*

A drunk outside a tavern grabbed her sleeve and sang three bars of a song about the good King Aldric, who kept the roads safe and the granaries full, and she gave him a coin to let go of her, and hated herself for how much the coin cost her.

By midnight she was standing in front of the shuttered charm-shop in Tanners' Lane, looking up at the dark windows, hating herself for that too.

She had warned him away from the palace. She had put him in handcuffs once and taken them off again, and neither of those things had been kindness exactly—they had been recognition, the wordless acknowledgment between two people who had both lost someone to the same hunger. But going to him now was different. Going to him now was treason, and she knew it, and she stood in the cold anyway, listening to her own breathing, waiting for the part of her that was still a captain of the Vigilance to talk her out of it.

It didn't come. That was the terrible thing. Twenty years of service, and when she reached for the voice of the institution, there was nothing there. Just the memory of a green wax seal cracking under her thumb, and a list of names, and the word *rendered* written in a hand so neat it might have been a recipe.

She knocked.

It took a long time. Long enough that she considered leaving twice, and had raised her fist a third time when the door cracked open on a chain and one wary eye.

"It's midnight," Silas Reyes said.

"I know."

"You're the witch-hunter."

"I'm unemployed," she said, "as of about an hour ago. Or I will be, by dawn, once they notice I haven't reported. May I come in before I make it official?"

He studied her for a long moment. Behind him, deeper in the shop, she saw movement—a small shape by the cold hearth, a child sitting perfectly still, watching her with eyes that seemed older than the room. The boy didn't blink. Witch-hunters, Vessa thought, probably featured in his nightmares, and here she was on his doorstep confirming them.

"There's a list," she said. "One hundred and nine names. Citizens. They've been feeding them to whatever wears the king's face, four years, maybe longer, to keep the binding from failing early. Your guild built that binding. I think you already know that. And I think you're planning to do something about it, and I am—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it would have cost more, "—I am done being on the side that files the hunger under 'stability.'"

Silence from the doorway. Then the chain slid free.

Inside smelled of ash and dried herbs and old paper. The child watched her from the hearth the whole time, unblinking, and when she sat down across from him, he spoke—not in the trade-tongue, but in that other language, the old one, the one that made the air feel heavier. Three words. Soft as a blessing.

"What did he say?" she asked.

Silas had gone pale. "He said, 'The thorn remembers the hand that pruned it.'" He looked at her, and then at the boy, and something passed between them that she couldn't read. "He knows why you're here."

"Do you?" she asked the child directly, uselessly, knowing he wouldn't answer her in words she owned.

The boy smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone remembering a very long story and deciding, graciously, to begin it soon.

Vessa pulled the folded copy of the list from inside her coat—all one hundred and nine names, copied out in her own hand before she left the Office, because if she was going to burn her life down she intended to at least carry the evidence—and laid it on the table between them.

"I need to know if any of them can still be saved," she said. "And if not, I need to help you end it. Whatever it costs."

The fire ticked. Outside, the bells marked the hour, and somewhere far off, beneath the palace, beneath the cisterns, beneath one hundred and nine names, something shifted in its sleep—and the lamp flames in the shop all leaned sideways at once, though there was no wind.

Silas looked at the leaning flames, and then at her, and said quietly:

"We don't have until dawn."
