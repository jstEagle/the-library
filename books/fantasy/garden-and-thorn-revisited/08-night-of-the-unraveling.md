## 8. Night of the Unraveling

The binding broke at moonrise, and it broke like a tooth.

Silas felt it first in his own wards—the little charms he'd hung around the safehouse, cheap things, baker's-work—going dark one by one, snuffed in a wave rolling from the palace outward, as if a great hand had passed over the city and pinched out every candle of craft. Then the child sat up in the dark and said, in the Old Tongue, in a voice that was not entirely a child's voice: *It is loose.*

Then every bell in Vellharren began to ring.

Not the measured peals of festival or alarm. This was chaos, all the bells at once, all the towers, the harbor chapel, the guildhall that hadn't rung since the plague years—a sound like the city itself screaming through bronze throats. And beneath the bells, beneath even the roar of the first fires, Silas heard the other thing: a pressure, a hum below hearing, seeping into the bones. Compulsion. He had read about it in his master's ledger with the detached horror of a man reading about drowning. Now he stood in it. It poured off the palace hill like fog off cold water, filling the streets, filling the lungs, whispering *find it, find the key, find the child* into ten thousand sleeping minds.

Vessa Corrane came through the door with her sword already drawn and her eyes wide as a woman waking from a nightmare she'd been having for years without knowing.

"It's in my head," she said. "Gods, Silas, it's in *everyone's* head."

"I know." He was already moving, sweeping the ledger, the salt, the rite-scrolls into a satchel. "How long before the whole city is its hand?"

"Hours. Maybe less. The garrison will mobilize by midnight—they won't know why they're marching, they'll just *know*, the way you know to breathe." She caught the child up onto her hip with a soldier's economy, and the child wrapped thin arms around her neck and pressed their face into her hair. "Where do we go?"

"The throne room. The rite has to be spoken where the binding anchors—in the presence of the crown." Silas slung the satchel across his back and looked at them both, this witch-hunter and this impossible child, and felt the absurd tenderness of people about to do something doomed together. "There's no other way. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize," Vessa said. "Run."

They went out through the kitchen and into the burning city.

* * *

Vellharren burned beautifully, which was the worst thing about it. The compulsion had not made the people mindless; that would have been mercy. Instead it had made them *certain*. Silas saw a fishwife standing ankle-deep in harbor water holding a lantern high, scanning the crowd with the serene focus of a woman looking for her lost child—and he knew she was looking for the child on Vessa's hip, knew it the way you know your own name, because the same knowledge hummed in his own skull, patient and warm and wrong. *Find it. Find the key.* The god's voice wore the shape of longing. That was how it worked. It didn't command; it convinced you the finding was your own idea, your heart's own wish.

A mob had gathered at the mouth of Cooper's Row, fifty strong, carrying torches and hammers and, absurdly, a church bell rope. They were not rampaging. They were *searching*, house to house, politely, terribly, knocking on doors with the courtesy of neighbors, and when no answer came, breaking them down with the same courtesy.

"The aqueduct," Vessa breathed. "Under it, along the channel—we come up inside the palace grounds. The old servants' way."

"You know the palace sewers?"

"I know the palace," she said grimly. "I've dragged eleven charm-sellers out of it."

They cut north through the tannery lanes, where the smoke lay thick and sweet-rotten, and the child rode silent against Vessa's shoulder, eyes open, watching everything. Twice Silas felt the compulsion tighten around his own thoughts like a hand testing a knot, and twice he bit his tongue until he tasted blood and used the pain to hold himself. Small magics still lived in him—the god's flood had drowned the great workings but missed what was too small to notice, the lesson of five years written in blood now paying itself out. A misdirection charm on the three of them, stitched from a button and a prayer. A hush-charm on the child's breathing. Nothing more. He was spending himself like a miser forced into generosity, coin by coin, knowing there was no purse left after tonight.

At the aqueduct arch a patrol found them anyway.

Six men of the city watch, torchlight on their halberds, faces slack with that terrible serenity. Their sergeant stepped forward and said, gently, "Friends. We're looking for a lost child."

"So are we," Vessa said, and her voice did something Silas had never heard it do—it cracked. "Ours. Please. Have you seen—" and she was crying, real tears, and Silas understood with a lurch that she wasn't acting, or wasn't only acting; she was using the truth of her sister, dead these fifteen years, taken by magic, and turning her grief into a key that fit the lock of any lie. The sergeant's serene face wavered. Serenity could not parse grief. The compulsion whispered *find the child*, but Vessa's tears said *I have already lost one*, and between those two voices the men stood frozen, courteous and confused, while three shadows slipped under the arch and into the dark of the water channel.

For a long time after, walking the black tunnel with the city roaring faintly above them, nobody spoke. Then the child said, in the Old Tongue, softly, the words landing in Silas's chest like stones into still water:

*She gave them her sorrow. That is a magic too. They never wrote that one down.*

"No," Silas managed. "They never did."

* * *

They came up out of the servants' stair into the palace kitchens, and the kitchens were empty, every pot cold, every fire out, and that emptiness frightened Silas more than any mob. The palace had been hollowed. Everyone in it was upstairs, or on the walls, or in the great court below the throne room windows, kneeling—thousands of them, visible through the leaded glass as Silas crept past, a field of white faces turned toward the royal tower, waiting, adoring, *certain*. The god was feeding. He could feel it drawing on them, drinking their certainty, growing denser behind Aldric's eyes.

Vessa led them up through the body of the palace, past tapestries of hunts that had happened two hundred years ago, past guards who stood at their posts with their eyes closed, dreaming the god's dream. On the third landing a chamberlain met them, an old man with kind eyes, and said pleasantly, "His Majesty is not receiving," and drew a knife from his sleeve, and Vessa broke his wrist and put him to sleep with a blow she softened at the last instant, catching him so he wouldn't fall loudly, lowering him to the stone like a tired child. Her hands were shaking afterward.

"He's my king," she said, to no one, to herself. "Whatever's wearing him. He's still—"

"I know," Silas said. "That's why we're doing it this way instead of the easy way."

The easy way was a knife. He had thought about it. He suspected Vessa had too. But the ledger was explicit, in his master's cramped and guilty hand: *kill the vessel and the thing slips free into whatever is nearest and willing. You cannot kill it. You can only hold it.* Hold it, or give it a better prison. The rite required a vessel that opened willingly. There were no shortcuts. There never were, in the Thornwright books; that was the whole cursed point of the Thornwrights.

The doors of the antechamber to the throne room stood ahead of them, gilt and enormous, carved with the garden-and-thorn motif the guild had once owned like a signature—roses grown through iron, beauty bound in barbed restraint. From beyond the doors came a sound that stopped Silas's breath.

Singing. Hundreds of voices, low and harmonized, coming from the great court below—but underneath the singing, closer, just past the doors, another sound. A voice speaking the Old Tongue. Pleading.

Aldric. Still in there. Still fighting.

The child slid down from Vessa's hip and stood before the doors, small hands opening and closing. When the child spoke this time, it was in the common tongue, and very quietly, and it was the most terrible thing Silas had heard all night:

"He's asking us to hurry."

Vessa set her shoulder against the door. Silas laid his palm flat against the wood and felt, through the grain, through the gilt, the vast slow heartbeat of the thing on the throne, and threaded through it, faint as a thread of silver in black wool, the rhythm of a man still refusing to let go.

Beyond the doors, the crown-thing turned its attention toward them—all of it, the whole weight of a god's regard, pressing through the oak like deep water against a dam.

And the doors began, gently, courteously, to open on their own.
