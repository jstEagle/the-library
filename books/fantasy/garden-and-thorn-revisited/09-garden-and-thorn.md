## 9. Garden and Thorn

The throne room doors had been torn from their hinges, and the smoke that poured through the gap was not smoke at all.

Silas knew this because smoke did not whisper. This did. It curled along the marble in slow grey ribbons, and each ribbon carried a voice—the voices of everyone the crown-thing had ever worn, he thought, or perhaps only everyone it had eaten since dawn. The city was screaming beyond the windows, and here, inside, there was only this soft, patient murmur, like a mother humming over a cradle that held something dead.

Vessa went through first, sword drawn, her burned orders still smelling of ash in the pocket of her coat. The child gripped Silas's hand with fingers gone cold and small and terribly certain.

"Remember," the child said—not in the Old Tongue now, but plainly, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "You are the garden. It is the thorn. A garden does not fight the thorn with fists."

"I know," Silas said. His mouth was dry as old paper. "I wrote it down five times."

"You copied it," the child said gently. "Writing is different."

* * *

The throne room was a wound.

Whoever had built it had intended light—tall windows of colored glass, pillars carved with the faces of kings going back nine hundred years—but the compulsion had reached here first, and the room wore its damage like a bruise. The glass was intact but wrong, its colors bleeding into one another, red into gold into a green that hurt to look at. Courtiers lay scattered across the floor like dropped puppets, breathing, eyes open, all of them facing the throne. None of them blinked.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

He had been a broad man once; Silas remembered him from the audience, from the momentary flicker of the man behind the mask. Now he seemed hollowed, his skin stretched over his skull like wet vellum, his hands gripping the arms of the throne so hard the gilding had flaked away beneath his nails. The crown sat on his head the way a spider sits on a wrapped fly.

Between them, in the air above the dais steps, hung the binding.

It was older than Silas had imagined from the ledger's diagrams. Not a circle, not a chain—a *garden*, an arrangement of living sigils grown through the king himself, roots sunk behind his eyes, thorned vines threaded down his spine, blossoms opening and closing along his arms in a rhythm almost like breath. The Thornwrights' masterwork. Five centuries of careful cultivation, and every blossom was browning at the edges.

"It knows we're here," Vessa said quietly.

The king's head turned. The movement was smooth, too smooth, the way water is smooth, and when the thing behind his eyes looked at Silas it did not look with menace. It looked with *recognition*, warm and terrible, the way a landlord looks at a tenant who has finally come home.

*"Little thornwright,"* it said, and the voice came from Aldric's throat and from the walls and from the mouths of the sleeping courtiers all at once. *"You brought me my key."*

Silas stepped forward. His knees wanted to buckle; he made them hold. "I brought your ending."

*"Yes,"* the god said, agreeable, almost fond. *"That is what they all bring. Your masters said it too, the night they planted themselves in my flesh. Come closer. I have been alone in this man for thirty years. He dreams very loudly."*

* * *

The rite required three things, and Silas had two of them.

The ledger had been clear as a knife: the words, spoken in the Old Tongue by a bearer of the guild-mark; the key, the living memory, to speak the final word; and a vessel—willing, awake, unafraid—to receive what was torn loose. Without the third, the rite would tear the god free and it would simply pour into the nearest open soul, and the nearest open souls were four hundred sleepwalking citizens and one witch-hunter who had followed him into hell on a promise she didn't fully believe.

So he began anyway. There was nothing else to do. He would buy minutes if he could not buy freedom.

He knelt at the base of the dais and spoke the first word, and the world changed key.

Every sound in the room dropped away except the Old Tongue, which was less a language than a place—he felt it around him like weather, like standing in a garden at the hour before dawn when everything is deciding whether to be real. The sigils on the king's body flared white. The browned blossoms shuddered. And Aldric screamed—

—and then stopped screaming, and looked at Silas with his own eyes.

They were ordinary eyes. Tired, bloodshot, human. Thirty years of imprisonment looked out of them, and beneath that, further down, something worse: hope.

"You're one of theirs," the king whispered. His voice was wrecked, rusted from disuse. "A thornwright. I prayed—I used to pray for—" He laughed, a broken sound. "Do you know what it's like? Being a garden? Feeling it put roots in you and being *grateful*, because the alternative is—"

"I know," Silas said. "I'm sorry. We did this to you. My guild did this to you."

"Yes," Aldric said simply. "You did." And there was no hatred in it, which was somehow the most terrible thing yet. "Can you take it out?"

"That's why I came."

"Then do it." The king's hands loosened on the throne's arms, finger by finger, like a man letting go of a ledge. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it takes out of you. Do you think I haven't watched it count the cost of *me*?" His gaze flicked past Silas, to Vessa at the door, to the child. "Get them out after. Promise me."

"I promise," Silas said, and meant it, and hated how small a promise it was against what he was about to attempt without a vessel.

He spoke the second word.

* * *

The god fought the way a forest fights fire—by being everywhere at once.

The compulsion hit Silas like a wave of hot iron. Every ward he'd ever woven, every charm too small to notice, every trick of his five hidden years—all of it was nothing, a candle held up to a sunrise that wanted to eat him. The god poured itself down the channels of the binding and out into the room, and Silas felt it reach for the courtiers, for Vessa, for the child, felt it find the shape of his own mind and begin, patiently, lovingly, to move in.

*You could rest,* it murmured inside his skull, in his own voice, in his master's voice, in the baker's voice, in everyone's. *You have been tired for five years, little thornwright. I have room. I have such room.*

Silas bit through his own lip and kept speaking. Blood filled his mouth; he spat it onto the marble and drew the next sigil in it, and the Old Tongue rose around him like floodwater. The binding blazed. Blossoms tore free of the king's arms and hung burning in the air. Somewhere far away Vessa was shouting, holding the door against something, and the child stood unmoved amid the storm, watching Silas with ancient, patient eyes.

But the rite stalled where the ledger had always known it would stall. The god was loose in the channels now, thrashing, and there was nowhere for it to go. No vessel. No willing soul. The words circled and foundered like birds in a closed hall, and Silas felt the failure coming the way you feel a bridge start to sway.

Then Aldric stood up.

The motion cost him everything—Silas could see it costing him, see the god wrenching at his spine, see the man override it through sheer, stubborn, mortal will, the last coin of a bankrupt treasury spent all at once. He came down the dais steps on legs that shook. He took Silas's face in his ruined hands.

"Fifty years," the king said. "It promised me fifty years of glory when I was young and stupid, and it has given me thirty years of being a house. I am done being a house." He smiled, and it was the first honest expression Silas had seen on a crowned face in his whole life. "I can't be willing. It's in too deep—it owns the wanting in me. But I can *hold*. I can hold it still while you finish. That much of me is still mine."

"Aldric—"

"Hold it," the king said, and turned, and climbed back up to his throne, and sat down in it, and gripped the arms, and *held*.

The god screamed through him. The whole palace screamed through him. But the channels of the binding went rigid, locked, frozen in the grip of a dying man's will, and for eleven heartbeats—Silas counted them, he would count them for the rest of his life—for eleven heartbeats the way was open.

"The word," Silas gasped, turning to the child. "Now. Give me the word."

The child opened its mouth.

Nothing came out.

The child's eyes went wide with horror—the first childish thing Silas had ever seen it do—and it tried again, and again, and the final word, the keystone, the name of unbinding, would not cross its lips. Because the word was not knowledge. The word was *memory*, and memory needs a vessel of its own, a throat, a grief, a self—and the child was only the guild's echo, and echoes cannot carry weight that far.

"It won't come," the child whispered. "Silas, it won't—"

Eleven heartbeats became ten. On the throne, Aldric's knuckles were white as bone. The god's attention swung toward the child, hungry, and Silas understood, in one blazing instant, what the ledger's last line had truly meant—the line he'd read five times and never understood:

*The vessel speaks. The speaker becomes.*

Not the child speaking through him. Him, becoming the channel, becoming the mouth, becoming the thing the word passed through—and a word that size, passing through a man, does not leave him unchanged.

Silas opened his own mouth, reached into the place where the child's memory lived pressed against his palm like a seed, and pulled.

The word came.

It came through him like a river through a reed, and it took the reed's shape with it. He felt his name go first—*Silas Reyes, last apprentice of the Thornwright Guild*—felt it loosen and lift away like a leaf off current. Then five years of hiding. Then the shop, the shutters, the smell of wax. He grabbed at what remained and found his hands still knew the sigils, and he used them, weeping blood, shaping the word as it unmade him, guiding the god out of Aldric's body and into the open air where it had no purchase, no root, no garden—

The crown-thing left the king the way a shadow leaves a wall at noon: all at once, and shrieking, and already thinning.

Aldric slumped. Alive. Breathing. Empty, and weeping without sound, and free.

And the god, torn loose, homeless, dying, whirled through the throne room like a storm looking for a window—and found Silas, whose name was half-gone, whose self was a door already ajar.

*There you are,* it said, almost tenderly. *Garden.*

"No," Silas said—or tried to say. What came out was the Old Tongue, and it sounded, even to him, like an acceptance.

The last thing he felt clearly was the child's hand in his, and Vessa's shout from the doorway, and the strange, enormous quiet of a man setting down a name he had carried for twenty-six years.

Then the thorn took root in the garden, and the garden closed over it, and Silas Reyes went down into the dark of himself like a stone into a well—falling past his own memories, past the ledger, past the boy who had knocked at dusk—deeper, deeper, toward a vault at the bottom of his soul where something vast was waiting to be kept.

Somewhere above him, impossibly far away, a child began to sing the closing words of the rite.

And somewhere below, in the dark, Silas—who was not quite Silas anymore, and not yet anything else—began to dream.
