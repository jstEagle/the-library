## 10. The Apprentice Remembers

The god came out of the king like smoke from a snuffed candle, and it had nowhere to go.

Silas felt its hunger the moment the child's final word tore it loose—a vast, formless need, five centuries of imprisonment compressed into a single shrieking instant of *want*. It filled the throne room the way floodwater fills a cellar, pressing against walls, against windows, against the burning city beyond, searching for a shape to pour itself into. Aldric collapsed on the dais, weeping, human, his hands empty for the first time in years. Vessa caught him before his head struck the marble.

And the god turned toward the child.

*No.* Silas was moving before he understood he had decided anything. The ledger had told him this much in drowned ink: *a vessel must be willing; a vessel must be named; and what is taken willingly cannot be torn free again.* He had read those lines by lantern-light beneath the harbor and thought himself prepared. He had not been prepared. Nothing prepares you for the cold.

It entered through his open mouth, or perhaps through the guild tattoo on his wrist—there was no telling where one ended and the other began—and Silas Reyes learned what it meant to be a garden with something buried in it that was not a seed.

The pain was not pain, exactly. It was subtraction. The god did not fight him; it *unmade* him, patiently, the way rot unmakes timber from the inside out. It began with the small things. The smell of his mother's bread—he reached for it and found only the word *bread*, the memory gone soft as wet paper. The name of the street he'd grown up on. The color of his master's eyes. Each loss was gentle, almost kind, like falling asleep one finger at a time.

He clung to what remained. He made a list, because lists were what apprentices did, and recited it into the roaring dark:

*I am Silas Reyes. I sold luck-charms too small to notice. I hid my tattoo from patrols. A boy knocked at my door at dusk and spoke the Old Tongue, and I remembered what I was for.*

*I am Silas Reyes. My masters did not die out. They were purged. They built the binding, and the binding was a lie we told a kingdom, and the kingdom believed us, and the belief held for five hundred years.*

*I am—*

*I am—*

Outside the throne room windows, the compulsion broke over the capital like a wave receding. People who had spent the night hunting a child through burning streets stopped mid-stride, looked at their torches, at their neighbors, at their own hands, and began to weep or scream or simply sit down in the ash. Somewhere a bell began to ring—not alarm, just ringing, the way bells ring when no one has given them permission to stop.

Vessa Corrane knelt beside King Aldric, holding his hand, saying nothing, because there was nothing in her training for this either. She looked up once at Silas—at the thing standing in Silas's shape, swaying, its face flickering between his features and features far older—and she gave him a hunter's nod. Short. Final. The nod she'd have given anyone who'd closed a wound she couldn't reach.

*Thank you,* her mouth said, without sound.

The child stood in the center of the ruined throne room, small and still among the toppled banners, watching him with eyes that were not a child's eyes. They were the guild's eyes. Every master Silas had ever mourned looked out of them at once.

"You're going," the child said. Not a question. In the Old Tongue, it sounded like a door closing gently.

"I'm going." His voice was already changing, thickening, acquiring harmonics it shouldn't have had. "Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I won't be able to say this twice."

"I remember everything you're about to say."

"Then say it back to me someday. That's all I ask." He smiled, and the smile cost him more than the binding had. "Someone has to remember that we were gardeners first. Before the thorns. Someone has to keep the ledger honest."

The god stirred inside him, testing the walls of its new house, and Silas felt his own name begin to loosen like a knot worked at by patient fingers. *Silas.* He held onto it. *Reyes.* It slipped. He grabbed for the shape of it rather than the sound—the boy on the step at dusk, the wards screaming, the terrible joy of being needed—

And then there was no one left in the body to hold the name, and the name went out of the world like breath off a mirror.

What remained sat down slowly on the steps below the empty throne, folded its hands, and closed its eyes. It did not speak again. It would not speak again for a very long time.

The child crossed the room, knelt, and pressed one small hand to the forehead of the thing that had been Silas Reyes. Then the child spoke the sealing words—the last of them, the ones no living throat had spoken in five centuries—and the vault opened beneath the throne room floor, old stone grinding aside to receive what the guild had always known it would one day need to hold.

Not a prison. A garden bed.

The stone closed over him like soil over a planted bulb.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and when it came, it came quietly, as if apologizing.

The house above the harbor had been Silas's once—shuttered, ward-marked, forgotten. Now its shutters stood open and its window boxes overflowed with things that should not have grown so well so fast: feverfew, rue, moonwort, and roses of a deep thornless red that no seed merchant in the capital could name. The shop sign had been repainted by hand, the letters careful and slightly uneven, the way letters look when written by someone learning the shapes anew:

**THORNWRIGHT & FOUNDLING — CHARMS, CURES, HONEST WORK**

The child ran the shop now. Or rather, the child *was* the shop now, in the way that the guild had always been less an institution than a continuity—a memory passed hand to hand like a flame that refused, stubbornly, to go out. The child had grown taller over the months. The voice had found its register. Customers came for luck-charms and cures for colicky infants and left feeling, without knowing why, that they had been listened to by something very old and very patient.

The Crown had sent inquiries, of course. A new regency council governed in Aldric's name—the king lived, but he had chosen to retire to a quiet estate in the hill country, and no one pressed him, because everyone who had seen his eyes during those final years understood that some recoveries are measured in decades. The council's letters asked, delicately, whether the Thornwright Guild intended to reconstitute itself formally, whether it would submit to licensing, whether it could perhaps advise on matters of state.

The child answered each letter in the same way: with a pressed rose, thornless, and a single line in the Old Tongue that the council's scholars translated, eventually, as *We were never gone. We were sleeping. Do not wake us rudely.*

The witch-hunters were disbanded in autumn. Vessa Corrane resigned her commission three days before the order came through, which meant she resigned as a private citizen, which meant history would record that the last captain of the hunt had simply walked away. She came to the shop every week. She never bought anything. She sat by the window with tea she didn't drink and talked about her sister, sometimes, for the first time in twenty years, and the child listened the way gardens listen—which is to say, completely.

"It's strange," Vessa said one gray afternoon, watching rain silver the harbor. "I spent my whole life hunting magic because it took her. And now the person who saved everyone is buried under the palace, and I bring flowers to a sealed vault like a widow at a grave, and I don't even know who I'm mourning. There's no name on the stone. You saw to that."

"The name dissolved," the child said. "That was the price. But—" and here the child did something no one expected, something that made Vessa set down her cup. The child reached across the table and took her hand, small fingers over scarred knuckles. "Names aren't the only way to keep someone. Stories hold people too. Better, maybe. A name belongs to one mouth. A story belongs to anyone willing to carry it."

"So tell me the story."

"I will. All of it. As many times as you ask." The child smiled, and behind the smile, briefly, five hundred years of masters smiled too. "That's what the guild *was*, Vessa. Not spells. Not bindings. Just people agreeing to remember each other."

Below the palace, beneath forty feet of consecrated stone, in a chamber the masons had built to the child's exact specifications—circular, windowless, walled with living rootwork that the gardeners above watered on a schedule no one questioned—the thing that had been Silas Reyes slept.

It did not dream as humans dream. Its dreams were slower, deeper, vegetative—images rising like silt in still water. A baker's warm doorway. A patrol's boots on cobblestone. A ledger opening in green light. A boy knocking at dusk. Sometimes, in the deepest part of the sleep, the god and the man were indistinguishable, and the dream was simply of *growing*: roots spreading through dark earth toward something it could not yet name, patient as any buried thing, waiting for a season it trusted would come.

Because the child had written one line in the new ledger, the first entry in the guild's reborn book, and the line was this:

*What is taken willingly can be given back willingly. We do not know how. We do not know when. But we sealed him like a seed, not like a corpse, and seeds are promises.*

On the first mild day of the second spring, the child went out early to the garden over the vault and found, pushing up through the thawing soil in a perfect circle around the oldest rosebush, a ring of white flowers that had never been planted there.

The child knelt, touched one petal, and went very still.

Then, softly, in the Old Tongue—the language that binds kings, the language that also, apparently, wakes them—the child spoke a single word of greeting.

And far below, in the dark, something turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to dream of morning.
