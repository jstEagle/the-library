## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The frost on Dominic Delacroix's market stall had opinions about him. It crept up the legs of the folding table each morning in delicate white ferns, as if to say, *we know what you are*, and he wiped it away with his sleeve the way a man wipes away an accusation — thoroughly, and without meeting its eye.

"Charms!" he called into the gray morning of Copperlane Market. "Warming charms! Hearth-blessed, grandmother-approved, guaranteed not to burst into flame, explode, summon anything, or otherwise attract the attention of His Majesty's Wardens!"

A woman bundled in three shawls paused at his stall, her breath pluming like a small dragon's. "Guaranteed," she said flatly.

"Lady, I put it on the sign."

She squinted at the sign, which read: *HARMLESS CHARMS — HARMLESS AS BREAD.* Below that, in smaller letters he'd added after his third visit from the city guard: *NO REFUNDS, NO MAGIC, NO TROUBLE.*

"That last part true?" she asked.

"The no-trouble part? Cross my heart." He did so, and privately noted that crossing one's heart was technically a warding gesture older than any guild, which made the whole performance either very brave or very stupid. He'd never decided which.

She bought two warming charms for a copper apiece and shuffled off toward the fishmongers, and Dominic exhaled and rubbed his hands together over the little brazier he was legally permitted to own because it burned coal like any honest citizen's and not, say, concentrated sunlight, which was illegal, or captured lightning, which was illegal, or a salamander, which was illegal and also cruel and also had once eaten his grandmother's hat.

His grandmother would have hated this stall. She'd have hated the sign most of all. *Harmless as bread,* she'd have said, in that voice like a bell struck in an empty chapel. *You were taught to hold dawn in your cupped hands, boy, and you advertise yourself as bread.*

But his grandmother had been dead eleven years, and the Order of the Verdant Hand — the guild, the last guild, the only guild — had been dead slightly longer, and dead things didn't set prices or pay rent on a room above a cobbler's shop that smelled permanently of leather and disappointment.

Dominic arranged his wares with the care of a man defusing something. Warming charms, which were just iron beads wrapped in copper wire and blessed with a word so old it had forgotten it was magic. Sleep-easy knots, braided cord that smelled faintly of lavender and worked, when it worked, by suggestion and good craftsmanship rather than anything the Wardens could hang him for. Luck-knots for fishermen. A jar of salt "for keeping out ill feeling," which was pure theater and sold beautifully.

And beneath the table, sewn into the lining of his coat where his heartbeat could keep it warm, the medallion.

He didn't touch it during market hours. He never touched it during market hours. But he knew it was there the way you know a scar is there, and some cold mornings that was enough to get him through until noon.

* * *

Copperlane Market in winter was a study in endurance. The stalls huddled under sagging canvas like old friends at a funeral. The fishmonger, Ottel, a man built like a barrel and nearly as cheerful, stamped his feet behind his iced display and shouted prices at customers who couldn't afford them. Two children chased each other between the barrels, breath streaming, laughing in that way children laughed in Vharrow — quickly, quietly, as if joy might be taxed.

Above it all hung the sky, which had been the color of dirty wool since Harvestmoon. Six weeks of winter already, and the almanac-sellers on Temple Row swore the frost would break by Feast of Lanterns. They'd sworn it last year too. And the year before. Each winter came a little earlier, dug in a little deeper, and left a little later, and nobody in authority said the obvious thing, because the obvious thing was bad for trade and possibly treasonous.

"The usual, Delacroix?" Ottel called over. "Or are you finally buying actual food like a person?"

"I'm having the usual."

"You're going to die of herring."

"A noble death. Put it on my account."

Ottel wrapped two smoked herrings in brown paper and slid them across with the practiced discretion of a man who'd been extending credit to half of Copperlane since the Sundering and expected to be repaid in the next life, if at all. "Heard the Wardens hit Tanners' Row last night," he said, too casually, looking at his fish instead of Dominic.

Dominic's hands went still on the counter. "Did they."

"Took the Chandler girl. You know her? Does candles. Nice candles."

"I know her." Everyone knew Liss Chandler. Her tallow-work was famous in three districts, and her candles burned longer than they should have, and everyone had known, and everyone had said nothing, because saying something got you a visit from men in gray coats with iron badges shaped like open hands.

*Open palms,* the badge meant. *We take nothing. We give nothing. We simply stop you.*

"She'll burn on Feast of Lanterns, probably," Ottel said. "Public square. They do it after the lantern parade now. Festive."

"Ottel."

"I'm just saying what I heard." He turned back to his fish with the terrible neutrality of a man who had survived forty years in Vharrow by hearing everything and repeating exactly enough of it.

Dominic looked down at his stall. At the warming charms in their neat rows. At the sign promising harmlessness. Harmless as bread. He thought of Liss Chandler's candles, and how he'd once bought one for his windowsill, and how it had burned steady and gold all night while the wind screamed, and how he'd known — *known* — and bought it anyway, because the dark had been cold and the candle had been warm and that was the whole arithmetic of survival in Vharrow.

Everyone was guilty of something warm.

* * *

The raid came at midday, as raids did, because midday was when the markets were fullest and fear travels fastest through crowds.

Dominic heard it before he saw it: the particular silence that moves through a market ahead of gray coats, a silence like water receding from a shore. Conversations dying mid-word. A mother pulling her children close. Ottel suddenly fascinated by the interior of a barrel.

Then they were there — six of them, moving down the lane between the stalls in their matched formation, boots striking frost in unison. Warden-Captain Corvane led them, which was unusual; captains didn't walk Copperlane. Captains walked the palace gardens and sent lieutenants to do the burning.

Corvane was tall and dry-looking, a man assembled entirely from straight lines, with a face like a closed ledger. His iron hand-badge caught the weak light and threw nothing back.

"Charm sellers," Corvane said, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't addressed to anyone, which somehow made it worse. He stopped at the first stall — old Pemmy's, who sold dream-pouches stuffed with hops and wishful thinking — and picked up one of her pouches between two fingers like something dead he'd found on his boot.

"Dream-pouches," Corvane said.

"Sachets," Pemmy quavered. "For sleep, m'lord. Hops and chamomile. My mother's recipe."

"Hops and chamomile." He turned the pouch over. Somewhere in the crowd, someone breathed. "My mother's recipe," Corvane repeated, in a voice that suggested his mother had kept no recipes and needed none. He dropped the pouch back on the stall. "Carry on."

He moved down the row. Stall by stall. Pick up, examine, drop. A luck-knot. A jar of salt. A bundle of dried feverfew. Each item handled with the same clinical disgust, each seller subjected to the same weighing look — not searching for guilt, exactly. Searching for the *shape* of guilt. Wardens could smell it, people said. Not magic itself — that was the Inquisitors' gift, and Inquisitors were rarer and worse — but the fear-shaped hole where magic used to be, or might be, or was being carefully not-thought-about.

Dominic stood behind his stall and performed the hardest magic he knew: he became boring.

He straightened his charms. He yawned. He scratched his nose. He let his shoulders slump into the posture of a man whose most pressing concern was whether the herring on his account had gone off. Inside, behind the boredom, he did what his grandmother had drilled into him across a hundred kitchen-table lessons: he folded himself small. Not hiding — hiding was a shape, and shapes could be found. *Emptying.* Becoming a room no one wants to enter.

*You are a man selling beads,* she'd say, tapping his sternum with one finger. *There is nothing behind your ribs but the price of herring.*

Corvane reached his stall.

Up close, the captain smelled of cold iron and, beneath it, something green and wrong — like the air in a cellar where vegetables have gone bad. Dominic filed that away and offered the smile of a tired tradesman.

"Warming charms," Corvane said. He picked one up — the iron bead in its copper wrapping — and held it to the light.

"Blessed at the shrine of Saint Aldous," Dominic said smoothly. "Iron and copper, m'lord. Old folk wisdom. Keeps the chill out of the joints."

"Mm." Corvane turned it. Once. Twice. Dominic watched the man's eyes and saw nothing in them at all, which was more frightening than malice. Malice wanted something. This was just procedure wearing a man's face. "Old folk wisdom," Corvane repeated. "And your family? Long line of charm-makers?"

"Grandmother made them. I carry on the trade." Every word pre-measured, pre-warmed, harmless as bread.

"Your grandmother's name?"

The question landed in Dominic's chest like a dropped stone. There was a correct answer to this question and an honest answer, and the distance between them was the width of a pyre.

"Delacroix," he said. "Same as mine. Common name, in Copperlane."

Something flickered then — not in Corvane's eyes, but behind them, a green glint like light through bottle glass, there and gone so fast Dominic could have imagined it. Probably had imagined it. Fear did that. Fear painted things on the world.

Corvane set the charm down. "Winter's long this year," he said.

"Yes, m'lord."

"Longer than last."

"So they say, m'lord."

"They say a great many things." He looked at Dominic for one more moment — a moment in which Dominic's heart performed a complicated maneuver involving his throat — and then moved on to the next stall, and the next, and finally out of the market altogether, his five shadows trailing him like a funeral that hadn't found its corpse.

The market exhaled. Ottel sat down heavily on his stool. Somewhere a child started crying and was hushed.

Dominic discovered he was gripping the edge of his table hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and that the medallion against his chest felt hot as a coal.

* * *

He closed early. He told himself it was the cold, which was even true, mostly.

The room above the cobbler's shop was narrow and low-beamed and held a bed, a chair, a stove, and eleven years of a life packed into boxes like a man perpetually ready to flee. He lit the stove — honestly, with matches, like a citizen — ate one herring, and gave the other to the cobbler's cat, a vast gray tyrant named Duchess who tolerated his existence in exchange for fish.

Then he took out the medallion.

It was silver, worn smooth, no bigger than a coin. On its face: a tree whose roots and branches met in a circle, the garden and the thorn grown into one ring. The sigil of the Order of the Verdant Hand. His thumb found the worn groove where four generations of Delacroix thumbs had worried the same spot.

They'd been gardeners, once. That was the joke of it, the beautiful stupid joke — the last great order of mages in Vharrow, hunted and burned and scattered to the winds, and their whole secret legacy was *gardening*. Growing things. Keeping things alive. The guild had bound itself to the land itself, old stories said, sworn to keep the spring coming round, and in return the land had given them their craft. Then the Sundering came — whatever it truly was; the histories disagreed, and the survivors burned the histories — and magic became contraband, and gardeners became criminals, and the Order of the Verdant Hand became a medallion in a coat lining and a woman teaching a boy word-craft at a kitchen table with the shutters barred.

*One day,* his grandmother had said, *you'll understand why we kept the old words alive even when saying them aloud was death.*

*I understand they're dangerous,* ten-year-old Dominic had said.

*Everything alive is dangerous,* she'd said. *That's how you know it's alive.*

He sat with the medallion in his palm and listened to the wind worrying the shutters, and outside, far off toward the palace hill, the bells rang the hour — and kept ringing. One. Two. Three extra strokes, the alarm pattern, the pattern that meant a capture, a burning scheduled, a name entered in the gray book.

Liss Chandler, probably. Nice candles.

Dominic closed his fist around the medallion until the edges bit.

The winter was growing longer. Everyone felt it, in their bones and their fuel bills and the frost ferns that climbed higher up the windows every morning. And now the Wardens were growing bolder to match — captains walking the common markets, asking grandmothers' names, carrying something green and rotten underneath their iron. The city was tightening around him like a closing hand, and his whole strategy — be small, be boring, be bread — had worked for eleven years and was working less well every season, the way a dam works less well every season.

He was still turning it over — the flicker of green behind Corvane's eyes, the lengthening winters, the arithmetic of staying versus running — when the knocking started.

Not on his door. On the street door below, heavy and urgent, and then footsteps on the stairs, uneven, dragging, and a voice through the wood, wet and broken:

"Delacroix. Are you — they said Copperlane — *Delacroix—*"

Dominic blew out the candle and stood very still in the dark, medallion hot against his palm, and the door handle began to turn.
