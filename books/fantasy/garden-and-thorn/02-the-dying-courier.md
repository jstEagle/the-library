## 2. The Dying Courier

The bell above Dominic Delacroix's door had not rung all day, which was, in the arithmetic of a charm-seller's winter, neither good nor bad. It simply meant that no one had come to buy protection against things that were not, strictly speaking, real — and that no one had come to arrest him for selling it.

He counted this among his small mercies.

The shop itself was less a shop than a closet with ambitions. One counter, scarred by forty years of knives and coins and once, according to his grandmother, a very determined badger. Shelves of charms in their little cloth wrappings: knots against nightmares, salt-glass beads against ill luck, copper pennies flattened smooth "for safe travel," which meant nothing and cost two coppers, which meant everything. Above the counter, nailed where he could see it from anywhere in the room, hung the guild medallion — bronze, worn faceless by generations of thumbs, its sigil so old that even Dominic had to squint at it on bad days.

*The Order of the Living Root,* his grandmother had called it, back when she'd still said the word *order* without flinching. Back before the Sundering made the word *mage* worth a pyre.

Dominic was re-stringing a bundle of dream-knots when the bell finally rang.

It did not ring politely.

The door burst inward hard enough to crack the frame, and a man came through it the way water comes through a failing dam — all at once, and already falling. He caught the counter's edge, dragged himself upright, and looked at Dominic with eyes gone fever-bright in a face the color of tallow.

"Door," the man rasped. "Lock it."

Now, Dominic had lived in Vharrow long enough to know that when a bleeding stranger tells you to lock your door, you have approximately one heartbeat to decide whose side you're on. He spent that heartbeat looking past the man, out into Copperlane Street, where the frost lay thick as spilled flour and the lamplighters were just beginning their rounds.

Nothing moved. But nothing moving had stopped meaning *safe* some seasons ago.

He turned the key.

"Good," the stranger breathed, and then his legs gave out entirely, and Dominic lunged across the counter to catch him before his skull could introduce itself to the floorboards.

They ended up in an undignified heap behind the counter, Dominic kneeling in a spreading stain of someone else's blood, holding a man who weighed about as much as a sack of wet wool. Up close, the stranger was younger than his voice suggested — twenty-five, maybe thirty, with the wind-scoured look of someone who'd been traveling hard for a very long time. His cloak was good wool once, now stiff with frozen mud. Three arrows' worth of holes in the back of it, though only one wound, low in his side, weeping dark and slow.

"You're—" The man coughed, wet and rattling. "You're a Delacroix."

"I'm a charm-seller," Dominic said automatically. It was practically a reflex. Somewhere in Vharrow there were probably men who introduced themselves as *tax collectors* with the same defensive warmth. "I sell knots and salt. Very dull merchandise. Nothing anyone would bleed on my floor over—"

"The sigil." The man's hand shot up and seized Dominic's collar with shocking strength, dragging them nose to nose. His breath smelled of iron and pine sap. "Above your head. I've carried this thing across half the realm looking for that sigil. Don't you dare tell me you're nobody."

Dominic looked up at the medallion. Faceless bronze. A spiral of root and thorn that most people took for a farmer's luck-mark.

"It was my grandmother's," he heard himself say. Which was true, and also, somehow, felt like a confession.

Something in the stranger's face loosened — relief, or maybe just the body giving up its last pretense of strength. His grip went slack. "Then it found you. Good. That's — hah — that's more than I managed." He fumbled at his belt, fingers clumsy with cold, and came up with a leather scroll-case sealed in black wax. He pressed it into Dominic's hands and folded Dominic's fingers closed over it, both of his bloodied hands wrapped around Dominic's one, like a man entrusting a coal to keep it lit.

"What is it?" Dominic asked, though some cold, sensible part of him was screaming not to ask. Sensible people didn't ask. Sensible people said *I'll fetch a surgeon* and *let me get you something warm* and other lies that let everyone die comfortably.

"A map." The stranger smiled, and it cracked the dried blood on his lips. "A road. The last road left." His eyes were losing focus, sliding toward the ceiling, toward somewhere past it. "Listen. Listen to me. They'll tell you the guild is dead. They'll tell you the binding holds. They'll tell you the king is only sick."

"The king is only sick," Dominic said, because that was what the criers said, what the broadsheets said, what every tongue in Vharrow said the way you'd repeat a prayer you didn't believe in but couldn't afford to drop.

"They told my grandmother the same thing." The laugh again, worse than the coughing. "The Thornwood remembers. Do you hear me? *The Thornwood remembers.* Only a Delacroix can read it. Only a Delacroix. I checked. Gods, how I checked." His hand slid down Dominic's wrist, leaving a red smear. "Don't let them burn it. Whatever else you do. Don't let them burn it like they burned—"

He never finished the sentence. His chest hitched once, twice, and then he exhaled a long breath that seemed to take everything in him with it, and the shop went very quiet — the particular quiet of a room where a person has just stopped being one.

Dominic knelt there for a while, holding a dead man's hand, listening to his own heart bang around inside his ribs like a trapped bird.

"Well," he said finally, to the corpse, because talking to corpses was marginally saner than talking to himself. "This is above my pay grade. I don't *have* a pay grade. I sell dream-knots."

The corpse declined to comment.

* * *

Here is what Dominic knew about dead strangers in his shop: everything, and nothing, and mostly that he needed to decide quickly.

He laid the man out flat as best he could, closed his eyes, and covered him with the good blanket from the cot in the back room — the one his grandmother had woven, the one he slept under every night, because apparently today was a day for surrendering precious things. Then he sat on his stool, turned the scroll-case over in his hands, and studied the black wax seal.

There was a mark pressed into it. A spiral of root and thorn.

His stomach performed a slow, complicated maneuver.

He should burn it. That was the sensible thought, arriving right on schedule, wearing its sensible hat. Burn the case, drag the body out back, tell the Wardens a drunk had collapsed, collect whatever pity-coppers the neighbors offered. That was the path that led to Dominic Delacroix dying old and unremarkable in his narrow bed, which was, statistically speaking, the best available fate for a man with guild blood in Vharrow.

Instead he broke the seal.

Inside was a map — not parchment but something stranger, a sheet of thin bark-pulp, pale as birch and faintly warm, as though it had recently been held near a fire. Inked lines crawled across it in a hand both precise and hurried: roads, rivers, the spine of the Greywatch Mountains. And there, in the far north beyond anything Dominic had ever traveled, a region marked in green ink gone brown with age, shaped like a great closed eye.

The Thornwood.

But it was the margins that stopped his breath. Little annotations curled around the borders of the map in a cipher of loops and barbs — and Dominic's hands began to shake, because he *knew* that cipher. Not from any book. From a childhood of rainy afternoons, sitting at his grandmother's knee while she drew him puzzles no other child in Vharrow had ever seen, teaching him letters she claimed were "family shorthand, nothing more, never write these down, never show anyone."

Root-script. Guild-hand. The lost writing of the Order of the Living Root, which had not officially existed in sixty years.

And beneath the cipher, pressed small and neat into the corner of the map as if by a signet ring, the same spiral of root and thorn.

*A route only a Delacroix could read.* The stranger hadn't been speaking in riddles. He'd meant it literally. Whoever had drawn this map had written it in a language the Wardens couldn't parse, the crown couldn't confiscate, and precisely one living man — as far as Dominic knew — could decipher.

He read the marginal notes slowly, sounding them out the way you'd sound out a lullaby half-remembered. Most were waymarks and warnings. *Ferry at the Weir runs only under new moon. Do not trust the shrine at Coldharrow; the warden there is not a warden.* And then, near the Thornwood's closed-eye border, a line that made the room feel suddenly colder:

*The binding frays. What the crown wears is not the crown. Bring the three. Only root may part root.*

Dominic sat with that for a long moment.

"What the crown wears is not the crown," he repeated aloud, and immediately wished he hadn't, because saying treasonous sentences out loud in your own shop was exactly how charm-sellers ended up as ash-statistics. He glanced guiltily at the shuttered window. The lamplighters had passed. Frost ferns were creeping up the glass.

Outside, faint but unmistakable, boots crunched on snow. Several pairs. In step.

Dominic had survived in Vharrow by developing a certain instinct — a market-seller's instinct, a hunted-man's instinct — for the difference between footsteps passing and footsteps *arriving*. These were arriving. Heavy, deliberate, spaced like soldiers who had practiced spacing. And they were slowing.

He looked at the corpse under his grandmother's blanket. He looked at the map in his hands. He looked at the blood on his floorboards, which no amount of scrubbing would explain away before the knock came.

Three arrows' worth of holes in the man's cloak. Someone had shot this courier, and whoever had shot him would very much like to know where the arrows' intended cargo had ended up. And if the boots outside belonged to the shooters, Dominic was about to become a footnote. If they belonged to the Wardens, he was about to become a pyre. If they belonged to both —

The knock came. Three blows, measured as a metronome.

"Open in the name of the Crown Wardens."

Ah, thought Dominic. Both.

He stood up too fast and knocked his stool over, which struck him as a fitting summary of his entire life. His eyes swept the shop in a panic — the blood, the body, the map — and landed on the loose hearthstone behind the woodbox, the hiding place his grandmother had installed along with so many other lessons he'd dismissed as paranoia. He stuffed the scroll-case into the gap, replaced the stone, kicked the rug over it, and had exactly enough time to seize the medallion off its nail — because that, at least, was *his*, and he was not leaving it — before the knocking became pounding.

"Delacroix! We know the courier entered your premises. Open, or we open it for you."

*We know.* Which meant they'd tracked the man here. Which meant the arrows had missed their true target, and the shooters had sent dogs instead.

Dominic's mind, remarkably calm in the way minds go when it is far too late for panic to be useful, presented him with a single observation: the back wall of his shop backed onto the tannery yard, and the tannery yard backed onto the mouth of the storm-drain that Maren Oduya had shown him years ago, laughing, saying, *"One day you'll thank me for knowing where Vharrow keeps its secrets, little rootling."*

He had assumed she was being colorful. Maren was always being colorful.

The front door shuddered under a boot heel. Wood split.

Dominic blew out the lamp, dropped the medallion around his neck, and went out the back into the freezing dark with nothing but the clothes he stood up in, a head full of forbidden letters, and the growing, terrible understanding that the dull, survivable life he had built brick by careful brick had just died on his floor under his grandmother's blanket.

Behind him, the door gave way. Lantern-light bloomed through the front windows, and a voice like a filed blade called out:

"Find the map. Burn the shop. The seller is to be taken alive — the captain has questions."

Dominic ran.

Snow swallowed the sound of his footsteps, and ahead of him, somewhere beneath the city, an old friend's storm-drain waited with its mouth full of dark. He clutched the medallion through his shirt as he ran, feeling its faceless bronze press against his sternum like a small, stubborn heart.

*The Thornwood remembers.*

Gods help him, he was starting to remember too.
