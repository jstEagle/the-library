## 3. What the Crown Wears

The courier was dead before Sera Vane reached him.

She knew it the way she knew most things — not by the stillness of the body, which could lie, but by the smell. Blood has a voice when it's fresh, and this blood had gone quiet in the cold. The man lay crumpled against the doorframe of a charm-seller's shop on Tallow Row, one hand pressed flat to the wood as if he'd been trying to knock when death caught him mid-gesture. His other hand was empty. That was the first wrong thing.

"Vane." Commander Aldric Hale stood at the mouth of the alley, his breath steaming around the grey of his beard. He had the look of a man who had been woken twice already that night and expected to be woken a third time. "Report."

"Male courier, royal seal on his satchel — or what's left of it. Stabbed twice, low and high. He walked here from wherever it happened. That's half a mile at least, maybe more." Sera crouched beside the body, careful not to touch. "And he was carrying something, because his right hand is clean while everything else is soaked. Someone took it off him."

"Took it, or he gave it away?"

She looked up. Hale was watching her with the flat patience he reserved for questions he already suspected the answer to.

"Gave it away," she said. "Look at his fingers. They're curled like they were holding something out. Pressing it into someone's hands." She nodded toward the shop door above the dead man. It hung slightly ajar, and warm light leaked through the gap and pooled gold on the frost-slick cobbles. "Charm-seller lives there. Delacroix. Harmless as bread, by all accounts."

"Harmless people don't get couriers dying on their thresholds."

"No," Sera agreed. "But harmless people do open their doors at midnight when someone bleeds on them."

Hale grunted. Around them, the Wardens were doing what Wardens did best, which was standing in a semicircle looking ominous and accomplishing very little. One of them — young Ferro, who still flinched at loud noises — was attempting to question the charm-seller, and Sera could hear the poor man stammering through his answers with the desperate politeness of someone who sold love-knots and luck-bringers for coppers and had never once imagined a corpse would complicate his bookkeeping.

She almost smiled. Almost. There was something about the little shops of Tallow Row that she'd always found quietly funny — the hand-painted signs promising *protection* for two bits, the jars of dried lavender that were supposed to ward off bad dreams, the whole economy of small comforts sold to people who couldn't afford large ones. Her brother would have loved it here. The thought arrived uninvited, as it always did, and she put it away where she kept all such thoughts, in the locked room behind her ribs that had no windows.

"The courier's name was Pellam," Hale said. "He ran the northern route. Thornwood way."

Sera went still. "Nobody runs the Thornwood route anymore. Not since—"

"Not since the roads closed, yes. Which means either Pellam was running somewhere he shouldn't have been, or he was carrying something somebody badly wanted delivered." Hale rubbed his jaw. "Find out which. Quietly. And find out what was in his hands when he died."

"And the charm-seller?"

"Ferro will take his statement. If the man's clean, he's clean." Hale turned to go, then paused. "Vane. The king holds council at dawn. You'll attend."

She blinked. Huntresses didn't attend council. Huntresses tracked, captured, and occasionally — though she tried not to think about those occasions — executed. Council was for ministers and marshals and men whose collars cost more than her yearly wage.

"Commander, I'm not—"

"You're the one who found the body, and you're the best eyes I have. The Lord Marshal wants a report from someone who isn't a politician." His mouth twitched. "Gods know we've enough of those. Dawn, Vane. Try to look presentable."

"I always look presentable, sir."

"You look like you slept in a snowdrift and lost the argument."

* * *

She did not sleep. Sleep had been an unreliable ally for eleven years, ever since the night she'd stood in a Warden courtyard and watched a bonfire do its work, so instead she cleaned her boots, sharpened her knives, and sat by her narrow window watching the frost climb the glass like something alive and hungry.

By the time the bells rang dawn, the palace of Vharrow rose white and vast against a sky the color of old pewter. Sera had walked its corridors a hundred times, but that morning they felt different — longer, colder, the torches burning with a strange blue tinge at their hearts. She told herself it was the season. Everything felt strange in deep winter. Even the stones seemed to hold their breath.

The council chamber doors were taller than two men, carved with the history of the realm: kings crowned, dragons slain, the Sundering itself rendered in marble — a great tree split down the middle, its halves bleeding light. Sera had always thought it an odd thing to carve over your own front door, a monument to the worst day your country ever had. But kings loved their warnings.

Inside, the chamber was warm in a way that felt manufactured, like warmth remembered rather than felt. Braziers burned. Courtiers murmured behind cupped hands. And at the far end, beneath the great window of colored glass, sat the king.

King Aldous IV had been on the throne for thirty-one years. Sera had seen him perhaps a dozen times — at her swearing-in, at parades, once from a distance during the harvest festival. He had been an old man even then, spare and upright, with a face like a folded map and a reputation for stubbornness that the ballads called wisdom.

This morning, something was wrong with him.

It took her a long moment to understand what, because nothing about him had changed, exactly. He sat the same. He wore the same crown — iron and green glass, an antique thing said to predate the Sundering, which every schoolchild learned was ceremonial and every adult learned to stop asking about. His ministers leaned in around him, speaking in low urgent voices about grain stores and garrison rotations, and the king listened, nodding slowly.

But his skin moved.

Not much. Not obviously. Just — beneath the jaw, along the temple, a shifting, as if something under the flesh had turned over in its sleep. Like watching a shadow pass behind a curtain and realizing the curtain is a man's face.

Sera stopped breathing.

And then the king raised his head, and looked directly across the chamber at her, and his eyes —

Green. A flicker of green behind the brown, deep in the iris, like sunlight through the canopy of a forest seen from underneath. It pulsed once, gently, almost kindly.

Then it was gone, and the king's gaze slid past her as if she were furniture, and he returned to his ministers, and the chamber went on murmuring, and nobody — *nobody* — reacted at all.

Sera's heart was hammering against her ribs like a bird trapped in a room. She made herself breathe. She made herself count the exits, an old habit: four, plus the servants' passage behind the tapestry, plus the window if one enjoyed falling forty feet onto marble. She made herself listen to the Lord Marshal droning about the northern garrisons, because listening was safer than thinking, and thinking was safer than saying anything, because what could she possibly say?

*I saw something move under the king's skin?*

They'd laugh. Or worse — they wouldn't laugh. They'd look at her the way Wardens looked at the accused, with that terrible patient interest, and she'd spend the rest of her life explaining a flicker of green light that might have been a trick of the brazier flames, except that braziers didn't pulse like heartbeats and fire wasn't *kind*.

"Vane."

She started. The Lord Marshal was looking at her, along with most of the council. She hadn't heard a single word of the last exchange.

"My lord Marshal."

"The courier Pellam. You examined the body." He was a broad man with a voice like a landslide, and he used it now the way other men used fingers pointed at throats. "What was taken from him?"

"We don't yet know, my lord. Something small enough to hold in one hand. The killer — or killers — left no other trace."

"Mm." He exchanged a glance with the Lord Chancellor, a thin man whose smile never reached further than his teeth. "A courier dies on a charm-seller's doorstep carrying something worth killing for, and no one knows what it was. How tidy."

"You suspect the charm-seller, my lord?" Sera kept her voice level. She was aware of the king at the edge of her vision, still and pale, his hands folded on the armrests. She was careful not to look at him. Looking felt dangerous now, like leaning over a well.

"I suspect everyone, huntress. It's the only reliable policy." The Chancellor's smile widened a fraction. "Though it does strike me as convenient. A dying man staggers into the one shop in Vharrow licensed to sell trinkets, and whatever he carried vanishes. Perhaps the trinket-seller knows more than he admits. Perhaps he's more than he admits."

"He sells luck-charms for two coppers," Sera said. "I've walked past his stall a hundred times. Half the city has. He can barely afford his own rent, let alone conspiracy."

"Poverty is an excellent disguise," the Chancellor said mildly. "Ask any spy."

Around the table, a few courtiers chuckled. Sera did not. She was watching the king again despite herself — she couldn't help it, the way you can't help touching a bruise — and she saw that his head had tilted, very slightly, toward the conversation. Toward her. As though the name *Delacroix* meant something to him. As though the name were a sound he'd been waiting for.

Behind his eyes, faint as moss on stone, the green flickered again.

And this time — she would swear this on her oath, on her mother's grave, on the ashes of everything she'd ever believed — this time the king's lips moved. No sound came out. But the shape of it was unmistakable, formed slow and deliberate, a single word shaped for no one but her:

*Find.*

The council moved on. Grain stores. Garrisons. The interminable machinery of governance grinding through its morning. Sera stood at attention and answered when addressed and heard none of it, because a dead man's word was echoing in her skull — the whisper the dying courier had supposedly breathed to whoever knelt beside him, according to the charm-seller's shaken statement, which Ferro had dutifully transcribed and which Sera had read three times by candlelight.

*The Thornwood remembers.*

And now the king — or the thing wearing the king like a coat — had looked at her and said *find*, and she did not need anyone to tell her what she was being asked to find. A map. A route. A charm-seller named Dominic Delacroix, harmless as bread, who had opened his door at midnight to a bleeding stranger and was now, at this very moment, probably hiding somewhere in his little shop, clutching whatever the courier had given him, terrified out of his wits.

*Run,* she thought at him, absurdly, silently, across the width of the council chamber. *You sweet, doomed, two-copper-charm idiot. Run.*

Because she had seen the green behind the king's eyes, and she understood — with the cold certainty of a woman who had spent eleven years learning exactly how far her oath would bend before it broke — that whatever happened next, the law she served was no longer pointing at justice. It was pointing where the green thing wanted it to point.

The council adjourned. Courtiers bowed and scattered like startled pigeons. The king rose last, moving with a stiffness that looked less like age than like wood remembering it had once been a tree, and as he passed her on his way to the doors, he paused.

For one heartbeat, the oldest man in the realm stood close enough that Sera could smell him — winter air and loam and something sweetly rotten, like flowers blooming in a closed room.

"Good hunting, huntress," he said. His voice was warm. His breath was cold.

Then he was gone, and Sera Vane stood alone in the emptying chamber with her duty in one hand and her dread in the other, weighing them, finding they weighed exactly the same.

She went to find her horse, and her pack, and the road north — telling herself, all the while, that she was simply following orders.

That was the story she'd keep telling herself, all the way to Tallow Row, all the way out of the city, all the way to the moment the story stopped being true.
