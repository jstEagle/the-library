## 4. Road Out of Vharrow

The sewers beneath Vharrow smelled like a promise that had been broken a very long time ago.

Dominic followed Maren's lantern down the dripping dark, one hand pressed to his nose, the other clutching the map against his chest as though the Warden-hounds could smell it through three layers of oilcloth and fear. Which, for all he knew, they could. He'd stopped assuming anything was impossible the moment a dying man had bled out on his shop floor.

"Keep up, boy," Maren said. She moved through the muck like a woman walking her own garden path, which, Dominic supposed, in a way she did. Forty years ago she'd been the guild's quartermaster. Now she was a squat, gray-haired woman with a limp, a lantern, and the particular confidence of someone who had bribed every sewer-grate keeper in the city twice over.

"I'm keeping up," Dominic said. "I'm keeping up beautifully. I'm simply also dying."

"You're not dying. You're inconvenienced. There's a difference, and the difference is about forty years of practice." She held up the lantern, and its light caught the arch of an old brick tunnel ahead — and there, carved into the keystone, worn nearly smooth by centuries of water: a thorn wrapped around a rose.

The guild sigil.

Dominic stopped. He hadn't seen it anywhere but on his grandmother's medallion since he was nine years old, since the night the Wardens came to the house on Candlemaker's Row and his grandmother had shoved him into a root cellar with a candle and instructions not to make a sound no matter what he heard. He had made a sound anyway. He still dreamed about it.

"They built the city on top of us," Maren said quietly. "Every aqueduct, every cistern, every drain — guild-work, all of it. The founders made Vharrow's water run clean and its cellars stay dry, and then the city outlawed them and forgot their names." She tapped the keystone with her stick. "But the stone remembers. Stone always remembers."

"The courier said something like that," Dominic murmured. "About the Thornwood."

Maren went very still. Then she turned, and in the lantern light her face had lost all its comfortable gruffness. "He spoke? Before he died?"

"He said—" Dominic swallowed. "He said *the Thornwood remembers.* And then he put this in my hands like I was supposed to know what to do with it." He unwrapped the map just enough to show her the corner, where the wax seal bore the same thorn-and-rose.

Maren looked at it for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Then we shouldn't stop here. Come on."

* * *

They walked for what felt like hours, through tunnels that narrowed and branched and narrowed again, until Dominic was certain they were hopelessly lost and equally certain that Maren would take it as a personal insult if he said so. The cold crept up through his boots. Somewhere far above, he could hear the muffled clang of the city — the market bells, the Warden patrols' boots on frost-slick cobbles.

"Tell me where we're going," he said finally. "You know where this map leads. I can see it in your face. You've been looking at me like I'm a funeral procession since we left my shop."

Maren snorted. "I've been looking at you like you're your grandmother, is what I've done. Same stubborn chin. Same talent for asking questions at exactly the wrong moment." She ducked under a low pipe. "Fine. You deserve to hear it before you're too far gone to turn back. That map leads north. Past the pinewastes, past Thorn Pass, into the Thornwood itself."

"The Thornwood's a story. A place mothers scare children with. Nobody goes there."

"People go there," Maren said. "They just don't come back. It's where the Verdant sleeps."

Dominic frowned. "The Verdant?"

She stopped so abruptly he nearly walked into her. She turned, and the lantern threw her shadow huge and crooked against the wet brick.

"You truly don't know." It wasn't a question; it was grief. "Your grandmother never told you. Of course she didn't. Why give a child another reason for the Wardens to burn him?" She sighed, and leaned against the tunnel wall, and for a moment she looked every one of her years. "Sit, boy. This part you hear standing or sitting, but you hear it whole."

Dominic sat on a dry ledge beside a rusted ladder. Above them, faintly, a grate clanged. Neither of them moved.

"Before the Sundering," Maren began, "the realm was dying. Not winter — rot. Blight in the fields, sickness in the herds, rivers running black. The old kings begged the guild for help, and the guild went into the Thornwood and found the thing that lived there. The Verdant. Older than the guild, older than the crown. A green mind, vast and slow and starving. It could make anything grow — and it grew hungry the way fire grows hungry, eating everything it touched."

"So they killed it."

"They couldn't kill it. You don't kill a thing like that any more than you kill the sea. So they did something worse, or better, depending on who you ask. They bound it. Bound its hunger into a vessel, chained its power to serve instead of devour. For two hundred years the realm bloomed because the Verdant was leashed. Every harvest, every spring — guild-work, all of it." Her mouth twisted. "And then the Sundering came, and the guild fell, and the binding was left untended. A knot nobody alive knows how to tie anymore."

"And now?"

"Now the knot is slipping. The Verdant has spent a century looking for a new anchor, something close to power, something wearing a crown." Maren met his eyes. "The king, Dominic. The Verdant wears the king. That endless winter you've been complaining about — that's not weather. That's a starving thing learning to use its new hands badly. It promised the court eternal spring. What it delivers is frost, because it's still learning, still fumbling, still hungry. And when it finishes learning—"

"It won't be winter anymore," Dominic said slowly. "It'll be a garden. And everything in it will be *theirs.*"

Maren nodded. "The map shows the road to the Thornwood. The old binding-places. If anyone's going to unmake what's coming, it has to be done from where the knot was first tied. And it has to be a Delacroix." She tapped the medallion hidden under his shirt. "That's not jewelry, boy. That's a key. Your grandmother died gathering what's needed to turn it. Or so we all believed."

Dominic sat with that for a long moment, listening to the water drip.

"I sold charms," he said finally. "Little ones. Protection knots, luck-braids, fever-charms. Ten years hiding in plain sight, hawking trinkets to people whose grandmothers taught them the same knots their grandmothers learned. And the whole time there was a *thing wearing the king*, and the guild knew, and nobody told me."

"We told you what telling you would cost," Maren said gently. "Look what happened in one afternoon. A dead courier. Wardens at your door. You're fleeing for your life through a sewer, and that's *before* you knew anything worth killing for."

Above them, the grate clanged again — closer this time. And then a third sound, one that made both of them freeze: the low, wet rattle of Warden-hounds, sniffing along the tunnel mouths.

Maren pinched out the lantern.

In the sudden black, she found his hand and pressed something into it — small, round, warm despite the cold. "Seed-charm," she whispered. "First of the relics, and the last thing I have of hers. Don't ask what it costs yet. Just run when I say run, and don't look back no matter what you hear."

"What will I hear?"

"Me being extremely annoying to some very expensive dogs."

* * *

They ran.

The tunnels became a blur of brick and black water and Maren's stick striking sparks off things Dominic didn't want identified. Behind them the hounds' rattle swelled and split — two of them, maybe three — and once, horribly, he heard a human voice join them, calling orders in the flat tone of a Warden-Captain's man.

Maren shoved him through a gap in the wall he'd never have found alone, into a drainage channel sloping steeply downward, and shouted, "*Now,* boy — north gate, the tanner's road, ask for Hesk! RUN!"

He ran. He didn't look back. He heard, behind him, a crackle of light that had no business existing underground, and a chorus of canine outrage, and Maren's voice laughing — actually *laughing* — and then he was out, blinking in the gray pre-dawn, stumbling through the tannery district's reek toward the north gate with a forged pass Maren had pressed on him hours ago and a lifetime of certainty burning off him like morning fog.

The gate guard barely glanced at the pass. The city let him go the way a fist lets go of sand.

* * *

The north road was a white ribbon stitched through a white world.

Dominic walked it alone for two days, sleeping in haybarns, avoiding the king's highway, following the map's thin ink line through country that grew barer and colder with every mile. The farms thinned. The pines began. The silence got bigger.

On the evening of the second day, caught in the open as the light failed and the temperature dropped like a dropped stone, he did the arithmetic every traveler in the north knew: no shelter, no firewood, and perhaps an hour before the cold started writing its name on him.

He should have kept walking. Instead, half-frozen and past caring, he knelt in the snow at the roadside, took out the seed-charm, and did what his grandmother had taught him when he was six years old, kneeling just like this in a kitchen full of bread-smell: he cupped his hands around it, breathed slow, and *asked.*

Not commanded. Asked. The way you'd ask a stubborn seed to consider sprouting.

For a long moment, nothing. The wind. His own heartbeat, loud and frightened.

Then warmth.

It rose out of the charm and up through his palms and into his wrists, his arms, his chest — a green, growing warmth utterly unlike fire, patient and deep, and with it came something else, something that made him gasp aloud in the empty white field: a *hum*. Faint, distant, like a harp string plucked in a house far away. But he knew it. He had known it as a child without knowing what it was — the feeling of the guild's old power, asleep in the world's bones, turning over in its sleep.

*Oh,* he thought, dazed. *Oh, it's real. It was always real.*

The snow around him softened. Not melted — softened, loosened, the crystals letting go of each other, and in the little circle where he knelt the frozen ground breathed out a smell of loam and green things. A single pale shoot pushed up between his fingers, uncurled one leaf, and stood there trembling in the wind, impossibly alive.

Dominic laughed. It came out cracked and startled and half a sob, and he laughed again, louder, because ten years of fear and smallness and pretending were cracking off him like ice, and underneath was this — *this* — the guild's power stirring in his blood like a remembered song.

He built a fire with ordinary flint, because some habits keep you humble, and sat beside it warming his hands while the little shoot glowed faintly green at the edge of the firelight. He ate hard bread and dried apples and felt, for the first time since the courier died on his floor, not hunted but *chosen.*

"All right, Grandmother," he said to the dark. "I'm listening. Show me the road."

The wind shifted. Far to the north, past the black ranks of the pines, he saw it — just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of sight: a flicker of green light among the trees, moving parallel to the road. Watching. Keeping pace.

The hum in his chest changed key. Warmer, and warning all at once.

Something else was out there in the snow. And it wasn't the Verdant.

It was tracking *him.*
