## 5. The Huntress's Trail

The order came at dawn, written in the commander's own hand, which meant it was either very important or very deniable. Sera Vane had learned to tell the difference by the weight of the wax seal. This one was heavy.

*Find the courier's killer. A charm-seller named Delacroix. Last seen fleeing the Lamplight quarter. Bring him alive if convenient.*

*If convenient*, she read again, and snorted. Nothing about the pinewastes in midwinter was convenient. The commander might as well have ordered her to bring back a live fish from a frozen lake, politely, without getting her boots wet.

She packed anyway. That was the thing about orders — they didn't care what you thought of them, and neither, officially, did she.

Her horse, a gray mare named Bitterroot with opinions about everything, stood in the royal stable chewing her bit like it owed her money.

"Don't start," Sera said.

Bitterroot started.

* * *

Three days out of Vharrow, the road gave up pretending to be a road and became a suggestion — a line of trampled snow winding through pines so dense the sky shrank to ribbons of gray between the boughs. The cold here was different from the city's cold. Vharrow's winter was a tax; this was a predator. It found the gaps in your coat and the cracks in your resolve and worked on both with equal patience.

Sera tracked the fugitive the way she'd been taught: not by looking for him, but by looking for what he couldn't help leaving behind. Everyone left something. Guilt, mostly, though that was hard to follow through snow.

He left other things too. A campsite on the second day where someone had burned green wood badly and slept too close to the fire — she could see the scorch marks where his bedroll had nearly caught. A dropped glove, patched at three fingers, smelling faintly of dried lavender and candle wax. Charm-seller's smell. Half the peddlers in Vharrow wore it like a uniform: herbs and tallow and the sweet rot of superstition.

But the courier hadn't been killed by a peddler. The courier had been killed by someone who knew how to use a knife, and the Wardens wanted Delacroix for it, and the Wardens were rarely wrong about who to burn.

Rarely. She turned the word over as she rode. It had developed an edge lately.

On the fourth day she found blood in the snow — a smear, not a pool, the kind a man leaves when he's fallen and gotten up and been too tired to be embarrassed about it. Fresh. Hours old. She dismounted and led Bitterroot into the trees, moving quiet, and told herself the quickening in her chest was just the hunt. It always was. That was the story she'd been telling herself for six years, since the last time her hands shook before a job, and she'd gotten very good at believing it.

* * *

She caught sight of him at dusk, and he was nothing.

That was her first honest impression. She'd built him up over four days of cold tracking into something worth the chase — a killer, a guild agent, maybe one of the old order's hidden knives, dangerous and deliberate. What she saw instead was a man crouched under a rock overhang trying to light a fire with wet kindling and losing the argument. He was thin. His coat was more patch than coat. He held his hands to the pitiful smoke like a beggar and muttered to himself, and when he finally got a flame no bigger than a coin, he said — distinctly, to no one —

"Ha."

Just *ha*. Triumphant. Pathetic. Sera crouched behind a pine and felt something dangerously close to affection, which she immediately drowned in duty like a cat in a sack.

Charm-seller Dominic Delacroix. Killer of couriers. Terror of the Lamplight quarter. He was now warming his fingers over a flame she could have smothered with a sneeze.

She could take him asleep. She could take him awake; he moved like a man whose only fight in life had been with kindling. The clean thing — the *procedure* — was to walk out there, announce herself, bind his wrists, and march him back to Vharrow where the Wardens would ask their questions and the answer would almost certainly be fire.

She'd delivered men to that fire before. Her brother's face surfaced, uninvited, the way it always did near flames, and she pressed it back down where it lived.

Procedure. Walk out. Bind him. Go home.

She stood up, and the wraith hit her.

* * *

Later, she would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. There was cold — a cold beyond cold, the cold of the grave remembering warmth and resenting it — and there was a shape made of frost and hunger unfolding out of the shadows between the pines, tall as two men, its face a smooth white absence where a face should be. Frost-wraiths belonged to stories told to keep children away from the north roads. They did not belong four days south of Vharrow, in pine country, in numbers that had been dwindling for a generation.

It came at her like winter given legs. She got her blade up — training does that even when the mind has quit — and the steel frosted white the instant it touched the thing's reaching arm, and then her arm went numb to the elbow and she went down hard into the snow, and she understood with perfect clarity that she was going to die in the pinewastes chasing a charm-seller, and that the report would say *killed in pursuit*, and nobody would ever know she'd spent her last living thought thinking, absurdly, *he said 'ha' at his own fire.*

Then the world turned gold.

Not firelight-gold. Deeper. Older. The light came from the fugitive's cupped hands — from a small carved disc he'd torn from around his neck — and it poured across the clearing like honey off a table edge, thick and slow and impossibly warm. Where it touched the snow, the snow didn't melt. It *glowed*, each crystal lit from within, and the wraith recoiled from it the way shadow recoils from a struck flint.

Delacroix was on his feet, holding the medallion out in both hands, and he was terrified — she could see it, the whites of his eyes, the shake in his arms — but he wasn't running. He was standing between her and the wraith.

"*No,*" he was saying, to the wraith, in the tone of a man scolding a large dog. "*No. Not her. Go on. Get.*"

The wraith shrieked — a sound like ice cracking on a pond, like a door opening on a room that should stay shut — and the medallion flared brighter, and the thing came apart. Not violently. It simply unraveled, frost to mist to nothing, and the gold light sank gently back into the little carved disc like a tide going home, and the clearing was dark and ordinary and silent except for Sera's own ragged breathing and the distant, unimpressed snort of a horse.

Delacroix turned around slowly. He looked at her lying in the snow with her sword still raised in a numb hand. He looked at the medallion. He looked back at her.

"So," he said. "You're probably here to arrest me."

* * *

She bound his wrists with proper huntress knots, because procedure was procedure, and if she abandoned procedure entirely she didn't know what would be left of her. But she let him sit close to the fire while she checked her arm — frostbitten but whole, the flesh pink and aching — and she watched him watch her watch him, and the silence stretched until he broke it, because of course he did.

"That was a frost-wraith," he said.

"Yes."

"They're extinct. Or nearly. My grandmother used to say you'd have to go past the Thornwood to see one." He flexed his bound hands uncomfortably. "What's one doing four days south of Vharrow?"

"You tell me. You're the one carrying guild relics around the countryside."

His face did something complicated — fear and grief and a strange fierce pride all fighting for the same ground. "This isn't a relic. It's my grandmother's medallion. It's—" He stopped. Started again. "It's a keepsake. That's all."

"It burned a wraith out of existence."

"It *discouraged* a wraith," he said. "There's a difference. Legally, I mean. I'd want that noted."

Despite everything — the arm, the wraith, the six years of careful deadness she'd built around herself like a second coat — Sera felt her mouth twitch. She killed the smile before it could commit fully. "You're funny. For a murderer."

"I'm not a murderer."

"The courier in the Lamplight quarter says otherwise."

"The courier can't say anything," Delacroix said quietly, "because he died in my shop with my hands on his wounds trying to keep him alive. And whatever the Wardens told you, I didn't kill him. Someone else did, and they wanted me blamed, and I don't know why yet, but I'm going to find out, because whoever it is put a map in my hands that I think a lot of people are willing to kill for."

Sera fed another branch to the fire and said nothing. It was the speech every fugitive gave. *I'm innocent. I'm being framed. It wasn't me.* She'd heard it from men with blood on their cuffs and women with knives still warm in their belts. It never once had been true.

Except that he had stood between her and a wraith. Except that hedge-magic — real magic, guild-magic, the kind that had supposedly died with the Sundering — had poured out of a keepsake in his shaking hands. Except that the Wardens' intelligence had described Delacroix as *dangerous*, and the most dangerous thing about him so far was his optimism.

"Tell me about the map," she said.

Something flickered behind his eyes. Calculation, or hope, or both. "Why? So you can confiscate it?"

"So I can decide whether you're worth dragging back to Vharrow alive, or whether I report you escaped and save us both a lot of unpleasantness."

He stared at her. "You'd do that?"

"I don't know yet. I'm deciding." She leaned forward. "Start talking."

And he did — haltingly at first, then with the helpless momentum of a man who'd been alone with a terrible secret too long. The courier. The bloodstained map marked with the guild's lost sigil, a route only a Delacroix could read. The word the dying man had whispered: *the Thornwood remembers.* And underneath it all, threaded through everything, a name spoken like a curse and a prayer at once:

The Verdant.

Sera sat very still. Because she had seen something, weeks ago, in the council chamber — a green flicker behind the king's eyes, a movement beneath his skin like a vine shifting under wallpaper — and she had told herself she imagined it, because the alternative was unthinkable, and she had gotten very good at telling herself things.

"I need to see the map," she said.

"Why?"

"Because three days ago I reported to my commander that the courier's death didn't sit right with me, and this morning I was ordered to hunt you specifically, by name, within hours of the body cooling. Somebody wanted you found fast, Delacroix. Or found dead. And the Wardens don't move that quickly unless the order comes from very high up." She paused. "Palace high."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh her — the oath on her shoulder, the sword at her hip, the frostburn on her arm she'd earned stepping between him and a monster. Whatever he saw, he seemed to decide it was enough, because he shifted his bound hands awkwardly toward the inner pocket of his ruined coat.

"It's wrapped in oilcloth. Left side. You'll have to—"

"I know where the left side is."

"Right. Yes. Obviously." He cleared his throat. "You have very commanding elbows."

"Thank you," Sera said, and fished out the map.

* * *

By full dark she had seen it — the guild sigil pressed into the wax like a brand, the route threading north toward the Thornwood in lines that seemed to shift slightly when she wasn't looking directly at them — and she had made her decision, or rather the decision had made itself somewhere in her chest during his story, quietly, the way ice forms on a pond: all at once, and long after you should have noticed.

She untied his wrists.

Delacroix rubbed them and stared at her. "I — thank you? Is this a trick? Are you going to shoot me?"

"If I were going to shoot you, I'd have done it while you were monologuing about your grandmother."

"She's a sensitive subject."

"I noticed." She stood, pulled her bedroll from Bitterroot's pack, and dropped it beside the fire between them. Procedure said she should bind him again come morning and start the march south. Procedure said a lot of things. "Sleep. We move at first light."

"Move *where*?" he asked carefully. "South? To Vharrow? To the Wardens?"

Sera looked north, where the pines stood black against a sky full of hard stars, where the cold came down off the Thornwood like a held breath, where a dying man's map pointed and a green flicker waited behind a king's eyes.

"North," she said. "We move north."

And because she was who she was — because six years of penance had taught her that oaths were the only bones some people had — she added, firmly, to herself and not to him:

*This is procedure. This is absolutely procedure.*

Delacroix smiled at her across the fire, warm and exhausted and grateful, and Sera Vane, huntress of the royal order, looked away first.

Somewhere far to the north, something in the dark turned over in its sleep — and began, patiently, to dream of spring.
