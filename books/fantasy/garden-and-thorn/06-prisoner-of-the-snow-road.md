## 6. Prisoner of the Snow Road

Sera Vane had marched prisoners before. She had marched poachers and smugglers, a counterfeiter who wept the entire way to Vharrow, and one memorable horse-thief who tried to escape three times in a single afternoon and each time ran directly into a tree.

Dominic Delacroix was not like any of them. Dominic Delacroix *talked*.

"—and that's the thing about hedge-craft, you see," he said, hands bound before him, breath puffing white in the grey morning air. "It's not really magic at all. It's more like... negotiation. You ask the frost to be kind to your window-boxes. Most mornings it says no. But it hears you."

"Prisoners don't talk."

"Prisoners who are guilty don't talk. Prisoners who are innocent talk constantly. It's a well-documented phenomenon." He stumbled on an ice-slick stone, caught himself, and continued as though nothing had happened. "You could look it up. If there were any books left about it. Which there aren't, because your Wardens burned them."

"The Wardens burned books about *magic*."

"Yes, that was my point." He smiled at her over his shoulder — a lopsided, apologetic smile, the smile of a man apologizing for the weather. "Sorry. I do this when I'm frightened. My grandmother used to say I'd narrate my own funeral."

*Your grandmother,* Sera thought, and her hand tightened on the lead-rope. The guild medallion hidden under his shirt. The map sewn into his coat lining, marked with a sigil she'd only seen once before, in a Warden training manual, under a chapter titled *Historical Threats: The Delacroix Line.*

She had arrested a legend. She wished he'd act like one.

The snow road wound north through the pinewastes, back toward Vharrow, three days' march if the weather held and five if it didn't. It never held. Sera had grown up in these pines; she knew their moods the way other people knew their mothers'. And the pines were saying something she didn't like. The frost lay too deep for midwinter, in patterns too regular — spirals, almost, feathering out from the road like ripples from a dropped stone.

"You feel that too," Dominic said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I feel cold. It's winter."

"It's *wrong* winter." His voice lost its chatter, and that, somehow, was worse. "Frost doesn't spiral. Frost doesn't have opinions. Something is pushing it ahead of itself, like a tide pulling back before—" He stopped himself.

"Before what?"

"Nothing. Forget it. You'll arrest me harder."

"I can't arrest you harder. You're already arrested."

"Then I'll say something seditious instead." He glanced north, toward the horizon where the Thornwood's dark line sat beneath the clouds. "There's a thing wearing the king. A green thing. And it's hungry, and it's eating the realm one winter at a time, and the map in my coat is the only way anyone's going to stop it. There. Arrested twice."

Sera said nothing. She had seen something behind the king's eyes — a flicker, green as new leaves, gone so fast she'd told herself she imagined it. She had reported the courier's death and been given this hunt within the hour, orders signed before the courier's body was even cold. She had told herself that was efficiency.

She told herself a great many things lately.

* * *

By midday the talking resumed, but it had changed key. Less performance now. More confession.

"My grandmother was guild-master," Dominic said. They had stopped to eat — hard bread for him, hard bread for her, though she pretended hers was better. "Elara Delacroix. Before the Sundering, before the ban. I was six when she went into exile. I don't even know if she's alive."

"The guild bound things," Sera said. "That's what the histories say. Bound monsters."

"They bound one monster. One." He turned his bread over in his bound hands. "The Verdant. Old beyond old — older than the city, maybe older than the kingdom. It wasn't evil, whatever the manuals say. It was *growing*, the way rivers flow and fire burns. It grew, and everything it touched bloomed, and everything that bloomed belonged to it. So the guild made a bargain. Bound it into a vessel. Fed it just enough to keep it sleeping. And the vessel they chose—"

He looked at her. Waited.

"The crown," Sera said flatly.

"The crown. Passed king to king, down the years, each one a little greener behind the eyes than the last. And the bargain required tending — relics, rites, a guild to keep the seals whole. Then came the Sundering, and the ban, and the Wardens, and the guild burned or scattered or forgot. Nobody tended the seals. Nobody fed the Verdant properly. So it started feeding itself." He gestured at the spiraling frost, the dead-still pines. "This is what starving looks like. Not spring. Never spring — it promised some fool at court eternal spring once, I'd wager, and eternal spring is just another word for endless hunger. Winter is what falls when it's done with a place."

"You're asking me to believe the king is possessed by a garden."

"I'm asking you to believe your own eyes. You've seen the villages. People sleepwalking through their lives, hearths cold, gardens green in January. That's the Verdant's touch, spreading ahead of its need. It's tasting the land." He paused. "You've seen the king. Haven't you? At council?"

Sera stood up abruptly and checked the hobbles on his boots, which needed no checking. "We move."

"Sera."

"Huntress Vane," she corrected, because his use of her given name felt like a hand on her arm.

"*Huntress Vane.* You saw it too. Whatever it was. Otherwise you wouldn't be marching me to the capital — you'd have put an arrow in me at the courier's body and collected your bounty."

She had considered the arrow. She would not tell him that. "I follow procedure."

"Procedure," he repeated, gently, the way you'd repeat the name of someone recently deceased.

They walked on. The light thickened toward evening, bruise-colored, and the frost spirals grew wider, and somewhere behind them — far behind, or perhaps not — something howled that was not wolf.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, and Sera built the fire small because small fires were safe fires, and Dominic watched her work with an expression she chose to interpret as respect rather than pity.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you're good at this. The tracking, the camping, the general competence. In another life you'd have made a fine guild scout."

"In another life I'd have made a fine *nothing*. The order took me at thirteen. It gave me everything."

"Did it?"

She threw a pinecone at him. He caught it against his chest with his bound hands, which rather ruined the gesture, and grinned, and she found herself fighting the grin back and losing by a narrow margin, which annoyed her considerably.

"Why did you report the courier's death?" he asked, softer. "You could have buried it. Taken the map yourself. Sold it."

"To whom? Everyone who'd want it wants it dead or wants it caged." She fed a twig to the flames. "I reported it because it was true. Things that are true get reported. That's the whole shape of my life."

"And if the truth turns out to be a green thing behind the king's eyes?"

"Then I'll report that too." She met his eyes across the fire. "Whatever it costs. That's the oath. Not to the man on the throne. To the truth underneath him."

Something passed between them then — brief, unspoken, the kind of thing that has no procedure. Dominic opened his mouth to say whatever it was, and that was when the howling came again, close enough to raise the hair on her arms.

Not wolf. She'd known it wasn't wolf. Wolves sounded like hunger. This sounded like *obedience* — long, disciplined cries, coordinated, calling to one another across the dark.

"Warden-hounds," she breathed.

"Here?" Dominic scrambled up. "Warden-hounds patrol the capital. They're leashed to the crown's will, they don't range the pinewastes, they—"

He stopped. She saw him arrive at the same conclusion she was arriving at, and she watched the color leave his face.

"They should only obey the crown," he whispered. "And the crown sent *you* after me. Quietly. One huntress, no escort, no writ posted. Why send one huntress, Sera? Unless you weren't meant to come back either."

The howls split — two voices east, two west. Closing.

"Down," Sera snapped, all warmth gone, all huntress now. She killed the fire with a sweep of her boot, dragged Dominic behind the granite shelf, and drew her bow in the same motion. "Can you run with those hobbles?"

"Not gracefully."

"Gracefully is optional." She cut the rope joining his wrists to his belt but left his hands bound — she wasn't a fool — and pressed her back to the stone beside his. In the darkness, eyes were opening among the trees. Four pairs. Six. They burned a color she had no name for: not the amber of beasts, but a deep, patient green, like lamplight through bottle glass.

Green. Of course it was green.

"Dominic," she said quietly. "Whatever hedge-negotiation you do with frost and window-boxes. Can you negotiate with *that*?"

"I can ask," he said. "It might say no."

"It's going to say no."

"Then I'll ask very sincerely."

The first hound came over the shelf in a single silent bound — huge, wrong-jointed, its hide moving like bark, its mouth full of too many teeth arranged in spirals. Spirals again. Everything was spirals now. Sera loosed an arrow into its chest and the shaft sank in like it had struck wet loam, and the thing didn't even slow, and she had time to think *oh, that's bad* before Dominic stepped past her, raised his bound hands, and *spoke*.

Not words, exactly. Or words, but the way a bell speaks — a sound that went through her chest and out the other side, that made the fire's death-smoke curl upward, that made every green eye in the dark flinch. The hound froze mid-lunge, trembling, its spiral teeth grinding against one another.

"*No,*" Dominic said, in ordinary speech now, ragged and human. "*No. Not her. Not us. Go home.*"

For one heartbeat, the whole pinewood held still.

Then the hounds screamed — all of them at once, a sound like tearing green wood — and broke apart into the night, fleeing, and the silence they left behind rang like a struck glass.

Sera lowered her bow slowly. Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with distant academic interest, the way one notices weather in another country.

"You," she said.

"That was hedge-craft," Dominic said weakly. He sat down rather suddenly in the snow. "Mostly. Some of it may have been slightly more than hedge-craft. Don't arrest me harder."

"You just commanded Warden-hounds. Crown-bound creatures. With your *voice*."

"I asked sincerely."

She stared at him. He stared back, snow-dusted, exhausted, his wrists still bound, looking for all the world like a man who had apologized to a pack of nightmares and been taken at his word. And Sera Vane, huntress of the royal order, sworn to the crown, felt the last load-bearing wall of her certainty crack straight down the middle.

Because the hounds should have obeyed the crown. Instead they had obeyed *him* — or fled from him — and either way, someone above her had sent them to kill them both, and the only power in the realm that could command Warden-hounds away from a kill sat bleeding and shivering in the snow in front of her, bound with her own rope.

Someone wanted them both dead. Which meant, whether she liked it or not, they were on the same side now.

She knelt, took his bound wrists, and drew her knife.

"Whoa," said Dominic. "I want you to know I've been extremely cooperative."

"Hold still." She sawed through the rope. His hands came free, chafed red, and he flexed them gingerly while she repacked the camp with sharp, furious efficiency, because fury was easier than the other thing she was feeling.

"We go south," she said. "Not to Vharrow. Not yet. Someone in the capital sent those hounds, and I'm not delivering you into their hands until I know whose."

"So I'm... not a prisoner anymore?"

"You're a prisoner of circumstances." She slung her pack. "Walk in front of me, keep talking, and don't make me regret this."

"What am I allowed to talk about?"

"Start with your grandmother. Start with the map. Start with why a charm-seller can shout down a Warden-hound." She paused at the edge of the firelight, looking back at him, and the gentleness that slipped into her voice surprised them both. "Start anywhere. Just — start walking, Dominic."

He smiled — that lopsided, apologetic smile — and fell into step beside her, and together they turned their backs on the capital and walked into the dark of the pinewastes, while behind them, far off, the frost went on spiraling outward like ripples from a stone that had not yet finished falling.
