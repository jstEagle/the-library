## 7. The Hollow Village

The village appeared out of the snow like a rumor.

One moment there was nothing but white pines and the grey road, and the next — rooftops. Chimneys. Smoke, or what should have been smoke, except that when Dominic squinted at it he realized the chimneys were breathing something else entirely: a faint green shimmer, like heat-haze in summer, rising from hearths that had no fire beneath them.

"Thornbrook," Sera said, reading the leaning signpost at the crossroads. The paint had peeled, but her voice went flat and careful in a way that told him she knew the place. "It was a wool town. Four hundred souls, last census."

"And now?"

She didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They'd been walking three days since the Warden-hounds came out of the dark and tried to kill them both — prisoner and captor alike, which had done more to loosen Sera's certainty than anything Dominic could have said. She still bound his wrists each morning. She'd simply stopped pretending the rope was tight.

"You could untie me," he offered, as they crunched down the rutted lane toward the first house. "For morale."

"I could also leave you tied to a fence post for the wolves," Sera said, but there was a ghost of warmth under it now, the kind of warmth that survives only in people who've decided not to examine it too closely. "Morale's fine."

The first thing wrong was the silence. Villages made noise even in winter — dogs, axes, someone shouting at someone about firewood. Thornbrook made none. The second thing wrong was the gardens.

Dominic stopped so abruptly that Sera nearly walked into him.

Roses. In the middle of deep winter, with snow piled to the eaves, every garden on the lane was in bloom. Roses and foxglove and things he didn't have names for, all of them open and nodding in a wind that should have killed them before breakfast. And the colors were wrong — too saturated, too eager, like a painting of flowers done by someone who had heard about flowers secondhand.

"Oh," Dominic said quietly. "Oh, no."

"What?"

He crouched at the edge of one garden, careful not to touch. Beneath the snow, threading through the soil like veins under skin, ran roots of a green so deep it was almost black. They pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"It's ahead of us," he said. "The Verdant. It's not just wearing the king — it's reaching out through him, feeding on the land along the roads he'll travel. Preparing the ground." He looked up at Sera, and for once he didn't try to soften it. "This is what my grandmother's order spent two hundred years making sure never happened. This is the garden it always wanted to plant."

A woman came around the corner of the house carrying a watering can.

That was the third thing wrong. Nobody watered roses in winter, and nobody moved the way she did — smooth, unhurried, her face pleasant and empty as an unsent letter. She poured water over the blooms without looking at them, then turned and walked back inside, and the door closed behind her with the soft finality of a held breath.

Sera's hand went to her sword. "Is she—"

"Alive? I think so." Dominic rose slowly. "Asleep, maybe. Walking dreams. The Verdant doesn't kill what it feeds on, not right away. It tucks it in." He swallowed. "My grandmother used to say the worst monsters are the ones that love you while they eat you."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a charm-seller. Cheer is most of the product."

* * *

They found the inn by its sign — *The Wool Ram*, though the painted ram had faded to a ghost of itself — and pushed open the door into a common room where perhaps thirty people sat at tables, all of them awake, none of them doing anything.

No one spoke. No one ate. A man near the hearth stared into a cold fireplace with an expression of mild contentment, the way other men stare into fires. A woman in the corner was knitting, but the needles hadn't moved; the yarn sat in her lap like a sleeping cat. On the bar, a tankard of ale had gone flat and sour days ago, judging by the smell, and no one had minded.

"They're breathing," Sera said softly. She'd moved through the room the way she moved through everything — quiet, controlled, checking threats. "Warm. Fed, somehow. It's like..."

"A house where everyone fell asleep at their own funeral," Dominic said. He touched the shoulder of the man by the hearth, gently. The man smiled at nothing and kept staring. "Hello. Can you hear me?" Nothing. "What's your name?" Nothing. Then, because he couldn't help himself: "Do you know any good jokes?"

"Dominic."

"Worth trying. Humor reaches places sense doesn't."

But the man's lips did move, just slightly, and Dominic leaned close. What came out was barely a breath, worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket too long:

*"Green grows the garden. Green grows the garden. Green grows—"*

Dominic straightened. His hands had gone cold, and not from the weather.

"We shouldn't stay," Sera said. She'd gone pale, which for Sera seemed to require genuine effort. "Whatever this is, we walk through it and keep moving. We sleep in the treeline."

"With the frost-wraiths," Dominic said. "Who tried very hard to eat us two nights ago."

"Yes."

"And you're wounded, and I'm half-frozen, and the nearest living village is two days further on." He gestured at the stairs. "There are beds up there. Warm ones. Whatever holds these people isn't interested in us — if it were, we'd already be watering roses."

Sera looked at the room full of smiling sleepers for a long moment. Then she said, "We take turns keeping watch. You first watch ends when I say it does, not when you get bored and start talking to the furniture."

"I would never talk to furniture."

"You talked to a hound corpse yesterday. Asked it who its master was."

"And did it answer? No. Because I'm thorough, not mad. There's a difference, and it's mostly paperwork."

Something happened to her mouth that in a better-lit room might have been called a smile. She took the chair by the door, sword across her knees, and pointed at the stairs. "Bed. Prisoner."

"Hostage," he corrected, climbing. "Prisoners have rights. Hostages just have hopes."

* * *

The child woke them.

Not a sound — worse than a sound. A wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the ears, the feeling Dominic had learned to read the hard way: magic moving nearby, and moving badly. He was down the stairs before Sera finished unsheathing her sword, and together they followed the pull of it out the inn's back door, into the garden behind the stable.

A little girl stood among the impossible roses. Six years old, maybe seven, in a nightgown far too thin for the season, barefoot in snow that didn't seem to chill her. Her eyes were open but nobody was home in them — and around her ankles, rising out of the earth like slow green serpents, curled the black-deep roots.

They were wrapped around her legs. Climbing. Patient as winter.

"No," Dominic breathed, and then he was moving, dropping to his knees in the snow beside her, and everything his grandmother had ever drilled into him — *hide it, smother it, small magic keeps you alive* — went out of his head like smoke through a broken window.

His hands closed around the roots, and he pulled.

They resisted. Of course they resisted; they were the fingers of something vast and old and hungry, and he was a charm-seller with rope-burned wrists. But underneath the fear, underneath three years of hiding, something older stirred — the guild training, the medallion warm against his chest, his grandmother's voice saying *magic is just attention, boy, attention and love and the nerve to spend them*. He pressed his palms flat against the girl's frozen feet and gave the magic everything he'd been rationing since Vharrow.

Warmth poured out of him. Not fire-warmth — growing warmth, spring warmth, the specific stubborn heat of a seed deciding to be a tree. He pushed it down through the soil, into the roots, and spoke the words his grandmother had taught him in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon, the old binding-words, the *un*-binding words:

*"You were invited. You are now uninvited. Go home."*

The roots shuddered. For one horrible moment they tightened, and the girl gasped — actually gasped, actually *woke*, her eyes flying wide and human and terrified — and then the green light in the soil guttered like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the roots slid backward into the earth, retreating, sulking, gone.

The garden around them wilted. All at once, every bloom in sight drooped and browned, and real cold rushed in to reclaim the ground, and the snow began to fall honestly again instead of glittering with borrowed light.

The little girl burst into tears.

Which was, Dominic thought dimly, the single best sound he'd heard in weeks.

* * *

Her name was Tilly. Her mother was one of the sleepwalkers upstairs — the woman with the unmoving knitting — and she alone had been left awake, Dominic realized, because children's dreams were too restless for the Verdant to hold comfortably. It had been feeding on her family for days and saving her for last. A dessert course.

She cried herself hoarse against his chest while he carried her inside, and Sera built a fire — a real one, ordinary wood and flint and spark, and the ordinary crackle of it sounded like a choir after the green silence. They got broth into the girl. They wrapped her in blankets. And when she finally slept, properly slept, in front of the hearth with the dog-eared stuffed hare she'd insisted on retrieving from her house, Dominic sat down on the floor across from her and discovered his hands wouldn't stop shaking.

"You spent too much," Sera said. She'd seen it, then. Of course she had; she noticed everything. She sat down across from him and put a bowl of broth in his hands and watched until he drank some of it. "I felt it from the doorway. Like a draft."

"Just a bit of hedge-craft."

"Don't." Her voice wasn't sharp. It was worse than sharp — it was careful. "Don't do that. Don't make it small. I've spent my whole life being taught your kind of magic is a crime, and I just watched you use it to pull a child out of the ground, and I need—" She stopped. Started again. "I need you not to make it small, so I can decide how big it actually is."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a sleeper murmured *green grows the garden* in her gentle stolen dream.

Dominic set down the bowl. "All right. Here's how big it is. What I did tonight — that's the least of what the guild could do. My grandmother could stop a river arguing. There were masters who could heal bones, speak with the dead, unmake curses laid by kings. Two hundred years of it. And the crown burned it down to hedge-charms and smuggled medallions because it was afraid." He met her eyes. "And now the crown itself has a god wearing it like a coat, and the god is eating villages, and the only people who remember how to fight it are dead, scattered, or standing in this room drinking bad broth."

Sera was quiet for a long time. The firelight moved on her face.

"When I was nine," she said finally, "my mother got a fever. Bad one. The village healer was a hedge-witch — unlicensed, guild-trained, the whole list of crimes. My father wanted to send for her. My mother said no. Law was law." Her jaw tightened. "She died on the fourth day. Law was law."

"Sera—"

"The healer came anyway. After. To sit with my father. She didn't charge anything. She just... sat, and let him cry, and told him stories about my mother when they were girls." Sera looked into the fire. "I reported her anyway. Two years later. Because law was law, and because I thought if I was perfect enough at the law, it would give my mother back." She laughed, short and joyless. "It didn't. It gave me a commission and a sword and a very long road to this exact fireside."

Dominic didn't say *I'm sorry*, because it wasn't a wound you apologized to; it was a wound you sat with. So he sat with it. He reached over and, very carefully, refilled her bowl.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the guild forgave worse. Forgiveness was sort of our whole business model. Right after charms."

"You're terrible at comfort."

"I'm excellent at comfort. I sell protective charms to frightened people for a living. Comfort is my core competency." He nudged the bowl toward her again. "Eat. You kept watch half the night and fought off a god's gardening schedule before dawn. Even huntresses are allowed soup."

She ate. And somewhere between the soup and the fire, without either of them announcing it or examining it, the distance between their chairs became smaller, and stayed smaller.

* * *

Near dawn, Tilly woke screaming.

Not nightmares — words. She sat bolt upright in her blankets, eyes wild and clear, and grabbed Dominic's sleeve with both hands, and said in a rush:

"He's coming. The green man. He's coming down the king's road with soldiers whose shadows are wrong, and he knows you're here, he *felt* you, you pulled his roots out and he *felt it*—" She gulped air. "He says the garden wants its gardener back."

Outside, far to the south, where the road bent toward the capital, the sky had begun to glow. Not sunrise. Sunrise came from the east.

This light came up green.

Dominic looked at Sera. Sera was already buckling her sword.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Tilly answered for him, in the flat reciting voice of someone repeating words planted in her sleep:

"Till the roses finish dying."
