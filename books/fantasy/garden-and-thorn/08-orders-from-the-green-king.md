## 8. Orders from the Green King

Warden-Captain Corvane had burned forty-one mages in his nineteen years of service, and he remembered every one of their names.

He kept them the way other men kept sins — not out of guilt, though guilt had its place, but out of discipline. A Warden who forgot a name was a Warden who might one day forget why the names mattered. So each morning, before he dressed, before he broke his fast, before he sharpened the long knife that was not technically a sword and therefore permitted within palace walls, Corvane recited them. Aldous Merrin. The twins, Petra and Joss Fell. Old Nan Copperkettle, who had only ever charmed milk to keep sweet and wept so pitifully at the stake that two of his own men had refused to light it.

Forty-one names. He said them in order. He did not skip.

This morning, standing in the antechamber of the king's private audience hall while a servant boy trembled through the business of adjusting his collar, Corvane got as far as name twenty-three before the doors opened and the summons came.

"His Majesty will see you now."

*His Majesty.* Corvane followed the chamberlain down the long gallery, past windows that had been shuttered against a winter now in its fourth month, and tried to put his finger on what had changed. Something about the phrase itself. *His Majesty will see you now.* It had always been said that way. But lately it sounded less like an announcement and more like an apology.

The shutters were the first wrongness, if you thought about it. Vharrow had seen hard winters before — three weeks of frozen canals, snow to the second-floor sills, the river ice thick enough to hold a market. This was different. This was winter like a tenant who had stopped paying rent and stopped pretending it intended to leave. Four months. The granaries were at half. The Wardens' own ledgers showed it: fewer arrests for witchcraft, more arrests for bread theft. Desperation, Corvane had learned, looked much the same whether it came from magic or hunger, and both kinds ended up in front of him eventually.

The second wrongness was the garden.

The royal gardens lay beyond the gallery's eastern windows, or rather, where the gardens used to be. Now there stood a wall of thorned hedge, black-green and glistening, higher than the palace roof, grown up in a single season from rosebushes and boxwood. No gardener tended it. No gardener *could*. Men sent to trim it came back with their shears fused to the branches, or didn't come back at all. The court had learned to walk on the north side of the gallery, where the windows were plain glass and frost, and to look straight ahead.

Corvane looked. It was a habit of his profession to look at things other people agreed not to see.

The hedge had grown another foot since Tuesday.

* * *

The king's audience chamber had once been a bright room, all white marble and tall windows meant to catch the southern sun. Now the shutters were drawn here too, and the light came from a hearth that burned green.

Not green-tinged. Not green because of some alchemical salt thrown on the coals, a trick Corvane had seen at festivals. The fire was green the way blood is red — entirely, essentially, without remainder. And it gave no heat. Corvane knew this because he had once, in an early and regrettable display of initiative, held his hand near it. His fingers had gone numb in a way that had nothing to do with cold, and for three days afterward he had dreamed of roots growing through his teeth.

King Aldric sat beneath the green fire on the Ironrose Throne, and Corvane performed the arithmetic he performed every visit: how much of the man was left?

Less than last time. That was the answer. Always less.

Aldric had been sixty when the Verdant took him — a broad, ruddy, loud man, famous for eating entire pheasants with his hands and laughing until the chandeliers rang. The thing on the throne wore his shape the way a glove wears a hand. The face was right. The bulk was right. But the skin had gone pale as candle wax, faintly translucent at the temples, and when the head turned there was sometimes a lag — a half-second delay, as if the order to move had to travel a very great distance to arrive.

And the eyes. Behind the gray irises, deep back where the light should have drowned, something green moved. Not reflected. *Moving.*

"Captain Corvane." The voice was the king's voice, mostly. There was a new undertone to it, a rustle, like wind through leaves that were not present. "You bring me good news, I hope."

"I bring you news, Majesty." Corvane knelt, because kneeling was procedure, and procedure was the spine that held him upright in rooms like this. "The courier who carried the map is dead. Slain in the shop of a charm-seller named Delacroix. But the map was not recovered. It passed into Delacroix's hands, and Delacroix has fled the city."

Silence. In the silence, Corvane heard — or thought he heard — a sound beneath the king's stillness. A slow creaking, organic, patient. Like a forest settling after snowfall. He had decided years ago that he did not want to know what made that sound, and he clung to that decision now the way drowning men cling to driftwood.

"A charm-seller," the king said at last. The word turned over slowly, tasted. "Harmless trinkets. Little bags of dried flowers. And yet my hunters cannot find him."

"The hunt continues, Majesty. Sera Vane—"

"Yes." The green fire flickered. "Vane. My little hound with her little conscience. Tell me, Captain — do you think she will catch him?"

Corvane chose his words the way he crossed rotten ice. "She has never lost a quarry."

"No," the king agreed softly. "She hasn't. Isn't that remarkable? Such a faithful creature. Do you know what I enjoy most about the faithful, Captain?"

"Majesty?"

"They believe the faithfulness is theirs." The king smiled. It was the king's smile, wide and warm, the one from the festival portraits. It reached the mouth and stopped there, at the border, like a traveler refused entry. "They think they chose it. They think it lives inside them, next to their heart, where they keep it safe. They have no idea how close to the surface such things grow. How easily they are... tended."

The creaking sound again. Closer. Corvane kept his eyes on a point six inches above the king's left shoulder, which was the traditional spot for looking at royalty you could not bear to look at directly.

"The map, Captain." The warmth drained out of the voice, leaving something older underneath, something that made the marble floor feel briefly like soil. "It shows the road to the Thornwood. It shows the way to the binding. If it reaches the Thornwood, if a hand with guild-blood opens what that map opens—" The king's hands, resting on the throne's arms, flexed. Bark rippled across the knuckles and smoothed again, quick as a shiver. "Everything I have built. Everything I have *grown*. Gone. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"I understand, Majesty."

"Then go. Take your Wardens. Find the charm-seller. Find the huntress, if she has found him first, and bring me both. And Captain—" the green eyes fixed on him, and this time Corvane could not look away, could not find the six inches above the shoulder, because the thing behind them was looking *out*, "—silence everyone who has seen the map. Everyone. I do not care if it is the boy who swept the courier's boots. Silence is a kindness. Winter is coming either way, but those who die warm do not notice."

Corvane bowed and walked backward from the room, as protocol demanded, and did not turn around until the doors closed behind him.

In the gallery, alone among the shuttered windows, he allowed himself thirty seconds to stand still and breathe. Then he took out his notebook and began writing orders.

* * *

The Warden barracks occupied a squat granite block in the Shadowmarket district, chosen generations ago for its proximity to the kind of alleys where hedge-witches brewed their teas and fortune-tellers read palms for copper. Corvane ran it like a ship. Every Warden knew his post, his patrol, his quota of reports. Every report crossed Corvane's desk. He read them all, even the tedious ones, especially the tedious ones, because in nineteen years he had learned that the truth about a city hides in tedium the way rats hide in walls.

By midday he had assembled his lieutenants: Brannoc, huge and reliable; the twins Ilse and Marta Kessler, who finished each other's sentences and each other's kills; young Petyr, whose mother Corvane had once pulled out of a burning tenement back when he'd believed the job was about protection, and who worshipped him with the uncomplicated devotion that Corvane found increasingly difficult to deserve.

"The quarry is Dominic Delacroix," Corvane told them, unrolling the sketch an informant had supplied. A mild face. Tired eyes. The kind of face you forgot in a crowd, which was precisely what made it dangerous. "Charm-seller, Lantern Row. Fled the city two nights ago, likely through the underways. He carries a document of the highest sensitivity. He is to be taken alive if possible."

"And if not possible?" Brannoc asked.

"Then dead, and we tell His Majesty it was not possible." Corvane let that sit. "There is also the matter of Huntress Vane, who was dispatched by the Crown before I was briefed. She may already have found him. If you encounter her, you are to treat her as an ally. You are also to observe her. Report everything."

Petyr frowned. "Report what, sir? She's one of ours. Royal Order."

"She is," Corvane said. "That is exactly why you will report everything."

He dismissed them to their preparations and remained at his desk with the reports, reading by lamplight while the afternoon died outside. This was his favorite hour and he would never have admitted it. The barracks quiet. The paperwork honest. Forty-one names said, the day accounted for.

It was in the tedium that he found it, as he always did. A grain merchant's complaint from the village of Hollowmere, filed by a Warden who clearly wished he were anywhere else: *inhabitants listless, hearths cold, fields blooming out of season, recommend investigation when convenient.*

Blooming. Out of season. In the fourth month of winter.

Corvane read it three times. Then he pulled the older files, and there it was, scattered across a dozen routine complaints nobody had connected because nobody was paid to connect them: villages along the northern roads, gardens green in the frost, people who answered questions slowly and smiled at nothing. A trail of impossible spring, leading north.

Leading toward the Thornwood.

He sat back. On his desk, in a clay pot he kept for no reason he could name except that it had been his wife's, a sprig of rosemary had been dying for two weeks. He had watered it, moved it to the light, done everything short of talking to it. Dead as a doornail, he'd told himself, and kept watering it anyway, because throwing it out felt like a second funeral and he wasn't ready.

As he watched, the rosemary lifted. One green needle, then another, unfurling from a stem he had personally confirmed was brown and brittle yesterday. It grew as he stared — an inch, two — reaching toward him with the unmistakable eagerness of a plant that knows it is being observed.

Corvane picked up the pot, carried it to the window, opened the shutter, and set it out on the sill in the freezing air. Then he closed the shutter and stood with his hand flat against the cold stone, listening to his own heartbeat, waiting for it to slow.

It did not slow.

*It gets into everything,* he thought. *It got into the king. It got into the hedge. It got into my wife's rosemary, and gods forgive me, it is getting into my reports, my city, my Wardens, and I am the only man in Vharrow who reads every page.*

He thought about the king's words. *Those who die warm do not notice.* He thought about old Nan Copperkettle, weeping at the stake over nothing worse than sweet milk, and how he had lit the pyre himself because his men wouldn't, because someone had to, because law was law.

He thought: *I have spent nineteen years burning people for lesser versions of the thing that sits on the throne giving me orders.*

Then he did what he always did with unbearable thoughts. He folded them small, put them in the drawer with the forty-one names, and went to work.

* * *

They rode out at dusk — twelve Wardens, supply carts, and a cage wagon that Corvane had ordered built to specifications he did not explain and no one dared ask about. The gates of Vharrow groaned open onto a world gone white and silent. Snow fell in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind of snow that intends to stay.

At the last crossroads before the north road, Corvane halted the column and looked back once at the city. From here he could see the palace on its hill, and rising above it, visible even in the dark, the vast black shape of the thorn-hedge, taller tonight than it had been that morning. Growing. Always growing. Somewhere inside that green dark, a king who was not a king sat beside a fire that gave no heat, tending his garden of faithful things.

Corvane touched his heels to his horse.

"Hollowmere by dawn," he called to his lieutenants. "Anyone who finds the charm-seller does not engage. Send signal. We take him together, we take him clean, and we take him *alive* — His Majesty wants him able to answer questions."

He did not say what questions. He suspected he did not want to know. He suspected, in the drawer behind the forty-one names, that he already did.

The column moved north into the falling snow, twelve torches swallowed quickly by the white, and behind them, unnoticed by any of them, the rosemary on the Warden-Captain's windowsill pressed its new green needles against the glass — reaching, patiently, toward the north.
