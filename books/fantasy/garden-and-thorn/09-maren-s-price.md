## 9. Maren's Price

The safehouse was hidden behind a coffin-maker's shop in the town of Greyhollow, which Dominic felt was a touch on the nose even for Maren.

"You couldn't have picked a bakery?" he muttered as Sera rapped the coded knock — three slow, two fast, one slow — on the back door while snow piled on their shoulders like impatient creditors.

"Maren doesn't do cheerful," Sera said. "She does *survivable*. There's a difference."

"There is," said the door, opening to reveal a small woman with iron-grey hair cropped short and eyes that had seen too much to bother blinking at anything anymore. "For instance, a bakery would have fed you. I'm going to feed you *yesterday's* stew and make you grateful for it." Maren Voss looked from Dominic to Sera, then to the frost-wraith scars on Sera's cloak, then back again. "Huh. You brought a huntress."

"She brought herself," Dominic said. "I've been trying to return her for days. No takers."

Sera elbowed him. It was, he reflected, becoming her primary form of communication, and he was beginning to worry he'd started to like it.

Maren stepped aside. "Inside, both of you, before the road notices you're gone."

* * *

The safehouse was three rooms below ground, lit by lamps that burned without oil — guild-lamps, Dominic realized, the kind his grandmother had once made before the Sundering made such things a hanging offense. The walls were lined with shelves of jars: dried herbs, preserved roots, and things he chose not to identify too closely. A cot. A stove. A table scarred by decades of maps being unrolled across it.

And on that table, waiting, as if Maren had known they were coming — because of course she had; Maren always knew — sat a small wooden box.

"Eat first," Maren said, pressing bowls into their hands. The stew was, as promised, yesterday's, and somehow still better than anything Dominic had eaten in a week. "Then we talk about why you look like a man who's been carrying a question around like a stone in his boot."

"I've been carrying several stones," Dominic said. "They multiply."

"Then let's take them out one at a time." She sat across from him, folded her hands, and became, in the space of a breath, the guild archivist she had once been — the woman who had memorized the Delacroix lineages when Dominic was still learning his letters. "You've read the map."

"Parts of it. The parts written in the old cipher. It leads to the Thornwood. To the Verdant." He hesitated. "It says the binding can be undone. But the middle section — the part that says *how* — is written in blood-ink. Grandmother's hand. And it's..." He pulled the map from his coat, unrolled it carefully between the bowls, and turned it toward Maren. "It's damaged. Burned. Right where the instructions should be."

Maren looked at the scorched patch for a long moment. Then she laughed — a dry, terrible sound with no humor in it at all.

"Burned," she repeated. "Of course it's burned. Elara Delacroix, may her stubborn soul rest badly, burned it herself. Because she didn't want anyone following her."

Dominic's stomach dropped. "Followed her where?"

"To gather the relics." Maren rose, went to the shelf, and took down the wooden box. "Sit down, boy. This is going to take a while, and you'll want to be sitting for the parts about your grandmother."

* * *

What Maren told him, over the next hour, rearranged everything Dominic thought he knew about his own family.

The unbinding of the Verdant required three things. First, the blood of a Delacroix — the line that had forged the original binding, and only that line, because the binding had been woven into the family's very gift, passed down like eye color or a crooked smile. Second, the guild's three relics: the seed-charm that had held the first seed of the bargain, the chalice that had held the first oath, and the blade that had cut the first wound. Third — and here Maren's voice went quiet — the willing surrender of the binder's magic entire. The binding could not simply be broken. It had to be *unmade*, thread by thread, and every thread ran through the Delacroix gift itself.

"Your grandmother figured this out eleven years ago," Maren said. "She spent six years gathering the relics. She found the chalice. She found the blade. And then she went after the seed-charm, and the Wardens caught her trail near Thorn Pass." Maren's jaw tightened. "We all thought she died there. Your parents told you she died, didn't they? Peacefully? In her garden?"

"That's what I was told."

"Your parents lied to protect you. Kind of them. Useless, but kind." Maren set the box on the table between them. "Elara escaped the Wardens. Barely. Badly burned, half her gift seared out of her. And do you know what she did with her last years? She didn't come home. She didn't send for you. She went into exile in the far north and she *burned the instructions out of the map* so that no one — no one, Dominic, least of all her grandson — would follow her path and die the way she nearly did."

Dominic stared at the box. His hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the winter outside.

"She left me a medallion," he said finally. "A charm shop. A life of selling luck-knots to farmers."

"She left you a *chance to live*. Whether you thank her for it is your business." Maren pushed the box an inch closer. "But the realm doesn't have time for your grandmother's caution. The Verdant is eating the king alive, the winter's getting longer every year, and the map's damage doesn't matter anymore because I can tell you what it said. I helped write it."

"You—"

"I was guild archivist, boy. Who do you think held the ink?" Maren opened the box.

Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a charm no bigger than an acorn — a carved seed of pale wood, veined with green so deep it seemed to glow from within. Even without touching it, Dominic felt it. Felt it the way you feel a struck bell through the floorboards. His grandmother's work. Older than his grandmother. *Guild-work.*

"The seed-charm," Maren said. "First relic. The seed the Verdant gave when the bargain was struck — the promise of eternal spring, pressed into wood so someone would remember what was promised. And what was actually delivered." Her mouth twisted. "Eternal spring, promised. Endless winter, delivered. The Verdant has been starving for three hundred years, Dominic. Bound to the crown, fed on the king's life instead of the land's, giving nothing back. That's why the winters lengthen. That's why the villages go hollow. It isn't malice. It's *hunger*."

"And the other two relics?"

"The chalice lies beneath the Abbey of Saint Verene, in the crypts. Warded. Your blood will open it — the warding recognizes Delacroix blood, nothing else." She paused. "The blade... the blade is harder. The blade's keeper won't give it up easily."

"Who keeps it?"

Maren met his eyes, and for the first time since he'd known her — since he'd known her his whole life, since she'd been the scary lady who visited at midwinter and pinched his cheek and told him he had his grandmother's hands — she looked away.

"You'll find out when you get there," she said. "Some news should be delivered in person."

"Maren."

"Some news," she repeated, with the finality of a door closing, "should be delivered in person."

* * *

He didn't sleep. Sera did, curled on the cot with her sword within reach even unconscious, because huntresses apparently slept the way cats did — lightly, and ready to commit violence. Dominic sat at the table with the seed-charm in his palm, feeling its faint pulse against his skin, and tried to add up the bill.

A Delacroix's blood. Fine. He had that, presumably; he'd been bleeding occasionally for twenty-six years and it had always looked correct.

Three relics. One acquired. Two to go, one of them guarded by a mystery Maren refused to spoil.

And the third requirement. The willing surrender of magic entire.

His magic. What little remained of it — the hedge-craft, the small warmth, the guild-gift thinned by generations of hiding but still, unmistakably, *his*. The thing that had stirred awake on the north road after years of dormancy, that had saved Sera from the wraith, that had healed a child in the hollow village while she watched him with an expression he was still deciding how to feel about.

To unbind the Verdant was to pour all of it out. Every drop. Forever.

"You're doing the arithmetic face," Sera said from the cot, without opening her eyes.

"I don't have an arithmetic face."

"You absolutely do. Your eyebrows meet in the middle and your mouth goes sideways, like a man reading a bill he can't pay." She sat up, hair flattened comically on one side, dignity entirely absent. "How bad is it?"

"It requires my magic. All of it. Permanently."

Sera was quiet for a moment. "Ah," she said. "That kind of bill."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You just haven't told yourself yet." She swung her legs off the cot and came to sit across from him, and in the lamplight her face was stripped of everything she wore for the world — the huntress mask, the officer's calm — leaving something younger and more frightened underneath. "Dominic. When I find out what I am without my oath, I'll let you know what to expect."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort is Maren's department, and she's asleep."

"She's not asleep. She's listening at the door. Aren't you, Maren?"

From behind the door, after a pause: "No."

* * *

Morning came grey and thin. Maren packed them provisions with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had sent people off to die before and had opinions about proper rations. Dried meat. Hard bread. A jar of something labeled, in her handwriting, *DO NOT EAT ALL AT ONCE*, which Dominic found less than reassuring.

At the door, she stopped him.

"The price," she said.

Dominic blinked. "You already gave me the charm."

"The charm was a loan. The price comes due after." She held up one finger, and her eyes — those tired, unblinking eyes — fixed on his with the weight of a signature. "When this is done, whatever it costs, however it ends — you rebuild the guild. Not in cellars. Not in cipher and secrets and children taught to fear their own hands. In daylight. A school. Openly. Because I have spent forty years watching gifted people burn or hide or rot, and I am *done*, Dominic Delacroix. Do you understand me? Swear it."

He thought of his grandmother's medallion against his chest. Of the child in the hollow village, breathing easy under his hands. Of the lamp burning without oil in the room behind him.

"I swear it," he said. "On the medallion. On the guild."

"On your grandmother's grave, whether she's in it or not."

"...On my grandmother's grave, wherever it is."

Maren studied him a moment longer, then nodded once, satisfied, and pressed the seed-charm's box into his hands. "Then go. And boy—" For just a moment, the archivist softened into the woman who had pinched his cheek at midwinter. "Your grandmother would be furious with you. And proud. Mostly furious. But proud."

"That's the most Delacroix thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I know. Go."

* * *

Sera was waiting for him in the alley behind the coffin-maker's, pack on her shoulder, and she was not alone in her hesitation. She stood very still, looking north, toward the capital — toward the order that had raised her, trained her, given her purpose after she'd burned the only other purpose she'd ever had.

"They'll know I've deserted by now," she said quietly. "Corvane will have sent word ahead. If I go back, I might be able to talk my way clear. Claim I lost you. Claim the wraith got you." She turned and looked at him, and Dominic saw her wrestling with it honestly, the way she wrestled with everything — openly, painfully, like a woman arm-wrestling her own reflection. "That's the smart move. Report in. Keep my rank. Work against them from inside."

"Is it?"

"No." She exhaled, long and white in the cold air. "I told myself for two weeks that taking you prisoner instead of killing you was procedure. I told myself marching you toward the capital instead of handing you to Corvane was procedure. I told myself staying with you after the hounds attacked was procedure." A ghost of a smile crossed her face. "I have used the word 'procedure' more in the past fortnight than in the previous five years, and I've run out of things to call procedure. So."

"So?"

"So I'm coming with you. To the abbey. To the blade. To the Thornwood, if it comes to that." She said it fast, the way people jump into cold water. "Not because of procedure. Because of you, you impossible, reckless, charm-selling fool. And if the Wardens catch me, they'll hang me for desertion, and I need you to know that I've done the arithmetic"—she tapped his forehead, right between the eyebrows where his arithmetic face lived—"and I've decided anyway."

Dominic's heart did something complicated and undignified.

"Sera Vane," he managed, "that is the single worst tactical decision I've ever heard."

"I know."

"I'm extremely glad you made it."

"I know that too." She shouldered her pack and started walking north, then paused. "Well? Are you coming, or are you going to stand there making that face?"

"What face?"

"The one where you're pleased and terrified and trying to look like neither."

"...I don't have a face for that."

"You absolutely do."

They walked out of Greyhollow together as the snow began again, soft and steady, and behind them in the coffin-maker's cellar a lamp burned without oil, keeping watch over an empty room until its keeper returned.

Two days later, on the frozen road to the Abbey of Saint Verene, they found the first sign that Corvane's web had reached this far: a Warden patrol, dead in the snow, killed by something that left green vines growing through the gaps in their armor.

Sera crouched beside the bodies, and when she stood, her face had gone pale as the drifts.

"Dominic," she said. "Whatever's doing this — it's moving faster than we are."
