## 10. Corvane's Web

Sera Vane had spent eleven years learning to read a city the way other people read a page, and what Vharrow's margins said tonight was *run*.

She saw it in the way the lamplighters worked in pairs now, when they'd always worked alone. In the beggars gone from the shrine-steps of Saint Aldric — not dead, she suspected, but *collected*, the way frost collects on glass. In the fact that three separate informants had failed to meet her this week and only one had sent an apology.

The city was holding its breath. She'd learned that particular silence in her first year of service, standing outside a burning house where a hedge-witch lived — had lived — with her brother's hand in hers and his eyes asking her a question she still hadn't answered.

"You're doing the face," Dominic said beside her.

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is the one that looks like it's doing arithmetic about how many ways I can die." He shifted the bundle under his cloak — map, seed-charm, medallion, all the small weight of everything that mattered. "For the record, it's more than you've calculated."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be impressive."

They moved down Tanner's Row with their collars up, two more shapes in a city full of shapes trying to be nobody. Maren's safehouse waited four streets east, behind a dyer's shop that hadn't produced dye in a decade. Maren had promised hot food, a locked door, and — her exact words, relayed through a child courier paid in copper — "a bath, because gods know the both of you smell like a wet dog's regrets."

Sera had almost smiled at that. Almost. Then she'd seen the boy who delivered the message glance over his shoulder twice before he ran off, and the arithmetic started again.

* * *

The safehouse door opened before Maren knocked — it always did; the woman claimed she could hear heartbeats through wood, which Sera had once dismissed as guild superstition and now filed under *probably true, add to list*.

Maren was sixty if she was a day, built like a rain barrel, with iron-gray hair cropped short and hands stained permanently blue from her old trade. She looked them both over on the threshold, then yanked them inside by their collars like a mother hauling in cats before a storm.

"You're late, you're filthy, and you were followed," she said.

"We weren't—" Dominic began.

"There's a man across the square who's been reading the same broadsheet for two hours. Either he's the slowest reader in Vharrow or he's Corvane's." She bolted the door, threw a bar Sera hadn't known existed, and turned. "Bath's through the back. Food's on the stove. Talk after."

The safehouse was low-ceilinged and warm, smelling of tallow and dried lavender, its walls hung with bolts of cloth gone stiff with age. A kettle muttered on the stove. It was, Sera thought, the first room since Vharrow that felt like a room instead of a hiding place, and she hated how badly she wanted to trust it.

Dominic did trust it immediately, because Dominic trusted rooms the way dogs trusted people — completely, stupidly, until bitten. He was already at the stove, lifting the pot lid, making a sound of genuine religious gratitude.

"Maren, if you weren't seventy and terrifying, I'd propose."

"I'm fifty-eight, flatterer, and I buried two husbands. Eat."

Sera ate too, though she kept her back to the wall and her eyes on the shuttered window. The stew was root vegetables and mystery meat and better than anything she'd had in a month. Across the table, Dominic talked with his mouth half-full — the pass ahead, the abbey in the deep pines where the second relic slept, the visions he'd had in the crypt that he still described carefully, like a man handling something that might cut him.

"The Verdant wasn't caged out of cruelty," he said. "It was starving. They bound it into a bargain it never agreed to, and bargains like that rot. You can't blame a wolf for eating when the men who penned it took away the deer."

"You sound like you feel sorry for it."

"I sound like someone who read the founders' own letters." He set down his spoon. "They wept while they did it, Sera. That's in the record too. Doesn't make it right. Makes it *human*, which is worse."

Maren snorted from the corner, where she was sorting herbs with the efficiency of a woman who'd done it ten thousand times. "Your grandmother said nearly the same thing, once. Word for word, near enough. It got her exiled."

"It got a lot of people exiled."

"It got a lot of people *burned*. Exile was the mercy." Maren didn't look up from her work. "You want to know why I'm helping you, boy? It's not sentiment. It's debt. Your grandmother pulled me out of a Warden cell in '61 and never asked me for a thing until the day she asked me to keep a door open for her grandson. Doors stay open. Debts get paid. That's the whole of my philosophy, and it's shorter than yours."

Sera watched Dominic absorb that — the way he always absorbed things, quietly, turning them over like river stones. She was beginning to know the shape of his silences. This one was grief and gratitude braided together, and she found she wanted to put her hand over his on the table, and did not, because Maren was watching, and because if she started touching him in warm kitchens she wasn't sure she'd remember why any of this mattered.

The window exploded inward.

* * *

Later, Sera would reconstruct it in pieces, the way she reconstructed every fight: the shutter bursting, the smoke-pot rolling across the floorboards trailing gray-green vapor that stank of sap and rot, and Maren moving faster than a woman of fifty-eight had any right to move — shoving the table over, shouting *"back door, NOW,"* and then doing something with her hands and a pinch of powder that made the air itself go thick as tar around the doorway.

Warden-hounds came through the tar anyway. Three of them. Not beasts exactly — shapes wearing beast-shapes, fur like frost-rimed wire, eyes green as bottle glass. Sera had seen hounds obey Wardens before. These didn't move like trained animals. They moved like *puppets*, and the hand on the strings smelled of springtime gone wrong.

"Dominic!" She grabbed his arm. He'd frozen — not from fear, she realized, but recognition, horror blooming across his face as he looked at the hounds' green eyes.

"They're wearing the guild's work," he breathed. "Someone's twisted binding-craft into—"

"*Later.*"

Maren's powder-flash lit the room white. One hound dropped, smoking. The other two came on. Sera put a knife into the second's throat and felt the blade sink into something that was not flesh and did not bleed, and the thing shrieked in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

The third hound ignored her entirely. It went for Dominic, and it went for the satchel, and Sera understood in one cold flash that these were not orders to kill. These were orders to *retrieve*.

Then Maren stepped between them.

What the old woman did next, Sera had no craft to name. The air filled with the smell of every flower that had ever grown in Vharrow, impossibly, in deep winter, and the hound stopped as if it had struck glass. Maren stood in the middle of her own working with her arms spread and her feet planted, a rain barrel of a woman holding back winter by main force, and she was laughing — actually laughing, breathless and furious.

"Fifty-eight years," she called over her shoulder, "and this is the thanks. *Go*, you idiot boy! Back door, cellar, the tunnel under the dyer's — GO!"

"Maren—"

"If you take one step toward me, Delacroix, I will haunt you personally, and I have *skills*." The light around her was guttering. Sweat ran down her face. "The map goes north. That is the whole mission. Everything else is decoration."

And there it was. The choice, arriving the way choices always arrived — not as a grand crossroads but as three seconds of screaming time in a burning room.

Sera saw it happen in Dominic's face. Saw him look at Maren, at the door, at Maren again, doing the arithmetic no one should ever have to do. She saw him start forward anyway — one step, two, his hand coming up, guild-light kindling along his fingers—

Sera caught him around the waist and *ran*.

He fought her the whole way down the cellar stairs, and she let him struggle and did not let go, and behind them Maren's light flared once, blindingly, and went out.

* * *

The tunnel was old — older than the sewers, older than the walls, guild-cut stone sweating cold. They ran by Sera's count of turns because neither of them spoke, and when they finally stopped in a junction where three passages met, Dominic put his fists against the wall and pressed his forehead to the stone and made a sound Sera had heard before, once, from soldiers who'd left someone behind.

"I could have gotten her out."

"Maybe."

"I *could have.*"

"Maybe." Sera leaned against the opposite wall, giving him the distance he needed and staying close enough to matter. "Or maybe we'd all three be in Warden chains right now, and the map with us, and then everyone burns for nothing. Including her."

"That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's always the arithmetic." His laugh came out cracked. "My grandmother died gathering relics so I could carry them north. Maren kept a door open thirty years so I could walk through it. And my contribution is running away while she covers the retreat. Some legacy."

"Yes," Sera said.

He looked up at that. She'd surprised him, and she was glad, because the honest thing deserved to land honestly.

"Yes," she said again. "It's ugly. It's the ugliest thing I know. I stood in a courtyard once and did the same sum — traded one life for what I was told was the greater good — and I have done that sum every night for eleven years, and I will tell you what I've learned." She pushed off the wall, closed the distance between them. "The sum doesn't get easier. It isn't supposed to. The day it gets easy is the day you've become Corvane. What matters is that you hate it. Hate is the proof you're still the person who should be carrying the map."

Dominic stared at her. In the dark, with the guild-light dying off his fingers, he looked very young and very tired and, she thought, unbearably dear.

"When did you get wise?" he said hoarsely.

"Eleven years of bad sums." She hesitated, then did the thing she'd wanted to do in the kitchen — took his hand, cold fingers, calloused palm, the faint tremor still in it. "Mercy can wait, Dominic. Not forever. But until the realm stops dying. Maren knew that when she stood in front of that hound. Let her sacrifice mean what she meant it to mean."

He held on to her hand like a drowning man holds a rope, and after a moment he nodded, and neither of them said anything else, because some agreements are made below the level of words.

* * *

They walked north through the tunnel for what felt like hours, emerging past midnight through a drainage grate into the pinewastes, where the snow lay blue under a moon like a coin rubbed thin. Sera scouted the treeline out of habit and found nothing — which worried her more than finding something would have.

Corvane didn't lose people. Corvane *spent* them, and counted the cost later, in other people's blood.

They made camp in a hollow beneath a leaning pine, no fire, sharing a single blanket against the cold because warmth was survival and neither of them pretended otherwise anymore. Dominic lay awake a long time. Sera felt it in his breathing, the way she'd learned to read breathing during years of tracking things through dark places.

"She'll escape," he said finally. "Maren. She's escaped Wardens before. She told me. Twice."

"Then she has experience."

"And a philosophy shorter than mine." A ghost of a laugh. Then, quieter: "If she dies because I chose a map—"

"Then you'll spend the rest of your life making it worth choosing. That's the only payment any of the dead ever ask." Sera shifted, settling his head against her shoulder, feeling him resist and then yield. "Sleep. Dawn's in four hours, and the abbey's two days out, and somewhere behind us a man with a spider's patience is counting his pieces."

Dominic slept. Sera did not.

She lay listening to the pines creak and did her own arithmetic, the old sum with new numbers: a brother burned, a huntress deserted, an old woman captured in her place, and somewhere south a king wearing a crown that wore him back. Every entry in the ledger was hers now. She'd chosen this side of it, freely, and the strange thing — the thing she'd have laughed at a season ago — was that lying in the snow with a fugitive charm-seller's heartbeat under her ear, she felt more certain of her oath than she had in eleven years of serving it.

She was still awake when she heard it: far to the south, thin on the wind, a horn. Not a hunting horn. A Warden signal-horn, three notes repeated, the pattern that meant *prey sighted, converge*.

Except the sound wasn't coming from behind them.

It was coming from ahead. From the direction of the abbey.

Sera sat up slowly, hand finding her knife, and watched the northern sky — where, above the black line of the pines, a sickly green glow was rising like a false dawn, and the frost on the branches nearest them began, very softly, to bloom.
