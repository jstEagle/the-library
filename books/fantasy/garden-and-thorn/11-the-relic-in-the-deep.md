## 11. The Relic in the Deep

The abbey had been called Saint Verene's once, Maren's map said, back when saints were allowed to have names and abbeys were allowed to be warm. Now it was a gray hulk of a building crouched on the shoulder of a frozen hill, its bell tower snapped off like a broken tooth, its windows blind with ice. Snow had drifted against the doors so deeply that the doors had given up being doors and become simply part of the wall.

"Well," Sera said, stamping her boots and breathing a cloud that froze almost before it finished leaving her mouth. "It's very atmospheric. I'll give it that."

"It's a guild site," Dominic said. "We were never much for comfort. Grandmother used to say comfort made mages soft, and soft mages made dead villages."

"Your grandmother sounds delightful."

"She is not," Dominic said, "and I resent how often you've started saying things she would say."

Sera smiled — one of her rare ones, the kind that arrived sideways, like something sneaking past a guard — and knocked her fist against the frozen doors. Ice cracked. Somewhere inside, something shifted with a groan of old wood.

They'd been three days out from Maren's safehouse, following the map's second thread north into the hill country where the frost grew strange. Not thicker, exactly. *Deliberate.* Frost here formed in spirals, in patterns too regular for weather, and twice Dominic had seen flowers blooming up through the snow — black-petaled, wrong-smelling things that wilted the moment he looked away. The Verdant's touch, reaching ahead of itself like fingers feeling along a wall in the dark.

His own wound ached under his bandages. The green thread in it had faded since Maren's poultices, but it hadn't left. It sat under his skin like a splinter of summer, warm when everything else was cold, and he had decided — firmly, deliberately, the way one decides not to look down from a height — not to think about what that meant until after the chalice.

*After,* he told himself now, hauling at the doors. *Everything can wait until after.*

The doors surrendered with a shriek. Cold air rolled out to meet them, but it was a different cold than outside — stiller, older, with a smell underneath it of stone dust and dried herbs and, faintly, impossibly, beeswax. Someone had kept candles here. Recently enough that the wax smell lingered.

"That's not right," Sera said quietly, hand going to her sword. "This place has been abandoned for sixty years."

"The wards keep things out," Dominic said. "Including time, if they're strong enough." He stepped over the threshold and felt them at once — a pressure against his skin, like walking into a spiderweb the size of a cathedral, and beneath the pressure a recognition, faint and formal, like an old servant squinting at a face. *A Delacroix. Well. Come in, then.*

He said as much to Sera, who gave him a look.

"You can *feel* that?"

"I could always feel it. I spent twenty years pretending I couldn't." He flexed his fingers, watching the faint gold shimmer that answered them now, unhidden, unashamed. Three weeks ago he'd have hidden it. Two months ago he couldn't have summoned it at all. The road had done what twenty years of fear could not: it had made him practice. "It's like hearing your name in a crowd. You don't have to answer. But you hear it."

Inside, the abbey nave stretched away into darkness, pews rotted to lace, the altar bare except for a ring of candle stubs burned down to puddles. And behind the altar, where a saint's statue should have stood, there was instead a stairway leading down.

Of course there was. There was always a stairway leading down. Dominic was beginning to suspect the guild had built nothing in its entire history without a secret basement.

"They were dramatic people," Sera observed, following his gaze.

"We preferred the term *thorough*."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the abbey stood tall. That was the first impossible thing. Dominic counted ninety-one steps and knew, with the certainty of a man who had grown up in Vharrow's crooked streets, that they should have come out under the hill's roots by step forty. Instead the walls stayed dressed stone, dry, uncracked, and the candle brackets along them held candles that were somehow neither burned nor melted, just *waiting*, wicks trimmed, as if the last guild-mage had stepped out for a moment and meant to return.

The second impossible thing was the warmth. It began around step sixty — a gentle, rising warmth, like walking toward a kitchen — and by the final step Dominic had to pull off his gloves. Sera kept hers on but stopped shivering, which for Sera constituted open wonder.

At the bottom: a round chamber, domed, its floor worked in mosaic — thousands of tiny tiles forming the guild's sigil, the tree-and-thorn, rendered so finely that each leaf was a single tile no bigger than a fingernail. In the center of the sigil, set into the floor like a keystone, lay a circle of pale stone about the width of a dinner plate.

And on a shelf carved into the far wall, waiting under sixty years of patient wardlight, sat a chalice.

It was small. That was Dominic's first thought, and his second, and it took him an embarrassing moment to get past it. He had imagined — well, he didn't know what he'd imagined. Gold, maybe. Enormous. Something that clanged. This was a cup the size of a teacup, dark silver gone nearly black with age, its stem worked into the shape of a sapling, its bowl rimmed with thorns so fine they looked spun rather than forged. It was beautiful the way winter light is beautiful: precise, austere, entirely indifferent to whether you approved of it.

"That's it?" Sera said. "That's the great relic?"

"Don't judge it by size. Grandmother's seed-charm was the size of a pea and it—" He stopped. He'd been about to say *it saved my life in the pass*, which was true, and also a story for a fire, not a crypt. "Guild relics are mostly modest. Modest things are easy to hide."

"And hard to steal," Sera admitted. She'd circled the room twice already, soldier-habit, checking sightlines in a chamber with no exits but the stairs. "I don't see any traps."

"You see the floor?"

"The mosaic."

"The seal." He knelt beside the pale circle of stone, careful not to touch it yet. Up close he could see the wardwork laid over it — lines finer than hair, gold thread sunk into stone, arranged in script so old it predated the letters Vharrow used. His grandmother had taught him this script at her kitchen table, tracing it in spilled flour, saying *this is how we wrote before we had to write small, before we had to write scared.* "Blood-seal. Delacroix work. My family sealed this place; my blood opens it."

"How do you know it's your family? The map only said—"

"Look at the corner of the sigil. Bottom left."

She knelt beside him. In the mosaic's bottom-left corner, half-hidden among the thorn-work, a single tile differed from all the others: darker, older, shaped like a droplet. And within the droplet, in the old flour-script, two words:

*DELACROIX. REMEMBER.*

"She helped build this," Dominic said. His voice came out strange. "Or her mother did. Or — it doesn't matter. We were the lockmakers." He laughed, short and unsteady. "All those years selling charms, hiding, terrified of my own hands. And my family built the locks the whole realm depends on. We weren't hiding from the law, Sera. We were hiding *for* it. Keeping the keys."

Sera was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than he'd ever heard it. "Then open it. Before either of us talks ourselves out of it."

He drew his knife — her knife, actually; she'd re-armed him at Maren's with the resigned air of a woman accepting a permanent loan — and opened his palm along the old scar line, where the skin had already learned the argument. Blood welled. He pressed his hand to the pale stone.

For a heartbeat, nothing.

Then the whole floor *inhaled*.

There was no other word for it. The mosaic brightened tile by tile, gold light running through the tree-and-thorn like sap through branches, and the pale circle drank his blood and softened — not melting, exactly, but becoming willing, the way a locked jaw becomes a kiss — and sank away into darkness, revealing steps. Air rose from below, warm and green-smelling, smelling of growing things, of soil turned in spring, of—

"Thornwood," Sera whispered. "It smells like the Thornwood in high summer."

"Come on," Dominic said, and his heart was hammering, and some traitor part of him was grinning. "Let's go meet the founders."

* * *

The crypt below was not a crypt.

It was a garden.

Dominic stood on the bottom step and stared. The chamber was vast — vaster than the abbey above, vaster than the hill could reasonably contain, and he was long past expecting things to be reasonable — and it was full of growing things. Vines climbed pillars of living stone. Fruit trees heavy with white blossoms arched over paths of moss. A stream ran somewhere he couldn't see, and its sound filled the space the way a heartbeat fills a chest. Overhead, where a ceiling should have been, there was only a soft golden glow, sourceless, like light remembered rather than seen.

In the center of the garden, on a plinth of woven roots, stood a pedestal. Empty.

"The chalice goes there," Dominic said. He knew it the way he knew his own name.

"Yes," said a voice. "But not yet."

The founders came out from between the trees the way memories come: all at once, and slightly wrong. There were seven of them. They were not ghosts, quite — they had weight, shadow, the small imperfections of real bodies — but their edges shimmered, and their clothes were styles four hundred years dead, and when they moved, the garden moved with them, blossoms turning to follow like faces.

The one who had spoken was a broad woman with grey-streaked braids and dirt permanently worked into her knuckles. She looked at Dominic, then at Sera, then at Dominic again, and her expression performed a complicated maneuver between delight and grief.

"A Delacroix," she said. "And armed. And accompanied by a huntress of the crown. Well. The world has certainly been busy since we left."

"I'm Dominic. This is Sera. Are you—"

"Founder Aldith. Keeper of soils." She gestured around. "These are my colleagues. Don't try to remember the names; even we struggled, and we chose them." Her eyes went to his bandaged arm, and her smile faded. "You carry a piece of it. The Verdant's touch."

"It's healing," Dominic said quickly. "Mostly."

"Mm." Aldith did not look convinced, but she let it go, which struck Dominic as remarkably courteous for a four-hundred-year-old ghost. "You've come for the chalice. Of course you have. The map found you, the seal knew you, and the wound on your arm tells me the rest. The binding is failing, and the Verdant wears the king."

"You know."

"We are the binding," Aldith said simply. "Where do you think it's kept? Not in the crown. The crown is just the handle. The binding lives here, in the deep places, in the old gardens, in every seed-charm and hedge-blessing our order ever laid down. And it is *starving*, boy. It has been starving for four hundred years, and lately it has begun to eat things it was never meant to eat." She turned, robes whispering, and walked deeper into the garden. "Come. If you're going to take the chalice, you'll take it knowing what it is. We didn't fight and bleed and bury our friends so our heirs could act in ignorance."

The other founders fell in around them as they walked — silent, watchful, their shimmering edges brushing the blossoms. Sera walked close to Dominic, hand never far from her sword, though Dominic noticed she'd stopped reaching for it. Progress.

"What was the bargain?" he asked. "Maren told me pieces. The Verdant promised eternal spring. The king of that age—"

"King Aldous the Third wanted to be loved forever," Aldith said. "Literally forever. He was old, and frightened, and vain, and the Verdant came to him in a dream and offered him a kingdom that would never know winter, and he said yes before it finished asking." Her voice hardened. "What he never understood — what none of them ever understood — is that the Verdant doesn't *rule*. It *grows*. It cannot make spring. Spring is a thing that happens when living things spend themselves freely and the world spends itself back. All the Verdant can do is *take* — draw the warmth and green and years out of everything near it and hold them close, hoarded, like a child clutching bread in a famine. Its eternal spring is a spring made of everything else's winter."

"So the Long Winter—"

"Is the Verdant's hunger, wearing a crown. It takes the land's spring to make the king's court bloom. Every flower in that palace is a field that died. Every young face in that court is an old one somewhere else." Aldith stopped at the heart of the garden, where the empty pedestal waited amid a ring of white roses. "We tried to tell Aldous. He wouldn't listen. So we did the harder thing. We bound it — not destroyed it, we *couldn't* destroy it, it's older than kings and possibly older than ground — but bound it. Chained its hunger to vessels. The chalice holds its thirst. The blade holds its reach. The seed holds its patience. Three relics, scattered, tended by our order, renewed by our blood, generation after generation."

"And the Verdant itself?" Sera asked. "Where has it been all this time?"

Aldith was quiet for a long moment. Around them, the garden seemed to lean in.

"Here," she said finally. "Look."

She touched the pedestal, and the garden changed.

* * *

The vision took him like deep water takes a swimmer.

He stood in a forest older than names — the Thornwood when it was truly a thornwood, vast and wild and *full*. And at its heart stood the Verdant.

Not a monster. That was the first thing, the thing that broke something open in his chest. Not a monster. A presence — vast, slow, green, thinking thoughts the length of centuries. It had watched the first humans hack farms from its edges and had felt about them the way a mountain feels about rain: curious, tolerant, faintly amused. It had given freely — good harvests, mild winters, springs that came early because the Verdant *liked* the little creatures and their little fires and their little songs.

And then the little creatures had grown kingdoms, and the kingdoms had grown kings, and one king had come into the deep wood with soldiers and iron and a hunger of his own, and he had not asked. He had *taken*. Bound the Verdant's will with chains of rite and blood, squeezed its vast slow mind into a vessel small enough for a crown to hold, and demanded spring on command, forever, as tribute.

The Verdant had screamed. Dominic felt the scream in his teeth, in his bones, in the wound on his arm. Four hundred years of screaming, muffled, bound, starved — a creature made of giving, forced only to take, its every gift twisted into theft, its nature inverted until it barely recognized itself. It wasn't evil. It had never been evil. It was starving the way a caged thing starves, mad the way a caged thing goes mad, and every horror it had visited on the realm was the flailing of something magnificent crushed into a shape too small for it.

*We did this,* Dominic thought, and the thought was the founders', echoing down four hundred years, worn smooth with grief. *We bound it to save the realm, and the binding is the wound, and the wound is the winter.*

The vision released him. He was on his knees in the moss, gasping, and Sera had his shoulders, and Aldith was looking down at him with terrible kindness.

"Now you understand," she said. "The Verdant is not your enemy, Dominic Delacroix. It is your *patient*. The guild were not warriors. We were healers who failed. And the unbinding you mean to perform — if you go into it hating the thing you're unbinding, you will kill the king with it. They are one flesh now. To sever them cleanly you must sever them *gently*."

Dominic pushed himself upright. His arm throbbed, and beneath the throb, faint and unmistakable, something green stirred — and for the first time, he didn't flinch from it. He let himself feel it. Warmth. Hunger. Loneliness so old it had forgotten it was loneliness.

"It's in my arm," he said quietly. "The touch. I think... I think it's how I saw all that. How I understood."

Aldith studied him. "Yes. And that is either a tragedy or a gift, depending entirely on what you do next."

Behind them, Sera had drawn her sword — not at anything, just holding it, grounding herself, an old habit. "If it's a patient," she said slowly, "then someone's been poisoning it for four hundred years. The crown. The Wardens. Corvane." Her jaw tightened. "*Me.* I hunted hedge-mages for six years. People doing what your guild did. Small kindnesses. And I called it justice."

"Sera—"

"No." She sheathed the sword with a snap. "I want the chalice. I want the blade. I want to finish this. Because I'm done being the poison."

The garden rustled — approval or wind, impossible to say. Aldith gestured, and the pedestal's rootwork opened like a flowering hand.

"Take it, then. But know the price the founders paid, and pay attention to the last lesson, because it's the one everyone forgets." Her shimmering form flickered, and for a moment Dominic saw through her — saw the woman she'd been, tired and stubborn and afraid, kneeling in this same garden with a cup in her hands. "The relics don't give power. They give *burden*, beautifully wrapped. Carry it well."

The chalice rose from the rootwork and settled into Dominic's palms, cool and light as a bird. The thorns on its rim pricked his bandaged arm — gently, almost apologetically — and the green thing beneath his skin sighed, and settled, and was quiet.

Above them, far above, through sixty feet of stone and hill and frozen earth, a bell began to ring.

Neither of them had passed a bell tower with anything left to ring.

Sera was on the stairs before the third toll. Dominic followed, chalice clutched to his chest, and behind him the garden's golden light dimmed — not dying, he realized. *Dimming.* Conserving. Bracing.

The bell rang on, deep and wrong, and under its tolling, faint but unmistakable, came a second sound: voices. Many voices. Marching.

Corvane had found them.
