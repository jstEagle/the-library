## 12. What Sera Buried

The abbey's crypt had given up its secrets, and now the two of them sat in the ruined chapel above it, where half the roof had fallen in and the other half seemed to be considering following. Sera had dragged a broken pew close enough to the fire that the heat stung her shins, and she sat there with her boots off — a small surrender she hadn't made since Vharrow — letting the flames work warmth back into her toes.

Dominic was across the fire, turning the chalice over in his hands for perhaps the hundredth time. It was plain silver, dented, ugly as an old cooking pot, and when he held it just so, the firelight caught something moving inside it — a shimmer like water seen through ice.

"You keep looking at it like it's going to apologize," Sera said.

"It might." He set it down on his bedroll with exaggerated care, as though it were a sleeping cat. "Relics have moods. My grandmother used to say the medallion sulked if you polished it wrong."

"Your grandmother sounds delightful."

"She was terrifying," Dominic said fondly. "Delightful is a strong word."

Sera almost smiled. Almost. The smile got as far as her mouth and then stalled out, because the fire had shifted, and in its new light she could see the frost-wraith scar on his forearm — the one he'd earned saving her life on the snow road — and beneath it, fainter, the green thread of the Verdant's touch from the pass. Two wounds. Both of them hers, in one way or another. One she'd failed to prevent; one she'd failed to prevent by being exactly what she'd always been: a huntress of the crown, standing next to him when Corvane's hounds came.

He caught her looking. "It doesn't hurt."

"Liar."

"Only at night," he admitted. "And only a little. Which is more than I can say for your cooking."

"My cooking kept us alive."

"Barely. That stew last night was less food than threat."

Sera threw a pinecone at him. He caught it, grinning, and for a moment the chapel was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire, and she felt the thing she'd been carrying all day shift against her ribs like a stone in a boot.

She'd been carrying it for eleven years, actually. But today it was heavier, because tomorrow they would ride for the Thornwood's heart, and men who rode toward things like that sometimes didn't come back, and she found she could not bear the thought of him walking into the Verdant's garden believing she was what she'd pretended to be.

"Sera?" He'd gone quiet, watching her. He was good at watching. Charm-sellers survived by reading faces, he'd told her once, and hers had apparently been telling stories all evening.

"I reported someone once," she said.

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark above them, snow slid off a broken rafter and landed with a soft whump.

"A smuggler?" Dominic asked gently. "A deserter?"

"A hedge-mage."

The word dropped between them like a coin into a well. She watched him understand it — watched his face go still, the grin fading not into judgment but into attention, which was somehow worse.

"His name was Tam," she said, because if she was going to do this, she was going to do it properly, all the way down, no ledges to stop on. "My brother. Older than me by two years. He could make things grow. That was all — nothing dramatic. He'd cup a seed in his hand and it would sprout. Beans in winter. Flowers out of season. Our mother sold them at market and we ate through three bad harvests on Tam's little miracles, and he never hurt anyone in his life, and he—" Her voice did something she hated, wobbled, and she pressed on before it could get away from her. "He used to make my hair ribbons bloom. Little blue flowers, all along the ribbon. I'd wear them to school and pretend I didn't know where they came from."

Dominic said nothing. He had pulled his knees up, arms around them, the way people sit when they intend to stay a long time.

"When I turned sixteen I took the huntress oath," Sera said. "I believed it. That's the part nobody understands — I *believed* it. Magic broke the world. The Sundering, the wars, the burning of the old capital. We were taught it like scripture: every mage is a spark looking for a fire. And Tam's magic wasn't getting bigger, but it wasn't hiding either, and I thought — I thought if I reported him first, they'd be gentle. I thought the law would be kinder to him if it came from family. A recommendation instead of a denunciation. I actually wrote it that way. *I recommend examination of my brother, Tam Vane, age nineteen, hedge-craft, harmless.*"

"Harmless," Dominic repeated softly.

"In my report. Yes." She stared into the fire. "They came at dawn. Tam didn't run. He saw the Wardens coming up the lane and he put his hands out and let them take him, because he knew if he ran, they'd burn our mother's farm too. And I stood at the window and watched them bind his wrists, and he looked up at my window — he knew, Sera, he *knew* it was me, because who else knew? — and he smiled at me. To tell me it was all right. My brother forgave me while they were tying his hands, and then they burned him in the square at Millbrook, and I stood in the crowd in my brand-new huntress uniform, and I swore afterward that I would serve the law so faithfully that his death would mean something. That it would have been *worth* it."

The silence after that was enormous. The fire worked at its logs. Outside, wind moved through the frozen pines with a sound like slow breathing.

"He wore blue flowers to the pyre," she said. "In his hair. They grew them right there, in the Warden's grip, and the Warden slapped them off, and they kept blooming in the snow where they fell. Until the fire took them too."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Please. Let me finish or I'll never start again. Eleven years, Dominic. Eleven years I told myself the law was the law, and grief makes liars of everyone, and Tam would have understood. And then I stood in the palace council chamber and watched something green move behind my king's eyes, and every courtier bowed to it, and I realized—" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I realized I'd spent my whole life serving a crown that eats people like my brother. That the Wardens I fed him to weren't protecting the realm from sparks. They were keeping the kindling dry. For *that.* For the thing in the throne room. Everything I burned my conscience down to ash for, and it was hungry the whole time."

She ran out of words then, which surprised her; she'd rehearsed this confession in her head for years, in a hundred sleepless variations, and none of the rehearsals had ever gotten past the pyre. She'd never imagined saying the rest aloud, because the rest had no shape until now.

"So there it is," she said. "You're traveling with the woman who burned her own brother. Not the Wardens. Me. I lit that fire as surely as they did. If you want to walk out of this chapel and take your chances alone in the Thornwood, I'll give you the map back and point you north, and I won't follow."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that she began composing the goodbye in her head, cold and competent, the way she composed everything.

Then he reached past the fire, picked up the chalice, and held it out to her.

"What are you doing?"

"Hold it," he said. "Just hold it. Cup it in both hands."

"This isn't the time for charm-seller theatrics—"

"Sera. Please."

Something in his voice made her obey. She took the ugly old cup in her hands, expecting nothing, and warmth bloomed up through the silver — not fire-warmth, something older and gentler, like sun on a wall in early spring — and inside the metal she felt a faint pulse, patient and vast, like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of seasons.

"The founders' visions," Dominic said quietly. "When my blood opened the seal. I saw them, Sera. The guild, before the Sundering. Do you know what most of them were? Hedge-mages. Farmers' sons and millers' daughters. People whose magic made beans grow and ribbons bloom. The guild wasn't built by archmages. It was built by Tams. Thousands of them, over centuries, each one adding a little, until together they could bind a starving god and save a kingdom." He leaned forward. "The guild forgave worse than you. The guild *was* worse than you — it did terrible things for good reasons, and it carried them, and it kept going. You know what my grandmother says about guilt?"

"That it's earned?"

"That it's only useful if it builds something. Otherwise it's just a way of punishing yourself forever instead of doing the harder work." He nodded at the chalice. "Tam's magic is in there somewhere. So is mine, someday, maybe. So is every hedge-mage the Wardens ever burned. They're all still in the work, Sera. You can be too. That's the only forgiveness that matters — the kind that lets you keep building."

Her eyes were burning. She would deny it later, to herself, with great conviction. "That's a terrible speech."

"I'm a charm-seller. We're better at trinkets than speeches."

"And the trinket is crying on me."

"They do that." He came around the fire then, slowly, giving her time to stop him, and sat down beside her on the broken pew. Close. Closer than procedure. "For what it's worth — I don't think Tam smiled at you because he forgave you. I think he smiled because he loved you, and loving someone means smiling at them even when they've handed you to the fire. Especially then. That's not absolution. It's just love, being stubborn."

"Stubborn love," she managed. "Is that a guild teaching too?"

"Chapter one." His shoulder was against hers now. Warm. Solid. Terribly distracting for a woman trying to have a spiritual crisis. "Right after *don't polish the medallion wrong* and *never trust a courier who looks healthy.*"

She laughed — really laughed, wet and cracked, but real — and it broke something loose in her chest that had been locked for eleven years, and in the space where it had been, something else settled. Lighter. Terrifying.

"Dominic." She turned to look at him, and he turned to look at her, and the firelight did what firelight does. "Why did you come back for me? At the pass. You could have run. Corvane's hounds were on you and I was the one who'd kept you prisoner for three days. Any sensible man runs."

"Any sensible man sells charms in Vharrow his whole life and dies poor and safe," he said. "I stopped being sensible when a dying man bled on my floor. Besides—" and here he did the thing with his mouth that was absolutely not fair, the crooked half-smile, "—you're the only person alive who's seen my magic and arrested me anyway. There's a symmetry there. I couldn't waste it."

"That's the worst reason anyone has ever—"

"And also," he said, quieter, "because I think you're the bravest person I've ever met. Braver than any huntress. Braver than my grandmother. Because you just did the thing that actually costs something. You told the truth about yourself to someone who had every reason to leave." His hand found hers on the pew between them. Callused fingers, ink-stained, trembling slightly — from the Verdant's touch, she told herself, though she suspected otherwise. "I'm not leaving, Sera. Whatever you buried, it stays buried with company now."

She should say something wise. Something worthy of the moment. Instead she heard herself say, "If you kiss me, I'm still technically deputizing you as my prisoner. It's in the oath. Probably."

"Very probably," Dominic agreed, and kissed her.

It was not a dramatic kiss. There was no sweeping music, no thunder of destiny. It was careful and a little clumsy and tasted faintly of terrible stew, and it was the single best thing that had happened to Sera Vane in eleven years, and when they finally drew apart, the fire had burned low and neither of them moved to feed it.

Above them, through the broken roof, the stars wheeled cold and clear. And beyond the northern ridge, past the sleeping pines, the Thornwood waited — black and vast and, tonight, for the first time in weeks, utterly silent.

Too silent.

Sera noticed it the same moment he did. They both went still, huntress and charm-seller, listening.

At the forest's edge, far off but unmistakable in the dead air, a light had appeared. Then another. Then a dozen — a slow procession of pale green flames moving along the tree line, unhurried, deliberate, like lanterns carried in a funeral march.

Dominic's hand closed around the medallion at his throat. "Grandmother said the Thornwood would show us the way when we were ready."

"Yes," Sera said, already reaching for her boots. "But she didn't say anything about it sending an escort."

The green lights paused. All together. All at once.

As if they had heard her.

* * *

They broke camp in under a minute — Sera kicking dirt over the fire while Dominic bundled the relics into his pack with hands that shook only a little. The lights hadn't moved. They hung at the tree line like a held breath, and the longer Sera looked at them, the more certain she became that they were not leading anywhere.

They were waiting.

"They know we have the chalice," Dominic murmured, slinging his pack. "The Verdant knows. It's known since the crypt."

"Then why hasn't it taken them?"

"Because it can't reach in here." He touched the abbey's fallen stones. "Guild wards. Old ones. This whole ruin is a knot the Verdant can't untie — that's why the founders built it here. But the wards don't extend past the walls, and we have to ride north eventually."

Sera checked her blades out of habit, though steel had never yet done much against the things the winter sent. "So we wait for dawn and run for it."

"Or," Dominic said slowly, and she heard the idea forming in his voice the way she'd learned to hear it — half excitement, half disaster, "we don't run at all."

"Dominic."

"The lights aren't attacking. They're *inviting*. My grandmother told me — the Thornwood remembers the guild. Every road in it, every ward-stone, every boundary. If those lights are what I think they are, they're not the Verdant's. They're the *guild's*. Old path-markers, waking up." He looked at her, firelight and starlight fighting in his eyes. "The founders left a road to the heart of the wood, Sera. A road only a Delacroix can walk. And I think it just opened."

Outside the walls, the green lights began to move — drifting deeper into the trees, pausing, waiting, drifting again. Patient. Insistent.

Like a door held open by someone who knows you're coming.

Sera pulled her cloak tight and looked at the darkness swallowing the lights one by one, and at the man beside her who was already reaching for the map, and she thought of Tam's flowers blooming in the snow, stubborn and doomed and alive.

"All right," she said. "But if this turns out to be a trap, I'm arresting you again."

"Wouldn't have it any other way."

Hand in hand, they walked out through the broken gate, past the last of the guild's old wards, and into the waiting dark — where the green lights closed gently around them like a garden closing around a seed, and the Thornwood, ancient and starving and remembering, began at last to lead them home.
