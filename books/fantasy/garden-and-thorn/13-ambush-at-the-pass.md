## 13. Ambush at the Pass

The morning they climbed into Thorn Pass, the world had gone the color of old pewter — sky, stone, and the frozen river far below, all of it one dull metal waiting to be struck. Dominic had learned to read weather like a market crowd: you could tell when something bad was coming by how quiet everything pretended to be.

This morning was very quiet indeed.

"Say it," Sera said. She walked ahead of him on the switchback trail, boots finding purchase on ice as if the mountain had personally agreed to hold her up. She'd been saying little else for two days. "You've been doing that thing with your face since dawn."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like a man who's about to tell me the stew's gone off but you're too polite."

Dominic adjusted the strap of his pack and considered lying. He was, after all, a professional liar of sorts — he'd spent fifteen years selling charms that did nothing to people who needed them to do something. But Sera Vane had a way of looking at him that made lying feel like a waste of good breath.

"The birds," he said. "There aren't any."

She stopped. Looked up. The pass walls rose sheer on either side, gray granite streaked with snow, and not so much as a crow wheeled in the cold air.

"Hunters' country," she said, but her hand had already drifted to her sword hilt, and he saw her weight shift forward onto the balls of her feet, the way it did when her body believed something before her mind agreed to.

"Warden-hounds don't scare birds," Dominic said. "They *are* birds, sometimes. Or crows. Or whatever's convenient." He touched the medallion through his shirt without thinking about it — an old habit, his grandmother's voice in his memory: *fear is just information, boy; read it, don't wear it.* "But frost-wraiths? Frost-wraiths are worse than silence. They're the reason for it."

Sera turned back up the trail. "Then we move fast and stop talking."

"That's my favorite kind of plan," Dominic muttered, and followed her into the throat of the pass.

* * *

They made two miles before the mountain betrayed them.

It happened the way these things always happen — all at once, in the middle of an ordinary thought. Dominic had been thinking about Maren's safehouse, and whether the second relic was truly beneath the abbey, and whether he could convince his knees to carry him another day, when the snow on the eastern ledge simply stood up.

Not slid. Not fell. *Stood.*

Three shapes unfolded out of the drifts, tall and thin as winter itself, their bodies ragged sheets of ice and shadow, faces nothing but hollows where cold pooled like breath in a cellar. Frost-wraiths. He'd seen one once, from a very great distance, when he was nine — his grandmother had pulled him behind a wall and held her hand over his mouth until it passed, and he remembered mostly her heartbeat, fast as a rabbit's, which had frightened him more than the wraith because grandmothers were supposed to be unafraid of everything.

These three were not distant.

"Sera—"

"I see them." Her sword was out. When had her sword come out? There was a sound he'd missed, a whisper of steel, and now the blade caught what little light there was and threw it back hard. "Down the trail. Go."

"They'll run us down in the open—"

"*Go*, Dominic."

He didn't go. This was, he would reflect later, either the bravest or stupidest decision of his life, and he genuinely could not have told you which. Instead he scrambled up onto a boulder beside the trail, because height mattered with wraiths — his grandmother's lessons again, rising up through fifteen years of disuse like fish surfacing in a frozen pond — and because if Sera was going to fight three of them, then the least he could do was be a target worth aiming at.

The first wraith came down the slope like water running backward. Sera met it.

He had watched her fight before — the skirmish on the snow road, the hounds outside the hollow village — but those had been fights. This was something else. She moved through the first wraith's reach the way other people moved through a doorway they'd walked a thousand times, no wasted motion, no hesitation, her blade taking its arm off at the joint and her shoulder following the cut through into a turn that put her behind the second before it finished forming. There was no fear in it. That was the part that frightened him. She fought like a woman crossing names off a list, and he understood, suddenly and horribly, that some of the names on that list were old ones — debts from a life she never talked about, a brother-shaped absence she carried the way he carried the medallion.

The first wraith reassembled its arm. Of course it did. That was the whole miserable trick of wraiths: you couldn't kill cold, you could only make it inconvenient.

"Sera! They knit! You can't cut them apart!"

"Then what do I *do*?"

"You—" And here was the problem, laid bare in the space of half a heartbeat. Everything he knew about fighting wraiths came from a guild that no longer existed, taught by a woman who might be dead, using magic he hadn't touched in fifteen years. "You burn them! Or you bind them! Fire or binding, nothing else holds!"

"We have neither!"

"We have *me*."

The words came out of him before he'd decided to say them, and having said them, he discovered they were true in a way that made his hands shake. Because the guild's power had been stirring in him since the north road — small things, hedge-tricks, warmth in his palms, a whisper of sight — and every mile toward the Thornwood it had grown louder, like a door left ajar in a house full of wind. He had been holding that door shut with both hands for days.

The third wraith solved the problem for him. It didn't come down the slope at all. It rose out of the ground at Sera's feet, made of the mountain's own frozen blood, and drove a claw of black ice toward her spine.

Later, Dominic could never reconstruct what he actually did. He knew the theory — his grandmother had drilled the binding-verse into him at eight years old, making him recite it over breakfast until he could say it backwards, *because you never know when breakfast will be interrupted, boy* — but theory and act were different countries, and he crossed between them blind.

He reached into himself, found the door, and tore it open.

Power came out of him like a shout. Not the polite candle-flame warmth of hedge-charms — this was the guild's inheritance, old and vast and utterly indifferent to his opinions, and it went through him the way a river goes through a straw. He heard himself speaking words he didn't know he knew, the binding-verse in the old tongue, and light answered — green-gold, spring-colored, absurd in that iron place — and it lanced down from the boulder and took the third wraith through its hollow chest.

The wraith screamed. It was a terrible sound, the sound of a lake cracking, and it kept screaming as the light *wove* it — threads of gold-green wrapping it, tightening, pulling it together instead of apart, binding its cold into a shape it couldn't escape. Then the shape collapsed inward, smaller and smaller, until all that remained was a fist-sized knot of ice, glowing faintly, sitting in the snow like a paperweight.

Silence. Real silence, the ringing kind.

On the trail below, Sera stood with her sword raised against the remaining two wraiths, staring up at him with an expression he had never seen on her before — not gratitude, not awe, but a kind of fierce recalibration, as if she were redrawing a map she'd trusted her whole life.

"Dominic," she said carefully, "what did you *do*?"

"I have absolutely no idea," he said honestly, "but I think I need to sit down."

* * *

The last two wraiths nearly killed them anyway.

Binding one had cost him more than he'd expected — his vision kept graying at the edges, and his hands wouldn't stop trembling, and there was a strange taste in his mouth like green tea and copper. So when the two survivors split and came at them from opposite sides, it was Sera who saved them both, fighting with a controlled fury that bordered on joyless, driving the first wraith back step by step until she'd herded it against the cliff wall — and then, in a move that made Dominic's stomach drop, she sheathed her sword, pulled the iron fire-striker from her pack, and struck sparks directly into the wraith's face while shouting at it in language that would have gotten her fined in any market in Vharrow.

Wraiths, it turned out, did not enjoy being insulted in fluent Warden's cant. It also turned out that enough sparks, struck fast enough, into a creature made of dry cold, will catch. The wraith burned with a sound like a sigh, and the last one, seeing its companion die, simply poured itself back into the scree and was gone, retreating eastward toward Vharrow.

Toward Corvane.

"He'll know we're close now," Sera said, breathing hard. She cleaned her blade with a strip of cloth, methodical, her hands steady again as though the fury had been borrowed and returned. "That wasn't a hunting party. That was a probe. He wanted to see what we could do."

"And now he knows." Dominic slid down off the boulder, and his legs informed him, politely but firmly, that sitting was non-negotiable. "I bound one and you set another on fire. If I were Corvane, I'd stop sending probes."

"If you were Corvane," Sera said, "you'd send everything."

As if the mountain had been waiting for its cue, the wind changed.

It came down the pass from the north, and it was wrong — not cold, exactly, though it was that too, but *hungry*. Dominic felt it against his skin and recognized it the way you recognize a smell from childhood: the Verdant's touch, the same wrongness that hung over the hollow village, the gardens blooming in snow. Except here it was inverted, starved, the entity's attention stretched thin across a hundred miles and arriving at them like a hand reaching through a keyhole.

And underneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hounds.

Not dog-hounds. Warden-hounds — the crown's bred horrors, all tendon and appetite, things that tracked magic the way sharks tracked blood. Dominic had sold a hundred customers charms against ordinary wolves. Nobody sold charms against these.

"How many?" Sera was beside him now, pulling him up, and her arm under his was the steadiest thing in the world.

He closed his eyes and let the guild-sense flicker open, just a crack. What he saw made him wish he hadn't. "A dozen. Maybe more. And something bigger behind them." He opened his eyes. "Sera, we can't outrun them, and I can't—" He flexed his fingers. They still trembled. "I don't know what I did back there. I don't know if I can do it again. I don't know if I can do it twice."

She looked at him for a long moment. Wind pulled at her hair, and somewhere above them a single bird finally dared to cross the sky, which meant even the crows thought the fight was over, which meant the crows were idiots.

"Then we don't run and we don't cast," she said. "We hold the narrows." She pointed up the trail, where the pass pinched between two shoulders of rock — a gap barely wide enough for three abreast, with a fallen slab angling across it like a lid. "Hounds are fast in the open and stupid in the tight. We make them come to us one at a time, and I make them regret it." A ghost of a smile, quick and sharp. "Besides. I owe them."

"You owe *hounds*?"

"I owe what sent them." The smile vanished. "Every one of these things wears the crown's mark. Every one of them is mine by rights. I served the order that made them, Dominic. Today I settle accounts."

He wanted to argue. He wanted to tell her that settling accounts was a young person's game and they were both too tired and too close to the end of this road to spend themselves on arithmetic. But he looked at her face and understood that this wasn't arithmetic to her. It was absolution. And he had learned, over these weeks on the road, that you did not stand between Sera Vane and her penance. You stood beside her.

"All right," he said. "The narrows. But Sera — if it goes wrong, you take the map and run. Promise me."

"No."

"Sera—"

"I'll promise you something better. It won't go wrong." She started up the trail, then paused, glancing back, and for one unguarded moment the huntress fell away and it was just Sera, tired and human and warm-eyed. "Because you're going to figure out what that light was. And next time, you're going to aim it properly instead of at your own feet." She tossed him something — a strip of dried venison. "Eat. Magic runs on food. Everyone knows that."

"It absolutely does not—"

"Eat, Dominic."

He ate.

* * *

The hounds came at dusk, and the narrows held, and it was the worst hour of Dominic's life.

Twelve hounds became eleven, then seven, then four, in a grinding close-quarters slaughter that he would spend years trying not to remember — Sera in the gap like a hinge of the mountain itself, blade flashing, breathing in counts, giving ground only to take it back twice over. Dominic did what he could from the slab above: he couldn't bind again, not yet, the power in him sullen and slow to answer, but he could heat stones in his palms until they glowed and drop them into the press of bodies, and he could shout the binding-verse half-formed just to make the hounds flinch, because they remembered what he'd done to their kin.

The big thing behind them never showed itself. It didn't need to. It waited at the mouth of the pass like patience given teeth, and when the fourth hound fell, it simply turned and withdrew, and the pressure of its attention lifted from Dominic's shoulders like a hand removed.

They won. That was the word for it, technically. They won.

Dominic knew it was over because Sera stopped moving, and then because she said his name twice, the second time differently, and then because he looked down and saw what she was looking at.

One of the hounds had gotten past her. Just one, in the chaos, for just a moment — long enough for a claw to open his thigh through wool and leather, a shallow line, hardly worth naming. Hardly bleeding at all, in fact.

That was the problem. It wasn't bleeding.

Around the edges of the cut, the skin had gone a pale, translucent green, and fine dark lines spread outward from the wound like roots seeking water — delicate, almost beautiful, the way frost on a window is beautiful. Even as he watched, another rootlet crept a quarter-inch toward his knee.

"Oh," Dominic said, with the calm of a man standing in a market watching a price rise beyond what he can pay. "Oh, that's not good."

Sera knelt, tore the trouser leg open, and went very still. He felt her go still — felt it in the grip she took on his ankle, careful and controlled, the grip of someone refusing to shake him.

"Can you heal it?" she asked. "Like the child. In the village."

He reached for the guild-power, that door he'd torn open on the boulder, and found it — but changed. Where before it had roared like a river, now it moved sluggishly, thickly, and when he drew a thread of it down toward the wound, the green lines *drank* it. He actually felt them pull, felt his own magic siphoned away into the spreading roots like water into thirsty soil.

He let go of the power and sat back, heart hammering.

"It's feeding on it," he said. "The Verdant's touch — it's in the wound, and it's drinking my magic. The more I use, the faster it grows." He laughed, and it came out thinner than he intended. "My grandmother used to say the Verdant was patient. That it never took anything by force. It just waited for you to give it a reason."

Sera tied off the bandage — snug, professional, her hands not quite as steady as before. Above them the first stars were coming out, hard and white, and the temperature was falling the way it did now everywhere, as if the whole world were slowly exhaling its warmth into some vast green lung.

"How long?" she asked. She didn't dress the question up. She never did. It was one of the ten thousand things he loved about her, and he noticed, distantly, that he had apparently decided to start counting.

"I don't know. Days? The abbey's two days' walk. The chalice might—" He stopped. Looked at his leg. Looked at her. "The chalice might cleanse it. Guild relics were made for exactly this. Binding what shouldn't spread."

"Then we walk tonight."

"Sera, I'm fine to—"

"You're not, and we're walking tonight anyway." She shouldered her pack, then offered him her hand, and he took it, and she pulled him upright with that effortless strength that never stopped surprising him. For a moment they stood close in the dark, her forehead nearly touching his, her breath fogging in the cold between them.

"When we get the chalice," she said quietly, "and the blade, and we've done what we set out to do — you and I are going to have a conversation about what you called me back on the snow road. When you were feverish. You probably don't remember."

His stomach performed a maneuver unrelated to the wound. "I remember everything."

Something moved through her expression — quick, unguarded, gone. "Good," she said. "Then walk faster."

She turned up the trail toward the abbey and the dark, and Dominic followed, limping, the green roots creeping silently beneath his bandage, counting his magic like a miser counts coins — and somewhere far to the south, in a palace going slowly to vine, something ancient and starving felt him coming, and began, patiently, to smile.
