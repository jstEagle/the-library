## 14. The Third Relic's Keeper

The mountain did not want them there.

Dominic felt it in the way the path kept narrowing, in the way the pines leaned close like gossips deciding whether to report them to someone. Sera walked ahead with her hand on her knife, which was her way of being friendly.

"You said the map showed a hermitage," she said over her shoulder.

"The map showed a sigil. A hermitage is my guess."

"Your guess is that we've climbed for two days toward a guess."

"My grandmother always said I had a gift for finding things," Dominic said. "She never specified what."

The wound in his side throbbed where the Verdant's touch had sunk into it — a cold green ache beneath the bandages, like a seed of winter planted under his ribs. He'd stopped mentioning it. Sera had stopped asking. Both of them understood that mentioning it made it more real, and neither of them could afford one more real thing.

Then the trees opened, and there it was: a stone cottage wedged against the mountainside, smoke rising from a crooked chimney, a garden growing in defiance of everything Dominic knew about altitude and season. Beans. Leeks. A row of roses, blooming red as a warning.

Nobody grew roses at this height in this winter. Nobody grew *anything*.

"She's alive," he whispered.

"You're sure it's her?"

"No." He swallowed. "But whoever lives here knows guild-craft. That garden is a hearth-warding turned inside out and multiplied by madness. Only three people alive could do it, and I buried two of them."

Sera looked at him sideways. "You buried your grandmother?"

"I buried what they sent home." He started down toward the cottage before his courage could reconsider. "It wasn't much to bury."

* * *

She opened the door before he knocked.

She was smaller than he remembered. That was the first betrayal. In his memory, Odile Delacroix had been a tower of a woman, all sharp elbows and sharper opinions, a person who filled rooms the way weather fills valleys. The woman in the doorway was thin and gray-braided, wrapped in a patched wool coat, with a face like a map of everywhere she'd refused to go back to.

Her eyes went from his face to the medallion at his throat, and something moved behind them — grief, maybe, or its fossil record.

"You have his chin," she said. "Your grandfather's. It got you nothing but trouble then either, I expect."

"Grandmother."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't stand on my step saying *grandmother* like we're about to share a pie. You're a Delacroix walking around Vharrow wearing the medallion out in the open, selling charms, getting hunted by Wardens, and now—" her eyes flicked to Sera, catalogued the royal order badge still hanging from her belt despite everything, and narrowed— "now you've brought a huntress to my door. Have you any idea how many people followed you here?"

"None," Dominic said. "Sera killed the ones who tried."

"Killed them or lost them?" His grandmother stepped aside anyway, jerking her head toward the interior. "In. Both of you. If Corvane's hounds are coming, I'd rather they find me mid-argument than mid-tea."

The cottage was one room and a lifetime. Herbs hung from every beam. Books were stacked in towers that leaned at angles suggesting long negotiations with gravity. And above the hearth, mounted on iron hooks, hung a sword.

Dominic stopped breathing.

It was not a grand sword. That was the second surprise. Guild stories had always described the blade as a thing of light, a relic forged in the Sundering itself. This was a plain longsword with a worn leather grip and a guard shaped like two curling leaves, its steel dark as pond water. But the air around it *bent*, very slightly, the way air bends above a summer road. Even across the room, Dominic could feel it watching him.

"The third relic," he said.

"Yes."

"You've had it all this time."

"Yes."

"People died looking for it. Maren spent twenty years—"

"Maren spent twenty years looking in the wrong places because I made sure the wrong places were well advertised." She busied herself at the kettle, not looking at him. "Sit down, both of you. You look like something a wraith chewed and spat out."

They sat. The tea, when it came, was bitter and perfect. Sera drank hers like a soldier — fast, alert, eyes moving — while Dominic held his and let the warmth work into his hands, and told her everything. The courier. The map. Maren. The chalice beneath the abbey, and the visions the founders had left in it: the Verdant starving, betrayed by the bargain, bound to the crown as much a prisoner as a jailer. The green wound in his own side. The king begging through the Verdant's grip.

His grandmother listened without interrupting, which from her was more alarming than shouting.

When he finished, she set down her cup with a small, precise click.

"So," she said. "You mean to unbind it."

"That's the whole point of—"

"You can't."

The word landed like a dropped anvil. Dominic stared at her. "Grandmother—"

"*Odile.* If I'm going to break your heart, boy, you'll do me the courtesy of using my name." She rose and stood before the hearth, her back to him, firelight carving her shadow tall against the stones. "I was there when the rite was written. I helped write it. Your blood opens the binding, yes. Your magic pours into the severance, yes. And when the Verdant lets go of the king — if it lets go — it will take everything it was given with it. Every drop of guild-gift in your veins. Gone. Forever. That's the price the founders set, and they set it high because they were afraid, and afraid men write cruel bargains."

"I know," Dominic said quietly. "The chalice showed me."

"Did it show you the other part?" She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious. "The binding has held for sixty years because the Verdant holds *on*. It's starving and it's frightened and the crown is the only warm thing it's ever been allowed to touch. You think you can walk into that throne room and ask it nicely to let go? When it feels you pulling, it will pull *back*. And the thing between it and the king—" her voice cracked, just once, "—the king will be torn apart. Or you will. Or both. There is a cleaner way, Dominic. The blade offers it. One stroke. Kill the king, kill the host, and the Verdant starves free of the world in a season. No sacrifice of yours. No tearing. Just grief, and then spring."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, wind worried the eaves.

"You've been sitting up here for fifteen years," Dominic said slowly, "with the answer to everything hanging over your fireplace. And you never went down the mountain."

"I went down the mountain twice." Her jaw tightened. "The first time, I got within a mile of the palace and my legs stopped working. Not fear — *craft*. The Verdant knew the blade was near and it shut my body down like a hand closing a book. The second time I brought help. Six of us. The best the guild had left." She looked at the fire. "Five graves. I dug them myself, in this frozen dirt, and I decided the world could keep its king."

"So you let the winter grow. You let villages hollow out. You let—" he gestured vaguely at his own bandaged side, "—this happen, because digging five graves was enough dying for one lifetime."

"Do not." She crossed the room in three steps and took his collar in her knotted fist, and even small and old, she was suddenly entirely the tower from his memory. "Do not stand in my house with your mother's eyes and judge me for surviving. You don't know what I—"

"I know Grandmother Odile taught me that a charm-seller who won't sell charms to the poor isn't a charm-seller, he's a shopkeeper." His voice didn't rise; it never did with her; it had learned better. "I know she taught me that magic hoarded is magic rotted. I know she cried at the Sundering remembrance every single year and told me it was the wind." Gently, he peeled her fingers from his collar and held her hand between both of his. "I'm not judging you. I'm telling you what you taught me. You built the person standing in front of you, and now you have to decide whether you believe in your own work."

For a long moment she just breathed. Then she laughed — a rusty, terrible, wonderful sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

"Arrogant pup," she said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve. "You get that from me too."

* * *

She fed them stew that tasted like someone had distilled stubbornness into broth, and while they ate she interrogated Sera with the cheerful ruthlessness of a grandmother meeting a granddaughter-in-law forty years ahead of schedule.

"Huntress. Royal order. How many mages did you bring in before you found your conscience?"

"One." Sera didn't flinch. "My brother."

The room went very quiet. Dominic reached under the table and found Sera's hand.

"And you stayed in the order afterward," Odile said, softer.

"Penance."

"Hm. Penance is a heavy coin. Most people spend it on themselves." She refilled Sera's bowl without asking. "You'll do."

"Grandmother," Dominic said, "we haven't actually—"

"I'm eighty-one, not blind. Eat your stew."

Later, when Sera had taken the watch outside — "your family argues like a landslide, I need air" — Odile led Dominic to the hearth and lifted the sword down from its hooks. In her arms it seemed heavier than iron had any right to be.

"This is Verdanhal," she said. "Forged before the Sundering, back when the guild and the Thornwood were friends instead of wardens and prisoner. It doesn't cut flesh, mostly. It cuts *truth*. Hold it, and it shows you what you are and what you choose — and it shows everyone nearby too, so mind your secrets." She almost smiled. "Your grandfather fainted during his judgment. Woke up and proposed to me out of sheer embarrassment."

"What does it show?"

"Two futures. Always two. The choice you're carrying and the choice you're avoiding." She held it out. "I won't give you the blade, Dominic. I'll give you the chance to take it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."

He took the grip.

The world went green.

* * *

He stood in the Thornwood — but a Thornwood in full leaf, sun-dappled, humming with bees, so beautiful it hurt. And through the trees came the king, young again, crowned in living branches, laughing at something. The court bloomed around him. Every face wore vines like jewelry. Every smile was serene and slightly too wide, and none of the eyes were entirely their own.

*This is mercy,* the wood whispered, in a voice like rain on leaves. *No one suffers here. No one chooses wrongly. The spring never ends and neither do you.*

And Dominic felt how easy it would be. Say yes, and the winter ends tomorrow. Say yes, and Sera's brother — no, that was the Verdant's other offer, and it knew exactly which buttons were soft — say yes and no one would ever burn again, ever grieve again, ever *choose* again. A garden with no gardener needed. Peace, at the price of being a flower.

He looked down at his hands. In the vision they were already going green at the fingertips.

"No," he said.

The vision shattered—

—and reassembled into the second future. A harder country. A realm waking from sixty years of winter into ordinary, imperfect seasons. Mud. Thaw-floods. Famine years and lean harvests and a generation that would have to learn farming all over again. A king, mortal and ashamed, ruling a kingdom that would not forgive him quickly or perhaps at all. A school in the woods with a leaky roof. Himself, old eventually, magicless, teaching children to coax warmth from candles.

Nothing in it was glorious. Everything in it was *real*, and real things could be loved.

"It costs everything," he said aloud, to the blade, to the founders, to his grandmother watching from beyond the green. "And it's still the right one. Unbind it. Save the king. Rebuild what comes after. Let the land heal honestly, even if it heals slowly, even if it hurts."

The vision released him.

He came back to himself on the cottage floor, Sera's arms around him, her voice calling his name, and Odile crouched beside him with tears running freely down her weathered face.

"There it is," she whispered. "There's the Delacroix answer. The harder mercy. Your grandfather gave it too, and so did I, once, before I got tired." She pressed the sword into his hands and closed his fingers over the grip with hers. "Take Verdanhal. Take my blessing, such as it is. And take one more thing."

She pulled open her coat and drew out a folded scrap of parchment, soft with age — a letter, sealed with the guild's sigil.

"A rider came three days ago. Warden livery, but the seal is Maren's." Her expression darkened. "It's dated four days ago, boy. Which means whatever it says, it's already happened."

Dominic broke the seal with shaking fingers and read:

*Maren's safehouse burned. They have me. Corvane knows the pass. Come to Vharrow or don't, but come soon — the Long Winter is coming, and I don't think the king has until spring.*

Outside, far to the south, the horizon flickered — once, twice — with a light that was not lightning and not dawn.

Green.
