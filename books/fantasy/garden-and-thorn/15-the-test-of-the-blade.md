## 15. The Test of the Blade

The hermit's hut smelled of woodsmoke and dried yarrow and something older underneath — the green, living smell of the Thornwood itself, pressed into every beam and rafter like a memory that refused to leave.

Dominic stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and his grandmother's face turned toward him across the single small room, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

"You look like your grandfather," Odile Delacroix said at last. "Stubborn jaw. The kind that gets people hanged." She was smaller than he remembered from the stories Maren told — a wiry woman in a patched wool coat, silver hair cropped short as a soldier's, hands brown and knuckled like tree roots. Her eyes were the same gray as his own, and they did not soften when they looked at him. They assessed. "You're bleeding through that bandage."

"It's nothing."

"Sit down before you fall down, then, since it's nothing."

He sat. Sera stayed standing by the door, one hand resting on her sword hilt out of habit rather than threat, and Odile's gaze flicked to her and lingered.

"A huntress," she said. "You brought a huntress to my door."

"She saved my life twice," Dominic said. "I've been trying to return the favor ever since. We're roughly even now."

"Roughly even," Sera murmured, "is generous accounting."

Odile snorted — a dry, unimpressed sound — and put a kettle on the fire without asking whether anyone wanted tea, because in her house apparently wanting was irrelevant. Dominic watched her move around the little room and felt something twist in his chest that wasn't entirely the festering wound in his side. This was the woman whose medallion hung against his skin right now. This was the last master of the guild, who had walked out of Vharrow thirty years ago and let the world believe she was dead, and she made tea the way any old woman would, clattering cups, muttering about damp firewood.

"You knew I'd come," he said.

"I knew *someone* would come eventually, carrying that map, bleeding that particular blood." She set three cups down hard enough to slosh. "The Verdant's been waking for years. Winters don't lengthen on their own. Kings don't go green behind the eyes on their own." She looked at him directly. "You've seen it. In him."

"Yes."

"And you still want to unbind it."

"I want to save the king."

"That," said his grandmother, "is not what I asked."

* * *

She fed them first — barley soup with more salt than substance, which Sera ate like a woman who had forgotten meals existed, and Dominic managed half a bowl before the fever-heat in his wound made the rest a negotiation he was losing. Odile watched him not-eat with an expression he was beginning to suspect was her only expression.

Then she went to the back wall of the hut, pressed her palm against a knot in the timber, and spoke four words in Old Guild cant that Dominic had only ever seen written down. The wall opened.

Inside, in a niche lined with moss that grew green despite the winter outside, lay a sword.

Not a grand sword. That was the first thing he noticed, and it disappointed him before he could stop himself. It was a working blade, plain-hilted, its scabbard wrapped in oiled leather gone dark with decades of handling. But the air around it *bent*, subtly, the way heat bends above a summer road, and when Odile lifted it free the whole room seemed to lean toward it — Sera's hand tightening on her sword, the fire flaring, the moss in the niche trembling like grass in wind.

"The guild's third relic," Odile said. "We called it Verdict. Not because it judged others." She turned it in her hands, and for just a moment her iron composure cracked, and Dominic saw how much the weight of it cost her. "Because it judged us. Every master who carried it learned that. It doesn't show you what you want. It shows you what is true, and lets you decide what to do about it."

"The chalice showed me the founders' binding," Dominic said. "The Verdant starving. The bargain they forced on it."

"The chalice shows the past. Easy." She laid the sword across the table between them. "The past can't argue. The blade shows what comes next, and next is where cowards live." She pushed it toward him. "Touch the hilt, boy. Let it read you. And understand — whatever it shows you, you'll have to choose afterward with no one to blame but yourself. That's the price of being the last Delacroix. You don't get to inherit certainty. Only consequences."

Sera shifted forward. "Is it safe?"

"No," Odile said pleasantly. "Nothing worth doing is. Sit down, huntress. If he's going to die of this, you'll want to be holding his hand, and if he isn't, you'll want to have been holding it anyway. Trust me. I buried a husband."

Sera sat. Her hand found Dominic's under the table and gripped it, and he was grateful enough that he didn't tease her about it, which for him was practically a marriage vow.

He touched the hilt.

* * *

The world went green.

Not the sickly green of the Verdant's touch in his wound — this was deep and ancient, the green of the Thornwood before winter, of leaves layered on leaves going back ten thousand years. He stood in a forest aisle with light falling through the canopy in gold coins, and the air was so full of growing things that breathing felt like drinking.

"This is spring," he said aloud. "Real spring."

*A gift,* said a voice that was everywhere, in the roots and the rain and the space between his heartbeats. *A preview. Look.*

The forest dissolved.

He stood in Vharrow — but a Vharrow transformed. The frost-choked markets were open-air gardens. Vines clothed every wall in blossom. The canals ran clear and warm, and people moved through the streets smiling, faces slack and serene, tending flowers that bloomed out of season, out of order, out of nature. A child laughed and the sound was perfect and hollow, like music from a music box.

He looked closer at the smiles. Behind each face, faint beneath the skin, green filaments threaded up from the collarbones, along the throat, into the eyes.

*Eternal spring,* the Verdant said, and its voice was warm as sun on soil, and terrible as a landslide. *No famine. No frost. No grief. Your king promised me this realm and I have kept my half of every bargain ever struck with men. I only ask to keep mine.*

"What does it cost?" Dominic asked, though he already knew.

*Nothing you will miss,* said the Verdant. *Only the choosing. They will never again need to choose, or want, or fear. Is that not mercy?*

And because the blade did not permit lies, Dominic saw the truth beneath the mercy: the sleepwalkers of the hollow village, the cold hearths, the queen already gone. He saw centuries stretch out — the Verdant feeding patiently on a kingdom that had stopped being a kingdom and become a garden bed. He saw Sera's face among the smiling ones, filaments in her throat, her fierce gray eyes gone soft and empty, and something in him recoiled so violently that the vision shattered—

—and reformed.

Now he stood in the same market square, and it was dying.

No vines here. No false bloom. Just honest rot and honest weather: roofs sagging, fields thinning year over year, the slow arithmetic of a land that had been farmed too hard and loved too little for too long. People worked in the fields with lined faces and bent backs, and they were *free* — he could see it in the argumentative set of a fishwife's mouth, the way children ran wild and unwashed and fully awake — but the land under their feet was tired. He watched seasons pass like pages turning. Good harvest, poor harvest, poorer. Rivers shrinking. The Thornwood retreating hill by hill. Not death. Decline. The slow, unglamorous decay of a thing left to fend for itself after a century of borrowed vigor.

*This is unbinding,* the Verdant said, and there was no warmth in it now, only a vast tired honesty. *Your founders drank deep of my strength for a hundred years and called it prosperity. Take me out of your crown and the bill comes due. The land will live. But it will live poorly, and honestly, and it will take your grandchildren's grandchildren to forgive you.*

"Which one is real?" Dominic asked the green light. "Which future happens?"

*Both are real,* said the Verdant. *That is what your blade never understood. I do not choose for you. I only make certain that whichever you choose, you choose it knowing.* A pause, and in the pause he felt it — the hunger, enormous and old and not evil, only starving, always starving, betrayed by the bargain it had been forced to accept. *Choose, Delacroix. Even your founders flinched. Be the first of your line not to.*

Dominic closed his eyes. He thought of the child in the hollow village, warm again under his hands. He thought of the king's face, begging through the Verdant's grip. He thought of Maren in a cell somewhere, and his grandmother alone in a hut for thirty years, and Sera's brother burning for the crime of small magic, and every hedge-witch and charm-seller who had ever hidden what they were.

False spring and slavery. Or honest winter and freedom, and the long work of rebuilding.

"There's no version," he said slowly, "where we get everything back for free. Where the land stays fat and everyone stays free."

*No.*

"Then I choose the debt." He opened his eyes. "Unbind it. All of it. Let the land be poor and honest and *ours*. We'll rebuild. That's what the guild was for before it got scared — not hoarding power, teaching people to grow things. I'll rebuild it. Even without magic. *Especially* without magic, maybe, because then no one can say I did it for the power."

The green light held very still.

*You understand,* the Verdant said, almost gently, *that this choice will cost you your gift entire. When the binding severs, what is Delacroix in you dies with it.*

"I know."

*And you choose it anyway.*

"I choose it anyway."

For the first time, the vast presence seemed to hesitate — a starved old thing confronted with an unexpected kindness. Then, so quietly he almost missed it:

*Your grandmother chose differently, once. Ask her why she has hated herself for thirty years.*

* * *

Dominic came back to himself on the floor of the hut with Sera's arms around him and his grandmother kneeling beside him, and the sword lying flat and quiet on the table as though it had never woken at all.

"He chose," Sera was saying, urgent. "Odile, he chose — the unbinding, he—"

"I heard." His grandmother's voice was strange. "The whole valley heard, probably. Guild magic carries." She helped him upright, and her hands were steadier than her voice. "What did it show you?"

"Two futures. False spring or slow decay." He swallowed. "It also said you chose differently once. That you've hated yourself for thirty years."

The silence that followed was long enough that the fire cracked twice.

"I stood where you stood," Odile said finally. "Forty years ago, when the crown first began to sicken and none of us understood why. The blade showed me the same two roads. And I looked at the decay — the hungry winters, the thin harvests, all that honest suffering — and I thought: *there must be a third road. There must be a clever answer.* Delacroixs are clever. It's our curse." She sat down heavily across from him. "So I didn't choose. I went looking for a way to cheat the test. And while I searched, the others acted without me, and the binding frayed, and the Verdant slipped its leash and found a lonely young king with a crown and a bargain in his heart. By the time I came back with my clever answer, the guild was ash and my granddaughter's son was selling charms in a market I couldn't walk through."

Her gray eyes met his, and for the first time they softened.

"You chose the debt. No cleverness. No third road. You looked at the worst option on the table and you picked it because it was *true*." She reached across the table and took the sword. "Thirty years I kept this, telling myself I was guarding it. I was hiding from it. From the day I failed to choose."

She rose, crossed to him, and held the blade out flat across both palms — the way, he realized, a master yields the relics to an heir.

"Verdict judges its bearer," she said. "It found you worthy. So do I, for whatever an old coward's word counts for now." Her voice roughened. "Take the blade, Dominic. Take my blessing, such as it is. And when you stand in front of that thing wearing the king — finish what our family started. Don't flinch like I did."

He took the sword. It was lighter than it looked, and warm, and it hummed faintly against his palms like a held breath.

"Grandmother," he said. "Come with us."

She laughed — short and disbelieving. "I'm seventy years old, boy."

"Maren's in a cell in Vharrow because of us. Corvane's hunting us with the whole Warden order. And the rite needs every Delacroix it can get." He looked at her steadily. "You spent thirty years hiding from one mistake. Come spend one week fixing it."

Behind him, Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Odile glared at him with an expression so exactly like his own stubborn jaw reflected back at him that he nearly laughed himself.

Then she snatched her coat off its peg.

"Well?" she snapped. "Are you waiting for the snow to melt? The Verdant won't wait, Corvane certainly won't wait, and if I'm going to die unforgiven I'd rather do it somewhere with worse weather and better company." She stamped toward the door, then paused, and added, gruffly, without turning around:

"Your grandfather would have liked you. He also would have gotten you both killed, so it's fortunate he's dead. Now move. Vharrow's five days hard riding, and we've a king to save and a guild to bury properly — and this time, boy, we do it *right*."
