## 16. The Green Court

The gates of Vharrow had never looked small to Sera Vane before.

She had ridden out of them a dozen times on the crown's business — hunting smugglers, hedge-mages, once an entire family of river-witches who turned out to be brewing nothing worse than headache tonics — and every time she'd come home, the walls had risen up white and certain against the sky, like a mother's arms. Now they rose up gray and wrong, and the banners above the gate hung so still in the windless cold that they might have been painted there.

She wore her huntress leathers, her order's silver stag pinned at her throat, and she had spent three days on the road rehearsing her story: *I found the charm-seller dead in the pinewastes. I burned the body per protocol. I came home to report.* A good lie has bones, her old sergeant used to say, and this one had a skeleton to spare. The only problem was that Sera had never been a good liar. Her face told truths she hadn't authorized. It was why she made a fine huntress and a terrible card player.

*You don't have to lie well,* Dominic had said, pressing her hand at the crossroads outside Maren's safehouse. *You just have to look tired enough that nobody wants to check.*

"That," she'd told him, "is the most cynical thing I've ever heard from a man who sells lucky rabbit feet."

"They're not actually rabbit feet," he'd said. "They're chicken feet dyed white. But hope is hope, isn't it?"

She missed him already, which was absurd. They'd been apart for exactly four hours.

Now she rode through the gate, past the Warden checkpoints, past the frost-sellers and the coal-hoarders, and let the city's misery swallow her whole. Vharrow was dying by inches. She could see it in the market squares where the ice never melted even around the braziers, in the beggars who no longer begged but simply sat, in the way people walked — heads down, shoulders hunched, as if the sky itself had become a debt collector.

And she could see it in the faces.

That was the first thing. Near the fountain in Coronet Square, a court clerk — she recognized his livery, the pale green of the Chancery — stood perfectly still while snow gathered on his shoulders. Not shivering. Not blinking. And beneath the skin of his throat, when he turned his head toward the light, something *moved*. A slow green tracery, like ivy seen through parchment.

Sera kept riding. She did not stop. She did not stare. Her heart hammered against the silver stag at her throat as if it wanted out.

*Half the court,* Dominic had said. *Maybe more. The Verdant doesn't take people all at once. It takes them the way winter takes a pond — from the edges in.*

The second thing was the queen.

Sera learned it in the huntresses' barracks, where she reported to the duty officer with her rehearsed lie and discovered, with a lurch, that nobody cared. The duty officer — Brannic, a heavy man with kind eyes and a wine habit — barely glanced at her papers.

"Vane. Thought you were dead." He stamped her report without reading it. "Everyone's saying you went after the courier's killer and vanished."

"I found him dead in the wastes." The lie came out smooth, which frightened her more than if it had stuck. "Frost took him. Nothing left to interrogate."

"Mm." Brannic poured himself a cup of something that was not water. "Well. You're back, and back is back. Keep your head down, though. Palace is strange lately."

"Strange how?"

He hesitated. That hesitation was the loudest thing Sera had heard in weeks. "Her Majesty's gone to the summer residence," he said. "For her health."

"It's the dead of winter."

"Yes," Brannic said, and drank.

Later, in the barracks dormitory, a young huntress named Pell — all elbows and earnestness, the kind of girl who still sharpened her knives because she liked the sound — sat down on the cot beside Sera's and spoke without moving her lips, the way soldiers do when the walls have ears.

"The queen didn't go anywhere," Pell murmured. "There was a litter carried out at night, three weeks ago. Curtained. My sister works the laundry for the household guard, and she says the linens from the royal chambers came back stained green. *Green*, Vane. Like sap. And the king—" Pell's voice dropped to almost nothing. "The king hasn't held audience in a month. Corvane speaks for him now. Corvane signs his decrees. And yesterday Corvane ordered the archives sealed. The *guild* archives. The ones that've been gathering dust since before either of us was born."

"Why would he seal those?"

Pell shrugged, elaborately casual. "Maybe he's sentimental about dust."

That night Sera lay awake under her issue blanket, listening to the barracks breathe, and made her plan the way she'd been taught to make plans: simple, stupid, and flexible enough to survive contact with reality. Tomorrow she would request an audience with the Warden-Captain — a returning huntress, loyal, reporting in. And while she stood in Corvane's antechamber being ignored, she would count doors, guards, and minutes, because somewhere above all that marble was a king who needed killing or saving, and she had not yet decided which.

* * *

Corvane received her in the Hall of Petition, which should have told her everything.

The Hall of Petition was where commoners came to beg the crown for clemency — tax relief, pardons, permission to bury their dead inside the walls during plague years. No Warden-Captain had ever held court there. It was beneath the office. It was, Sera realized, watching him, precisely the point. Corvane wanted to be seen among the petitioners. Wanted them to learn what power looked like now.

He sat on a plain chair on the dais where the throne ought to be — the throne itself was curtained off behind black velvet, which raised questions Sera filed away for later — and he listened to each petitioner with his hands folded and his head tilted, patient as a spider in a web that was also a garden.

He had changed. Sera had known Corvane for six years; he'd signed her first commission. He had always been lean, precise, a man carved rather than grown. Now there was a softness to him that was worse than fat — a swelling, as though he were ripening. His skin had a faint olive cast. When he smiled at a weeping widow whose son was in debtor's prison, the smile went too wide by half a tooth on each side.

"Vane." His eyes found her in the line of waiting officers, and she felt the attention like a hand on the back of her neck. "The prodigal huntress. You'll wait. We'll speak after."

So she waited, two hours, among clerks with green threads crawling beneath their collars and guardsmen who stood too still and breathed too slowly, and she counted what she'd come to count: three doors out of the hall, twelve guards, one corridor to the royal apartments that was barred by a gate of black iron wrapped — she squinted, disbelieving — wrapped in living briar, its thorns long as her finger, its leaves impossibly green in a palace where nothing green had survived the year.

When the last petitioner was dismissed, Corvane descended the dais and walked to her, and up close the smell of him was loam and cut stems, spring soil in a frozen room.

"You were seen," he said pleasantly. "On the north road. With the Delacroix boy."

Sera's blood went to ice water. Every rehearsal, every lie, every bone in the story — gone. What came out of her mouth instead was pure instinct, honed by six years of reports to men who punished weakness: *"I took him prisoner. He escaped."*

"Did he." Corvane studied her. Somewhere behind his eyes, something green leaned closer, the way a child leans over an anthill. "You had him bound, armed, and alone on an empty road, and he escaped from *you*. Sera Vane, who brought in the Kessington brothers single-handed. Who tracked the Marsh Witch through flood season."

"He had help," Sera said. "A woman. Older. Guild-trained. She—"

"There is no guild," Corvane said softly. "There has been no guild for thirty years. There are only relics of it, and relics can be broken." He reached out and touched the silver stag at her throat, and his fingers were cool and faintly damp, like a leaf at dawn. "You look tired, Vane. Grief does that. I hear your brother's anniversary approaches."

The floor tilted. Sera kept her face still through six years of practice and one lifetime of need. "I don't know what you mean, Captain."

"No," Corvane agreed. "Of course you don't." He smiled that too-wide smile. "Go rest. Report to me tomorrow. And Vane — the king will see you soon. He's been asking after you particularly. Isn't that flattering? He remembers every huntress who ever served him well."

He remembers, Sera thought, walking out on legs that belonged to someone else. Or *it* does. It knows I've seen its face.

She had come to the palace to find a door into the king's chamber. Corvane had just handed her one, wrapped in menace like a gift in thorn-paper.

She intended to walk through it.

* * *

The summons came at midnight, which surprised her not at all.

Two guards escorted her up the spiral stair — guards who did not speak, whose breath fogged in the cold corridors but whose skin, at the collar, showed that same slow green lace — through the barred gate of living briar, which parted before them like a curtain of snakes, and along a gallery hung with portraits of kings long dead. In every portrait, Sera noticed, the eyes had been scratched out. Recently. The gilt frames still held fresh flecks of gold paint where the scraping knife had slipped.

*It doesn't like to be watched,* she thought. *Or remembered. Kings are how people remember what a true face looks like.*

The guards stopped at a door of black oak carved with roses — the old royal sigil, before the stag — and one of them opened it, and neither followed her in.

The king's chamber was a greenhouse.

That was her first thought, absurd and immediate: someone had built a conservatory inside the palace and forgotten to tell anyone. Vines climbed the bedposts and the canopy and the stone walls themselves, splitting mortar, drinking torchlight. The air was wet and warm and smelled overwhelmingly of growth, of pollen, of the green smell of a forest floor in June — here, in the heart of the endless winter, where the world outside was locked so hard that the harbor had frozen solid a mile out.

And in the middle of it, in a great bed draped in flowering boughs, lay King Aldric Vharrow the Third.

Sera had seen him twice in her life, both times at distance: a broad, ruddy man with a laugh that shook the rafters, famous for remembering servants' names and for eating entire pies at festivals. The thing in the bed wore his body the way a glove wears a hand. He was gaunt. His skin was translucent at the temples, and beneath it the green was not a tracery here but a *forest* — a whole root-system glowing faintly, branching down his throat, across his chest, up into his skull. His eyes opened as she entered, and they were green to the rim, green as new leaves, green as the moment before a storm breaks over a wheat field.

"Huntress," the king said — and then the voice changed mid-word, deepened, multiplied, became a chorus speaking through one mouth, a choir of roots and rain and slow centuries. "*Sera Vane. Little wolf of the crown. Come closer. I have waited so long to meet the one who saw me.*"

Every animal instinct Sera owned screamed at her to run. She had faced frost-wraiths and Warden-hounds and a man with an axe in the pinewastes, and none of it had felt like this. This was standing at the edge of a depth with no bottom.

She made herself walk forward. Three steps. Four. She stopped at the foot of the bed, hand nowhere near her sword, because some part of her understood that drawing steel in this room would be like drawing it against the ocean.

"You're hurting him," she said. Her voice only shook a little. She counted that a victory.

"*Hurting?*" The Verdant — she understood suddenly that the king's mouth was merely a door it had borrowed — sounded genuinely puzzled, and the puzzlement was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "*I am keeping him alive. Do you know what I was promised, little wolf? Do you know the bargain your founders' founders made? Spring eternal, they said. A realm that never starves, never freezes, never buries its children in the cold months. And then they bound me — bound me into this crown, this iron circlet, this cage of dead metal — and fed me a kingdom drop by drop, a tithe of green, a sip of growing things, and called it protection.*" The vines stirred. Flowers opened along them, all at once, a ripple of white blossoms. "*Your guild starved me for thirty years. Your Wardens burned every hedge-witch who might have fed me freely. So I take what I am owed. Slowly. Fairly. And when the bargain completes, there will be no more winter. Never again. Is that not mercy?*"

"It's theft," Sera said. "You're wearing a man."

"*I am offering him rest,*" the Verdant said, and for one flickering instant the voice was only Aldric's again, cracked and human and drowning: "*help me* — " and then the green closed over it like water over a stone.

Sera's eyes stung. She thought of Pell's laundry-stained sheets. Of a curtained litter carried out at night. "Where is the queen?"

"*Gone to the summer residence,*" the Verdant said, and the humor in it was ancient and terrible. "*She would not accept the gift. She fought me. She was very brave, your queen, right until the end. The garden needed feeding, little wolf. Winter is expensive.*"

Sera's hand did move then, halfway to her sword, and the vines shifted — lazily, the way a cat shifts — and she forced her arm still. *Not yet. Not here. You cannot kill weather with a sword, and this thing IS the weather now.*

"You sent for me," she said. "Why?"

"*Because I know what walks toward my Thornwood,*" the Verdant said. "*A Delacroix, with the map, with the seed-charm, with the chalice's secret in his blood. Coming to unbind me. To send me back into the dark between seasons. To give your realm back its honest, brutal, freezing winters, where children die of coughing and the harvest fails one year in five.*" The king's ruined face inclined toward her, courteous as a host. "*And I know he travels with you. I know what you two are to each other — oh yes, little wolf, I taste it in the roots of the world, love is such a loud thing — and I know what you did to your brother.*"

The word went through her like a spear through wet paper.

"*Tomas,*" the Verdant said gently. "*Hedge-mage. Burned in the yard at Corvane's order, on your testimony. Nineteen years old. You have said his name aloud perhaps four times in seven years. You say it every night, though. In dreams. Dreams are loud too.*"

"Stop." Her voice cracked clean down the middle.

"*I do not need to trick you,*" the Verdant said. "*I only need to offer. Hear the offer, huntress, and then refuse it if you can. Give me the Delacroix. Turn him back at the gates, or simply tell me where he crosses the Thornwood's edge — one word, whispered to any green thing, and the roots will carry it to me. Do this, and I will give him back to you. Tomas, whole. Tomas, warm. I hold what the fire released — every spark of him, every laughing hour, and I can gather sparks into a man again. I have done it for lesser loves than yours. The courtiers you saw, the ones with gardens under their skin — each of them bought back someone. Ask them. Ask the clerk in Coronet Square whom he purchased. Ask them all, and count how many regret it.*"

The chamber was silent except for the sound of growing things, which was a sound like whispering, like rain, like pages turning in a book that read itself.

And Sera stood at the heart of power, at the center of the web, and felt the temptation settle over her the way snow settles on a roof — softly, softly, with all its weight to come.

Because she could see it. That was the horror of it. She could see Tomas so clearly: his crooked grin, the way he'd cupped a dying sparrow in his hands and it had flown away whole, the way he'd whispered *it's not wicked, Sera, it's just help* the night before she gave him to the Wardens. Seven years she had carried him like a stone in her chest. Seven years of penance dressed up as duty. And here was a voice offering to set the stone down. Offering to make the burning undone. All it cost was one man — a charming, foolish, brave man who dyed chicken feet white and called them hope, who talked too much when he was frightened, who had kissed her under a frozen waterfall and made her laugh for the first time since the fire.

One man against her brother. The arithmetic was obscene. The arithmetic was easy.

That was how she knew it was a lie — or worse, a truth.

"You can't bring back the dead," Sera said quietly. "Even you. Especially you. Whatever you'd give me would wear his face and have your roots in it, and I'd spend forever knowing. That's not a gift. That's just a longer way of burning him."

The green eyes regarded her for a long, long moment. Somewhere in the depths of them, she thought she saw something flicker that was not malice — something old and starving and almost sad.

"*The grandmother chose the blade,*" the Verdant said at last. "*The boy will choose the severing. And you, little wolf — you may yet choose me. Choices keep. Mine especially. Go, then. Climb down from my mountain of flowers. Tell your Delacroix I await him in the Thornwood, and that the door will be open, and that I remember every kindness ever done me and every betrayal too.*" The king's eyelids drooped, the chorus fading to a single exhausted human murmur: "*...and tell him... hurry... please... whatever he means to do... hurry...*"

The vines drew back from the door. The guards outside had not moved. As Sera descended the spiral stair, her legs shaking, her mind blazing, she passed a window looking north over the frozen city — and saw, far off across the dark fields, a faint green glow on the horizon where the Thornwood stood.

It was pulsing. Slowly. Steadily.

Like a heartbeat. Like a countdown.

Like something waking up.
