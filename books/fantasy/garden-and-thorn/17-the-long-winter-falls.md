## 17. The Long Winter Falls

Corvane had not slept in nine days.

He no longer needed to. Sleep was a human weakness, and he was becoming something else entirely — something that drank the green light of the Verdant through his veins like cold wine and found it sweeter than rest, sweeter than the old satisfactions of rank and duty. He stood at the window of the Warden-Captain's tower and watched frost crawl across Vharrow's rooftops in patterns too deliberate to be weather: spirals, branching veins, the slow geometry of a thing thinking.

Below, in the palace square, his Wardens were assembling the court.

They came quietly, as he had ordered. No chains, no shouting. The heralds had gone door to door at dawn with a simple message: *His Majesty commands the presence of all lords and ladies at the winter proclamation.* The word "commands" did most of the work. That, and the hounds pacing at the heralds' heels — great pale beasts with frost on their breath and nothing behind their eyes but obedience and appetite.

Corvane descended the tower stairs without touching the rail. His boots made no sound anymore. He had noticed that three days ago and decided it was an improvement.

In the square, the court of Vharrow stood in the falling snow, breath steaming, faces pinched with the particular misery of aristocrats asked to be cold in public. Corvane walked out onto the palace steps and let them see him. He had learned, these past weeks, that fear traveled faster than any decree.

"My lords. My ladies." His voice carried without effort; the Verdant lent it weight, the way a bell lends weight to a clapper. "You have wondered why the winter lengthens. Why the harvests fail. Why your king has not been seen at feast or hunt these two seasons past."

A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat. Somewhere near the front, old Lord Cassel — a man who had opposed Corvane's appointment from the first day — opened his mouth to speak.

The frost answered for him. It climbed Lord Cassel's boots, his knees, his chest, in a single smooth motion, sheathing him to the chin in clear ice. He did not die. Corvane could feel him still alive inside it, heart hammering, eyes wide and rolling above the frozen collar. The Verdant did not waste what it could keep.

"The king," Corvane continued, as though nothing had happened, "has entered into a new covenant. An ancient one. In exchange for his service, the land will be renewed. Not by spring — spring is a fickle servant, always late, always leaving. But by something older. Something patient." He spread his hands, and green light moved beneath the skin of his wrists like fish under ice. "I declare the Long Winter. A season of gathering. When it ends, Vharrow will bloom as it has never bloomed. Until it ends, you will endure. You will obey. And you will be *kept*."

The keeping began that hour. Vines took root in the palace gutters and spread down the streets, black-green and glistening, threading through doorways, up drainpipes, into chimneys. By nightfall half the nobility of Vharrow stood rooted in their own gardens, glassy-eyed, ivy laced beneath their skin, wearing expressions of vague contentment that were worse than screaming. The other half barricaded themselves indoors and prayed to gods who had stopped answering after the Sundering.

Corvane felt none of it as cruelty. That was the gift of the Verdant's touch: it smoothed the edges off conscience the way water smooths stone. He had been a hard man all his life — a man who burned mages because burning was necessary, who signed death warrants over breakfast without losing his appetite. What was happening now was only the same work, larger. The realm had always been a garden. Someone had merely neglected the pruning.

*And the Delacroix boy,* said the voice that lived behind his eyes, vast and slow and green. *The map. The relics.*

"Still north," Corvane murmured, alone again in his tower. He could feel them now, faintly — the grandmother's blade like a splinter of cold iron in his awareness, the boy himself like a candle flame seen across a frozen lake. "Two weeks out. Perhaps less, if they push."

*Faster,* the Verdant said. *Before the Thornwood. Once he stands in the wood, the old binding wakes, and I am not what I was there.*

"I know my work." Corvane flexed his fingers and watched frost bloom between them. "But I'll want more hounds. And the woman in the cells — the smuggler. She knows the tunnels. If anyone can slip a message out of this city, it's her."

*Then move her somewhere nothing slips out of.*

"I intend to." He smiled, and it was almost the smile he'd worn when he was only a man. "Tonight. After the last of the court is planted."

* * *

Maren Ostwick had spent four days in a cell six feet by eight, and she had used every hour of it productively: sleeping, eating the questionable stew, memorizing the guard rotations, and insulting her captors in three languages, one of which she'd invented herself on the second day just to keep her hand in.

"You know what your problem is?" she told the guard outside her door, a young man with chilblains on his ears and the posture of someone deeply regretting his career choices. "No imagination. A proper jailer would've taken my bootlaces. My hairpins. My belt. Instead you left me everything, because nobody taught you that a prisoner is just a locksmith with extra steps."

"Captain says you're to be moved tonight," the guard said, in the flat tone of a man reciting scripture to ward off conversation.

"Moved where?"

"Deep cells. Under the palace."

Maren sat back on the cot and whistled low. The deep cells. She'd helped build the smugglers' bypass around the deep cells twenty years ago, back when the guild was still a guild and not a memory and a medallion. Which meant she knew exactly how much stone stood between those cells and daylight, and exactly how little of it had ever been properly mortared.

"Well," she said cheerfully. "Best get comfortable, then. Long walk."

She went back to work on the lock the moment his footsteps faded. It was a poor lock. Everything in Vharrow had become poor lately — the gates, the patrols, the watch schedules. The city was sick, and sick things grew careless. Even the cold was wrong: it came in waves now, pulsing, like breath. Like something enormous inhaling.

The lock gave with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a thunderclap. Maren froze, counted to thirty, then eased the door open.

The corridor beyond was empty. Frost furred the walls in spirals — the same spirals she'd seen creeping over Dominic's map, over the courier's bloodstained parchment, over everything the Verdant touched. She'd spent her whole life in the cracks of this city, and she knew rot when she saw it. This wasn't rot. This was *appetite*.

She moved the way she'd taught Dominic to move, years ago, back when he was a skinny boy with his grandmother's eyes and no idea what he'd inherited: heel to toe, close to the wall, breathing through the nose. Two guards at the stairhead, backs turned, watching something in the courtyard below. She slipped past them so closely she could have picked either pocket, and was honestly tempted.

The deep cells were down, down, down — spiral stairs slick with ice, torches burning green instead of orange. Maren passed the open doors and did not look inside, because she had looked once already, on the way in, and what she'd seen had kept her awake for a night she couldn't afford. People standing in their cells. Standing perfectly still, vines threaded through their wrists, smiling at nothing.

Instead she took the maintenance passage behind the cistern — the one the builders had bricked over in her grandfather's time and the smugglers had unbricked in her mother's — and crawled through forty yards of freezing dark, elbow over elbow, whispering curses with genuine affection.

The passage let out behind a tannery on Ropewalk Lane, through a hatch she'd oiled every season for thirty years. She emerged into a night so cold it felt personal, and looked up at a sky full of stars that seemed to be going out one by one, and allowed herself exactly ten seconds of despair. She timed it against her heartbeat, an old guild trick. Ten seconds was enough to feel a thing fully. Eleven was enough for it to start feeling you back.

Then she got moving.

The safehouse on Candle Street had been burned — she saw the ruin from three alleys away, its roof caved in, frost blooming from the wreckage like white flowers. So she went to the second place, the one only she and Dominic knew, the chandlery cellar with the false wall. Her hands shook as she worked the hidden catch, and not entirely from the cold.

Inside: paper, ink, a stub of candle, and — gods bless her own paranoid heart — a carrier pigeon in a wicker cage, sulking in the dark.

"All right, Your Majesty," she told the bird, feeding it grain from a pouch in her boot. "You're about to earn your seed."

The message took her three tries. There wasn't room for poetry. She wrote:

*BOY. LONG WINTER DECLARED. CITY FALLING. COURT PLANTED LIKE TURNIPS. CORVANE COMES FOR YOU WITH EVERY HOUND HE HAS. DO NOT ENTER VHARROW BY ANY KNOWN GATE. THE TUNNELS UNDER THE TANNERY ON SALT ROW STILL OPEN. GRANDMOTHER KNOWS WHICH ONES. HURRY. THE SKY IS EATING ITSELF. — M*

She rolled it small, tied it to the pigeon's leg, carried the bird up through the chandlery's coal chute, and threw it into the freezing air. It beat upward twice, circled once — and vanished south into the dark, toward the north road, toward two travelers who thought they had time.

Maren watched until her eyes watered. Then she went back underground, because the only thing more dangerous than staying in Vharrow was being caught above ground in it, and because she had one more errand before dawn: the old guild cache beneath the Fountain of Weeping Saints, where she'd hidden everything worth saving when the Wardens came the first time.

If the boy was going to save the world, somebody had to make sure he arrived supplied.

* * *

Three hundred miles north, the Thornwood was dying.

Dominic felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the air like a held breath, like the pause before bad news. He reined the cart horse to a stop at the crest of the ridge and looked down at the forest that had dominated his grandmother's stories his entire childhood: the ancient wood, the guild's first sanctuary, the place where the binding had been woven.

Frost covered it from edge to edge. Not the honest white of snowfall, but a rime that crept along the branches in spirals, silvering the bark, sealing every bud, every hollow, every sleeping bird. As he watched, the frost line advanced — visibly, steadily, a tide of white rolling through the trees at the pace of a walking man.

"It's eating the wood," Sera said quietly beside him. She had her bow strung, though there was nothing yet to shoot. "I thought the Verdant wanted spring. Promised spring."

"It wants *life*," Dominic said. "Spring is just how it takes it." He touched the medallion at his throat, and the metal was warm — warmer than it should have been, warm as a living thing. "Grandmother. How far to the border shrine?"

His grandmother stood at the cart's tail, wrapped in furs, looking younger and older than her years all at once, the way she always did when the guild's business was on the table. "Half a day, if the roads hold. Less if we abandon the cart." She glanced at the horse, a placid creature named Buttercup whose chief virtues were patience and an iron stomach. "Which will break your heart, I know."

"Buttercup is a member of this expedition," Dominic said gravely.

"Buttercup is dinner if the hounds find us, and hounds smell carts for miles." She was already unbuckling the harness. "We go light. We go fast. And Dominic—" She caught his arm, and her grip was still strong, still sure. "When we cross into the wood, the old wards will wake. They'll know my blood. They may test yours. Whatever you see in there — whatever the wood shows you — keep walking. Promise me."

"What will it show me?"

"Whatever will make you stop." She said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "That's what tests do."

Sera appeared at his other side, checking her quiver with quick, economical movements. "We should reach the shrine before full dark," she said. "After dark, in territory that's actively dying, I stop liking our odds." She paused, then added, with the ghost of her old formality, "Sir."

"You don't have to call me sir."

"You rescued me from a frost-wraith. I'm contractually obligated to call you sir for at least a year."

Despite everything — despite the dying forest below and the green wound festering along his ribs where Corvane's ambush had marked him, despite the fact that his magic was guttering like a candle in wind — Dominic laughed. It came out ragged, but real. That was Sera's gift, he was learning. She made the end of the world feel survivable, one dry remark at a time.

They abandoned the cart, said their goodbyes to Buttercup (who received half their oats and a solemn promise of rescue), and started down into the frost.

The descent took them through the outer farms, and that was where the horror truly began. Fields frozen mid-furrow. A well with ice growing *upward* out of it, in a spiral, like a frozen fountain. A scarecrow sheathed in rime, and — Dominic made himself look twice — a farmer beside it, standing in the same attitude, arms out, vines laced beneath the skin of his face, smiling his terrible contented smile.

Sera drew her knife and put herself between Dominic and the figure before he could speak. Then she saw the vines, and lowered it slowly.

"He's alive," Dominic said softly. He could feel it now, the Verdant's thread running through the man, thin as spider silk, drinking. "They're all alive. It keeps them. That's the worst part — it doesn't kill what it can keep."

"Can you help him?"

"With what?" He held up his hands. Pale light flickered weakly at his fingertips and died. The wound in his side pulsed green in answer, hungry, and he clenched his jaw against it. "I'm running on fumes, Sera. Every mile closer to Vharrow, the Verdant pulls harder. It knows I'm coming. It's *drinking* me ahead of time."

His grandmother had not stopped walking. Now she turned, and the wind pulled at her white hair, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and every year of the guild's grief besides. "Then we don't stop either," she said. "Grief later. Mercy later. Walk."

They walked.

Through the afternoon the frost line moved with them, parallel, patient, a white wall advancing through the trees to the east. Twice Sera spotted movement in the deep woods — pale shapes, hound-shaped, keeping pace — and twice the shapes melted away rather than close, as if under orders to herd rather than take. Corvane was driving them, Dominic realized. Driving them south, toward Vharrow, toward the palace, the way a shepherd drives sheep. The Verdant didn't want them dead in the wilderness.

It wanted them delivered.

Dusk came early, the way dusk had come everywhere since the Sundering's oldest bargain soured. They reached the border shrine as the last light failed: a ring of standing stones furred with frost, a lintel carved with the guild's sigil — the tree within the thorn — half-erased by centuries and half-reclaimed by ice.

His grandmother pressed her palm to the stone. For a long moment nothing happened. Then, deep beneath the frozen earth, something groaned — a sound like a door opening after a hundred years — and warmth bled up through the frost, a single thread of it, green and gold and stubbornly alive.

"The wards wake," she said. "Slowly. Starved. But they wake." She looked back at them both, and in the green light her eyes were bright and fierce. "Rest here tonight. Tomorrow we cross into the Thornwood, and after that, nothing between us and Vharrow but Corvane's army and the end of the world."

"That's a short list," Sera said.

"It's shorter than it was," Dominic said. And then, because the light was fading and the words had been sitting in his chest for days: "Sera. When this is done — if it's done —"

"Don't." But she was smiling as she said it, there in the green-gold glow of the waking wards, her hand finding his in the dark. "Save it. Say it in spring."

Far to the south, unseen, a pigeon beat its tired wings through the freezing dark, carrying a scrap of paper toward a road where no one waited to receive it — and behind it, in Vharrow, the Long Winter settled over the capital like a lid closing on a jar.

And in the Warden-Captain's tower, Corvane opened his eyes in the dark, feeling the boy cross the border of the Thornwood, and smiled, and sent the hounds.
