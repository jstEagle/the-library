## 18. Storming the Pale Palace

The guild tunnel mouth opened behind a collapsed wine cellar in the Low Ward, and it smelled like a grave that had been pickled.

"Charming," Sera said, holding her lantern high. The light caught a ceiling furred with frost that should not have existed underground — white rime creeping down old brick like the palace above had been weeping winter into its own foundations for years.

"It's called history," said Odile Delacroix, ducking through the gap with the stiff dignity of a woman who had once commanded an order of mages and had never quite forgiven the world for forgetting. She was seventy if she was a day, walked with a cane she mostly used as a weapon, and had spent the entire journey north correcting everything Sera said about anything. "Your order sealed these tunnels when they outlawed us. Sloppy work. We kept the keys."

"You kept the keys to tunnels under the royal palace," Sera said, "for forty years."

"We kept *everything*," Odile said. "That was rather the point of a guild."

Dominic squeezed past them both, map in hand, breath fogging. He looked terrible — the green wound at his shoulder had spread overnight into a lacework of veins that glowed faintly when he moved wrong, and he'd stopped pretending otherwise around dawn. But his eyes were steady. "Which way, Gran?"

"Left at the cistern. Right past the old foundry. And if anyone touches the warding stones without asking, I will know, and I will be insufferable about it."

They went left at the cistern.

* * *

Sera had walked these halls her whole adult life — above ground, at least, in the bright corridors where huntresses were welcome and the law was clean and simple. Below, Vharrow was another city entirely. Guild-built galleries arched overhead, carved with leaves and stars, and every few hundred paces a stone stood embedded in the wall, dark and dormant, humming faintly against her palm like a sleeping animal.

"They're listening," Dominic said quietly, catching her hand there. "The wards. Grandmother says stone remembers who asks politely."

"And what happens if you ask impolitely?"

"Nothing good." He hesitated, then didn't let go. "Sera. If this goes wrong down there—"

"It won't."

"If it does. If the Verdant takes me the way it took Corvane, or worse, if the unbinding needs more than—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I need you to promise you'll finish it. You, not her. She'll try to do it herself and it will kill her."

"She'll hear you saying that."

"I know. That's why I'm saying it now, while she's forty paces back telling a wall off for crumbling."

Sera looked at him — this charm-seller with frostbitten ears and a dead man's map and magic leaking out of him like light through a cracked jar — and made the promise before she could think better of it. "I'll finish it."

He kissed her then, quick and cold and certain, and Odile's voice floated up the gallery: "*If you two are quite finished defiling my architecture—*"

* * *

The tunnel forked beneath the palace proper, and here the plan came apart into three directions and one bad feeling.

"The throne room sits over the old guildhall," Odile said, tapping the map. "Of course it does. Aldric built his palace on our bones and called it peace. We come up through the foundation vault. Dominic, with me."

"And me?" Sera asked.

Odile looked at her for a long moment. In the lantern light her eyes were the same grey-green as Dominic's, and just as hard to argue with. "Corvane will have posted his hounds at every gate and stair. Someone has to make noise on the upper floors. Someone who knows the patrol rotations. Someone who can fight."

"I'm deserting, not deaf," Sera said. "I know the rotations."

"Then you'll draw them east, toward the council wing, and give us the time we need. And Sera—" the old woman's voice dropped, lost its edge for exactly one sentence, "—my son's daughter-in-law would have been proud of you. Don't die proving it."

Then she turned and limped into the dark with her grandson, and Sera was alone with a sword, a knife, three firepots, and a palace full of things that used to be people.

She took a breath. Made herself smile, because that was what you did before the worst hour of your life — you smiled at it first so it couldn't surprise you.

"Right," she told the empty tunnel. "Let's go wake the neighbors."

* * *

The east guard post was empty except for one Warden, and he wasn't empty for long — Sera put him down with the flat of her blade before he finished turning, because whatever else Corvane had done to his men, some of them still bled like men, and she wasn't ready to find out which ones didn't.

She rang the alarm bell herself.

That was the ugly genius of it: no one questioned a huntress sounding the alarm. Within minutes the corridors above were full of running boots, shouting, torchlight — half of it hunting her, all of it looking east, away from the foundation vault, away from the two Delacroixes climbing out of the palace's bones with a seed-charm, a chalice, and a blade older than the crown.

She led them a merry chase. Through the council gallery, over the frozen fountain court, across the long colonnade where the portraits of kings watched with painted eyes — and somewhere between the third corridor and the fourth, she realized the shouting had stopped.

That was worse.

"Huntress Vane."

Corvane stood at the end of the colonnade, alone, framed by frost-covered windows. He had been a broad, bluff man once; now he looked like a tree that had died standing up, bark-grey skin stretched over something green that moved underneath. Vines threaded his neck like a collar. His voice was still his voice, which was the most horrible part.

"You're supposed to be dead in a ditch outside Thorn Pass," he said.

"I'm told I'm difficult to kill. It's in my file."

Something that might have been humor flickered behind his eyes — the last of Corvane, maybe, drowning. Then the green rose, and his hand came up, and the frost on the windows crawled inward like living things.

"Sera Vane," he said, in a voice that was not entirely his, layered deep with something vast and patient and starving. "Little oath-keeper. The king's garden remembers you. Your brother grew such lovely things."

Her knife was in her hand before she knew she'd drawn it. "Don't you say his name."

"He could bloom again. All you have to do is stand aside. Let the garden have the boy Delacroix. One small gardener, traded for everyone you've ever mourned—"

"I buried my brother once," Sera said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "I'm not going to help you dig him up as a decoration."

She threw the firepot.

* * *

Fire bloomed across the colonnade, and frost answered it, and for ninety seconds the longest hallway in Vharrow became a war between seasons. Sera fought the way she'd promised herself she would — like someone settling debts. A Warden-hound came apart under her blade. A second one she simply outran, because Odile had taught her, in three bitter days of drilling, that guild-fighting was not about winning every exchange; it was about choosing which exchanges mattered.

The vault door mattered.

She reached it bleeding from a graze along her ribs, lungs full of smoke, and wrenched the old mechanism open — guild-made, guild-keyed, and thank every forgotten god, it still turned — and fell through into the foundation hall as Corvane's roar shook dust from the rafters above.

The throne room lay beyond, up a short stair, through doors that had not opened honestly in twenty years.

And standing at the bottom of the stair, waiting, was Odile Delacroix, blade drawn, planting herself between Sera and the pursuit like a gate that intended to be shut.

"Go," the old woman said. "We're out of time. I can hear the roots coming through the walls."

"Gran—"

"*Go*, girl. Someone has to hold the door, and I am the only person in this palace who knows how to lock it properly." Her grin was fierce and crooked and exactly like her grandson's. "Besides. I've been waiting forty years to hit something belonging to the crown. Consider this my retirement present."

Sera ran up the stairs.

* * *

The throne room doors were oak and iron, and they were breathing.

Not metaphorically — the wood swelled and shrank, swelled and shrank, slow as sleep, and frost ferned across the iron studs in patterns like ferns, like leaves, like veins. Sera set her shoulder against them anyway, because doors were doors, and hers was the shoulder available.

They gave.

Warmth hit her first — wet, green, rot-and-rose warmth, air like a greenhouse gone to ruin. Vines sheeted the walls. They'd grown up through the floor, through the dais, through the throne itself, and at their center sat King Aldric IV, thirty-eight years old and looking nine hundred, white-haired, hollow-cheeked, bound in green so thoroughly it was impossible to say where the man ended and the garden began.

And his eyes — when they found her — were his own.

"*Huntress.*" His voice came out in pieces, dragged through the leaves. "Huntress, listen — it sleeps, it sleeps at moonset, please — my wife, is my wife—"

"The queen is gone, Majesty." Sera crossed the ruined floor slowly, blade low. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

He wept then, soundlessly, vines trembling with it. "It promised spring. It promised — I only wanted the famine ended, I only wanted—" His jaw seized. The green behind his eyes flared, and when he spoke again the words were slower, thicker, fighting upstream. "The binding. Under the throne. There's a — a seam. Old gold. It *hurts* it, the seam, it keeps the door—"

Behind her, below, steel rang against steel, and Odile's voice rose in a battle-cry in a language Sera didn't know but understood completely.

She found the seam. Old gold, yes — a hairline band circling the base of the dais, guild-work, worn nearly smooth, humming the same hum as the stones in the tunnels. She laid her palm on it and the Verdant *shrieked*, a soundless pressure that buckled her knees and filled the room with the smell of sap and copper.

The vines moved.

They came off the walls all at once, and Sera did the only reasonable thing, which was to stand over the seam with her sword out and inform the ancient hunger of the realm: "No."

It was, in retrospect, not going to be enough. But it was very sincere.

The doors burst open below — Corvane, half-unmade, bark and bloom, with a dozen hounds at his back — and Odile Delacroix met them at the top of the stairs with the guild-blade blazing blue-white in her hands, an old woman holding a doorway against the end of the world, laughing like a girl.

Sera planted her feet over the golden seam, raised her blade, and waited for Dominic to arrive and save them all.

From the sound of his footsteps on the stair — running, alive, cursing fluently in guild-cant — he was going to be just in time.

Or just barely too late. With the Delacroix family, it had always been one or the other.

The Verdant gathered itself like a wave.

Sera braced, and did not step aside.
