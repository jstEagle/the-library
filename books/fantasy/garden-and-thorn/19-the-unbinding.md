## 19. The Unbinding

The throne room of the Pale Palace had not always been pale. Dominic could see it in the bones of the place — the faint rose-gold warmth still clinging to the marble beneath the frost, the ghost of murals behind sheets of ice that climbed the walls like ivy made of winter. Once, this room had been painted with orchards. He could just make out an apple tree under the rime, its branches heavy, a painter's dream of a summer that no one in Vharrow could remember anymore.

Now the only green was the king.

He sat slumped on the Ironrose Throne — so called because roses of black iron twined up its back, and because kings had bled on it often enough to earn the name. Aldric IV had been a broad man once; Dominic had seen his likeness stamped on old coins. What remained was a husk wrapped in ermine, his skin the gray-green of lichen, his chest barely moving. And through that skin, everywhere, moved *roots*. Thin as thread, dark as veins, they spread from his hands and throat and temples into the arms of the throne itself, into the floor, into the very stone of the palace. The Verdant did not sit upon the throne.

The Verdant *was* the throne.

"Dom." His grandmother's voice came low beside him, steady as a hearthstone. Elara Delacroix stood at the foot of the dais with the guild's blade bare in her hands, and even now, even here, she looked less like a hermit of the Thornwood and more like what she truly was: the last master of the Order of the Garden, dressed in patched wool and seventy years of stubbornness. "The circle's yours to draw. Mine to hold. You remember the words?"

"I've been reciting them since the pass," Dominic said. "In my sleep. In my dreams. Once, I think, while being chased by Warden-hounds."

"You were awake for that."

"I was *mostly* awake for that."

Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth and vanished, spent like a candle flame in wind. She pressed the blade into his hands. It was lighter than it should have been, warm as a living thing, and along its edge ran script older than the Sundering — the guild's covenant, written in a language that answered to blood.

"Then let's be about it," she said. "Before Corvane finds his courage."

* * *

They had come through the guild tunnels — Maren's maps, Maren's keys, Maren's furious insistence from three days' ride away that they'd better not die before she got there to yell at them properly. Sera had gone ahead alone into the palace proper, wearing her huntress whites like armor, buying them time by being exactly who she used to be. Somewhere above or below them, he knew, she was holding a corridor against men she had once called brothers, and every minute she bought was a gift he intended to repay by surviving.

He drew the circle in chalk and blood and memory. Three relics, three anchors: the seed-charm buried at the north point, quickening already, a green spark in the frozen floor; the chalice at the west, filled with melted snow that refused to stay cold; the blade in his own two hands at the south. And at the east point, nothing but the king — because the fourth anchor had never been an object at all. The fourth anchor was a Delacroix willing to give everything.

His grandmother took her place at the circle's edge, palms flat against the invisible boundary, and began to speak.

The words were old. Older than the ban, older than the Sundering, older than the shame that had driven the guild underground. They were not words of power, exactly — they were words of *invitation*, which was worse. Magic in Vharrow had been outlawed as a weapon. The guild had always known it was something else entirely: a conversation. A bargain struck between what lives in the soil and what walks on top of it.

And somewhere in the walls of the world, something heard.

The frost on the windows bloomed. Not cracked — *bloomed*, ferning outward in patterns like leaves, like feathers, like the veins of a leaf held up to sun. The temperature dropped so fast his breath turned to falling stars. And beneath the floor, beneath the city, beneath the whole sleeping country, Dominic felt the Verdant turn its attention toward him like a mountain deciding to notice an ant.

*Little gardener,* said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived not through his ears but through his marrow, through the guild-gift coiled dormant in his blood, waking it like spring waking seeds. *You bring me the old words. Who taught you grief?*

"A grandmother," Dominic said aloud, because the rite required speech, and because he would not give this thing the satisfaction of silence. "She taught me that bargains have two sides. You took one. We're here to take it back."

*The bargain kept me alive.* The roots on the throne shifted, and the king's body shuddered, a puppet feeling its strings pulled taut. *Your founders found me starving in the deep wood, dying of a hunger older than their kingdoms. They bound me to the crown and called it mercy. Feed me one life, they said, and I will hold back the frost. One life, renewed each generation. I have kept my half for three hundred years.*

"And the winters?" Dominic set the blade's tip against the circle's south point. "The villages gone quiet? The gardens that bloom with sleepwalkers tending them? Was that your half?"

A long pause. When the voice came again, it was smaller. That was the horror of it — a consciousness vast enough to wear a kingdom, sounding suddenly like something lost.

*I am hungry. Hunger does not do arithmetic.*

"No," Dominic agreed quietly. "It doesn't. That's why we're ending it."

He spoke the next line of the rite, and the circle lit.

* * *

It went wrong the way he should have known it would, because nothing in his life had ever gone right quietly.

The doors burst open at the rite's third turning — not the great doors but the small ones behind the dais, the servants' way, and through them came Corvane.

The Warden-Captain was barely a man anymore. The Verdant's touch had taken him fully in the weeks since Thorn Pass, and what stood there wore Corvane's uniform and Corvane's scars around something else entirely — vines moving visibly beneath his skin like eels under ice, his eyes gone the deep patient green of pond water. In his hand burned a sword of black iron that drank the light.

"The map," the thing that had been Corvane rasped. "The relics. The *line*." Its gaze found Dominic and hungered. "The Verdant sends its regards, little gardener. It would prefer you rooted, not severed. But it accepts either."

"Elara," Dominic said, without taking his eyes off it. "How attached are you to finishing the fourth line?"

"Deeply," his grandmother said, and stepped out of the circle.

"Grandmother—"

"Hold the words, boy. Hold the *blade*." She walked toward Corvane with her empty hands open, seventy years old and utterly unafraid, and Dominic understood with a sick lurch what she meant to do. "I've been the last master for forty years. Let me be the last wall instead."

Corvane-thing lunged. She met it — and Dominic learned in one terrible instant what a guild master could do with no relics, no circle, nothing but a lifetime of craft and a body too old for it. She caught the black blade's first stroke on a shield of woven light that shattered like sugar-glass. She caught the second on nothing but positioning, turning it past her ribs by a finger's width. She caught the third—

She caught the third with her body.

The sound she made was small. That was the worst part. Not a cry — a small, surprised sound, like someone noticing rain had started. And then Elara Delacroix, last master of the Order of the Garden, sank down against the dais steps with black iron in her chest, and looked at her grandson with eyes already going bright and far away.

"The line," she said. "Finish the *line*, Dom. Don't you dare stop for me. Don't you dare make this cost more than it has to."

The Verdant-voice roared through the room — whether in triumph or fury, he couldn't tell. The roots on the throne writhed. Frost raced across the ceiling like a living thing. And Dominic Delacroix stood in a burning circle with a dead woman's blessing and a dying king's salvation in his hands, and finished the line.

* * *

Later — much later, in a life he could not yet imagine — he would try to describe what unbinding felt like, and fail every time.

The closest he ever got was this: it felt like being a river asked to become rain.

The rite's final verse required a Delacroix's blood and magic entire. Blood was easy; he opened his palm on the blade's edge without feeling it. But magic entire — magic entire meant the guild-gift, the thing in him that had woken on the north road and sung in the hollow village and blazed at Thorn Pass. The inheritance passed grandmother to grandson down nine generations. The thing that made him, finally and truly, a mage.

The thing that would not survive the severing. He had known that since the abbey. He had known it when he kissed Sera by the fire, and again this morning in the tunnels, and she had known it too, and neither of them had said it aloud because saying it would have made it a price instead of a choice, and they had agreed — wordlessly, desperately — that this would only ever be a choice.

He poured it out.

The guild-gift rose in him like dawn and he gave it away like water, all of it, the whole bright river of nine generations, and the circle became a column of light that punched through the ceiling and through the storm above and through the Long Winter itself. He felt the binding — three hundred years of it, root wound round root round crown round skull — and he took hold of it the way his grandmother had taught him to take hold of anything:

Gently. Firmly. Like a living thing.

And he cut.

The Verdant screamed. Not in rage — in *relief*, a sound three centuries long, a starving animal finally released from a trap it had forgotten how to leave. The roots tore free of the king's flesh in smoking rivers. The crown of Aldric IV split down the middle and fell in two black halves that rang on the marble like funeral bells. The vast green presence that had worn a kingdom convulsed, contracted, gathered itself — and for one moment, one impossible moment, it was not a monster or a god or a hunger at all.

It was a tired old tree in a deep wood, letting go of its leaves.

*Little gardener,* it said, and its voice was almost kind, and it was fading even as it spoke, dissolving into the light like morning mist. *You cut true. Take back what I stole — I have no more use for stolen things. Tell your order... tell them the garden was never the thorn's enemy. Only its keeper.*

And then it was gone, and the spring came home.

Dominic felt it pass through him on its way to the world — a warmth so total it was nearly pain, three hundred years of hoarded growing-season released in a single tide. Through the frosted windows he saw it go: snow sliding from rooftops in sheets, ice groaning and giving way on the river, and far to the north, beyond the city walls, the Thornwood shivering out of its white death like a sleeper stretching. Somewhere in Vharrow, he knew, crocuses were breaking frozen ground. Somewhere, a hollow village's gardens were filling with people waking from a long walk in a dream.

The light died. The circle guttered out. And Dominic Delacroix knelt on the marble of the Pale Palace, reaching inward for the guild-gift that had lived in him since birth, and finding —

Quiet.

Not emptiness. Quiet. The way a hearth is quiet after the fire goes down. He reached for the little hedge-tricks that had gotten him through thirty years of charm-selling, the warming cantrips, the luck-blessings, the small honest magics of a small honest trade, and found only his own two hands, human and ordinary and shaking.

His magic was dead. The binding was broken, and it had taken the gift with it, and he knelt there in the sudden ordinary silence of his own skull and grieved — briefly, fiercely, privately — for the river he had been.

Then he remembered the dais, and stopped grieving, and moved.

* * *

The king was breathing.

Really breathing — ragged and human and horrified, the breaths of a man surfacing from deep water. Aldric IV lay crumpled at the base of his ruined throne, gray-skinned still but graying toward pink, toward life, his eyes open and fixed on the ceiling and streaming tears that froze on his cheeks and then, slowly, didn't.

"You're the charm-seller," the king whispered. His voice was rusted from disuse. "From the market. The one with the little brass bells."

"That's me," Dominic said. He knelt, checked the man's pulse out of pure habit, and found it thready but stubborn. "Congratulations, Your Majesty. You're mortal again."

The king laughed — a broken, disbelieving sound — and then wept, openly and without dignity, the way men do when something enormous is lifted off them and they finally have the strength to feel what it cost. "Three hundred years," he managed. "I watched. All of it. From inside. I *watched* what it did with my hands—" His gaze slid past Dominic and found the body on the dais steps, and whatever he saw in Dominic's face made him go silent.

Dominic gathered his grandmother up as gently as if she were sleeping, which she wasn't, which she never would again. She was lighter than he expected. Last masters, apparently, weighed less than grandmothers, or perhaps it was only that everything heavy in her had finally been put down.

"You held the line," he told her quietly. "Both lines. The whole blasted garden."

Somewhere beyond the doors, steel rang and voices shouted — Sera, still fighting, still holding, and coming closer. Somewhere below, the palace was waking into chaos, into surrender, into the strange raw morning of a realm with its king returned and its winter ended. Somewhere in a cell, he'd have bet his last coin, Maren was already picking the lock and composing the lecture she'd deliver over his supposed corpse.

All of that waited. None of it mattered yet.

What mattered was the small weight in his breast pocket, where he'd carried it since a Warden raid nearly ended him in a frost-choked market a lifetime ago: his grandmother's guild medallion, warm against his heart, its sigil — the garden and the thorn — catching the new light coming through the clearing windows.

The last master was dead. The last mage was magicless. And the legacy of the Order of the Garden, nine generations of it, now rested entirely in the hands of a charm-seller with a dead gift, a live huntress, and absolutely no idea what came next.

The doors banged open. Sera stood in the frame, bloody, breathing hard, her sword lowered — and behind her, limping, singed, and insufferably pleased with herself, came Maren.

"Well," Maren said, surveying the ruined throne room, the split crown, the weeping king, and Dominic kneeling amid all of it with his grandmother in his arms. "You're alive. Good. Because we are going to need to talk about what happens now — and Dom... why are you looking at that medallion like it just asked you a question?"

Because it had. Because the guild's sigil seemed, in the returning sunlight, almost to glow — and because somewhere in the recovering world outside, the Thornwood was waiting to learn what its keepers would become.

Dominic looked up at the women in the doorway, at the king weeping quietly on the floor, at the spring pouring through the windows like a tide coming home.

"Now," he said, and his voice only shook a little, "we rebuild."
